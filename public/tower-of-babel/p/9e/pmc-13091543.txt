Design and rationale of the SPARC trial: a phase II, single-system randomized controlled trial of immediate transfer to specialty post-arrest care
Authors: Jonathan Elmer, Sara DiFiore-Sprouse, Francis X Guyette, Charity G Patterson, Steffen Ventz, Clifton W Callaway, University of Pittsburgh Post-Cardiac Arrest Service
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOut-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) is common and often fatal. Survivors who reach the hospital require complex, time-sensitive care, but outcomes vary widely across hospitals. Observational data suggest regionalized specialty post-arrest care may improve outcomes, yet the causal effect of an early transfer strategy is uncertain and must be balanced against the risks of interfacility transfer.MethodsSPARC (“The effect of SPecialty cAre on Recovery from Cardiac arrest”) is a phase II, parallel-group randomized controlled trial in a single US health system. We will enroll 1618 adults resuscitated from OHCA who survive to emergency department (ED) care. Under Exception from Informed Consent (21 CFR 50.24), participants will be randomly assigned 1:1 to (1) immediate ED-to-ED transfer to a post-cardiac arrest specialty (PCAS) center or (2) usual care at the presenting hospital. The primary outcome is 90-day modified Rankin Scale (mRS) assessed by a trained, blinded outcome assessor. The primary analysis will be intention-to-treat using a covariate-adjusted Bayesian partial proportional odds model. Interim analyses for superiority/futility will occur at 50% and 75%, with a prespecified adaptive sample-size re-estimation at the first interim analysis.DiscussionSPARC directly tests whether an early transfer strategy to a mature PCAS center improves functional outcomes compared with usual care in a mixed urban, suburban, and rural region. Introduction

Sudden cardiac arrest is a major public health problem in the United States (US) and internationally.1, 2 In the US, over 600,000 people experience cardiac arrest annually, and approximately 150,000 survive to hospital care.1 Despite incremental advances, cardiac arrest remains a leading cause of death, disability, and loss of economic productivity.1, 2, 3, 4

Risk-adjusted outcomes vary by more than 50% across hospitals and are associated with large between-hospital differences in case volume and the provision of recommended treatments.5, 6, 7, 8, 9 These observations prompted the American Heart Association and the National Academies of Medicine to recommend developing comprehensive systems of post-arrest care that may include transferring patients to specialized centers.10, 11, 12, 13 However, these proposed benefits of specialty care may be offset by risks associated with interfacility transfer, including delays in time-sensitive care and transport-related risks. The optimal configuration of post-arrest systems of care and the direct causal effect of specialty care on outcomes remain uncertain.

In our region, short- and long-term survival and functional recovery are more frequent among patients treated at a post-cardiac arrest specialty care (PCAS) center.14, 15 We hypothesize that several specific practices emphasized at the PCAS center contribute to these improved outcomes (Fig. 1), including rapid identification and treatment of reversible arrest etiologies, standardized multimodal neuroprognostication, and coordinated post-acute rehabilitation. Observational data to date cannot exclude whether selection bias nor identify which practices account for improved outcomes at the PCAS center. Therefore, the SPARC trial (“The effect of SPecialty cAre on Recovery from Cardiac arrest”) will use a randomized controlled trial design to provide a strong test of the causal relationship between PCAS center care and improved outcomes.

Hypothesized mechanisms through which a strategy of immediate transfer to specialty post-arrest care might improve outcomes after OHCA will be explored in mechanistic analyses (changes in care processes). The specialty center includes (a) mature infrastructure, expertise, and volume to provide time-sensitive, multidisciplinary post-arrest treatment; (b) validated risk stratification tools; (c) standardized prognostication; and (d) coordinated post-acute rehabilitation.

Study objectives

Primary objective

To determine whether allocation to immediate transfer to a post-cardiac arrest specialty care (PCAS) center improves 90-day modified Rankin Scale compared with usual care.

Key secondary objectives

To compare safety; to assess mechanistic processes of care; to evaluate downstream effects on organ donation; to quantify cost-effectiveness; to understand patient and family experiences; and to assess implementation barriers and facilitators.

Trial design and oversight

SPARC is a phase II, single-system, parallel-group randomized controlled trial (1:1 allocation) with an intention-to-treat analysis. The full study protocol is available online (Supplemental Appendix). The study will be conducted across a single integrated health system with multiple enrolling hospitals and one PCAS center, all governed by a single Institutional Review Board (the University of Pittsburgh Human Research Protection Office [HRPO]). The HRPO reviewed and approved this protocol (STUDY25050152). This protocol manuscript is reported in accordance with SPIRIT guidance.16 Final trial results will be reported according to CONSORT.17 Oversight includes an NHLBI-appointed Data and Safety Monitoring Board (DSMB), a Scientific Advisory Board, a Community Advisory Board, and an Independent Medical Monitor. The trial will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice.

Setting and regional context

Our regional health system includes multiple hospitals with adult general emergency departments (EDs) that serve a mixed urban, suburban, and rural population. A quaternary PCAS center delivers comprehensive post-arrest care via an established Post-Cardiac Arrest Service that treats over 400 post-arrest patients annually. Interfacility transfer is coordinated centrally, with STAT MedEvac providing critical care transport primarily via air ambulance (Fig. 2).

Eligibility will be determined using information available at the time of randomization, recognizing that some criteria will be based on clinical assessment or verbal report. Participants who are subsequently found to be ineligible will remain in the intention-to-treat analysis but will be excluded from a prespecified per-protocol analysis.

Recruitment, consent, and EFIC

ED providers at participating hospitals will screen potentially eligible patients as soon as restoration of pulses (without waiting 20 min to meet traditional criteria for return of spontaneous circulation) so that, if allocated, critical care transport can be arranged in parallel with immediate post-arrest care. The treating clinician will contact on-call study staff to complete screening and, for eligible participants, obtain the randomized allocation; informing the treating team of the allocation constitutes trial enrollment. Because the therapeutic window for initiating interfacility transfer is effectively zero, we will enroll patients immediately after return of pulses rather than requiring 20 min of sustained return of spontaneous circulation.

Given the acute, unplanned nature of OHCA and the frequent absence of a legally authorized representative (LAR) during the narrow therapeutic window, SPARC will primarily enroll under Exception from Informed Consent (EFIC) in accordance with US federal regulations (21 CFR 50.24). This process includes community consultation and public disclosure (CC/PD), an opportunity for potential participants to opt out of trial participation, participant/LAR notification as soon as feasible, and the opportunity for participants to withdraw from continued participation. CC/PD activities include information on multiple options to opt out of trial participation: wearables (a silicone bracelet reading “NO SPARC”); a request via phone, in person conversation or email to be added to an opt out list maintained by the investigative team; and, an opportunity to flag the desire to opt out in the health system’s electronic health record. Prospective informed consent (in person or via secure video) may be obtained when patients have capacity or an LAR is available prior to completion of enrollment procedures. After enrollment, patients who regain capacity or LARs will have the opportunity to withdraw from continued participation.

Randomization, allocation concealment, and blinding

Randomization will be stratified by enrolling site with permuted blocks of variable size, generated by the Data Coordinating Center. Allocation will be concealed in the electronic data capture system until eligibility is confirmed in real time and will then be returned to the treating clinician by on-call study staff. Blinding of patients and clinicians to destination is not possible, but the trial outcome assessor (90-day mRS) will be blinded to treatment allocation.

Because interfacility transfer to the specialty center may occur in both arms based on clinical need or patient/family preference, we distinguish between randomized allocation (the trial intervention) and referral (actual transfer to the specialty center). For example, the treating team for a patient assigned to usual care may decide after some hours that clinical requirements exceed local resources and refer the patient to the specialty center. Referral decisions and timing (e.g., within 12 h, 12–120 h, or >120 h after randomization) will be captured prospectively and incorporated into prespecified secondary and sensitivity analyses.

Interventions

Eligible participants will be randomized to receive one of two treatment strategies:

•Immediate transfer to specialty post-arrest care: coordinated interfacility transfer (ED-to-ED) to the quaternary specialty center with a dedicated Post-Cardiac Arrest Service

Immediate transfer to specialty post-arrest care: coordinated interfacility transfer (ED-to-ED) to the quaternary specialty center with a dedicated Post-Cardiac Arrest Service

•Usual care: admission locally or transfer to the nearest capable hospital based on clinical need, capacity, and standard pathways; this may include referral to the PCAS center when clinically indicated.

Usual care: admission locally or transfer to the nearest capable hospital based on clinical need, capacity, and standard pathways; this may include referral to the PCAS center when clinically indicated.

The only SPARC trial intervention is allocation to an immediate transfer to specialty care versus usual care; all prehospital, in-hospital, and transport care will otherwise be provided per usual clinical practice, and no post-arrest treatments are protocolized by the trial. We anticipate that some patients in each allocated strategy will be treated with the opposite strategy (e.g., transfer in the usual-care arm based on clinical need; deferred/declined transfer in the immediate transfer arm due to weather or family wishes), and we have incorporated this into sample size planning.

Immediate transfer to specialty care

When allocated to immediate referral, a critical care transport team (flight nurse and paramedic) will be dispatched to the referring ED, where they will typically assume bedside care based on standardized post-arrest treatment protocols with real-time physician medical oversight. These crews routinely manage advanced ventilation and vasoactive therapy, can support mechanical circulatory devices in transit, and use continuous physiologic monitoring. After initial stabilization, participants will be transported to the PCAS center by air or ground if air transport is not feasible. Transfer may be deferred to permit time-sensitive interventions at the enrolling hospital (e.g., stabilization, imaging, or procedures) or declined if the treating clinician or patient/family subsequently determines that transfer is not in the participant's best interest; all such decisions will be documented.

On arrival to the hospital with center, clinically-trained members of the University of Pittsburgh Post-Cardiac Arrest Service will meet the patient in the ED to direct continued initial resuscitation, expedite early risk stratification, identify reversible or time-sensitive etiologies, and coordinate coronary angiography and/or mechanical circulatory support when indicated. This will be directed by an advanced practice provider, with an attending physician either physically present or immediately available. The Post-Cardiac Arrest Service currently manages more than 400 patients, most of whom arrive via interfacility transfer.18 This coordination of care by an specialized provider at a well-resourced, high-volume tertiary care center constitutes the primary trial intervention.

Risk stratification will include validated tools such as the Pittsburgh Cardiac Arrest Category (PCAC) along with a focused assessment of neurologic and cardiopulmonary failure.19 After initial stabilization, care will transition to the admitting intensive care unit (ICU) team, while the Post-Cardiac Arrest Service will continue to provide consultation and co-manage neuroprotection and prognostication. Care will include multimodal evaluations and monitoring (e.g., active temperature management, electroencephalography, targeted hemodynamic goals, and adjunctive neuromonitoring in selected cases) with round-the-clock attending support and in-house advanced practice providers.20 For survivors who leave the ICU, the Post-Cardiac Arrest Service will guide secondary prevention and collaborate with Physical Medicine & Rehabilitation to deliver individualized rehabilitation plans and coordinated post-acute follow-up.

Usual care

Participants randomized to usual care will continue management at the presenting hospital or be transferred, using usual interfacility transport practices, to the nearest capable facility according to clinical need, bed availability, and established clinical pathways. Small critical-access hospitals without ICU capability will request interfacility transfer for all participants. Community hospitals with on-site ICUs will deliver general critical care with escalation (when appropriate) to tertiary hospitals in the network that offer advanced care such as mechanical circulatory support.

Care pathways in the usual care arm are not protocolized by the trial and will reflect local practice patterns across treating hospitals. Referral to the specialty center may occur in the usual-care arm when clinically indicated; these referrals will be recorded to support interpretation and to inform future systems-of-care trials.

Outcomes

Primary outcome

Modified Rankin Scale (mRS) at 90 days after arrest, assessed by a trained outcome assessor who is blinded to allocation. For survivors, assessors will determine mRS in person (if participants prefer a visit by an outcome assessor to their current residence or care facility) or by a validated telephone instrument.21, 22 Outcome assessors will complete standardized mRS training/certification and follow a structured contact protocol with multiple modes and attempts to minimize loss to follow-up. For participants whom we cannot contact at 90 days, we will determine vital status via the electronic health record, contact with next of kin, and/or public sources. If needed, 30-day mRS will be used to impute missing 90-day outcomes.

Secondary outcomes

Additional secondary outcomes (Table 1) include key processes of care (e.g., coronary angiography/revascularization, temperature management, neuroprognostic testing, and withdrawal of life-sustaining therapies), discharge disposition and rehabilitation referral, organ donation outcomes, and psychosocial outcomes among survivors at 90 days (Patient Health Questionnaire-9, Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7, Disability Rating Scale, modified Fatigue Impact Scale, and EQ-5D).

Statistical analysis

The primary estimand is the intention-to-treat effect of allocation to an immediate referral strategy versus usual care on the distribution of 90-day mRS. The primary analysis will use an covariate-adjusted Bayesian partial proportional odds ordinal regression with weakly informative priors and adjustment for shockable rhythm, witnessed collapse, layperson cardiopulmonary resuscitation, age, and arrest duration.23, 24 For transparency, we will report unadjusted Bayesian and frequentist secondary 90-day mRS analyses.

Two interim analyses are planned at 50% and 75% information. Interim stopping for superiority or futility will be considered. Posterior probability thresholds will be calibrated via a prespecified resampling-based algorithm to control a one-sided familywise type I error of 0.10, with alpha allocated across the interim and final analyses as 0.01, 0.01, and 0.08. If the trial continues beyond the first interim analysis, we will consider a single sample-size re-estimation up to a maximum of N = 2100 patients if (i) the Bayesian predictive power at the planned maximum sample size (N = 1618) is less than 0.85, (ii) but the posterior probability of a positive treatment effect exceeds 0.65. Futility stopping will be considered if Bayesian predictive power at the original sample size (first interim analysis) or revised sample size (second interim analysis) is less than 0.10 at either interim analysis.

Secondary and exploratory analyses will evaluate key safety endpoints, psychosocial outcomes, processes of care, and mechanistic mediation using models appropriate to each outcome type.25, 26, 27 Detailed methods are prespecified in the statistical analysis plan.

We powered the trial for the ordinal 90-day mRS using a Bayesian partial proportional odds model. Simulations incorporated key real-world features of post-arrest care, including 5–15% early deaths prior to receiving allocated treatment, approximately 44% treatment non-responders due to irrecoverable primary brain injury among those surviving to initiation of care, anticipated referral patterns (approximately 10% referral to the PCAS center in the usual-care arm and 5% deferred/declined transfer in the immediate transfer arm), and 5% loss to follow-up.

Under the alternative hypothesis in which immediate transfer to specialty care produces a clinically meaningful improvement in mRS distribution equivalent to a 5% absolute reduction in overall mortality (80–75%) with corresponding 1.25% absolute increases in each of mRS 0–3, the unadjusted odds ratio for improved outcome is approximately 1.37. With a one-sided type I error of 0.10, a total sample of 1618 provides 85% power. A one-sided 0.10 error rate is appropriate for a phase II trial in a high-mortality condition where a null or harmful effect yields the same policy conclusion (do not proceed), and any positive result will require confirmation in a larger multicenter trial. Although the primary analysis will be covariate-adjusted to improve precision, we did not assume any power gain from covariate adjustment in sample size simulations.

Monitoring and safety

The trial’s DSMB will monitor enrollment, data quality, safety, and interim analyses. Because adverse events are common after cardiac arrest and many reflect underlying illness, active safety surveillance will focus on events plausibly related to transfer strategy, including prespecified physiologic and device-related safety outcomes during the interval from randomization through ICU admission and through 24 h after randomization. Serious adverse events will be captured through 7 days after enrollment, with reporting to the IRB, sponsor, DSMB, and Independent Medical Monitor per institutional and sponsor requirements.

Data management, auditing, and access to data

Trial data will be captured via case report forms in a secure, role-based electronic data capture system hosted by the University of Pittsburgh School of Health and Rehabilitation Sciences Data Center, with encrypted transmission, firewall protection, single-sign-on with multifactor authentication, and a complete audit trail of all create/edit/delete events.

Data quality will be promoted by pre-deployment user-acceptance testing, real-time range and logic checks, query resolution by the Data Coordinating Center, monthly quality reports with manual verification of prespecified critical variables, and a prespecified database lock approximately 3 months after the last primary outcome. The University of Pittsburgh Human Research Protection Office may conduct periodic or for-cause audits independent of the investigative team.

Only an independent statistician and the DSMB will have access to group-level unblinded data. Access to the working dataset will be controlled by the Data Coordinating Center through role-restricted permissions and user-access logs. Following lock, fully de-identified datasets and comprehensive documentation will be deposited with NHLBI repositories in accordance with NIH data sharing policy.

Comparator trials and distinctiveness

The ARREST trial compared prehospital randomization to direct EMS transport to cardiac arrest centers versus usual care in the United Kingdom and reported neutral results.28 SPARC addresses a complementary but distinct question: whether an early interfacility referral strategy after usual care ED presentation improves outcomes in a U.S. system where most post-arrest patients initially present to community hospitals and where post-arrest care capabilities and volumes vary substantially. In SPARC, randomization will occur at the presenting ED instead of the prehospital setting and will occur in parallel with hospital-based clinical efforts to stabilize the participant.

In ARREST, specialty centers were defined mainly by cardiac procedural capability and cardiac critical care; many lacked ED infrastructure, established expertise in prognostication, or organized post-acute rehabilitation. In contrast, SPARC evaluates a comprehensive specialty-care bundle spanning emergency and neurocritical care, standardized multimodal prognostication, and organized post-acute services. In addition, because ARREST mandated protocolized prognostication in both arms, it may have attenuated a key mechanism of benefit that SPARC is designed to test.

Patient and public involvement

We have and will continue to involve patients, caregivers, emergency medical services (EMS) professionals, and community partners throughout trial design, conduct, and dissemination. A Community Advisory Board comprising OHCA survivors, family members, EMS personnel, emergency and critical care clinicians, and representatives from rural and historically underserved communities will meet at least twice annually to review study plans, recruitment and notification materials, and emerging findings.

Because enrollment proceeds under EFIC, we conducted community consultation and public disclosure (CC/PD) prior to first enrollment. Community consultation included town-hall style events at hospitals and community venues, small-group discussions with emergency department staff, and outreach to civic and faith-based organizations. Public disclosure used multiple channels – the trial website (https://www.postarrest.org/sparc), printed materials at emergency departments and clinics, social media, and press releases and local media to explain trial rationale, procedures, risks, potential benefits, and opt-out options. Feedback from consultation sessions and brief surveys were summarized and used to refine CC/PD materials and operational procedures; plans were reviewed and approved by the Human Research Protections Office prior to implementation. Study results will be disseminated to clinicians, health systems, and policymakers.

Funding, trial status, and timeline

Funding is provided via the NHLBI (R61HL175113 and R33HL175113). The sponsor has no role in the analysis plan beyond standard data and safety monitoring and trial oversight. Protocol version 1.22 has been approved by the DSMB and the single IRB. Community consultation and public disclosure has been completed. The first enrollment is expected in May 2026 with a planned recruitment period of approximately 4.5 years. The trial was prospectively registered on ClinicalTrials.gov on February 5, 2026 (NCT07002294); any protocol amendments and updates to recruitment dates will be reflected on ClinicalTrials.gov.

Conclusion

Based on mixed observational evidence and analogy to other acute conditions,29, 30, 31 professional bodies including the American Heart Association and the National Academies of Medicine have called for development of systems of post-arrest care.10, 11, 12, 13 How best to design and implement these systems, and whether a strategy of immediate transfer to specialized care improves patient-centered outcomes without undue harms, remain key knowledge gaps11 that will be addressed by the SPARC trial.
