Effects of Computer Assisted Design‐Computer Assisted Manufacturing Nickel‐Titanium Fixed Lingual Retainers Versus Conventional Flexible Multistrand Retainers on Tooth Displacements and Periodontal Ligament Stress Distributions in the Mandibular Anterior Teeth at Four Different Inclinations: A Finite Element Analysis
Authors: Hanie Daryanavard, Marzieh Mazhari, Mohsen Mazhary, Hooman Ghazi Oskouei, Mehrnaz Moradinejad, Shole Shahi, Vahid Rakhshan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsLittle information is available on tooth displacement and periodontal ligament (PDL) stress distribution caused by computer assisted design‐computer assisted manufacturing (CAD/CAM) retainers. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to compare CAD/CAM and multistrand retainers in terms of PDL stresses, risk of root resorption, and displacements of mandibular anterior teeth with 4 different inclinations using finite element analysis.MethodsIn this computer‐based finite element simulation, eight models consisting of the six mandibular anterior teeth, their PDLs, and surrounding alveolar bones were created. Four models simulated conventional fixed retainers with multistrand wires; four others simulated CAD/CAM nickel‐titanium fixed retainers. Within each retainer group, four different tooth inclinations relative to the horizontal plane (inclination angles of 110°, 100°, 90°, and 80°) were modeled. All models were subjected to a vertical pressure of 42 psi (0.289 MPa, exerted on the incisal surfaces) and a lingual force of 10 N. Calculated were PDL stresses, the risk of root resorption (defined as areas where PDL stress exceeded 0.0047 MPa), and dental displacements along the buccolingual and intrusive/extrusive axes.ResultsWith fixed multi‐strand retainers, the buccolingual displacements of incisor and canine crowns are 3.10, 6.10, and between 2.82 and 5.32 µm in the lingual side. As the inclination increases, displacement and strain are often increased. In the CAD/CAM models, the buccolingual displacements of the crown of incisors and canines were between 2.94 and 4.76 µm and 3.34 and 4.01 µm in the lingual side, respectively. By increasing the inclination, the displacement and strain often decreased.ConclusionAlthough this FEA study showed some differences between models, no clinical recommendation is possible. Clinical decisions must also consider breakage modes, undetected debonding risk, and patient follow‐up.

Introduction

The stability of orthodontic treatment is a major clinical concern and the most challenging part of treatment [1, 2]. In many orthodontic treatments, the teeth are in an unstable position, and after removing the orthodontic appliance, orthodontists either have to accept relapse or use permanent retainers [2, 3]. In other words, in many cases, long‐term or permanent retention is the only way to maintain the alignment after orthodontic treatment [4]. Retainers can be removable or fixed [2, 5]. Removable retainers, such as Hawley and clear retainers are commonly used in the clinic [5]. However, the gold standard of stability is fixed multistrand retainers that are placed lingually [6]. Fixed retainers include multistrand wires or reinforced composite fibers [7].

Removable retainers provide the benefits of requiring less chair time and retaining occlusion [5]. However, the primary issue with these retainers is their reliance on patient compliance [5]. Fixed retainers have their own advantages and drawbacks. The most important advantage of multistrand wires is their irregular surface, which increases the mechanical retention of bonding materials [7]. Besides, these types of retainers are flexible and allow the teeth to move physiologically [8]. Disadvantages of fixed retainers include high failure rates, plaque accumulation, and poor oral hygiene [9]. Technological advancements reduce these disadvantages.

One of the newest lingual retainers is nickel‐titanium retainer made using computer assisted design‐computer assisted manufacturing (CAD/CAM) [10, 11], which may have multiple enhancements compared to traditional fixed retainers: CAD/CAM nickel‐titanium retainers follow the lingual contours and embrasures of the anterior teeth, providing better stability and reducing occlusal interferences and tongue irritation [6, 11]. A proper adaptation of the CAD/CAM nickel‐titanium wire might reduce the rate of breakage and deformation of the wire, noting that the failure of fixed retainers commonly occurs in lingual embrasure spaces [10, 12]. Nickel‐titanium wires that are bent using CAD/CAM and then electropolished might have a smooth, polished, and corrosion‐resistant surface, resulting in less bacterial plaque accumulation [6, 11]. Besides, nickel‐titanium wires might be more flexible than multi‐stranded stainless‐steel wires; this might improve physiological tooth movements [6, 11].

The inclination of the teeth plays a key role in their stress distribution and displacement under masticatory forces. Protruded teeth may be the most vulnerable to vertical masticatory forces [13]. Depending on treatment plan, the posttreatment inclinations of mandibular incisors may vary [13]. After treatments involving extraction, incisors will be upright. However, after nonext treatments, incisors may tip labially [13]. The retrusion or protrusion of incisors increase the risk of complications such as bone resorption, gingival recession, fenestration, and attached gingiva loss [14].

Various factors should be taken into account while choosing the retainer type [2, 3, 5]. These include minimizing periodontal damage. This topic has not been yet studied for CAD/CAM nickel‐titanium lingual retainers. Hence, their biomechanical advantages and disadvantages remain unknown. Therefore, this study was conducted. Its primary aim was to compare stability and clinical efficiency of multistrand fixed and CAD/CAM nickel‐titanium retainers using 3D stimulation of the stresses and displacements of mandibular anterior teeth with four different inclinations. As secondary outcomes, we also compared CAD/CAM and multistrand retainers in terms of PDL stresses and risk of root resorption. As clinical implications, the results of this study can help to choose the correct fixed retainer in different clinical situations.

Materials and Methods

This was an in silico finite element study (FEA). The finite element (FE) model was constructed from a CBCT (cone beam computed tomography) scan acquired using a Siemens system, with a slice thickness and isotropic voxel size of 0.2 mm. The imaging parameters followed standard clinical protocols. Segmentation was performed using dedicated medical imaging software with a semi‐automatic approach, followed by manual refinement to ensure anatomical accuracy. The segmented models were reviewed by experienced clinicians, confirming their validity for use in the study. The CBCT scan was taken retrospectively, and only because of diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. The protocol and its ethics were approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences, Ahvaz, Iran (ethical code: IR.AJUMS.REC.1401.114). Since the CBCT scan was available retrospectively before the study, the need for any informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki; all experimental protocols were approved by the IRB of Ahvaz Jundishapur University of Medical Sciences, Ahvaz, Iran.

Models

Using 3‐Matic (Materialise NV; Brussels, Belgium) and Mimics 3D image processing software (Mimics Research 21; Materialise), a standard model of mandible comprising the bone, teeth, and PDLs was created. Initially, Mimics was used to import 16‐bit monochrome CBCT scan slices (NewTom VGi; Finland) of a young man with a resolution of 768 × 768 and a 1 mm gap between the slices. Masks for the PDLs, teeth, and bones were made using segmentation tools. The Calculate 3D command was then used to construct a 3D model of these parts. The initial model included cancellous bone, 1 mm of cortical bone, PDL with a thickness of 0.25 mm, and the anterior and posterior teeth. The teeth had normal anatomical shapes of crowns and roots. The PDL was modeled as an isotropic, linear elastic material to maintain numerical stability and to allow clear interpretation of the comparative outcomes. While hyperelastic constitutive models such as Ogden's formulation can represent the nonlinear behavior of the PDL in greater detail, their implementation requires subject‐specific material parameters that remain highly variable and insufficiently standardized in the literature. The isotropic assumption adopted here has been widely used and validated in orthodontic FE studies and is considered appropriate for comparative analyses under small orthodontic force levels. Spongy (cancellous) bone was explicitly considered in the FE model and was defined as a distinct anatomical structure during the segmentation process. Its material properties were specified in the manuscript and incorporated into the analysis. The modeling approach ensured that the presence and mechanical behavior of cancellous bone were appropriately accounted for in the simulation of tooth displacement and periodontal ligament (PDL) loading.

By changing the inclination angles of the anterior teeth, eight three‐dimensional models were derived from the initial model. The models were divided into two groups of four models each. In the first group, the fixed multistrand retainer was simulated on the lingual surface of the anterior teeth using Mimics software. In the second group, the CAD/CAM nickel‐titanium retainer was modeled. In each group, the inclinations of the teeth in the four models (i.e., their angle with the horizontal plane) were 80°, 90°, 100°, and 110° [13]. Therefore, Models 1–4 were mandibular fixed multistrand wire retainer with a size of 0.0195 inches on the lingual surface of the ‎anterior mandibular teeth (canine to canine) with an IMPA (lower incisor mandibular plane angle) of 110°‎ (model 1), 100° (model 2), 90° (model 3), and 80° (model 4). Models 5–8 were mandibular CAD/CAM retainer of NiTi alloy with a size of 14 × 14” on the lingual surface of the anterior mandibular teeth (canine to canine) with an IMPA of 110° (model 5), 100° (model 6), 90° (model 7), and 80° (model 8).

Geometry Conversion

After creating all the models, different parts in ‘.stl’ format were extracted from the software and entered into Geomagic software (3D Systems, Morrisville, North Carolina, United States) to convert to ‘.stp’ file format. In Geomagic, using the ‘Auto Surface’ command, all components were meshed and converted to parasolid or ‘.stp’ format.

Meshing and Analysis

After converting all geometries into ‘.stp’ format, these geometries were transferred to Ansys software (ANSYS Workbench 2021, ANSYS Inc, Canonsburg, Pennsylvania, USA) for analysis. For point analysis, all components were meshed in Ansys. The number of nodes was 194828. The FE model was discretized using three‐dimensional tetrahedral elements. A global element size of approximately 0.01 mm was applied. While local mesh refinement was performed in critical regions, particularly the PDL, the element size was reduced to approximately 0.001 mm to capture stress and strain gradients accurately. Mesh quality was assessed based on standard criteria, including element skewness and aspect ratio, ensuring acceptable numerical performance. In addition, a mesh convergence study was conducted by progressively refining the mesh until variations in the primary outcome measures became negligible, confirming the independence of the results (Figure 1).

An example of meshing.

Boundary Conditions

Boundary conditions were then set. These involved identifying the model's fixed components and exerting pressure on them. At the top, the mandible was immobilized at the condyles. In the FEA models, the locks were assumed to be rigidly bonded to the bracket surfaces on the anterior teeth, simulating the clinical attachment. This simplification ensures that the applied forces are effectively transmitted to the teeth while maintaining numerical stability.

Material Properties

All the elastic, homogeneous, and isotopic living tissues were defined in the program. Elastic properties, Young's coefficient, and Poisson's ratio were applied for each material (Table 1). The relationships between the teeth, their PDL, the spongy and cortical bones, and also between the multi‐strand wire with composite and the teeth were provided by contact elements.

Occlusal Force and Movement

The condyles were fixed to simulate boundary conditions [13]. The wire load was simulated by applying a 10‐Newton lingual force at one‐third of the labio‐incisal length of the six anterior teeth. This approach represents the action of the wire on the teeth within the FE model. Hence, it simulates clinically relevant tooth movements.

According to a textbook, the bite pressure on incisors ranges between 35 and 50 psi [19]. We calculated the rounded median of this range, as the a bite pressure equivalent to 42 psi, which also translates to 0.289 MPa [19]. The bite force is expressed in stress units because of compatibility with the PDL and tooth pressures, and also for compatibility with the textbook [19].

Outcomes

All 3D displacements were assessed, in mm, at three parts of each tooth: its tip or incisal edge, cervical region, and apex. The von Mises stresses (in MPa) were as well computed. Moreover, PDL stress of the anterior teeth in the apical and cervical regions were calculated. The maximum and minimum stress of PDL were recorded in MPa or KPa (1/1000 of MPa, 1000 Pascals). Dental displacements in the buccolingual and intrusive/extrusive (vertical) axes were estimated at the middle of the incisal edge, the cervical area, and the apices. The averages of the displacements on the right and left sides were calculated for all teeth. Similarly, the averages of PDL hydrostatic stresses on the right and left sides were calculated for each tooth. Root resorption risk may increase because of reduced blood flow caused by PDL hydrostatic pressures exceeding local capillary pressure. A PDL capillary pressure may be 0.002–0.005 MPa [20]. Therefore, a critical pressure of 0.0047 MPa (i.e., 4700 Pascals or 4.7 KPa) was used as a threshold for a substantial increase in the probability of external root resorption when comparing compressive hydrostatic stresses at the PDLs [20, 21, 22, 23].

In model 7 (IMPA = 90°, CAD/CAM retainer), (Table 3) the maximum PDL stresses for the central and lateral incisors exceeded 0.0047 MPa. In no other models, PDL stresses exceeded this threshold (Figures 4 and 5, Table 4).

Comparing Retainer Types in Terms of Displacements and PDL Stress

Buccolingual movements: In models with FMS retainers, in all the assessed three dental areas, the amount of buccolingual movement increased with the increase of the angle (Figure 6). In models with CAD/CAM retainers, as the inclination angle increases, the amount of lingual displacement at the tooth tip decreases. In the apical area, a typical trend was not seen, and only the lateral incisors and canines with an angle of 80° showed the most displacement (Figure 6).

Comparing Retainer Types in Terms of the Role of IMPA

At IMPA = 110°, the displacements of PDL stress were higher in the FMS model than CAD/CAM. At IMPA = 100°, these parameters were greater in the FMS model, except in the case of average PDL stress of the central incisors was higher in the CAD/CAM model. At IMPA = 90°, these were higher in the CAD/CAM model, except for the average PDL stress of canines, which was similar in both retainer types. At IMPA = 80°, these parameters were higher in the CAD/CAM model (Figures 6, 7, 8, Tables 2, 3, 4).

Discussion

The most important challenge in orthodontic treatment is the retention of treatment results [1, 2]. According to previous clinical trials, it has been shown that the mandibular anterior teeth are the most susceptible to relapse [24]. Little [25] found that 70%–90% of cases had undergone some degree of relapse in the mandibular teeth, while the maxillary teeth were less affected [25, 26]. Myser et al. [27] reported that 17% of the population showed severe relapse [27]. In a study by Gerami et al. [13], the incisal edge movement in multistrand models was between 20.4 and 453 µm, while in the apical region, the range was 10.9–311 µm [13]. In the present study, in models using flexible multistrand retainers, the buccolingual movements of the tips ranged, respectively, between 3.10 and 6.10 µm for incisors and between 2.82 and 5.32 µm for canines. Apices moved between 1.50 and 3.07 µm towards the lingual, and the higher rate of movement occurred in the central incisor compared to the other teeth. The difference in the range of movements may be due to the greater forces applied in the other study (187 N). Gerami et al. [13] observed opposite movements in the incisal and apex regions [13], while in the present study, all teeth moved lingually. The possible cause may be that the previous study did not consider the force applied to the teeth due to protrusive movements of the jaw [13]. In the current study, the central incisors had the greatest extents of displacement, while the canines had the least amounts of movement. Gerami et al. confirmed this pattern of movement [13]. In terms of stress as well, central incisors were more affected by IMPA alterations, compared with other teeth. Unlike the centrals, the canines did not show considerable changes in stress as a function of IMPA alterations. The findings of Grami et al. as well showed more changes in the incisor teeth compared to the canines [13]. The reason might be the original inclination of these teeth as compared to the direction of the force, as well as their morphological differences, for example, mandibular canines have a much larger roots compared to incisors.

According to Gerami et al. [13], incisal edge movements in multistrand models ranged between 20.4 and 453 µm, while in the apical region, the range was 10.9 to 311 µm [13]. In the present study, in models using multistrand fixed retainers, the buccolingual movements of the tips of the teeth ranged between 3.10 and 6.10 µm for incisors and between 2.82 and 5.32 µm for canines. Root apices moved lingually; the central incisor showed the greatest movement. The difference in the range of movements may be due to the greater forces applied in the other study (187 N). Another point to consider is the directions of movements. Gerami et al. [13] observed opposite movements in the incisal and apex regions [13]. However, in the present study, all tooth areas moved lingually. A possible reason for the different results may be that the force applied to the teeth due to protrusive movements of the jaw was not considered in the previous study [13]. In the present study, the canines displaced less than other teeth. This was in line with the results of Gerami et al. [13].

The posttreatment inclination of mandibular incisors may have a role. Orthodontic cases involving extraction usually present more upright incisors, while nonext cases often show labial tipping. Since vertical masticatory forces can act unfavorably on labially tipped teeth, these teeth may be at a higher risk of displacement. In the present study, multistrand retainers showed greater tooth movements and PDL stresses in models with greater IMPA values. This was in agreement with the results of Gerami et al. [13]. In the incisal and cervical regions, multistrand retainers caused greater movement, while CAD/CAM retainers showed higher apical displacement and stress. In both groups, canines showed the least movement and stress, confirming their stabilizing role. Our findings also showed that central incisors were more affected by inclination changes compared with canines. This is in agreement with the observations by Gerami et al. [13], and may be due to morphological differences and the direction of applied forces. Higher PDL stress levels were observed in CAD/CAM models of the present study compared to multistrand models. The method of splinting may change the pattern of tooth movement in the apical and incisal regions. Shraddha et al. [28] as well showed that CAD/CAM retainers might induce more uniform and smaller displacements, while multistrand wires concentrated forces on the central incisors.

Finite element analysis (FEA) is a non‐invasive method that is gaining popularity in dentistry and medicine [29, 30, 31]. Finite element models (FEM) derived from medical imaging have evolved considerably over the past decades, driven by advances in imaging, segmentation, and mesh generation methods. Early efforts in the 1990s and 2000s focused on extracting geometry from stacks of computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) slices and converting them into 3D meshes suitable for FEA, often requiring substantial manual intervention and specialized software (e.g., early biomedical imaging analysis and mesh generation workflows) [30, 32]. With the introduction of automated and semi‐automated tools, researchers were able to segment regions of interest directly from volumetric DICOM data and generate FE meshes for biomechanical simulation, demonstrating proof‐of‐concept for image‐based FE modeling in soft tissues and organs [30]. More recent literature emphasizes structured workflows that include DICOM image segmentation, 3D reconstruction, surface improvement, meshing, and assignment of material properties, enabling anatomically accurate, subject‐specific models for musculoskeletal applications and beyond [29, 30, 31].

Increased PDL stresses above 4.7 KPa suggest a theoretically higher risk of external apical root resorption (EARR), but this must be interpreted cautiously because FEA does not model remodeling, repair, or patient specific biology. The assessment of PDL hydrostatic pressures in this study showed merely a few cases that might potentially be associated with root resorption. These were in models 2 (only central incisors), 6 (only central incisors), and 7 (both central and lateral incisors). All of these might happen only at the maximum PDL pressures estimated, not in the case of average PDL pressures which were much lower. Of course, orthodontic retention is not expected to considerably increase the risk of root resorption the way orthodontic tooth movement can. No other study was available in this regard. Overall, it can be concluded that to stabilize treatment outcomes, a CAD/CAM retainer is recommended when the IMPA is about 100° or 110°; whereas, fixed multistrand retainer provides better stability when IMPA is 80° or 90° and thus is recommended in such cases. CAD/CAM retainers can be used safely in the case of proclined teeth, while long‐term use of multistrand retainers should be avoided in these cases. Also, it is better to use a multistrand retainer when the teeth are upright. Of course, these suggestions require clinical verification because of FEA studies' limitation in simulating long‐term biological mechanisms. There are several possible processes for root resorption, including the root's interaction with the cortical bone. On the other hand, the roots' long‐term migration in the direction of the bone cortex and their interaction with the cortical bone are caused by biological processes. Consequently, research using FEs are unable to investigate these long‐term, biologically‐based processes. Accurately representing the PDL in FEA is essential for realistic predictions of stress, strain, and mechanical behavior in dental biomechanics, especially in orthodontic and mastication studies [33]. Many early FE studies model the PDL as a linear elastic material due to ease of implementation and convergence stability. However, reported elastic moduli vary widely in the literature (from as low as ~0.01 MPa up to orders of magnitude higher) leading to inconsistencies in results [34, 35, 36]. Linear elastic models often assume isotropy and homogeneity, but these assumptions can oversimplify the true mechanical behavior [35]. Hyperelastic constitutive models capture nonlinear stress–strain behavior observed experimentally, where the PDL stiffness increases with applied load. Such models (e.g., Mooney‐Rivlin or Ogden) better fit large deformation behavior than linear elasticity [37]. Some studies use bilinear or exponential stress–strain relationships to capture initial compliance followed by increased stiffness [35]. Viscoelastic models incorporate time‐dependent behavior (e.g., creep or stress relaxation), reflecting fluid movements and microstructural rearrangements in the ligament [36, 37, 38]. Combined hyperelastic‐viscoelastic formulations have been developed and validated against in vitro and in vivo experiments, showing better agreement with experimental load–displacement responses [36, 37, 39]. Some FE models integrate structural features like collagen fiber orientation and distribution, moving beyond homogeneous material assumptions to fiber‐matrix constitutive formulations. These attempts aim to reflect tissue anisotropy and the underlying microstructure [35]. Another factor to consider is determination of PDL Thickness in FE models. Many FE studies adopt a uniform PDL thickness (e.g., 0.25 mm) for simplicity, based on average anatomical measurements [13, 40, 41]. However, actual PDL thickness varies locally around the tooth root, often thicker near the alveolar crest and apical regions and thinner mid‐root [37].

This study was limited by some factors. In this study, the pressure exerted over the anterior teeth was different from previous FEA studies that had simulated the anterior bite force [13, 41]. It should be noted that we used the median of the range of bite pressures reported in a textbook [19]. Therefore, their results must be confirmed through in vitro and clinical studies. In addition, this FEA study is essentially static and difficult to implement in clinical settings since it is based on hypotheses drawn from the average qualities of bone, teeth, or other structures [42]; in this regard, the assumption of a 0.25 mm uniform PDL thickness and linear elastic, isotropic material behavior is standard, but in comparison with more advanced nonlinear PDL models, it is a limited model. Studies of FE only look at very rapid, very short‐lived, and static mechanical interactions. The biological changes occurring over an extended length of time are not and cannot be examined by them [43]. Nevertheless, FEM research can serve as a starting point for a series of investigations that will eventually lead to animal and human trials, as clinical testing of innovative and unproven techniques might be unethical [22, 23, 43]. Besides, the FE technique has an advantage over clinical or even in vitro research, since it can provide a very accurate and thorough estimation of mechanics of the entire system as well as each of its component pieces. This might not be achieved with any other method. Future research should consider the clinical comparison of multistrand and CAD/CAM retainers in different inclinations. They should also compare CAD/CAM retainers with different alloys. It would be of interest to also compare multistrand and CAD/CAM retainers in teeth with root and bone surface resorptions.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this FEA simulation, it can be concluded that: 1.In models with CAD/CAM retainers, teeth with 80° of IMPA had the greatest displacement and PDL stress, while the teeth with 110° of inclination had the smallest movement and PDL stress.2.In models with fixed multistrand retainers, teeth had the most displacement and stress at IMPAs of 110° and 100°, respectively.3.PDL stresses may change as a function of IMPA alterations. In this regard, the stress of centrals' PDLs changes more than those of canines and lateral incisors as a function of IMPA. Canine teeth displace less under masticatory forces and maintain their stability better than the other anterior teeth.
