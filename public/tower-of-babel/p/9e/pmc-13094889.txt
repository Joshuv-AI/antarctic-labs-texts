Investigating the efficacy of mentalization-based psychoeducational intervention on self-harm, difficulty in emotional regulation, and mentalization of soldiers with borderline personality disorder who engage in self-harm behaviors
Authors: Amir Mahdi Katani, Mohammad Reza Ebrahimi, Amir Mohsen Rahnejat, Mojgan Mohammadimehr, Hassan Shahmiri Barzoki, Leili Jamil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:One of the populations at high risk for self-harm are soldiers. Specifically, this risk is even greater if they have borderline personality disorder. The purpose of this study was to determine the extent to which mentalization-based training is effective in reducing self-harm behavior, difficulties in emotion regulation, and mentalization in soldiers with borderline personality disorder.MATERIALS AND METHODS:The study was a quasiexperimental study with a control group and pretest, post-test, and 2-month follow-up periods. This research was conducted in 2023–2024. Fifty soldiers with borderline personality disorder who self-harmed were selected as subjects using the convenience sampling method. One experimental group (25 people) and one control group (25 people) were randomly selected from the subjects. The control group was wait-listed, while the experimental group received six mentalization-based training sessions. Subjects completed questionnaires measuring self-harm (SHI), mentalizing (MZQ), and difficulties in emotion regulation (DERS). The data were analyzed in the descriptive section using mean, frequency, and standard deviation and in the inferential statistics section using repeated measures analysis of variance.RESULTS:The results showed that mentalization-based training significantly improved self-harm behaviors, emotion regulation problems, and mentalization in soldiers with borderline personality disorder and a history of self-harm (P < 0.01).CONCLUSIONS:Training based on mentalizing appears to be an effective way to treat emotional issues and self-harming behaviors in soldiers with borderline personality disorder, given the role that mentalizing capacity plays in preventing these behaviors.

Introduction

Soldiers often suffer from mental disorders,[1] and their special circumstances and psychological stresses of military service increase the likelihood of suicide and self-harm.[2] Findings show that self-harm is common among conscripts at about 6.3% and among recruits at 7.9%. Furthermore, nearly 47% of soldiers reported that they had seriously considered suicide at least once.[3] A study on suicide rates in Iran found that more than 30% of soldiers seriously considered suicide.[2] Although this is lower than other countries, it is still an alarming figure compared to Middle Eastern countries.[4]

On the other hand, there is a link between the likelihood of self-harming and certain mental illnesses. Borderline personality disorder is among the most significant of these conditions.[5] Studies have shown that 10% of people with borderline personality disorder will commit suicide, and 65–80% of those affected will engage in self-harm.[6] A study with 24 years of follow-up found that approximately 6% of people with borderline personality disorder committed suicide, and 87% of suicide victims had not been treated for their condition before suicide.[7] The suicide rate among these people was approximately six times higher than that of other patients.[7] Therefore, it is especially important to screen and treat self-harm behavior in these patients who serve in the military as working in military service is also a factor associated with the likelihood of self-harm.

Also, problems with emotion regulation are one of the most common symptoms in these patients and are also an important factor contributing to the occurrence of self-harm[8] since impulsivity and emotional instability characterize people with borderline personality disorder, making them much more likely to harm themselves.[910] In fact, these patients engage in self-harm as a way of coping with essentially intolerable emotions. Moreover, emotional problems are associated with increased interpersonal problems, such as rejection, which borderline people are particularly prone to.[11] People may resort to self-harm to get rid of these negative feelings of rejection, which in turn increases feelings of burden and a sense of neutral belonging.[12] For this reason, it is particularly important that treatment and research focus on difficulties in emotion regulation in these individuals.

To date, numerous treatments, especially pharmacological treatments, have been developed for borderline people. However, this approach has significant problems, particularly with regard to adverse effects.[13] However, the mentalization approach, designed specifically for patients with BPD, addresses the issues of emotion regulation and self-identity, which are one of the most important factors contributing to the improvement of symptoms in patients with BPD.[14] The ability to understand oneself and others through mental states, including thoughts, feelings, and emotions, is called mentalization.[15] Patients with BPD were inherently uncertain in their relationships with their primary environment and caregivers, where their mentalizing abilities are established.[16] In this sense, people with BPD are 13 times more likely to report childhood adversity than nonclinical controls.[17] The purpose of this study was to examine the effectiveness of mentalization-based training on self-harm behavior, emotion regulation, and mentalization abilities in soldiers with borderline personality disorder. To date, there have been a number of studies that have investigated the effect of mentalization-based treatment on the symptoms of borderline patients, but to the best of the researchers’ knowledge, there has been no comprehensive study that has investigated the effect of mentalization-based training on these behaviors, emotion regulation, and mentalizing abilities. Therefore, the aim of this study was to examine the effects of mentalization-based training on self-harm behavior, emotion actions, and mentalization abilities of soldiers with borderline personality disorder.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

The study was a randomized controlled clinical trial with a 2-month follow-up. Fifty patients participated in the study. They were randomly divided into two experimental groups (25 people) and a control group (25 people).

Study participants and sampling

All soldiers with borderline personality disorder who engaged in self-harming behaviors in Tehran between 2023 and 2024 were included in the statistical population. The sample size was calculated using G*Power software considering an effect size of 0.40, an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80. The estimated sample size of the study was 32 participants based on previous studies and findings on self-harm[18] but was increased to 50 participants to account for the attrition of participants. The patients included soldiers referred to the 505 Army Neuropsychiatric Hospital, where, in collaboration with psychotherapists and psychiatrists, phone numbers of people who met the diagnostic criteria for borderline personality disorder were recorded, with their consent. Patients’ telephone numbers were obtained, they were contacted, and the study was discussed in advance, after which they were asked to participate in the study if they wished. After the patients were referred, a psychiatrist conducted a diagnostic interview using a Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 Disorders (SCID-5-CV) and those who met the diagnostic criteria were included in the study. The inclusion criteria included willingness to participate in the research, suffering from borderline personality disorder, no substance abuse, having self-harming behaviors based on the cut score of the questionnaire, and no disorders that affect realism such as bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, or other psychotic disorders. Exclusion criteria include having a history of severe psychiatric problems, receiving medication or other psychotherapeutic interventions simultaneously during the study, and having substance abuse. The two groups ran in parallel, so the experimental group received mental training, while the control group did not. This process continued from the pretest measurements to the end of the follow-up period measurements. Therefore, this study was a parallel study. A nonsimple or bounded random method was used to assign subjects into two experimental and control groups. In this way, 25 balls were designated for the experimental group and 25 balls for the control group and were dropped into the container. The balls were then randomly taken from the container without replacement, and the sequence created was recorded. Furthermore, an opaque envelope containing the random sequence was used to conceal the random sequence. The subjects were matched so that there were no significant differences between them in terms of demographic variables. The period of sampling lasted approximately 2 months. After the implementation of the intervention, participants completed the necessary questionnaires again as post-test and during the 2-month follow-up period. There were 22 individuals in the experimental group and 21 in the control group after three participants from the experimental group and two from the control group departed the study during the training phase [Diagram 1].

Data collection tool and technique

The intervention was carried out in face-to-face and group settings. Before the start of each session, subjects turned off their mobile phones so as not to interrupt the learning process. After soldiers who met the inclusion criteria were identified, they were contacted to schedule an intervention session. Furthermore, essential cooperation was established with the relevant administrative centers to ensure that soldiers who wished to participate in the study could do so. Training consisted of six weekly sessions delivered in groups. After the last intervention session or 6 weeks after training, subjects completed a post-test. Subjects completed a follow-up phase 8 weeks after the last mentalization training session after completing post-test questionnaires. All sessions were conducted by a clinical psychology PhD psychotherapist under the supervision of a faculty member. Due to the nature of the study, blinding was not possible, but therapists were aware of the group type and patients were aware of their group status in relation to the intervention. Participants were randomly assigned to two experimental and control groups. The training group then received six sessions, each lasting 90 minute, and the training group was based on mentalization.[19] Its contents are shown in Table 1. Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 disorders (SCID-5-CV)

The most commonly used structured diagnostic tool to assess DSM-5 disorders is the SCID-5-CV, published in 2013 by First et al., developed in 2013. The validity and reliability of the clinical version of this tool were tested, and for most diagnoses, the positive agreement between the interview and clinical diagnosis was 0.73–0.97, the diagnostic sensitivity was 0. 70, and the interpreter agreement was 0.75 or higher. The kappa value was 0.70.[20] In a study by Abbasi et al., the instrument achieved an internal consistency of 0.95–0.99, a test–retest reliability of 0.60–0.79, and a kappa coefficient of 0.57–0.72.[21]

The mentalization questionnaire (MZQ)

This questionnaire was developed by Hausberg et al.[22] (2012) to develop and assess mentalizing skills using 15 items on a 5-point Likert scale (1: disagree, 5: agree). According to the developers of this tool, it is better to sum up all the ratings of the 15 items and calculate one rating from them. They also reported that the Cronbach’s alpha of this tool was 0.81. A one-factor structure was proposed for this valid tool, and its Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81 was reported in Iran.[23]

Self-harm inventory (SHI)

The self-harm inventory (SHI) was developed by Sanson et al.[24] (1998) and is designed to measure intentional self-harm and a broad and relatively comprehensive range of these behaviors, including drug, narcotic, and alcohol abuse; self-injury; self-immolation and self-injury; dangerous driving; risky sexual relations; and traumatic emotional relations and even suicide attempts. The questionnaire consists of 22 items that respondents should answer with yes (1) or no (0). Higher scores indicate greater involvement in self-harm and a higher likelihood of an individual’s borderline personality traits. In fact, this questionnaire is also considered as a screening tool for borderline personality disorder. A study by Tahbaz Hosseinzadeh et al.[25] confirmed the content, face, and criterion validity of this questionnaire, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.74.

Difficulty in emotion regulation (DERS)

In this study, emotion regulation difficulties were measured using the Emotion Regulation Difficulties Questionnaire by Gratz and Romer (2004). Gratz and Romer developed the 36-statement Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) in 2004 as a self-report tool to measure emotion regulation problems more thoroughly than scales currently used in the field. A 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (rarely) to 5 (always) will be used to rate responses. According to the reliability results, the internal consistency of the whole scale was high at 0.93. According to the convergent validity calculation of the results, the overall score of the scale correlated with the Emotional Avoidance Scale by 0.60%.[26] According to the study by Mazaheri et al.,[27] the reliability coefficient of this scale in Iran is 0.90 based on Cronbach’s alpha. According to the results of the study by Mazaheri et al., the scale showed a strong positive association with stress (0.64), indicating the convergent validity of the questionnaire.

Ethical consideration

The present research was started after receiving the code of ethics (IR.AJAUMS.REC.1402.164) from the Army University of Medical Sciences. Subjects expressed their willingness to participate in the treatment sessions. Also, they completed the informed consent form. After the results showed that the intervention significantly improved emotion regulation, mentalizing abilities, and self-harming difficulties, the control group was given the same training that the experimental group received.

Statistical analysis method

SPSS-25 software was used for data analysis. Frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations were reported in the descriptive statistics section, while repeated measures analysis of variance was used for the inferential statistics.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to examine the effects of the mentalizing-based training on soldiers with a history of suicide in terms of self-harm behavior, mentalization, and emotion regulation. The results of this study on the effectiveness of self-harm behavior showed that soldiers with borderline personality disorder who engaged in self-harm behaviors showed significant improvement in their self-harm behavior after metalizing training. This finding is consistent with studies that have demonstrated the effectiveness of mentalizing therapy for suicidal behavior and self-harm.[1828] To explain this conclusion, we can say that the inability to understand the psychological states of others can lead to feelings of loneliness, which in turn can lead to suicidal thoughts and self-harm as a means of manipulation and regaining connection.[1829] Patients with borderline personality disorder, who suffer from an extreme fear of abandonment, are particularly affected by this problem. The individual may engage in self-harm or other types of avoidance behaviors in a desperate attempt to maintain mental stability, externalize their own nonmentalized experiences, and regulate the behavior of others.[30] However, during the process of mentalization, the individual is often able to understand the emotions and states of others. In such a way that during mentalization training, an individual learns that human behavior is extremely multifaceted and complex and contains competing aspects. This helps the individual to act in a way that makes the other individuals understandable by their actions and act in a controlled, thought-out manner rather than impulsively or emotionally and do not devalue or misinterpret them. Moreover, mentalization is the ability to comprehend that one’s actions are a manifestation of one’s thoughts and emotions As a result, mentalization strengthens a person’s link between thoughts and feelings, which in turn leads to increased efficiency and self-control over desires to harm oneself.[31] This is because behaviors such as self-harm occur when a person is unable to express their thoughts and feelings. Additionally, when mentalization of social experience fails, impulsive behaviors and subjective states that lead to self-harm are more likely to develop.[18] A person will exhibit empathy and nonimpulsivity if, during mentalization training, they represent mental states and discover a receptive view of their own and other people’s thoughts and feelings. This results in a significant improvement in impulsive and self-harming behavior. Many studies have shown that a lack of mentalized social experiences, both internal and external, is generally associated with less self-control and more self-destructive behavior. If, during mentalization training, individuals consider alternative perspectives and react in a controlled way, instead of reacting emotionally and automatically, their self-harming behavior will decrease.[29]

Also, the results of this study showed that soldiers with borderline personality disorder and a history of suicide benefited from mentalization-based training in terms of better emotion regulation. This result is in line with studies that have demonstrated the effectiveness of mentalization therapy in emotion regulation.[3233] To explain this result, it can be said that the use of a retrospective method of backward chaining is one of the most important aspects of mentalization and emotion regulation. In other words, this is identifying a problematic behavior and tracing it back to the psychological state that caused it. By using this strategy, a person can comprehend how leaving mentalization was accompanied with the feeling of harmful emotions. Following this awareness, one realizes that instead of reacting in an incompatible way, one should try to keep in mind that this is what happens when one experiences strong and incompatible emotions in other interpersonal and nonpersonal situations. By maintaining the capacity to mentalize, one can experience emotions and high levels of emotional arousal in an adaptive way.[19] In fact, mentalizing capacity can protect individuals from emotional arousal through the mentalizing-facilitated development of an integrated view of the self.[34] The “STOP” technique is another important method of mentalization-based training for borderline patients, for whom the management of destructive emotions is one of their most important characteristics. This means that the patient acquires the ability to apply skills that enhance mentalization during emotional arousal. This skill is usually developed in weak mentalization states, which are associated with a reduction in impulsivity and the cessation of the experience of negative emotions. In essence, impulsivity, inhibition, and avoidance are inappropriate emotion regulation techniques used by people with limited mentalizing skills. A person’s inability to express or understand their own and others’ mental states makes it impossible to predict their own and others’ behavior, which is associated with a decline in the use of adaptive strategies. However, by improving mentalizing skills, a person is able to empathically understand both their own and others’ behavior. Instead of using nonadaptive techniques to regulate their emotions, they use adaptive strategies such as emotion acceptance, cognitive appraisal, and openness to emotions.[1935] This is especially true when it comes to understanding one’s own emotional states. This is a method that helps individuals better understand and accept their emotions, rather than suppressing or avoiding them, and deal with and modify them more effectively.

The final finding of the study was that soldiers with BPD and self-harming behaviors significantly improved their mentalization skills when they received mentalization-based instruction. This conclusion can be explained by the idea that insecure attachment in childhood prevents people with BPD from forming stable relationships in adulthood. However, during mentalization training, participants develop a secure relationship with both the members and the psychotherapist, making it easier to express their own opinions about others, which promotes the development of mentalization skills. However, over time, participants come to understand that others have different mental states than they do, and their ability to mentalize is strengthened.

Limitations and recommendations

Despite the above findings, it is important to consider the limitations of the present study when generalizing the findings. First, only men with borderline personality disorder were included, so caution should be used when generalizing the results of this study to women. Second, the sample in this study was assembled in a convenience manner. Third, the current research method was intensive psychological training, not clinical intervention. Therefore, future studies are recommended to focus on long-term treatment, random selection and sampling, and gender-balanced groups.

Conclusion

Training based on mentalizing appears to be an effective way to treat emotional issues and self-harming behaviors in soldiers with borderline personality disorder, given the role that mentalizing capacity plays in preventing these behaviors.
