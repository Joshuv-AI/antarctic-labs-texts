Association of preoperative vertebral kyphosis and other factors with cement leakage after percutaneous kyphoplasty: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Baran Taskala, Hasan Kamil Sucu, Ismail Ertan Sevin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPercutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) is an established treatment modality for painful vertebral compression fractures; however, cement leakage remains its most frequent complication. Although factors such as cortical disruption and fracture type have been widely studied, the influence of preoperative sagittal deformity on leakage risk remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between preoperative vertebral kyphosis and cement leakage after PKP, with particular attention to CT-based bone quality.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed patients who underwent PKP for thoracolumbar compression fractures. Bone quality was assessed using CT-derived Hounsfield unit (HU) measurements, and patients were stratified into two groups using a threshold of 114.8 HU: low HU (≤ 114.8) and high HU (> 114.8). Cement leakage was evaluated on routine postoperative CT scans. Radiologic parameters included vertebral kyphosis, segmental kyphosis, Beck index, and posterior wall involvement. To account for multiple procedures within the same patient, we used a generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) with patient ID as a random effect.ResultsCement leakage was detected in 57.4% of procedures (224/390). Leakage patterns included intradiscal leakage in 28.2%, venous leakage in 2.8%, anterior/lateral leakage in 9.5%, spinal canal leakage in 1.8%, and mixed leakage in 15.1%. Despite the relatively high radiographic leakage rate, all leakage events were clinically silent, with no symptomatic neurological deficits or embolic complications observed. In both HU groups, leakage was associated with greater preoperative vertebral kyphosis and a lower Beck index. In the low HU group, each 1° increase in preoperative vertebral kyphosis increased the odds of leakage by 7% (OR 1.07; 95% CI 1.02–1.12; p = 0.009), whereas surgery performed within 3 days was associated with a reduced risk of leakage (OR 0.36; 95% CI 0.14–0.90; p = 0.028).ConclusionAlthough radiographic cement leakage was common after PKP, clinically significant complications were rare. An increased preoperative vertebral kyphosis may be associated with a higher risk of cement leakage and should be kept in mind during patient assessment. In addition, delayed intervention in patients with poor CT-based bone quality may be associated with an increased leakage risk.

Introduction

Vertebral augmentation techniques, initially described by Galibert et al. in 1987, have become one of the main treatments for painful vertebral compression fractures and spinal tumors [1, 2]. Among these, percutaneous kyphoplasty (PKP) is widely used for its ability to stabilize fractures, restore vertebral body height, and correct vertebral kyphosis [3]. Although PKP is generally associated with a lower risk of cement leakage than vertebroplasty, extravasation remains its most frequent complication [4].

Most cement leaks are clinically silent; however, cement leakage into the spinal canal, neural foramina, or vascular structures can lead to devastating neurological or embolic events [5–7]. In addition, intradiscal leakage has been associated with an increased risk of subsequent adjacent-segment fractures [8]. Therefore, identifying modifiable or predictable risk factors remains clinically important.

Previous studies have identified several fracture-related risk factors for cement leakage, including intervertebral clefts, cortical disruption, and fracture type [9–11]. However, the association between vertebral morphology and leakage risk remains insufficiently understood. Although established fracture classification systems [12, 13] are useful for describing fracture patterns, they rely on discrete categories and may not fully reflect the continuous extent of sagittal collapse. For this reason, we used vertebral kyphosis as a continuous quantitative measure of fracture morphology. We hypothesize that greater kyphosis may alter the needle trajectory, reduce the safe zone for cement injection, and facilitate leakage through structurally compromised vertebral segments [14].

CT-based bone quality assessment has recently emerged as a practical and accessible tool for evaluating skeletal fragility on routine preoperative imaging and may therefore be useful for risk stratification in patients undergoing PKP [15–18]. Accordingly, the present study aimed to investigate whether preoperative vertebral kyphosis and other radiological and procedural parameters were associated with cement leakage after PKP for thoracolumbar fractures, with the effect of kyphosis analyzed after stratification by CT-based bone quality.

Materials and methods

Patient selection and study design

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study conducted at a tertiary referral hospital, including patients who underwent PKP for thoracolumbar fractures between January 2016 and December 2018. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local ethics committee (No: 0044, 02/15/2024). Due to the retrospective design, informed consent requirements were waived according to institutional policy.

The study cohort consisted of neurologically intact patients treated with PKP for thoracolumbar vertebral compression fractures, including traumatic and fragility-related fractures. Patients with pathological fractures were excluded. To ensure data independence, one patient who underwent repeat procedures on the same vertebra in separate sessions was also excluded.

Bone quality assessment

Bone quality was assessed using Hounsfield unit (HU) values measured on preoperative CT scans. All scans were performed using a standardized clinical protocol with a tube voltage of 120 kVp, which is critical for maintaining consistency in HU measurements across different scanners. A region of interest (ROI) was drawn as large as possible at the pedicle level of the adjacent proximal vertebra; when the adjacent proximal vertebra was fractured, the nearest radiographically intact vertebra, either proximal or distal, was used. Specifically, the ROI was placed within the trabecular portion of the vertebral body, carefully excluding the cortical endplates, the posterior venous plexus, and any focal sclerotic areas to ensure accurate representation of the bone density. Based on the threshold for bone quality identification as suggested by Pinto et al. (114.8 HU) [19], procedures were categorized into low HU group (HU ≤ 114.8) and high HU group (HU > 114.8).

Radiological measurements were obtained from preoperative imaging and included the following:

All measurements were performed by the first author. Interobserver reliability for fracture morphology was confirmed by a senior neurosurgeon with more than 10 years of experience, yielding a Cohen’s kappa coefficient of 0.834, which indicates almost perfect agreement.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed in the prone position under sedation using a transpedicular or parapedicular approach. Under fluoroscopic guidance, balloon catheters were inflated within the anterior one-third of the vertebral body (Fig. 1). Cement, either low or high viscosity, was prepared and injected only after reaching a stage, often described as a ‘toothpaste-like’ consistency, to prevent extravasation (Fig. 2). At this stage, the cement no longer flows freely under gravity and requires consistent pressure for injection, thereby ensuring procedural consistency.

To maximize safety, cement was administered in 0.3 cc increments, with a total of 1.5 cc per delivery device. This incremental approach limited potential leakage to a maximum of 0.3 cc per injection. In cases of leakage into the disc space, anterior or lateral portion of the vertebral body, the injection was either redirected from the contralateral side, or delayed allowing hardening, or the cannula was repositioned. Procedures were terminated if leakage persisted or if cement reached the posterior quarter of the vertebral body.

Time to surgery

Time to surgery was defined as the interval between the documented fracture date, or symptom onset date leading to diagnostic imaging when a discrete fracture date was not available, and the date of PKP. Procedures were categorized as ≤ 3 days or > 3 days.

Outcome: cement leakage on postoperative CT

All patients underwent routine postoperative CT to detect cement leakage (Fig. 3). Leakage was categorized as intradiscal, venous, anterior/lateral to the vertebral body, spinal canal, or mixed (more than one location without spinal canal involvement).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R and JASP. Continuous variables were tested for normality and compared using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared with chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.

Because some patients underwent multiple PKP procedures, univariate comparisons were considered exploratory. Independent predictors of cement leakage were assessed using a generalized linear mixed-effects model (GLMM) with patient ID as a random intercept. Missing values were handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with predictive mean matching to preserve sample size and reduce potential bias. Variables with clinical relevance and/or significant univariate associations were considered for inclusion in the model. Multicollinearity was evaluated; because of strong correlation between Beck index and vertebral kyphosis, Beck index was not included in the final multivariable model. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 414 procedures were initially identified. After excluding procedures performed for pathological fractures and repeat procedures (n = 2) on the same vertebra, 390 procedures remained for the final analysis.

Cement leakage rate and patterns

Leakage occurred in 57.4% (224/390) of procedures. The observed patterns per procedure were intradiscal: 28.2% (110/390), venous: 2.8% (11/390), anterior/lateral: 9.5% (37/390), spinal canal: 1.8% (7/390), and mixed: 15.1% (59/390).

The high incidence of leakage was primarily driven by small, asymptomatic intradiscal and paravertebral leaks detected by postoperative CT. When evaluating procedural safety, a distinction was made between these common radiographic findings and rare clinically significant events. No patient developed radiculopathy, myelopathy, or symptomatic pulmonary/systemic embolism attributable to cement leakage during hospitalization.

For further detailed analysis, patients with available preoperative HU measurements were stratified into two groups to evaluate the impact of bone density more comprehensively.

Low HU group (HU ≤ 114.8)

Within this cohort, leakage and non-leakage procedures did not differ significantly with respect to age or sex. However, the leakage group showed higher preoperative vertebral kyphosis (p = 0.003) and lower Beck index (p < 0.001) (Table 1). The occurrence of posterior vertebral body wall involvement was more frequent in these procedures (p = 0.046). Analysis of surgical timing revealed that leakage was less frequent when surgery was performed within 3 days (p = 0.019) (Table 2). No significant differences were found for segmental kyphosis, injected cement volume, or unilateral/bilateral approach.

High HU group (HU > 114.8)

Demographic variables did not differ significantly between the leakage and non-leakage procedures. However, affected cases showed higher vertebral kyphosis (p = 0.012) and lower Beck index (p = 0.005) (Table 4). Posterior wall involvement was more frequent among leakage procedures but did not reach statistical significance. No significant associations were found for time to surgery, injected cement volume, segmental kyphosis, or surgical approach (Table 5). In the Generalized Linear Mixed Model (GLMM) analysis, each unit increase in preoperative vertebral kyphosis was found to significantly increase the risk of cement leakage by 7% (OR: 1.07, 95% CI: 1.00–1.13, p = 0.040). The model demonstrated a good discrimination (AUC: 0.862), with marginal R² = 0.043 and conditional R² = 0.166. However, calibration was suboptimal, as indicated by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (χ² = 35.943, df = 8, p < 0.001). Despite the high discriminative power, the low marginal R² suggests that only a small proportion of the variance in cement leakage is explained by preoperative vertebral kyphosis, indicating that other unmeasured factors may also play a significant role.

Discussion

Reported rates of serious complications after vertebral cement augmentation range from 2% to 11.5% [20]. In our study, the overall cement leakage rate was 57.4%, which is higher than the 18.4%–54.7% range reported in many previous studies [9, 21, 22]. However, this rate should be interpreted as a radiographic leakage rate rather than a rate of clinical complication. Leakage after kyphoplasty is most often detected in relatively low-risk locations such as the paravertebral soft tissues, veins, or intervertebral disc space, whereas leakage into the spinal canal remains uncommon [23]. Consistent with this pattern, most leakages in our series occurred in the disc space or in the anterior/lateral aspect of the vertebral body, and potentially serious spinal canal leakage was observed in only 1.7% of cases. Importantly, no patients developed symptomatic neurological deficits or embolic events during hospitalization. These findings suggest that the elevated leakage rate in our study primarily reflects the high sensitivity of CT imaging for detecting clinically silent extravasation rather than an increased procedural morbidity.

In clinical practice, not all leakage types carry the same significance: while cement leakage into the spinal canal or vascular structures may pose meaningful risk, small leaks into the disc space or adjacent soft tissues are usually of limited clinical consequence. Accordingly, the clinical relevance of a leakage event depends strongly on its location and symptoms, not merely on its radiographic detection. Thus, although radiographic leakage was frequent, it did not necessarily translate into clinical complications. Clinicians should therefore focus on preventing symptomatic leaks, while recognizing that a high rate of asymptomatic leaks detected only by CT may be an expected consequence of high-resolution imaging.

There are limited data specifically addressing the effect of vertebral kyphosis on cement leakage. Our findings indicate that greater vertebral kyphosis is associated with a higher frequency of cement leakage, irrespective of bone quality. Consistent with this, patients who experienced cement leakage had a lower Beck index, which is inversely related to vertebral kyphosis. This association could be explained by kyphosis-related alterations in vertebral body and increased procedural difficulty. Pronounced vertebral kyphosis often reflects advanced anterior column injury and subtle cortical discontinuities, which may create favorable pathways for cement leakage. Beyond these morphological changes, kyphosis may also reflect greater fracture severity and vertebral instability, both of which have been linked to cement leakage [24–26]. In line with this, prior research has shown that dynamic kyphotic changes, such as those observed between standing and prone radiographs, are associated with higher leakage rates [26]. In addition, severe kyphosis can technically complicate the procedure by making the needle trajectory more difficult and narrowing the safe zone for anterior cement placement. Because cement is typically intended to be deposited in the anterior portion of the vertebral body for optimal support, kyphotic collapse with anterior wedging and reduced anterior vertebral body volume may further predispose to leakage through cortical defects or fracture clefts. Overall, increased vertebral kyphosis likely predisposes to leakage through a combination of structural destruction, which compromises the bone’s ability to contain cement, and the technical difficulties encountered during needle placement in a deformed vertebral body.

In our clinical practice, early kyphoplasty is used as a targeted minimally invasive internal stabilization strategy in carefully selected patients. By providing immediate structural support to the fractured vertebra, this approach aims to achieve rapid pain relief and prevent progressive collapse, which remains a significant concern during conservative management [27], particularly in patients with poor bone quality. Regarding surgical timing, our findings demonstrated that intervention within the first three days was independently associated with 64% lower odds of cement leakage. The rationale for selecting the 3-day cutoff was to define the hyperacute phase of injury [28, 29], distinguish direct traumatic referrals from delayed referrals, and evaluate the therapeutic window before significant structural remodeling occurs. These results suggest that, beyond facilitating prompt analgesia and halting deformity progression [30, 31], earlier surgical intervention may be associated with a reduced risk of cement extravasation.

The literature on this topic remains controversial. While Ren et al. [6] found no significant difference in leakage risk between acute and chronic fractures, as defined by MRI signal characteristics, whereas Guan et al. [32] reported a higher leakage risk when surgery was performed within 14 days of trauma compared with later intervention. In contrast to these reports, we hypothesize that the higher leakage rates observed in our cohort beyond the 3-day window may be attributed to progressive microstructural deterioration, micro-instability, and the development of pseudoarthrosis over time, specifically in patients with poor bone quality. These pathological changes can create irregular intravertebral cavities and fissures that facilitate cement escape. This interpretation is indirectly supported by prior radiological studies showing that kyphotic deformity in osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures tends to progress over time, particularly in the presence of superior endplate disruption, disc injury, ligamentous injury, and extensive bone edema [14, 33, 34]. While selection bias inherent to retrospective studies cannot be excluded, our findings suggest that the procedural window may be most favorable when intervention is performed earlier. Nevertheless, these results should be interpreted cautiously until they are confirmed in prospective studies.

Although posterior wall involvement did not reach statistical significance, our post hoc power analysis revealed limited statistical power to detect this specific effect, suggesting this 1.99-fold increase in odds remains a clinically relevant trend that warrants investigation in larger cohorts. Overall, these findings suggest that vertebral kyphosis and possibly early surgical timing in patients with lower HU-based bone quality may be clinically relevant factors for leakage risk, but the moderate explanatory power, suboptimal calibration, and limited power for fracture morphology indicate that further studies with larger cohorts are needed to confirm these associations and improve predictive performance.

Consistent with previous reports [6], segmental kyphosis was not significantly associated with cement leakage. This distinction is critical: the finding that vertebral kyphosis (the local angle of the fractured bone) correlates with leakage, while segmental kyphosis (the broader alignment of the spinal segment) does not, suggests that the mechanism of cement extravasation is driven by local angular changes rather than segmental alterations.

In this study, we focused on relatively underexplored parameters in an effort to clarify the risk factors for cement leakage. We believe that our findings, particularly those meaningful to vertebral kyphosis and surgical timing, contribute data to ongoing debates in the literature, supported by a comparatively large number of treated vertebrae.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective and single-center design may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader clinical contexts. Second, certain procedural variables lacked objective standardization; the timing of cement injection was based on the surgeon’s subjective assessment of a ‘toothpaste-like’ consistency, rather than a fixed temporal or rheological measure. In addition, specific technical parameters, such as cement viscosity, injection pressure and balloon inflation volumes, were not quantitatively recorded, which may limit the reproducibility of the procedural nuances. Third, the study did not include a formal fracture morphology classification or a quantitative analysis of cortical defects. While we adjusted for posterior wall involvement, the lack of a detailed assessment of all cortical boundaries means that we cannot fully exclude the independent biomechanical effect of vertebral kyphosis from the risk inherent to a more severely disrupted vertebral shell. Finally, surgeon-related variability and the relatively small size of certain subgroups may have reduced statistical power, particularly for detecting subtle associations in subgroup analyses.

Despite these limitations, our results support two clinically relevant observations: first, that higher preoperative vertebral kyphosis is associated with an increased risk of cement leakage; and second, that performing kyphoplasty within 3 days may reduce this risk in patients with lower HU-based bone quality. These findings, if corroborated by prospective multicenter studies, may help refine risk stratification and inform operative decision-making in patients with thoracolumbar fractures undergoing kyphoplasty.

Conclusion

In conclusion, while cement leakage following kyphoplasty was frequently observed in our series, it was rarely associated with symptomatic neurological or embolic complications, and no symptomatic events occurred. This suggests that radiographic leakage findings, particularly those detected only by CT, often lack clinical impact. An increased preoperative vertebral kyphosis may be associated with a higher risk of cement leakage and should be kept in mind during patient assessment. In addition, delayed intervention in patients with poor CT-based bone quality may be associated with an increased leakage risk. However, these findings should be interpreted as potential trends that warrant further investigation in larger, prospective multicenter studies.
