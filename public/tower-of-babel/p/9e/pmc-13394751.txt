Rest‐activity patterns across development in two mouse models of autism and epilepsy
Authors: Ipsa Dash, Filip Fredlund, Jenny Wickham, My Andersson, Christine T Ekdahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSleep disturbances are common in individuals with autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and epilepsy and are increasingly recognized as comorbidities that affect disease severity and quality of life. This study investigated rest‐activity patterns across development in two genetic mouse models relevant to ASD and epilepsy: synapsin 2 (Syn2) and contactin‐associated protein‐like 2 (Cntnap2) knockout (KO) mice, in which epileptic seizures typically emerge at 2–3 months and around 6 months of age, respectively.MethodsActigraphy, a noninvasive method for monitoring rest‐locomotor activity patterns, was performed in male and female Syn2 KO (n = 25), Cntnap2 KO (n = 32), and wild type (n = 31) mice at 1–2, 3–5, and 5–7 months of age. Recordings consisted of 72 h of continuous monitoring during a 12‐h light/12‐h dark cycle following a 12‐h acclimatization period. Infrared sensors at two cage heights detected both subtle and prominent movements, allowing analysis of age‐dependent rest–activity rhythms.ResultsAcross all genotypes and ages, mice showed higher activity during the dark phase, confirming the light phase as the preferred rest/sleep period. Adolescent (1–2 months) Syn2 KO mice exhibited normal rest–activity patterns, whereas adult (3–5 months) Syn2 KO mice displayed increased activity during the dark phase. In contrast, no robust age‐dependent rest‐activity alterations were detected in either adolescent or adult Cntnap2 KO mice after correction for multiple comparisons.SignificanceAdult Syn2 KO mice exhibit hyperactivity during their preferred wake period, coinciding with the age range associated with seizure emergence in this model, whereas Cntnap2 KO mice do not exhibit robust developmental alterations in rest‐activity behavior. These findings highlight genotype‐ and age‐dependent differences in mouse models relevant to ASD and epilepsy.Plain Language SummarySleep problems are common in people with autism and epilepsy, but why they occur is still unclear. In this study, we examined rest‐activity patterns in mice carrying two different genes linked to autism and epilepsy using a noninvasive method that tracks movement over time. We found that mice lacking the Syn2 gene developed hyperactivity in adulthood, during a life stage associated with seizures in this model, whereas mice lacking the Cntnap2 gene did not show consistent changes in rest‐activity patterns across development. These findings suggest that activity patterns in genetic models of autism and epilepsy depend on age and genetic background.Keywords: actigraphy, autism, cntnap2, epilepsy, sleep, synapsin2

INTRODUCTION

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and epilepsy frequently co‐occur, with reported prevalence rates of epilepsy among individuals with ASD ranging from 1.8% to 60%. 1 , 2 Both conditions are associated with cognitive, behavioral, and physiological challenges, including disrupted sleep patterns. Sleep disturbances are a well‐recognized yet often overlooked comorbidity in ASD and epilepsy, with affected individuals experiencing symptoms such as prolonged sleep latency, frequent nocturnal awakenings, sleep fragmentation, and excessive daytime sleepiness. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Given the crucial role of sleep in neurodevelopment, memory formation, and emotional regulation, 10 disturbances in sleep can exacerbate cognitive impairments, emotional dysregulation, and seizure susceptibility. 11 A better understanding of sleep dysfunction in ASD and epilepsy is therefore essential for improving patient management and developing targeted treatment strategies.

Animal models provide valuable tools for investigating the neurobiological mechanisms underlying ASD, epilepsy, and their shared comorbidities, including sleep disturbances. Synapsin 2 (Syn2) knockout (KO) mice exhibit ASD‐like behaviors characterized by reduced social interaction and presence of handling‐induced electroclinical focal to bilateral tonic–clonic seizures in young adults at approximately 2–3 months of age, 12 , 13 , 14 making them a suitable model for studying the intersection of ASD, epilepsy, and sleep regulation. Similarly, contactin‐associated protein‐like 2 (Cntnap2) KO mice display repetitive behaviors, impaired social interactions from a young age, but begin to exhibit electroclinical absence‐like and convulsive seizures later in adulthood, typically from about 6 months of age. While the behavioral and seizure phenotypes of these two mouse models are well characterized, their sleep–wake patterns during development, particularly in Syn2 KO mice, remain less explored. 12 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19

Previous research with EEG recordings in adult Cntnap2 KO rodents has revealed altered sleep and circadian patterns, including fragmented wake and sleep phases, disrupted locomotor rhythms under dim‐light conditions, and reduced activity of the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN). 17 , 20 , 21 In contrast, sleep–wake patterns in Syn2 KO mice have been scarcely investigated, 22 with most studies focusing on seizure or behavioral phenotypes. 12 , 15 , 19 , 22

This highlights a knowledge gap in understanding how sleep–wake patterns are affected across development and epilepsy progression.

The present study aimed to evaluate locomotor activity patterns during sleep and wake periods in Syn2 and Cntnap2 KO mice using noninvasive actigraphy. Recordings were performed at two different developmental stages, adolescence and adulthood, corresponding to periods before and during or after the age range at which seizures typically begin to manifest, to assess how rest–activity organization evolves with age and comorbidity. Despite previous work on related aspects of these models, our study uniquely provides a developmental and comparative perspective on rest–activity patterns in freely moving Syn2 and Cntnap2 KO mice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals and group assignments

Homozygous Syn2 KO mouse founders were kindly provided by Dr. Lars Etholm (Oslo, Norway) and all subsequent breeding was conducted in‐house. B6.129(Cg)‐Cntnap2tm1Pele/J mice (strain #017482) were obtained from the Jackson Laboratory. Genotypes were confirmed by PCR using the following primers: forward primer (CTGCCAGCCCAGAACTGG), wild‐type (WT) reverse primer (CGCTTCCTCGTGCTTTACGGTAT), and mutant reverse primer (ACACCAGGGGCAAGAATTG), and all subsequent breeding was conducted in‐house.

A total of 88 mice were used in this study, comprising n = 25 Syn2 KO mice (1 and 2–3 months old), n = 32 Cntnap2 KO mice (1 and 5–7 months old), and n = 31 sex and strain‐matched WT C57BL/6J mice (1, 2–3, 5–7 months old male and female; The Jackson Laboratory). Mice were housed in a pathogen‐free and climate‐controlled environment with ad libitum access to water and standard rodent chow. Ambient room temperature was 22°C with Lights‐on (698 Lux) at 07:00 am and Lights‐off (20 Lux) at 07:00 pm with a 30 min emulated sunrise and sunset. Groups of 2–10 sex‐matched mice were housed together in type III high cages equipped with bedding, nesting, and environmental enrichment. All experiments were conducted in accordance with the European Directive 2010/63/EU for animal experiments and approved by the local ethics committee in the Malmö‐Lund region of Sweden (permit number 9057/‐19).

Actigraphy recordings and rest–activity analysis

To assess rest–activity patterns, mice were individually monitored in 210 × 210 mm actigraphy cages (situated in the same room as the breeding facility) for a total of 84 h (12‐h acclimatization followed by 72‐h recording) at either 1 to 2 months, 3–5 months, or 5–7 months of age, using an infrared (IR) actimeter (Panlab, USA) (Figures 1 and 2). The initial 12‐h acclimatization period was excluded from all subsequent analyses. The light/dark period was maintained on a 12‐h light (Lights ON)/12‐h dark (Lights OFF) schedule. IR frames were positioned at two heights within each cage to capture distinct types of movement: a lower frame (3 cm above the cage floor) to detect general locomotor activity, and an upper frame (6 cm) to record prominent vertical movements during rearing when mice stood on their hind legs. 22

Experimental setup of actigraphy monitoring in mice. The experimental setup comprised a transparent housing cage equipped with bedding, food, and a water bowl. Surrounding the cage were two infrared (IR) sensor frames: an upper frame used to detect rearing behavior (when the mouse stands on its hind legs) and a lower frame for recording general locomotor activity. This configuration enabled continuous, noninvasive monitoring of sleep–wake rhythms and locomotor activity under controlled environmental conditions.

Locomotor activity was recorded and analyzed as follows: (1) Rearing activity (upper IR frame): the number of rearing events during Lights ON and Lights OFF was quantified from recordings binned into 1‐min intervals. (2) General activity (lower IR frame): locomotor activity was recorded in 1‐min intervals during Lights ON and Lights OFF and smoothed using a 5‐min rolling average to reduce noise. (3) From the lower frame recordings, the following parameters were extracted: Latency to rest onset, defined as the time from Lights ON until the mean activity decreased to ≤25 units per 10‐min interval (onset of presumed first sleep cycle/sleep latency); Latency to activity onset, defined as the time from Lights OFF until the mean activity increased to ≥50 units per 10‐min interval (time until presumed awake); Total activity, calculated as the cumulative duration and number of activity peaks exceeding either 25 or 50 units (arbitrary selected activity thresholds clearly separated from baseline rest activity levels) per 10‐min interval during both Lights ON and Lights OFF periods. All parameters were calculated separately for each 24‐h recording period (07:00–07:00).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 2023.12.1 + 402). Data were analyzed for three consecutive 24‐h segments per mouse, excluding the initial 12‐h acclimatization period. A custom R script, adapted from our previous study, 22 was modified to improve readability, enable automated processing of multiple subjects, and generate graphical outputs. Data were expressed as activity units per 24‐h recording. For each mouse, mean values were calculated across the three consecutive 24‐h periods and are presented as group means ± standard deviation (SD) with individual data points shown for each experimental group (Figures 2, 3, 4, 5). Statistical significance was determined using QQ‐plots for evaluating normal distribution followed by Welch's unpaired t‐test, with uncorrected p < 0.05 considered significant. Welch's paired t‐test was also used for comparisons between Lights ON and Lights OFF activity. Correction for multiple comparisons was performed using false discovery rate (FDR) method across 12 comparisons. FDR correction was applied separately within each age × genotype comparison set, such that statistical adjustment was performed across all behavioral parameters analyzed within a given experimental group rather than across the full dataset.

RESULTS

Higher activity scores during the dark compared with the light period

Across all genotypes and ages, mice exhibited significantly higher activity scores (mean time spent >25 units) during Lights OFF compared with Lights ON period, confirming the Lights ON as the preferred sleep period and Lights OFF as the preferred wake period. This pattern was consistent in WT, Syn2 KO, and Cntnap2 KO mice at 1–2 months of age (WT Lights OFF 527 ± 43 vs. Lights ON 313 ± 63; Syn2 KO Lights OFF 553 ± 42 vs. Lights ON 300 ± 30; Cntnap2 KO Lights OFF 522 ± 55 vs. Lights ON 343 ± 56), in WT and Syn2 KO mice at 3–5 months of age (WT Lights OFF 479 ± 42 vs. Lights ON 294 ± 25; Syn2 KO Lights OFF 498 ± 47 vs. Lights ON 286 ± 45), and in WT and Cntnap2 KO mice at 5–7 months of age (WT Lights OFF 418 ± 81 vs. Lights ON 318 ± 47; Cntnap2 KO Lights OFF 440 ± 86 vs. Lights ON 348 ± 48) (FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05; all comparisons).

No differences in rest‐activity patterns between adolescent Syn2 KO and WT mice

No significant differences were observed between Syn2 KO and WT mice at 1–2 months of age in the number of rearings during the Lights ON or Lights OFF period (Figure 3A–D). Similarly, no group differences were found in latency to rest onset during the Lights ON period (Figure 3E), activity during the Lights ON period (Figure 3F–I), latency to activity onset during the Lights OFF period (Figure 3J), or activity during the Lights OFF period (Figure 3K–N). Exploratory sex‐stratified analyses within the Syn2 KO group showed no sex differences, although sample sizes were small and no statistical comparisons were performed with WT males due to low numbers (WT n = 11; two males, nine females; Syn2 KO n = 13; six males, seven females; data not shown).

Increased activity during the wake period in adult Syn2 KO mice coincide with the age range at which seizures are known to occur

Syn2 KO mice aged 3–5 months showed no difference in the number of rearings compared with WT controls (Figure 4A–D). Nominal differences were observed in latency to rest at the onset of the Lights ON period (Figure 4E; uncorrected p = 0.015) and in activity during the Lights ON period (Figure 4F–I; uncorrected p = 0.050 and p = 0.032 for number of peaks and time spent >50 units, respectively). However, these effects did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, although latency to rest onset showed a trend toward a significant reduction (FDR‐adjusted p = 0.059). In contrast, increased activity scores during the Lights OFF period remained significant after FDR correction, indicating increased activity during the active phase (Figure 4J–N). Exploratory sex‐stratified analyses revealed no significant sex differences (WT n = 12; seven males, five females; Syn2 KO n = 12; seven males, five females; data not shown).

No epileptic seizures were observed during handling of 1–2 months old Syn2 KO mice. In contrast, seizures with bilateral tonic–clonic movements were observed during handling in all of the 3–5‐month‐old Syn2 KO mice, particularly during cage changes, as previously reported. 14 , 22 However, as the mice were undisturbed during actigraphy recordings, apart from daily supervision and water supply, provoked convulsive seizures were unlikely to have occurred during the 72‐h recording period.

No robust differences in rest‐activity patterns between adolescent Cntnap2 KO and WT mice

1–2 months old Cntnap2 KO mice did not differ from WT mice in the number of rearings during either the Lights ON or Lights OFF periods, nor in latency to rest onset during Lights ON (Figure 5A–E). In uncorrected analyses, Cntnap2 KO mice exhibited an increased number of activity peaks and increased time with mean activity ≥50 units during the Lights ON period (uncorrected p = 0.037 and 0.018, respectively). However, these effects did not remain significant after FDR correction for multiple comparisons (Figure 5F–I). No differences between genotypes were observed during the Lights OFF period (Figure 5J–N).

Exploratory sex‐stratified analyses within the Cntnap2 KO group revealed that females exhibited a higher number of rearings during both Lights ON (FDR‐adjusted p = 0.0003) and Lights OFF (FDR‐adjusted p = 0.04) compared with males. In contrast, males showed longer latency to rest onset during Lights ON (FDR‐adjusted p = 0.004) and longer latency to activity onset during Lights OFF (FDR‐adjusted p = 0.041). When genotypes were stratified by sex, no significant differences were detected between WT and Cntnap2 KO mice in rearings, latency to rest onset, or activity onset. Statistical comparisons involving WT males were not performed due to low numbers (WT n = 11; two males, nine females; Cntnap2 KO n = 23; eight males, 15 females; data not shown).

No differences in rest‐activity patterns between adult Cntnap2 KO and WT mice

Cntnap2 KO mice are known to exhibit spontaneous seizures within an age range of approximately 6–8 months of age. 16 , 17 , 21 As actigraphy was not combined with videoEEG monitoring, electroclinical or electrographic seizures could not be consistently verified or quantified across all animals. Only occasional convulsive seizures were observed during routine handling. Adult Cntnap2 KO animals showed no differences compared to WT controls in the number of rearings during either Lights ON or Lights OFF periods (Figure 6A–D), nor in latency to rest onset during the Lights ON period (Figure 6E). Furthermore, no alterations in activity‐related parameters were observed during the Lights ON period (Figure 6F–I) or the Lights OFF period (Figure 6J–N), indicating comparable levels between Cntnap2 KO and WT mice.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we examined rest‐activity patterns using actigraphy recordings in two genetic mouse models associated with ASD and epilepsy, Syn2 KO and Cntnap2 KO mice. We found that adult (3–5 months), but not adolescent (1–2 months), Syn2 KO mice displayed increased activity during the preferred wake period compared with age‐ and sex‐matched WT controls. These findings are consistent with a hyperactive phenotype coinciding with the age at which seizures are known to occur in this model. In uncorrected analyses, reduced latency to rest onset was observed during the preferred sleep period. However, this effect did not survive correction for multiple comparisons. In contrast, analyses in Cntnap2 KO mice did not reveal robust age‐dependent alterations in rest‐activity behavior. Although uncorrected analyses suggested increased locomotor activity during the preferred sleep period in adolescent, but not adult, these effects also did not survive correction for multiple comparisons and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

Actigraphy is a well‐established method for assessing locomotor activity reflecting sleep–wake cycles in humans. Unlike electroencephalography (EEG), which requires surgical implantation in animals, actigraphy enables continuous monitoring of behavior in freely moving subjects. However, actigraphy does not differentiate between rest and sleep, cannot measure cortical activity, and does not allow discrimination between specific sleep stages (e.g., REM and non‐REM sleep), nor can it detect brief, movement‐free awakenings that may affect sleep quality. Thus, while EEG remains the gold standard for detailed sleep analysis, actigraphy serves as an effective complementary tool for examining overall rest–activity patterns. Future studies could benefit from combining actigraphy with EEG‐based sleep staging to enhance sensitivity and precision. Nevertheless, the current findings demonstrate that actigraphy can reliably detect distinct alterations in rest–activity patterns.

Although circadian regulation of Syn2 expression has been reported, its role in sleep–wake control remains poorly understood. Synapsins are key regulators of synaptic vesicle trafficking and neurotransmitter release, particularly in modulating inhibitory GABAergic transmission. 23 As altered inhibitory neurotransmission has been associated with both epilepsy and sleep disturbances, Syn2 deletion may affect sleep structure directly or as a consequence of epileptogenesis. The emergence of altered rest‐activity pattern in adult, but not adolescent, Syn2 KO mice suggests that these changes coincide with, rather than precede, the epileptic state. Because the mice were undisturbed and not handled during the consecutive 3 days of actigraphy recordings, the increased locomotor activity observed during wakefulness is unlikely to reflect clinical seizure events per se. Instead, it may indicate intrinsic instability in neural networks, contributing to motor hyperactivity and increased seizure susceptibility. Nevertheless, electrographic seizures without overt or only subtle clinical manifestations have been reported in SynI/II/II double/triple KO mice. 15 , 19 , 24 Thus, the possibility that increased wake‐period activity in Syn2 KO mice reflects subtle spontaneous seizures that do not progress to generalized tonic–clonic seizures cannot be fully excluded in the absence of video‐EEG monitoring. However, previous neuroethiological studies of clinical seizures in Syn2 KO mice do not support the presence of myoclonic jerks or brief focal seizures. 12 , 25 To our knowledge, no EEG‐based studies have yet characterized sleep–wake cycles or sleep architecture in Syn2 KO mice, whereas electroclinical seizures have been well documented in SynI/II/III double and triple KO mice. 12 , 15 , 19 Increased activity during the wake period in the adult Syn2 KO mice may affect sleep by increasing sleep pressure, which in turn could lead to decreased latency to rest onset, similar to the known effects of physical activity on sleep latency. 26 Although analyses of latency to rest onset in adult Syn2 KO mice did not survive corrections for multiple comparisons, a trend toward reduced latency was observed in the FDR‐adjusted p‐value, suggesting that a larger cohort may be required to determine whether this effect is robust. We previously reported reduced rearing activity during the preferred sleep period in adult Syn2 KO mice. 22 However, this finding was not replicated in the present study, possibly due to interindividual variability that may have obscured subtle differences.

In the present study, analyses in adolescent Cntnap2 KO mice did not provide robust evidence for altered sleep–wake regulation after correction for multiple comparisons at any age. While uncorrected analyses suggested subtle changes in rest‐activity patterns, including increased activity during the preferred sleep period in adolescence, these effects did not remain significant following FDR adjustment and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. In contrast, previous EEG‐based studies have reported both fragmented wakefulness and altered sleep architecture in adult Cntnap2 KO mice, including shorter NREM and REM sleep periods, increased sleep during the Lights OFF period and reduced sleep during the Lights ON period. 17 , 21 These findings indicate that Cntnap2 deficiency can influence sleep‐related processes, even though our actigraphy‐based approach did not reveal consistent or statistically robust disruptions in rest‐activity rhythms across development. Rather than representing stable genotype effects, the subtle alterations observed here may reflect transient or developmentally restricted behavioral variability, highlighting the need for independent replication and longer‐term monitoring before firm conclusions can be drawn regarding Cntnap2‐associated sleep–wake alterations. Environmental factors, such as lighting conditions, may contribute to discrepancies between studies. For example, dim light at night has been shown to disrupt locomotor rhythms and exacerbate repetitive behaviors in Cntnap2 KO mice. 20 , 21 Although environmental lighting was not manipulated in the present study, our findings suggest that overt alterations in sleep–wake behavior are not readily detected under standard housing conditions using actigraphy.

Sex hormones are known to modulate both seizure susceptibility and sleep regulation, 27 , 28 with substantial evidence for sex‐dependent effects of steroid hormones on neuronal excitability and network function. In mice, reproductive hormones have been shown to contribute to sex differences in baseline sleep–wake behavior and homeostatic sleep regulation, highlighting the importance of hormonal modulation of sleep physiology. 29 In parallel, experimental and clinical evidence demonstrates that fluctuations in sex hormones influence seizure susceptibility and epilepsy phenotypes in a sex‐specific manner. 30 A recent EEG‐based study in Cntnap2 KO mice reported sex differences in sleep architecture, with increased REM sleep in males compared with females. 21 Although sex was included as a biological variable in the present study, sleep architecture could not be addressed without EEG, and the sample size was not sufficient to robustly assess sex‐specific effects on locomotor activity. Consequently, only limited differences were detected. The potential contribution of sex‐dependent mechanisms to sleep–wake regulation and epilepsy phenotypes therefore remains an important avenue for future investigation.

Sleep architecture is regulated by complex interactions among genetic, environmental, neurobiological and neurophysiological factors, 31 many of which may also be altered in ASD and epilepsy. Neurons in the SCN of the hypothalamus exhibit circadian changes in their intrinsic properties and clock‐gene transcription, thereby governing the primary oscillatory signals that coordinate cellular rhythms through the body, including the sleep–wake cycle. SCN neurons are influenced by both endogenous and external factors and are modulated by neural input, including GABAergic projections from thalamus and glutamatergic input from retina. Mechanisms linking sleep dysregulation with ASD and epilepsy may involve pathways related to core clock proteins and their downstream regulation of genes associated with seizure susceptibility. In addition, imbalances in neurotransmitter systems, including GABAergic and glutamatergic signaling, are implicated in both ASD and epilepsy 32 , 33 and may hypothetically contribute to altered excitatory/inhibitory balance, cortical hyperexcitability, and impaired sleep organization. However, this area remains incompletely understood. 34 , 35 , 36 By applying actigraphy, a noninvasive technique enabling prolonged monitoring of locomotor activity in freely moving animals, this study bridges the gap between clinical and preclinical research. Through the analysis of clinically relevant rest‐activity parameters, we provide new insights into how genetic disruptions may affect sleep–wake organization. Given its practicality and translational value, actigraphy should be further promoted as a complementary tool to EEG‐studies in preclinical sleep research.

These findings underscore the importance of assessing sleep–wake regulation as a translational biomarker in genetic models of epilepsy and ASD. Understanding how rest‐activity disturbances evolve with age and disease progression may offer translational implications for early detection and intervention strategies targeting sleep–wake regulation to mitigate cognitive and seizure‐related outcomes in neurodevelopmental epilepsies.
