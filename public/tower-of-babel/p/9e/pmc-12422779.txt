Monocyte Anisocytosis Can Discriminate Between Sepsis and Sterile Inflammation, but not Mortality, in Critically Ill Surgical/Trauma Patients: A Secondary Prospective Analysis
Authors: Miguel Hernández-Ríos, Ruoxuan Wu, Valerie A. Polcz, Rachel D. Burnside, Lael M. Yonker, Daniel Irimia, Feifei Xiao, Jaimar C. Rincon, Tyler J. Loftus, Lyle L. Moldawer, Robert Maile, Philip A. Efron, Muxuan Liang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES (BACKGROUND):Monocyte anisocytosis (monocyte distribution width [MDW]) has been previously validated to predict sepsis and outcome in patients presenting in the emergency department and mixed-population ICUs. Determining sepsis in a critically ill surgical/trauma population is often difficult due to concomitant inflammation and stress. We examined whether MDW could identify sepsis among patients admitted to a surgical/trauma ICU and predict clinical outcome.DESIGN:Secondary analysis of three prospective observational clinical studies.SETTING:Single institution ICU.PATIENTS/SUBJECTS:Two hundred thirty-eight participants were included in this study: 107 patients who were admitted to the ICU and adjudicated to have sepsis, 80 patients who were considered critically ill nonseptic (CINS), and 51 healthy control participants.INTERVENTIONS:MDW was measured among hospitalized patients admitted to the ICU with the diagnosis of sepsis or CINS patients at risk of developing sepsis. Blood samples were collected at admission and at intervals during ICU admission.MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:MDW significantly differed between septic and CINS patients on ICU admission (26.4, interquartile range [IQR, 23.5–30.8] vs. 20.1 [IQR, 17.9–21.9]; p < 0.001) and could discriminate with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.85 (95% CI, 0.79–0.91; p < 0.001). An MDW of greater than 22.0 at admission to the ICU could identify sepsis with a 78% specificity and a 90% sensitivity but could not discriminate in-hospital, 30-day, or 90-day mortality.LIMITATIONS:Small sample size from a single institution. Our analysis did not include other relevant biomarkers such as procalcitonin, C-reactive protein, and interleukin-6. In the imputation of missing values, linear mixed-effect models were used, risking model misspecification and the violation of the missing-at-random assumption.CONCLUSIONS:Among surgical/trauma ICU patients, MDW can discriminate between sepsis and nonseptic inflammation, but it is a weak predictor of mortality.

KEY POINTS

Question: Can monocyte distribution width (MDW) volumes discriminate between sepsis and sterile inflammation and predict mortality among critically ill surgical/trauma patients?

Findings: Admission to ICU MDW values greater than or equal to 22.0 can discriminate between sepsis and sterile inflammation among critically ill surgical/trauma patients with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.85, 78% of specificity, and 90% of sensitivity. MDW was a weak predictor of in-hospital, 30-day, or 90-day mortality.

Meaning: When suspecting sepsis among surgical/trauma patients, an MDW greater than or equal to 22.0 may aid diagnosis and commencement of treatment.

Sepsis is a substantial public health concern in the United States, characterized by the body’s pathologic response to infection, which can lead to tissue damage, organ failure, and death (1). Each year, sepsis affects over 1.7 million Americans and is a leading cause of hospitalization (2). Despite advances in medical care, the prevalence of sepsis has been rising, partly due to an aging population, increased awareness, earlier diagnosis, and the prevalence of comorbidities that compromise the immune system (3). The morbidity associated with sepsis is substantial, and those who survive face long-term health issues such as cognitive impairment, physical disabilities, and an overall reduced quality of life (4, 5).

Precision medicine has the potential to revolutionize the treatment of sepsis by tailoring treatments to individual patients at increased risk of morbidity. Precision medicine leverages biomarkers that can provide early warning signs, allowing for timely and targeted interventions and suggesting appropriate therapeutics.

Monocyte anisocytosis or monocyte distribution width (MDW) has emerged as a potentially important biomarker in the early detection of sepsis. MDW reflects the variability in monocyte cell size, which increases during inflammatory responses, including sepsis (6). Multiple studies (6, 7) led to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) 510(k) clearance in 2019 of the Early Sepsis Indicator by Beckman Coulter (Danaher Corp., Washington, DC) measuring MDW for early detection of sepsis in the emergency department (ED). Crouser et al (6) found that MDW had a high sensitivity for identifying sepsis in ED patients, often preceding clinical signs and symptoms. Furthermore, a multicenter study conducted by Hausfater et al (8) reinforced these findings, highlighting that MDW could significantly reduce the time to diagnosis and improve patient outcomes by enabling earlier intervention.

Most studies to date have examined predictive potential of MDW in patients who present to the ED with community-acquired sepsis. Agnello et al (9), in a pilot study, examined MDW in patients admitted to a mixed-use ICU and demonstrated that MDW could identify sepsis patients.

Less is known about the predictive role of MDW in purely hospital-acquired sepsis as seen in surgical/trauma ICUs. Unlike community-acquired sepsis and mixed-use ICUs, patients admitted to surgical/trauma ICUs often have ongoing concomitant inflammation from surgical or traumatic injury (which is clinically difficult to differentiate from sepsis), and are at increased risk of developing sepsis due to invasive procedures, indwelling devices (e.g., catheters, ventilators), and a generally compromised immune state from prolonged hospitalization or underlying health conditions (10, 11). Hospital-acquired sepsis is frequently associated with more treatment-resistant pathogens, including Clostridium difficile, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, necessitating different empirical antibiotic choices. The timely diagnosis can be more challenging, as these patients may present with subtle or atypical signs of sepsis, superimposed on their existing critical illness. We hypothesized that monocyte anisocytosis could discriminate between sepsis and sterile inflammation among critically ill surgical/trauma patients. We also asked whether MDW could discriminate in-hospital and long-term survival comparable to previously reported findings in ED patients (12).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patient Cohorts

The present study is a secondary analysis of subsets of patients obtained from three prospective, observational studies conducted on hospitalized patients admitted to a single surgical ICU at a quaternary-care academic health science center with a putative diagnosis of sepsis or considered to be at risk of developing sepsis (13, 14). Samples were obtained at the time of blood collection and analyzed within 1 hour of collection. Clinical data were obtained post hoc and are obtainable in the public domain from the University of Florida Clinical and Translational Science Institute Biorepository (https://www.ctsi.ufl.edu/research/laboratory-services/ctsi-biorepository-2/).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

This is a secondary analysis of a patient cohort that combines data from three prospective, observational studies. Two are UF Sepsis and Critical Illness Research Center (SCIRC) studies registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04414189, NCT05110937), one of which has been published (13). The third study included subjects enrolled at Shands-UF Hospital from an National Institute of General Medical Sciences-funded study (R01 GM-139046-05; Stratifying Patient Immune Endotypes in Sepsis [SPIES]) consortium (14). In all three studies, diagnosis of initial sepsis or septic shock was made according to the 2016 Society of Critical Care Medicine/European Society of Intensive Care Medicine International Sepsis Definitions Conference [Sepsis-3 [1]). Very briefly, an existing electronic medical record-based system was used (the Modified Early Warning Score-Sepsis Recognition System). Once identified via this system, patients with sepsis were subsequently treated with protocolized care based on the Surviving Sepsis guidelines (15).

Inclusion criteria consisted of the following: admission to the surgical/trauma ICU with either presumed sepsis or determined to be at risk of surgical sepsis; age older than 18 years; and management of sepsis patients via the sepsis clinical management protocol (15). All enrolled septic and at-risk for sepsis patients were adjudicated by physician-investigators and classified into septic or septic shock. The enrolled patients who did not meet sepsis criteria were classified as critically ill nonseptic (CINS) to represent ongoing sterile inflammation with ICU needs. All adjudicators were blinded to MDW results and completed adjudication was obtained within 1 week of patient enrollment. Detailed enrollment criteria from all parent studies are included in the Supplementary Materials (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B547). Exclusion criteria, for all patients, consisted of: refractory shock; inability to achieve source control; presepsis expected lifespan less than 3 months; expected withdrawal of care; severe congestive heart failure (New York Heart Association Class IV); Child-Pugh Class C liver disease or undergoing evaluation for liver transplant; HIV infection with CD4+ count less than 200 cells/mm3; prior solid organ transplant; use of chronic steroids or immunosuppressive agents; pregnancy; institutionalized or other vulnerable patients; chemotherapy or radiotherapy treatment within 30 days of sepsis onset; severe traumatic brain injury (defined by radiologic evidence and Glasgow Coma Scale < 8); spinal cord injury with permanent deficits; or unable to obtain informed consent. For visualization, please refer to our flow chart of screening and adjudication in the Supplementary Materials (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B547).

Healthy Participants

Healthy participants were recruited from the Shands-UF Health facilities. Every attempt was made to age, sex, and race/ethnicity match the cohort to the sepsis and CINS patients. Participants denied any active infectious or inflammatory event or taking any prescribed medication other than statins, including nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Patients were designated as being cancer-free and had not received any antineoplastic therapy for at least 6 months.

Sample Analyses

Blood samples were collected at admission, days 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, and 14 from patients while residing in the surgical/trauma ICU. The actual study collection day was determined by the primary study enrolled and the availability of the collection. Total and differential leukocyte counts, including MDW, were determined on a Beckman Coulter DxH900 hematology analyzer (Brea, CA). MDW results were not available to clinicians or research staff during hospitalization of the patient, nor during adjudication of their clinical status. MDW is not captured in the medical record since it is not FDA-approved for our patient population.

Clinical Outcomes

All enrolled subjects underwent post hoc adjudication by physician-investigators within 1 week of cohort enrollment to confirm sepsis diagnosis, severity, and source. Individual clinical outcome variables included all-cause in-hospital, 30-day, and 90-day mortality.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive data are presented as frequencies and percentages, median and inner quantiles, or means and sds. Missing measurements in MDW and other biomarkers were imputed using linear mixed-effect models and best linear unbiased predictions. The Fisher exact test or Pearson chi-square test and t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test/Kruskal-Wallis test were used for the comparison of categorical and continuous variables, respectively. All significance tests were two-sided, with a raw “p ≤ 0.05” considered statistically significant. Significance levels “0.01 ≤ p < 0.05” are reported precisely; “0.001 ≤ p < 0.01” are reported as “p < 0.01,” and lower values are all reported as “p < 0.001.” Univariable logistic regressions were performed to predict in-hospital mortality and septic status using Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II, WBC count, absolute neutrophil count (ANC), absolute lymphocyte count (ALC), absolute neutrophil:lymphocyte count ratio (NLR), and MDW. Multivariate logistic regressions were performed with covariates, including MDW and WBC/ANC/WBC + ANC; no variable selection methods, such as Akaike information criterion/Bayesian information criterion and Lasso regression, were employed. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was employed to create models to distinguish sepsis and CINS patients and in-hospital mortality. The time-dependent AUROC curves were employed to predict survivor and death at 30- and 90-day post-admission, using Kaplan-Meier estimators as inverse probability weighting. A p values and 95% CIs were constructed by performing 1000 bootstrapped iterations to compare AUROCs among models. Analyses were performed using the R Statistical Package, Version 4.2.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Study Approval

Ethics approvals were obtained from the University of Florida and Western Institutional Review Boards (IRB No. 202100559 Dysfunctional Myelopoiesis and Myeloid Derived Suppressor Cells in Sepsis Pathobiology; SPIES-IRB No. 202000924 Stratifying Patient Immune Endotypes in Sepsis—Explainable, Fair, Reproducible and Collaborative Surgical Artificial Intelligence: Integrating Data, Algorithms and Clinical Reasoning for Surgical Risk Assessment [XAI-IDEALIST]; and Inflammatix-WIRB No. 2020074 Clinical Validation of HostDx Sepsis; on Nanostring: A Prospective Observational Validation Trial; approved April 07, 2021, June 05, 2020, and April 03, 2020, respectively). Informed consent was obtained from each subject or their surrogate decision-maker. Self-reported or proxy-reported race and ethnicity category data were collected as per National Institutes of Health reporting guidelines and requirements. Our study was performed in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines (16), the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation (institutional or regional) and with the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

Differences in MDW and Other Hematological Parameters Among Critically Ill Patients

MDW, as a component of a complete blood count (CBC) and differential, was measured at admission and at repeated intervals during hospitalization (Table 1). Healthy participants had a median MDW of 15.9 (14.9–17.5). MDW were significantly elevated in both the sepsis (26.4 [23.5–30.8]) and CINS patients (20.1 [17.9–21.9]) at admission to the ICU (p < 0.001), with the sepsis patients being significantly higher (p < 0.001) than CINS patients. MDW remained elevated in sepsis patients throughout hospitalization when compared with either CINS patients or healthy participants (all p < 0.05; Fig. 1A).

Modeling MDW and Other Biomarkers to Discriminate Sepsis and Clinical Outcome

Receiver operating characteristic curves determined the capability of MDW and other biomarkers to distinguish newly admitted surgical ICU patients who were septic from CINS patients. In this case, admission MDW was compared with admission WBC, ALC, ANC, and NLR (Fig. 3A). MDW on ICU admission had an AUROC of 0.85 (95% CI, 0.79–0.91; p < 0.001) discriminating septic from CINS patients. In comparison, the AUROC was 0.77 (95% CI, 0.66–0.87; p < 0.001) for admission WBC, 0.75 (95% CI, 0.67–0.82; p < 0.001) for ANC, 0.56 (95% CI, 0.47–0.64; p = 0.192) for ALC, and 0.63 (95% CI, 0.55–0.72; p < 0.01) for the NLR. When admission MDW was combined with WBC, ALC, ANC, or NLR, AUROCs were only modestly and insignificantly improved (Table 2; Supplemental Table 3, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B547; and Supplemental Table 8, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B547).

MDW and other biomarkers were also examined at later time points during hospitalization in combined sepsis and CINS cohorts to better reflect the overall ICU demographics. MDW’s ability to discriminate in-hospital, 30-day, or 90-day survival did not significantly improve over time. In fact, the ability of MDW to discriminate survival during hospitalization was not better than either ANC, ALC, or WBC. Only NLR could discriminate in-hospital (NLR at day 5) and 90-day mortality (NLR at days 4 and 5; and Supplemental Tables 4, A and B, 5A, and 6, A and B, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B547) in the sepsis and CINS cohorts. The NLR AUROC on day 5 of hospital admission most closely approximated the discriminatory power of APACHE II at admission for 90-day mortality (Fig. 3, B and C; and Supplemental Table 7A, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B547); with no significant difference between the two (Supplemental Table 7B, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B547). APACHE II remained the best overall predictor of in-hospital mortality.

Sensitivity and specificity were used to identify the optimal threshold for admission MDW that maximizes the specificity and controls the sensitivity of predicting who is septic at (or close to) 90%. An MDW threshold of 22.0 gave a sensitivity of 90%, specificity of 78%, positive predictive value (PPV) of 77%, and negative predictive value (NPV) of 91% (Fig. 4A). Sensitivity and specificity were also used to optimize the threshold for day 5 NLR that maximizes the specificity and controls the sensitivity of predicting in-hospital mortality at (or close to) 90% (Fig. 4B). Similar methodology was applied for maximizing specificity and controlling the sensitivity of predicting 90-day mortality with day 5 NLR (Fig. 4, C and D). Using a threshold of 8.41 gave a sensitivity of 90%, specificity of 60%, PPV of 25%, and NPV of 98%.

DISCUSSION

Sepsis remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in ICUs worldwide. Early detection is crucial, as prompt intervention can significantly improve patient outcomes. Traditional diagnostic methods for identifying sepsis, such as blood cultures or the polymerase chain reaction, often require extended processing times, delaying critical treatment decisions. In this context, MDW has emerged as a promising biomarker for the early identification of sepsis, particularly in the critical care setting where leukocyte counts and cell phenotypes are routinely available in near-immediate timeframes.

Studies suggest that MDW can identify sepsis in its early stages, offering a valuable adjunct to clinical assessments and traditional laboratory markers such as lactate, procalcitonin, C-reactive protein (CRP), WBC, and absolute lymphocyte or neutrophil counts (6, 8, 17, 18). Seminal work by Crouser et al (6) observed that MDW, compared with other parameters, showed high sensitivity (77%) and specificity (73%) and an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.79 (0.73–0.84) for sepsis detection in ED patients presenting with systemic inflammatory response syndrome or infection, with a threshold of 20.5. Notably, the FDA-cleared threshold for MDW discriminating sepsis in ED populations is 20.0, which is also lower than the value determined in this study. MDW was particularly effective when used as part of a multibiomarker approach. For example, when combined with WBC, AUC increased to 0.89. A negative WBC and MDW had a NPV of 98% (6).

In a more complex clinical situation with post-surgical/trauma patients admitted to an ICU for management, we saw that MDW alone had a predictive power of 0.85 and did not significantly benefit further when combined with individual leukocyte measurements, most likely due to insufficient sample size. Nevertheless, we report similar predictive capabilities and threshold values of MDW (22.0) in the context of sepsis near those reported by Crouser et al (6) and other studies (19).

The utility of procalcitonin and CRP as early detectors of sepsis has been extensively studied and debated, procalcitonin was championed as an improved biomarker for sepsis, with a maximum reported PPV of 75% (6, 8, 18, 20–29). Crouser et al (6) emphasized that MDW and WBC are not comparable with procalcitonin, CRP, and lactate; MDW and WBC are available to clinicians in an earlier stage of clinical evaluation, many times when sepsis has not been considered. Recent studies revealed improved performance of MDW over procalcitonin and CRP predicting sepsis in the ED (18, 20, 21). MDW also emerges as a diagnostic biomarker when applying to Sepsis-2 and Sepsis-3 criteria, validating MDW’s reliability to predict sepsis among ED patients (7, 8, 20).

More recently, several studies have highlighted the utility of MDW in the early detection of sepsis among other hospitalized patient cohorts (9, 20, 30–34). Diagnosis of sepsis in these patients is often more difficult due to ongoing inflammatory processes associated with hospitalization (i.e., underlying chronic illness, multiple devices/peripheral access points as nidus for infection, and/or surgical or traumatic injury). For instance, Polilli et al (31) conducted a preliminary analysis of MDW as a predictor of sepsis in mixed-use ICU patients, where MDW in combination with procalcitonin enhanced early recognition of sepsis. However, both our and the study by Polilli et al (31) report similar discriminatory ability of MDW alone (0.85 vs. 0.84, respectively) predicting sepsis and similar threshold values (> 22 vs. > 23, respectively). Surprisingly, we did not see any significant improvement in MDW’s ability to discriminate sepsis during hospitalization. Polilli et al (31) did not examine whether MDW evolved over time or whether changes in MDW were associated with differences in clinical trajectories or long-term outcome. These findings suggest that it is the initial or early host response to sepsis that is most defining in terms of discriminating from nonseptic, critical illness.

Unexpectedly, MDW could not discriminate in-hospital, 30-day, and 90-day mortality in our sepsis and CINS patients, at any time point during their ICU stay. This differs from reports from Agnello et al (9, 32, 34–36) who examined MDW values over time in both the ED and in medical ICUs. In their most recent study (34), they found no association between ICU mortality and admission biomarkers, but there was an association during the first 5 days of hospitalization, with MDW showing the greatest accuracy, specifically an increase or decrease in MDW from days 1–4 and 5 discriminated survival. Therefore, our results suggest that MDW may be more useful for initial diagnostic discrimination than for mortality prediction.

Interestingly, in our study, only NLR was a significant predictor of in-hospital and 90-day mortality, consistently on day 5 of ICU admission for both in-hospital and 90-day survival both outcomes (Fig. 3, B and C). ANC, ALC, and NLR ratio have been reported previously as useful biomarkers to predict in-hospital, 30-day, and 90-day mortality (37–40). Importantly, the power to predict clinical outcomes, although statistically significant with NLR, was less than the predictive power of MDW to discriminate sepsis and less than the predictive power of APACHE discriminating in-hospital and 90-day mortality.

Clinical Significance

In a critically ill cohort where patients often have ongoing inflammatory processes secondary to surgical/traumatic injury or nosocomial infection, the risk of sepsis is increased, and rapid and accurate diagnostic tools are essential. In the search for a standardized diagnostic with an actionable threshold value, Piccioni et al (19) suggested that MDW would not be helpful in identifying septic patients due to its low specificity attributed to variabilities in comorbidities and chronic inflammation seen in a mixed-population ICU. The data from this study is not consistent with this conclusion for a post-surgical/trauma mixed ICU population. In contrast to Piccioni et al (19), we would suggest that MDW may offer several advantages in this surgical ICU environment. MDW can be measured rapidly as part of routine CBC tests, providing immediate data that could inform clinical decisions during the critical early hours of ICU admission. The clinician may more confidently begin a prophylactic antimicrobial regimen or if appropriate, terminate a prolonged antibiotic course in the setting of a false-positive septic patient (41). Paoli et al (42) describes the potential economic benefits of using MDW as a biomarker for early detection of sepsis, projecting to save more than $700,000 for the hospital annually using their model.

In the same setting, NLR proved discriminatory when measured during the hospital course for both early and late mortality. It is important to note that the observed low in-hospital mortality in our study is consistent with the improvement in delivery of critical care, as well as implementation of early warning systems. This has resulted in a rise in chronic critical illness and increased outpatient death within 1 year of discharge (43). In September 2024, the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality released its annual report demonstrating in-hospital sepsis mortality of 11.8% in 2019 (44). Meyer et al (45) reported similar trends for sepsis-related in-hospital mortality. In addition, in one of our parent studies, we reported an in-hospital mortality rate of 7.8%. Furthermore, the seven institutions included within the additional parent study, SPIES consortium (14), report rates of 13% in-hospital mortality.

In a series of reviews, retrospective analyses, and meta-analyses (38–40, 46), an altered NLR was associated with sepsis mortality, indicating that an aggressive inflammatory response and impaired adaptive immunity can contribute to poor prognosis. This type of information is invaluable for critically ill patients, especially the septic elderly who are known for the worst mortality rates (47), to involve palliative care early (48–50).

Limitations

The current study has several limitations that require caution when extrapolating these findings to more generalized clinical practice. Sample sizes were relatively small, and although the data were extracted from three clinical studies spanning 7 years, they came from a single institution with standardized clinical management and decision-making. Nevertheless, this small sample size is intended to represent a cohort of patients who have not been extensively studied in the past for MDW, and pose challenges not seen with other at-risk sepsis cohorts. Whether similar relationships exist at other tertiary- or quaternary-care healthcare centers or hospitals worldwide require experimental validation. The current study also did not enroll all subjects admitted to the surgical/trauma ICUs, but rather selected consecutive ICU-admitted patients who met predefined criteria of sepsis or were designated by protocol as being at risk of developing sepsis. Although post hoc adjudicators were blinded to MDW results, selection and confounding bias may remain since the original source studies applied slightly different criteria. Every attempt was made to match patients from the three source studies to our secondary analysis inclusion/exclusion criteria. This may also explain our high incidence of septic shock (53.8% of septic patients).

In addition, procalcitonin, CRP, interleukin-6, and other potentially relevant biomarkers were not included. In the imputation of missing values, linear mixed-effect models were used, which might suffer from model misspecification and the violation of the missing-at-random assumption (Supplemental Table 10, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B547).

CONCLUSIONS

MDW represents a potentially valuable and readily available tool for the early detection of sepsis in critically ill surgical/trauma patients, particularly during the initial period of ICU admission when rapid decision-making is crucial. Its integration into routine CBC analyses allows for timely consideration of sepsis, potentially improving patient outcomes through earlier intervention. This study also highlights MDW’s limitations in predicting short- and long-term mortality in these post-surgical/trauma patients. A comprehensive, multimodal approach is still required when managing sepsis in the ICU.
