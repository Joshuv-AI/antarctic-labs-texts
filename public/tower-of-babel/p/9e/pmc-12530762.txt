Scalp schwannomas as a rare entity with common misdiagnoses: A case report and systematic literature review
Authors: Ola Mohammed Bin Shilash, Nouf Faris Alanazi, Aseel Ahmed Alamoudi, Yazeed Musaad Alotaibi, Abdulrahman K. Alabdulsalam, Farid Bouhouf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Scalp schwannomas are rare, benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors often misdiagnosed due to their nonspecific presentation. They typically present as slow-growing, painless subcutaneous nodules, most commonly in the occipital region.Case Description: We report a 29-year-old female with a gradually enlarging, painless occipital scalp nodule for 7 months. Clinically, it was suspected to be a lipoma or pilar cyst. Excision under local anesthesia revealed a well-encapsulated, yellowish mass. Histopathology confirmed a benign schwannoma. The patient had an uneventful recovery with no recurrence at 6-month follow-up.Conclusion: Scalp schwannomas, though rare, exhibit characteristic clinical and histopathological features. They are frequently misdiagnosed due to their rare occurrence as well as their clinical and radiological resemblance to other benign lesions. Complete surgical excision is the preferred treatment, offering an excellent prognosis with minimal risk of recurrence. Extended follow-up is recommended to monitor potential late recurrences.

INTRODUCTION

Schwannoma, which is known as neurilemmoma, is a rare benign encapsulated tumor that originates from a nerve sheath.[19] Schwannomas commonly occur in specific areas intracranially, such as the cerebellopontine angle, but are less commonly observed extracranially, particularly in cutaneous nerves. Notably, cutaneous schwannomas are more commonly found in the deep dermis or subcutaneous tissue rather than in superficial nerves of the upper dermis.[3,13,16] Due to rarity and lack of a typical presentation, schwannomas are often misdiagnosed as lipoma, epidermal cysts, or other benign tumors.[21] In addition, it is uncommon for schwannoma to present on the scalp as it only accounts for 25% of extracranial schwannomas.[19] Patients mainly manifest asymptomatically with solitary, pedunculated, soft, and slow-growing nodules, which may be painful or painless.[4]

In the present review, we illustrate a rare case of scalp schwannoma, discussing its features, diagnostic and therapeutic components, and systematically review topics from existing case reports to provide a comprehensive summary of the current evidence.

CASE PRESENTATION

We present the case of a 29-year-old female, medically and surgically free, who noticed a painless swelling over the back of her scalp that had been growing gradually for 7 months, which caused her mild discomfort, particularly while lying down. However, the patient denied any fever, weight changes, history of trauma, previous surgery, and similar growth over the scalp. Family history of similar complaints was negative. She has no personal or family history of Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) (von Recklinghausen’s disease). On examination, the lesion was well defined, soft, regular, mobile, non-tender subcutaneous nodule measuring about 1.4 cm. The overlying skin was intact and normal in color; no palpable lymph nodes were detected regionally, and there was no pathologic finding on the neurologic examination. Given the clinical presentation, the leading differential diagnosis included a sebaceous cyst or lipoma. The growth was excised under local anesthesia, through a 2-cm incision, and a yellowish, encapsulated soft-tissue mass was carefully dissected and removed in its entirety without complication. Post-operative recovery was uneventful. The specimen was firm white-tan in color, measuring 1.4 × 1.0 × 0.8 cm in greatest dimension, and has a homogeneous glistening cut surface. Microscopic assessment showed an encapsulated tumor composed of biphasic hypercellular and hypocellular myxoid areas [Figure 1a]. The tumor cells are spindled and focally showing nuclear palisading [Figure 1b]. No atypia or necrosis is seen. On immunohistochemical staining, the tumor cells showed diffuse positive nuclear reaction to S100 [Figure 1c] and SOX-10 [Figure 1d], while negative for epithelial membrane antigen (EMA) and neurofilament protein (NFP). The Ki-67 index was under 5%, supporting its benign nature. The overall findings are consistent with the diagnosis of schwannoma.

Grossly, the mass consisted of white, yellow soft tissue with a smooth outer surface, measuring 1.4 × 1.0 × 0.8 cm. Homogeneous white–yellow glistening cut surface typical of schwannomas. Subsequent follow-up, including a brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), showed no residual lesion or recurrence.

METHODS

Data sources

Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, a comprehensive systematic review was conducted on PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Web of Science databases from inception to March 2025.

The search strategy utilized included: (“schwannoma” OR “neurilemmoma” OR “neurinomas”) AND (“scalp”) AND (“clinical presentation” OR “outcome”). After removing duplicates, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts to identify relevant studies. Full-text articles were then assessed based on predefined eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer.

Eligibility criteria

Studies published in English reporting clinical presentation, histopathological features, and surgical outcomes of patients diagnosed with scalp schwannoma confirmed by histopathology with no restrictions on age or gender. Review articles, conference abstracts, and non-peer-reviewed literature were excluded. Studies focused on intracranial or non-scalp peripheral nerve schwannomas were also excluded.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data using a standardized data collection form. Extracted variables included patient demographics, clinical presentation, tumor size and location, histopathological findings, S100 protein positivity status, management approach, recurrence rates, and follow-up duration. Any discrepancies were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer.

RESULTS

The database search performed in PubMed, ScienceDirect, and Web of Science revealed a total of 1418 articles. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 24 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Following the application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, 11 studies were included in the final analysis. Across the included cases, patient ages ranged from 17 to 80 years, with a mean age of 40.9 years. There was an equal gender distribution, with 6 males and 6 females. The majority of cases presented as a solitary, painless, and slow-growing subcutaneous nodule. However, four patients reported discomfort or tenderness, particularly when pressure was applied.[7,14,15,21] Lesion sizes varied from 0.5 cm to 20 cm. The occipital region was the most involved site, reported in 5 cases (41.7%), followed by the vertex of the scalp (2 cases) and the right parietal or paramedian regions (4 cases). A single case involved the retroauricular area.[14] Our patient, a 29-year-old female, presented with a 1.4 cm soft, mobile nodule over the occipital region of the scalp, causing mild discomfort when lying down. None of the patients, including ours, had a known personal or family history of NF1.

All cases demonstrated the classic features of benign schwannoma on histopathology, with Antoni A and Antoni B patterns variably present, except for two studies by Demir and Tokyol and Hasturk et al., who presented a malignant form of schwannoma.[5,8] Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed strong S-100 protein positivity in nearly all reported cases. SOX-10 positivity was also noted when performed, further supporting the diagnosis. Our case also showed S-100 and SOX-10 positivity, with a Ki-67 index <5%, supporting its benign nature. EMA and NFP staining were negative. Complete surgical excision was the treatment of choice in all reported cases, with one patient requiring adjuvant radiotherapy.[5] The post-operative courses were uncomplicated, including our patient, who had an uneventful recovery. Follow-up data, available for six cases, revealed no recurrences were reported, including in our patient, who remained symptom-free with no radiologic evidence of residual disease at 6 months of follow-up brain MRI. However, one case reported by Asuquo et al. initially presented with recurrence and underwent wide excision; the lesion progressed further within 3 months post-surgery, and no additional treatment details or follow-up outcomes were reported.[2]

DISCUSSION

Schwannomas are benign, slow-growing peripheral nerve sheath tumors composed entirely of Schwann cells. They commonly arise as solitary, well-encapsulated nodules and are rarely associated with hereditary conditions.[19] Although most schwannomas occur sporadically, familial cases have been reported, particularly in individuals with genetic predispositions, including mutations in NF2, LZTR1, and SMARCB1.[17] Such cases are more likely to be present at an earlier age and may warrant further genetic evaluation. However, in the absence of systemic features or multiple lesions, sporadic schwannomas remain the predominant presentation, comprising over 90% of cases.[17] In our case, as well as in the literature, scalp schwannomas present diagnostic challenges due to their clinical resemblance to more common scalp lesions such as lipomas and epidermal or pilar cysts.[10] The role of imaging in diagnosing scalp schwannomas is limited. Computed tomography (CT) scans, while frequently employed in evaluating scalp masses, demonstrate poor sensitivity for schwannomas, with a correct diagnosis in only 14% of cases.[20] The findings on CT, which include low to intermediate attenuation cystic masses, make the differentiation from other common scalp cystic lesions difficult.[20] Conversely, MRI offers superior diagnostic accuracy, correctly identifying schwannomas in almost 80% of reported cases. MRI characteristics of schwannomas often include well-demarcated, encapsulated lesions with heterogeneous enhancement, findings that aid in preoperative planning.[20] Nonetheless, a histopathological confirmation of schwannoma remains the gold standard of diagnosis due to the clinical and radiologic mimicry of schwannoma with other common benign scalp masses.[12] Histological examination typically reveals a biphasic pattern with Antoni A and Antoni B areas, along with Verocay bodies in hypercellular Antoni A regions. Immunohistochemical staining further confirms the diagnosis, with Schwann cells demonstrating strong S100 positivity and the perineurial capsule staining positive for EMA. The absence of axons, as confirmed by neurofilament staining, further differentiates schwannomas from other peripheral nerve sheath tumors.[18] Complete surgical excision remains the treatment of choice for scalp schwannomas, yielding excellent outcomes with a low recurrence rate.[9] While schwannomas generally do not recur following total resection, data on the recurrence rates specifically for scalp schwannomas remain sparse due to the rarity of reported cases. Given their benign nature, the post-operative prognosis is favorable, with patients typically experiencing full recovery without complications.[9]

CONCLUSION

Despite the rarity of scalp schwannoma, multiple reports internationally have documented a growing number of cases. This current paper was undertaken to provide the first systematic review of this condition, assessing various elements associated with the treatment, diagnosis, and the presentation. The findings suggest a large portion of patients present with solitary, painless, and slow-growing subcutaneous nodules on the scalp, predominantly in the occipital region. Neither a family history nor an NF1 history was influencing the etiology. The typical histopathological characteristics include Antoni A and Antoni B patterns. Immunohistochemical analysis consistently demonstrated strong S-100 protein positivity in almost all cases, along with positivity of SOX-10. Regarding the prognosis, no malignant transformations were observed, and on follow-up, a reasonable number of cases revealed no recurrence within a 6-month period with utility of brain MRI, except in one study, which showed contrasting findings for the recurrence. Overall, the reviewed reports highlighted excellent outcomes that emphasize on performing complete surgical excision without administering adjuvant therapy. Our findings warrant the need for an extended follow-up period to evaluate the recurrence rate with better insight and to provide valuable information on long-term outcomes.
