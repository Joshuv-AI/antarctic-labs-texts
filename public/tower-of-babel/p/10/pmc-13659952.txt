Heart-shaped tension-reducing suturing versus conventional interrupted suturing alleviates perineal pain, accelerates resolution of suture reactions, and improves scar quality in primiparous women undergoing perineal repair
Authors: Ju Li, Ling Li, Hui Zhou, Ya Nan Shao, Hong Mei Zhu, Yue Ning Zhu, Jing Wang, Tie Cheng Sun, Li Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the efficacy of the heart-shaped tension-reducing suture (HSTRS) versus interrupted suture (IS) in scar quality and postoperative pain relief in primiparous women.MethodsIn this study, 142 women were allocated to HSTRS (n = 61) or IS (n = 81). Pain score was assessed via NRS at multiple timepoints; suture reactions were graded (0–III), and scar quality was evaluated using VSS, OSAS, and PSAS at 42 days, 90 days, and 180 days.ResultsThe HSTRS group showed significantly lower pain scores (P < 0.0001), faster resolution of suture reactions, and superior scar outcomes across all scales and timepoints (P < 0.01).ConclusionsHSTRS significantly improves perineal scar quality, alleviates postoperative perineal pain, and accelerates resolution of suture reactions compared with interrupted suturing, supporting its application for better postpartum perineal recovery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-04050-4.

Introduction

Perineal lacerations are a common complication during childbirth, with an incidence rate as high as 85% among primiparous women, of which approximately 89% are first- and second-degree tears [1]. These injuries can significantly impact postpartum quality of life and reproductive health. Poor healing and scar formation of perineal wounds may lead to infections, pain, and functional impairments, thereby severely affecting the physical and mental well-being [1, 2].

To date, the clinical management of perineal wounds primarily relies on two conventional suturing techniques: interrupted and continuous suturing [3, 4]. While interrupted suturing is straightforward to perform, it often results in poor tissue apposition, increasing the risk of wound dehiscence [4]. Continuous suturing, although reducing the number of sutures, may cause uneven tension distribution, leading to suboptimal healing outcomes [3]. Additionally, improper handling of deep tissues during suturing can elevate the incidence of postoperative complications, such as increased infection rates and re-tearing [1, 5].

The innovative approach employs a heart-shaped tension-reducing suture (HSTRS) technique that better conforms to the anatomical structure of the perineum, achieving more uniform tension distribution and promoting scar quality and postoperative pain relief [6]. Studies in other surgical fields, such as laparoscopic-assisted surgeries and the management of hypertrophic scars in cosmetic surgery, have demonstrated that the HSTRS can significantly reduce the incidence of postoperative complications [6–8]. The HSTRS is characterized by its meticulous tissue handling, which minimizes tissue damage while preserving blood supply, thereby facilitating scar quality and postoperative pain relief [6–8]. This technique has shown promise in reducing postoperative pain, improving scar formation, and lowering the risk of complications compared to traditional methods [6–8]. In recent years, the HSTRS has been gradually used in cesarean Sect [6]., but it has not yet been applied in the context of perineal wound repair in primiparous women.

Our study aimed to investigate the application and effectiveness of the HSTRS technique compared to the interrupted suture (IS) technique in promoting scar quality and postoperative pain relief in primiparous women, specifically evaluating its impact on postoperative pain, suture reactions, and scar formation.

Patients and methods

Patients information

Participants were recruited from the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology at Peking University International Hospital between January 2023 and April 2025. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University International Hospital (Approval No. PKUIH-LL-2023-KY-0074-02). Randomization was performed using a random number table, and participants were allocated to either the HSTRS or IS group. The allocation sequence was generated by an independent research assistant using a computer-generated random number table and was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. Envelopes were opened only after participant eligibility was confirmed and informed consent was obtained. The outcome assessors who performed all postoperative evaluations were blinded to group allocation. The imbalance in the number of participants between the two groups (61 in HSTRS and 81 in IS) was a chance occurrence due to the simple randomization; nonetheless, all baseline characteristics were well balanced between groups (Table 1), and both group sizes exceeded the minimum required by our power calculation. All suturing procedures were performed by experienced midwives to minimize operator-related variability. The study was designed and reported in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 guidelines for randomized controlled trials (in Fig. 1). The completed CONSORT checklist is provided as Supplementary Appendix.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) age 25–35 years; (II) singleton pregnancy with cephalic presentation; gestational age of 37–41 weeks; (III) regular prenatal care at our hospital and planned delivery there; (IV) ability to read and understand the questionnaires independently; (V) and provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (I) a history of keloid scarring; (II) perineal lesions (e.g., perineal leukoplakia); (III) severe cardiopulmonary disease; (IV) autoimmune disorders; (V) coagulation abnormalities; (VI) preterm birth; (VII) breech presentation; (VIII) gestational diabetes; (IX) psychiatric disorders; (X) or inability to cooperate with this study.

Heart-shaped tension-reducing suture (HSTRS) technique

All suturing procedures were performed by one of five experienced midwives who had received standardized training on both techniques prior to the study, using a uniform protocol to minimize operator-related variability. For patients requiring episiotomy, a mediolateral incision was made at a 45° angle from the posterior midline. The HSTRS group underwent perineal wound closure using a novel “ღ”-shaped tension-reducing suture technique [6–8]. HSTRS technique involved the placement of full-layer interrupted sutures that geometrically formed a heart-like pattern at the wound site [6–8]. The core technical principle was to achieve deep and secure engagement of the muscle and fascial layers, thereby promoting tight tissue. The “heart-shaped” suturing technique is performed using 2 − 0 absorbable sutures (VCP345, 2 − 0 Coated VICRYL, Ethicon Inc., Johnson & Johnson) to approximate the muscle and subcutaneous tissue. The suture is initiated with the needle tip facing upward, entering at point A and advancing through the subcutaneous layer toward the dermis. The needle then enters the dermis at point B and follows an arcuate path within the dermal layer, reaching the apex of the arch at point C. The needle curves downward symmetrically, ensuring that point C and its contralateral counterpart, point C1, lie at the same horizontal level—superficial to the mid-dermal papillary layer. The suture exits at point D, located at the dermal–subcutaneous junction. The contralateral wound edge is sutured in a symmetrical sequence through points D1, C1, B1, and A1, thereby forming a “heart-shaped” configuration. Sutures are placed at intervals of 1–2 cm. Finally, the skin is closed with a continuous intracuticular suture using 2 − 0 absorbable suture material (Fig. 1A).

Interrupted suturing (IS) technique

In our study, we utilized interrupted suturing (IS) for wound closure [9, 10]. The procedure involved inserting the needle from the subcutaneous fascial layer, traversing the dermal layer, and exiting on the opposite side. The needle was then reinserted into the dermal layer on the opposite side and passed back through the fascial layer. The knot was securely tied beneath the fascia to ensure proper closure. We used 2 − 0 absorbable sutures (VCP345, 2 − 0 Coated VICRYL, Ethicon Inc., Johnson & Johnson) for this purpose, as shown in Fig. 1B. The skin was closed with a continuous intracuticular suture, which was identical to the skin closure technique used in the HSTRS. Postoperative care included irrigation with chlorhexidine acetate solution twice daily to maintain cleanliness and promote scar quality and postoperative pain relief in both groups, ensuring comparability of postoperative management.

Postoperative outcomes evaluation

Pain assessment was conducted using the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), a validated and widely adopted instrument for self-reported pain measurement [11]. In our study, pain evaluations were performed at five key postoperative time points: 24 h, 72 h, 5 days, 14 days, and 42 days after the surgical procedure. The NRS requires patients to rate their pain on an 10-point scale ranging from 0 (representing “no pain”) to 10 (representing the “worst pain imaginable”). This multi-timepoint approach allowed for a comprehensive and dynamic tracking of postoperative pain progression and resolution in both groups (Supplementary 1). All outcome assessments were performed by three trained assessors who were blinded to group allocation. Standardized procedures and protocols were used for all measurements at each timepoint.

Suture reactions were assessed and graded according to a standardized four-point categorical scale: Grade 0: indicated a smooth incision surface without exudate, palpable nodules, or abnormal patient sensation; Grade I: referred to the presence of a palpable nodule at the incision site; Grade II: was defined as a palpable nodule accompanied by patient-reported tenderness upon touch; and Grade III: represented the presence of localized skin redness, swelling, pustule formation, or extrusion of dark suture material (Supplementary 2) [12].

Scar formation was evaluated at 42 days, 90 days, and 180 days postoperatively using a comprehensive multi-dimensional assessment system. Objective scar characteristics were quantified using the Vancouver Scar Scale (VSS), which assesses pigmentation, vascularity, pliability, and scar height (Supplementary 3) [7]. Additionally, both observer-rated and patient-reported outcomes were captured through the Observer Scar Assessment Scale (OSAS) and the Patient Scar Assessment Scale (PSAS), respectively (Supplementary 4 and 5) [7, 13].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0. Continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. For longitudinal outcomes (pain scores, suture reactions, and scar scales), repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, with group (HSTRS vs. IS) as the between-subjects factor, time as the within-subjects factor, and the group-by-time interaction term. The Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when sphericity was violated. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Sidak’s correction for multiple comparisons. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using χ² tests. Baseline characteristics between groups were compared using independent samples t-tests or χ² tests as appropriate. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of primiparous women

This study included 142 primiparous women who were randomly allocated to either the HSTRS group (n = 61) or the IS group (n = 81). Basic characteristics of primiparous women including maternal age, pregnancy week, duration of the first and second stages of labor, total labor duration, and postpartum blood loss was shown in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in these characteristics, except for the duration of the third stage of labor. Specifically, the third stage of labor was significantly shorter in the HSTRS group compared to the IS group (7.66 ± 4.88 vs. 10.3 ± 8.48, P = 0.025), this reduction did not translate into a significant difference in total labor duration between the groups (P = 0.498).

Pain assessment following suturing techniques

The levels of postoperative pain, assessed using the NRS scale, were significantly lower in the HSTRS group compared to the IS group across all evaluated time points (Table 2). At 24 h post-surgery, the pain score was 3.07 ± 0.31 in the HSTRS group versus 5.09 ± 0.39 in the IS group. This significant reduction in pain was maintained at 72 h (1.51 ± 0.67 vs. 4.02 ± 0.16, P = 0.000), 5 days (1.08 ± 0.33 vs. 3.02 ± 0.16, P = 0.025), and 42 days (0.08 ± 0.33 vs. 2.0 ± 0, P = 0.036). These results demonstrate a consistent and sustained analgesic benefit associated with the HSTRS technique throughout the postoperative period, suggesting its superiority in pain management following perineal repair.

Analysis of suture reactions

Suture reaction scores were compared between the two groups at multiple postoperative time points (Fig. 2). In IS group, all incisions (100%) exhibited a Grade II reaction at the 24-hour time point. The resolution of these reactions was notably slow, with a high prevalence of 69.14% still presenting as Grade II at 14 days. Concurrently, the emergence of Grade I reactions in IS group was delayed and remained at a lower incidence throughout the observation period compared to HSTRS group. Consequently, the achievement of a Grade 0 status (no reaction) was both delayed and much less frequent in IS group. In contrast, HSTRS group also presented with 100% Grade II reactions at 24 h. However, these reactions resolved rapidly, declining to a mere 4.9% by 14 days. This swift resolution was accompanied by a faster and more pronounced shift towards milder Grade I reactions and, ultimately, a much higher and earlier incidence of Grade 0 healing compared to IS group. Analysis of scar formation

Scar formation was assessed using three standardized scales (VSS, OSAS, and PSAS) at 42, 90, and 180 days postoperatively (Table 3). The HSTRS group demonstrated significantly lower scar scores across all three assessment tools at most time points. For VSS, significant reductions were observed at 42 days (8.34 ± 0.81 vs. 10.01 ± 0.8, P < 0.0001) and 180 days (1.31 ± 0.85 vs. 2.75 ± 0.83, P < 0.0001), with a non-significant difference at 90 days (P = 0.064). Similarly, OSAS scores were significantly lower in the HSTRS group at all intervals: 42 days (31.97 ± 2.45 vs. 34.46 ± 1.9, P = 0.0003), 90 days (28.3 ± 1.72 vs. 29.8 ± 1.81, P = 0.008), and 180 days (21.44 ± 3.05 vs. 24.88 ± 2.22, P < 0.0001). The PSAS scale showed consistently significant reductions in the HSTRS group at all time points (P < 0.0001). These results indicate that the HSTRS technique is associated with superior scar quality outcomes compared to conventional interrupted suturing.

Discussion

The present study conclusively demonstrates the superiority of the heart-shaped tension-reducing suture (HSTRS) technique over interrupted suture (IS) technique in enhancing postoperative pain relief and minimizing scar formation in primiparous women. The findings align with emerging evidence that innovative suturing techniques, such as the less invasive and anatomically adaptive HSTRS, can significantly improve postpartum clinical outcomes, underscoring its potential to become a new standard in obstetric practice.

Postpartum perineal pain significantly impacts maternal well-being, early mobilization, and mother-infant bonding, and inadequate control has been linked to persistent pain [14, 15]. Thus, optimizing scar quality and postoperative pain relief through refined suturing techniques is clinically important. Several studies have shown that there is a connection between persistent pain during the postpartum period [16–18]. This suggests that women may be more sensitive to pain during this time. Perineal pain related to episiotomy can negatively affect the mother’s quality of life and may also impact her ability to bond with the newborn infant [14]. The pain associated with perineal suturing, especially following an episiotomy, is a significant concern for postpartum women, as it can severely impact their quality of life [19]. In our study, the most significant advantage of the HSTRS technique over IS lies in its superior management of perineal pain. A key innovation of this research is the demonstration that HSTRS was associated with reduced perineal pain, particularly at early postoperative time points (24 h, 72 h, and 5 days), which may contribute to enhanced maternal comfort and facilitate early mobilization. This pronounced analgesic benefit, which has not been thoroughly investigated in prior studies, establishes HSTRS as an advanced strategy for perineal repair. Beyond statistical significance, the reductions in pain (≥ 2 NRS points) and scar scores observed in the HSTRS group are considered clinically meaningful, supporting the technique’s potential value in routine practice.

Suture reactions are immune-mediated responses to surgical sutures, which can manifest as localized inflammation, itching, or even systemic allergic reactions [20]. In obstetrics, these reactions are particularly concerning following perineal sutures or uterine compression sutures like the B-Lynch technique [21]. They can significantly impact postpartum recovery, causing discomfort and potentially complicating the healing process [21]. Understanding and managing suture reactions is crucial for optimizing patient outcomes and ensuring a smooth recovery. In our study, compared to the traditional IS technique, the HSTRS technique presents several unique benefits in managing suture reactions and promoting postoperative recovery. Firstly, the traditional method (IS technique) often leads to an edema phase around three days post-surgery, which can cause significant discomfort and prolong the healing process. In contrast, the HSTRS technique is designed to reduce tension on the wound, thereby alleviating suture reactions and minimizing the risk of edema. This not only enhances patient comfort but also facilitates a smoother healing trajectory. Secondly, the HSTRS technique offers superior wound closure with reduced tension, which can lead to better healing outcomes and a lower risk of complications. Our study innovatively points out that while the HSTRS technique may elicit an initial suture reaction comparable to that of the IS group, its resolution is substantially faster and does not persist into the later healing phases. This transient inflammatory response could be indicative of the body’s natural healing process being effectively engaged, suggesting a biologically intelligent design that triggers an initial reaction that subsides as the body heals [6–8].

Although mediolateral episiotomy can reduce delivery-related pain and enhance the safety of both parturients and newborns, it is associated with prolonged recovery and perineal scar formation, which may impair postoperative quality of life [22, 23]. While the method of suture closure may influence healing outcomes, post-episiotomy pain and scar formation are multifactorial and may be affected by infection, laceration degree, tissue trauma extent, and patient-related factors. Conventional interrupted suturing is often technically demanding and time-consuming, and may be associated with risks such as suture exposure, wound inflammation, and prolonged healing. By contrast, continuous suturing techniques have been shown to reduce short-term pain, require less suture material, and shorten repair time [9, 10]. Moreover, they carry risks such as suture exposure, higher incidence of wound inflammation, redness, swelling, and extended healing time. Notably, these methods offer limited improvement in the cosmetic appearance of the surgical scar. In contrast, the HSTRS technique effectively addresses these limitations [6]. By utilizing sutures that are naturally absorbed by subcutaneous tissues, this approach not only reduces the risk of wound infection but also significantly minimizes and improves scar appearance. Furthermore, this technique has been refined through long-term clinical practice, demonstrating simplified operation and enhanced practical utility. According to our data, compared to the IS group, the observation group exhibited significantly improved cosmetic outcomes of the scar, lower postoperative pain scores, reduced scar formation, and accelerated scar quality and postoperative pain relief in primiparous women.

In recent years, the HSTRS technique has been increasingly utilized in high-tension areas, especially in obstetric and gynecologic procedures such as cesarean Sect [6]. This technique was further supported in a recent study, which showed that the use of subcutaneous HSTRS in abdominal incisions after cesarean sections not only significantly reduces scar formation but also enhances aesthetic outcomes, producing precise and cosmetically favorable closure results [6]. However, this technique has not yet been applied to perineal suturing. To our knowledge, this was the first time that the HSTRS technique has been applied and systematically evaluated in primiparous women. This method not only enhances cosmetic outcomes but also contributes to reduced postoperative scar formation, offering a promising alternative to conventional suturing methods in obstetric practice. While optimizing surgical technique is fundamental, adjunctive regenerative strategies may further enhance scar quality and postoperative pain relief. Emerging approaches—such as topical lactic acid formulations and platelet-rich plasma—have shown promise in improving post-episiotomy recovery [24]. These biologically active strategies could potentially complement the HSTRS technique, and their combined application warrants investigation in future studies.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations or clinical settings. Second, the sample size is relatively modest, although the study was adequately powered for the primary outcomes. Third, the follow-up period extended to 6 months, which is sufficient to evaluate early scar outcomes but insufficient to assess long-term scar maturation or chronic pelvic pain. Fouth, we did not include validated instruments for postpartum quality of life, sexual function, or dyspareunia—outcomes that are particularly relevant to scar quality and postoperative pain relief. Fifth, neonatal head circumference was not recorded in this study, although randomized allocation minimized confounding by this factor. Additionally, pelvic floor functional outcomes were not evaluated. Future multicenter studies with larger sample sizes, extended follow-up, and comprehensive patient-reported outcome measures—including sexual function and quality-of-life questionnaires—are warranted to confirm and extend our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that the HSTRS technique offers significant advantages over conventional suturing methods for perineal repair in primiparous women. The technique not only reduces postoperative pain and suture-related complications but also improves cosmetic outcomes by minimizing scar formation. These findings support the adoption of HSTRS as a safe and effective option for perineal wound management. Further multicenter studies are warranted to confirm its long-term benefits and effects on pelvic floor function.
