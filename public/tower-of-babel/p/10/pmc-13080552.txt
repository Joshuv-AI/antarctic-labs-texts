Comparative Strategies to Overcome Diuretic Resistance in Heart Failure
Authors: Ivna GCV Lima, Jairo T Nunes, Adriana C Luk, Darshan H Brahmbhatt, Lucas C Godoy, Michael McDonald, Filio Billia, Edimar A Bocchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiuretic resistance complicates decongestion in heart failure (HF). Multiple add-on strategies are used, yet comparative efficacy and safety remain uncertain.ObjectivesThe objective of the study was to compare pharmacologic strategies for diuretic resistance in HF using a random-effects network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs).MethodsWe searched PubMed/Embase (January 2000–June 2024) for adult HF RCTs with diuretic resistance. Primary outcomes were 72-h urine output and standardized 72-h weight; secondary outcomes were heart-failure hospitalization and cardiovascular mortality; safety was acute kidney injury (AKI). A random-effects network meta-analysis pooled mean differences (MDs) for urine, standardized MDs (SMDs) for weight, and ORs for binary outcomes. Mortality was also assessed by follow-up–adjusted meta-regression of incidence rate ratios.ResultsNineteen RCTs (n = 9,685) were included. Compared with low-dose loop diuretics, 72-hour urine output increased with tolvaptan (MD: 1,840 mL; 95% CI: 441-3,240) and low-dose loop plus tolvaptan (MD: 1,643 mL; 95% CI: 53-3,234). For 72-hour weight change, hydrochlorothiazide (SMD: 1.33; 95% CI: 0.49-2.18) and tolvaptan (SMD: 0.69; 95% CI: 0.09-1.29) produced the greatest standardized reductions. Dapagliflozin (OR: 0.15; 95% CI: 0.03-0.73) and empagliflozin (OR: 0.19; 95% CI: 0.04-0.94) reduced heart failure hospitalizations. Follow-up–adjusted mortality, expressed as incidence rate ratios, was neutral to favorable but imprecise and consistent in ≤60-day sensitivity analyses. High-dose loop diuretics (OR: 2.27; 95% CI: 1.20-4.30) and hydrochlorothiazide (OR: 4.14; 95% CI: 2.03-8.42) were associated with increased acute kidney injury.ConclusionsIn HF with diuretic resistance, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors may lower rehospitalization with a favorable renal profile, whereas nephron-segment add-ons enhance short-term decongestion but warrant AKI/electrolyte monitoring; rankings are exploratory, and choices should emphasize absolute effects and 95% CIs.Key words: acute heart failure, diuretic resistance, network meta-analysis

Congestion is the most common clinical presentation in patients with acute heart failure (HF) (AHF). Residual congestion is associated with cardiovascular mortality, HF hospitalization (HFH), and ongoing symptoms. Despite the escalating dose of loop diuretic agents, those who do not achieve adequate decongestion are diagnosed with diuretic resistance. When adequate loop-diuretic therapy fails to relieve congestion, clinicians typically escalate by combining agents with complementary renal tubular targets and, when appropriate, introduce adjuncts. Recently, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in the diuretic resistance setting, such as ADVOR (Acetazolamide in Decompensated Heart Failure with Volume OveRload)1 with acetazolamide and CLOROTIC (Combining loop with thiazide diuretic agents for decompensated heart failure)2 with thiazides have shown that the combination of diuretic agents improves decongestion but is not associated with reductions in mortality and HFH. A combination of diuretic agents may also cause acute kidney injury (AKI) and electrolyte abnormalities.

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) represent a class of medications that have demonstrated significant benefits in chronic HF management. Beyond improving clinical outcomes, their potential role in addressing diuretic resistance has garnered considerable interest. In the DAPA-RESIST trial (Dapagliflozin vs Metolazone in Heart Failure Resistant to Loop Diuretics),3 dapagliflozin showed similar efficacy to metolazone in relieving congestion, with a notable reduction in adverse events, highlighting its potential as a safer alternative. The existing uncertainty surrounding the optimal diuretic combinations for enhanced clinical outcomes highlights a significant knowledge gap. Identifying the most effective diuretic association that maximizes clinical benefits while preserving diuretic efficacy remains a pivotal question. To address this gap, we performed a network meta-analysis to explore the efficacy and safety of various diuretic therapeutic strategies compared to the use of furosemide alone in treating patients with diuretic-resistant AHF.

Methods

This systematic review and network meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Collaboration Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines. This study is a systematic review and network meta-analysis of published randomized trials and did not involve collection of new individual-level data; as such, formal ethics approval was not required.

Data source and search strategy

We systematically searched PubMed (Medline) and EMBASE for RCTs published between January 2000 and June 2024. Two authors (I.G.C.V.L and J.T.N.) independently performed the review, resolving disagreements through panel discussions with the senior author (E.A.B.). The search strategy used medical subject headings, accessible text terms, and Boolean operators. The complete search strategies are provided in the Supplemental Material.

Titles and abstracts were screened, followed by full-text evaluations for potentially eligible studies. All records from the initial search were imported into Zotero for reference management. A third reviewer resolved any discrepancies during the selection process through discussion or mediation. We did not prospectively register this review. A date-stamped protocol detailing objectives, eligibility criteria, outcomes, and planned analyses has been provided in the supplement (Supplemental Section S9). Deviations from this protocol are explicitly described and justified.

Eligibility criteria

We included studies that enrolled patients aged ≥18 years with diuretic resistance, as defined by the individual study criteria. Eligible studies were RCTs comparing HF medications to placebo or control groups. Trials had to be published between January 2000 and June 2024 to be included, because we want to study contemporary data. We included RCTs of pharmacologic strategies intended to influence decongestion (e.g., SGLT2i, thiazides, acetazolamide, vasopressin antagonists, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists) to reflect real-world escalation pathways in diuretic resistance. Clinical setting (hospitalized AHF vs ambulatory) was treated as a prespecified effect modifier and assessed in subgroup/sensitivity analyses.

Interventions and node definitions

To preserve clinical interpretability and the transitivity assumption, we modeled non–loop-diuretic agents as add-on strategies to loop diuretic agents vs loop diuretic alone (reference: low-dose furosemide), unless a trial compared 2 loop strategies directly (eg, bolus vs continuous infusion, low-vs high-dose, torsemide vs furosemide). Accordingly, nonloop agents (eg, acetazolamide, hydrochlorothiazide, metolazone, tolvaptan, SGLT2i, eplerenone, hypertonic saline, and dopamine) are modeled as add-ons rather than monotherapies.

Outcomes

Primary efficacy outcomes were 72-hour urine output (mL) and standardized 72-hour weight change. The secondary efficacy endpoint was cardiovascular mortality and HFH within the longest available follow-up per trial. The safety endpoint was AKI. AKI definitions were taken as reported in each trial; when definitions differed, we harmonized to a conservative operational threshold of an increase in serum creatinine ≥0.3 mg/dL within 72 hours.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

RCT quality assessment was conducted using Cochrane’s Risk of Bias 2 tool for randomized studies.

Subgroup analysis and sensitivity analysis

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity and better understand treatment effects across different patient populations and clinical contexts, prespecified subgroup analyses were performed. The subgroup analyses were stratified based on 3 factors: diuretic type, furosemide dose, and patient setting (hospitalized or outpatient). These categories were chosen based on their clinical relevance and prior evidence suggesting variability in treatment efficacy and safety across these dimensions. 1) Stratification by diuretic type: subgroup comparisons were conducted for individual diuretic classes, including loop diuretic agents (eg, high-dose and low-dose furosemide), thiazides (eg, hydrochlorothiazide, metolazone), SGLT2i (eg, empagliflozin, dapagliflozin), and combination therapies (eg, furosemide with dopamine or hypertonic saline). This analysis allowed us to identify the relative efficacy and safety profiles of each diuretic type within the network. 2) Stratification by furosemide dose: furosemide doses were categorized into low dose (≤80 mg/day) and high dose (>80 mg/day). Oral doses were converted using a 2:1 oral: intravenous potency ratio, and the daily dose refers to the total 24-hour dose administered during the first 72 hours postrandomization. Treatment effects for these subgroups were analyzed separately to assess dose-response relationships, particularly for outcomes such as urine output, weight loss, and AKI. 3) Stratification by patient setting: the trials were further categorized based on whether they were conducted in a hospitalized or an outpatient setting. Hospitalized patients often represent a population with more severe HF and fluid overload requiring immediate decongestion, whereas ambulatory patients typically have more stable conditions with a focus on long-term outcomes.

Because the follow-up duration varied across trials, we complemented the binary synthesis with a rate-based meta-regression of mortality. For each arm we computed incidence rates as deaths divided by person-time; when person-time was unavailable, we approximated exposure as randomized sample size multiplied by the arm-specific median (or mean) follow-up. Study-level incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were formed as treatment-to-control incidence rates and pooled using a random-effects meta-regression on log(IRR) with restricted maximum likelihood. The covariate was average follow-up duration (days). Influence diagnostics (leave-one-out), a bubble plot of weights vs follow-up, and a sensitivity analysis restricted to trials with follow-up ≤60 days were prespecified.

Statistical analysis

We performed a random-effects network meta-analysis using the netmeta package (R version 4.3.1). For continuous outcomes (72-hour urine output in mL and standardized 72-hour weight change), we pooled mean differences (MDs) and standardized MDs (SMDs), respectively, with 95% CIs using inverse-variance weighting. For binary outcomes (AKI, HF hospitalization, and cardiovascular mortality), we pooled ORs with 95% CIs (Mantel-Haenszel). We chose the SMD to report 72-hour weight change because trials varied in their units of measurement, baseline weight distributions, and statistical scales. The SMD approach allows for comparability across studies by accounting for differences in variability, enabling the integration of heterogeneous data into a unified effect size. Network inconsistency was assessed with a design-by-treatment interaction model; heterogeneity was summarized by τ2 and I2 >50 indicating high heterogeneity. P scores (0-1) rank treatments by the mean probability of being better than others across the network, even when pairwise differences are not statistically significant; higher P scores indicate a better treatment probability. Multiarm trials were modeled, preserving within-study correlations. Continuity corrections were applied for sparse binary outcomes where needed.

To ensure a robust network, all treatments were directly or indirectly connected for comparative analysis. Reporting bias was formally assessed using the Egger test, which evaluates funnel plot symmetry. Nonsignificant findings were interpreted as a low risk of reporting bias. Transitivity (an assumption enabling indirect comparisons) was assessed and deemed appropriate following published guidelines. Clinically relevant effect sizes, such as an OR of 2.0, were used to assess imprecision by comparing the 95% CIs of treatment effects with this range.

To harmonize heterogeneous follow-up across trials, we analyzed all-cause/cardiovascular mortality as an IRR using person-time at risk. For each study arm, we abstracted the number of deaths and total exposure time. When person-time was reported, we used it directly; otherwise, we approximated exposure as N × median (or mean) follow-up. When only a follow-up window was given, we used the midpoint. We then computed study-specific log(IRR) and corresponding standard errors and synthesized effects with a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood). Heterogeneity was summarized by τ2 and I2, and 95% CIs were derived via the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman small-sample adjustment. Trials with zero events in 1 arm were handled with a continuity correction of 0.5; studies with zero events in both arms contributed to the meta-regression (through the rate formulation) but not to the arm-based IRR if no person-time contrast could be formed.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic search yielded 902 articles. Following review of abstracts and removal of duplicates, 79 studies were selected for full-text assessment. Nineteen RCTs met the inclusion criteria (Supplemental Figure 1, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses diagram), encompassing a total of 9,685 patients with diuretic resistance across diverse clinical contexts. Of these, 14 trials enrolled hospitalized patients with AHF, whereas 5 trials included ambulatory patients with chronic decompensated HF. The mean age across studies ranged from 55 to 74 years, with a balanced distribution of males and females in most trials. Most patients presented with NYHA functional class III or IV HF, reflecting significant symptomatic disease. Regarding left ventricular ejection fraction, 9 trials specifically enrolled patients with reduced ejection fraction, none were restricted to preserved ejection fraction, and 10 included mixed populations with both phenotypes. Baseline renal function varied widely, with estimated glomerular filtration rates (eGFRs) between 25 and 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (Table 1, Supplemental Table 1).

Outcomes

Urine output

Across 9,685 patients, all comparisons were made against low-dose furosemide. In the analysis of 72-hour urine output, add-on tolvaptan produced the largest and statistically significant increase compared with low-dose loop diuretics (MD: 1,840 mL; 95% CI: 441-3,240), and low-dose loop plus tolvaptan was likewise favorable (MD: 1,643 mL; 95% CI: 53-3,234). Directionally positive but imprecise estimates were observed for hypertonic saline 1.4% (MD: 1,326 mL; 95% CI: −431 to 3,083), dapagliflozin (MD: 1,215 mL; 95% CI: −224 to 2,655), continuous loop (MD: 730 mL; 95% CI: −1,905 to 3,364), empagliflozin (MD: 612 mL; 95% CI: −442 to 1,666), hydrochlorothiazide (MD: 609 mL; 95% CI: −636 to 1,854), acetazolamide (MD: 500 mL; 95% CI: −916 to 1,916), low-dose loop + dopamine (MD: 454 mL; 95% CI: −917 to 1,824), furosemide bolus (MD: 425 mL; 95% CI: −2,338 to 3,188), loop + metolazone (MD: 376 mL; 95% CI: −1,652 to +2,404), and high-dose loop (MD: 302 mL; 95% CI: −756 to 1,359). Bumetanide + loop showed a large but imprecise effect (MD: 1,991 mL; 95% CI: −1,185 to 5,166). These estimates are consistent with the primary network findings while emphasizing absolute diuretic gains and their uncertainty (Figure 1A, Supplemental Appendix Section 2).

Weight loss

Standardized weight change (72 h). Across included trials, all comparisons were against low-dose furosemide and effect sizes S reported as SMDs. High-dose furosemide showed SMD 0.46 (95% CI: −0.45 to 1.37; P score 0.63) and empagliflozin 0.59 (95% CI: 0.11-1.29; P score 0.56). In addition, tolvaptan demonstrated 0.69 (95% CI: 0.09-1.29; P score 0.51), whereas dapagliflozin yielded 0.80 (95% CI: −0.26 to 1.86; P score 0.47). Moreover, furosemide + dopamine was essentially neutral (0.03; 95% CI: −0.96 to 1.01; P score 0.84), whereas hydrochlorothiazide showed the largest standardized effect (1.33; 95% CI: 0.49-2.18; P score 0.20). Similarly, tolvaptan + low-dose furosemide produced 0.85 (95% CI: −0.06 to 1.75; P score 0.43), metolazone (no direct comparisons) 1.11 (95% CI: −0.11 to 2.34; P score 0.32), and hypertonic saline 1.4% 1.96 (95% CI: −0.16 to 4.08; P score 0.14). Taken together, hydrochlorothiazide and tolvaptan achieved statistically significantly greater 72-hour weight loss, whereas most other estimates were imprecise with wide CIs. Although hydrochlorothiazide and tolvaptan achieved statistically significant standardized weight reductions (95% CI excluding 0), P scores reflect the entire network (precision/heterogeneity across all contrasts) and can be lower despite significant pairwise effects (Figure 1B and Appendix Section 3).

Cardiovascular mortality

Empagliflozin, evaluated in a single comparison, demonstrated an OR 0.31 (95% CI: 0.07-1.25; P score = 0.91). Eplerenone showed an OR 0.63 (95% CI: 0.24-1.66; P score = 0.74). The combination of low-dose furosemide and dopamine had an OR 0.59 (95% CI: 0.11-3.02; P score = 0.70). Bolus furosemide resulted in an OR 0.90 (95% CI: 0.58-1.39; P score = 0.60). High-dose furosemide, assessed in 2 direct comparisons, showed an OR 1.00 (95% CI: 0.64-1.54; P score = 0.49). Torsemide displayed an OR 0.99 (95% CI: 0.62-1.58; P score = 0.50). Tolvaptan presented an OR 0.98 (95% CI: 0.85-1.12; P score = 0.52).

Dapagliflozin, based on a single direct comparison, demonstrated an OR 1.43 (95% CI: 0.23-9.00; P score = 0.35). Continuous furosemide administration showed an OR 1.11 (95% CI: 0.72-1.71; P score = 0.37). Metolazone, lacking direct comparisons, had an OR 1.38 (95% CI: 0.11-16.71; P score = 0.39). Acetazolamide, based on 1 comparison, showed an OR 1.32 (95% CI: 0.80-2.19; P score = 0.26). Hydrochlorothiazide, with an OR 1.66 (95% CI: 0.62-4.45; P score = 0.21), had the lowest P score among the evaluated treatments. Lower P scores indicate a lower probability of being the best choice across the network; this does not contradict individual nonsignificant ORs but contextualizes the treatment’s relative position (Supplemental Appendix Section 4).

We synthesized cardiovascular mortality using a follow-up–adjusted, rate-based random-effects model and report IRRs vs low-dose loop diuretic agents. Pooled effects were neutral to favorable but imprecise across nodes: empagliflozin IRR 0.38 (95% CI: 0.10-1.41), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists IRR 0.64 (95% CI: 0.25-1.64), loop low dose + dopamine IRR 0.64 (95% CI: 0.13-3.17), loop + hydrochlorothiazide IRR 0.83 (95% CI: 0.45-1.52), furosemide bolus IRR 0.96 (95% CI :0.68-1.34), loop + tolvaptan IRR 0.99 (95% CI: 0.88-1.11), torsemide IRR 1.03 (95% CI: 0.71-1.49), loop high dose IRR 1.03 (95% CI: 0.74-1.45), loop IRR 1.08 (95% CI: 0.77-1.50), loop + acetazolamide IRR 1.26 (95% CI: 0.79-2.02), dapagliflozin IRR 1.50 (95% CI: 0.25-8.98), and loop + metolazone IRR 2.10 (95% CI: 0.25-17.59). The bubble plot (log[IRR] vs mean follow-up) showed a shallow slope and larger, more precise bubbles clustering near no effect (IRR≈1), indicating that accounting for person-time did not materially change the direction or relative ordering of treatment effects compared with the binary analysis (Supplemental Appendix Section 8).

Heart failure hospitalization

Dapagliflozin demonstrated significant efficacy, with an OR 0.15 (95% CI: 0.03-0.73; P score = 0.94), followed closely by empagliflozin, evaluated through 2 direct comparisons, with an OR 0.19 (95% CI: 0.04-0.94; P score = 0.90). Metolazone, despite lacking direct comparisons, showed an OR 0.26 (95% CI: 0.03-2.33; P score = 0.79). Torsemide had an OR 0.85 (95% CI: 0.52-1.38; P score = 0.60), whereas continuous furosemide infusion demonstrated an OR 0.94 (95% CI: 0.58-1.53; P score = 0.48). High-dose furosemide, assessed in 2 direct comparisons, yielded an OR 0.96 (95% CI: 0.60-1.53; P score = 0.44).

Tolvaptan presented an OR 1.03 (95% CI: 0.91-1.17; P score = 0.37), comparable to acetazolamide, which had an OR 0.96 (95% CI: 0.61-1.51; P score = 0.46). Furosemide bolus treatment resulted in an OR 1.01 (95% CI: 0.63-1.64; P score = 0.40). Combination therapy with low-dose furosemide and dopamine yielded an OR 1.18 (95% CI: 0.50-2.76; P score = 0.30), whereas eplerenone showed an OR 1.16 (95% CI: 0.65-2.09; P score = 0.29). Lastly, hydrochlorothiazide, with the lowest P score of 0.11, demonstrated an OR 1.58 (95% CI: 0.82-3.05; P score = 0.11) (Figure 2A, Supplemental Appendix Section 5).

Safety outcome for AKI

Empagliflozin, evaluated in 2 direct comparisons, demonstrated an OR 0.74 (95% CI: 0.32-1.71; P score = 0.87). Furosemide combined with dopamine, assessed in 1 direct comparison, had OR 1.03 (95% CI: 0.32-3.30; P score = 0.73). Tolvaptan, examined in 1 direct comparison, had an OR of 1.16 (95% CI: 0.71-1.89; P score = 0.69), followed by eplerenone, which had an OR of 1.14 (95% CI: 0.41-3.15; P score = 0.69). Metolazone, not involved in direct comparisons, showed an OR 1.10 (95% CI: 0.18-6.59; P score = 0.68).

Furosemide (continuous), examined in 1 direct comparison, demonstrated an OR 1.45 (95% CI: 0.73-2.86; P score = 0.59). Dapagliflozin and furosemide bolus, each assessed in 1 direct comparison, yielded OR 1.86 (95% CI: 0.50-6.95; P score = 0.49) and OR 1.75 (95% CI: 0.88-3.46; P score = 0.49). Furosemide (high dose), included in 2 direct comparisons, showed an OR 2.27 (95% CI: 1.20-4.30; P score = 0.37). Acetazolamide, assessed in 1 direct comparison, had an OR 4.10 (95% CI: 0.44-38.41; P score = 0.31).

Hydrochlorothiazide, included in 1 direct comparison, demonstrated an OR 4.14 (95% CI: 2.03-8.42; P score = 0.22). Bumetanide, not involved in direct comparisons, had the highest OR 23.62 (95% CI: 7.48-74.58; P score = 0.02). (Figure 2B, Supplemental Appendix Section 6).

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

The subgroup analysis revealed significant variability in treatment efficacy based on diuretic type, furosemide dose, and patient setting (hospitalized or ambulatory). By diuretic type, SGLT2i, particularly empagliflozin and dapagliflozin, consistently demonstrated the highest efficacy in reducing AHF hospitalization (OR: 0.19; 95% CI: 0.04-0.94 and OR: 0.15; 95% CI: 0.03-0.73, respectively) with an improvement of urine output (MD: 363 mL; 95% CI: −524 to 1,252 mL). Combination therapies, such as low-dose furosemide with hydrochlorothiazide or hypertonic saline, were associated with superior short-term weight loss and fluid removal but showed wide CIs (eg, hypertonic saline: MD: 1,330 mL; 95% CI: −502 to 3,162 mL), reflecting heterogeneity in study populations.

Stratifying by intravenous-equivalent loop diuretic dose (≤80 vs >80 mg/day during the first 72 hours), the higher-dose stratum showed greater 72-hour urine output overall. In a subgroup analysis, the interaction by dose was significant for urine output (Pint = 0.001).

In analyses restricted to hospitalized AHF, estimates for 72-hour urine output was directionally consistent with the main analysis but remained imprecise. There was a statistically significant interaction by setting for urine output (Pint = 0.001), indicating that treatment effects differed between hospitalized and ambulatory trials. Several treatments were not represented among ambulatory trials, resulting in network disconnections; therefore, subgroup findings should be considered exploratory. Full statistics are provided in Supplemental Appendix Section 8.

(3) Modeling choices: alternative variance estimators (Hartung-Knapp for pairwise, restricted maximum likelihood vs DerSimonian-Laird in exploratory runs) and continuity-correction checks produced negligible differences in direction and significance. (4) Network diagnostics: global and local inconsistency tests and comparison-adjusted funnel plots did not reveal contradictions large enough to overturn conclusions; small-study effects were not decisive.

Risk of bias and publication bias

The Cochrane Collaboration tool was used for quality assessment. There were no studies with high overall bias, but 4 with some concerns, most of them in the first and second domains. There was no evidence of funnel plot asymmetry or systematic reporting bias for the primary (Egger test, P = 0.51) (Supplemental Appendix Sections 1 and 7).

Discussion

Our synthesis shows a consistent benefit-risk gradient. (1) For clinical events, SGLT2i reduced HHF and were the only therapies with statistically significant signals, whereas conventional intensification strategies were neutral. (2) For decongestion surrogates, the adjusted urine output (UO) analysis favored tolvaptan with directionally positive but imprecise effects for hypertonic saline, SGLT2i. For weight, hydrochlorothiazide yielded the largest standardized reduction and tolvaptan was also favorable. (3) In contrast, AKI risk increased with intensification: high-dose loop and hydrochlorothiazide were most concerning, whereas empagliflozin showed a favorable/neutral profile. (4) Cardiovascular mortality, analyzed as IRRs adjusted for follow-up, was neutral to favorable but imprecise across nodes with a shallow follow-up slope and consistent ≤60-day sensitivity.

Our results are consistent with contemporary RCTs evaluating adjunctive decongestive strategies. Acetazolamide (ADVOR) improved decongestion in hospitalized AHF without clear reductions in events.1 Thiazide augmentation (CLOROTIC) enhanced weight loss but did not translate into early clinical outcome benefits.2 Selective vasopressin antagonism (EVEREST [Efficacy of Vasopressin Antagonism in Heart Failure Outcome Study with Tolvaptan]) improved short-term fluid metrics but had neutral mortality effects overall.13 In parallel, SGLT2i continue to demonstrate benefits across HF settings. In EMPA-RESPONSE-AHF (Empagliflozin in Acute Heart Failure), early empagliflozin improved surrogate clinical measures in hospitalized patients,11 and EMPAG-HF (Empagliflozin in Heart Failure) showed favorable effects on diuresis and renal function trajectories.12 In patients with loop diuretic resistance, DAPA-RESIST suggested comparable decongestion to metolazone with fewer adverse events,3 whereas DAPA-RESPONSE-AHF (Dapagliflozin in Acute Heart Failure) reported clinically meaningful signals in acute settings.7 Taken together, these trial-level observations align with our network’s pattern: SGLT2i reduce HF hospitalization and exhibit a favorable safety signal, whereas segmental nephron blockade (eg, thiazides, acetazolamide) primarily accelerates short-term fluid removal. Although hypertonic saline20 augments short-term natriuresis, its long-term role (particularly alongside oral loop diuretic agents and sodium repletion) remains uncertain and warrants dedicated prospective evaluation, as suggested by prior reports.2,3,1,7,11, 12, 13

Taken together, “more diuretic” is not necessarily “better”: strategies that push natriuresis (higher loop dose, thiazide stacking, and proximal blockade) do not reduce HHF and raise AKI risk, despite improving UO or weight in some settings. A pragmatic approach is to prioritize agents that move clinical outcomes and are kidney-safe/neutral: start with an SGLT2i (dapagliflozin or empagliflozin) early during admission unless contraindicated; both reduce HHF and do not show excess AKI. When rapid aquaresis is needed (marked hyponatremia or water-predominant congestion), add tolvaptan (it delivers the largest UO gains with neutral HHF and no AKI penalty). Consider hypertonic saline selectively as an adjunct. Use thiazide/thiazide-like or acetazolamide only for refractory cases, with tight creatinine/electrolyte monitoring and rapid de-escalation once euvolemia is achieved, given the AKI signal (notably with hydrochlorothiazide) despite favorable weight change. Avoid reflex high-dose loop unless clearly indicated; the AKI trade-off outweighs modest and imprecise gains in UO. Rather than suggesting equivalence, our network meta-analysis aims to provide comparative evidence to inform therapeutic escalation under conditions of diuretic resistance, recognizing that clinical context should guide individualized treatment selection.

This integrated pattern, SGLT2i improving clinical outcomes with favorable renal safety, tolvaptan excelling on early decongestion with neutral events, and intensification raising AKI without lowering HHF, is concordant with the broader acute-HF literature (eg, vasopressin antagonism improving fluid metrics but neutral on mortality; carbonic anhydrase/thiazide augmentation boosting decongestion; early SGLT2i associated with better clinical trajectories). Our contribution is to quantify the trade-offs on a single canvas, reporting absolute UO/weight effects, event-level ORs, and follow-up–adjusted IRRs while keeping node definitions as add-on to loop. This clarifies a decision-oriented default: start with SGLT2i, add tolvaptan when aquaresis is prioritized, and reserve further intensification for refractory patients under close renal surveillance. In short, when decongestion and kidney safety conflict, choose the option that nudges outcomes without inviting AKI. Treatment rankings (P scores) are de-emphasized and should be interpreted alongside absolute effect sizes and CIs. We focus on clinical magnitude and precision rather than hierarchy alone.

Study Limitations

This study provides important insights into the comparative efficacy and safety of various diuretic therapies in patients with diuretic resistance; however, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, we explicitly considered transitivity by examining potential effect modifiers, including clinical setting (hospitalized vs ambulatory), baseline renal function, and diuretic resistance definitions. The inclusion of a minority of ambulatory trials (eg, RECEDE-CHF [Renal Effects of Dapagliflozin in Chronic Heart Failure],9 TORNADO [Torsemide Comparison With Furosemide for Management of Heart Failure]10) may introduce clinical heterogeneity relative to hospitalized AHF cohorts where short-term decongestion endpoints dominate. To address this, we emphasize results from hospital-based nodes when interpreting 72-hour outcomes and recommend sensitivity analyses that exclude ambulatory trials for these endpoints. We also highlight that diuretic resistance was variably defined across trials (eg, prior diuretic exposure, urine output thresholds, clinician-determined congestion), which likely contributed to heterogeneity and wide CIs. These limitations temper certainty in cross-node comparisons despite acceptable risk-of-bias profiles. Although subgroup and sensitivity analyses were used to explore these factors, residual confounding and effect modification cannot be excluded and may limit the generalizability of some findings.

Second, some treatment effects were derived from indirect comparisons through the network meta-analysis framework. Interventions like tolvaptan and hypertonic saline lacked direct head-to-head comparisons, which could introduce additional uncertainty. Although transitivity was assessed and deemed acceptable, the lack of direct evidence for certain treatments underscores the need for cautious interpretation. Although P scores provide a useful summary of relative treatment performance, they should not be interpreted in isolation. Given the degree of clinical heterogeneity and reliance on indirect comparisons in some treatment nodes, we caution against overinterpreting the rank order of therapies.

Third, the endpoints analyzed included surrogate outcomes such as urine output and standardized weight loss, which, although clinically relevant, may not fully capture long-term patient-centered outcomes like mortality, quality of life, and HFH. In addition, variations in the measurement and reporting of outcomes across trials may have contributed to the inconsistency in the results.

Finally, a major limitation is heterogeneity in trial-level definitions of diuretic resistance (eg, prior loop exposure, output thresholds, clinician-determined congestion). We therefore adopted a harmonized operational definition and present a trial-by-trial mapping in Supplements.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the management of diuretic resistance and highlights key areas for future research, such as the need for large-scale, head-to-head trials and the evaluation of long-term patient-centered outcomes.

Conclusions

In patients with AHF and diuretic resistance, our network meta-analysis suggests that SGLT2i may be associated with a reduced risk of HFH and a potentially favorable renal safety profile. Add-on decongestive strategies, including thiazides, acetazolamide, tolvaptan, and hypertonic saline, were associated with greater short-term urine output and weight loss; however, most estimates were imprecise and carried an increased risk of AKI. These findings support a tailored approach to diuretic resistance, balancing short-term decongestion needs against safety concerns. Given the predominance of indirect comparisons and heterogeneity across trials, these results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and warrant confirmation in prospective, head-to-head trials.

Clinical implications

Among patients with AHF who exhibit suboptimal response to loop diuretic agents, nephron-segment–targeted therapies (such as thiazides, acetazolamide, tolvaptan, or hypertonic saline) may facilitate short-term decongestion, but require close monitoring for electrolyte disturbances and renal function decline. SGLT2i have shown potential to reduce HFHs, with a favorable renal safety signal in selected trials. These agents may be considered early in the treatment pathway, particularly in ambulatory or hemodynamically stable patients. However, the current evidence base is limited by wide CIs and indirect comparisons, underscoring the need for adequately powered, direct comparative trials to better define their role in managing diuretic resistance.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Diuretic resistance is a common and challenging clinical problem in AHF. This network meta-analysis synthesizes current evidence from randomized trials to compare the efficacy and safety of multiple pharmacologic strategies, including SGLT2i and nephron-segment–targeted therapies. SGLT2i may offer a favorable balance between decongestive effect and renal safety, whereas more aggressive decongestive agents can augment fluid removal at the cost of an increased AKI risk.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Given the heterogeneity in patient populations and outcome definitions, prospective head-to-head randomized trials are needed to confirm the optimal sequence and combination of diuretic therapies in resistant HF. Future studies should prioritize patient-centered outcomes such as long-term renal function, hospitalization rates, and quality of life, while also exploring the mechanistic role of SGLT2i in modulating diuretic response.
