Body image victimization experiences and appearance anxiety in cosmetic surgery: the serial mediating roles of body image shame and body compassion
Authors: Sajad Khanjani, Aliakbar Foroughi, Shima Rahimi, Mahboobeh Soleymani Moghadam, Fatemeh Faraji, Moslem Rajabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe growing popularity of cosmetic surgery over the past two decades reflects increasing concern with physical appearance. However, many individuals do not experience the expected psychological benefits following cosmetic procedures. body image victimization experiences, particularly those involving peers or caregivers, may play a critical role in shaping body-related shame and appearance anxiety later in life.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between body image victimization experiences and appearance anxiety among individuals seeking cosmetic surgery, with a focus on the serial mediating roles of body image shame and body compassion.MethodsA total of 409 patients attending cosmetic surgery clinics in Tehran were recruited through convenience sampling. Participants completed standardized self-report measures assessing appearance anxiety, body image victimization experiences, body image shame, and body compassion. Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24. Simple and serial mediation models were tested using bootstrapping procedures, with age and gender entered simultaneously as covariates in all analyses.ResultsBody image victimization experiences were positively associated with appearance anxiety. Body image shame was positively associated with appearance anxiety, whereas body compassion was negatively associated with appearance anxiety. Mediation analyses indicated that body image shame and body compassion were statistically associated with the relationship between body image victimization experiences and appearance anxiety within the tested models. These associations remained significant after controlling for age and gender.ConclusionsThe findings are consistent with theoretical models suggesting that body image shame and body compassion may be relevant psychological correlates of appearance anxiety among individuals seeking cosmetic procedures. However, given the cross-sectional design, causal inferences cannot be drawn. Longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to clarify the temporal ordering of these variables and to determine their potential relevance for future interventions.

Introduction

Cosmetic treatments and surgeries have increased substantially over the past decade. Global statistics indicate that Iran has one of the highest rates of cosmetic procedures relative to population size. In 2013, Iran was ranked first worldwide, with more than 175,000 surgical and non-surgical cosmetic procedures performed [1]. Appearance anxiety is commonly observed among individuals seeking cosmetic procedures and represents a subclinical manifestation of body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) [2]. Epidemiological studies estimate the point prevalence of BDD in the general population to be approximately 2.2% to 2.4% [2, 3]. However, prevalence rates are substantially higher among individuals seeking cosmetic surgery or dermatological treatment, ranging from 6.7% to 33% [2, 4–7]. These findings suggest that appearance anxiety is a clinically relevant concern in cosmetic surgery settings and underscore the importance of identifying psychological factors associated with its development and maintenance.

The literature review shows various developmental, psychological, social, and cultural factors can affect appearance anxiety. Disrupted developmental experiences often underlie negative body image [8–10]. Research shows that these experiences can be recorded in autobiographical memory as the center of identity and life story [11]. This part of the memory affects attention and emotional and cognitive processing [12–14]. Based on previous research, adverse educational experiences and lack of warmth and security in early attachment experiences activate defensive processes and behaviors (such as shame, dominance, and self-criticism) [15–17]. These threatening interpersonal experiences play an essential role in creating a sense of shame in such a way that a person sees themself in the eyes of others as socially weak, inferior, or incomplete [11, 18].

Body image shame involves negative perceptions of the body, leading to defensive behaviors like avoidance or concealment [19, 20]. body image shame stems from internal or external sources. Internal body image shame arises in response to negative self-evaluations of physical appearance. External body image shame arises from the anticipation of social evaluation and rejection of one’s appearance [19, 20]. Shame-related feelings may be rooted in early negative shame experiences, such as being criticized by parents, bullied by peers, sexually or physically abused, or displaying negative attributes of self to others [18, 21, 22]. External shame predicts concern about appearance deformity in college students [23]. The construct of body image shame appears to be more related to eating and body image-related problems than the general concept of shame [24, 25]. Research shows that body image shame links early experiences to eating and body image-related issues [26, 27].

Unlike body image shame, which is a risk factor for body image-related problems, compassion can be an antidote for these problems [23, 24, 28–32]. Self-compassion is defined as the ability to perceive one’s pain in a non-judgmental way and understand one’s suffering as part of common humanity [33]. Empirical evidence shows that abilities based on self-compassion can have a buffering effect against the effect of body dissatisfaction, body shame, and the severity of disordered eating [28, 30, 34, 35]. Altman et al. [36] put forward the multidimensional structure of body compassion, which suggests a new conceptualization of body-related problems and well-being under the approaches of acceptance, compassion, and mindfulness. Body compassion can be described as compassion for the body with kindness and mindfulness [37]. This concept consists of three components:1. Defusion, which means the attitude of mindfulness versus over-identification with the defects, limitations, or inadequacies of one’s body. 2. Common humanity, which means the ability to understand the negative aspects of the body image as a larger part of common humanity instead of adopting an isolated or shameful perspective. 3. Acceptance, which means accepting painful thoughts, perceptions, and feelings related to the body generously instead of adopting a self-judgment attitude [36]. Altman et al. emphasized that a higher level of body compassion is positively associated with body image flexibility and positive affect and negatively associated with disordered eating and higher levels of negative affect [36].A study showed that body compassion moderates the relationship between external and body image shame and eating problems [24]. Another study showed that self-compassion mediates the relationship between external shame and concern about body dysmorphic in college students [23].

A literature review shows that body image–related victimization experiences, such as criticism, teasing, or negative evaluation of physical appearance by peers or caregivers, may play a critical role in the formation of body image shame and then appearance anxiety. Although adverse childhood experiences encompass a broad range of abuse, neglect, and family dysfunction, the present study focuses specifically on body image victimization experiences, which have been shown to be particularly relevant to body image shame and appearance anxiety. On the other hand, body compassion can moderate the effect of these early experiences and the shame caused by them. There is limited research on serial mediation models, particularly in non-Western cultures. Method

Participants

A total of 409 individuals seeking elective cosmetic procedures were recruited from cosmetic surgery clinics in Tehran between May 2020 and February 2021. Participants were assessed during the initial consultation or preoperative evaluation phase, prior to undergoing any cosmetic intervention. The sample primarily included individuals seeking common surgical and non-surgical aesthetic procedures (e.g., facial or body-related cosmetic treatments), while detailed categorization of procedures by type or body area was not systematically documented. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were aged 15 years or older and had completed at least an eighth-grade level of education. The lower age cutoff was set at 15 years because elective cosmetic procedures are extremely uncommon below this age in routine clinical practice in Iran, and cosmetic interventions in younger individuals are typically limited to reconstructive or medically indicated cases, which were excluded from the present study. This age threshold therefore reflects the population seeking elective cosmetic consultation and helps ensure a clinically relevant and homogeneous sample. Exclusion criteria were determined by board-certified cosmetic surgery specialists during the initial clinical assessment. Individuals were excluded if they required reconstructive or medically indicated procedures due to clinically significant appearance defects, such as congenital anomalies (e.g., cleft lip or palate), traumatic injuries, burns, or physical deformities requiring corrective or reconstructive intervention. This criterion was applied to ensure a homogeneous sample consisting exclusively of individuals seeking elective cosmetic procedures, as reconstructive cases may involve different psychological motivations and clinical profiles. All participants voluntarily completed questionnaires on body image shame, body compassion, appearance anxiety, and body image victimization.

Ethical considerations

All participants were informed about the study objectives and procedures and provided written informed consent prior to participation. For participants younger than 18 years of age, written informed consent was obtained from a parent or legal guardian, and assent was obtained from the adolescent participant. In cases where participants were younger than 16 years of age, informed consent to participate was obtained from their parents or legal guardians in accordance with ethical requirements. All consent and assent procedures were conducted in accordance with local regulations and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Iran University of Medical Sciences (IR.IUMS.REC.1398.555).

Measurement tools

Appearance anxiety Inventory (AAI)

The AAI was developed by Vale et al. This questionnaire is a 10-question self-report tool. This questionnaire focuses on the cognitive processes and safety-seeking behaviors of people with BDD. Each question is scored on a scale from 0 (not at all) to 4 (always). A sample item is “I compare aspects of my appearance to others.” The total score of this questionnaire is in the range of 0 to 40. Scores of 19 or higher suggest the possibility of BDD [25]. The AAI was found to have good test-retest reliability and convergent validity in the measurement of appearance anxiety. It was also sensitive to change during treatment [25] In Iran, Cronbach’s alpha and test-retest reliability have been reported as 0.86 and 0.92 [38]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.82.

Body Compassion Scale (BCS)

The BCS was designed by Altman, Linfield, Salmon, and Beacham in 2017. This scale is a 23-question tool that includes a total score and three subscales. These subscales include defusion, human commonality, and acceptance. Questions are scored on a scale from 1 (rarely) to 5 (almost always). A sample item is “am tolerant of my body’s flaws and inadequacies.” Higher scores indicate greater compassion for the body. Ratings are aggregated for each item. The internal consistency scores for the defusion, human commonality, and acceptance subscales were 0.92, 0.91, and 0.87, respectively. Cronbach’s alpha for the 23 items is 0.92, supporting its use as a total score [36]. In a study in Iran, the three-factor structure of the BCS had a good fit. in this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.88.

Body image shame scale (BISS)

The BISS was developed by Duarte, Pinto-Gouveia, Ferreira, and Bautista in 2015. This scale has 14 questions that measure body image shame. BISS has two subscales (external body image shame and internal body image shame). Each question is graded on a 5-point Likert scale (from 0 = never to 4 = almost always) based on how often people experience body shame. A sample item is “I avoid social situations (e.g., going out, parties) because of my physical appearance.” This scale has been validated in non-clinical samples of women in the general population. The scale has shown very good divergent and construct validity, temporal stability (about 0.75 in 4 weeks), and high internal consistency has been reported so that alpha Cronbach’s total score is 0.96 reported [19]. In a study in Iran, the two-factor structure of the BISS had a good fit, and the internal consistency for the total score, internal body image shame, and external body image shame were 0.85, 0.79, and 0.82 [39]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.85.

Body Image Victimization Experiences Scale (BIVES)

Duarte and Pinto-Gouveia designed the BIVES in 2017. This scale measures adolescent and childhood experiences of being bullied and bullied by peers (friends or classmates; BIVES-Peers) or parents (or other significant caregivers; BIVES-Parents) related to physical appearance. The BIVES includes 12 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale, the frequency with which they experienced each described situation (from 1 = never to 5 = very much) and the emotional impact the experience had on them (from 1 = none to 5 = a lot). A sample item is “My mother/father commented aloud my physical appearance with other family members.” The average score of the two subscales ranges from 1 to 5. BIVES validation research has been carried out in a non-clinical sample of women in the general population, based on the results of two subscales, excellent psychometric properties, including construct validity, retest reliability from 0.80 to 0.89, and internal consistency of the composite score of both subscales. They had a reliability scale of 0.95 [40]. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.82.

Data analysis

Mediation analyses were conducted using Preacher and Hayes’ PROCESS macro (version 4.2) [41] for SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics version 24). Specifically, Model 6 was applied to test the hypothesized mediation models, in which body image shame and body compassion were specified as sequential mediators in the association between body image–related victimization experiences and appearance anxiety. Age and gender were entered simultaneously as covariates in all mediation and serial mediation models in order to control for their potential confounding effects. Accordingly, all reported direct and indirect effects reflect associations adjusted for age and gender. Indirect effects were tested using a bootstrapping procedure with 5,000 resamples and bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals. An indirect effect was considered statistically significant when the confidence interval did not include zero. Unstandardized regression coefficients (b), Standardized regression coefficients (B), standard errors (SE), and confidence intervals are reported for all paths, in line with current recommendations for mediation analysis. Pearson correlation coefficients were also computed to examine bivariate associations among the study variables.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the mediating role of body image shame and body compassion in the relationship between adverse experiences related to body image and appearance anxiety in the form of simple and serial mediation models. This study showed that body image shame mediates the relationship between victimization experiences related to body image and appearance anxiety, similar to Gois, Ferreira, and Mendes [26] and Duarte and Pinto-Gouveia’s [27] studies. They showed that body image shame mediates the relationship between early traumatic memories and eating and body image problems. Past research shows that shame is related to early experiences and interactions in childhood and adolescence [11, 18]. Early experiences involving body image and interactions with parents or peers may be related to shame-related memories. The present findings extend Gilbert’s psycho-social-evolutionary model [21] by empirically demonstrating that body image shame functions as a key psychological mechanism linking early appearance-related victimization to current appearance anxiety in individuals seeking cosmetic surgery. Rather than merely supporting the theoretical association between shame and early interpersonal threat, the results suggest that shame specifically directed toward the body may represent a proximal pathway through which early negative appearance-related experiences are translated into ongoing cognitive and behavioral dysregulation. The significant mediating effect of body image shame indicates that early victimization alone may not be sufficient to explain appearance anxiety unless these experiences are internalized as shame-based self-evaluations focused on physical appearance. This interpretation helps clarify why some individuals exposed to early appearance-related criticism develop persistent appearance anxiety, whereas others may not. Individuals who report higher levels of body-related shame also tend to report greater involvement in cognitive and behavioral processes related to body image, as indexed by appearance anxiety. These experiences are often accompanied by expectations that shame-related memories may be activated in social contexts and by behaviors oriented toward managing or reducing feelings of shame.

The results of this research also show that body compassion mediates the relationship between victimization experiences related to body image and appearance anxiety, which is consistent with the results of Barnett and Sharp [42], Ferreira, Pinto-Gouveia, and Duarte [32], Foroughi, Khanjani and Mousavi [23] and Rogers et al. [43]. Body compassion appears to be related to lower anxiety levels in the context of body image concerns. Processes such as self-acceptance, forgiveness, and self-care are often described in relation to a shift from appearance-based evaluations toward a more holistic perspective on the body. According to Gilbert’s theory [44], compassion can be an antidote to an individual’s initial adverse experiences. Importantly, the mediating role of body compassion observed in this study provides empirical support for compassion-based emotion regulation models within the context of appearance anxiety. Consistent with Neff and Germer’s conceptualization of compassion as an adaptive emotion regulation strategy [45], the findings suggest that higher levels of body compassion are associated with lower appearance anxiety even in the presence of early adverse appearance-related experiences. Rather than functioning merely as a general protective trait, body compassion appears to specifically attenuate maladaptive cognitive and behavioral processes related to appearance anxiety, such as avoidance and self-focused attention. The significant indirect effect observed in the mediation model indicates that individuals who are better able to respond to body-related distress with kindness, acceptance, and a sense of common humanity may be less likely to engage in anxiety-driven appearance-related behaviors.

On the other hand, the serial mediation model suggested that body compassion was involved in the association between body image shame related to body image–related victimization experiences and appearance anxiety, that is consistent with the results of Oliveira, Trindade, and Ferreira’s research [24]. Similarly, previous studies have indicated that self-compassion may play a buffering role in the association between body image shame and body image dissatisfaction, potentially attenuating the salience of shame-related experiences [32, 43, 46]. From this perspective, the ways in which individuals relate to their bodies appear to be associated with more compassionate attitudes toward the self, which are linked to lower levels of negative body-related emotions, such as shame. The present study contributes to the literature by examining the associations among body image–related victimization experiences, body image shame, appearance anxiety, and body compassion within an integrated model. The findings suggest that individuals seeking cosmetic surgery tend to report higher levels of body image shame in the context of early appearance-related negative experiences and critical comments about their appearance. At the same time, higher levels of body compassion were associated with a more accepting and kinder stance toward perceived bodily flaws and negative appearance-related experiences. Individuals with greater body compassion were also less likely to perceive themselves as isolated in their experiences, instead viewing physical imperfections as common human characteristics. Similarly, higher body compassion was associated with an ability to acknowledge perceived appearance-related flaws without over-identifying with them, a pattern that was linked to lower self-focused attention and fewer maladaptive behavioral responses.

An additional contextual consideration is the timing of data collection, which occurred between 2020 and 2021 during the COVID-19 pandemic. This period was associated with heightened psychological distress, increased health-related anxiety, and disruptions in access to healthcare services, which may have influenced levels of appearance anxiety and body image concerns. Increased social media exposure, changes in self-presentation due to video-based communication, and pandemic-related stressors may also have amplified appearance-related self-focus. Although these factors were not directly measured, they should be considered when interpreting the findings and may limit the generalizability of the results to non-pandemic contexts. Taken together, these contextual factors highlight the importance of interpreting the present findings within the specific clinical and temporal setting in which the data were collected.

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional correlation design prevents establishing causality. Future research should extend these findings using longitudinal and experimental designs. In particular, randomized controlled trials could examine whether interventions targeting body image shame and body compassion lead to reductions in appearance anxiety among individuals seeking cosmetic surgery. For example, compassion-focused therapy, mindfulness-based interventions, or acceptance- and compassion-based cognitive-behavioral protocols could be adapted to explicitly address shame-based body representations and promote compassionate engagement with body-related distress. Such trials could test body image shame as a treatment mechanism and body compassion as a modifiable protective factor, thereby clarifying their causal roles and clinical utility in reducing appearance anxiety. Second, data collection relied on self-report tools, susceptible to social desirability bias, recall errors, and situational influences. Using behavioral or physiological measures alongside self-reports in future studies is recommended. Third, the sample was restricted to Tehran, limiting generalizability to other regions and cultures. Conducting similar studies in diverse cultural settings is suggested. Fourth, the availability sampling method may not fully represent individuals seeking cosmetic surgery, introducing potential sampling bias. Lastly, not all clinics approached for participation agreed to cooperate, further limiting the representativeness of the sample and potentially introducing additional bias.

Conclusion

This study examined the associations among body image victimization experiences, body image shame, body compassion, and appearance anxiety in individuals seeking cosmetic surgery. The findings indicate that body image shame and body compassion are associated with the relationship between body image victimization experiences and appearance anxiety. Participants reporting higher levels of early body image-related negative experiences also tended to report greater body image shame, lower body compassion, and higher appearance anxiety. Given the cross-sectional design, these associations should be interpreted as relational rather than causal, and the temporal ordering of the constructs cannot be determined. The findings are consistent with theoretical perspectives emphasizing the relevance of shame-based body representations and compassionate self-relating in body image-related distress. Rather than offering definitive clinical recommendations, the results should be viewed as hypothesis-generating. They suggest that early body image -related experiences, body image shame, and body compassion may represent relevant psychological factors to consider in the assessment and conceptualization of body image-related concerns. Longitudinal and experimental studies are needed to clarify temporal relationships and to examine whether targeting these processes is associated with changes in appearance anxiety.
