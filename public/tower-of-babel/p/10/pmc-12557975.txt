Airborne chloride exposure and benign prostatic hyperplasia risk
Authors: JiaHao Shi, Anuchit Phanumartwiwath
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGiven that airborne chloride (Cl⁻), is an understudied component of particulate matter that may drive oxidative and inflammatory pathways, yet its long-term association with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) remains unknown. We aimed to investigate the association between exposure to airborne chloride and the risk of benign prostatic hyperplasia among middle-aged and older Chinese men.ResultsWe utilized nationally representative data from 5,452 males aged 45 years and older in the 2015 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and correlated it with province-level chloride exposure estimates from the China High Air Pollutants (CHAP) dataset. Mixed-effects logistic regression models incorporating province-level random intercepts were employed. Elevated ambient Cl⁻ exposure was consistently linked to heightened risks of BPH in both unadjusted and fully adjusted models. In the comprehensively adjusted model, each 1 µg/m³ increment in Cl⁻ correlated with a 26% rise in the risks of BPH (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.07–1.49; p < 0.05). The robustness and temporal consistency of the findings were confirmed by sensitivity analyses that employed Z-score standardized Cl⁻ and lagged exposures from 2013 to 2015. These results suggest airborne chloride may be a novel environmental risk factor for BPH.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07535-2.Keywords: Airborne chloride, Benign prostatic hyperplasia, Environmental risk factors

Introduction

BPH is a condition that is extremely prevalent among middle-aged and elderly men, with an estimated 94 million cases globally in 2019 among men aged ≥ 40 years—a nearly 84% increase from 2000 [1]. In China, more than half of men aged 70 and above are affected [2]. BPH has been associated to considerable morbidity, including lower urinary tract symptoms, diminished quality of life, and escalating healthcare expenses [3, 4].

Air pollution, a significant global health hazard accountable for over 6.6 million fatalities each year, is extensively documented in cardiovascular and respiratory illness studies; nevertheless, its effects on male urogenital health are little investigated [5, 6]. Recent studies implicate air pollutants in urinary and prostate disorders through oxidative stress and inflammation [7–9]. However most research on conventional pollutants (PM, NOₓ, SOₓ, ozone) is restricted to small, localized samples, such as urban cohorts in South Korea or data from Sichuan [2, 10, 11]. In contrast, airborne chloride (Cl⁻) has received far less attention despite being a key component of atmospheric chemistry and secondary particle formation. Experimental and epidemiological evidence suggests that reactive chlorine species contribute to oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, mechanisms also implicated in BPH pathogenesis. Yet, studies specifically addressing Cl⁻ and male urogenital health are virtually absent [12–15]. This study employs nationally representative data from CHARLS and high-quality air pollutant data from CHAP to investigate the association between airborne chloride exposure and the risk of benign prostatic hyperplasia, or BPH, in middle-aged and older Chinese males. The study seeks to find new environmental risk factors for BPH by integrating high-resolution estimates of an underexamined pollutant and controlling for significant confounders across 28 provinces.

Methods

Study population

Initiated in 2011 using multistage sampling across 28 provinces, the CHARLS is a countrywide study of individuals in China who are 45 years old or older. Every two to three years, there is a follow-up wave. Written informed permission was acquired from all participants, and the study was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015; IRB00001052-11014) [16].

This research employed data from Wave 3 (2015), which included 21,095 participants. We initially refined the dataset to encompass just male individuals aged 45 years or above and excluded individuals with missing values for sex or age. Then participants were excluded if they had missing data on any of the following key variables: body mass index (BMI), residence, marital status, educational level, smoking status, drinking status, self-reported BPH diagnosis, history of stroke, arthritis, psychological conditions, pension status, or province of residence. After applying these criteria, the final analytical sample consisted of 5,452 participants. The sample selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flow chart of participants selection

Assessment of airborne chloride exposure

Ambient chloride (Cl⁻) exposure was assessed by matching each participant’s province with 1 km-resolution data from the CHAP dataset, created by Wei et al. via a space-time extremely randomized trees (STET) model [17]. For each year, grid-level estimates were aggregated to the province level by calculating annual mean concentrations. Participants’ residential province codes in CHARLS were then matched to the corresponding provincial averages. For the primary analysis, exposure was defined as the province-level annual mean chloride concentration in the survey year (2015). In sensitivity analyses, we further examined lagged exposure windows, including annual averages for each year from 2013 to 2015. Participants with missing province information or missing exposure data were excluded, although such cases were rare. Because CHARLS does not capture short-term migration, we assumed participants maintained stable residence during the study period. This assumption is reasonable, as residential mobility among middle-aged and older adults in China is generally low and, when relocation does occur, it is usually within the same province [18]. Therefore, any potential misclassification of exposure due to migration is likely to be minimal. While assigning province-level averages may introduce some degree of exposure misclassification, this method ensures complete coverage across 28 provinces and minimizes bias in relative risk estimation.

Assessment of benign prostatic hyperplasia

According to CHARLS protocol, BPH was identified through self-reported diagnosis in response to the question: “Have you ever been diagnosed with a prostate illness, such as prostate hyperplasia, excluding prostate cancer?” This definition aligns with prior studies and reflects symptomatic BPH presentations in the population [19].

Covariates

Covariates were selected based on prior literature and their relevance to BPH-related risk. These included demographic and socioeconomic factors (age, residence, educational status, marital status, and pension status), behavioral and health-related conditions (body mass index, smoking status, drinking status, history of stroke, arthritis, and psychological problems) [11, 20–24].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate the baseline characteristics of participants with and without BPH. t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were employed to compare continuous variables, which were summarized as mean ± SD or median [IQR], depending on their normality. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and compared using chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests, as relevant.

To investigate the association between long-term exposure to the environment chloride (Cl⁻), quantified as the three-year average concentration from 2013 to 2015, and benign prostatic hyperplasia, mixed-effects logistic regression models were employed, incorporating a province-level random intercept to address geographic clustering. Fixed effects comprised the continuous province-level Cl⁻ concentration (per 1 µg/m³ increment) and the covariates listed above (age and BMI as continuous variables; residence [urban/rural], educational status, marital status, smoking status, drinking status, history of stroke, arthritis, psychological problems, and pension status as categorical variables). The only random effect was a province-specific intercept (28 provinces), included a priori to absorb unmeasured contextual differences across provinces (e.g., health-care access, socioeconomic and environmental background) and to account for intra-province clustering; no random slopes were specified. We constructed three models sequentially: Model 1: unadjusted (crude) model. Model 2: adjusted for demographic and socioeconomic covariates, including age, residence, educational status, marital status, and pension status. Model 3: further adjusted for behavioral and health-related variables, including BMI, smoking status, drinking status, history of stroke, arthritis, and psychological conditions. We also conducted sensitivity analyses using Z-score standardized Cl⁻ concentrations to facilitate comparison of effect sizes. Additionally, lagged exposure models were calculated utilizing Cl⁻ values from 2013, 2014, and 2015 to evaluate temporal resilience. All statistical analyses were conducted with R version 4.4.1. P-values less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

The analytic dataset was cross-sectional (2015 wave), and each participant contributed a single observation; therefore, the mixed-effects structure was used to model between-province clustering rather than within-person repetition, and no subject-level random effects were included. Year-specific odds ratios shown in Figure S2 were obtained by refitting the same mixed-effects model three times, the sample and all other model terms were held constant, so these analyses evaluate temporal consistency rather than within-person change. importantly, the mixed-effects model used here does not require ≥ 3 repeated measures per person because clustering is specified at the province level, not the individual level.

Results

Table 1 and Table S1 indicate that 12.3% of the 5,452 male participants reported benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH). Individuals with BPH were older (median age: 65 compared to 60 years), exhibited a higher BMI (23.9 versus 23.0 kg/m²), and were more frequently residents of rural regions. and less inclined to possess simply elementary education or below. They had a lower propensity for smoking and a reduced likelihood of lacking a state pension, although the frequency of BPH differed markedly among provinces.

We assessed the relationship between long-term exposure to ambient chloride (Cl⁻) and benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) by employing a series of mixed-effects logistic regression models with province-level random intercepts to address regional clustering. As shown in Fig. 2 and Table S2, in the crude model (Model 1), Cl⁻ exposure was significantly associated with increased odds of BPH (OR = 1.23; 95% CI: 1.03–1.47; p < 0.05). After adjusting for demographic and socioeconomic factors (Model 2), the association remained robust (OR = 1.24; 95% CI: 1.05–1.48; p < 0.05). The effect estimate was marginally enhanced (OR = 1.26; 95% CI: 1.07–1.49; p < 0.05) in the fully adjusted model (Model 3), which also included health-related and behavioral covariates. The robustness of the observed association is emphasized by the consistency of effect estimates across all models, despite progressive covariate adjustment. These findings suggest that Cl⁻ exposure may be an independent risk factor for BPH, beyond the influence of known demographic, behavioral, and health-related confounders.

In order to facilitate effect size comparability and account for scale differences, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using Z-score standardized chloride concentrations. All models produced consistent effect estimates, as illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1. Statistically significant associations were observed between higher standardized Cl⁻ concentrations and an increased likelihood of benign prostatic hyperplasia. The robustness of the association across analytical strategies was underscored by the fact that each one-unit increase in standardized Cl⁻ was associated with 21% greater odds of BPH in the fully adjusted model (OR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.06–1.38; p < 0.05). To further examine the temporal stability of the observed association, we conducted lagged exposure analyses for each year from 2013 to 2015. Supplementary Figure S2 illustrates that yearly lagged exposure analysis revealed a consistently significant association between Cl⁻ exposure and the risk of BPH across 2013 to 2015. Specifically, each 1 µg/m³ increase in Cl⁻ concentration was associated with a 25% to 26% higher odds of BPH: OR = 1.25 (95% CI: 1.07–1.46, p = 0.005) for 2013, OR = 1.26 (95% CI: 1.07–1.49, p = 0.005) for 2014, and OR = 1.26 (95% CI: 1.07–1.49, p = 0.007) for 2015. As a whole, these findings demonstrate a strong and temporally consistent association between airborne chloride exposure and an elevated risk of BPH.

Discussion and conclusion

Exposure to airborne chloride was substantially correlated with an elevated incidence of benign prostatic hyperplasia, with similar findings across all models and sensitivity tests. The identified correlation may be biologically feasible, given that chlorine is a powerful oxidant that may provoke systemic oxidative stress and inflammation. Empirical evidence indicates that chlorine exposure undermines antioxidant defenses and induces mitochondrial and DNA damage outside the respiratory system [25–27]. Similar to other environmental toxins like cadmium, chronic exposure to chloride may trigger cellular stress responses that contribute to prostatic hyperplasia through inflammation-mediated pathways [28–32]. This study identified chloride as an understudied air pollutant associated with the risk of BPH, thereby resolving the deficiencies of previous research that utilized small samples and a restricted geographic scope by employing nationally representative data [2, 10, 11]. These results add to what is already known about how air pollution affects the health of men’s genitalia. To further understand how chloride contributes to the development of BPH, longitudinal and mechanistic investigations are required. Airborne chloride should be regarded as a potential contaminant for regulatory oversight and public health measures. Our findings emphasize the necessity of integrating new contaminants such as chloride into forthcoming environmental health research and policy frameworks.

Limitations

This research possesses many limitations. BPH was self-reported, perhaps resulting in misclassification. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference. Third, chloride exposure was evaluated at the province level, which may not accurately represent individual exposure. Ultimately, residual confounding from unmeasured variables such as occupation, diet, medications, or co-pollutants cannot be excluded. Fourth, meteorological factors (e.g., temperature, humidity, precipitation) were not available and thus could not be adjusted for, which may have introduced residual confounding. Fifth, because CHARLS does not capture short-term migration, we assumed stable residence; given the generally low mobility of middle-aged and older adults in China, any exposure misclassification due to migration is likely minimal.
