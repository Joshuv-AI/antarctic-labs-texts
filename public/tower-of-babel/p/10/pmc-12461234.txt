Performance‐Related Pain Among Professional Orchestra Musicians—The First Portuguese Nationwide Study (POMPS)
Authors: A Zão, E Altennmüller, L Azevedo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPain is a major health problem among musicians. However, data concerning pain in a nationwide perspective are scarce, particularly among Portuguese musicians. Therefore, we aim to evaluate performance‐related pain among professional orchestra musicians throughout a nationwide characterisation of all Portuguese orchestras.MethodsWe performed a multicenter cross‐sectional study involving all Portuguese professional orchestras, which included 432 musicians (response rate of 72.1%). The main outcome was performance‐related pain (evaluated by the Performance‐related Pain among Musicians Questionnaire—PPAM). The secondary outcomes were: pain approach, physical activity levels, fatigue, distress, perfectionism, and health‐related quality of life. We calculated the prevalence of PRP and performed a descriptive analysis of the main characteristics of our sample (distinguishing musicians with and without PRP) and of PRP, fatigue, distress, perfectionism, and health‐related quality of life among musicians with pain, comparing full‐time and partial‐time musicians.ResultsThe lifetime and point prevalence of performance‐related pain were 71.8% and 33.3%, respectively. Pain intensity score was 14.9 (out of 40), pain interference in general activities was 21.8 (out of 70) and pain interference in performance was even higher (28.3 out of 50). Pain intensity and interference, fatigue, and perfectionism were higher, and quality of life was lower among full‐time musicians. Pain was significantly more prevalent among full‐time musicians, string instrumentalists, older and more sedentary musicians, with longer careers, and those who play more hours per week. Multivariate logistic regression defined a model containing five factors significantly associated with higher probability of performance‐related pain.ConclusionsWe highlight the high prevalence of pain and the significant negative impact of pain on performance and quality of life.Significance StatementThis first Portuguese nationwide study demonstrated that pain is a highly disabling condition among professional orchestra musicians, particularly among full‐time musicians, supporting the integration of healthcare professionals in the professional orchestra framework for a closed follow‐up, early assessment and treatment, and implementation of preventive programs. The proper evaluation and management of pain seems to still be neglected, contributing to significant impairments and reduced quality of life.Keywords: disability evaluation, musicians, pain

Introduction

Studies on professional orchestra musicians have demonstrated the high prevalence of performance‐related musculoskeletal pain or disorders, ranging from 43% to 90% (Abreu‐Ramos and Micheo 2007; Ackermann et al. 2012; Berque et al. 2016; Gasenzer et al. 2017; Kaufman‐Cohen and Ratzon 2011; Leaver et al. 2011; Nygaard et al. 2013; Paarup et al. 2011; Panebianco 2021; Rickert et al. 2013; Rodríguez‐Gude et al. 2023; Rotter et al. 2020; Sousa et al. 2014, 2016; Steinmetz et al. 2015; Zão et al. 2023). However, none of them specifically studied the performance‐related pain (PRP) (including pain arising from other disorders rather than musculoskeletal, such as neurological, orofacial, headache, and other types of pain) (Nugraha et al. 2019), using a validated tool specifically designed by considering the artistic context. Performance‐related pain “can manifest as a variety of symptoms such as pain, electric shock sensation, numbness, tingling, pinching, weakness, spasms or others that interfere with your ability to play your instrument/sing/conduct at your usual level. In this definition, mild transient aches or pains should not be considered” (Zão et al. 2024a).

Besides that, most studies are based on non‐validated tools (Ackermann et al. 2012; Sousa et al. 2014, 2017, 2016; Steinmetz et al. 2015). The few studies using validated tools designed for musicians were based on the Musculoskeletal Pain Intensity and Interference Questionnaire for Musicians (MPIIQM) (Berque et al. 2014, 2016; Panebianco 2021; Zão et al. 2023) and the Musculoskeletal Pain Questionnaire for Musicians (Lamontagne and Bélanger 2012), but these questionnaires present some limitations (Zão et al. 2024b). The Performance‐related Pain Among Musicians Questionnaire (PPAM) (Zão et al. 2024b, 2024c) is a tool designed and validated to evaluate PRP among musicians with different backgrounds, which allows a comprehensive characterisation of the performance profile (Zão et al. 2024b, 2024c).

When analysing PRP, considering the multidimensional nature of pain, it is vital to focus not only on the prevalence of pain and pain intensity measurement but also to evaluate the impact of pain in different activities, including in performance. Previous research has also shown that some pain disorders may be associated with higher levels of fatigue, anxiety and depression, as well as lower levels of physical activity and health‐related quality of life (Depreli et al. 2024; Roberts et al. 2024; Roman‐Juan et al. 2024; Zimmers et al. 2024). However, data on musicians' population are still scarce. When studying this occupational context, performance‐related variables should also be considered, such as the performance routines and the perfectionism levels (Zão et al. 2024a).

In Portugal, epidemiological data concerning pain among musicians are scarce. Studies performed by Sousa et al., including musicians from three Portuguese professional orchestras, are not based on validated questionnaires for musicians (Sousa et al. 2014, 2017, 2016). These authors only measured pain through one item—pain intensity (using the numeric rating scale) (Sousa et al. 2017). As stated by the International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP), when assessing pain, the use of multidimensional tools is encouraged and is more informative than unidimensional intensity scales (Nugraha et al. 2019). Portuguese orchestra musicians were also studied by Zão et al. (2024c); Zão et al. (2024d); Zão et al. (2024a); Zão et al. (2023), but in those studies, professional orchestra musicians were not analysed as an individualised subgroup, except in the validation studies of the MPIIQM‐Pt (Zão et al. 2023).

Therefore, the comprehensive assessment of PRP among professional orchestra musicians is an important step to improve both treatment and prevention strategies, given the specificities of this particular occupational context.

We aim to evaluate the PRP among professional orchestra musicians, throughout a nationwide characterisation of all Portuguese professional orchestras, based on a validated tool specifically designed for musicians. We also aim to compare the characteristics of pain and associated disability among full‐time and part‐time orchestra musicians.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

A multicenter nationwide cross‐sectional study was performed.

All musicians integrating all Portuguese professional orchestras were invited to participate. The following were exclusion criteria: amateur musicians; musicians not playing regularly in the orchestra (who were only occasionally invited to replace a full‐time musician); conductors; musicians of other nationalities living in Portugal for less than 1 year; illiteracy or poor understanding of the Portuguese language.

There are 16 professional orchestras in Portugal, with a total of 657 musicians. The 58 (8.8%) excluded were foreign musicians reporting a poor understanding of the Portuguese language. From the remaining 599, we expected a final sample size of about 360 participants, considering a response rate of 60%. All participants were classical musicians. Among those 599 musicians initially included, 432 agreed to participate and completed the CRF (response rate of 72.1%).

Data Collection Methods and Outcomes

The main outcome was PRP, particularly the prevalence of pain, pain intensity, pain interference in general activities and pain interference in performance. Secondary outcomes were pain location, PRP management, fatigue, anxiety, depression, perfectionism, levels of physical activity, and health‐related quality of life.

Data were collected using a case report form (CRF) comprising the Portuguese versions of the PPAM (for the main outcome, as well as pain location and attitudes towards pain), Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS), 10‐item Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10), Frost Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale—Brief (FMPS‐B), short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ‐SF) and Medical Outcomes Study Short Form 36‐Item Health Survey version 2 (SF‐36). The CRF was delivered by e‐mail to the musicians through the artistic Director of each Institution.

PPAM is a self‐report questionnaire specifically designed and validated to evaluate PRP among musicians. It evaluates three main constructs: pain intensity, pain interference in general activities, and pain interference in performance, comprising 33 items, and an optional module on predictive factors (32 items) (Zão et al. 2024b). Pain intensity and interference are measured on a numeric rating scale (NRS) ranging from 0 (“no pain” or “does not interfere”) to 10 (“pains as bad as you can imagine” or “completely interferes”, respectively) (Zão et al. 2024b). The scoring is focused on the 3 subscales (calculated as the sum of raw scores of the component items): pain intensity (0–40), pain interference in general activities (0–70) and pain interference in performance (0–50) subscales (Zão et al. 2024b, 2024c). The PPAM also allows for the characterisation of pain prevalence, duration, location, and pain management (Zão et al. 2024b, 2024c).

The MFIS is a 21‐item scale designed to evaluate fatigue, which includes three subscales: physical (ranging from 0 to 36), cognitive (ranging from 0 to 40), and psychosocial (ranging from 0 to 8), with a total MFIS score ranging from 0 to 84 (higher scores indicate a greater impact of fatigue) (Gomes 2011; Pavan et al. 2007; Schiehser et al. 2013).

The K10, a 10‐item questionnaire, measures psychological distress (anxiety and depression) on a five‐level response scale. The score ranges from 10 to 50, and a score higher than 22 represents a risk of a mental disorder (Pereira et al. 2019). The cut‐off scores estimate four levels of psychological distress: “low distress” (10 to 15), “moderate” (16 to 21), “high” (22 to 29), and “very high” (30 to 50 points) (Pereira et al. 2019).

The FMPS‐B includes eight items to evaluate perfectionism in two subscales (evaluative concerns and strivings), with a total score ranging from 8 to 40 (higher scores representing more perfectionistic tendencies) (Woodfin et al. 2020).

The IPAQ‐SF assesses the levels of physical activity participation levels, with moderate to high relative reliability (between 0.66 and 0.88) (Meh et al. 2021). The IPAQ‐SF sum score is expressed in Physical Activity Metabolic Equivalent of Task (MET)‐min per week (MET‐min/week). Higher scores indicate higher intensity levels of physical activity (Craig et al. 2003; Meh et al. 2021). After the calculation of the IPAQ score, the participants were divided into the following categories of intensity level: category 1—low (< 600 MET‐min/week), category 2—moderate (≥ 600 and < 3000 MET‐min/week), and category 3—high (≥ 3000 MET‐min/week) (Craig et al. 2003).

The SF‐36 is a widely used instrument to assess the health‐related quality of life (Ferreira 2000a, 2000b; The RAND Corporation 2023). It consists of two summary measures (physical and mental health) and eight scales. The physical health measure includes four scales: physical functioning (10 items), role‐physical (4 items), bodily pain (2 items), and general health (5 items). The mental health measure is composed of four other scales: vitality (4 items), social functioning (2 items), role‐emotional (3 items), and mental health (5 items). Each subscale is obtained by a weighted average of a set of items (from 2 to 10), and it is measured with a numerical percentage scale from 0 to 100 points (0 represents the worst health status or health‐related quality of life possible and 100 the best) (Ferreira 2000a, 2000b; The RAND Corporation 2023).

The self‐report CRF was sent online by e‐mail to the musicians, through the respective Orchestra Directors, after previous contact by the authors and approval of the artistic institutions. Participants were invited to answer the questionnaire anonymously. All participating musicians were previously informed about the study objectives and signed an informed consent form. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Faculty of Medicine of the University of Porto (Application 27/CEFMUP/2021).

Data were collected between July and October 2022.

Statistical Analysis

We calculated the prevalence of PRP and performed a descriptive analysis of the main characteristics of our sample, distinguishing musicians with and without PRP. We also performed a descriptive analysis of PRP, fatigue, distress, perfectionism, and health‐related quality of life among musicians with pain, comparing full‐time and part‐time musicians. Categorical variables are presented as absolute (n) and relative frequencies (%). Continuous variables are summarised as means and SD for normally distributed data and median (M) and interquartile range ([P25—P75]—percentile 25 to percentile 75) for non‐normally distributed data. Normality of the distributions of continuous variables was checked by using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and visual inspection of the histograms. To compare proportions and means between musicians with and without PRP, and between full‐time and part‐time musicians, we used the Chi Square χ 2 test and the Student t test. For continuous variables with non‐normal distributions, the non‐parametric Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare variables' distributions between groups.

An exploratory analysis was conducted to identify relevant factors associated with PRP, using logistic regression modelling. Covariate selection for the multivariable model was informed by univariate logistic regression analysis. All variables presenting a p < 0.25 in the univariate analysis or those considered clinically relevant were included. Multivariable logistic regression was then performed to examine the association between potential risk factors and the presence of PRP (dependent variable), employing a backward stepwise selection method. Results are reported as adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

The software program SPSS version 27.0 was used for statistical analyses. A p‐value of 0.05 was considered significant for all tests.

Performance‐Related Pain

The lifetime prevalence of PRP was 71.8% [95% CI: 67.3%, 75.8%.] (310 musicians), with 232 (53.7% [95% CI: 49.0%, 58.4%]) musicians reporting pain in the last year, 172 (39.8% [95% CI: 35.3%, 44.5%]) in the last month and 144 (33.3% [95% CI: 29.1%, 37.9%]) presenting pain at the time of evaluation.

PRP was significantly more prevalent among full‐time musicians (p < 0.001), musicians who have been playing for more years (p = 0.008) and with longer careers (p < 0.001), musicians reporting lower intensity levels of physical activity (p = 0.004), as well as among string (p = 0.049) and woodwind (p = 0.043) musicians. In contrast, pain was significantly less prevalent among percussionists (p < 0.001).

Musicians with PRP played more hours per week in orchestra activities (p = 0.017), but the differences were not statistically significant when comparing the total of hours played per week in all performative activities (inside and outside the orchestra, p = 0.250).

Musicians with pain reported significantly higher levels of fatigue and psychological distress, as well as lower levels of physical activity and quality of life.

Pain intensity and interference scores (PPAM) were globally moderate, with higher scores in all items of the pain interference in performance subscale, particularly in the items of pain interference playing without effort or tiredness (6.29 out of 10) and spending the usual amount of time playing (5.90 out of 10). Professional orchestra musicians reporting pain also presented high levels of fatigue (27.23 ± 10.0, p < 0.001) and distress (20.19 ± 6.9, p < 0.001), as well as low levels of health‐related quality of life, particularly in the vitality dimension (52.34 ± 16.6, p < 0.001), general health (65.78 ± 15.6, p < 0.001) and bodily pain (66.68 ± 20.7, p < 0.001). The summary scales were globally low, mainly mental health (46.67 ± 10.0, p = 0.027).

Comparing to partial‐time musicians, full‐time musicians presented significantly higher levels of pain intensity (p = 0.038), pain interference in general activities (p < 0.001), pain interference in performance in the items “playing because of symptoms” (p = 0.043) and “playing as well as you would like” (p = 0.040), as well as higher levels of fatigue (in the physical subscale, p = 0.008), perfectionism (p = 0.045), and lower levels of quality of life (summary physical health measure p < 0.001). Partial‐time musicians only presented a lower level of quality of life in the mental health dimension (p = 0.038).

Pain lasted an average of 20.7 ± 11.3 months (mean ± SD), and began, on average, at 28.2 ± 12.5 years old (mean ± SD). Pain is triggered by the performance in 270 musicians (87.1%), and the remaining 40 (12.9%) considered that performance exacerbates pain. For 194 musicians (62.6%), the pain persists even after they stop playing.

Musicians also reported some pain descriptors, such as numbness and tingling (n = 138, 44.5%), electric shock sensation (n = 64, 20.6%), burning (n = 42, 13.5%), and sensibility changes (n = 30, 9.6%).

Regarding pain location, neck (73.8%), lower back (52.2%), shoulder (49.0%) and wrist (48.1%) were the most common body regions affected.

Among musicians with PRP, 102 (32.9%) reported to have continued playing without any modification even with the pain being present, 198 (63.9%) continued playing but with some modifications in performance routines, and 10 (3.2%) stopped playing for at least 1 week. Additionally, 105 musicians (33.8%) had taken at least 1 day off from orchestra work for such pain (p < 0.001).

Among the 310 musicians who developed PRP, 98 (31.6%) stated they never have looked for healthcare, but 271 (87.4%) take analgesic medication on a regular basis (most of them without a medical prescription). Among the 212 who had performed some therapeutic intervention or strategy for pain relief, the interventions that provided greater pain relief (measured by NRS, ranging from 0—no pain relief, to 10—complete pain relief) were: stretching exercises (M = 7.12, SD = 1.9), rest (M = 6.97, SD = 2.7), physiotherapy (M = 6.4, SD = 3.1), massage (M = 6.34, SD = 2.9), modification of the artistic gesture or performance routines (M = 5.71, SD = 2.7), therapeutic interventions using the instrument (M = 5.55 ± 3.5), yoga (M = 5.54 ± 3.2), and ice (M = 5.29 ± 2.7). Only 90 (29.0%) reported to have completely recovered.

Discussion

We present the first study on pain among professional orchestra musicians including all Portuguese professional orchestras, allowing for a broad characterisation of PRP in this demanding occupational context.

Our results were consistent with most studies on professional orchestra musicians, showing a high life‐time prevalence of pain (71.8%). Zão et al. (2023), in a study including three Portuguese professional orchestras, found even higher life‐time and current prevalence data (86.6% and 47.0%, respectively). Gasenzer et al. showed that 66.2% of the German professional orchestra musicians presented chronic pain. This study, including 740 musicians (response rate of 8.6%) was based on a tool not specifically designed for musicians; therefore, it did not deeply characterise the interference of pain in performance (Gasenzer et al. 2017). The lifetime prevalence of PRMD in the study of Berque et al. (2016) on 101 Scottish professional orchestra musicians was 77.2%; in the study of Panebianco (2021) including 79 South African professional orchestra musicians, it was 76% and 30%; and in the study of Steinmetz et al. (2015) on 408 German professional orchestra musicians, it was 89.5% and 8.6%. However, the few studies that invited all the professional orchestra musicians of their respective country had significantly lower response rates (8.6% to 33%) (Gasenzer et al. 2017; Viljamaa et al. 2017), creating some bias in the analysis and possibly calling into question the generalisation of the results to those national populations of professional orchestra musicians. When comparing those data, we should also be aware that PRP, PRMD, and playing‐related musculoskeletal pain or injuries are different outcomes. In fact, when analysing most of those studies, what authors intend to measure is PRP, and they use a self‐report questionnaire (more appropriate to evaluate pain, a subjective outcome, rather than disorders or injuries, for which a deeper clinical evaluation is needed). And so, to be consistent and to contribute to a more accurate and homogeneous terminology, we encourage the use of the term PRP when the aim is to evaluate pain related to the performance (encompassing different types of pain and different performative musical contexts).

Regarding pain characterisation, our results demonstrated that intensity and interference are moderate, with an average of pain interference in performance superior to 5 out of 10 and a score of 29 out of 50 for full‐time musicians. The higher scores of pain interference in performance when comparing to pain interference in general activities (21.79/70, mean 3.15) and pain intensity (14.94/40, mean 3.78) are corroborated by a previous study on professional musicians using the same tool—PPAM (pain interference in performance 28.96 versus intensity 15.14 and interference in general 22.59) (Zão et al. 2024d). Berque et al. evaluated pain interference with a score of 15.2 (out of 50), but did not distinguish between the impact of pain in general activities and in performance as different constructs and presented a poorer characterisation of the impact on performance (Berque et al. 2016). Panebianco et al., using the same tool as Berque et al. (MPIIQM) also showed lower scores of pain interference (21.2), comparing to our sample (Panebianco 2021).

Analysing the few studies on Portuguese professional orchestra musicians, Zão et al. 2023 found a pain intensity score/mean of 14.99/3.75 and pain interference (using the MPIIQM) of 17.45/3.49 (Zão et al. 2023). In the study of Sousa et al., the pain intensity, measured by the numeric rating scale, was, on average, 4.3 for string musicians and 4.7 for wind musicians (Sousa et al. 2017). However, it was not specified the maximum, minimum, mean, and current pain levels, the pain interference in performance, or other potential associated disabilities (Sousa et al. 2014, 2017).

The items “playing without effort or tiredness” and “spending your usual amount of time playing” presented the higher scores (6.41 and 6.03 for full‐time musicians), reinforcing the importance of measuring these parameters for a better understanding of the participation restrictions in the artistic context. The higher scores in the pain interference subscales are consistent with the previous results of Zão et al., reinforcing the high adding value of PPAM Zão et al. (2024d).

Moreover, the high scores of the item pain interference when “playing without effort or tiredness” are consistent with the high levels of fatigue and the low quality of life, particularly in the vitality dimension, also reported by musicians. The lower levels of physical activity reported by musicians with PRP may also correlate with the higher levels of fatigue they presented, since it is well known the importance of some physical activity modalities for improving muscle strength and resistance and cardiovascular and pulmonary conditioning, minimising fatigue and exertion sensation (Liguori 2022).

Fulltime musicians with PRP seem to be significantly more perfectionist than part‐time musicians. Additionally, perfectionism levels in musicians with PRP were higher (54.0) than in another sample of Portuguese musicians including music students and professional musicians (50.0) (Zão et al. 2024a). However, in contrast with that study (which found a significant association between higher levels of perfectionism and PRP), in our sample of professional orchestra musicians, the higher levels of perfectionism among musicians with pain were not statistically significant (p = 0.084).

Pain intensity and pain interference in general activities, two items of pain interference in the performance subscale, perfectionism levels, and fatigue were significantly higher among full‐time musicians, and health‐related quality of life was lower, which can be explained by the higher commitment and demands they are exposed to on a regular basis. Studies on music students corroborate the lower levels of quality of life (Cruder et al. 2020; Piątkowska et al. 2016), but no study was found on professional orchestra musicians using a validated tool for health‐related quality of life.

Musicians with PRP also reported higher levels of anxiety and depression. However, these results should be interpreted with caution, because a reverse causality may be underlying, given that it is expected that musicians with pain are more likely to present anxiety and depression. On the other hand, anxiety and depression may influence the experience of pain.

Despite the long duration of pain and the reported significant interference of pain, one third of musicians never looked for healthcare and 29.0% completely recovered. Besides that, the analgesic medication consumption was high, regardless of the scarce demand and search for medical care by musicians (therefore, they often self‐medicate without prior medical evaluation and prescription). The proportion of musicians who reported having completely recovered was lower than among Australian professional orchestra musicians in the study of Ackerman et al. (almost 50%) (Ackermann et al. 2012) and the amount of musicians who had taken at least 1 day off from orchestra work was higher in our sample (33.8% versus 28%) (Ackermann et al. 2012). Almost one third of our sample reported continuing to play (without any modification) despite the pain. The most efficient strategies for pain relief were similar to those pointed out in a previous study of Zão et al. on professional musicians and music students (Zão et al. 2024d).

Our multivariate analysis suggests that biopsychosocial and performance‐related factors play a key role in the development of PRP among orchestra musicians, with fatigue, orchestra status and instrument group emerging as the strongest potential predictors. Our results are in line with previous studies, suggesting that string instrumentalists (Gasenzer et al. 2017; Kaufman‐Cohen et al. 2018; Leaver et al. 2011), with longer careers (Berque et al. 2016; Steinmetz et al. 2015), who play more hours per week in the orchestra (Kaufman‐Cohen and Ratzon 2011; Kochem and Silva 2017), who are older (Abreu‐Ramos and Micheo 2007; Kochem and Silva 2017; Steinmetz et al. 2015; Viljamaa et al. 2017) and more sedentary (Rodríguez‐Gude et al. 2023; Rodríguez‐Romero et al. 2016) have a higher probability of PRP. However, it should be noted that some of those studies do not focus specifically on professional orchestra musicians nor specifically study PRP, since the literature is still scarce on associated or predictive factors of PRP among professional orchestra musicians.

We also found that the prevalence of PRP seems to be higher among full‐time orchestra musicians, supporting the integration of healthcare professionals in the professional orchestra framework for a closed medical follow‐up, early assessment and treatment, and implementation of preventive programmes.

Our work has important strengths. It is the first study on PRP among Portuguese professional orchestra musicians, since previous studies did not focus on PRP but rather on performance‐related musculoskeletal disorders (including other problems that may not cause pain and do not consider pain arising from neurological or other disorders beyond the musculoskeletal) (Sousa et al. 2014, 2017). This is the first study including all Portuguese professional orchestras; therefore, our sample is highly representative of our population.

Furthermore, our findings are based on a validated tool specifically designed to evaluate PRP among musicians, which allows a broader and deeper understanding of associated disability, including the interference in performance. This is also the first study including a standardised assessment of fatigue, anxiety, and depression, quality of life, and physical activity levels on professional orchestra musicians, using validated tools. Another strength is the high response rate (72.1%) when comparing to previous nationwide studies performed in other countries (ranging from 33% to 57%) (Leaver et al. 2011; Steinmetz et al. 2015; Viljamaa et al. 2017).

We also performed the first comparative study on PRP between full‐time and part‐time professional orchestra musicians.

Finally, our results are based on a multidimensional tool, which represents a clear added value compared to the unidimensional characterisation of pain presented in most studies (Sousa et al. 2014, 2017, 2016; Steinmetz et al. 2015). Analysing the few studies using multidimensional tools (Berque et al. 2016; Panebianco 2021; Zão et al. 2023), our study allowed a deeper and more specific evaluation of associated disability and pain interference in performance.

We also highlight some limitations, such as the different number of musicians in the full‐time and partial‐time subgroups. However, we recruited all musicians from Portuguese professional orchestras and the number of full‐time musicians is usually higher than that of partial‐time musicians. In the subgroup analysis, the number of musicians per instrument group also differed, but it represents the usual proportion of musicians per group in an orchestra. On the other hand, we excluded 8.8% of the professional orchestra musicians since they were foreign musicians reporting poor understanding of the Portuguese language, which diminished our sample size. Furthermore, we are aware that our population is small and has fewer professional orchestras when comparing to larger countries, but this is our national reality and we contributed, with this study, to a better knowledge of PRP among professional orchestras which will allow for improvements in the healthcare provided. Even though, to our knowledge, our sample size and response rate are the largest of all published studies on PRP among professional orchestra musicians using a validated tool designed for musicians.
