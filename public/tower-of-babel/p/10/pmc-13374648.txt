Endometrial Cancer by ERBB2 Amplification (ERBB2amp) Status: Differences in Molecular Subtypes, Ancestry, and Real-World Outcomes
Authors: Evelyn Cantillo, Vivek Podder, Natalie Danziger, Dale Lobo, Douglas I Lin, Ryon P Graf, Robert L Coleman, Bhavana Pothuri, Ramez N Eskander, Thomas J Herzog, Brian M Slomovitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEEndometrial cancer (EC) has rising mortality rates and persistent racial disparities, particularly among non-Hispanic Black women who are more likely to present with aggressive histologic subtypes associated with worse survival. ERBB2 amplification (ERBB2amp), a biomarker enriched in aggressive EC subtypes, is a therapeutic target. This study investigates ERBB2amp prevalence, molecular subtype, and histologic associations, as well as prognostic impact across diverse genetic ancestries using a large clinicogenomic database (CGDB).METHODSWe analyzed 16,073 patients with EC who underwent tissue comprehensive genomic profiling via Foundation Medicine. Genetic ancestry was inferred using a single nucleotide polymorphism–based classifier. An outcomes cohort of 1,275 patients with advanced or recurrent EC from the Flatiron Health-Foundation Medicine CGDB was used to assess survival outcomes. Patients receiving human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)–targeted therapy were excluded from the overall survival (OS) analysis. ERBB2amp prevalence, histologic and molecular correlations, and associations with OS were examined.RESULTSERBB2amp was identified in 8% of EC tumors, with the highest prevalence among patients of African ancestry (12% v 7% European ancestry; P < .001). There was a significant association between ERBB2amp and serous histology, TP53 mutation presence, low tumor mutational burden, and microsatellite stability. In the outcomes cohort, ERBB2amp was not independently associated with OS (hazard ratio, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.65 to 1.17]; P = .37). In contrast, TP53mutation and poor performance status were independently associated with decreased OS. Notably, of patients with documented HER2 immunohistochemistry (IHC; n = 305), 22% of nonamplified tumors exhibited HER2 IHC 2+/3+ staining.CONCLUSIONERBB2amp is more common among patients with African genetic ancestry and frequently co-occurs with TP53mutations, providing molecular rationale for EC disparities. Self-reported race does not fully capture genetic ancestry; therefore, comprehensive tumor profiling and HER2 IHC testing should be performed to improve selection for targeted therapies and help address disparities in EC outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Endometrial cancer (EC) is the most common gynecologic malignancy in the United States, with an estimated 67,880 new cases and 13,250 deaths projected in 2024.1 Unlike most cancers where mortality has declined, the EC death rates have continued to rise by approximately 1% annually among non-Hispanic White (NHW) women and 2%-3% per year among other racial and ethnic groups from 2010 to 2019.1,2 These trends highlight significant and persistent racial disparities in outcomes, with non-Hispanic Black (NHB) women disproportionately affected.

NHB women are more likely to present with aggressive histologic subtypes, such as uterine serous carcinoma (USC) and uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS), both associated with worse survival.3-8 Between 2010 and 2017, death rates from these aggressive subtypes rose by 2.7% annually, demonstrating the urgent need for research to address these disparities.9 Racial disparities in EC outcomes are multifactorial and driven by systemic racism, social determinants of health, inequalities in health care access, lack of guideline-accordant care, and insurance-related barriers.10-12

Advances in molecular profiling offer new opportunities for precision medicine. Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), which is encoded by the ERBB2 gene, is a key molecular target. HER2 overexpression and ERBB2 gene amplification (ERBB2amp) show a high concordance across disease sites.13-16 HER2 overexpression is present in 30%-35% of USC and 15%-20% of UCS cases.3 Both histologies are strongly associated with poor prognosis. In one study, Black patients constituted 44% of patients with USC, and they had worse outcomes.17

ERBB2amp is a commonly observed alteration leading to abnormal HER2 activation resulting in tumorigenesis.14 Targeting HER2 has demonstrated therapeutic promise; a randomized phase II trial investigating the addition of trastuzumab to carboplatin and paclitaxel in HER2-positive USC significantly improved progression-free survival, establishing this combination as a viable treatment option for advanced or recurrent USC.18 Furthermore, the US Food and Drug Administration granted accelerated approval to the antibody drug conjugate (ADC) trastuzumab deruxtecan for treating recurrent HER2 (immunohistochemistry [IHC] 3+/2+) expressing EC.14,15 The investigator-assessed overall response rate for the endometrial cohort in the phase II DESTINY-PanTumor02 trial was 57.5%.19

Despite the clinical relevance of ERBB2amp, its relationship with race and genetic ancestry in EC remains poorly understood. Recent data emphasize the importance of investigating ERBB2 amplifications across subgroups. A large-scale analysis presented at the 2024 ESMO Congress, which included data from over 100,000 patients in the GENIE registry, reported ERBB2amp in 4.7% of EC.20 Black patients with EC showed higher rates of ERBB2 amplifications compared with White patients. These findings further emphasize the clinical significance of HER2 as a target and its potential role in contributing to racial disparities in outcomes.

Race, widely used as a proxy for ancestry, is an inherently limited sociocultural construct based on physical characteristics and fails to capture the genetic diversity within populations. In contrast, genetic ancestry, defined through genetic markers, provides a more precise and biologically relevant framework for understanding disparities in tumor biology and treatment response.21 Genetic ancestry may reveal subtle, ancestry-specific molecular differences that race alone cannot capture.

To address this gap, we leveraged a single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP)–based genetic ancestry classification system to investigate the prevalence of ERBB2amp across five ancestry groups: predominant African (AFR), European (EUR), admixed American (AMR), South Asian (SAS), and East Asian (EAS). By integrating genomic, histologic, and clinical data, we aimed to examine the prevalence of ERBB2amp across ancestries, its association with molecular subtypes and histology, and its impact on overall survival (OS). This research addresses a critical knowledge gap, with implications for improving ancestry-informed targeted therapies and reducing disparities in EC outcomes.

METHODS

Cohort Selection

This study was performed in compliance with all relevant ethical regulations including the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval for this study was obtained from the WCG Institutional Review Board for the genomics cohort (Protocol No. 20152817) and the outcomes cohort (Protocol No. 420180044).

Genomics Cohort

The genomics cohort (n = 16,073) included patients with a diagnosis of endometrial carcinoma, who received tissue comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) by FoundationOne or FoundationOneCDx. Patients were grouped by the presence or absence of ERBB2 amplification (ERBB2amp) by CGP. Patient selection diagram is shown in Figure 1A.

Cohort selection diagrams for the (A) genomics-only cohort and (B) the outcomes cohort. ERBB2amp, ERBB2 amplified; FMI, Foundation Medicine, Inc; pts, patients; tx, therapy.

Outcomes Cohort

The outcomes cohort (n = 1,275) included patients with advanced endometrial carcinoma (recurrent or stage III/IV at the time of diagnosis) from the US-wide Flatiron Health and Foundation Medicine endometrial clinicogenomic database (CGDB), who had tissue CGP. Clinical data from the Flatiron Health Research Database are linked to genomic data, derived from FMI's CGP tests FoundationOneCDx and FoundationOne in the FH-FMI CGDB by deterministic matching, providing a deidentified data set.18,19 See Figure 1B for patient selection diagram. Lines of HER2-targeted therapy included trastuzumab (and biosimilars), pertuzumab, ado-trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1), and fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan (T-DXD). First-line therapy refers to the first line of treatment received in the advanced setting.

Comprehensive Genomic Profiling

CGP was performed using hybrid-capture–based next-generation sequencing in Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments–certified, College of American Pathologists–accredited laboratory (Foundation Medicine, Cambridge, MA) as previously described.22,23 In addition to evaluation of genomic alterations in 324 genes, microsatellite instability (MSI), TMB, and a homologous recombination deficiency signature (HRDsig), provided as a laboratory professional service, were calculated as previously described.24,25

HER2 IHC

HER2 IHC results were available for a subset of patients in the outcomes cohort (n = 305, 24%). Results were abstracted from the electronic health record (EHR). 0 and 1+ scores were both considered negative and not distinguished in the available data. For patients with multiple HER2 IHC tests, the most recent conclusive test before the start of first-line therapy was selected.

Genetic Ancestry, Race, and Ethnicity

Genetic ancestry for each patient was derived from the CGP assay using an SNP-based approach as previously described.26,27 In brief, a random forest classifier was trained using genetic variation at over >40,000 germline SNPs with cross-validation performed using the 1,000 genomes project cohort.26 Genetic variation was defined by 10 features based on allele-count variation as determined by principal component analysis. This classifier was trained to assign samples to one of the five following groups: European (EUR), African (AFR), East Asian (EAS), South Asian (SAS), and admixed American (AMR). For the outcomes cohort, patient-reported race (White, Black or African American, Asian, Other or multiple races) and ethnicity (Hispanic or Latino or Not Hispanic or Latino) were abstracted from the EHR.

Outcomes

To evaluate the prognosis of patients in the outcomes cohort, OS was the evaluated end point. OS was calculated from initiation of the first line of therapy to death. Patients not yet reaching death were right censored at the date of last EHR activity or CGP report. Risk set adjustment was performed for the OS analysis to account for left truncation. The mortality information in the Flatiron Health database is a composite derived from documents within the EHR, Social Security Death Index, and a commercial death data set mining data from obituaries and funeral homes and has been externally validated.28 To better interrogate the effect of ERBB2amp status as a prognostic biomarker rather than its interaction with corresponding targeted treatments, we excluded those patients who received HER2-targeted therapy in the first line from the OS analysis (n = 36; 21 ERBB2 amplified and 15 ERBB2 not amplified).

Statistics

Rank-sum tests and chi-square tests were used to evaluate differences in continuous and categorical variables, respectively. Differences in OS were evaluated by Cox proportional hazard models. Kaplan-Meier plots were used to visualize unadjusted differences between groups. The multivariable model incorporated Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance score, stage at diagnosis, patient-identified race, age, BMI, ERRB2 amplification status, and molecular subtype. Multiple comparison adjustments of P values for the outcome analysis were not performed. Significance was defined as P < .05. R (v4.4.1) software was used for all statistical analyses.

RESULTS

Genomics Cohort

Baseline characteristics of the genomics cohort are shown in Table 1. Among the 16,073 patients in the genomics cohort, 1,287 (8.0%) were identified as ERBB2amp. Patients with ERBB2amp were older, with a median age of 68 years (IQR, 64-74) compared with 65 years (IQR, 59-71) in the nonamplified group (P < .001). Histologically, ERBB2amp tumors were disproportionately enriched for serous carcinoma, accounting for 46.7% of amplified cases compared with 19.6% in the nonamplified group (P < .001). Conversely, endometrioid carcinoma was notably underrepresented in the ERBB2amp group (6.4% v 26.2%). Patients of African ancestry were significantly more likely to harbor ERBB2amp than patients of European ancestry (12% v 7%, P = .001; Fig 2A).

Molecular profiling revealed that ERBB2amp tumors exhibited significantly lower TMB, with a median TMB of 2.5 mut/Mb (IQR, 1.3-3.8) compared with 3.5 mut/Mb (IQR, 1.3-7.0) in nonamplified tumors (P < .001). Serous carcinoma was the predominant histology in the ERBB2amp group, accounting for 67.0% of cases compared with 22.4% in the nonamplified group (P < .001). Rates of ERBB2amp compared with nonamplification by histology were 13.2% versus 46.5% in endometrioid, 6.6% versus 15.1% in carcinosarcoma, 8.5% versus 5.9% in clear cell, and 4.7% versus 10% in EC not otherwise specified (NOS).

Molecular subtype analysis demonstrated a strong association between ERBB2 amplification and TP53 mutations. Among ERBB2amp tumors, 85.8% were classified as TP53-mutant, compared with 43.9% in the nonamplified group (P < .001). In contrast, MSI-H and POLEmut subtypes were absent among ERBB2amp tumors, and NSMP tumors were significantly underrepresented. In all, 21 (20%) patients with ERBB2amp and 15 (1.2%) patients without ERBB2amp received HER2-targeted therapy in the first line. Most patients received chemotherapy plus or minus bevacizumab but without additional agents in both groups.

Survival Analysis

To evaluate ERBB2amp as a prognostic marker for survival, OS was evaluated. Patients who received HER2-targeted therapy in the first line were excluded from the analysis (n = 36). In univariable analysis of patients with EC, ERBB2amp tumors had numerically less favorable OS compared with patients without ERBB2amp (median 16.8 months v 20.1 months, hazard ratio, 0.28 [95% CI, 0.96 to 1.70], P = .09; Fig 3A). In the multivariable analysis, factors associated with less favorable survival outcomes included performance status: ECOG 1 (95% CI, 1.18 to 1.77, P < .001, HR, 1.44) and 2+ (95% CI, 2.32 to 3.98, P < .001, HR, 3.03), and stage III disease (95% CI, 1.04 to 1.54, P = .020, HR, 1.26; Fig 3B). Among molecular categories, TP53 molecular subtype was associated with the least favorable outcomes (HR, 2.43 [95% CI, 1.91 to 3.10], P < .001), whereas ERBB2 amplification was not independently associated with OS (HR, 0.88 [95% CI, 0.65 to 1.17], P = .370).

Ancestry Versus Race in Cohort

In the outcomes cohort, excluding 38 cases with ancestry data unavailable, patients had the following distribution of predominant genetic ancestry: 945 (76%) EUR, 203 (16%) AFR, 27 (2%) EAS, 15 (1%) SAS, and 47 (4%) AMR. Co-occurrence of predominant genetic ancestry and self-identified race was as follows: EUR (83.9% White), AFR (83.7% Black/African-American), EAS (77.8% Asian), SAS (46.7% Asian), and AMR (46.8% Other/multiple races). Patients with predominant SAS and AMR ancestry had a heterogeneous mix of self-identified races.

DISCUSSION

This study provides new insights into the distribution of ERBB2 amplification in EC across self-identified racial groups and genetic ancestries. Our findings revealed a higher prevalence of ERBB2amp tumors among patients with predominant AFR ancestry and frequent overlap with TP53mut. Consistent with prior studies, TP53mut was strongly associated with poorer OS.29-31 These results align with existing literature highlighting higher incidence of histologically aggressive EC subtypes among Black women.4,8,32,33 Using self-identified race as a surrogate for genetic ancestry has known limitations. As a sociocultural construct, race does not capture genetic diversity, which is better reflected in genetic ancestry.21 In our cohort, concordance between self-identified race and genetic ancestry was highest among individuals identifying as Black/African American and White. Although there is an overlap between race and genetic ancestry, relying solely on racial categorization may obscure clinically relevant genomic differences.34

ERBB2amp was predominantly identified in serous carcinoma (46.7%), consistent with its enrichment in high-grade, aggressive EC subtypes. Notably, ERBB2amp was also detected in carcinosarcomas (11.6%) and adenocarcinoma NOS (25.6%), as well as in a smaller proportion of endometrioid histology cases (approximately 7%). This highlights CGP's value in identifying actionable targets across all histologic subtypes.

Interestingly, although ERBB2amp tumors were more likely to exhibit HER2 overexpression (IHC 2+/3+), 22% of patients without ERBB2amp also demonstrated HER2 positivity (IHC 2+ or 3+). Although ERBB2amp is the primary mechanism for HER2 overexpression, other types of gene alterations, such as mutations, rearrangements, and epigenetic modification, can lead to oncogenic HER2 activation.14,35 This finding has therapeutic implications, suggesting that HER2-targeted therapies may benefit a subset of patients without ERBB2 amplification. HER2 IHC testing is a valuable adjunct to identifying additional candidates for HER2-directed therapies, an area warranting further prospective validation in clinical trials.36 Although there is a scarcity of data for HER2-targeted treatment in HER2-negative ERBB2-altered tumors, some data exist suggesting a possible signal. One study of 34 patients with ERBB2amp non–small cell lung cancer found that eight (34%) were HER2-negative, and two of these patients experienced a partial response when treated with an anti-HER2 ADC.37 In another study, 19 patients with HER2-negative breast cancer were found to carry an ERBB2 mutation; eight patients were treated with neratinib, an oral ERBB2 inhibitor. No statistically significant differences in OS were observed.38 Those results suggest that evaluation of both HER2 IHC and ERBB2amp may allow for the identification of the maximum number of patients benefitting from HER2-targeted treatments.

Our multivariable survival analysis demonstrated that TP53 mutations were strongly associated with poor OS, independent of other prognostic factors. This finding reinforces TP53mut as a critical driver of tumor aggressiveness in EC. In contrast, ERBB2amp was not independently associated with survival despite a trend toward worse outcomes in the Kaplan-Meier analysis, suggesting that the effect of ERBB2amp on clinical outcomes may be mediated by co-occurring molecular alterations or clinicopathologic factors.

Understanding molecular differences that predispose certain populations to aggressive EC is essential for addressing racial disparities in outcomes. In this study, ERBB2amp was not independently associated with OS, a finding consistent with prior research showing that even after adjusting for stage, grade, and histology, survival rates remain lower among NHB women compared with NHW women.3,7,21,39,40 This highlights the multifactorial nature of these disparities, which extend beyond genomic alterations to include social determinants of health and inequities in access to care.

Recent studies have further elucidated the molecular landscape of EC in diverse populations. For instance, Weigelt et al40 conducted a comprehensive analysis of ECs in Black and White patients, revealing significant differences in histologic and molecular subtypes, as well as the presence of therapeutic targets. Their findings emphasize the importance of incorporating diverse populations into molecular studies and clinical trials to address EC disparities. Additional research has highlighted racial differences in the mutational landscape of serous EC, noting that TP53 mutations were more prevalent in tumors from Black patients, which may contribute to observed disparities in outcomes.41

A major strength of this study is the integration of CGP with longitudinal clinical data, allowing for a robust analysis of ERBB2amp, its molecular associations, and clinical outcomes in EC. The large, diverse cohort from the CGDB enabled detailed subgroup analyses across self-identified racial groups and genetic ancestries. The novel application of an SNP-based genetic ancestry classification system provides a clearer understanding of ancestry-informed tumor biology, moving beyond the limitations of self-identified race as a surrogate for genetic ancestry. This study also offers key insights into the frequent co-occurrence of ERBB2amp and TP53mut, with implications for aggressive disease phenotypes and poor survival outcomes. Furthermore, the findings suggest actionable clinical applications, including universal HER2 IHC testing in addition to next-generation sequencing (NGS) to identify ERBB2amp candidates for HER2-targeted therapy.

The study does have inherent limitations. As a retrospective study, inclusion was restricted to patients with available genomic and clinical data, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader EC population. The inability to subclassify adenocarcinoma NOS tumors, which accounted for approximately 26% of ERBB2amp cases, limited deeper insights into the molecular and clinical characteristics of this subgroup. Additionally, although the mortality end point used in this study has been previously validated against the national death index, reliance on EHR may result in incomplete data capture, particularly regarding treatment adherence, comorbidities, and detailed clinical variables.28 Finally, the exclusion of patients who received HER2-targeted therapies from survival analyses limits the ability to evaluate the real-world efficacy of these treatments in ERBB2amp EC.

In conclusion, ERBB2amp was more prevalent among patients with predominant AFR ancestry and frequently co-occurred with TP53mut. ERBB2amp itself was not independently prognostic of survival, but its association with poor outcomes may be mediated by co-occurring molecular alterations and clinicopathologic factors. Overlap between ERBB2amp and HER2 IHC expression was high but not 100%, suggesting that testing by both methods should be considered for all patients. Beyond genomic insights, this study underscores the importance of addressing systemic barriers to equitable health care delivery. Molecular differences alone do not fully explain EC mortality disparities; social and systemic inequities remain major contributors, particularly among racial and ethnic minorities.
