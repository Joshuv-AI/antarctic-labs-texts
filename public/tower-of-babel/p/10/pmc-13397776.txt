Unveiling the black box between decent work and employee innovation behavior: the roles of intrinsic motivation, work engagement, and organizational innovation climate
Authors: Han Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnterprise innovation relies on employee innovation behavior, which is enhanced by favorable working conditions. This study analyzed data from 686 employees across 57 organizations using SPSS 25.0, Mplus 8.0, and HLM 6.08 to examine the mechanisms underlying the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior. The key findings are as follows: (1) Decent work is positively correlated with employee innovation behavior. (2) Intrinsic motivation mediates the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior. (3) Work engagement also mediates this relationship. (4) Intrinsic motivation and work engagement serve a chain-mediating role between decent work and employee innovation behavior. (5) Furthermore, organizational innovation climate is positively correlated with employee innovation behavior. (6) Organizational innovation climate moderates the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior. These findings not only reveal the latent mechanisms linking decent work to employee innovation behavior but also provide valuable insights for improving the efficiency of organizations’ human resource management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04868-w.Keywords: Decent work, Intrinsic motivation, Work engagement, Organizational innovation climate, Employee innovation behavior

Introduction

Employee innovation behavior, defined as the generation and implementation of novel ideas during work processes [1–3], has emerged as a pivotal element for organizational survival and competitive edge in dynamic settings [4, 5]. In today’s business environment, the external environment is characterized by increasing complexity and uncertainty [6, 7]. To address these challenges and secure sustainable performance, organizations are obliged to strengthen their innovation capabilities [8]. As essential components and valuable assets of an organization, employees play a crucial role. Research indicates that the “bottom-up” employee innovation behavior, based on workers’ daily work practices, serves not only as a significant vehicle but also as a key source for organizational innovation [9]. Consequently, cultivating employee innovation behavior has become a non-negotiable necessity for organizations navigating the turbulent waters of digital transformation and rapid market fluctuations [3, 8]. Although the academic community has identified numerous antecedents of employee innovation behavior, employment quality factors related to employees’ labor rights and interests have not received sufficient attention [10]. With the deepening of economic globalization, liberalization, and digitalization, as well as the advancement of neoliberal transformation and labor market deregulation, a vast number of workers are under immense pressure from the expansion of the proportion of non-regular employment, the increasing risk of structural unemployment, the weakening of social protection, and the polarization of income and wealth distribution [11]. The employment quality of the majority of workers is facing unprecedented challenges. In response to this reality, at the 87th International Labour Conference in 1999, Juan Somavia put forward the global governance objective of “decent work for all”. According to the official statement of the International Labour Organization (ILO), decent work encompasses all pursuits related to work experience. It includes productive employment opportunities that offer fair remuneration, safety in the workplace, social protection for workers and their families, better prospects for personal development and social integration, as well as the freedom and right to voice concerns, form organizations, and participate in decision-making on all matters that affect work and life, and thus pertain to equal opportunities and treatment for all men and women [12].

Recent years have witnessed a flourishing of research on decent work in vocational and organizational psychology, which has inspired studies and provided investigative tools. Blustein and colleagues [13] laid the groundwork for further empirical research by proposing a theoretical framework. Ferraro and colleagues examined the positive effects of decent work on autonomy, work engagement, and psychological capital [14, 15]. Additionally, Ferraro et al. [15] proposed that decent work encompasses fundamental principles and values within the workplace, appropriate working hours and intensity, fulfilling and productive job roles, a remuneration level that enables the fulfillment of civic responsibilities, social protection, opportunities, and health and safety. Likewise, Liu et al. [16] argued that decent work should integrate dimensions such as income, job security, work environment, labor intensity, democratic participation, career development, and social status and dignity. Regarding the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior, scholars have mainly conducted research from the following perspectives: Firstly, from the perspective of material incentives and job security, it is believed that pay fairness [17] and job stability [18] are both positively correlated with employee innovation behavior. Secondly, from the perspective of work-environment and workload balance, it is considered that moderate working hours [19] and work-life balance [20] are conducive to employee innovation behavior. Thirdly, from the perspective of organizational support and development space, it has been found that employee involvement [21, 22], supportive relationships with colleagues [23], and career development [11, 24] can stimulate employee innovation behavior.

The existing literature has provided valuable references for research on related topics. However, two aspects of the existing literature still require further improvement. On the one hand, the majority of the existing literature has concentrated on a single dimension of decent work. Scholars seldom explore the comprehensive concept of decent work and its role in promoting employee innovation behavior [25, 26]. On the other hand, the existing literature often examines the impact of decent work on innovation behavior from the perspective of individual psychological needs and states [27, 28]. Nevertheless, the role of decent work in promoting innovation behavior may extend beyond the satisfaction of basic psychological needs and positive work states. This indicates that the current body of research fails to fully elucidate the diverse mechanisms by which decent work stimulates employee innovation behavior [10]. This gap motivates the present study to address two critical questions: What is the fundamental mechanism that translates decent work into innovation behavior, and what are the precise transmission pathways? Successfully answering these questions is vital for developing the OBHRM theoretical framework and, concurrently, for providing managers with the novel insights needed to effectively leverage decent work for enhancing innovation outcomes.

Theoretical review and research hypothesis

Theoretical review

Self-Determination Theory (SDT) and the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) Model offer complementary theoretical perspectives on workplace motivation and behavior. SDT posits that human motivation exists along a continuum from amotivation to intrinsic motivation. Intrinsic motivation emerges when three fundamental psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness are satisfied [29]. This theory underscores the significance of autonomous regulation and voluntary engagement, suggesting that individuals inherently seek challenges and growth opportunities when their psychological needs are met. Conversely, the JD-R Model focuses on how job characteristics affect employee well-being and performance [30]. It posits that job resources, including physical, psychological, social, and organizational elements, play a pivotal role in achieving work goals, reducing job demands, and promoting personal growth and development. Together, these frameworks provide a comprehensive framework for examining how workplace factors influence employee outcomes. SDT offers a perspective grounded in psychological needs, whereas the JD-R Model emphasizes contextual and resource-based mechanisms. Within this integrated theoretical framework, the introduction of three key variables becomes essential for unraveling the complex relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior.

Intrinsic motivation, deriving from the inherent enjoyment of an activity rather than external rewards, significantly impacts employee innovation behavior [31]. As a fundamental concept within SDT, intrinsic motivation theory posits that such individuals exhibit greater interest, excitement, and confidence in activities, leading to better performance and demonstrating greater perseverance and creativity. Consequently, it’s widely regarded as a pivotal determinant of employees’ creativity, rooted in their intrinsic interest and enjoyment of the work itself [32]. This variable captures the quality of motivation engendered by decent work conditions, representing the psychological mechanism through which need satisfaction translates into innovative outcomes.

Work engagement, conceptualized within the JD-R framework, is a positive work-related state that predicts job performance and fosters creativity [33]. Engaged employees demonstrate positivity, energy, dedication, and a willingness to contribute innovations [34]. This variable reflects the energetic state resulting from resource availability, helping to explain how decent work conditions translate into the behavioral activation necessary for innovation.

Organizational innovation climate constitutes a pivotal contextual factor that encapsulates the internal work environment perceived by organizational members [35]. This climate serves as the “soft environment” that facilitates innovative activities among talents within the organization, exerting direct or indirect influence on innovation behavior and capabilities. Current research on employee innovation behavior shows a predominant focus on individual factors such as abilities, cognitive levels, values, knowledge structures, and life satisfaction [3, 36], as well as organizational aspects like structure/culture, organizational citizenship behavior, leadership styles, and company incentives [2, 37, 38]. However, comparatively less attention has been paid to employees’ psychological perceptions and job characteristics influenced by organizational climate [39]. Additionally, in real-world contexts, employees are embedded within organizations, where they may share common characteristics or be affected by shared organizational factors, such as the organizational climate [2], which, in turn, affects their innovation behavior. Organizational innovation climate represents the situational strength that shapes how employees interpret and respond to decent work initiatives. Actively exploring its role in the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior holds crucial practical significance for enhancing organizational HR effectiveness. The existing literature primarily discusses the relationship between organizational climate and employee innovation behavior at a single level [3, 40], yet whether group differences in organizational-level factors can be ignored requires rigorous empirical analysis.

Therefore, this study incorporates intrinsic motivation, work engagement, and organizational innovation climate as variables, constructs a cross-level moderated mediation model, and examines the underlying mechanisms linking decent work to employee innovation behavior.

Decent work and employee innovation behavior

Decent work is perceived by employees as meeting their needs, emphasizing workers’ fundamental rights and human dignity, and enabling them to experience joy and happiness in the course of work [25]. Such a perception can promote employee innovation behavior from two perspectives. On the one hand, decent work enhances employees’ job satisfaction and work happiness, associated with positive emotions that “broaden the scope of information acquisition, expand the set of ideas attended to, and encourage flexible thinking in identifying patterns and relationships among ideas” [41], thus stimulating creativity or fostering innovative performance. On the other hand, decent work strengthens employees’ sense of belonging, pride, or emotional commitment to the organization, or internal identity perception and organizational self-esteem, making employees more inclined to regard contributions beyond routine work as “part of the job,” thus being more willing to engage in innovation behaviors, such as “extra-role” behaviors [42]. In addition to the overall perceptual effects of decent work, numerous studies have shown that the promotion effects of various dimensions of decent work on employees’ innovative capability are also supported. First, the perception of income fairness has been shown to positively predict employee innovation behavior [17]. Secondly, relatively stable employment can convey tolerance for mistakes, making employees more willing to undertake uncertainty and risk associated with innovation behaviors [19]. Thirdly, work relationships characterized by trust, support, positive feedback, and effective communication not only provide necessary information and emotional support across the various stages of innovation behavior but also promote the knowledge sharing and transformation required for innovation [23]. Fourthly, moderate working hours provide employees with sufficient time and energy to engage in innovative activities beyond routine work [18] and to replenish the physiological, cognitive, and emotional resources required for innovation. Decent work, as a result of integrated institutional arrangements and enterprise management practices, reflects the coordination and interaction of different dimensions, and its overall effect is bound to be more significant than the effects of individual dimensions [11]. Given this, the following hypothesis is proposed in this study.

H1. Decent work is positively correlated with employee innovation behavior.

H1. Decent work is positively correlated with employee innovation behavior.

Mediating role of intrinsic motivation

SDT posits that motivation is multidimensional [43]. Various forms of externally controlled motivation can take different forms. Motivation encompasses six types, ranging from amotivation to intrinsic motivation, according to SDT [43]. Individuals universally require autonomy, competence, and relatedness for health and self-development [44]. Fulfilling these needs in social environments, such as work, stimulates motivation. Decent work satisfies autonomy and relatedness needs through trust, respect, and support [14]. Professional development and job characteristics fulfill competence and autonomy needs [15]. Based on the above analysis, this study formulates the following hypothesis (H2a): There is a positive correlation between decent work and intrinsic motivation.

SDT posits intrinsic motivation as a spontaneous self-integration mechanism and emotional experience [45], emphasizing joy and achievement in work pursuit. High intrinsic motivation levels drive desire for learning, emotional commitment, happiness, and job satisfaction [46], fostering work engagement and learning propensity, thus influencing innovation behavior. Additionally, intrinsic motivation enhances resilience in the face of challenges, thereby promoting innovative problem-solving [46, 47]. Based on the above analysis, this study formulates the following hypothesis (H2b): There is a positive correlation between intrinsic motivation and employee innovation behavior.

Based on Hypotheses H2a and H2b, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H2. Intrinsic motivation mediates the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior.

H2. Intrinsic motivation mediates the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior.

Mediating role of work engagement

Work engagement refers to the state in which employees fully invest their physical, cognitive, and emotional resources in their work roles [48]. According to Bakker et al. [30], work resources “exist at various levels within the organization (such as compensation, career development opportunities, job stability), interpersonal and work relationship levels (such as supervisor or colleague support, team atmosphere), job level (such as role clarity, involvement in decision-making), and even task level (such as skill variety, task identity, significance, autonomy, performance feedback).” These resources are implicit in or closely related to various dimensions of decent work. Decent work can enhance positive motivation by providing relevant resources and alleviating job demands, thereby mitigating occupational burnout and health issues [28]. Factors such as the work environment, job security, democratic participation, and opportunities for career development promote active motivation, whereas perceptions of appropriate labor intensity and a healthy and safe work environment can mitigate job demands and alleviate negative effects [28]. Based on the above analysis, this study formulates the following hypothesis (H3a): There is a positive correlation between decent work and work engagement.

Schaufeli et al. [49] define work engagement as “a positive, fulfilling, work-related state of mind that is characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption,” encapsulating the affective-motivational states required for employee innovation behavior [50]. According to the JD-R Model, job demands deplete employees’ physiological, psychological, or emotional resources, leading to occupational burnout or health issues and thereby inclining employees to avoid positive work behaviors. However, access to relevant resources can offset the costs of job demands, eliciting positive affective-motivational states, including work engagement [30]. As Bakker et al. [30] pointed out, “the higher the job demands, the more pronounced the impact of work resources on motivation or work engagement.” Since innovation behavior requires employees to tackle complex problems, withstand uncertainty about outcomes, and may even face pressure from colleagues due to changes in the status quo, it undoubtedly qualifies as high-demand work. Therefore, work engagement positively affects individual innovation behavior [18]. Based on the above analysis, this study formulates the following hypothesis (H3b): There is a positive correlation between work engagement and employee innovation behavior.

Based on Hypotheses H3a and H3b, this study proposes the following hypothesis:

H3. Work engagement mediates the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior.

H3. Work engagement mediates the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior.

Chain mediation of intrinsic motivation and work engagement

SDT posits that when employees’ fundamental psychological needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are satisfied [29], they experience a significant boost in intrinsic motivation. This is because meeting these needs leads employees to perceive work as meaningful and self-concordant, thereby prompting them to engage in work autonomously. In the context of the JD-R model, intrinsic motivation serves as a crucial personal resource. As a resource, it empowers individuals to allocate cognitive and emotional energy towards task completion [30]. When employees’ intrinsic motivation is heightened due to the satisfaction of psychological needs as described by SDT, their work engagement, which is characterized by vigor, dedication, and absorption [49], is naturally strengthened. The JD-R model further elaborates that work engagement originates from work resources [30] and is propelled by intrinsic motivation, an indicator of positive well-being [50]. When employees find work intrinsically interesting, as SDT implies, they are more likely to be engaged [51]. Moreover, the core components of intrinsic motivation, autonomy, and competence enhance employees’ initiative and enthusiasm, thereby directly contributing to increased work engagement. In conclusion, based on the complementary insights of SDT and JD-R, this study hypothesizes (H4b) that intrinsic motivation is positively correlated with work engagement.

Building upon H2a (H4a), H4b, and H3b (H4c), this study puts forward the following hypothesis:

H4. Intrinsic motivation and work engagement serve a chain-mediating role between decent work and employee innovation behavior.

H4. Intrinsic motivation and work engagement serve a chain-mediating role between decent work and employee innovation behavior.

Organizational innovation climate and employee innovation behavior

Organizational innovation climate serves as a critical contextual factor that systematically influences employee innovation behavior through multiple theoretical pathways [1]. Drawing from social exchange theory, when organizations establish a strong organizational innovation climate, employees perceive substantial organizational support for creative activities, thereby developing a heightened sense of obligation and responsibility toward organizational goals [39]. This psychological contract motivates employees to engage in innovation behaviors that benefit the organization and align with strategic expectations. Conversely, underdeveloped organizational innovation climates generate adverse emotional stimuli that undermine psychological satisfaction and reciprocal desires, consequently diminishing workplace initiative and innovation proactivity.The successful implementation of innovation activities fundamentally depends on robust information exchange mechanisms characteristic of well-established organizational innovation climates [1]. A strong organizational innovation climate fosters psychological safety, enabling members to freely imagine, experiment with novel ideas, and openly exchange diverse viewpoints, thereby enhancing internal harmony and coordinating collective efforts toward common organizational goals [1]. The resource-based view further emphasizes that leadership recognition and encouragement, coupled with adequate resource allocation, including funding, time, and technological support, constitute essential elements that facilitate employee innovation behavior within supportive climates [1].The climate-strength perspective indicates that the intensity and consistency of innovation signals within an organization determine their impact on individual behavior [1]. Essentially, organizations demonstrating stronger and more consistent organizational innovation climates through policies and practices are more likely to provide comprehensive support systems that encourage innovation, thereby strengthening employees’ willingness to innovate [39]. Therefore, we propose the hypothesis:

H5. Organizational innovation climate is positively correlated with employee innovation behavior.

H5. Organizational innovation climate is positively correlated with employee innovation behavior.

Organizational innovation climate as a boundary condition

Organizational innovation climate functions as a crucial boundary condition that moderates the relationship between decent work and innovation behavior through several interconnected mechanisms [1, 39]. From an organizational culture perspective, organizational innovation climate shapes shared values and behavioral norms that either amplify or constrain the innovation-enhancing effects of decent work practices [1]. When employees experience dignified working conditions that generate psychological fulfillment [25], they develop strong reciprocal motivations [42]. However, the translation of these motivations into actual innovation behaviors critically depends on the prevailing organizational innovation climate [39].The situational strength framework explains how organizational innovation climate operates as a boundary condition by influencing behavioral expectations and consequences [1]. In strong organizational innovation climates, employees perceive innovation as a professional obligation rather than discretionary behavior, thereby transcending traditional task-completion mentalities [39]. This cognitive transformation enables employees to reinterpret psychological contracts, viewing innovation as an employment relationship component [42].Resource allocation theory provides additional insight into organizational innovation climate’s moderating function [1]. While decent work enhances intrinsic motivation for innovation [52], its actualization requires organizational support that robust organizational innovation climates provide [39]. The interactionist perspective clarifies that organizational innovation climate strengthens the decent work-innovation relationship by creating alignment between individual motivations and organizational support systems [39]. This theoretical reasoning leads to our hypothesis:

H6. Organizational innovation climate moderates the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior.

Method

Sample and data collection procedures

Prior to conducting the formal investigation, this study received approval from the Academic Committee of the School of Economics and Management at Pingdingshan University (approval number: EA-2025021; approval date: March 15, 2025). Acknowledging that cultural and social variations across regions may influence individual behavioral preferences, this research incorporated samples from diverse geographic regions including Asia, the Americas, Europe, and Oceania in its sampling design to enhance the external validity of the findings. Under the practical constraints of budget and operational feasibility, the study employed convenience sampling and strategically selected seven countries to reflect varying sociocultural contexts: China and Korea representing the Confucian heritage circle, Thailand and Malaysia reflecting Southeast Asian traditions, the United States exemplifying Western individualistic values, the United Kingdom providing a European perspective, and New Zealand contributing an Oceania viewpoint. This approach provided multiple cultural lenses for the investigation.

The initial points of contact for the study were 30 coordinators from the seven countries mentioned above. Each coordinator randomly selected three organizations in their country. Subsequently, consistent with the study’s framework, each coordinator identified multiple “decent” job positions within each organization based on local cultural and social norms, and then randomly invited participants in these positions to complete the online questionnaire. The questionnaire consisted of two parts: the first outlined the study’s purpose and significance to ensure participants’ informed consent, while the second included the variable measures. To ensure conceptual equivalence across the multiple language versions, we implemented a systematic translation approach based on established back-translation principles.

Before the formal survey administration, a comprehensive pre-test was conducted from April 10 to 28, 2025, to evaluate the psychometric properties of the translated instruments across different cultural contexts. The pre-test involved 70 participants, with 8–12 individuals recruited from each target country to ensure adequate cultural representation. Analysis showed that Cronbach’s α coefficients for all measured variables exceeded 0.75, indicating excellent internal consistency reliability. Furthermore, all scale items had factor loadings greater than 0.60, and the average variance extracted for each construct surpassed the recommended threshold of 0.50. These findings collectively confirm that the translated instruments maintained robust construct validity and measurement accuracy across the diverse cultural settings included in this study.

To establish cross-cultural measurement validity, we conducted systematic measurement invariance testing across the seven countries using multi-group confirmatory factor analysis. The analysis began by establishing configural invariance, confirming that the fundamental factor structure was consistent across all country samples. We then tested metric invariance, the results of which indicated that the factor loadings were statistically equivalent, ensuring that relationships between constructs could be meaningfully compared. Finally, we assessed scalar invariance to verify the equivalence of item intercepts, thereby enabling valid comparisons of latent means across countries. The results supported full configural and metric invariance across countries, indicating that the instrument captures identical constructs and permits correlational comparisons. Partial scalar invariance was achieved, allowing for cautious interpretation of mean differences. This rigorous approach strengthened the cross-cultural validity of our findings, ensuring that observed differences are more likely to reflect substantive variations rather than measurement artifacts.

Formal data collection was conducted from May 1 to August 28, 2025. We initially distributed 860 questionnaires and received 747 responses. All respondents provided informed consent, an essential ethical step in our research. After careful review, we excluded questionnaires that were incomplete or contained logical flaws, leaving 701 valid questionnaires. To meet the common data requirements for Hierarchical Linear Modeling analysis regarding data structure, we further refined the sample by excluding submissions from organizations that provided fewer than 5 valid questionnaires. The final analytical sample comprised 686 valid questionnaires from 57 organizations, with each organization contributing more than 5 questionnaires. This meets the basic data requirements for employing HLM in the analysis [53].

Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics of the survey respondents. The sample has a relatively balanced gender distribution (54.4% male, 45.6% female). In terms of age, the largest proportion of respondents (38.3%) falls within the 26–35 years range, with a mean age of approximately 38.6 years. Regarding work experience, the majority of respondents (64.0%) have 6–15 years of experience, and the mean work tenure is about 12.1 years. The distribution of educational attainment is relatively balanced across different levels. Among the represented industries, manufacturing (29.0%) and retail (26.2%) constitute the largest sectors, which aligns with the employment focus of this study. Overall, this well-structured final sample (N = 686) provides a substantive basis for the subsequent data analysis.

Measure

Measurement of decent work utilized a 38-item scale developed by Liu et al. [16], covering 7 dimensions: income, job security, work environment, labor intensity, democratic participation, career development, and social status dignity. Representative items include “The income from this job enables me to lead an economically independent and dignified life,” “I feel that my current job is sufficiently stable,” “My superiors give me enough trust and respect,” “My average daily working hours are adequate and appropriate,” “I have the opportunity to participate in the unit’s production and operation decisions,” “The unit provides me with sufficient opportunities for skill development,” and “I feel proud when my job is mentioned in front of relatives and friends.”

Intrinsic motivation was assessed with Grant’s [54] scale, comprising 4 items, e.g., “ I enjoy this work very much.”

Work engagement was assessed using the UWES-9 [55], which comprises 3 items for each of the following: vigor, dedication, and absorption. Research suggests these dimensions can be aggregated, with mean item scores as the measure [10], exemplified by: “At my work, I feel bursting with energy.”

The organizational innovation climate was assessed using the scale developed by Wang et al. [56], which comprises 8 items, such as “Smooth internal communication channels within the organization” and “Leaders provide resources and channels to help employees and offer support.”

Employee innovation behavior was measured using a 4-item scale developed by Bani et al. [57]. Examples of items assessing employee innovation behavior include “I am constantly generating new ideas and creativity” and “I often adopt new ways to solve problems in my work.”

All items were assessed on a 7-point Likert scale from (1) strongly disagree to (7) strongly agree.

Tools

This study employed SPSS 25.0, Mplus 8.0, and HLM 6.08 for data analysis. Specifically, SPSS 25.0 was used for common method bias tests, Mplus 8.0 for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and mediation effect tests, and HLM 6.08 for cross-level analysis. The methodology and procedures during the cross-level analysis with HLM 6.08 were primarily based on Hox [58].

Common method bias test

To address potential common method bias, this study adopted both procedural and statistical remedies. Procedurally, measures such as balanced item ordering and guaranteed respondent anonymity were implemented during data collection to reduce evaluation apprehension and response bias. Statistically, Harman’s single‑factor test was conducted, and the results showed that the first unrotated principal component accounted for 21.13% of the total variance, which was below the 50% threshold. In addition, the latent common method factor analysis showed that the method factor explained approximately 20.49% of the variance. After adding the common method factor, model fit changed only slightly, with ΔCFI = 0.002 and ΔRMSEA = 0.003. These results suggest that common method bias is unlikely to seriously threaten the validity of the findings.

Basic characteristic test

In this study, organizational innovation climate is a shared construct. Data from individuals across different organizations were collected, and was used to test the appropriateness of aggregation. In practice, greater than 0.70 is generally considered acceptable [59]. Here, the average of organizational innovation climate was 0.94, which met the relevant requirements.

Discussion

The findings provide strong support for the proposed model and show that decent work is positively associated with employee innovation behavior through both individual psychological mechanisms and organizational contextual conditions. More importantly, the study moves beyond treating decent work as a general employment-quality condition and clarifies how it is translated into employees’ innovative actions.

First, intrinsic motivation and work engagement were identified as complementary mediating mechanisms. Decent work appears to enhance employees’ internal willingness to engage in meaningful and self-endorsed work, while also strengthening their energetic and persistent involvement in work tasks. This finding extends previous studies that examined specific dimensions of decent work, such as pay fairness, job stability, work-life balance, employee involvement, supportive relationships, and career development [11, 18, 20–23], by demonstrating that decent work as an integrated construct can promote innovation through multiple psychological routes.

Second, the supported chain mediation indicates that intrinsic motivation may further enhance work engagement, thereby forming a sequential motivational process from decent work to employee innovation behavior. This finding provides a more integrated explanation of the SDT- and JD-R-based mechanisms: decent work may first activate employees’ internal motivation and then convert this motivation into a more engaged work state. However, because the study adopted a cross-sectional design, the temporal order implied by chain mediation should be interpreted cautiously and examined further through longitudinal or experimental research.

Third, organizational innovation climate was found to strengthen the direct relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior. This suggests that decent work is more likely to generate innovative outcomes when employees are embedded in an organizational environment that supports experimentation, collaboration, and idea implementation. Thus, innovation climate does not merely represent a favorable background condition; it functions as a contextual amplifier that determines whether employees can convert decent work experiences into innovative action. This finding responds to calls for greater attention to organizational climate and shared contextual factors in employee innovation research [1, 2, 35, 39]. Nevertheless, this study modeled organizational innovation climate only as a cross-level moderator of the direct relationship. Its potential mediating role or moderating effect on the indirect paths through intrinsic motivation and work engagement was not tested, which represents a limitation and a valuable direction for future research.

Theoretical contribution

First, this study extends decent work research by shifting the focus from single employment-quality factors to the integrated construct of decent work. Previous studies have shown that specific elements related to decent work, including pay fairness, job stability, moderate working hours, work-life balance, employee involvement, supportive relationships, and career development, are associated with employee innovation behavior [11, 17–23]. However, these studies have generally examined isolated dimensions rather than the comprehensive meaning of decent work. By treating decent work as a multidimensional employment condition involving income, security, development, participation, dignity, and social protection [12, 60, 61], this study provides a more holistic explanation of how employment quality contributes to employee innovation behavior.

Second, this study clarifies the psychological transmission mechanisms through which decent work relates to employee innovation behavior. Prior research has often explained the effect of decent work from the perspective of individual psychological needs or positive psychological states [27, 28], but the specific pathways connecting decent work to innovation have remained insufficiently differentiated. Drawing on SDT and the JD-R Model, this study identifies intrinsic motivation and work engagement as two distinct but complementary mechanisms [29, 30]. The intrinsic motivation pathway explains why employees become internally willing to generate and implement novel ideas, whereas the work engagement pathway explains how employees obtain the energy, dedication, and persistence required for innovation. This dual-path explanation deepens the understanding of how decent work stimulates innovation.

Third, the study contributes to the integration of SDT and the JD-R Model in organizational behavior and human resource management research. Rather than using the two theories separately, this study shows that they can be connected within a single explanatory framework. The supported chain mediation suggests that intrinsic motivation may serve as a psychological foundation for work engagement, indicating that the need-based mechanism emphasized by SDT and the resource-based mechanism emphasized by the JD-R Model may operate sequentially rather than independently. This finding enriches the theoretical understanding of employee innovation behavior by showing how internal motivation can be transformed into sustained behavioral activation.

Fourth, this study advances employee innovation research by introducing organizational innovation climate as a cross-level boundary condition. Existing studies have emphasized individual factors such as ability, cognition, values, knowledge structure, and life satisfaction, as well as organizational factors such as leadership, incentives, structure, and culture [2, 3, 36, 38]. However, relatively less attention has been paid to how shared organizational climates shape employees’ responses to decent work. By confirming the moderating role of organizational innovation climate, this study shows that decent work and innovation climate jointly influence employee innovation behavior. This provides a more contextualized explanation of bottom-up innovation in organizations [9].

Fifth, this research provides methodological value by adopting a multilevel analytical design. Previous organizational climate studies have often relied on single-level analyses, which may obscure group-level effects by reducing organizational characteristics to individual perceptions [3, 40]. Using hierarchical linear modeling, this study distinguishes individual-level mechanisms from organizational-level contextual influences and captures the cross-level interaction between organizational innovation climate and decent work. This provides a more rigorous framework for future studies examining how organizational contexts shape employee outcomes.

Finally, the multinational composition of the sample, consisting of 686 employees from 57 organizations across varied national contexts, provides preliminary evidence for the cross-cultural relevance of the proposed model. The findings suggest that the psychological pathways linking decent work to innovation may operate across different cultural environments. Although further cross-cultural comparison is needed, this study indicates that the relationship among decent work, intrinsic motivation, work engagement, organizational innovation climate, and employee innovation behavior may not be confined to a single cultural setting.

Practical implications

First, organizations should strengthen employees’ experience of decent work as a foundation for innovation. The findings indicate that decent work is not only an ethical or compliance-oriented goal but also a practical driver of employee innovation behavior. Organizations should therefore improve employees’ work experience through fair remuneration, safe working conditions, social protection, transparent promotion systems, and opportunities for participation in decision-making. Managers should also promote respectful communication, recognition, and inclusive leadership practices.

Second, organizations should cultivate intrinsic motivation by giving employees greater autonomy, meaningful tasks, and opportunities for competence development. Since intrinsic motivation mediates the relationship between decent work and innovation, managers should design work that allows employees to make decisions, explore alternative solutions, and experience a sense of ownership over their tasks. Clear career development pathways, constructive feedback, and timely recognition of creative contributions can further strengthen employees’ internal willingness to innovate.

Third, organizations should enhance work engagement by ensuring that employees have sufficient resources to invest energy and persistence in innovative tasks. Practical measures include improving physical and psychological work conditions, providing skill-based training, maintaining transparent communication channels, and offering adequate resources for task completion. Managers should also conduct regular check-ins to understand workload pressure and remove obstacles that prevent employees from fully engaging in their work.

Fourth, organizations should build an innovation climate that enables employees to convert decent work into employee innovation behavior. The moderating effect found in this study suggests that decent work has a stronger impact on innovation when the organizational environment encourages experimentation and the implementation of ideas. Organizations should therefore create open platforms for idea sharing, support cross-functional collaboration, tolerate reasonable failure, and allocate dedicated time and resources for innovation projects. Leaders should publicly support innovation and recognize both individual and team-based innovative efforts.

Limitations and future directions

First, the cross-sectional design restricts the ability to examine dynamic relationships among variables over time. Future research should employ longitudinal approaches to better capture causal pathways and temporal dynamics in the linkage between decent work and innovation behavior.

Second, the multinational samples offer initial cross-national evidence. Support for the hypothesized relationships across different settings indicates that key psychological and motivational processes may be universal. However, this study did not examine cultural or institutional factors as moderators. This provides preliminary insights into cross-cultural decent work research and opens the door for future studies to explicitly investigate cultural dimensions as boundary conditions.

Third, while this study identifies intrinsic motivation and work engagement as mediators between decent work and innovation behavior, it does not test potential bidirectional relationships between organizational-level factors and individual outcomes. Future research could explore reciprocal effects over time.

Fourth, the moderator (organizational innovation climate) was conceptualized and measured at the organizational level, primarily testing its effect on the direct relationship between decent work and innovation behavior. However, to fully support a conditional indirect effect, future studies should examine whether the mediation pathways depend on this moderator.

Finally, this study has its limitations as it primarily centers on the perspective of psychological needs. Future research should examine social, structural, and organizational mechanisms. By doing so, it will be possible to develop a more comprehensive understanding of how decent work affects employee innovation behavior from diverse theoretical perspectives.

Conclusion

This study investigates how decent work stimulates employee innovation behavior. The findings reveal a significant correlation between decent work and employee innovation behavior. Additionally, intrinsic motivation and work engagement mediate the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior. Moreover, the study confirms the importance of accounting for group differences and highlights the limitations of general regression analysis in this context. Using HLM for cross-level analysis, it was found that the organizational innovation climate correlates positively with employee innovation behavior and moderates the relationship between decent work and employee innovation behavior. These findings elucidate the mechanisms by which decent work influences employee innovation behavior, offering valuable insights for optimizing HRM strategies within organizations.
