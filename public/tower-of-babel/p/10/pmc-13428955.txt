Homocysteine-induced endothelial dysfunction through mediating endoplasmic reticulum stress
Authors: Xiaoxue Zhang, Shengtao Xiong, Liang Long, Yue Xuan, Hongjing You, Yue Xu, Minyi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEndothelial dysfunction is a key pathological process in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. Herein, we aimed to investigate the effect of homocysteine on endoplasmic reticulum stress in a model of endothelial dysfunction, focusing on glucose-regulated protein 78 mediation by homocysteine.MethodsWe established a hyperhomocysteinemia model in mice to examine the effect of homocysteine on cerebral microcirculation. In vitro, endothelial cells were used to explore the effect of homocysteine on endoplasmic reticulum stress. We conducted a bioinformatics study to investigate the impact of homocysteine on the vascular microenvironment. Furthermore, molecular dynamics simulation analysis was performed to explore the binding mode and stability between homocysteine and glucose-regulated protein 78.ResultsIn vivo, homocysteine increased the expression of vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 and intercellular adhesion molecule 1 in brain tissue, whereas reducing the expression of tight junction protein 1. Furthermore, inhibition of endoplasmic reticulum stress effectively improved endothelial dysfunction induced by homocysteine. Finally, molecular dynamics simulations revealed a critical interaction between homocysteine and glucose-regulated protein 78, which likely impacts binding affinity.ConclusionsHomocysteine causes cerebral microcirculatory disorders. The mechanism is related to homocysteine-induced endoplasmic reticulum stress through regulation of glucose-regulated protein 78 protein function.

Introduction

Homocysteine (HCY; Figure 1) is a sulfur-containing amino acid and an intermediate metabolite in the methionine cycle. Hyperhomocysteinemia (HHCY) is defined as a condition when serum HCY levels exceed 15 μM. HHCY is one of the most important risk factors for cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and peripheral vascular diseases. 1 Similarly, elevated plasma HCY levels are associated with cerebrovascular diseases, including vascular dementia, seizures, stroke, and Alzheimer's disease (AD). 2 HCY regulates microvascular permeability by attenuating GABA-A/B receptors and increasing redox stress, thereby leading to disruption of the blood–brain barrier.2,3 However, the mechanisms underlying HHCY in cerebrovascular diseases remain to be fully elucidated.

Chemical structure of homocystine.

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) plays key roles in protein synthesis and processing, lipid synthesis, detoxification, calcium ion storage, and intracellular transport, and it is essential for maintaining cellular metabolic homeostasis. Endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) occurs when large amounts of misfolded proteins accumulate within the membranes and cavities of the ER. Therefore, the unfolded protein response (UPR) is a cellular adaptive program that enables cells to respond to ERS. 4 AD is a degenerative neurological condition characterized by the accumulation of amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaques, which initiate a cascade of events including neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, tau pathology, oxidative stress, impaired protein clearance, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disrupted calcium homeostasis. 5 Cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) is characterized by amyloid-β deposition in the walls of leptomeningeal and cortical blood vessels. In most cases, CAA is sporadic and exhibits overlap with AD pathology. In both CAA and AD, levels of amyloid-β40 and amyloid-β42 isoforms are reduced in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients, whereas tau levels are elevated. CAA is primarily associated with vascular deposition of amyloid-β40, whereas AD is associated with parenchymal deposition of predominantly amyloid-β42. 6 Notably, Aβ1–40 induces ER stress in brain endothelial cells, which further triggers mitochondria-mediated apoptosis. 7 UPR is regulated by three ERS sensors: PKR-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6). 8 ERS is strongly associated with endothelial dysfunction and reduced nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability in the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease. 9

HCY-induced endothelial dysfunction and ERS play major roles in vascular pathology. HCY can damage endothelial cells via activation of the ER stress pathway. 10 Studies have shown that inhibition of ER stress can effectively alleviate endothelial cell apoptosis induced by HCY. 11 Some studies report that HCY induces ERS and subsequently mediates suppression of intermediate-conductance calcium-activated potassium channels (IKCa) and small-conductance calcium-activated potassium channels (SKCa) channels, leading to endothelial dysfunction in vascular disorders. 12 To date, the cellular mechanisms underlying ERS-associated cerebrovascular dysfunction induced by HCY have not been fully elucidated. In this study, we aimed to investigate the mechanism by which inhibition of ERS improves cerebral endothelial dysfunction induced by HCY.

Materials and methods

Drugs and reagents

HCY (purity >95%, verified by titration) was purchased from Merck (Germany, H4628). 4-phenylbutyric acid (4-PBA) was also obtained from Merck (Germany, P21005).

Animals and models

Male C57BL/6J mice, with the body weight of 20 ± 2 g, were obtained from Guangdong Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Company. All mice were maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle in a temperature- and humidity-controlled environment. Mice were randomly divided into two groups (N = 6): a normal control group (Ctrl) and a model group (HHCY, induced by DL-HCY, 1.8 g/L). After 6 weeks, all mice were euthanized. This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine (ZYD-2024-082) and was conducted to minimize suffering and reduce the number of animals used. Prior to euthanasia, all mice were anesthetized with avertin (MeilunBio, 75-80-9), and brain tissue samples were subsequently collected.

Cell line

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) (iCell Bioscience Inc.; Shanghai, China) were cultured in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM) (C11995500BT, Gibco; China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (ExCell, FSP500; Uruguay) and 1% penicillin streptomycin (Gibco, 15140-122; USA). The brain-derived endothelial cells.3 (bEnd.3) were obtained from American type culture collection (ATCC) (CRL-2299) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 12% FBS and 1% penicillin streptomycin. Cells were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Small interfering ribonucleic acid (siRNA) interference

Based on the Mus musculus (house mouse) Grp78 sequence (gene ID: 14828), three siRNA sequences (Grp78 siRNA1, Grp78 siRNA2, and Grp78 siRNA3) and a control siRNA (negative control siRNA) were designed and synthesized by TSINGKE Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The transfection reagent Lipo8000TM (C0533, Beyotime; China) was used to transfect bEnd.3 cells with mouse Grp78 siRNAs or negative control siRNA following the manufacturer's instructions. Briefly, Lipo8000 and siRNAs were separately diluted in serum-free medium and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the two solutions were mixed and incubated for 20 min to form transfection complexes. The bEnd.3 cells were cultured in medium with Ctrl siRNA, Grp78 siRNA1, Grp78 siRNA2, and Grp78 siRNA3 using the prepared transfection complexes. After 6 h of co-incubation, the medium was replaced with a complete medium for another 48 h treatment. For drug administration treatment, cells were maintained with HCY (500 μM) for 24 h after Grp78 siRNA transfection. Cells were harvested for Western blotting assays. Molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulation

To explore the binding mode and stability of compounds and proteins, MD simulations were conducted on empty proteins and compound–protein complexes. MD was performed using AutoDock 4.2 using default parameters. 13 The published crystal structure of glucose-regulated protein 78 (GRP78) and its ligand (PDB ID: 3LDO) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank (http://www.rcsb.org) and used for modeling potential binding modes. The three-dimensional structure of HCY was constructed using ChemBio3D Ultra 20.0 software. The protein crystal structure used for MD analysis was prepared using PyMOL by removing water molecules and adding hydrogen atoms. The protein–ligand complex with the lowest binding energy was selected, and the structure was exported as the initial model for subsequent MD simulation. The LEaP program in AMBER22 was used to add hydrogen atoms to the system. 14 A 10 Å cubic box of TIP3P water was then added to each model using AmberTools. 15 At the same time, the charges of each model were neutralized by adding counter ions (sodium or chloride ions). All MD simulations were conducted in AMBER22 using the GPU-accelerated pmemd program. 16 The AMBER FF14SB force field was used for proteins, and the GAFF2 force field was used for the ligand.17,18 Missing force field parameters in GAFF2 were supplemented using the antechamber program. 19 Restrained electrostatic potential (RESP) charges of the ligand were calculated at the Gaussian 16 HF/6-31G* level to obtain electrostatic potential (ESP) charges, followed by restrained fitting using the antechamber program. Each model underwent three optimization steps. In the first step, the entire protein and ligand were restrained, and only the solvent molecules were optimized. All protein atoms were limited by an electric potential of 3000 kcal/mol·Å2. The second step involved restricting the protein's backbone and ligand molecules, optimizing the protein's side chains, and continuing to optimize the solvent molecules. This stage of minimization was carried out under the condition of protein backbone restriction (500 kcal/mol·Å2). Finally, the protein underwent 4000 cycles of energy minimization without any restraints. Subsequently, under the canonical ensemble (NVT) condition, a heating process of 50 ps was carried out to gradually increase the temperature of each system from 0 to 300 K. Subsequently, a 100 ps isothermal-isobaric ensemble (NPT) was conducted to bring the system density back to approximately 1.0 g/cm3. Throughout the entire MD process, the cutoff values for van der Waals and electrostatic interactions were set at 12 Å. The Langevin method 20 and the Beeman algorithm 21 were used to control the temperature of the system and to solve the Newtonian equations of motion for the system, respectively. The SHAKE algorithm 22 was employed to constrain the bonds involving hydrogen atoms, with a time step of 2 fs for each.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from brain tissues or cells using lysis buffer (radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) buffer with protease inhibitors), and protein concentration was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (TransGen Biotech, DQ111-01) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Samples were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and immunoblotted with antibodies against PERK (CST, 3192S, 1:1000), nod-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) (Proteintech, 30109-1-AP, 1:2000), acid sphingomyelinase (ASM) (Proteintech, 14609-1-AP, 1:1000), GRP78 (Proteintech, 11587–1-AP, 1:1000), tight junction protein 1 (ZO-1) (Proteintech, 21773-1-AP, 1:5000), tight junction protein 2 (ZO-2) (Invitrogen, 71-1400, 1:1000), intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) (Proteintech, 16174-1-AP, 1:1000), glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) (Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig, 1:10,000), or β-actin (Santa Cruz, sc-8432, 1:1000) at 4°C overnight. After incubation with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated goat anti-rabbit/mouse secondary antibodies (CST), protein bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Monad, PW30601S) and imaged. Band intensities were measured using ImageJ software. Relative protein expression was normalized to GAPDH or β-actin levels.

Cell permeability assays

Cell permeability assays were performed to assess endothelial barrier function of bEnd.3 cells using fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)–conjugated dextran. Briefly, bEnd.3 cells were seeded on Transwell chambers and incubated with HCY or 4-PBA. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with 300 μL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) in the lower chamber and 100 μL PBS containing 0.1 mg/mL FITC-dextran (MW 4000, HY-128868A, MedChemExpress) in the upper chamber. After incubation at 37°C for 1 h, samples from the upper and lower compartments were collected and measured using a fluorescence plate reader with excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 535 nm, respectively.

Immunofluorescence

Brain tissues were embedded in Tissue-Tek O.C.T. compound (Sakura, 4583) and sectioned at 8 μm thickness. Frozen sections were fixed in acetone for 20 min, followed by incubation with anti–ZO-1 (Invitrogen, 40-2200, 1:200), anti–vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (anti–VCAM-1) (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-13160, 1:200), or anti–ICAM-1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, sc-107, 1:200) overnight at 4°C. Subsequently, slides were washed and incubated with corresponding Alexa Fluor-555–conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen), followed by mounting with an antifade solution (Invitrogen, P10144). Cerebral microvascular endothelium was visualized by co-staining with lectin antibodies (Vector Laboratories, 1:100).

Single-cell sequencing analysis

To further investigate the impact of HHCY on vascular diseases, we analyzed an existing single-cell RNA-seq dataset (GSE190220). The dataset was processed through quality control filtering, followed by normalization (LogNormalize) and principal component analysis using the Seurat pipeline. 23 Cellular clustering was performed using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions, followed by dimensionality reduction and visualization using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP). Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the FindAllMarkers function. Genes meeting the thresholds of p ≤0.05 and |avg_log2FC|>0.25 were considered significantly differentially expressed and selected for downstream analyses.

Statistical methods

Data were expressed as the mean ± SD and were representative of at least three independent experiments. Differences between two groups were evaluated using a two-tailed student's t-test. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by appropriate post hoc tests, was used for comparisons involving more than three groups. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant and is indicated by asterisks or pound signs in the figures.

Results

HCY induces cerebral microvascular endothelium dysfunction

HCY is a risk factor for multiple chronic diseases, and vascular endothelial cell injury is considered an initiating step in this process. To investigate the effect of HCY on brain tissue, we first examined the expression of genes associated with endothelial function. ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 are upregulated in various cerebrovascular diseases and are associated with cerebrovascular damage. ZO-1 is widely expressed in epithelial and endothelial cells and is a core component of intercellular tight junctions. It plays a crucial role in maintaining cell polarity and participates in regulating barrier function and signal transduction. The immunofluorescence findings revealed that, compared with the mice in the control group, the expressions of VCAM-1 (Figure 2(a)) and ICAM-1 (Figure 2(b)) were regulated by HCY. Furthermore, the expression of ZO-1 (Figure 2(c)) in the cerebral microvascular endothelium of mice in the HHCY group was decreased. These results indicate that HCY induces microvascular endothelial dysfunction.

Representative fluorescent confocal images and quantification of VCAM-1 (a), ICAM-1 (b), and ZO-1 (c) in vascular endothelial cells of the prefrontal cortex. The endothelium was also labeled with lectin. N = 3–5. Scale bar = 50 μm. *p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, Ctrl vs. HHCY group.

HHCY accelerates atherosclerotic aortas through mediating immune microenvironment

As an intermediate product of methionine metabolism, elevated plasma HCY levels (>15 μmol/L) are considered an independent risk factor for atherosclerosis. To further investigate the impact of HHCY on vascular diseases, we analyzed an existing single-cell dataset. Unsupervised graph-based clustering analysis revealed distinct immune cell clusters in the UMAP dimensional reduction plot (Figure 3(a)). All identified cell clusters were present in the apolipoprotein E (ApoE) ApoE−/− + HHCY group and were divided into six immune cell types, including B cells, macrophages, monocytes, granulocytes, T cells, and fibroblasts. Furthermore, a total of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified, of which 403 were downregulated and 558 were upregulated in ApoE−/− + HHCY samples compared with ApoE−/− samples. Volcano plot filtering was applied to identify genes with significant differences (p ≤ 0.05 and |avg_log2FC|>0.25; Figure 3(b)). Among the DEGs, Hspa5 (GRP78) was upregulated following HCY treatment. These results indicate that HCY regulates the inflammatory microenvironment and induces ERS in blood vessels, thereby exacerbating the progression of vascular diseases.

HHCY accelerates atherosclerosis. (a) UMAP plots showing color-coded cell clusters in ApoE−/− + HHCY groups; (b) volcano plot of DEGs in ApoE−/− + HHCY compared with ApoE−/− groups.

HCY induces ERS in brain

ERS contributes to neuronal death and inflammation through the C/EBP homologs protein (CHOP) pathway in the hippocampus of T2D rats and can also activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in the hippocampus of high-fat diet rat models. 24 Herein, we examined the expression of genes related to inflammation and ERS in brain tissue. The results showed that the expression of the inflammation-related gene NLRP3 (Figure 4(c)) was increased in association with the ERS-related proteins PERK (Figure 4(a)) and GRP78 (Figure 4(b)), which was also increased in the HHCY model. Furthermore, many studies have found that the increase in the level of ASM in brain cells and blood is involved in the occurrence or development process of various neurological pathological diseases. 25 In our study, the level of ASM was also upregulated in the HHCY model (Figure 4(d)).The results indicate that HCY induces the occurrence of ERS and inflammation in brain tissue.

Inhibition of ERS effectively alleviates endothelial dysfunction induced by HCY

4-PBA sodium is a selective inhibitor of ERS. It has been reported to exert protective effects against cardiovascular diseases by inhibiting ERS, oxidative stress, and associated apoptosis. 26 The immunoblotting findings (Figure 5) demonstrate that, when ERS is inhibited, it can alleviate the inhibition of HCY on the expression of ZO-1 (Figure 5(a)) and ZO-2 (Figure 5(b)). These results indicate that inhibition of ERS can mitigate endothelial dysfunction caused by HCY, and the mechanism may be related to interference with the interaction between HCY and GRP78.

Analysis of stability results of GRP78-HCY systems

To understand the stability of the system's dynamic process, we conducted conventional MD simulations on both the protein and complex systems. The root mean square deviation (RMSD) of the two system backbones and the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) of the system residues were calculated (Figure 8(a) and (b)). The RMSD value is used to measure the extent to which atoms deviate from their aligned positions and also serves as an indicator of whether the simulated system has reached stability. Taking the first conformation of the dynamic equilibrium stage of the system as the reference, only the backbone atoms (CA, N, and C) of the system were considered. As shown in Figure 8(a), the empty protein system began to reach a relatively stable state after 40 ns, whereas the complex system reached equilibrium at approximately 20 ns, with the average RMSD fluctuation being <0.5 Å. At this stage, the system's dynamic trajectory was suitable for subsequent analysis. Further analysis revealed that the order of RMSD values for the two systems is GRP78 (2.07 ± 0.30 Å) > complex (1.31 ± 0.11 Å), indicating that the GRP78–HCY complex is more stable than the empty protein structure. The RMSF value reflects the degree of freedom or flexibility of atoms and is commonly used to evaluate protein flexibility during dynamic processes. Larger RMSF values indicate higher flexibility and more pronounced fluctuations during the simulation. We further analyzed the RMSF curves of each residue in the GRP78 protein and the complex (Figure 8(b)). The results indicated that GRP78 exhibits greater flexibility than the HCY-bound complex system. It can also be observed from the figure that fluctuations of residues near the binding pocket are relatively smaller than those in other regions, including residues Y14, E268, K271, R272, and R342. However, for domains located farther from the active pocket and exhibiting greater flexibility (e.g. residues K100–F115 and G185–I195), the fluctuation values of residues in the two systems do not differ substantially. These findings indicate that the presence of HCY reduces flexibility near the binding site as well as across the overall protein, thereby enhancing system stability. Structural observations further demonstrated that HCY interacts with the GRP78 protein, with the interaction site in the ATPase domain. Distance analysis (Figure 8(c)) and hydrogen bond analysis (Figure 8(d)) demonstrated the formation of relatively stable hydrogen bond interactions between HCY and residues Y14, E268, and R272.

(a) The RMSD of GRP78 (blue) and HCY–GRP78 complexes (red) over time during the molecular dynamics simulation; (b) RMSF of the residues in GRP78 (blue) and HCY–GRP78 complexes (red) model; (c) the distance analyses of hydrogen bond atoms between the HCY and GRP78 protein residue; (d) the stable hydrogen bond number between HCY and GRP78 protein over time.

HCY interacts with GRP78 through structural and dynamic interactions

As GRP78 is a key protein involved in ERS, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between GRP78 and HCY. We selected the complex structure at 100 ns as the representative structure and further analyzed its binding mode. The results are shown in Figure 9. As shown in Figure 9(a) and (b), HCY binds to the ATP-binding pocket of the GRP78 protein, and the amino groups of HCY form polar interactions (or water-mediated polar interactions) with residues Y14, E268, K271, and R272. The acidic carboxyl group forms salt bridge and water-bridge interactions with the basic residues R272 and R342. In addition, a CH/π interaction was observed between the methylene group of HCY and the Y14 benzene ring, which further enhances binding stability. The surface ESP map of HCY bound to the protein (Figure 9(c)) demonstrates that the electrostatic forces of the small molecule and the protein are mutually coupled, which is conducive to ligand binding. In conclusion, these findings suggest that HCY may inhibit the enzymatic activity of GRP78 by competitively binding to the ATPase domain of ATP.

Discussion

HCY is an intermediate metabolite of methionine that contains sulfur and is derived from foods such as eggs, cheese, fish, meat, and poultry. 27 HHCY is a pathological condition characterized by elevated plasma concentrations of total HCY. Recent studies have shown that HHCY is an indicator of adverse cardiovascular events in patients with ischemic heart disease and stroke. 1 HCY can induce oxidative stress and ERS, resulting in inflammatory responses, growth arrest, and programmed cell death in cultured human vascular endothelial cells. Elevated plasma HCY levels are associated with cerebrovascular disease and activate matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), resulting in vascular remodeling that may disrupt the blood–brain barrier. 28 In this study, we first established a HHCY mouse model by adding HCY to the drinking water. Consistent with previous studies, we observed endothelial dysfunction in the cerebral cortex (Figure 2). In the cerebral cortex of the HHCY model, the expression of the endothelial tight junction protein ZO-1 decreased, whereas the expression of adhesion molecules VCAM-1 and ICAM-1 increased. As an independent risk factor for atherosclerosis, HCY can cause endothelial impairment and mitochondrial dysfunction in human aortic endothelial cells (HAECs), and drugs can alleviate these effects in HAECs induced by HCY through the Nox4/NF-κB and GRP78/PERK pathways, thereby contributing to the treatment and prevention of atherosclerosis. 29 Herein, we further conducted bioinformatics analysis of genes related to HCY-accelerated atherosclerosis. The results showed that, compared with the ApoE knockout mice, the expression of GRP78 protein was increased in the ApoE knockout combined with HCY treatment group. The findings suggest that HCY may participate in ER activation by regulating GRP78 (Figure 3). Our experimental results suggest that HCY induces ERS, which leads to endothelial dysfunction and thereby accelerates the progression of vascular diseases.

Recent evidence suggests that HHCY is a risk factor of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. In AD, Carey et al. reported that HHCY exacerbates cerebral endothelial cells (cECs) apoptosis, barrier dysfunction, and angiogenic impairment, which were induced by Aβ. 30 HCY has been reported to stimulate the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome and accelerate atherosclerosis progression by promoting ERS, ER–mitochondria coupling, and disruption of calcium homeostasis. 31 In this study, based on immunoblotting results of brain tissue homogenates, we found that high levels of HCY contributed to an increase in the expression of inflammatory factors and ERS proteins in the brain tissue (Figure 4). The findings indicate that the brain microcirculation disorder induced by HCY is related to the activation of ERS. To demonstrate that ERS is involved in HCY-induced endothelial dysfunction, in an in vitro experiment, endothelial cells were treated with a high concentration of HCY and an ERS inhibitor. We found that inhibiting ERS increased the expression of the endothelial tight junction proteins ZO-1 and ZO-2, thereby improving endothelial dysfunction induced by HCY (Figure 5). Inhibiting ERS or reducing the expression of Grp78 can both improve the inflammatory microenvironment and endothelial dysfunction induced by HCY (Figures 6 and 7).

GRP78 can be found in all eukaryotes on the membrane of ER and plays a role in correcting protein folding and assembly and prevents the transport of proteins or protein subunits that are folded incorrectly. 32 We further conducted molecular simulation analysis to examine the interaction between HCY and the GRP78 protein. The findings revealed that HCY can bind inside the pocket and form hydrogen bonds with Arg272, Glu268, Tyr14, Arg342, and Lys271. The molecular ESP analysis of the GRP78 proteins (Figures 8 and 9) primarily reflectedelectrostatic forces of the HCY and the GRP78 proteins are mutually coupled. The RMSF value measures the fluctuation in amino acid residues within a protein, in which higher values imply greater fluctuations. Therefore, the RMSF is often used to assess the flexibility of residues, which can reveal local conformational changes. 33 These findings suggested that HCY and GRP78 can form stable complexes, thereby regulating the function of GRP78. Small molecule compounds regulate their activity by binding to GRP78. 34 Through our molecular simulation analysis, we speculate that HCY not only affects the expression of GRP78 but also regulates its function by interacting with GRP78. When the function of GRP78 is inhibited, it will cause the accumulation of unfolded proteins, leading to an exacerbation of ERS. This results in vascular damage and exacerbates the course of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.

Conclusion

Thes findings suggest that GRP78 plays a key role in endothelial dysfunction in HHCY-induced ERS-related diseases, and that regulation of GRP78 activity may serve as a potential therapeutic target for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases induced by HCY.
