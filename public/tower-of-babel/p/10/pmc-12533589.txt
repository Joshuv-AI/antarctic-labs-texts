Enhanced FOS expression improves tumor clearance and resists exhaustion in NR4A3-deficient CAR T cells under chronic antigen exposure
Authors: Peidi Yin, Jigui Yang, Yiyang Jiang, Linhong Han, Tianchen Xiong, Haofei Liu, Yiliang Fang, Wenchen Ruan, Jiayang Wu, Longjuan Chen, Leyong Tan, Yonglin Zuo, Xin Wu, Fangyuan Mao, Rui Huang, Xindong Liu, Xiu-Wu Bian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The dysfunction of chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells in the tumor microenvironment is a major obstacle to their therapeutic efficacy against solid tumors. Through single-cell RNA sequencing analysis of tumor-infiltrating T cells from patients with glioma, NR4A family genes were identified as closely associated with T cell exhaustion and were coexpressed with dysfunctional genes HAVCR2 and TIGIT. Notably, CAR T cells with NR4A3 knockdown exhibited enhanced cytotoxic activity against tumors, leading to improved tumor clearance and prolonged survival in vivo. However, the promoted antiexhausted phenotype diminished with prolonged tumor burden. This decline in T cell function correlates with the compensatory down-regulation of FOS induced by chronic antigen exposure following NR4A3 knockdown. Overexpressing FOS alongside NR4A3 knockdown robustly boosted the antitumor responses of CAR T cells by skewing their phenotypes and transcriptional profiles away from exhaustion and toward increased effector function. These findings offer a promising strategy for the clinical modification of CAR T cell therapy.

INTRODUCTION

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy has achieved remarkable success in the treatment of hematologic malignancies such as leukemia and lymphoma (1, 2), leading to its investigation in various solid tumors (3–6). Among these, glioblastoma (GBM) represents a particularly challenging target due to its aggressive nature and the lack of effective treatment options (7–10). While early clinical trials have shown promise, the outcomes have often failed to meet expectations, largely because of T cell dysfunction resulting from prolonged antigen exposure (8, 11). This dysfunction, commonly referred to as T cell exhaustion, is characterized by reduced proliferative capacity, impaired effector function, diminished cytokine production, and the up-regulation of multiple inhibitory receptors (12–14). Critically, this state of exhaustion is not fully reversed by immune checkpoint blockade therapies, in part because of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) (15–18).

Recent advances in single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) from both patients and mouse patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models have provided valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying T cell exhaustion. Genes such as DNMT3A, SOX4, and PRDM1 have been identified as key regulators that limit the antitumor activity of T cells (19–21). Through individual knockout of each gene, the authors potentiated CAR T cells’ exhaustion resistance. These findings suggest that genetic modifications targeting such exhaustion-related genes could be an effective strategy to enhance CAR T cell efficacy in the TME.

In our recent study (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/omix: accession no.OMIX002375) (22), scRNA-seq analysis of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and TILs from patients with glioma revealed significant up-regulation of nuclear receptor subfamily 4 group A (NR4A) family genes in TILs, especially in terminally exhausted T cells. This observation aligns with our previous work, where genome-wide analysis identified NR4A1 as a key regulator of T cell dysfunction (23). Complementing this mechanistic insight, Rao et al. showed that genetic ablation of the entire NR4A family genes in CAR T cells resulted in enhanced mouse melanoma (B16 cell line) clearance, directly linking Nr4a-driven hyporesponsiveness to therapeutic outcomes (16). Collectively, these findings led us to postulate that targeted suppression of NR4A protein expression would rescue the dysfunctional state of tumor-infiltrating CAR T cells and restore their antitumor efficacy in human GBM microenvironments.

In CAR T cell therapy for GBM, several cell surface proteins have been targeted in preclinical studies, including human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), interleukin-13Rα2 (IL-13Rα2), and epidermal growth factor receptor vIII (EGFRvIII) (7, 24, 25). Among these, HER2-specific CAR T cells have shownsignificant efficacy in GBM and other central nervous system tumors (26–28). On the basis of this success, we developed a second-generation HER2-targeted CAR to further investigate its therapeutic potential. Specifically, we assessed the cytotoxic activity of CAR T cells with knockdown (KD) of NR4A family genes in both in vitro and in vivo models. Notably, only NR4A3 KD substantially enhanced the antitumor capacity of CAR T cells.

Although NR4A3-deficient CAR T cells displayed a memory-like phenotype during early antigen exposure, they eventually succumbed to exhaustion after prolonged exposure to the TME. To better understand this progression, we developed a continuous antigen exposure (CAE) model to observe the gradual decline in T cell function. In this model, NR4A3 KD led to reduced expression of FOS, which encodes the c-Fos protein—a component of the activator protein-1 (AP-1) transcription factor complex. FOS encodes the basic-region leucine zipper (bZIP) c-Fos, which can dimerize with c-Jun to form the transcription factor complex AP-1 (29). While c-Jun was reported to be the regulator of cell proliferation, differentiation, and transformation (30), the loss of AP-1 activity has been identified as a key driver of T cell exhaustion. In the absence of AP-1, NFAT signaling dominates, driving the expression of exhaustion-related genes and increasing inhibitory receptor expression, ultimately leading to reduced effector function (16, 31, 32). Our scRNA-seq analysis confirmed that cells with high FOS expression also coexpressed antiexhaustion markers such as TCF7 and IL7R, which are associated with enhanced T cell persistence and functionality (33, 34). Furthermore, recent studies have shown that increasing AP-1 activity, either by overexpressing c-Jun or BATF (basic leucine zipper ATF-like transcription factor), can improve CAR T cell persistence and tumor control, highlighting the therapeutic potential of restoring AP-1 signaling to combat T cell exhaustion (30, 35).

Accordingly, we overexpressed FOS while simultaneously knocking down NR4A3. This strategy effectively increased AP-1 function, leading to higher effector cytokine production and enhanced tumor-killing activity in vitro. In a PDX model with GBM, this approach also resulted in significantly improved antitumor efficacy, extended survival, and reduced expression of inhibitory checkpoints compared to conventional CAR T cell treatments. These findings suggest that FOS deficiency contributes to the dysfunction of NR4A3 KD CAR T cells during prolonged antigen exposure. By engineering CAR T cells to overexpress FOS, we rendered them more resistant to exhaustion. This approach represents a key advancement in CAR T cell therapy and offers promising therapeutic strategies for targeting solid tumors, including GBM.

RESULTS

Expression of NR4A family genes is associated with dysfunction of TILs in patients with glioma

Our team previously isolated T cells from 12 freshly dissociated tumors from patients with glioma, along with paired PBMCs from 7 of these samples. In a published article (22), we analyzed the heterogeneity of T cell compartment within TME of five paired patients (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/omix: accession no. OMIX002375). In this study, we used a nonlinear dimensionality-reduction technique, Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), to comprehensively reanalyze the scRNA-seq data and identify key genes involved in T cell exhaustion in glioma. From the 166,495 cells analyzed (84,893 from PBMCs and 81,602 from tumors), we identified 11 distinct groups, including five CD4 T cell groups and six CD8 T cell groups (table S1, Fig. 1A, and fig. S1, A to C). Notably, the CD8 naive cluster was almost exclusively present in PBMCs, while the CD8 exhausted (CD8 Ex) cluster was specifically found in tumors. This was consistent with the high expression of inhibitory genes such as LAG3, TOX, and KLRC1 (20) in TILs, whereas the genes associated with T cell functionality, including TCF7, SELL, CCR7, and LEF1, were expressed at lower levels (Fig. 1B). These findings confirm that TILs in glioma are in a dysfunctional and exhausted state. Moreover, the overall gene expression patterns were consistent across the seven patients with glioma with paired PBMC and TIL samples (Fig. 1C). Compared to PBMCs, TILs exhibited higher expression of exhaustion-related genes [NR4A1, NR4A2, NR4A3, PDCD1, HAVCR2 (TIM3), LAG3, TOX, TIGIT] and lower expression of stemness-associated genes (CCR7, TCF7, SELL, LEF1, IL7R). Notably, the percentage of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells expressing dysfunction markers, including NR4A1 and NR4A3, was significantly higher in TILs than in PBMCs (Fig. 1D). In addition, we compared the up-regulated genes in CD8+ TILs with those identified in exhausted T cells from chronic lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) clone-13 system (36) and mouse tumor models (14). The results showed that NR4A1, NR4A2, and NR4A3 were all highly expressed in exhausted T cells (fig. S1D).

In parallel, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis on CD8+ cells alone from above CD3+ TILs (Fig. 1E). The identified top marker genes for each cell cluster were shown (fig. S1E). Cells expressing NR4A family genes showed the highest average expression intensity in the exhausted (Ex) cluster (Fig. 1F). To address sequencing dropout, we applied the Markov Affinity–Based Graph Imputation of Cells (MAGIC) algorithm (37), which enabled us to reconstruct NR4A and dysfunction-related gene signatures. This analysis revealed a strong positive correlation between dysfunction-related gene signatures and NR4A expression (Fig. 1G). Furthermore, NR4A1, NR4A2, and NR4A3 expression positively correlated with HAVCR2 and TIGIT expression. These genes also showed positive correlations with other exhaustion-related genes, including LAG3, KLRK1, KLRC2, KLRC3, ID3, and SOX4 (Fig. 1H and fig. S1F). To model transcriptional changes as CD8+ functional T cells transition to exhausted T cells, we performed pseudotime analysis (Fig. 1I). This analysis revealed that the six different clusters formed developmental sequences along three trajectories, with the naive T cluster at the earliest stage, followed by memory, effector, proliferation, and exhaustion clusters. Consistent with this progression, cells expressing the dysfunction signature predominantly occupied the end of the trajectory (Fig. 1J). In the high dysfunction signature region of route one, TILs exhibited significant expression of NR4A1, NR4A2, and NR4A3 (Fig. 1J and fig. S1G), suggesting an association between the dysfunction signature and NR4A family genes.

KD of NR4A3 enhances the effector function of CAR T cells and promotes memory formation

To investigate the role of NR4A family genes in CAR T cell exhaustion, we developed HER2-specific CAR T cells with KDs of these genes. The CAR construct, designated HER2CAR, features a human HER2-targeted single-chain variable fragment linked to the CD8α hinge and transmembrane domains, along with the cytoplasmic signalling domains of 4-1BB and CD3-ζ (Fig. 2A). This design allows us to study the effects of NR4A genes on CAR T cell functionality and exhaustion. To infect CD3 T cells with the double lentiviruses, we used an optimized infection strategy (fig. S1A). Following infection, the CAR T cells with gene KD were sorted using flow cytometry (fig. S2, B to D).

To evaluate the effector functions of NR4A family genes during short-term antigen exposure, we first assessed the target cell killing ability of CAR T cells after 24 hours of coculturing with two HER2-overexpressing GBM cell lines, U251 and GBM1 (fig. S2E). The KD of NR4A3 significantly enhanced the killing capability of CAR T cells compared to control CAR T cells (Fig. 2B and fig. S2F), while KDs of NR4A1 and NR4A2 did not show a significant difference. In addition, we used laser confocal microscopy to observe the dynamics of cell killing in real time. NR4A3 KD CAR T cells rapidly eliminated both U251 and GBM1 cells within 20 hours (Fig. 2, C and D, and movies S1 to S12). Compared with the vehicle control, NR4A3 KD CAR T cells significantly proliferated after coculture with tumor cells for 24 hours (Fig. 2E) and released more cytokines [including interferon-γ (IFN-γ), tumor necrosis factor–α (TNF-α), and granzyme B] (Fig. 2F) during this period. The expression of CD44 in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells was lower than that in the vehicle control under resting conditions, but it significantly increased after stimulation with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)/ionomycin (Fig. 2G). Consistent with flow cytometry analyses, NR4A3 KD CAR T cells exhibited increased expression of CD62L, CCR7, and CD45RO, indicating a skewing toward central memory cell formation (Fig. 2H). Thus, NR4A3 KD can enhance the effector function of CAR T cells and promotes memory formation.

NR4A3 KD enhances the ability of CAR T cells to clear solid tumors by delaying cellular exhaustion

Our observations of the enhanced tumor-killing effect of NR4A3 KD CAR T cells in vitro prompted us to investigate whether this effect translates to in vivo tumor elimination. We established GBM1 tumor cells in mice and administered NR4A3 KD CARHER2 T cells via intravenous infusion. The results showed that these CAR T cells were able to suppress tumor growth and prolong mouse survival compared to the vehicle control within a few weeks postinfusion (fig. S3A). However, tumors were not completely cleared, and the overall treatment effect was limited (fig. S3, B and C). According to Mackall’s research (38), a significantly higher proportion of CAR T cells was detected in the cerebrospinal fluid following intracerebroventricular GD2-CAR T cell infusion compared to intravenous infusion. This discrepancy may be attributed to the blood-brain barrier (39), which hinders CAR T cells from reaching the tumor site effectively. To address this, we switched to intracranial infusion of CAR T cells for treatment (fig. S4A). This approach resulted in significant tumor clearance in the brain (Fig. 3A). NR4A3 KD CAR T cells significantly increased mouse survival, with 30% of the mice remaining tumor-free within 2 months (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, although several mice experienced relapse 1 month later, those treated with NR4A3 KD CAR T cells exhibited smaller tumor sizes compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 3, C and D).

To investigate the reasons behind the enhanced therapeutic effect, we euthanized the mice 3 weeks after CAR T cell injection and harvested TILs on day 28. Our analysis revealed that NR4A3 KD significantly increased the absolute number of CAR T cells within the tumor and resulted in a higher proportion of CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3, E and F, and fig. S4, B to D). The population exhibiting a terminal exhausted phenotype (PD-1+ LAG3+) was reduced in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 3G and fig. S4E). Notably, the significant down-regulation of PD-1 resulting from NR4A3 KD played a crucial role here. In addition, NR4A3 KD CAR T cells maintained higher levels of cytokine release, including IFN-γ, granzyme B, and perforin, after restimulation with PMA/ionomycin for 5 hours (Fig. 3, H and I, and fig. S4, F and G). This indicates that NR4A3 deficiency enhances CAR T cell cytotoxic effects by promoting cytokine release. Furthermore, NR4A3 KD CAR T cells preserved a greater proportion of central memory phenotype cells compared to the control group (Fig. 3J and fig. S4H), supporting the idea that NR4A3 KD counteracts the exhaustion program. Collectively, these findings suggest that NR4A3 KD mitigates T cell exhaustion and improves CAR T cell expansion, persistence, and antitumor efficacy.

Contrary to our expectations, NR4A3 KD did not alleviate the exhaustion phenotype in TILs derived from day 35. The expression frequencies of PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3 on CAR T cells showed no significant differences compared to the control group (Fig. 3K and fig. S5). This indicates that while the disruption of NR4A3 may help T cells combat exhaustion within the TME during the early phase, it ultimately fails to prevent exhaustion under prolonged chronic antigen stimulation.

Establishment and validation of an in vitro CAE model to visualize the effects of NR4A3 KD on CAR T cell dysfunction

To dynamically observe the complete process of CAR T cell dysfunction, we established a stable CAE model in vitro, as described in Materials and Methods (Fig. 4A). We performed bulk RNA-seq of CAR T cells to capture the dynamic changes in gene expression. After six rounds of antigen stimulation, CAR T cells exhibited a significant increase in the expression of exhaustion markers (LAG3, TOX, and TIGIT) and natural killer (NK) cell receptors (KLRK1 and KLRC2), along with recently identified dysfunctional genes SOX4 and ID3 (fig. S6, A to C). In contrast, the expression of stemness-related genes (TCF7, SELL, CCR7, and LEF1), effector genes (IFNG, GZMA, GZMB, and IL2RA), and cell cycle–related genes (TOP2A, TUBA1B, and PCNA) was significantly reduced. Next, we assessed how well our model correlates with established in vivo models of T cell exhaustion. A series of genes up-regulated in CAE day 24 CAR T cells overlapped with those up-regulated in exhausted T cells, including key genes associated with T cell exhaustion (LAG3, TIGIT, KLRB1, KLRC1, and KLRC2) (fig. S6D). In addition, there was a significant overlap between genes down-regulated in the CAE model and introtumoral T cells, which included genes essential for the long-term preservation of T cell self-renewal capacity [LEF1, MAL, and MYB (40)]. Notably, genes up-regulated in CAE day 24 CAR T cells also showed a significant overlap with those highly expressed in glioma-infiltrating CD8+ T cells (related to Fig. 1), including several dysfunction-associated genes (LAG3, TIGIT, LAYN, and KLRC1) (fig. S6E). These findings suggest that CAR T cells in the CAE model exhibit exhaustion levels comparable to those in tumor-infiltrating T cells.

Consistent with previous cell killing experiments, tumor cells were completely eliminated after 4 days of coculture with NR4A3 KD CAR T cells (Fig. 4B and fig. S7A). This effect may be attributed to the rapid differentiation of central memory T cells (Tcm, CD62L+ CD45RO+) into effector memory T cells (Tem, CD62L− CD45RO+) following initial tumor cell stimulation (Fig. 4C and fig. S7B). As the CAE process progressed, NR4A3 KD facilitated the differentiation of CAR T cells into a greater proportion of memory precursor effector cells (KLRG1lo CD127hi) and fewer short-lived effector cells (KLRG1hi CD127lo), thereby prolonging T cell survival (Fig. 4D and fig. S7C). Furthermore, NR4A3 KD resulted in lower expression of T cell inhibitory receptors (Fig. 4E and fig. S7D), indicating that NR4A3 contributes to T cell dysfunction. However, the benefits conferred by NR4A3 KD were not sustained under prolonged antigen exposure. By day 24 in the CAE model, NR4A3 KD CAR T cells exhibited a dysfunctional phenotype comparable to that of the vehicle control (Fig. 4, D and E). Although NR4A3 KD does not prevent CAR T cells from ultimately reaching a state of exhaustion, it allows them to display more memory cell phenotypes and improves their status as they gradually become dysfunctional.

NR4A3 KD CAR T cells fail to resist exhaustion with aberrant down-regulation of FOS

To better understand how NR4A3 KD CAR T cells fail to resist exhaustion after CAE, we analyzed bulk RNA-seq data on CAR T cells at various time points during tumor stimulation. Our results revealed temporal changes in gene expression across different durations (days 0, 12, and 24) (Fig. 4F and figs. S7E and S8, A to C). For instance, the expression of many stemness, effector, and cell cycle–related genes gradually decreased over time (cluster 1: SELL, IFNG, and GZMA; cluster 2: IL7R, LEF1, PCNA, and TOP2A). In contrast, some exhaustion markers and NK receptors exhibited moderate expression by day 12, peaking by day 24 (cluster 5: ID3, SOX4, TOX, CD9, KLRK1, KLRC2, and KLRC3). Notably, some exhaustion-related genes [cluster 3: ENTPD1, HAVCR2, CTLA4, and OSR2 (41)] were acutely activated on day 12 but down-regulated by day 24, although they remained at higher expression levels in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells. On day 0, NR4A3 KD CAR T cells showed high expression of stemness genes such as TCF7 and IL7R, as well as bZIP genes FOS and FOSB. Conversely, inhibitory molecules (LAG3) and chemokine-encoding genes (CXCL8 and CXCL13) were down-regulated in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells (figs. S7E and S8B), suggesting that NR4A3 KD enhances T cell stemness before chronic tumor stimulation. However, from day 12 to day 24, the expression of FOS, FOSB, and TCF7 in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells was lower than in the control group, while the expression of dysfunction signatures, including TIGIT, HAVCR2, CTLA4, ENTPD1, KLRB1 (20), and PRDM1 (21), was higher. To further elucidate the biological functions of NR4A3 KD CAR T cells after CAE, we conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) using Gene Ontology, KEGG pathways, and HALLMARK databases. Although the up-regulated genes in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells were enriched in pathways related to positive regulation of activation and proliferation, several pathways associated with negative regulation were also enriched (Fig. 4G and fig. S8D). Next, we identified potential transcription factors that regulate stemness gene expression in CAE CAR T cells. This list included both up-regulated (BCL6, IRF4, JUNB, JUND, and BATF) and down-regulated (EGR1, FOS, FOSB, TCF7, LEF1, and SOX4) genes in NR4A3 KD T cells compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 4H). Among them, JUNB, JUND, BATF, FOS, and FOSB are all bZIP genes. The protein they encode constitutes AP-1, which plays an important role in maintaining T cell activation and resistance to exhaustion (16, 31). Although JUNB, JUND, and BATF were up-regulated in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells, the expression of FOS was lower than that in the vehicle control. This observation aligns with the down-regulation of TCF7 and LEF1, which are indicative of a loss of stemness (33, 42). These findings led us to ask whether the attenuated expression of FOS after CAE is associated with the exhaustion of NR4A3 KD CAR T cells by day 24 in the CAE model.

Single-cell analysis of CAE CAR T cells reveals genes maintaining T cell stemness

We performed scRNA-seq on vehicle and NR4A3 KD CAR T cells at day 24 of CAE. The analysis identified eight distinct clusters among all CD3 T cells (fig. S9, A and B). Notably, we focused on UMAP analysis of CD8+ T cells, which play a crucial role in the antitumor response (Fig. 5A). Top marker genes were identified for each cell cluster (fig. S9C). The CD8+ naive subset was significantly reduced in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells compared to the control group (Fig. 5B). This subset was characterized by high expression of TCF7, CCR7, and LEF1. Conversely, the exhaustion subset, marked by high expression of HAVCR2, LAG3, SOX4, and LAYN, showed a slight increase. Consistent with previous bulk RNA-seq results (fig. S8B), NR4A3 KD CAR T cells on CAE day 24 exhibited a loss of stemness, with lower expression levels of FOS, TCF7, IL7R, and LEF1 compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 5C and fig. S9D). This prompted us to investigate the expression distribution of FOS. FOS and several classical stemness genes (TCF7, IL7R, and LEF1) were predominantly expressed in the naive cluster, contrasting with the expression trends of dysfunctional genes (Fig. 5D). LAG3 and HAVCR2 expression showed a negative correlation with FOS, while they exhibited a strong positive correlation with TOX, TIGIT, SOX4, and KLRD1 (Fig. 5E). In addition, cells expressing FOS had the highest average expression intensity within the CD8 naive cluster (fig. S9E). Further analysis, similar to that described in Fig. 1G, revealed a strong correlation between naive/memory gene signatures and FOS expression (Fig. 5F). Cells coexpressed FOS family genes alongside stemness genes. We also performed pseudotime analysis to characterize the trajectory of T cell exhaustion (Fig. 5G), finding that the strongest dysfunction signatures appeared at the end point of the trajectory (Fig. 5H). In contrast, FOS expression was concentrated in the initial region, where there was minimal evidence of dysfunction (Fig. 5I). This indicates that cells with high FOS expression maintain strong stemness.

Furthermore, we subsequently analyzed gene expression profiles in CD4+ T cells. Consistent with previous data in CD8+ T cells, the CD4+ naive subset was also significantly reduced in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells compared to the control group (fig. S10, A and B). On day 24 post-CAE, NR4A3 KD CD4+ CAR T cells exhibited reduced stemness, as evidenced by significantly lower expression of stemness-associated markers (FOS, TCF7, IL7R, CD27, and LEF1) compared to vehicle control (fig. Notably, FOS and stemness-associated genes (TCF7, IL7R, and LEF1) were predominantly enriched in the naive and memory cluster, exhibiting an inverse expression pattern relative to dysfunction-associated markers (HAVCR2 and LAG3) (fig. In addition, in CD4+ T cells, FOS expression demonstrated a strong correlation between naive/memory gene signatures (fig. S10F). Our analysis demonstrates that FOS plays a similar role in CD4+ T cells in maintaining cell stemness.

Combinatorial KD of NR4A3 and overexpression of FOS enhances the effector function and proliferation of CAR T cells

Although FOS expression in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells was reduced after CAE compared to the control, NR4A3 KD initially up-regulated FOS at day 0 (Fig. 6A). To elucidate the regulatory role of NR4A3 on FOS, we examined FOS expression levels before and after PMA/ionomycin stimulation in CAR T cells. The results consistently indicated that NR4A3 suppresses FOS expression (Fig. 6B). Further, we cloned the promoter sequence of FOS into the fluorescent reporter plasmid. The luciferase reporter assay further demonstrated that the transcription factor NR4A3 inhibits FOS transcription by directly binding to its promoter sequence (Fig. 6C). We hypothesize that the initially up-regulated FOS in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells is compensatorily suppressed during prolonged antigen stimulation, leading to lower expression compared to the control group. This suppression diminishes the antiexhaustion and tumor elimination benefits attributed to NR4A3 KD in CAR T cells. To address this issue, we overexpressed FOS while knocking down NR4A3 (FOS OE/NR4A3 KD) (fig. S11A). c-Fos overexpression concomitantly increased c-Jun expression, suggesting enhanced AP-1 complex activity in engineered CAR T cells. Our results demonstrate that FOS-overexpressing CAR T cells (FOS OE and FOS OE/ NR4A3 KD) exhibited significantly enhanced target cell killing efficacy (Fig. 6D). These cytotoxic effects were further validated in an additional breast cancer cell line panel (Her2-overexpressed MDA-MB-231), demonstrating consistent results (fig. S11B). Conversely, additional KD of FOS decreased the tumor lysis rate of NR4A3 KD CAR T cells, suggesting that NR4A3 deficiency enhances CAR T cytotoxic effects, at least in part, through FOS up-regulation. Given that enhanced cytokine secretion is a hallmark of polyfunctional T cells, we next stimulated a series of CAR T cells and measured the expression of IFN-γ, TNF-α, granzyme B, and perforin (Fig. 6E and fig. S11C). FOS OE/NR4A3 KD CAR T cells exhibited increased frequencies of polyfunctional T cells compared to those with NR4A3 single KD, aligning with the observed increase in cytotoxic effects. In contrast, NR4A3/FOS double KD CAR T cells showed reduced cytokine secretion compared to NR4A3 single KD CAR T cells. Notably, while FOS OE/NR4A3 KD CAR T cells maintained a similar proportion of naive phenotype (Tn; CD62L+ CD45RO−) compared to NR4A3 single KD CAR T cells, they retained a higher proportion of effector (Teff; CD62L− CD45RO−) cells (43) rather than Tcm (CD62L+ CD45RO+) (Fig. 6, F and G). This finding is consistent with recent reports indicating that FOS expression maintains T cell activation but also reduces differentiation into Tcm (34, 44). In addition, while the expansion capacity of NR4A3 single KD CAR T cells was not significantly increased, FOS OE/NR4A3 KD CAR T cells demonstrated higher proliferative capacity after gentle stimulation over four consecutive days (Fig. 6, H and I). To assay the metabolic capacity of T cells, we measured mitochondrial function and mass by flow cytometry using MitoTracker Deep Red FM [a membrane permeable, carbocyanine-based dye for mitochondria used previously to stain mitochondrial mass (45)] (fig. S11D). The results demonstrated enhanced mitochondrial activity across all three CAR T cell groups (NR4A3 KD, FOS OE, and FOS OE/NR4A3 KD) compared to vehicle control. In our CAE model, FOS OE/NR4A3 KD CAR T cells demonstrated significantly reduced expression of exhaustion markers (PD-1 and LAG-3) compared to NR4A3KD CAR T cells (Fig. 6J and fig. S11E). Together, these data suggest that FOS OE/NR4A3 KD further enhances the tumor-killing and proliferative abilities of CAR T cells beyond the effects of NR4A3 single KD and protects CAR T cells from exhaustion during CAE.

FOS OE/NR4A3 KD enhances in vivo CAR T cell antitumor activity by preserving T cells from exhaustion

On the basis of our observations that FOS overexpression combined with NR4A3 KD improves the effector functions and proliferative ability of CAR T cells, we next examined whether these modified CAR T cells would elicit enhanced antitumor activity in vivo. To prolong T cell infiltration time in tumors, we doubled the cell numbers for both the infusion tumor and CAR T cells compared to the previous strategy outlined in Fig. 2 (Fig. 7A). In the GBM1 xenograft model, while vehicle and FOS single overexpression CAR T cells initially controlled tumor growth, NR4A3 single KD and FOS OE/NR4A3 KD CAR T cells successfully suppressed tumor growth over a longer duration (Fig. 7, B, D, and E), which was associated with an overall prolongation of survival (Fig. 7C). Furthermore, FOS OE/NR4A3 KD CAR T cells exhibited widespread infiltration in the local tumor as observed under a fluorescence microscope, indicating stronger proliferation and antitumor capability (Fig. 7F and fig. S12A). These findings suggest that while overexpression of FOS alone does not enhance the antitumor ability of CAR T cells in vivo, combining FOS overexpression with NR4A3 KD substantially improves T cell effector function and prolongs mouse survival.

To investigate how FOS OE/NR4A3 KD affects T cell dysfunction, we collected tumors to characterize the immunophenotype of CAR T cells. Although the proportion of PD-1+ TIM-3+ was reduced in NR4A3 single KD CAR T cells, the proportion of PD-1+ LAG-3+ cells remained unchanged. Nevertheless, FOS OE/NR4A3 KD CAR T cells showed a significant decrease in the frequencies of PD-1+ TIM-3+ and PD-1+ LAG-3+ exhausted T cells within the tumor (Fig. 7G). These collective findings suggest that NR4A3 KD diminishes the ability to resist exhaustion after chronic antigen stimulation due to aberrant down-regulation of FOS. Overexpression of FOS alongside NR4A3 KD can reduce the proportion of terminally exhausted T cells, thereby enhancing the antitumor ability of CAR T cells.

To validate our findings, we further analyzed our scRNA-seq data of TILs from Fig. 1. CD8+ T cells with high FOS expression also exhibited higher expression of stemness genes (TCF7, LEF1, IL7R, CCR7, and SELL) compared to those with low FOS expression (fig. S13, A and B), while expressing fewer exhaustion-related genes, including TOX and TNFRSF9. The highly expressed genes in FOShigh and FOSlow cells overlapped with naive and exhausted cells in the LCMV Cl-13 model, respectively (fig. S13, C and D). Consistent with this, FOS expression was enriched at the starting point in the pseudotime series (fig. S13E). In addition, analysis using the MAGIC algorithm identified FOShigh/NR4A3low TILs as expressing more stemness genes, including TCF7, SELL, and LEF1 (Fig. 7H), indicating that NR4A3 and FOS play an antagonized role in the process of T cell exhaustion during the chronic antigen stimulation. This conclusion is consistent with previously published clinical CAR T treatment data by Hu et al. (46). By analyzing the scRNA-seq data (GSE151310) of CD8+ CAR T cells isolated from the PBMCs of patients with refractory primary plasma cell leukemia, we found that FOShigh/NR4A3low CAR T cells expressed higher levels of stemness-associated genes (TCF7, CCR7, SELL, and LEF1), while FOSlow/NR4A3high cells exhibited increased expression of exhaustion-related genes (PDCD1, HAVCR2, LAG3, TOX, and ID3) (fig. S13F). Furthermore, we investigated the average expression of FOS and NR4A3 in preinfusion CAR T cells and its effects on the CAR vector copy number in peripheral blood at peak expansion, as reported by Dhodapkar et al. (47). scRNA-seq data (GSE210079) showed that patients treated with CAR T cells exhibiting different gene phenotypes had varying peak copy numbers (fig. S13G). With NR4A3 expression as a covariate, the peak copy number showed a significant linear positive correlation for FOS expression (fig. S13H), whereas with FOS expression as a covariate, peak copy number showed a significant linear negative correlation for NR4A3 expression (fig. S13I). Subsequently, we conducted a binary general linear regression with both FOS and NR4A3 expression as independent variables and peak copy number as the dependent variable (fig. S13J), the result proved that FOShigh/NR4A3low CAR T cells could form higher CAR peak copies in patients’ peripheral blood. Together, our results suggest that knocking down NR4A3 while simultaneously overexpressing FOS to enhance the stemness of CAR T cells and prevent exhaustion represents a promising clinical strategy for treating solid tumors.

DISCUSSION

T cell dysfunction within solid tumors poses a major challenge for CAR T cell therapy, limiting its antitumor efficacy and resulting in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes (10, 48, 49). The progressive exhaustion of TILs develops through coordinated actions of transcription factors that enforce exhaustion by altering chromatin structure and gene transcription (12, 31, 35). Our research, along with others, has identified that NR4A KD can help prevent T cell exhaustion within the TME in in vivo models (16, 23). In this study, we demonstrate that KD of NR4A3 in CAR T cells significantly enhances their antitumor efficacy against solid tumors both in vitro and in vivo. This finding reinforces the concept that different members of the NR4A family have distinct, nonoverlapping roles in regulating CAR T cell responses. However, NR4A3 KD alone is insufficient to protect CAR T cells from exhaustion in continuous antigen stimulation. We further characterize the development of a CAR T dysfunction gene signature and the transcription factors driving this transition. In addition, our study proposes an engineered strategy to sustain the antiexhaustion properties of CAR T cells, offering a promising approach to enhance their therapeutic potential.

In our study, CAR T cells demonstrated enhanced specific cytotoxicity and memory cell formation following NR4A3 KD. This is consistent with Labrecque’s findings that NR4A3 limits the generation of central memory T cells while restraining cytokine production during the early response (50). Moreover, NR4A3-deficient CAR T cells significantly suppressed tumor growth and prolonged the survival of tumor-bearing mice, attributed to their enhanced antitumor capabilities and reduced expression of inhibitory receptors. A key finding from our study is that NR4A3 KD increases the presence of tumor-infiltrating CAR T cells with a central memory phenotype (43). These cells have stem cell–like capacities to expand, differentiate, and self-renew (51). Our results align with previous research demonstrating that CD8+ T cells with a central memory phenotype play a crucial role in sustaining cytolytic activity (52, 53). Unfortunately, this improvement is not sustainable. Twenty-eight days after being transferred, NR4A3 KD CAR T cells in mice tumor exhibited exhaustion hallmarks similar to the control group, indicating that NR4A3 deficiency cannot protect CAR T cells from exhaustion during prolonged antigen exposure. To accurately observe the dynamic changes in CAR T cells under CAE, it is essential to establish a model that closely reflects known in vivo biology, rather than capturing slightly different aspects of exhaustion (54, 55). Building on June’s research (20), we visualized the full progression from initial to terminal exhaustion in CAR T cells, highlighting both the advantages and limitations of NR4A3 KD CAR T cells in tumor therapy. Following repeated rounds of in vitro stimulation, NR4A3 KD enhanced memory generation and decreased the expression of inhibitory receptors (PD-1 and LAG-3) in CAR T cells (56, 57). However, with prolonged tumor stimulation, CD127 expression gradually declined, while inhibitory receptor expression increased during later stages. This result indicates that the antiexhaustion effect conferred by NR4A3 KD on CAR T cells diminishes under continuous antigen stimulation.

Through whole-genome sequencing analysis, we examined the trends in gene expression in CAR T cells at days 0, 12, and 24. The data revealed that by day 24, although memory-related and cytotoxicity genes were more highly expressed in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells, some exhaustion-associated genes were also up-regulated. This suggests that the benefits of NR4A3 KD on CAR T cells are partially lost under CAE. Given that T cell dysfunction is regulated by multiple transcription factors (34, 41, 58), we investigated whether other transcription factors might compensate for the decreased NR4A3 and influence exhaustion. Our results showed that NR4A3 KD CAR T cells during CAE displayed up-regulation of several AP-1 (bZIP) family members, including JUNB, JUND, BATF, and BATF3, which have been reported to drive an effector phenotype in T cells, enabling them to secrete large amounts of effector cytokines in tumors (30, 32). In addition, IRF4, known to interact with transcription factors like BATF (59), was up-regulated, promoting lymphocyte survival and differentiation, and enhancing resistance to infections and tumors (35, 60). In contrast to these transcription factors, c-Fos (encoded by FOS), which forms the classical AP-1 heterodimer with c-Jun (29, 61), showed reduced expression in CAE NR4A3 KD CAR T cells. Notably, NFAT predominantly induces the effector program by cooperating with AP-1, or it promotes dysfunction when AP-1 is insufficient (31, 62, 63). The observed down-regulation of FOS in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells during CAE demonstrates a parallel trend with TCF7, which encodes the pivotal stemness-associated transcription factor TCF1. This coordinated directional change reinforces the association between diminished FOS expression and the functional decline observed in post-CAE NR4A3 KD CAR T cells. These findings led us to focus on the specific regulatory role of FOS in T cell function. Single-cell gene expression data from CAE CAR–transduced CD8+ T cells revealed naive, memory, effector, transition, and exhaustion clusters. The exhaustion subset was defined by a robust gene expression signature, including genes implicated in T cell exhaustion such as HAVCR2, LAG3, SOX4, and LAYN, with low expression of FOS. In contrast, FOS was highly expressed in the naive subset alongside TCF7. These findings suggest that FOS expression is associated with maintaining T cell stemness and resistance to exhaustion.

Our previous research indicates that overexpression of NR4A1 displaces chromatin-bound c-Jun, suggesting that competition with all three NR4A family members might interfere with AP-1 formation (23). We further demonstrated that NR4A3 can bind to the promoter sequence of FOS, thereby inhibiting c-Fos expression. This aligns with recent findings that most differentially accessible regions are more open and significantly enriched for the bZIP family transcription factor motif in the absence of NR4A3 (50). Although NR4A3 KD initially increases FOS expression, chronic antigen exposure leads to compensatory down-regulation of FOS in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells. To enhance the antiexhaustion properties of CAR T cells, we overexpressed FOS following NR4A3 KD, counteracting FOS down-regulation and ensuring its effector function under prolonged antigen exposure. Unlike c-Jun, c-Fos overexpression alone has been reported to have no effect on enhancing T cell function (30). However, CAR T cells with simultaneous NR4A3 KD and FOS overexpression exhibited robust tumor elimination and strong antiexhaustion capabilities. Conversely, knocking down FOS in NR4A3 KD CAR T cells reduced their effector function and cytokine release. While the antitumor effect of NR4A3 deficiency is not solely due to increased FOS, our observations underscore the critical role of AP-1 in maintaining T cell activation and function, as previously reported (64, 65).

In comparison to previous studies analyzing the effects of NR4A knockout on antitumor T cell responses in the CAE model (66), our study reveals several critical differences. While we build on the observation that NR4A3 KD enhances CAR T cell tumor clearance and antiexhaustion abilities both in vitro and in vivo, we also highlight the diminishment of these functions due to prolonged antigen exposure, a phenomenon not previously reported. Similar to our findings, Yoshimura et al. demonstrated that NR4A triple knockout induces consistent naive memory and effector-like effects in CAR T cells (66). However, the changes in the transcriptional profile of CAR T cells under chronic antigen stimulation were not fully characterized in their model because of insufficient exposure time. June et al. exhibited gene expression changes in CAR T cells at three time points (day 0, day 16, and day 28) in the CAE model (20), revealing significant differences between day 16 and day 28, which aligns with our findings.

This study has several limitations. First, while we achieved satisfactory results by knocking down NR4A3 in CAR T cells, we omitted the use of CRISPR-Cas9 for complete gene knockout. Since our analysis was restricted to NR4A3 KD and did not include successive NR4A family knockouts, we cannot establish the superiority of the FOS OE/NR4A3 KD strategy over a broader NR4A inhibition strategy for preventing exhaustion. In addition, although we identified and selected FOS from a pool of bZIP genes for further study, we excluded other top candidates from the CAE model, such as FOSB, JUNB, and JUND, from functional validation. This omission weakens our central claim that FOS is the key transcription factor mediating T cell exhaustion, as we cannot rule out the possibility that other bZIP members are equally critical. Last, our observation of dysfunction following NR4A3 KD could not serve as direct evidence of a causal link to FOS down-regulation, leaving the precise regulatory mechanism of this compensatory effect unresolved.

In summary, our work highlights the critical role of NR4A3 in regulating human T cell cytotoxicity, memory formation, and antiexhaustion capabilities during the early stages of antigen exposure. In addition, we present a combined gene modification strategy for CAR T cells that involves NR4A3 KD and FOS overexpression, aimed at achieving durable clearance of solid tumors in clinical settings.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This study aimed to characterize the functional consequences of NR4A3 KD and FOS OE in CAR T cells. All experimental procedures are detailed in the figure legends, including the number of independent samples, the specific statistical tests used, and the corresponding P values. No a priori power calculations were performed to determine sample size. For in vitro assays presented in this study, a minimum of three biologically independent replicates were conducted per experiment, with no data points excluded as outliers. In vivo studies used nonobese diabetic–severe combined immunodeficient (NOD-SCID) mice injected with CAR T cells derived from one or two unique healthy donors. Mice were randomized by cage to treatment groups. While technicians administering injections and performing imaging were aware of group assignments, they remained blinded to the specific treatment identities and anticipated outcomes. Unless explicitly stated otherwise, each experimental protocol was successfully replicated a minimum of three times using biologically independent samples.

Cell line

U251 (ATCC-CRL-1605), MDA-MB-231 (ATCC-HTB-26), and human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T (ATCC-CRL-11268) cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Primary GBM cell line 1 (GBMl) was established from the tumor specimens from patients with GBM (Southwest Hospital), tumor cells were isolated with the Papain Dissociation System (Worthington Biochemical, catalog no. LK003150). Healthy donor PBMCs were provided by the Southwest Hospital, and the CD3-positive T lymphocytes were selected with human CD3 MicroBeads (Miltenyi, catalog no. 130-097-043).

U251, GBM1, MDA-MB-231, and HEK293T cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; Gibco, catalog no. 11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, catalog no. 10099) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. U251, GBM1, and MDA-MB-231 cells were transfected with lentivirus carrying HER2-Luc–red fluorescent protein or green fluorescent protein (GFP), and the positive cells were sorted by flow cytometry to generate an HER2 overexpression luciferase-stable cell line.

Lentiviral vector production

HEK293T cells were transfected with lentiviral and packaging plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. L3000015) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Lentiviral supernatants were collected at 48 hours posttransfection and concentrated using polyethylene glycol, molecular weight 8000 following the manufacturer’s protocol. To generate the lentiviral stocks, the resulting concentrated lentivirus batches were resuspended in cold X-VIVO 15 serum-free medium (Lonza, catalog no. 04-418) and stored at −80°C.

Lentiviral transduction and T cell culture

Primary T lymphocytes were activated by CD3x28 DynaBeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. 11131D) at a bead-to-cell ratio in X-VIVO 15 serum-free medium with 10% FBS and IL-2 (100 U/ml; Wolcavi, catalog no. TL-104). Approximately after 24 hours, T cells were transduced with the lentivirus encoding the GFP-HER2 CAR at a multiplicity of infection of 5 according to the following method. Twenty-four–well plates that were precoated with RetroNectin (12.5 mg/ml; TaKaRa, catalog no. T100A) per well were used to load 1 ml of virus and were centrifuged for 2 hours at 3000g at 32°C. Viral supernatant was aspirated from plates, and 1 × 106 T cells in 1 ml X-VIVO 15 serum-free medium were added to each virus-coated well. Plates were then centrifuged at 800g for 30 min at 32°C and returned to incubators. Twenty-four hours later, T cells were transduced with the lentivirus encoding mCherry–short hairpin RNA (shRNA) or overexpression gene as described above. After 24 hours, T cells were harvested and resuspended in complete X-VIVO 15. Four days after the second transduction, magnetic beads were removed and modified CAR T cells were sorted by flow cytometry.

Cytotoxicity assay

The killing capacity of CAR T cells against HER2 overexpression tumor cells was assessed using a luciferase-based killing assay. CAR T cells were incubated with luciferase-expressing tumor targets at indicated effector to target (E:T) ratios (tumor cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells per well) for 24 hours (cocultured in the complete DMEM). Then, 60 μl of diluted luciferin (4.5 mg/ml) (Promega, catalog no. P1043) was added in each well, and the luciferase activity at that time point was measured using a luminescence microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Percentage specific lysis was calculated as the following: (target cells–only relative luminescence units (RLUs) − total RLU with CAR T cells)/(target cells–only RLU) × 100%.

ELISA assay

CAR T cells were incubated with U251 at E:T = 1 (tumor cells were seeded at 5 × 104 cells per well) for 24 hours. The culture supernatant was centrifuged at 4°C, 2000g for 20 min after collection, and the cytokine concentrations were detected using the following kits: human IFN-γ precoated enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Dakewe, #1110002), human TNF-α precoated ELISA hit (Dakewe, #1117202), and human granzyme B precoated ELISA kit (Dakewe, #1118502).

Time-lapse confocal microscopy assay

CAR T cells were stained with the Vybrant Cell-Labeling Solutions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. V22887) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, the CAR T cells were added at a 4:1 ratio in the 96-well white flat-bottom plates (Corning, catalog no. 3922) with U251 or GBM1 tumor cells that adhered for 2 hours (tumor cells were seeded at 2.5 × 104 cells per well), just before the first frame of the time lapse. Live-cell imaging was performed by the laser confocal microscope (Olympus); the samples were taken every 30 min for a total of 20 hours. Temperature was maintained at 37°C and CO2 concentration was set to 5%, using a live-cell imaging system.

CAR T cell in vitro dysfunction model

Tumor cells were routinely seeded in six-well plates at 1 × 106 cells per well the day preceding T cell adding. Sorted CAR T cells were rested and cultured in the complete DMEM. After 24 hours, the T cell number was calculated and 2.5 × 105 cells per well were transferred to the tumor plates. The plates were photographed four days later. The stained CD3-positive CAR T cells in half of the cocultures were sorted by flow cytometry, and sorted cells were reseeded in the new tumor plates at 2.5 × 105 cells per well. The remaining cocultures were thoroughly suspended by frequent pipetting, and the cell suspension was used for further flow cytometry analysis. This process was repeated for six rounds in total.

Flow cytometry

Cell surface anti-human antibodies were diluted in fluorescence-activated cell sorting buffer (phosphate-buffered saline + 2% FBS). T cell immunophenotyping was carried out using the following antibodies: CD3-BV510 (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #317332), CD3-PerCP/cyanine5.5 (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #300328), CD4-PerCP/cyanine5.5 (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #300530), CD8-BV605 (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #344742), CD62L-PE/cy7 (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #304822), CD45RO-PB (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #304216), CCR7–allophycocyanin (APC, 1/100 μl; BioLegend, #353214), CD27–PB (Pacific Blue) (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #356414), CD127 (IL7-Rα)–APC (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #351316), PD-1–PE/cy7 (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #329918), TIM-3–PB (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #345042), LAG-3–APC (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #369212), IFN-γ–PE/cy7 (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #502528), IL-2–PB (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #500324), TNF-α–APC (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #502912), granzyme B–PE/cy7 (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #372214), perforin–BV510 (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #308120), perforin-APC (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #308111), Ki67-APC (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #350514), CD44-PB (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #338823), HER2-PE (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #324405), HER2-BV421 (1/100 μl; BioLegend, #324420), and MitoTracker Deep Red FM (Invitrogen, #M22426). Samples were then analyzed using LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences), FlowJo software (FlowJo LLC).

Western blot

The expression level of proteins in T cells was measured using the following antibodies: NR4A1 (1/500 μl; Cell Signaling Technology, #3960S), NR4A2 (1/200 μl; Abnova, #H00004929-M08), NR4A3 (1/200 μl; Abnova, # H00008013-M02), c-Fos (1/500 μl; Thermo Fisher Scientific, # MA5-17076), c-Jun (1/500 μl; Thermo Fisher Scientific, # MA5-15881), and β-actin mouse monoclonal antibody (1/5000 μl; ABclonal, # AC004).

Cell proliferation assay

CAR T cells were stained with the CellTrace Violet Cell Proliferation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, catalog no. C34571) following the manufacturer’s instruction and cultured in the 12-well plate with complete X-VIVO 15 medium. T Cell TransAct (5 μl/1 ml; Miltenyi Biotec, catalog no. 130-111-160) was used to ensure gentle and efficient activation of T cells while maintaining high viability. The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was detected every 24 hours. Proliferation index was calculated as the following: (day0 MFI)/(day0 MFI − current MFI).

Quantitative real-time PCR

RNA was extracted after CAR T cells were sorted by TRIzol (Invitrogen, catalog no. 15596026). The cDNA was synthesized with a reverse transcriptase kit (Takara, catalog no. RR047A), and real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed with a SYBR Premix Ex Taq Kit (Takara, catalog no. CN830S) in a CFX96 Touch Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad). The primer sets used for these experiments were listed in table S2.

Vector construction and production

Lentiviral vectors expressing shRNAs against NR4A1, NR4A2, NR3A3, and shNC were constructed and produced by Shanghai Sunbio Bio-Medicine Technology with the shRNA sequences listed in table S3. For FOS OE/NR4A3 KD, the sequences encoding FOS and shRNA which against NR4A3 were inserted into the vector in tandem.

Luciferase reporter assay

HEK293T cells were simultaneously transfected with luciferase vectors containing the FOS promoter region [2000 base pairs (bp) ahead of the transcription start site] and NR4A3 overexpression plasmid (or vehicle control), and then the cells were lysed for 48 hours posttransfection. A Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, catalog no. E2920) was used to measure the Renilla and Firefly luminescence signals, with the value of Renilla luminescence used for normalization.

Mouse tumor xenograft models

Female NOD-SCID mice were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd. and housed at the laboratory animal room in the Southwest Hospital. All animal experiments were performed in accordance with the protocols approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of Army Medical University.

GBM1 tumor cells expressing luciferase reporter and HER2 were implanted into the right frontal lobe of NOD-SCID mice to establish intracranial xenografts as previously described (22). Briefly, 1 × 105 GBM1 or 2 × 105 GBM1 cells in 3 μl were intracranially injected in the right corpus callosum of NOD-SCID mice at day 0. Seven days after tumor cell implanting, mice were treated with CAR T cells. For intravenous infusion, 1 × 106 CAR T cells in 100 μl were transferred intravenously after being sorted by flow cytometer as described (fig. S2). For intracranial infusion, 1 × 106 (Fig. 2) or 2 × 106 (Fig. 7) CAR T cells in 5 μl were transferred into the brain through the previous surgical needle hole. Besides, the same volume of medium was injected as the mock group. Tumor growth was monitored by bioluminescence imaging using In Vivo Animal Imaging System (BioLight Biotechnology) (mice should be intraperitoneally injected with VivoGlo Luciferin (Promega, catalog no. P1043) before detection). Mice were euthanized at the indicated time points or upon the manifestation of neurological symptoms, and xenografts were harvested, digested, and processed. The single-cell suspension obtained was then treated with Brain Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, catalog no. 130-095-942) following manufacturer’s protocol, and tumor-infiltrating T cells were measured by flow cytometry.

Immunofluorescence staining

Tissue sections of GBM xenografts were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, blocked with 1% donkey serum for 30 min at 37°C, and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The primary antibodies include CD3 (Proteintech, catalog no. 17617-1-AP) and CD31 (R&D Systems, catalog no. AF3628). Then, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies labeled with Alexa Fluor 488, 555 (1:200; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (Beyotime, catalog no. AR1177). Confocal images were acquired using a TissueFAXS SL7.1 microscope at 20× objective and magnification.

scRNA-seq analysis

For clinical scRNA-seq data, the gene expression profile at single-cell level of T cells from 7 PBMCs and 12 glioma tumor tissues (table S1) were downloaded from dataset OMIX002375 (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/omix/ release/OMIX002375) in China National Center for Bioinformation. For scRNA-seq data from CAE model, CD3+ CAR T cells were sorted from the coculture plate at day 24 by flow cytometer. The scRNA-seq libraries were generated using the Chromium Single Cell 3’ Library and Bead Kit v.2 (10x Genomics). After reverse transcription, the gel beads within the emulsion were lysed. The barcoded cDNA was then extracted and subjected to PCR amplification. Through fragmentation, end repair, and A-tailing processes, unique sample indices were incorporated during the indexing PCR. The resultant purified libraries underwent sequencing on a NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina).

Alignment to the human genome GRCh38, filtering, barcode counting, and unique molecular identifier counting were performed by using Cell Ranger (version 8.0.1). Matriculated data were further analyzed using Seurat (version 5.0.1). Cells with percentage of mitochondrial genes more than 10% or no CD3 reads were excluded. For data from OMIX002375, cells extracted from different patients were integrated via R package harmony (version 1.2.0). For data from CAE CAR T in this research, cells from different plates were integrated using RPCAIntegration. Clustering was performed by FindClusters. CD3+ cells were clustered into CD4+ T cells and CD8+ T cells. For OMIX002375, γδ T cells were also separated. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were subsequently subclustered into different developmental stages using T cell markers summarized by Zhang et al. (67). To illustrate the interaction between interested genes, MAGIC was introduced for denoising and imputation (37). Cells exhibiting expression levels of a particular gene above its median threshold was isolated, and, subsequently, the correlation of this gene with the expression levels of other genes within this subset of cells was computed. Pseudotime and trajectory for CD8+ T cells were analyzed using R package slingshot (version 2.7.0) (68). Dysfunctional signature score was calculated by AddModuleScore from Seurat v5 and was demonstrated along with the trajectory (table S4). The significance of high expression within the cluster 2 CD8+ Tex was calculated via χ2 test.

Data from the GSE210079 dataset were used for clinical validation, specifically examining infusion product samples. The unique molecular identifier (UMI) matrix was analyzed using ScanPy (version 1.10.3), with cells exhibiting mitochondrial gene expression greater than 20% or ribosomal gene expression less than 5% excluded from the analysis. Cross-sample integration was performed using BBKNN (version 1.6.0). T cells expressing CD3D+ and CD3E+ were isolated, and the average expression levels of FOS and NR4A3 for each sample were computed. A general linear model was applied to assess the relationship between the peak copies of CAR T cells and the average expression levels of these genes using the statsmodels package (version 0.14.4). For further clinical validation, data from the GSE151310 dataset were downloaded, focusing on CD8+ CAR T cells from the day 15 remission phase. This dataset was also analyzed with ScanPy. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted between the FOShigh/FOSlow and NR4A3high/NR4A3low groups using the rank_genes_groups function in ScanPy.

Bulk RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, catalog no. 15596026) from sorted CD3+ T cells at CAE days 0, 12, and 24. RNA library preparation was conducted by using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Then, the library sequencing was carried out on the Hiseq 2500 system (paired-end 150 bp). Reads were aligned to the human genome (GRCh38) with Hisat2 (version 2.2.1).

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between each time point and between each group were calculated via R package Limma (version 3.58.1). The DEGs were then intersected with DEGs from Gene Expression Omnibus datasets GSE41867 and GSE229611. The statistical significance was calculated by hypergeometric test (table S5). GSEA was performed using R package clusterProfiler (version 4.10.0).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism (version 10.1; GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Analyses required a minimum of three replicates. Significance was defined as P < 0.05 for all experiments. Comparisons between two groups used paired or unpaired t tests according to experimental design. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test. Survival data were assessed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.
