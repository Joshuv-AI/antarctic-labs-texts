Omega-3 fatty acid supplement use among saudi adults: prevalence, independent associations, and knowledge assessment — a cross-sectional survey
Authors: Nawaf W. Alruwaili, Bader Aljarallah, Aljazi Bin Zarah, Nora Alafif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOmega-3 fatty acids (n-3 FAs) are essential polyunsaturated fatty acids with well-documented cardiovascular, cognitive, and anti-inflammatory benefits. Although global consumption of supplements is rising rapidly, the sociodemographic and lifestyle determinants of n-3 FA supplement uptake in Saudi Arabia remain incompletely characterized. This study aimed to: (i) estimate the prevalence and patterns of n-3 FA supplement ever-use across Saudi adult demographic strata; (ii) identify barriers among non-users and conditions facilitating future uptake; (iii) assess knowledge and attitudes toward n-3 FA supplements among ever-users; and (iv) determine independent associations with supplement use.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted between January and March 2025. A bilingual (Arabic/English) self-administered online questionnaire adapted from a previously validated instrument (see Additional file 2) was distributed via social media and community networks to 321 adults (≥ 20 years) across all five Saudi geographic regions. A 10-item knowledge and attitudes scale demonstrated good validity (item-total r = 0.516–0.872; all p ≤ 0.003) and reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.84). Descriptive statistics, chi-square tests with Cramér’s V effect sizes, and binary logistic regression were used for analysis.ResultsThe prevalence of n-3 FA supplement ever-use was 39.3% (126/321). Age, education, employment, settlement type, marital status, smoking status, physical activity, and quality of life were significantly associated with ever-use (all p ≤ 0.026). In multivariate analysis, physical activity (OR = 1.89, 95% CI 1.37–2.61, p < 0.001), single marital status (OR = 3.00, 95% CI 1.07–8.39, p = 0.036), and married status (OR = 2.08, 95% CI 1.003–4.33, p = 0.049) were significant independent associations. Gender and monthly income were not associated with ever-use, suggesting relative equity in access across demographic strata. Cardiovascular and cognitive health were the leading motivations; supplement aversion (23.6%), lack of interest (22.6%), and insufficient information (22.1%) were the dominant barriers. Overall, 50.8% of non-users reported willingness to initiate supplementation upon a healthcare professional’s recommendation.ConclusionsPhysical activity and marital status are the primary independent associations with ever-use of n-3 FA supplements among Saudi adults. High non-user willingness to supplement under professional guidance, combined with 46.0% of ever-users not disclosing supplement use to their healthcare provider, underscores the central role of clinicians in promoting evidence-based, safe supplementation. These findings provide an empirical foundation for culturally tailored public health strategies in Saudi Arabia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28384-z.

Background

Few nutritional compounds have attracted as sustained a level of scientific and public attention as omega-3 fatty acids. Beginning with Bang and Dyerberg’s landmark observation that the exceptionally low cardiovascular mortality observed among Greenlandic Inuit in the 1970s was attributable to their marine-food-rich diet [1], the n-3 FA evidence base has expanded substantially. Three members are physiologically paramount: alpha-linolenic acid (ALA; 18:3n-3), a plant-derived essential fatty acid present in linseed, soybean, and canola oils; eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA; 20:5n-3); and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA; 22:6n-3), the latter two representing long-chain marine-sourced forms that mediate most observed clinical benefits.

Endogenous elongation of ALA to EPA and DHA is metabolically constrained, with reported conversion rates not typically exceeding 5–15% [2, 3]; direct provision through oily fish consumption or supplementation therefore represents the principal means of sustaining adequate tissue concentrations.

The clinical evidence supporting EPA and DHA is extensive. From a cardiometabolic perspective, these long-chain PUFAs lower fasting triglyceride concentrations, reduce platelet aggregability, and exert modest antihypertensive effects, contributing to reduced cardiovascular risk in specific patient populations; this evidence base underlies the American Heart Association’s recommendation of n-3 FA supplementation specifically for patients with established CVD and hypertriglyceridemia [4, 5]. DHA constitutes a substantial proportion of the fatty acid composition of the cerebral cortex and serves as the dominant structural lipid of photoreceptor membranes; adequate supply is accordingly linked to neuronal membrane integrity, synaptic plasticity, and a reduced trajectory of age-related cognitive deterioration, including Alzheimer’s disease, though findings from randomized trials remain inconsistent [6, 7]. EPA additionally acts as a competitive substrate within the arachidonic acid cascade, limiting the generation of pro-inflammatory series-2 prostaglandins and series-4 leukotrienes, moderating chronic low-grade inflammation in conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease [8].

Global recognition of these health-promoting properties has fueled remarkable expansion in the n-3 FA supplement market, which exceeded USD 2 billion in 2023 with year-on-year growth exceeding 20% [9]. Saudi Arabia presents a distinctive context in which this trend is amplified by local dietary realities: the national diet has historically centered on meat, rice, and dairy products, with comparatively limited intake of marine fish — the richest dietary source of EPA and DHA — creating a meaningful gap between recommended and actual long-chain n-3 FA consumption [10]. Against this backdrop, the Saudi n-3 FA supplement segment is forecast to expand from USD 27.8 million in 2023 to USD 44.3 million by 2030 [11].

Despite this upward trajectory, n-3 FA supplement uptake in Saudi Arabia remains heterogeneous and is shaped by intersecting sociodemographic and cultural forces. Published factors associated with dietary supplement use in the Saudi context include gender, age, educational attainment, socioeconomic status, and healthcare professional recommendation [12–14]. A preference for natural or herbal alternatives to pharmaceutical products, along with questions about halal certification, further shapes supplementation behavior in this setting [10].

Healthcare professionals themselves exert substantial influence: among physicians across Middle Eastern countries, approximately 59% actively use n-3 FA supplements, and close to half of supplement users in some Saudi regions cite professional recommendation as their initiating trigger [15, 16].

Published studies examining knowledge and attitudes (KA) regarding n-3 FA supplements in Saudi Arabia have primarily been conducted among university students [13, 17], female populations [12], and primary healthcare attendees [16], leaving geographically diverse data across demographic groups scarce. No study to date has examined ever-use across all five Saudi geographic regions using logistic regression with standardized effect-size reporting (literature search conducted via PubMed, Google Scholar, and Saudi Digital Library, up to December 2024). The present study therefore aimed to: (i) estimate the prevalence and characterize the patterns of n-3 FA supplement ever-use across Saudi adult demographic strata; (ii) identify barriers among non-users and conditions that would facilitate future uptake; (iii) assess KA toward n-3 FA supplements among ever-users; and (iv) determine independent sociodemographic and lifestyle associations with ever-use. The findings are intended to provide an empirical foundation for evidence-based public health strategies in Saudi Arabia and the broader Middle East.

Methods

Study design and participants

A cross-sectional survey was conducted between January and March 2025. Ethical approval was obtained from the Subcommittee for Human and Social Research Ethics at King Saud University (IRB approval no. 25–093) prior to the commencement of data collection. A bilingual (Arabic and English) self-administered questionnaire was distributed online via Google Forms through social media platforms (WhatsApp groups and X [formerly Twitter]) and informal community networks. To minimize duplicate responses, Google Forms was configured to allow only one submission per Google account; no participant incentives were provided. Participants were eligible if they were Saudi residents aged ≥ 20 years. Individuals below this age threshold or not residing in the Kingdom at the time of data collection were excluded. All participants provided informed electronic consent prior to completing the survey. Participation was entirely voluntary.

The final sample consisted of 321 completed responses drawn from a geographically diverse convenience sample across all five Saudi geographic regions. The questionnaire was configured to require a response on all sociodemographic and lifestyle items before submission; accordingly, no missing data were present on any predictor variable (n = 321 complete cases for all bivariate and logistic regression analyses). For the knowledge and attitudes (KA) module in Domain 3, individual items could be left unselected; n valid per KA item therefore ranged from 121 to 123 of 123 ever-users (see Table 6 footnote). No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed, as this was an exploratory survey; the sample was determined by feasibility and response rate during data collection. The absence of an a priori sample size calculation is acknowledged as a limitation. For reference, the 95% confidence intervals around the primary logistic regression estimates (e.g., physical activity OR 1.89, 95% CI 1.37–2.61) indicate adequately precise point estimates; caution is nonetheless warranted for subgroup analyses with smaller cell sizes. Reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies [18] (see Additional file 1).

Questionnaire structure and validation

The questionnaire was adapted from the instrument of Hitl et al. [19]; an English-language version is provided as Additional file 2. Domains 2, 3, and 4 — covering lifestyle variables, ever-user supplement-use patterns (including the KA module), and never-user barriers and enabling factors — were adapted from the corresponding sections of that instrument; Domain 1 comprised standard sociodemographic items developed specifically for the Saudi context. Item adaptations were limited to: replacing EUR-denominated income brackets with SR equivalents, replacing Balkan geographic references with Saudi regional names, and removing sections not relevant to the primary research questions (e.g., alcohol consumption, pediatric, and pregnancy sub-modules). The item text of Domains 2–4 was otherwise preserved from the original instrument.

In total, the questionnaire comprised four domains. Domain 1 captured sociodemographic characteristics: gender, age group, educational level, employment status, monthly income, geographic region, settlement type (urban, suburban, rural), and marital status. Domain 2 assessed health-related lifestyle variables: tobacco use, caffeinated beverage consumption, self-rated physical activity level (low, satisfactory, moderate, or intensive), and perceived quality of life (below average, average, very good, or excellent). Domain 3 was directed to ever-users (current or former supplement users) and elicited information on the type and brand of n-3 FA supplement used, reasons for use (multiple-choice, multiple selections permitted), frequency and duration of use, dose selection method, source of recommendation, place of purchase, and whether supplement use had been disclosed to a physician or dietitian. Domain 4 was directed to never-users and explored reasons for non-use and conditions that would encourage future supplementation. Because the KA module was embedded in Domain 3, knowledge and attitudes were assessed only among ever-users; no comparative KA data were collected from never-users.

The 10-item KA module comprised five-point Likert-scale items (1 = completely disagree to 5 = completely agree) assessing perceived naturalness, safety, efficacy, and practical use parameters of n-3 FA supplements (Table 1). Items were adapted from the validated instrument developed by Hitl et al. [19], which was specifically designed to assess n-3 FA supplement KA in cross-sectional survey settings. Cross-cultural adaptation followed standard guidelines: items were translated into Arabic by two independent bilingual health professionals; the Arabic version was back-translated into English by a third independent translator; discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The adapted instrument was subsequently culturally reviewed for appropriateness in the Saudi context by a panel of three nutrition faculty members. Content validity was evaluated using a pilot sample of 30 adults (excluded from the main analysis); item-total Pearson correlation coefficients ranged from r = 0.516 to r = 0.872 (all p ≤ 0.003), confirming adequate internal consistency. Internal reliability was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) [20].

Statistical analysis

Data were organized in Microsoft Excel (v2019) and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (v26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. The primary outcome was n-3 FA supplement ever-use (current or former use, binary: yes/no). Bivariate associations between sociodemographic/lifestyle variables and ever-use were evaluated using Pearson chi-square (χ²) tests; Fisher’s exact test was applied for the gender variable, where expected cell frequencies were verified. Effect sizes were quantified as Cramér’s V (small: 0.10–0.20; medium: 0.21–0.40; large: > 0.40). Binary logistic regression (enter method) was used to identify independent associations with ever-use, with theoretically plausible sociodemographic and lifestyle variables entered simultaneously. Ordinal predictors (age, education, income, physical activity, quality of life) were modeled as linear ordered terms; a likelihood-ratio test comparing this parameterization against a fully dummy-coded specification confirmed that the linear assumption was adequate for the primary predictor, physical activity (χ²(2) = 4.46, p = 0.108). Employment status and smoking status were initially evaluated but excluded from the final model following a likelihood-ratio test showing that their addition did not significantly improve model fit (χ²(4) = 3.53, p = 0.474) and given the moderate collinearity between employment status and age (Spearman r = 0.56, p < 0.001). Divorced (n = 38) and widowed (n = 37) participants were combined as the reference category for marital status because their similar ever-use rates (15.8% and 18.9%, respectively) supported grouping, and because treating them as separate categories with fewer than 40 observations each would have produced unstable regression estimates. Ordinal predictors were coded to reflect natural ordering (age: 1–5; education: 1–6; income: 1–5; physical activity: 1–4; quality of life: 1–4); binary dummy variables were created for gender, urban settlement, and marital status categories. Model fit was assessed by the omnibus likelihood ratio test, Nagelkerke R², and the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (10 groups, df = 8). Odds ratios (ORs) are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). KA item-level means and standard deviations (SDs) were computed for ever-users. All tests were two-tailed; statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied, given the exploratory nature of the study; bivariate chi-square findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Results

Sociodemographic profile of participants

A total of 321 adults completed the survey: 171 males (53.3%) and 150 females (46.7%). The modal age group was 30–39 years (n = 99; 30.8%), followed by 20–29 years (n = 74; 23.1%), 40–49 years (n = 64; 19.9%), 50–59 years (n = 44; 13.7%), and ≥ 60 years (n = 40; 12.5%). Most participants held at least a bachelor’s degree (n = 120; 37.4%), and nearly half were employed (n = 158; 49.2%). Monthly income was concentrated in the lowest bracket (< 5000 SR/month, n = 143; 44.5%). Most participants were married (n = 195; 60.7%) and resided in urban settings (n = 174; 54.2%). Participants were distributed across all five Saudi regions, with the Central Region contributing the largest share (n = 104; 32.4%). Prevalence and patterns of n-3 FA supplement ever-use

The survey instrument did not distinguish between current and former users; ‘ever-use’ therefore encompasses both, and findings should be interpreted in this context. Overall, 126 of 321 participants (39.3%) reported ever-use (current or former use) of n-3 FA supplements. Among ever-users, consumption was frequent: 42 (33.3%) supplemented every day, 46 (36.5%) two or more times per week (combined high-frequency rate 69.8%), 16 (12.7%) weekly, 13 (10.3%) fortnightly, and 9 (7.1%) monthly. Duration was predominantly short-term: 67 ever-users (53.2%) had supplemented for less than three months, 37 (29.4%) for three to six months, 21 (16.7%) for six to twelve months, and only one user (0.8%) for longer than twelve months. This pattern suggests that n-3 FA supplementation represents a trial-phase rather than an entrenched long-term behavior for the majority of ever-users.

Regarding dose selection, 57 ever-users (45.2%) adhered to a recommended dose, 33 (26.2%) followed the manufacturer’s labeling, 21 (16.7%) used supplements without a defined regimen, 12 (9.5%) determined the dose from the literature or internet, and 3 (2.4%) specified other approaches. Healthcare professionals (physicians, dietitians, pharmacists, or dentists) were the most common recommendation source (52 users, 41.3%), followed by close contacts (32, 25.4%), self-decision (31, 24.6%), advertisement (9, 7.1%), and other sources (2, 1.6%).

Supplements were purchased predominantly from pharmacies (81, 64.3%), online platforms (50, 39.7%), and supplement stores (11, 8.7%); multiple channels could be selected. Notably, 58 of 126 ever-users (46.0%) had not informed their physician or dietitian of their supplement use, representing a clinically significant disclosure gap.

Reasons for ever-use and never-use, and enabling factors

Cardiovascular health (35.7%), immune function (31.7%), and brain/cognitive health (31.0%) were the leading motivations among ever-users (Table 3). Among never-users (n = 195), the most common barriers were supplement aversion (23.6%), lack of interest (22.6%), and insufficient information (22.1%). Religious concerns were rare (1.0%), likely reflecting that n-3 FA supplements derived from non-porcine, halal-certified marine sources are widely available in Saudi Arabia, thereby reducing perceived religious barriers. Crucially, 50.8% of never-users (n = 99) indicated they would initiate supplementation upon healthcare professional recommendation, making this the single most powerful enabling factor identified. Additional enabling conditions were health necessity (30.8%), lower price (20.0%), and confirmation of safety or effectiveness (16.9% each).

Bivariate associations with n-3 FA supplement ever-use

Eight of twelve variables were significantly associated with ever-use in bivariate analysis (Table 4). Physical activity showed the strongest association (Cramér’s V = 0.352, medium-to-large effect), with a striking dose-response gradient: ever-use rates were 16.5% among those with low activity, 38.2% among those with satisfactory activity, 45.8% among those with moderate activity, and 80.8% among intensive exercisers — a five-fold difference across the spectrum (Fig. 1). Marital status (V = 0.283) showed similarly strong associations: single individuals had the highest ever-use rate (60.8%), compared with 42.1% among married, 18.9% among widowed, and 15.8% among divorced participants. Age was also strongly associated (V = 0.284), with ever-use declining from 60.8% in the youngest cohort (20–29 years) to 15.0% among those ≥ 60 years. Regarding gender, ever-use rates were statistically identical between males (39.2%) and females (39.3%; χ² = 0.000, p = 1.000), and monthly income was not associated with ever-use across any bracket (p = 0.885).

Knowledge and attitudes among ever-users

The KA module was completed by 123 of 126 ever-users (97.6%). Item-level results with mean scores and standard deviations are reported in Table 6. Since the KA module was administered only to ever-users, no between-group comparison with never-users’ knowledge levels are possible; any inference about differential knowledge across supplement-use groups cannot be drawn from this study. The overall mean KA score was 3.44 ± 0.55 out of 5 (range: 1.60–5.00), indicating a moderately positive orientation toward n-3 FA supplements. Perceptions of naturalness (mean 4.15 ± 0.92; 81.1% agreement) and efficacy (mean 4.06 ± 0.92; 78.7% agreement) were the most strongly endorsed items. Suitability for the elderly was also positively perceived (mean 3.88 ± 0.92; 64.8% agreement).

In contrast, perceived safety showed a striking bimodal distribution (mean 3.07 ± 1.47): while 46.3% agreed that n-3 FAs are free of adverse effects, 22.3% completely disagreed, reflecting genuine heterogeneity — likely confusion between standard-dose safety and dose-dependent risks [21]. The overall mean KA score of 3.44/5 was closely concordant with the 3.52/5 reported by Hitl et al. Discussion

This geographically diverse cross-sectional survey provides the most comprehensive nationally distributed assessment to date of n-3 FA supplement ever-use, patterns, enabling factors, and KA among Saudi adults. The headline finding — a 39.3% ever-use prevalence — represents a meaningful population-level exposure to n-3 FA supplementation. For appropriate contextual comparison, a broader national survey of all dietary supplement categories in Saudi Arabia reported a combined ever-use rate of approximately 52.7% [14]; our lower figure is expected, as our survey captured omega-3 specifically rather than all supplement types. Internationally, our figure is modestly above the 34.2% documented in a comparable Balkan cross-sectional study [19] and close to the 38.1% reported in Asian countries [22], while substantially exceeding the 23–25% observed in a primary healthcare setting in Abha City, Saudi Arabia [16]. The higher rate in the present study likely reflects our broader online sampling frame, which over-represents younger and more health-engaged adults, rather than a genuine population-level difference.

The most striking finding is the near-monotonic five-fold gradient in ever-use between sedentary adults (16.5%) and intensive exercisers (80.8%), with physical activity showing the strongest independent association in multivariate analysis (OR = 1.89 per activity-level increment). This pattern is consistent across diverse populations [19, 22, 23] and reflects the well-characterized health-conscious behavior cluster in which regular exercise co-occurs with proactive nutritional supplementation. From a clinical standpoint, this gradient reveals a critical paradox: sedentary adults — who carry the greatest cardiovascular and metabolic burden and arguably stand to gain the most from n-3 FA supplementation [4, 5] — are the least likely to use it. Conversely, intensive exercisers who frequently supplement may do so primarily for performance rather than for clinical risk reduction. This paradox should directly inform public health targeting strategies: rather than engaging already-active supplement consumers, interventions should prioritize reaching sedentary and low-activity adults through primary care, occupational health, and community-based channels.

The independent association of marital status with ever-use has not been reported prominently in prior Saudi or regional supplement literature and constitutes a novel contribution of this study.

Both single (OR = 3.00) and married (OR = 2.08) adults had significantly higher odds of ever-use than divorced or widowed individuals. While the married association is borderline (lower CI = 1.003, p = 0.049) and warrants cautious interpretation, the single association (OR = 3.00, 95% CI 1.07–8.39) is more robust. These findings are consistent with the broader health behavior literature demonstrating that bereavement and marital dissolution disrupt health-promoting routines and are associated with elevated health risks [24, 25]. We speculate that psychosocial disruptions associated with divorce and widowhood may diminish health-promoting self-care behaviors, though the study did not measure depression, social support, or bereavement timing and cannot test this mechanism directly. Future studies should examine whether marital transitions moderate longitudinal changes in supplement use.

The complete absence of a gender difference in ever-use (39.2% males vs. 39.3% females; p = 1.000) is a noteworthy departure from some international cohort data reporting higher supplement use among women [26] but aligns with prior Saudi surveys [14]. This likely reflects the gender-neutral framing of n-3 FA health benefits in Saudi public health messaging and the absence of gender-specific n-3 FA marketing in the Saudi market. Equally striking is the non-significant income effect across all five income brackets (p = 0.885). Despite the relatively high cost of imported omega-3 supplements, use rates were similar across the full income spectrum.

This apparent absence of demographic stratification may reflect the proliferation of e-commerce platforms, competitive generic fish oil formulations, and the broad availability of health information via social media [11], though these null findings should be interpreted cautiously, given the convenience-sample design and the possibility that gender and income inequities exist but were undetected in this sample. For public health planning, if replicated in probability-based samples, these findings would suggest that campaigns need not be income- or gender-targeted, allowing resources to be directed to behavioral (activity level) and social (marital status, clinical engagement) factors instead.

The urban–rural gradient in ever-use (urban: 45.4%; suburban: 35.5%; rural: 25.9%; p = 0.026) is consistent with known disparities in healthcare access and health-information infrastructure between Saudi urban centers and rural communities [10]. Although settlement type did not retain significance in multivariate analysis, the near-twofold absolute difference in use rates between urban and rural residents represents a meaningful health equity concern. Mobile health platforms, community pharmacy networks, and primary care outreach in peri-urban and rural settings could help address this disparity.

Regular tobacco use was inversely associated with ever-use in bivariate analysis (regular smokers: 20.0% vs. 41.1% for never-smokers; p = 0.025). This inverse relationship reflects a well-established phenomenon across supplement research: smokers are less likely to engage in health-promoting supplement behaviors [23]. Since regular tobacco users carry elevated cardiovascular inflammatory risk — precisely the indication for which n-3 FAs offer maximal benefit [4] — this represents a preventive care gap. Smoking cessation programs and cardiometabolic clinics should systematically address n-3 FA supplementation in tobacco-using patients.

The motivational profile — dominated by cardiovascular health (35.7%), immune function (31.7%), and cognitive health (31.0%) — mirrors findings from other Saudi and regional studies [15, 16, 27] and accurately reflects the scientific evidence base for EPA and DHA [4–8]. The relatively high endorsement of immune function (31.7%) is consistent with documented global increases in the use of immune-supporting supplements during and after the COVID-19 pandemic [28]. The non-use barrier profile reveals an engagement and information deficit rather than principally an economic one: the three most common barriers (supplement aversion, lack of interest, insufficient information) are all amenable to evidence-based communication and clinical counseling [29]. Critically, 50.8% of never-users stated they would initiate supplementation upon a healthcare professional’s recommendation. This finding has meaningful practice implications: a proactive clinician inquiry about n-3 FA supplementation during routine consultations — particularly among patients with hypertriglyceridemia, established CVD, or low dietary fish intake — could contribute to a substantial increase in evidence-based supplement uptake. It should be noted, however, that self-reported willingness does not necessarily predict actual behavior change, and this relationship warrants prospective investigation.

The predominance of short-term ever-use (53.2% supplementing for less than three months) suggests that n-3 FA supplementation is primarily a trial-phase behavior rather than a sustained practice in this population. Possible explanations include unmet expectations for rapid health improvement, cost fatigue, gastrointestinal side effects (fishy aftertaste, nausea), and uncertainty about long-term necessity. Healthcare providers should set realistic expectations at the initiation of supplementation, acknowledging the gradual nature of n-3 FA metabolic effects. The parallel finding that 46.0% of ever-users had not disclosed supplement use to their physician or dietitian is clinically alarming: at high doses, n-3 FAs may potentiate anticoagulant and antiplatelet medications [21], and this information gap exposes patients to avoidable drug–supplement interaction risk. Routine medication reconciliation should explicitly include an inquiry about dietary supplements.

The KA profile reveals a pattern of moderately positive attitudes alongside critical dosing and safety knowledge gaps. The bimodal safety distribution (mean 3.07 ± 1.47) reflects genuine uncertainty among ever-users about dose-dependent adverse effects, particularly bleeding risk at high doses [21]. The poor knowledge regarding appropriateness for infants (mean 2.98) and concurrent use with other medicines (mean 3.31) represents specific educational targets for clinical pharmacists and dietitians. The overall KA mean of 3.44/5 aligns closely with Hitl et al.’s 3.52/5 [19] in the original validation population, supporting good cross-cultural scale equivalence. This convergence also suggests that the Saudi and Balkan populations share broadly similar supplement attitude profiles at this stage of market maturation, despite markedly different cultural and dietary contexts.

This study has notable strengths: geographically diverse sampling across all five Saudi regions, a cross-culturally adapted and validated bilingual instrument, comprehensive assessment of both ever-users and never-users, including enabling-factor data, and the use of effect-size quantification alongside multivariate logistic regression. These features collectively enable more robust inference than studies relying solely on descriptive statistics. Several limitations must be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Online convenience sampling via social media over-represents younger, more educated, and more digitally connected adults, limiting generalizability to the broader Saudi population; findings should not be extrapolated to nationally representative estimates. The survey conflates current and former users into a single “ever-use” outcome; these groups are behaviorally distinct, and the study cannot characterize adherence, discontinuation, or behavior change. The KA module was administered only to ever-users, preventing comparison with never-users’ knowledge levels. The reference category for marital status in the logistic regression (divorced/widowed; combined n = 75) is relatively small, which may limit the precision of the marital status ORs. Objective biomarker data (e.g., erythrocyte EPA + DHA omega-3 index [26]) were not collected, precluding corroboration of self-reported supplement use with actual n-3 FA tissue status. Finally, the absence of a formal a priori sample size calculation is acknowledged. Beyond the pilot content-validity assessment (n = 30), the adapted Arabic-language instrument was not subjected to comprehensive psychometric revalidation in the Saudi context; future studies should consider more rigorous validation in larger Saudi samples. Future studies employing probability-based sampling, longitudinal follow-up with biomarker validation, and KA assessment in both user and non-user groups are also warranted.

Conclusions

In this geographically diverse cross-sectional survey of 321 Saudi adults, 39.3% reported ever-use of n-3 FA supplements. Physical activity level and marital status emerged as the primary independent associations, with a compelling five-fold activity gradient from 16.5% ever-use among sedentary adults to 80.8% among intensive exercisers — a cross-sectional association that does not establish temporal precedence but suggests a behaviorally coherent pattern. Gender and monthly income were not independently associated with ever-use, suggesting that access to n-3 FA supplementation is relatively equitable across demographic strata in Saudi Arabia.

Cardiovascular and cognitive health were the leading motivations, while supplement aversion, lack of interest, and insufficient information were the principal barriers. Short-term trial behavior (53.2% supplementing for < 3 months) and a 46.0% rate of non-disclosure to healthcare providers represent immediate targets for clinical intervention.

Two findings carry the clearest practical significance. First, 50.8% of never-users stated they would initiate supplementation upon a healthcare professional’s recommendation—an actionable lever that puts clinicians at the center of any strategy to increase evidence-based uptake. Second, persistent knowledge gaps regarding safe dosing, concurrent medication use, and appropriateness for special populations highlight the need for targeted patient education by clinical pharmacists and dietitians. Together, these findings provide an initial empirical foundation for the design of culturally tailored public health programs to promote rational, safe omega-3 supplementation in Saudi Arabia. Replication using probability-based samples and, subsequently, in other countries of the Middle East is needed before broader regional generalizations can be made.
