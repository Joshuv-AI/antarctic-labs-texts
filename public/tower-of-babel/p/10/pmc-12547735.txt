Provider Behavioral Determinants and Preferences for Lung Cancer Screening Implementation: A Brief Report
Authors: Jennifer A Lewis, Lauren R Samuels, Lucy B Spalluto, Christopher Lindsell, Claudia I Henschke, David F Yankelevitz, Carol Callaway-Lane, Robert S Dittus, Hilary A Tindle, Renda Soylemez Wiener, Christopher G Slatore, Drew Moghanaki, Carolyn M Audet, Christianne L Roumie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionImplementation of lung cancer screening is suboptimal. Understanding health care provider preferences and behavior is important for implementation. In this work, provider preferences for lung cancer screening implementation and self-reported determinants of lung cancer screening behavior were reported using the theoretical domains framework.MethodsIn this mixed-methods evaluation, health care providers at nine Veterans Affairs were surveyed to list factors influencing their decision to screen patients for lung cancer in free-text responses and rank implementation strategies by usefulness in clinical practice. Free-text data were coded and mapped to the theoretical domains framework. Quantitative ranking data were descriptively analyzed overall and by specialty (primary care versus radiology), clinic setting (hospital versus community), and provider type (physician versus advanced practice provider).ResultsOf 234/254 eligible providers analyzed, most were primary care (83.8%), community-based (52.1%), and physicians (66.2%). Respondents identified social influences (69.2%), knowledge (55.6%), and environmental context and resources (15.4%) as influential behavioral determinants. Overall, patient reminders (29.9%), provider reminders (26.1%), and learning collaboratives (24.4%) were reported most frequently as useful implementation strategies. Strategy preferences differed by specialty, practice setting, and provider type: primary care (30.1%), physician (34.2%), and hospital-based (33.0%) providers most frequently ranked patient reminders; radiology providers most frequently ranked learning collaborative (42.1%); advanced practice providers (24.1%) and community-based providers (27.0%) most frequently ranked provider reminders as most useful.ConclusionsDesigning implementation strategies that target three behavioral determinants (social influences, knowledge, and environmental context and resources) and are tailored to providers’ preferences may effectively change providers’ lung cancer screening behavior.Keywords: Lung cancer screening, Provider behavior, Behavioral determinants, Implementation strategies, Theoretical domains framework, Intervention development

Introduction

Implementation of lung cancer screening with low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) is low internationally.1, 2, 3 Successful implementation of evidence-based practices, such as lung cancer screening, depends on patient-, provider-, clinic-, and organizational-level factors.4 The theoretical domains framework describes 15 behavioral determinants that influence provider behavior (Supplementary Table 1).5 The degree to which interventions influence provider behavior may depend on these determinants along with providers’ preferences for certain implementation strategies. The relative impact and interaction of the behavioral determinants and providers’ preferred implementation strategies on screening practices remain unknown. The aim of this work was to evaluate the theoretical domains framework behavioral determinants and providers’ preferred implementation strategies for lung cancer screening. It was hypothesized that these may differ by provider characteristics and practice setting.

Methods

Study Design, Setting, and Population

A cross-sectional survey was conducted among radiology and primary care services at nine Veterans Affairs Medical Centers (VAMCs) between July 2020 and March 2021. The survey was part of a larger questionnaire assessing organizational readiness for lung cancer screening.6 Each VAMC participated in the Enterprise-Wide Initiative, Veteran Affairs Partnership to increase Access to Lung Screening (VA-PALS), which has been previously described.7

Survey participants were identified from administrative and email lists for primary care and radiology and included administrators, physicians, advanced practice providers, and staff. The questionnaire was administered electronically using VA Research Electronic Data Capture in a 12-week period with weekly email reminders and daily reminders during the final three days. This analysis was restricted to responders who self-identified as clinical providers.

Survey Content

Questionnaire items included respondent demographics, health care role, and clinical practice characteristics. There were three main survey sections. The first section was a free-text item which asked about factors influencing provider lung cancer screening behavior. The second section asked respondents to rank nine implementation strategies for lung cancer screening from most to least useful. These strategies fell into two broad categories: reminders and education. Reminder strategies included audit and feedback, provider reminders, patient reminders, and clinical tools (standardized note templates with Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services criteria, billing codes, etc.). Educational strategies included educational tools (frequently asked questions handout, pocket guide, or resource list), academic detailing (lung cancer screening training including processes specific to clinic), workflow training, educational session (lecture), and a learning collaborative (group learning focused on improvement). The third survey section asked respondents to rate different types of reminders and educational strategies on a 5-point Likert scale from “not useful” to “very useful” (Supplementary Table 2).

Mixed-Methods Statistical Analysis

Respondents who did not rank any items in the second survey section were excluded. Proportions, means, and medians for quantitative data, including respondents’ demographic and practice characteristics, were reported. Free-text responses from the first survey section were coded and mapped to the theoretical domains dramework.5 A random 5% sample was coded by a second investigator (CA), and where discrepancies were found, the team discussed and resolved these. Coding and code frequencies were calculated with MAXQDA 2022 software. For survey sections two and three, overall results and those stratified by specialty (primary care versus radiology), clinic setting (hospital versus community), and professional training (physician versus advanced practice provider) were presented.

This study was approved by VA Central Institutional Review Board (C-IRB E19-05), the VA Tennessee Valley Healthcare System Research & Development Committee, the VA Organizational Assessment Subcommittee, and the VA Office of Labor and Management Relations. A waiver of documentation of consent was obtained from the institutional review board.

Results

There were 3822 individuals invited to participate in the full survey, of whom 1049 returned the surveys (overall response rate of 27.4%). One medical center (n = 282 respondents) is not included because it did not receive the questionnaire with provider behavioral determinants and implementation preference items. In addition, 513 participants whose self-reported roles were inconsistent with a clinical ordering provider and 20 who did not answer the ranking items were excluded. The final analytical sample consisted of 234 respondents (Supplementary Fig. 1). Respondents in this sample were predominantly physicians (66.2%), White (74.7%), practiced in primary care (83.8%), and in the community (52.1%) (Table 1).

Survey Section Two: Rank Order of Lung Cancer Screening Implementation Strategies

Rankings 1 to 3 were combined into “most useful” and 7 to 9 into “least useful.” The three implementation strategies most frequently ranked as “most useful” were patient reminder (29.9%), provider reminder (26.1%), and learning collaborative (24.4%). The three strategies most frequently ranked “least useful” were learning collaborative (35.0%), patient reminder (27.4%), and audit and feedback (22.6%) (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 3).

Provider implementation strategy preferences. Rankings of 1 to 3 were combined into “most useful” and 7 to 9 into “least useful.”

Rankings of implementation strategies differed by specialty, provider type, and clinic practice setting. Among primary care, the strategy most frequently ranked “most useful” was patient reminder (30.1%); among radiology, it was learning collaborative (42.1%). Among advance practice providers, the strategy most frequently ranked “most useful” was provider reminder (24.1%); among physicians, it was patient reminder (34.2%). Among hospital-based providers, the strategy most frequently ranked “most useful” was patient reminder (33.0%); among community-based providers, it was provider reminder (27.0%) (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 3).

Survey Section Three: Likert Scores of Reminders and Education Implementation Types

Respondents rated specific types of reminders and education strategies. Every reminder and education strategy type was endorsed as “very useful” or “moderately useful” by at least 30% of providers overall. Of the provider reminder strategies, the strategy most frequently considered “very useful” or “moderately useful” was a special notation in the patient chart (74.8%); of the patient reminder strategies, it was reminders from ancillary staff to patients during clinic visit (72.6%). Of the provider education strategies, the strategy most frequently considered “very useful” or “moderately useful” was online lectures (68.4%); of the patient education strategies, it was pamphlets (57.3%). Ratings varied by specialty, provider type, and clinic setting (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5).

Discussion

In this mixed-methods evaluation, two main study findings were noted. First, key provider determinants of lung cancer screening behavior include social influences (patients), knowledge (guidelines) and environmental context and resources. Second, in the ranking of implementation strategies, providers noted reminders for patients and providers and a learning collaborative as key strategies.

A recent systematic review using the theoretical domains framework to assess barriers and facilitators of cervical cancer screening among patients found that emotions, social influences, knowledge, environment context, and resources and belief about consequences were key determinants of patient screening behavior.8 Another study on colorectal cancer screening demonstrated that knowledge was the most frequently reported patient-level barrier and knowledge and environmental context and resources were the most frequently reported provider- and system-level barriers.9 This study reports similar findings and is the first to describe provider determinants of lung cancer screening behavior using the theoretical domains framework, based on the authors’ knowledge.

Despite the importance of partnering with providers to implement evidence-based practices, there are limited data in this area. Among a national sample of primary care providers surveyed on their perspectives of implementation strategies for cancer prevention,10 investigators noted variability in perspectives. They also reported that adaptations to local practice, provider training and education, quality improvement initiatives, and engaging patients had the highest “helpfulness” scores.10 In the present study, certain strategies, such as the patient reminder and learning collaborative, were found to rank as “most useful” and “least useful” by approximately the same percent of respondents. This further supports the idea that a tailored implementation approach, in which the provider can choose the strategy or the strategy is selected based on practice characteristics, should be considered.

The findings of this study reinforce the idea that future lung cancer screening implementation strategies should consider targeting the following three main determinants: social influences (patients), knowledge (guidelines), and environmental context and resources. These data are foundational to intervention design and mapping. In the landmark paper, Michie et al.11 propose “environmental restructuring” as the target to influence two key behavioral determinants (knowledge and environment context and resources). They note that a different approach or “persuasion” should be used to target the social influences behavioral determinant. A future clinical trial that tests a behavioral intervention targeting these three determinants (social influences, knowledge, and environmental context and resources) and each provider’s preferred implementation strategy is needed to determine effects on provider lung cancer screening behavior.

There are limitations to this evaluation, including the low response rate. There is the possibility of selection bias and social desirability bias. Providers who practice within VAMCs were surveyed, and results may not generalize.

Conclusion

This mixed-methods, cross-sectional study found that three determinants—social influences (patients), knowledge (guidelines), and environmental context and resources—were important to provider lung cancer screening behavior. Furthermore, providers had preferences for implementation strategies (provider and patient reminders and learning collaborative). However, these preferences varied by specialty, clinical setting, and provider type. Designing implementation strategies that target three behavioral determinants (social influences, knowledge, and environmental context and resources) and are tailored to providers’ preferences may effectively change providers’ lung cancer screening behavior.
