Cuproptosis inducers mediate cold lethality via SLCR-46.1 in C. elegans
Authors: Serina Yamashiro, Satomi Mizuno, Haruka Motomura, Aidan T. Pezacki, Yukina Mori, Christopher J. Chang, Akane Ohta, Atsushi Kuhara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cuproptosis denotes cell death triggered by copper accumulation. However, its role in natural environmental responses remains unclear. Here, we demonstrate that cuproptosis contributes to cold-induced lethality in C. elegans. A mutant for the lysosomal copper transporter gene slcr-46.1 exhibited defective cold tolerance due to copper accumulation in the pharyngeal muscle upon cold exposure. The abnormal cold tolerance of the slcr-46.1 mutant was suppressed by interference with copper homeostasis and cuproptosis-signaling genes. Chelating copper ions in the slcr-46.1 mutant rescued the abnormal cold tolerance, while inhibiting cuproptosis signaling in wild-type worms inhibited cold-induced lethality. In addition, the cold lethality in the slcr-46.1 mutant was not caused by activation of the oxidative stress pathway observed in ferroptosis (iron-dependent cell death). Here, we show evidence that cuproptosis plays a critical role in cold-induced lethality in an animal.

Introduction

Copper is an essential trace element which can have oxidized and reduced forms and a catalytic cofactor in various biochemical processes in animals, including mitochondrial ATP production, hormone maturation, iron distribution and disproportionation of toxic superoxide1,2. In humans, copper is mainly absorbed in the proximal part of the small intestine and transported to the liver via the portal vein3. While essential for normal physiological processes, excess copper ions induce apoptosis through multiple mechanisms2. One major pathway is related to their redox activity: copper ions generate reactive oxygen species (ROS) via Fenton or Fenton-like reactions2,4. Furthermore, although iron has long been considered the primary metal ion responsible for ferroptosis, recent studies suggest that copper may promote ferroptosis under certain conditions2.

Outside the cell, copper exists as Cu(II), bound to the STEAP family of metal reductases and reduced to Cu(I), and is taken up into the cell2. Cu(I) primarily enters the human intestinal tract and/or somatic cells via SLC31A1/CTR1 and SLC31A2/CTR2 (solute carrier family 31 member 1)2. The unbound free copper ions that can generate ROS and cytotoxicity are sequestered by metallothionein and glutathione binding2. In higher organisms, the metalloadapter RAD23B can receive copper from SLC31A1/CTR1 and regulate copper transfer to multiple cytoplasmic copper chaperones5, such as CCS and SOD1, to supply copper to different cell organelles6. Copper transport to mitochondria is mainly dependent on cytochrome c oxidase Cu chaperone (COX17); the intracellular copper pool is associated with mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation7. Copper-transporting ATPases ATP7A and B play a central role in the efflux of copper ions in mammalian cells2,8. In a basal state, with low copper concentrations, ATP7A and ATP7B are localized in the trans-Golgi network. As copper concentrations increase, ATP7A and ATP7B translocate from the Golgi to the post-Golgi and lysosomes, where they remove excess copper out of the cell9,10. Mutations in ATP7A and ATP7B cause Menkes and Wilson disease, respectively, both disorders related to copper transport2,11.

Protein lipoylation is a conserved posttranslational modification on lysine residues affecting only four mitochondrial metabolic complexes: pyruvate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, branched-chain α-ketoacid dehydrogenase, and the glycine decarboxylase complex12. This modification is evolutionarily conserved from bacteria to humans12.

Humans have two ferredoxins with distinct functions, FDX1 and FDX212,13. FDX1 supports steroid hormone synthesis by donating electrons to cytochrome P450 enzymes like CYP11A1 and is highly expressed in the adrenal glands12. FDX1 also acts as a key upstream regulator of protein lipoylation through interaction with the lipoylation enzyme LIAS, thereby contributing to mitochondrial respiration12,14,15. Further, FDX1 functions as a reductase that converts Cu(II) to Cu(I) and is a direct target of the copper ionophore elesclomol. Knocking out FDX1 in cancer cells leads to a complete loss of protein lipoylation, highlighting its central role in this pathway15. These findings are further supported by in vitro evidence showing that FDX1 initiates LIAS-mediated lipoylation12,14. However, its role in non-steroidogenic cells remains poorly understood. FDX2 is essential for iron–sulfur cluster biosynthesis12. Recently, FDX1 was reported as a crucial regulator of protein lipoylation and copper-dependent cell death (cuproptosis)12,15. Excessive intracellular copper levels induce cell death mechanisms such as autophagy and ferroptosis. Recently, cell death caused by copper-induced mitochondrial stress and damage has been defined as cuproptosis2,15. Cuproptosis occurs due to mitochondrial respiratory damage and subsequent mitochondrial protein stress, rather than mitochondrial oxidative stress as in ferroptosis2,15,16. In the presence of the ES, copper binds to lipoylated proteins such as DLAT—components of the mitochondrial TCA cycle—causing their toxic oligomerization2. Further, cuproptosis is not inhibited by blockers of apoptosis or other known programmed cell death pathways, but it is suppressed by inhibitors of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I and III (rotenone and antimycin A)15.

C. elegans is a simple model organism suitable for studying intracellular signal transduction mechanisms and a model system for human disease research due to the presence of numerous human homologs in its genome. C. elegans has also been utilized for pharmacological studies and those focused on the metabolism of metals such as copper and iron. For example, CUA-1, a C. elegans ortholog of human ATP7A and B, changes its subcellular localization in a copper concentration-dependent manner17. Under copper depletion, CUA-1 localizes to the basement membrane and effluxes copper into the surrounding tissues17, but under copper-rich conditions, CUA-1 is redistributed to lysosome-like organelles in the intestine, the intestinal granules, to detoxify copper17. In C. elegans, how dietary copper is transported to intestinal cells and the mechanisms underlying copper homeostasis remain largely unknown. Although cuproptosis has not been reported in C. elegans so far, since the lipoylation pathway and some of its components, such as LIAS, are conserved in C. elegans18, we hypothesized that cuproptosis may also occur in C. elegans.

Cold tolerance in C. elegans, which can be used to analyze the temperature response mechanism of animal, at the tissue-network level, such as in muscle, intestine, and neurons19–22. C. elegans can store information about its cultivation temperature and adjust its cold tolerance accordingly19,23. For example, animals grown at 15 °C can survive after 48 h of cold stimulation at 2 °C, whereas C. elegans grown at 20 °C or 25°C die at 2 °C. This phenomenon is called cold tolerance19. In addition, wild-type animals grown at 25 °C and kept at 15 °C for 3–5 h can survive at 2 °C, which is defined as temperature acclimation20,22. In the regulation of cold tolerance, thermosensory neurons at the head establish tissue-level circuits with muscle, intestine, and sperm19–22,24. Under 15 °C conditions, intestinal fat accumulates abundantly, whereas at higher temperatures this accumulation is reduced, leading to decreased cold tolerance20. However, the machinery underlying cold-induced lethality is currently unknown.

In this study, we identified slcr-46.1 as the gene responsible for a cold-tolerant C. elegans mutant. SLCR-46.1 is a homolog of a mouse copper transporter localized in lysosomes in the pharyngeal muscle, where it regulates cold tolerance. In the slcr-46.1 mutant exposed to cold, copper ions abnormally aggregate in head cells, leading to cold-induced death. However, knocking down genes associated with cuproptosis unexpectedly suppressed cold lethality in this mutant. Furthermore, knocking down cuproptosis-related genes in wild-type animals repressed cold lethality, suggesting that cuproptosis contributes to cold lethality. These results indicate that SLCR-46.1 is involved in the copper metabolism within lysosomes of the pharyngeal muscle under low temperatures; its functional deficiency leads to copper ion aggregation and induction of cuproptosis, ultimately causing cold-induced death in C. elegans.

Results

Wild-type C. elegans N2 animals grown at 15 °C can survive at 2 °C but not when grown at 20 °C (Fig. 1a). To isolate a novel mutant defective in cold tolerance, wild-type animals were treated with the mutagen ethyl methanesulfonate (EMS); this way, a KHR018 chr10 mutant with abnormal cold tolerance was isolated (Fig. 1b). Backcrossing this mutant allowed isolating the KHR277 chr10 mutant (Fig. 1b), and its genomic DNA sequence was decoded using deep DNA sequencing (Supplementary Table S1). Five genes, including C46C11.2 on the X chromosome, were selected based on comparison with the genomic information of the wild-type strain (Fig. 1c). After cultivation at 15 °C, the slcr-46.1 knockout mutant exhibited abnormal survival rates at 2 °C for 48 h, compared with the wild-type (Fig. 1f). The abnormal cold tolerance of the slcr-46.1 mutant was rescued by introducing the wild-type slcr-46.1 gene (Fig. 1f). To determine the expression pattern of slcr-46.1, TagRFP-T and mCherry were inserted into the endogenous slcr-46.1 genomic locus using CRISPR/Cas9. However, fluorescence of the SLCR-46.1 fusion protein was not detected due to the presence of only a single copy of the fluorescent gene. Then, the full-length genomic slcr-46.1 was fused with VENUS and expressed as a trans gene in wild-type worms under the slcr-46.1 promoter (Fig. 2a, b). SLCR-46.1::VENUS was expressed in the pharyngeal muscle, intestine, and epidermis, but not in neurons (Fig. 2a, b). Pharyngeal and intestinal expressions of SLCR-46.1 are consistent with a previous tissue-specific transcriptome analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1e)26. Next, when slcr-46.1cDNA::GFP was expressed in wild-type worms under the same slcr-46.1 promoter, slcr-46.1::GFP was expressed in two pairs of neurons: ASG and BAG, as well as in the intestine and pharyngeal muscle (Fig. 2c). These results suggest that the intron in slcr-46.1 is essential for normal slcr-46.1 expression in wild-type worms, inhibiting its neuronal expression. In the intestine, SLCR-46.1::VENUS was mainly observed in its anterior part and in granular form (Fig. 2a). To investigate the subcellular localization of C. elegans SLCR-46.1, we performed double immunostaining using anti-LMP-1 and anti-GFP antibodies (Fig. 2d–f). LMP-1 is a lysosome-associated membrane protein in C. elegans, but LMP-1::RFP appears to fail to localize properly to lysosomes, instead accumulating ectopically in the cytoplasm27. In contrast, the polyclonal anti-LMP-1 antibody can robustly label lysosome-like granules in the intestine of wild-type worms28. Immunostaining of wild-type worms expressing SLCR-46.1::VENUS revealed that SLCR-46.1 co-localized with anti-LMP-1 (Fig. 2f). To identify tissues involved in SLCR-46.1-dependent cold tolerance, we expressed slcr-46.1 cDNA in the slcr-46.1 mutant under various tissue-specific promoters (Fig. 2g). The abnormal cold tolerance of the slcr-46.1 mutant was not rescued by expression of slcr-46.1 cDNA in almost all neurons using the unc-14 promoter (Fig. 2g, slcr-46.1(chr51); Ex[unc-14p:: slcr-46.1 cDNA]) or in the intestine using the ges-1 promoter (Fig. 2g, slcr-46.1(chr51); Ex[ges-1p:: slcr-46.1 cDNA]). In contrast, the abnormal cold tolerance of the slcr-46.1 mutant was rescued by expression of slcr-46.1 cDNA in the pharyngeal muscle at the head and intestine, using myo-2 and ges-1 promoters (Fig. 2g, slcr-46.1(chr51); Ex[(myo-2p and ges-1p):: slcr-46.1 cDNA]). Similarly, expressing slcr-46.1 cDNA only in pharyngeal muscle rescued the slcr-46.1 abnormal cold tolerance (Fig. 2g, slcr-46.1(chr51); Ex[myo-2p:: slcr-46.1 cDNA]). These results suggest that SLCR-46.1 expression in the pharyngeal muscle is essential for cold tolerance in wild-type worms.

Simultaneous expression of slcr-46.1 cDNA in the pharyngeal muscle as well as in ASG and BAG neurons in the slcr-46.1 mutant only partially rescued the slcr-46.1 abnormal cold tolerance (Fig. 2g, slcr-46.1(chr51); Ex[(myo-2p, gcy-15p, and gcy-33p):: slcr-46.1 cDNA]). This result is consistent with partial rescue of the abnormally decreased cold tolerance of the slcr-46.1 mutant obtained by expressing slcr-46.1 cDNA under the slcr-46.1 promoter (Fig. 1f, slcr-46.1(chr51); Ex[slcr-46.1p:: slcr-46.1 cDNA]), which drives expression in various tissues, including neurons. These results suggest that excessive neuronal expression of SLCR-46.1 negatively regulates cold tolerance in wild-type worms. For example, because ASG neurons are involved in cold tolerance29, overexpression of slcr-46.1 in ASG, where its expression is normally inhibited, may disrupt copper homeostasis and impair neuronal signaling required for cold tolerance.

We also examined whether mammalian homologs of slcr-46.1 could rescue the abnormal cold tolerance of the slcr-46.1 mutant (Fig. 2h). The mouse and human homologs of SLC46A3 have different substrates and transport directions: mouse SLC46A3 localizes at the lysosomal membrane and transports copper ions into the lysosome, whereas human SLC46A3 transports steroid conjugates and bile acids toward the cytoplasm25. Nonetheless, their physiological roles are unclear. Interestingly, expression of human SLC46A3 in the C. elegans slcr-46.1 mutant partially rescued its abnormal cold tolerance (Fig. 2h). Similarly, mouse SLC46A3 also partially rescued the slcr-46.1 mutant phenotype (Fig. 2h).

To quantify whether lysosomal activity is altered in the slcr-46.1 mutant, we used Lysosomal-METRIQ (Measurement of protein transporting integrity by RatIo Quantification)30. This fusion protein consists of a lysosomal protein, superfolder GFP, a self-cleaving T2A peptide, and mCherry. When trafficking Lysosomal-METRIQ to the lysosome, the T2A peptide is cleaved, leaving mCherry in the cytoplasm30. The lysosomal protein and sfGFP delivered to the lysosome are degraded and inactivated. Thus, lysosomal integrity can be assessed by measuring the fluorescence ratio of cytoplasmic mCherry to lysosomal sfGFP30. We expressed Lysosomal-METRIQ in wild-type and slcr-46.1 mutant worms. In wild-type animals, lysosomal activity reduced in response to cold stimuli, whereas in the slcr-46.1 mutant, it was slightly increased (Fig. 2i, j). Notably, under normal conditions, lysosomal activity was higher in wild-type worms than in slcr-46.1 mutant worms (Fig. 2i, j). These results suggest that cold stimulation decreases lysosomal activity in wild-type animals and that SLCR-46.1 is at least partially required for normal and cold-induced regulation of lysosomal activity.

Although previous studies reported that copper ions in C. elegans accumulate in the intestine, whether they also accumulate in the pharyngeal muscle remains unclear17. We quantified copper levels in the pharyngeal muscle of wild-type and slcr-46.1 mutant worms grown at 15 °C (Fig. 3a–f). As a copper ion marker, we used CF4, a fluorescence probe that increases its emission in response to monovalent copper ions. Compared with wild-type worms, the quantification revealed higher copper abundance near the edge of pharyngeal muscle in the slcr-46.1 mutant (Fig. 3g, h), likely reflecting increased copper accumulation in the mutant. In 15 °C-grown wild-type animals, copper levels increased after exposure to 2 °C (Fig. 3g, h), returning to pre-exposure values within 24 h (Fig. 3i). A similar pattern was observed in the slcr-46.1 mutant surviving the cold exposure (Fig. 3h, i). In contrast, the slcr-46.1 mutant that died following the 2 °C treatment exhibited a marked increase in CF4 fluorescence at the terminal bulb of the pharyngeal muscle (Fig. 3e, j), whereas CF4 fluorescence near the edge of the pharyngeal muscle was reduced in the dead slcr-46.1 mutant (Fig. 3i). Such abnormal copper accumulation at the pharyngeal terminal bulb was not observed in wild-type animals (Fig. 3j). To examine whether SLCR − 46.1 transports copper, we performed functional analyses using S2R+ insect cultured cells that were forced to express SLCR-46.1 (Fig. 3k–r). A Drosophila codon–optimized C. elegans SLCR-46.1::mCherry was generated and expressed in S2R+ cells, and intracellular copper localization was assessed using CF4 as a copper-sensitive probe together with LysoTracker Deep Red as a lysosomal marker. These results suggest that SLCR-46.1 promotes copper accumulation within lysosomes and may contribute to lysosomal copper transport, although SLCR-46.1 could also transport other molecules.

Since a recent report demonstrated that copper ion accumulation induces cuproptosis in human cultured cells15 (Fig. 4a), we hypothesized that the cold-induced death in the slcr-46.1 mutant may be mediated by cuproptosis. As in human cells, FDX1 and LIAS are key regulators of cuproptosis and their C. elegans homologues are FDX-2 (Y73F8A.27) and LIAS-1 (M01F1.3)18 (Fig. 4b). To test our hypothesis, we inhibited fdx-2 or lias-1 expression in the slcr-46.1 mutant. Surprisingly, fdx-2 or lias-1 knockdown significantly suppressed the abnormal cold tolerance phenotype of the slcr-46.1 mutant (Fig. 4c–g). Although cuproptosis inhibition effectively suppressed the cold-induced death of the slcr-46.1 mutant, the role of this pathway in the cold tolerance of wild-type worms remains unclear. Interestingly, fdx-2 or lias-1 knockdown in 15 °C-grown wild-type worms also increased cold tolerance (Fig. 4c). In 20 °C-grown wild-type worms, fdx-2 knockdown increased cold tolerance (Fig. 4h), suggesting that cuproptosis inhibition enhances cold tolerance not only in the slcr-46.1 mutant but also in wild-type worms. Although fdx-2 and lias-1 are cuproptosis-related genes, they also function as components of the mitochondrial TCA cycle12,15,31,32, and we cannot rule out the possibility that functions of FDX-2 and LIAS-1 beyond cuproptosis contribute to cold lethality. Because a molecular component specifically involved in cuproptosis had not been identified, the original discovery paper on cuproptosis employed a copper-ion chelator to suppress the cuproptosis phenotype15. Following this approach, we used the copper chelator bathocuproinedisulfonic acid (BCS), which selectively binds copper(I) to form an orange-yellow complex. To inhibit copper accumulation, synchronized L1 larvae were transferred to BCS-containing plates and subsequently tested for cold tolerance. Interestingly, BCS-mediated inhibition of copper accumulation restored the abnormal cold tolerance in the slcr-46.1 mutant (Fig. 4i). In contrast, BCS treatment had no effect on the cold tolerance of wild-type worms (Fig. 4i). These results indicate that the abnormal cold tolerance of the slcr-46.1 mutant can be rescued by decreasing copper accumulation. However, the extent of the rescue was slightly lower than that observed after knockdown of cuproptosis-related genes (fdx-2, lias-1) in the slcr-46.1 mutant (Fig. 4c), suggesting that copper accumulation may not be the only cause of cold-induced death in the slcr-46.1 mutant.

Since abnormal mitochondrial morphology has been reported as a cuproptosis phenotype in human cultured cells33,34, we examined mitochondrial morphology in the pharyngeal muscle of the slcr-46.1 mutant. Marked mitochondrial accumulation was observed in the pharyngeal muscles of the slcr-46.1 mutant following cold stimuli (Fig. 5a–d). During cold-induced death of the mutant, the pharyngeal muscles collapsed, and blister-like structures were detected (Fig. 5e, f). A previous study using the oxidative stress marker gst-4p::GFP as an indicator of increased ROS reported elevated ROS levels in nematodes exposed to copper or exhibiting excessive copper accumulation35. Therefore, we measured gst-4p::GFP expression in wild-type and slcr-46.1 mutant worms (Fig. 5g). As a result, the slcr-46.1 mutant showed an increase in gst-4p::GFP expression compared to the wild-type at 15 °C (Fig. 5g). This suggests an increase in ROS in slcr-46.1 mutants showing cuproptosis. Increased intracellular copper ion concentrations have been proposed to induce cell death through mechanisms other than cuproptosis, such as ferroptosis2. To test this possibility, we treated the slcr-46.1 mutant with ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1), a ferroptosis inhibitor. However, Fer-1 treatment did not restore the abnormal cold tolerance of the slcr-46.1 mutant (Fig. 5h), suggesting that ferroptosis is not involved in the cold-induced death observed in the slcr-46.1 mutant. To examine the possible involvement of other metal ion-dependent-cell death pathways in wild-type worms, we treated wild-type animals with Fer-1 and the iron chelator salicylaldehyde isonicotinoyl hydrazone (SIH)36. However, neither Fer-1 nor SIH improved cold tolerance in 20 °C-grown wild-type worms (Fig. 5i). These results, consistent with those obtained in the slcr-46.1 mutant at 15 °C, suggest that cold-induced death in C. elegans is not mediated by ferroptosis.

To determine whether the abnormal cold-induced lethality observed in the slcr-46.1 mutant is a common feature of mutants with impaired copper metabolism, we assessed the cold tolerance of the copper metabolism mutant cua-1(knu790). cua-1 encodes a C. elegans homolog of human ATP7A and ATP7B; cua-1 mutants accumulate copper ions in the intestine, exhibiting developmental defects under copper excess35. We found that 15 °C-grown cua-1 mutants displayed normal cold tolerance when exposed to 2 °C (Fig. 5j). Given that slcr-46.1 is expressed in the pharyngeal muscle and regulates cold tolerance, the cold-induced lethality of the slcr-46.1 mutant is likely attributable to pharyngeal muscle–specific defects in copper metabolism. Thus, we next examined whether the slcr-46.1 mutant exhibits developmental defects under copper excess similar to the cua-1 mutant35, but found no such defects (Fig. 5k). These results suggest that the copper metabolism in the slcr-46.1 mutant causes neither lethal nor severe phenotypic abnormalities under normal cultivation conditions and that defects arise only upon cold exposure.

We next assessed cold tolerance in chca-1(tm6506), mtl-1(tm1770), and lys-3(tm2505) mutants. All displayed normal cold tolerance when transferred from 15 °C to 2 °C (Fig. 6e). However, changing from 20 °C to 2 °C, only chca-1 and mtl-1 mutants showed a modest increase in survival rate, a phenotype similar to that observed with fdx-2 knockdown in wild-type and slcr-46.1 mutant animals (Fig. 6f). This increase was weaker than that observed with fdx-2 or lias-1 knockdown in wild-type and slcr-46.1 mutant worms, indicating that partial loss of copper transport or binding capacity alone is insufficient to suppress cuproptosis.

In the cuproptosis pathway, the direct cause of cell death is copper accumulation in the mitochondria15. Under normal conditions, the copper taken up into the cytoplasm is delivered to organelles by copper chaperones2. In human cells, mitochondrial copper import is mediated by COX17, a cytochrome c oxidase copper chaperone in the mitochondrial intermembrane space7. COX17 supplies copper to the cochaperones SCO1 and COX11, which in turn transfer it to the COX2 and COX1 subunits7,34. To test whether limiting mitochondrial copper import suppresses cuproptosis, we knocked down the C. elegans homologs of COX11 and SCO1 using RNAi. In C. elegans, the ortholog of human SCO1 is encoded by sco-1, but there is no SCO2 homolog and those of human COX11 and COX17 are encoded by cox-11 and cox-17 (F40G9.2), respectively38. Cold tolerance assays of 15°C-grown slcr-46.1 mutant worms revealed that a cox-11 or sco-1 knockdown significantly suppressed the defective cold tolerance of slcr-46.1 mutant (Fig. 6g). These results suggest that, excessive mitochondrial copper transport is required for activation of the cuproptosis pathway, and that inhibiting this transport can inactivate cuproptosis. Furthermore, both wild-type and slcr-46.1 mutant worms with sco-1 or cox-11 knockdown showed a 1-day developmental delay (Fig. 6h–k) similar to that caused by fdx-2 or lias-1 knockdown (Fig. 4f, g).

Discussion

Our study found that the slcr-46.1 mutant, defective in lysosomal copper transport, exhibited abnormal cold tolerance and Cu⁺ accumulation in the pharyngeal muscle. RNAi knockdown of genes related to the cuproptosis pathway suppressed the abnormal cold tolerance of the slcr-46.1 mutant. Similarly, in wild-type animals, knockdown of cuproptosis-related genes enhanced survival after cold exposure, indicating that cuproptosis contributes to cold-induced lethality. Direct inhibition of copper accumulation with the chelator BCS rescued the cold-tolerance defect of the slcr-46.1 mutant, whereas inhibition of the ferroptosis pathway did not. These findings support a model in which cold lethality of the slcr-46.1 mutant is not attributable to general sickness, but rather occurs in a copper-ion–dependent manner.

As previously reported, in C. elegans, dietary copper is taken up by the pharyngeal muscle and subsequently transported to the intestine, where it is absorbed into the body38. Previous studies have shown that some copper transporter genes, including chca-1 and cuc-1, are not expressed in the pharyngeal muscle37,40. Accordingly, copper may preferentially accumulate in this tissue, potentially facilitating activation of the copper-induced cell death pathway. Consequently, in slcr-46.1 mutant or wild-type animals under cold conditions, copper accumulation in the pharyngeal muscle may promote copper-induced cell death, thereby increasing cold-induced lethality.

Copper is an essential metal ion required for the development and respiration of organisms. However, intracellular Cu is strictly regulated due to its cytotoxicity. Abnormal Cu accumulation has been observed in certain diseases, including Wilson’s disease (in hepatocytes) and Menkes disease (in neural cells), as well as in tumor cells, particularly some cancer cells41. Studies have reported that Cu accumulation in cancer cells promotes tumor growth and metastasis42,43. Furthermore, Cu-chelating agents such as BCS effectively suppress cancer cell proliferation42,43. These insights propose that, in biological systems, cuproptosis may help eliminate Cu-accumulation and cell toxicity, thereby maintaining cellular homeostasis. Despite advances on the application of copper-induced cell death as a cancer treatment, there are no reports on the role of copper-induced cell death in cold-induced death. Therefore, this study suggests evidence that cold-induced death in C. elegans is caused by cuproptosis.

To examine whether low temperature activates cuproptosis signaling in mammalian cells, we used a published transcriptome dataset of mouse embryonic stem cells cultured at 31 °C versus 37 °C44. The cuproptosis-related genes fdx1 and lias showed increased expression at low temperatures in B6J mouse cells, with levels about twice as high in cells from non–cold-tolerant B6J mice compared with cold-tolerant STM2 mice (Supplementary Table S2). These results raise the possibility that low temperature may induce the cuproptosis pathway in mouse cells.

Similar to cuproptosis, ferroptosis is a metal ion-dependent form of cell death, and previous studies have reported that cold-induced cell death in human cancer cells resembles ferroptosis45. Ferroptosis suppression at low temperatures is thought to prevent cold-induced death in human cancer cells45. However, in C. elegans, ferroptosis inhibition by Fer-1 or SIH did not suppress cold-induced death. While copper accumulation activates ferroptosis and cuproptosis in mammalian cells, knockdown of fdx-2, a gene involved in cuproptosis, increased cold tolerance of wild-type C. elegans grown at 15 °C and 20 °C, suggesting that cold-induced lethality in C. elegans is mainly induced by cuproptosis. Future molecular elucidation of the cold lethality via cuproptosis using C. elegans may shed light on a new signaling process important for cuproptosis in animal.

Limitations

The precise molecular mechanisms underlying cuproptosis remain unclear. Unlike apoptosis, which involves chromatin condensation and nuclear fragmentation, or ferroptosis, which leads to oxidative damage-induced membrane rupture, specific markers and cell death mechanisms of cuproptosis have yet to be fully elucidated. Furthermore, no organism-level physiological responses related to cuproptosis have been identified. The animal experimental system for cold-induced, copper-mediated cell death established in this study is expected to uncover novel molecules involved in the cuproptosis pathway and may reveal previously unknown regulatory mechanisms, thereby contributing to and facilitating future research in cuproptosis.

Methods

Strains

C. elegans was grown under standard conditions. The N2 (Bristol) strain was used as wild-type for all experiments. The strains used in this study are listed in the Supplementary Information (Supplementary Table 3).

Cold tolerance assay

The cold tolerance assay was conducted based on a previous report19,46. Well-fed worms were cultured on nematode growth media plates, 2% (wt/vol) agar plates in 3.5-cm-diameter plastic dishes inoculated with Escherichia coli OP50. For the experiments, 2 well-fed adult(s) (P0) were placed on a plate at the experimental temperature for a 15–20 h incubation period, which was terminated once ∼ 100 eggs were laid. P0 adults were then removed to synchronize growth. Progenies were incubated from egg to adulthood at 15 °C for 5–6 days. When animals reached adulthood, assay plates were chilled on ice for 20 min and then transferred to a 2 °C refrigerator (CRB-41A, DAIWA Industries LTD, Japan) for 48 h. Thereafter, assay plates were kept at 15 °C for 4–24 h before counting the number of living and dead worms. Each assay was performed ≥ 3 times. The temperature of every shelf inside the incubators was monitored using a wireless digital thermometer (SaverisH3D; Testo, Germany).

Genetic screen of cold tolerance mutants using EMS

L4-stage wild-type (N2) hermaphrodites were mutagenized with 0.02 mM EMS (Fujifilm Wako, 325-81522) for 4 h, being rocked and stirred once every hour. Then, animals were washed twice with NG buffer and allowed to recover on standard NGM plates. F1 animals of mutagenized P0 animals were cultivated at 20 °C. Approximately 2,127 F2 animals grown at 15 °C were transferred to 2 °C for 48 h and returned to 15 °C overnight. Each dead F2 animal carrying eggs was transferred to a new NGM plate. Hatched F3 larvae were raised to adulthood, and their progeny tested for cold tolerance. Cold tolerance assays for each mutant candidate were repeated ≥ 4 times to confirm the phenotype. KHR018 exhibited a significant reduction in cold tolerance and was backcrossed with wild-type worms seven times before further characterization.

Whole-genome DNA sequencing

Whole-genome DNA sequencing was performed as previously reported29. For genome extraction, worms were cultured in NGM under well-fed conditions at 15 °C. High-quality genomic DNA was extracted using a Gentra Puregene Tissue Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), following manufacturer’s instructions. KHR018 and N2 genomic sequences were obtained using an Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform and a TruSeq DNA PCR-Free LT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA), which generated 50–130 million reads (each read was 150 bp in length). Over 90% of the genome was sequenced at a coverage rate of 10 ×. The DNA sequence data reported in this paper have been deposited in the DNA Data Bank of Japan Sequence Read Archive. Whole-genome sequencing data have been deposited in the DRA of DDBJ under BioProject PRJDB18776.

Mapping the mutation responsible for the abnormal cold tolerance of the KHR018 mutant

We identified the gene responsible for the KHR018 mutation based on the large phenotypic differences between wild-type animals and KHR018 mutants. We crossed +/+; ExUDA17[AIYp::GFP, ges-1p::GFP] with a KHR018 mutant (P0) and placed F1 heterozygotes on fresh NGM and incubated from the egg stage to L4 or early adulthood. Animals expressing GFP were isolated as F2, yielding 20 recombinant lines. We then observed the phenotypes of these 20 recombinant lines from F3 to F4 generations; some exhibited abnormal cold tolerance. Finally, we crossed +/+; Ex[pKDK66 ges-1p::nls-gfp, pAK62 AIYp::GFP] with the KHR276 strain, a strain with abnormal cold tolerance. By repeating this procedure four times, a recombinant line KHR277 was obtained. In addition, we identified SNP patterns between the wild-type and KHR277 strain by deep sequencing. Simultaneously, we outcrossed KHR277 > 3 times and obtained KHR281 as a seven-times backcrossed strain.

Excluding multiple mutations within the same gene, mutations in the wild-type strain used in the laboratory, and mutations in the same position as other phenotypic backcross strains decoded by next-generation DNA sequencing, the number of candidate genes was reduced to 42. Accordingly, the chromosomal locations of the responsible genes in the mutants were mapped, decreasing the number of candidate responsible genes to nine genes on the X chromosome. A mutant strain lacking one of those showed abnormal cold tolerance. This gene was named slcr-46.1 as it is a homolog of SLC46A3 in mouse and human lysosomal membranes.

CRISPR/Cas9

Construction of a slcr-46.1 knockout mutant by CRISPR/Cas9 was conducted based on a previous report47. The slcr-46.1(chr51) mutant was developed using a co-CRISPR protocol with the dpy-10 marker48. pDD162 (Peft-3::Cas9) was injected into wild-type animals at 50 ng/μL with a targeted gRNA and dpy-10 gRNA in plasmid vectors at 25 ng/μL, the targeted donor oligonucleotide (gRNA1: GGAGGATATAGTGATCATCGAGG, gRNA2: CCCCCTTTCACGTGGGACACGTC), and the dpy-10(cn64) donor oligonucleotide at 500 nM. As the dpy-10(cn64) mutant exhibited a roller phenotype, we picked roller animals at F1 and screened for the mutation in slcr-46.1 by PCR and digestion with restriction enzymes. After detecting the mutation in slcr-46.1, the roller mutant was crossed with wild-type worms. Then, the slcr-46.1(chr51) mutation was verified by direct sequencing. A 1730 bp deletion including the 3rd–7th exons of slcr-46.1 (3443 bp) was detected in the slcr-46.1(chr51) mutant (Fig. 1d).

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from wild-type and slcr-46.1 mutant strains. Animals were cultivated on 2% (w/v) agar NGM plates seeded with E. coli OP50 in 6.0-cm plastic dishes. Several well-conditioned P0 adults were allowed to lay approximately 100 eggs before being removed to synchronize the F1 population. F1 animals were incubated from egg to young adult stage at 15 °C for about six days. After cultivation, animals were transferred to 2 °C for 2 h and incubated at 15 °C for 4 h before collection. Animals were harvested from the cultivation plates using M9 buffer (22 mM KH₂PO₄, 42 mM Na₂HPO₄, 85 mM NaCl, and 1 mM MgSO₄), placed in 1.5 mL tubes, and washed twice with M9 buffer. Total RNA was extracted using the Maxwell RSC Simply RNA Tissue Kit (Promega; AS1340) and the Maxwell RSC Instrument (Promega; AS4500), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Sequencing libraries were prepared with the TruSeq Stranded mRNA Sample Prep Kit (Illumina) according to manufacturer’s protocols. Library quality was assessed using a Bioanalyzer (Agilent). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating ≥ 60 million 150-bp paired-end reads per sample. RNA-seq data have been deposited in the DDBJ Sequence Read Archive under accession number BioProject: PRJDB35783. Raw RNA-seq reads were analyzed using CLC Genomics Workbench 21 (Qiagen, Germany). Gene set enrichment analysis was conducted using WormCat, an online annotation and visualization tool. Genes significantly upregulated (p < 0.01, log₂FC > 1) or downregulated (p < 0.01, log₂FC < – 1) by cold stimulation and fdx-2 or lias-1 knockdown were categorized and mapped to the C. elegans whole genome (version 2) following standard WormCat analysis parameters.

Confocal microscopy

Worms were placed on thin 2% agar pads formed on glass microscope slides and immobilized in 10 µL drops of 100 mM NaN3 before being coverslipped for confocal microscopy. Fluorescent images were acquired with confocal laser microscopes (FV1000-IX81 with a GaAsP photomultiplier tube, Olympus, Japan) using FV10-ASW (Ver. 04.02) software (Olympus, Japan) or (FV3000-IX83 with a GaAsP photomultiplier tube, Olympus, Japan) using FV31-SSW (Ver. 2.6.1.243) software (Olympus, Japan). We observed wild-type animals expressing plasmid constructs containing slcr-46.1cDNA::GFP and slcr-46.1ORF::VENUS driven by the slcr-46.1 promoter (3.0 kb) [slcr-46.1p_slcr-46.1cDNA::GFP (pSTM028), slcr-46.1p_slcr-46.1ORF::VENUS (pSTM016)].

Cellular expression and localization analysis of SLCR-46.1

For the identification of SLCR-46.1 expression patterns in neurons, a plasmid containing slcr-46.1cDNA::GFP driven by the slcr-46.1 promoter was introduced into NeuroPAL (OH16298) worms by microinjection together with pKDK66 ges-1p::NLS::GFP as an injection marker. SLCR-46.1::GFP-expressing cells were observed using an IX83 with CSUW1 disc confocal microscope. For the analysis of SLCR-46.1 cellular localization, immunostaining was performed as previously described28. Secondary antibodies used to detect binding of the primary antibodies (anti-LMP-1 or anti-GFP pAb (Medical & Biological Laboratories; Cat. no. 598; dilution 1:500)) included goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor® 488 (ThermoFisher Scientific: A-11001) and goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor® 546 (ThermoFisher Scientific: A-11010), each at a 1:1000 dilution. The anti-LMP-1 antibody was obtained from the Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank (https://dshb.biology.uiowa.edu). We use an antibody staining method with freeze and cracking cuticle to penetrate antibodies into the pharyngeal muscle (WormBook https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK19743/). Because the immunostaining signals were much stronger than the endogenous fluorescence, the background signal of autofluorescent granules in the intestine was markedly reduced.

Quantification of lysosomal activity in the pharyngeal muscle

Lysosomal activity was quantified using the Lysosomal-METRIQ protein probe30. Lysosomal-METRIQ contains a lysosomal protein DNaseIIα, mCherry, T2A and super fold GFP (sfGFP). The mCherry in Lysosomal-METRIQ is released during lysosomal transport and stays on the cytoplasm, while the DNaseIIα with sfGFP is transported into lysosomes. With high lysosomal activity, sfGFP will be degraded, and sfGFP fluorescence decreases. The fluorescent ratio between the sfGFP in the lysosome and the mCherry in the cytoplasm determines lysosomal activity. Lysosomal-METRIQ was expressed in the pharyngeal muscles of wild-type N2 and the slcr-46.1 mutant, driven by the myo-2 promoter. Transgenic worms were grown at 15 °C, and lysosomal activity measured with Lysosomal-METRIQ using a sCMOS camera ORCA-Fusion (C14440-20UP, Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) with CSU-W1 (Yokogawa Electric Corporation, Japan) in an inverted microscope IX83 (EVIDENT, Japan). Exposure time was 1 s. MetaMorph version 7.10.5 (Molecular device, USA) was used for quantification. Only fluorescence in the pharyngeal muscles of individual worms was used for analysis. RFP was excited by a 555 nm laser (LDi-7, 89 North, USA) and emission was collected at 617 nm by sCMOS camera ORCA-Fusion (C14440-20UP, Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) with CSU-W1 (Yokogawa Electric Corporation, Japan) in an inverted microscope IX83 (EVIDENT, Japan). sfGFP was excited by a 470 nm laser (LDi-7, 89 North, USA) and emission was collected at 520 nm by sCMOS camera ORCA-Fusion (C14440-20UP, Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) with CSU-W1 (Yokogawa Electric Corporation, Japan) in an inverted microscope IX83 (EVIDENT, Japan).

RNA interference of cuproptosis pathway genes

Since the homozygous Y73F8A.27(fdx-2) mutant is reported to exhibit embryonic lethality or sterility (as noted in Wormbase Phenotype), we performed RNAi knockdown experiments. Previous studies have shown that an RNAi knockdown of lias-1 does not cause developmental defects in F1 but induces larval arrest in F218. As previously reported, the E. coli strain HT115 used for dsRNA production in RNAi experiments can lipoylate proteins. However, even when feeding RNAi using HT115, larval arrest is still observed in F218, suggesting that exogenous lipoic acid cannot be used for mitochondrial protein lipoylation18. Accordingly, we used HT115 for RNAi, as with fdx-2 and lias-1, and limited cold tolerance assays to the F1.

RNAi screening was performed using the “feeding RNAi” method as previously described using the RNAi library constructed by Julie Ahringer’s group47,49. RNAi was conducted at 20 °C on NGM plates supplemented with 1 mM IPTG (Wako, Osaka, Japan). RNAi food, an HT115 bacterial strain expressing dsRNA against a target gene, was cultured at 37 °C with shaking for 5 h in LB medium consisting of 100 mg/mL ampicillin. Pelleted bacteria dissolved in 75 μL LB were spread onto an NGM plate and used as an RNAi plate. The next day, L4 larva P0 animals were placed on the RNAi plate and cultivated overnight at 20 °C; then, these animals were transferred onto another RNAi plate for egg laying overnight. Then, P0 adult animals were removed from the RNAi plate, and a cold tolerance assay [15 °C → 2 °C] was performed after 3 days. Portions of FDX-2(Y73F8A.27) and LIAS-1(M01F1.3) DNA for RNAi were amplified by PCR using the following primers and cloned into the L4440 vector. Primers: FDX-2(Y73F8A.27) F: 5′-GGG AGA CCG GCA GAT CTG ATT GTC CTC CGT GCT CAG GCT G-3′ and R: 5′-ACG GTA TCG ATA AGC TTG ATA ATG AGG TTT CGG CAC ATG C-3′, LIAS-1(M01F1.3) F: 5′-GGG AGA CCG GCA GAT CTG ATC TCG TGG ATC TCT GAC CCA T-3′ and R: 5′- ACG GTA TCG ATA AGC TTG ATC ACG ATT TTC GCC TGA TTT T-3′. Each primer was observed using JBrowse v2 15.4 (https://jbrowse.org/jb2/) to determine the RNAi target. Then, these plasmids were transformed into HT115 bacteria.

CF4 assay

The CF4 probe was prepared according to previously published methods50. The wild-type strain and the slcr-46.1 mutant were used for the CF4 assay. After cultivating the worms at 15 °C until young adulthood, 300 µL of 25 µM CF4 was added to each plate with worms. For worms without cold stimulation, the plate was left in the dark at 15 °C for 12–16 h; worms were then transferred to a new plate after washing with M9 buffer three times. After 1 h of storage in the dark at 15 °C, CF4 signals were detected using a confocal microscope, IX83. For the assay immediately after cold stimulation, 300 µL of 25 µM CF4 was added to the plate, and worms were incubated at 15 °C in the dark for 12–16 h. During CF4 staining, the plate was subjected to 2 °C for 2 h. Worms were then washed three times with M9 buffer, transferred to a fresh plate, and maintained at 15 °C in the dark for 1 h before CF4 fluorescence was detected using a confocal microscope. For the recovery assay after following cold stimulation, worms were placed at 2 °C for 2 h, followed by the addition of 300 µL of 25 µM CF4 to the plate. Worms were then incubated at 15 °C in the dark for 12–16 h, washed three times with M9, transferred to a fresh plate, and maintained at 15 °C in the dark for 1 h before CF4 fluorescence was detected. CF4 was excited by a 445 nm laser (LDi-7, 89 North, USA) and emission collected at 482/35 nm using an sCMOS camera ORCA-Fusion (C14440-20UP, Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) with CSU-W1 (Yokogawa Electric Corporation, Japan) in an inverted microscope IX83 (EVIDENT, Japan). All CF4-stained images were acquired with a 1 s exposure time with 1 × 1 binning. For each animal, CF4 fluorescence was acquired using MetaMorph version 7.10.5 (Molecular Devices, USA). CF4 fluorescence intensity was quantified by subtracting the background value using MetaMorph version 7.10.5 (Molecular Devices, USA) for Fig. 3g–i, and with cellSens version 4.4 image analysis software (Evident, Japan) for Fig. 3j. For Fig. 3g–i, the average CF4 fluorescence intensity per animal was calculated by outlining the edge of the pharyngeal muscle in focus as the regions of interest (ROI). For Fig. 3j, pixels exceeding the background noise level were extracted, and their total area within the terminal bulb of the pharyngeal muscle was calculated as the ROI.

Copper imaging in S2R+ cells

Drosophila Schneider 2 R+ (S2R+ ) cells were obtained from the Drosophila Genomics Resource Center (DGRC Stock 150; RRID:CVCL_Z831) and maintained in Schneider’s Drosophila medium (Gibco, 21720024) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Corning, 35-010-CV) at 25 °C. To establish a stable S2R+ cell line harboring an inducible slcr-46.1::mCherry gene, cells were co-transfected with 210 ng of pMT-slcr-46.1::mCherry and 0.2 μg of pUChsNEO using XtremeGENE9 DNA transfection reagent (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasmids were diluted in 1 × OPTI-MEM medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY) before transfection. After 24 h, the transfected cells were washed twice with Schneider’s medium and further cultured at 25 °C. After 2 days, cells were reseeded onto a 100-mm dish and cultured in Schneider’s medium containing 300 μg/mL G-418 for selection. The culture medium containing G-418 was replaced every 3–4 days. After 2 weeks, the dish was washed with fresh medium, and the surviving cells were collected and maintained as polyclonal stable cell lines. For copper imaging experiments, cells expressing slcr-46.1::mCherry were seeded at 1–2 × 10⁵ cells per well. Cells were treated with 50 μM CuCl₂ diluted in culture medium for 1 h at 25 °C, followed by one wash with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). For lysosomal labeling, cells were incubated with 100 nM LysoTracker Deep Red (Invitrogen, L12492) for 1 h at 25 °C, followed by one PBS wash. Intracellular Cu⁺ was detected using CF4 dye (final concentration 1 μM in PBS) for 1 h at 25 °C, followed by a PBS wash. Fluorescence images were acquired using an FV3000 confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with a 60 × objective lens. Excitation/emission settings were as follows: CF4, 445 nm excitation and 520–540 nm detection; mCherry, 561 nm excitation and 590–620 nm detection; LysoTracker Deep Red, 640 nm excitation and 655–700 nm detection. Spectral settings were optimized to avoid channel overlap. CF4 fluorescence was quantified at regions where the lysosomal marker (Lysotracker Deep Red) colocalized with SLCR-46.1::mCherry. In control cells, CF4 fluorescence was quantified at regions positive for Lysotracker Deep Red.

Cold tolerance assay with chemicals

Animals were cultivated on 3.5 cm-diameter plastic plates containing 2% (w/v) agar NGM plates with Escherichia coli OP50. Adult wild-type (N2) and slcr-46.1 mutant hermaphrodites (P0) were washed three times with M9 buffer and allowed to recover for 30 min on NGM plates without OP50. Then, adults were collected in M9 buffer, a few drops of OP50 were added, and the eggs were incubated overnight at 15 °C. On the following day, F1 larvae hatched from eggs were transferred with M9 buffer onto drug-treated NGM plates, raised to adulthood, and subsequently tested for cold tolerance. Chemical stocks (Ferrostatin-1: 250 mM or SIH: 250 mM) were prepared in pure dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and BCS (50 mM) in deionized H2O. These solutions were then added to the NGM agar to final concentrations of: BCS (CAS No. 98645-85-3) 100 μM, Ferrostatin-1 (CAS No. 347174-05-4) 250 μM, or SIH (CAS No. 495-84-1) 250 μM, in 0.1% DMSO as the solvent. The final concentrations of each of the three chemicals were based on previous reports17,36,51. NGM agar without chemicals was used as the control and was prepared by adding DMSO to NGM to a final concentration of 0.1% DMSO. Each cold tolerance and temperature acclimation test was conducted using > 3 plates per day. Plates containing > 30 worms were used for counting live and dead animals. All data were obtained from ≥ 3 independent experimental days.

Mitochondria labeling

Adult animals synchronized from eggs were incubated with 0.1 μM tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE; T669, Thermo Fisher Scientific), a mitochondrial membrane dye, for 16 h. The TMRE probe was prepared according to previously published methods17. Fluorescence imaging was performed using an inverted microscope (IX83, EVIDENT, Japan) equipped with a spinning disk confocal unit (CSU-W1, Yokogawa Electric Corporation, Japan) and an sCMOS camera (ORCA-Fusion C14440-20UP, Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan). TMRE was excited using a 555 nm laser (LDi-7, 89 North, USA), and emission was collected at 565–625 nm. Animals showing punctate TMRE signal accumulation were considered to exhibit marked mitochondrial accumulation in pharyngeal muscle, defined as more than 10 puncta distributed along the longitudinal axis in each animal, and the percentage of such animals was quantified.

Activation of the oxidative stress pathway assay

To analyze the activation of the oxidative stress pathway, we used the CL2166 dvIs19 [(pAF15) gst-4p::GFP::NLS] strain, which expresses GFP under the gst-4 promoter35. By crossing this strain with the slcr-46.1 mutant, we quantified gst-4p::GFP in the slcr-46.1 mutant. Wild-type worms and the slcr-46.1 mutant expressing gst-4p::GFP were synchronously cultured for 6 days at 15 °C and observed under an IX83 microscope (EVIDENT, Japan). Photographic conditions were x40, bin = 1, exposure time 1000 ms. MetaMorph version 7.10.5 (Molecular device, USA) was used to quantify gst-4p::GFP signals.

Locomotion behavior

The locomotory rate assay was based on a method previously described52. Assay plates (6 cm in diameter) containing 2% agar with 1 mM CaCl₂, 1 mM MgSO₄, and 25 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) were used. After transferring C. elegans animals to the assay plate, body bends were counted at 2 min after placement.

Growth rate

The fraction of animals laying eggs at each time point after hatching was calculated. The time when egg laying started was used as an indicator of adulthood.

Brood size

The brood size was counted based on a previously reported24. A single animal was grown at 15 °C on an NGM plate (3.5 cm in diameter) until the adult stage and transferred to a new plate every 24 h for the next 9 days. We counted the number of eggs laid at each time point.

Body length

The length of adult animals was measured on lateral views observed under a Nomarski microscope in FV3000 (Olympus, Japan). Length was calculated using cellSens version 4.4 image analysis software (Evident, Japan).

Fatty acid composition

Lipids were extracted from synchronized cultures of young adult wild-type and slcr-46.1 mutant worms and transmethylated as previously described20,23. The fatty acid methyl esters in wild-type and the slcr-46.1 mutant were analyzed by gas–liquid chromatography and identified by comparing peak retention times with authentic standards. Fatty acid composition is presented on a percentage weight basis. All samples were collected at three independent times.

Statistical analysis

Cold tolerance assays were prepared on >9 plates on ≥ 3 independent days. In the figures, error bars indicate the standard error of the mean (SEM). For cold tolerance assays, the number of assays indicates independent experimental replicates performed on separate plates and/or days. For quantitative analyses (e.g., fluorescence intensity measurements), “n” indicates the number of independent biological replicates, as specified in each figure legend. Assuming that all data followed a normal distribution, all statistical analyses were performed using parametric tests: the Tukey–Kramer method, or the unpaired t test (Welch). Multiple comparisons were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA); the comparison was tested using the Tukey–Kramer method. Comparisons between two groups were performed using the unpaired two-tailed t test (Welch). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01. All tests were two-sided with a family-wise significance level (α) of 0.05. Adjustments for multiple comparisons were applied using Tukey’s method where appropriate. Where applicable, 95% confidence intervals are reported in the figure legends or Source Data file. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.2). Test statistics, degrees of freedom, effect sizes, and exact P- values are reported in the figure legends where applicable.
