Antibacterial and anti-biofilm mechanisms of 1,8-cineole against colistin-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii: an integrated in vitro, gene expression, and in silico study
Authors: Milad Kashi, Aboalhasan Basil Sami Alshemri, Zahra Alimardan, Mohammad Arjomandzadegan, Yasaman Hariri, Zahra Chegini, Aref Shariati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rise of colistin-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (ColR Ab) poses a major clinical challenge, underscoring the urgent need for alternative antimicrobial agents. This study evaluated the antibacterial and anti-biofilm mechanisms of 1,8-cineole against ColR Ab using in vitro, transcriptional, and in silico approaches. Minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) and anti-biofilm activities were determined against three ColR Ab isolates. Membrane integrity was assessed via protein and nucleic acid leakage assays. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed bacterial morphology and biofilm structure, and expression of biofilm-associated genes was analyzed by qRT-PCR. Additionally, molecular docking analysis was employed to evaluate the binding affinity of 1,8-cineole against key target proteins of A. baumannii. The inhibition zone in the disk diffusion method and MIC were 10.3 ± 0.8 mm and 3.6 mg/mL, respectively. 1,8-cineole also caused significant protein and nucleic acid leakage and, at 4× MIC, significantly disrupted the mature biofilm structure. SEM confirmed bacterial cell membrane rupture, intracellular content leakage, and ultrastructural damage following treatment. qRT-PCR validation demonstrated that 1,8-cineole significantly downregulated the expression of the bfmR, bap, csuE, ompA, and pgaB, while abaI expression was not significantly affected. Molecular docking simulations predicted favorable binding of 1,8-cineole to quorum-sensing regulators AbaR and LuxR-family regulator. 1,8-cineole exhibits significant bactericidal and anti-biofilm activity against ColR Ab. Its mechanism of action may involve disruption of cell membrane integrity and potential interference with bacterial key quorum-sensing regulators. This study provides a theoretical basis for developing 1,8-cineole as a potential therapeutic strategy against ColR Ab infections.

Introduction

Acinetobacter baumannii is a major Gram-negative, aerobic, and non-motile microorganism responsible for healthcare-associated infections. Notably, this non-fermentative bacterium is characterized by its prolonged environmental persistence on solid surfaces and extensive natural and acquired antibiotic resistance profiles1. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) A. baumannii has emerged as a formidable clinical threat, frequently causing recalcitrant skin and soft tissue infections. This issue is especially problematic among burn victims, where the pathogen triggers severe secondary complications, including impaired wound healing, skin graft rejection, and life-threatening sepsis2. As a leading cause of healthcare-associated infections, this pathogen exhibits rapid dissemination within clinical environments, significantly elevating morbidity and mortality rates, especially among critically ill individuals. With MDR phenotypes emerging in up to 70% of clinical isolates in specific geographic regions, the World Health Organization (WHO) has designated A. baumannii as a top-tier critical priority pathogen, underscoring the pressing need for innovative therapeutic strategies3,4.

A. baumannii employs a diverse array of mechanisms to confer antimicrobial resistance. These strategies encompass the secretion of β-lactam-hydrolyzing enzymes, the upregulation of active efflux systems, and the target-specific enzymatic alteration of aminoglycosides. Furthermore, resistance is mediated by modifications at the antibiotic target site and structural changes in outer membrane porins, which consequently diminish cellular permeability5. These processes facilitate the formation of A. baumannii strains that exhibit multi-resistance to a majority of antibiotics, including aminoglycosides, cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and carbapenems1. The emergence of carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii poses a critical clinical challenge, as these antibiotics are typically the final therapeutic option for MDR Gram-negative infections. In Acinetobacter species, this resistance is predominantly mediated by the acquisition of carbapenemase enzymes. Consequently, the rising incidence of carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii has necessitated the use of alternative, second-line antimicrobials, such as colistin and tigecycline, for clinical management6.

Despite colistin being designated as a last-resort treatment, colistin-resistant A. baumannii strains (ColR Ab) have been documented globally. The worldwide incidence of ColR Ab isolates is estimated at around 4%7. A. baumannii employs multiple strategies to evade colistin toxicity. Primary mechanisms include the complete loss of lipopolysaccharides (LPS) resulting from mutations in biosynthetic pathways, or the structural alteration of lipid A via the attachment of phosphoethanolamine groups. This latter modification is typically driven by the upregulation of intrinsic chromosomal genes, such as eptA and the pmrCAB operon, or through the horizontal acquisition of plasmid-borne mcr determinants. Furthermore, the bacterium employs additional defense mechanisms, including active drug extrusion via efflux pumps, altered outer membrane permeability via porin modifications, and heteroresistance8.

Furthermore, biofilm formation has emerged as a critical pathogenic characteristic among A. baumannii virulence factors, conferring resistance to stressors such as desiccation, immune evasion, and antibiotics3. The biofilm-producing capacity of A. baumannii significantly enhances its prolonged survival within healthcare environments, particularly on medical equipment and in intensive care units (ICUs). This biofilm architecture, coupled with the bacterium’s inherent genetic plasticity, grants the pathogen a remarkable proficiency in acquiring broad-spectrum antibiotic resistance and facilitating the nosocomial spread of these resistance traits9. Consequently, the discovery of innovative antimicrobial agents and therapeutic interventions to manage ColR Ab infections is critically urgent. Driven by the escalating rates of drug resistance in recalcitrant pathogens, researchers are increasingly exploring unconventional treatment modalities. These emerging paradigms encompass a diverse array of options, such as phytochemicals derived from medicinal plants10.

Secondary metabolites derived from plant extracts play a significant role in alternative antibacterial methods, primarily owing to their low toxicity and extensive antimicrobial range. These bioactive compounds, which enhance antimicrobial activity, can be assessed individually or in conjunction with antibiotics as a potential solution to antibiotic resistance11,12.

1,8-cineole, commonly known as eucalyptol, is a prominent bioactive compound naturally present in several essential oils, notably those derived from eucalyptus and rosemary. This phytochemical has been extensively documented for its diverse biological properties, including significant antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects. Pure 1,8-cineole is used to treat respiratory infections, such as bronchitis or the common cold, and as an additional treatment for sinusitis13,14. Several studies reported that 1,8-cineole exhibits antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Salmonella, Escherichia coli, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa15–17. Although 1,8-cineole exhibits well-established anti-inflammatory and antibacterial properties against various bacterial strains, its precise mechanisms of action remain poorly understood, particularly against MDR pathogens such as ColR Ab. Therefore, this study primarily aims to evaluate the antimicrobial and antibiofilm efficacy of 1,8-cineole as a prospective therapeutic alternative for ColR Ab infections. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation exploring the compound’s potential against ColR Ab, with the ultimate goal of elucidating its mechanism of action against this highly resistant pathogen.

Methods and materials

Phytochemicals and bacterial isolates

1,8-cineole (CAS number 470-82-6) was purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The culture medium and antibiotic disks were purchased from Quelab Laboratories (Montreal, QC, Canada) and Padtan-Teb Co. (Tehran, Iran). In our previous work, we isolated and characterized three ColR Ab strains from clinical settings in Tehran, Iran. Briefly, these isolates exhibited MDR profiles and high colistin MICs (≥ 4 µg/mL), driven by documented mutations in the pmrA/B and lpxA/C/D genes18. The standard reference strain, A. baumannii ATCC 19,606, was used as a control.

Disk diffusion method

A 100 µL aliquot of bacterial suspension, adjusted to 0.5 McFarland, was spread evenly onto Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA) plates. A sterile disk was impregnated with 20 µL of 1,8-cineole (18.42 mg/disk) and placed on the agar surface. In addition, disks containing a standard concentration of meropenem (10 µg) were used as positive controls to compare its antibacterial activity with that of the 1,8-cineole. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, the diameter of the inhibition zone was measured in millimeters with a ruler, and the results were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD)10.

Protein and nucleic acid leakage assay

The assessment of released nucleic acids and proteins was performed based on our previously described protocol. Briefly, the isolates were incubated in TSB medium with and without 1× MIC of 1,8-cineole for 6 h at 37 °C. After centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 15 min) and filtration (0.22 μm) of the cultures, the extracellular nucleic acid content was quantified by measuring the absorbance at 260 nm using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer. Furthermore, the protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using the Bradford assay, with absorbance measured at 595 nm20.

Minimal biofilm eradication concentration (MBEC)

To evaluate the eradicative impact of 1,8-cineole on pre-formed biofilms, strong biofilm-producing ColR Ab strains were cultured statically in flat-bottom 96-well microtiter plates for 24 h to establish mature biofilms. Following the removal of non-adherent planktonic bacteria, 200 µl of 1,8-cineole at 2× MIC and 4× MIC was added to the respective wells, followed by another 24-hour incubation period. Unattached cells were again discarded, and the wells were washed three times with 200 µl of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The remaining biofilm biomass was stained using a 0.1% crystal violet solution for five minutes at ambient temperature. Excess stain was discarded, and the wells were washed thrice with distilled water. The bound crystal violet was then dissolved in 95% ethanol, and the optical density was recorded at 600 nm. The biofilm eradication rate was calculated as a percentage compared to the untreated control group10. Light microscopy (LM)

To visually validate the efficacy of 1,8-cineole in eradicating biofilm, light microscopy (LM) was employed. Initially, sterile glass coverslips (1 × 1 cm) were placed into a 6-well microtiter plate. Each well was inoculated with 1 mL of a bacterial culture (10⁸ CFU/mL) prepared in tryptic soy broth (TSB) containing 1% glucose. After a 48-h static incubation at 37 °C to establish robust biofilms, the non-adherent cells were aspirated, and the coverslips were washed three times with PBS. The mature biofilms were then treated with 500 µL of 1,8-cineole at 2× MIC and 4× MIC concentrations. Plates were incubated statically for another 24 h at 37 °C. Post-incubation, coverslips were gently rinsed with PBS, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and subsequently washed with deionized water to remove unbound dye. Once air-dried, the biofilm structures on the coverslips were visualized using a Leitz HM-LUX 3 light microscope at 400× magnification21.

Scanning electron microscope analysis (SEM)

The antibiofilm efficacy of 1,8-cineole against the established biofilms was further evaluated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). An 8-well chamber slide (SPL Life Sciences, Gyeonggi-do, Korea) was utilized for biofilm development. After incubation, the biofilms were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by three washes with PBS. The samples were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, air-dried, and sputter-coated with gold. Morphological analysis of the biofilms was performed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Model AIS2300C, Seron Technologies)22.

Assessing the impact of 1,8-cineole on the expression of biofilm-related genes

To evaluate the effect of 1,8-cineole at a sub-inhibitory concentration (½ MIC) on the expression levels of biofilm-related genes (abaI, bfmR, bap, csuE, and pgaB), qRT-PCR assays were used. The bacterial cells were grown statically in 50 mL Falcon tubes containing TSB medium, either in the presence or absence of ½ MIC 1,8-cineole, for 24 h at 37 °C. Total RNA was extracted using the SinaPureTM RNA kit (Sinaclon, Iran) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The qRT-PCR assays were performed in triplicate on a LightCycler 96 system (Roche, USA) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Sinaclon, Iran). Molecular modeling and docking procedure

The crystallographic structures of OmpA and LpxA from A. baumannii (OmpA: PDB ID=4G88; LpxA: PDB ID=4E6T) were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (RCSB; https://www.rcsb.org/)29. Before docking, all non-essential molecules, including co-crystallized ligands and water molecules, were removed from the protein structures using Accelrys Discovery Studio Visualizer 4.0.230. The three-dimensional structure of 1,8-cineole was constructed using MarvinSketch v5.7 (ChemAxon)31. As no experimentally resolved structures were available for AbaR, BfmR, LuxR-family regulator (ASF75575.1), and PmrB proteins in the PDB, a homology modeling approach was applied. The amino acid sequences of these proteins were obtained from the NCBI database. Subsequently, their three-dimensional models were generated using the SWISS-MODEL server, which builds protein structures based on homologous templates. Template selection was automatically performed according to sequence identity, structural coverage, and Global Model Quality Estimation (GMQE) scores, and the top-ranked models were selected for further processing. To evaluate the quality and reliability of the predicted models, structural validation was conducted using the ProTSAV platform32. This integrated server assesses stereochemical properties, residue environment compatibility, and overall structural quality using parameters such as Ramachandran plot distributions, Z-score analyses, and non-bonded interaction assessments. Only models exhibiting acceptable validation metrics were retained. Potential ligand-binding sites and active pockets were identified using the CavityPlus web server33, which predicts cavities based on geometric and physicochemical features and ranks them according to drug ability. The predicted binding sites were used to define the docking grid parameters. Further protein preparation steps were carried out using AutoDockTools. Polar hydrogen atoms were added, and Kollman and Gasteiger charges were assigned to proteins and ligands, respectively. Non-polar hydrogens were merged, and all structures were saved in PDBQT format. The grid box center was defined based on the coordinates of key residues within the predicted active site. A grid box of 60 × 60 × 60 points with a spacing of 0.345 Å along the x, y, and z axes was applied. Docking simulations were performed using AutoDock with 100 independent runs for each ligand–protein complex. The resulting conformations were ranked according to their binding free energies. Finally, ligand–protein interactions were analyzed and visualized using AutoDockTools based on the generated docking log (DLG) files.

Statistical analysis

Three independent bacterial isolates were evaluated in this study, and each experiment was performed in triplicate. Data are presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical analyses of gene expression data were conducted using paired t-tests and Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank tests. Differences in protein and nucleic acid leakage were assessed using paired t-tests, and differences in antibiofilm activity among the treatment groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparison test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Assessment of the antibacterial activity of 1,8-cineole

According to Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion test results, 1,8-cineole exhibited antibacterial activity, with 10.3 ± 0.8 mm of clear inhibition zones forming around the disk (Fig. 1). The inhibition zone observed for meropenem (7 mm) suggests that the isolates are resistant to this antibiotic. The MIC, determined by broth microdilution, was 3.6 mg/mL for 1,8-cineole against ColR Ab isolates.

Effects of 1,8-cineole on the protein and nucleic acid leakage

The absorbance of the supernatant from ColR Ab isolates at 260 nm was measured to determine the release of nucleic acids. Figure 2 shows the absorbance values obtained when the strains were treated with 1,8-cineole at the MIC. Bacteria not exposed to 1,8-cineole were used as the control. A statistically significant difference was found when the absorbance values of the 1,8-cineole treatment (OD₂₆₀ = 1.363) were compared with the control (OD₂₆₀ = 0.523). In the Bradford assay, the effect of 1,8-cineole on protein leakage from the ColR Ab isolates was studied. The results, shown in Fig. 2, indicate that 1,8-cineole resulted in leakage of proteins from ColR Ab. Protein leakage in the untreated control (0.62 µg/mL) was lower than that in cells treated with 1,8-cineole (9.65 µg/mL).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The scanning electron micrographs of the treated bacterial cells (Fig. 3) revealed drastic morphological alterations compared to the typical coccobacillary morphology of untreated cells. The most prominent feature observed is the severe flattening and complete structural collapse of the bacterial bodies. Instead of maintaining a typical turgid, coccobacillary geometry, the cells appear extensively shriveled and deflated, indicating a catastrophic loss of intracellular turgor pressure. The cell envelopes exhibit significant wrinkling, irregularity, and evident loss of membrane integrity. Furthermore, extensive cell lysis is apparent, with individual cells fusing into amorphous aggregations of cellular debris. These profound physical deformities suggest that the treatment severely disrupts the bacterial cell wall and membrane, resulting in massive cytoplasmic leakage, structural disintegration, and ultimately, cell death.

Effect of 1,8-cineole in biofilm eradication

The strains employed for this study were those with the best biofilm production and highest biofilm-forming capacity, as determined in a previous investigation10. Quantitative crystal violet assays demonstrated that biofilm formed by ColR Ab isolates was significantly eradicated after exposure to 1,8-cineole (Fig. 4). Treatment at 2× MIC resulted in a 22% reduction in biofilm biomass, which increased to 55% at 4× MIC, indicating a dose-dependent disruption of established biofilms (Fig. 4).

Molecular docking analysis

The interactions between 1,8-cineole and the proteins AbaR, BfmR, LuxR-family regulator, PmrB, OmpA, and LpxA are presented in Figs. 8 and 9, and Table 2. The docking results suggest that 1,8-cineole may bind favorably to the studied proteins, with negative binding energies (− 8.87 to − 11.8 kcal/mol), suggesting spontaneous and stable interactions. The strongest affinities were observed for AbaR and LuxR-family regulator, while LpxA showed the lowest Ki value, suggesting better inhibitory potential. Overall, Ki values in the micromolar range reflect moderate inhibition. The interactions are mainly driven by hydrophobic and van der Waals forces, consistent with the non-polar nature of 1,8-cineole, while a few hydrogen bonds (e.g., with Lys6, Gly40, Lys242, and Glu161) enhance stability and specificity. Collectively, these findings suggest that 1,8-cineole may interact with multiple targets, including proteins involved in key bacterial processes, and could be considered a candidate for antibacterial development.

Discussion

The growing prevalence of MDR Gram-negative bacteria, coupled with the scarcity of novel antibiotics, has created an urgent need for innovative strategies to circumvent resistance mechanisms, particularly against last-resort drugs such as colistin. Addressing this critical challenge, the present study demonstrates the antibacterial and anti-biofilm properties of 1,8-cineole as a promising new approach to combat ColR Ab.

All bacterial strains selected for the study were found to be resistant to most routinely used antibiotics, including colistin. Under these conditions, 1,8-cineole showed remarkable antimicrobial activity against these isolates. Although previous studies have not directly investigated the effect of 1,8-cineole on ColR Ab, several studies have examined 1,8-cineole or EOs in which 1,8-cineole represents a dominant constituent against less susceptible strains of A. baumannii. For example, Pimenta dioica leaf oil displayed MIC ranging from 0.52 to 5.18 µg/mL against MDR clinical isolates of A. baumannii34. Consistently, another report documented MIC values for Eucalyptus spp. EOs spanning from 14.1 ± 1 to 16.1 ± 2 µg/mL against A. baumannii ATCC 1960635. Similarly, EO obtained from Cinnamomum camphora demonstrated inhibitory activity against MDR A. baumannii isolates, with MICs ranging from 1.04 to 6.25% (v/v)36. In a separate study, essential oils derived from Eucalyptus spp. exhibited MIC values between 0.25 and 2% (v/v), while purified 1,8-cineole showed an MIC of 1% (v/v) against A. baumannii NCTC 784437.

The comparatively higher MIC values observed in our study (3.6 mg/mL) are likely attributable to the use of highly resistant clinical strains, which differ fundamentally from the less resistant isolates examined in most previous reports. Also, such values are not unexpected for hydrophobic natural compounds, which often exhibit limited aqueous solubility and reduced apparent activity in broth dilution assays. Additionally, it should be considered that many earlier studies evaluated EOs rather than purified compounds. Notably, EOs comprise complex mixtures of bioactive constituents that may influence antibacterial activity. Notably, our results extend previous observations by demonstrating effective inhibitory activity against ColR Ab. It is believed that the antibacterial activity of natural compounds is linked to their hydrophobicity, which can disrupt bacterial membranes, consequently increasing cell permeability and causing leakage of cellular constituents38. In this context, for the explicit mechanism of action 1,8-cineole against ColR Ab, we evaluated an intracellular components leakage assay. Our results showed leakage of proteins and nucleic acids following treatment with 1,8-cineole, suggesting that the bacterial membrane/cell wall may be affected. Although the effect of pure 1,8-cineole on A. baumannii has not been previously reported, evidence from EOs containing this compound, such as Eucalyptus camaldulensis, suggests that it may act via membrane disruption and increased permeability39. Observations in other bacterial species support this hypothesis, as 1,8-cineole has been reported to enhance membrane permeability and induce leakage of nucleic acids and proteins in methicillin-resistant S. aureus and to cause comparable effects in carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae40,41. Further supporting these findings, we performed SEM observations after treatment with 1,8-cineole to examine potential morphological alterations in the bacterial cells. Following treatment with the compound, the surviving cells exhibited significant morphological alterations, including shrinkage, structural collapse, gap formation, and detachment. These changes are attributed to cell wall disruption, membrane degradation, and subsequent cytoplasmic loss. Consequently, the primary antimicrobial mechanism of 1,8-cineole appears to involve severe damage to the bacterial membrane, leading to elevated permeability, leakage of intracellular components, and ultimate cell death.

Biofilm formation by pathogens like A. baumannii significantly contributes to antimicrobial resistance and clinical treatment failure. Consequently, identifying novel agents capable of inhibiting biofilms offers substantial benefits for managing biofilm-related infections and mitigating the spread of antibiotic resistance. In the subsequent phase of this research, we evaluated the antibiofilm efficacy of the tested compound against isolates classified as strong biofilm producers. Most reports emphasize that natural compounds are more effective during the early stages of biofilm formation42. However, the present study demonstrates that 1,8-cineole also has the ability to destroy mature biofilm, highlighting its potential to act against the more resistant biofilm stage. The decrease in cell viability was further supported by SEM micrographs, which revealed severe membrane damage and cell lysis following exposure to 1,8-cineole. Additionally, microscopic observations showed bacterial dispersal within the biofilm and a significant decrease in the covered area after exposure to 1,8-cineole, indicating an overall loss of biofilm structural integrity. Considering these results, the antibiofilm properties of 1,8-cineole may have potential clinical relevance. The ability to target both early- and mature-stage biofilms could be valuable for managing biofilm-associated infections and may help slow the spread of antibiotic resistance.

It is essential to recognize that disruption of specific cell structures can trigger a cascade of events, compromising the overall stability of other cellular components. Although natural compounds typically interact with multiple targets within bacterial cells, these effects are frequently linked, either directly or indirectly, to initial damage sustained by the cell envelope. To further investigate the molecular basis of the observed antibiofilm activity, we investigated critical genes associated with biofilm formation, specifically abaI, bfmR, pgaB, csuE, bap, and ompA. 1,8-Cineole has an impressive suppression of the bfmR, pgaB, csuE, bap, and ompA gene expression. Notably, the down-regulated genes were mainly those related to initial stages of biofilm formation, particularly cellular attachment. In A. baumannii, the csuE, integral to the usher–chaperone assembly mechanism, is crucial for promoting attachment and biofilm formation. Similarly, ompA facilitates adhesion and biofilm development. Biofilm-associated protein (Bap) is essential for initial biofilm adhesion, promotes biofilm development, and maintains the structure of mature biofilms10. The pgaB gene is involved in the export of poly-β-(1–6)-N-acetylglucosamine (PNAG), one of the major components of extracellular polysaccharides and an important factor in biofilm development43.

In contrast, a non-statistically significant downregulation was observed for the abaI gene, which encodes a quorum-sensing molecule that catalyzes the synthesis of acyl-homoserine lactone signals and is essential for biofilm development on abiotic surfaces44. Since these genes play essential roles in bacterial adhesion, biofilm development, and extracellular matrix production, their reduced expression is consistent with the significant decrease in biofilm biomass and the disruption of mature biofilm architecture observed in the crystal violet and SEM analyses. These findings suggest that the antibiofilm activity of 1,8-cineole is associated not only with direct cellular damage but also with the modulation of key genetic determinants required for biofilm development and maintenance.

To further explore the therapeutic potential of 1,8-cineole, molecular docking studies were conducted to examine its interactions with bacterial targets involved in biofilm formation. The molecular docking results suggest that 1,8-cineole may interact favorably with several key proteins of A. baumannii (AbaR, BfmR, LuxR-family regulator, PmrB, OmpA, and LpxA), with binding energies ranging from − 8.87 to − 11.8 kcal/mol. The strongest affinities were observed for AbaR and LuxR-family regulator, suggesting these as potential primary targets. Across all complexes, non-covalent interactions, particularly hydrophobic and van der Waals forces, were dominant, consistent with the non-polar nature of 1,8-cineole. Hydrophobic residues such as methionine, valine, and phenylalanine played key roles in stabilizing the ligand–protein complexes. In addition, hydrogen bonds (e.g., with Gly40, Lys242, and Glu161) contributed to binding specificity and further stabilization. Targeting multiple proteins involved in quorum sensing, membrane integrity, lipid A biosynthesis, and regulatory pathways suggests a potential multi-target mechanism of action, which may be advantageous against bacterial resistance. Overall, these findings highlight 1,8-cineole as a promising candidate for antibacterial development, although further experimental validation is required. In conclusion, the docking results suggest that 1,8-cineole exhibits favorable binding to several key proteins of A. baumannii, primarily through hydrophobic interactions supported by occasional hydrogen bonds. This multi-target interaction pattern highlights 1,8-cineole’s potential as a promising lead compound for the development of novel antibacterial agents against MDR strains.

Among the potential pathways proposed in previous studies for antibiofilm activity of natural compounds, our findings suggest that 1,8-cineole may exert its antibiofilm activity through multiple mechanisms, including impeding bacterial adhesion, decreasing cell viability within the biofilm, disrupting the production or stability of biofilm biomass, dispersing bacteria within the biofilm, and interacting with key biofilm-related genes and proteins. This multi-target profile is an antibacterial mechanism that could effectively disrupt biofilm formation and maintenance, which is often implicated in antibiotic resistance and chronic infections.

Together, the in silico and in vitro results suggest that 1,8-cineole interacts with multiple bacterial targets, including membrane components, key proteins, and biofilm-related genes. This property of 1,8-cineole, which acts on multiple bacterial targets, unlike conventional antibiotics that target specific bacterial pathways, reduces the likelihood of selecting resistant mutants; however, dedicated studies are required to evaluate this possibility. Meanwhile, several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. The cytotoxicity assays were not performed, and antibacterial and antibiofilm activities were evaluated only in vitro; no in vivo validation was conducted. Further studies, including cytotoxicity evaluation, ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) profiling, formulation optimization, pharmacokinetic investigations, and in vivo experiments, are required before considering this compound for therapeutic development.

Conclusion

The emergence of drug resistance to conventional antibiotics has necessitated the development of alternative antimicrobial agents. For the first time, we demonstrated that 1,8-cineole has antibacterial and antibiofilm effects against ColR Ab. Analysis of intracellular content release and SEM observations suggested that 1,8-cineole’s antibacterial activity may primarily be associated with disrupting cell wall/membrane integrity and altering cellular morphology. Sub-inhibitory doses of this compound effectively reduced biofilm formation and expression of key biofilm-related genes, suggesting a potential role in impeding biofilm development. It has the ability to disrupt mature biofilms and interact with biofilm-associated proteins. Based on the present findings, 1,8-cineole represents a promising candidate for further investigation as a potential therapeutic agent against resistant strains of A. baumannii and possibly other antibiotic-resistant pathogens. The present study provides a foundational proof-of-concept for the antibacterial potential of this compound. However, subsequent in vivo investigations are required to optimize its formulation, comprehensively assess its safety profile, and validate its clinical efficacy, thereby translating these in vitro observations into practical applications.
