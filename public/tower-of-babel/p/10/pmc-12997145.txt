Uptake and engagement of a clinically moderated digital peer support platform to support the mental well‐being older adults: a qualitative investigation
Authors: Jessica Wilson, Milena Heinsch, Campbell Tickner, Bronte Speirs, Hannah Wells, Frances Kay‐Lambkin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe uptake of traditional mental health services among older adults remains low. Digital peer support offers older adults a resource for engaging with others to independently support their mental well‐being. This qualitative study explored the uptake and engagement of a clinically moderated digital peer support platform (Breathing Space) for older adults with depressive symptoms and alcohol use concerns.MethodsSemi‐structured interviews with 30 participants aged 60–80 years explored participants' uptake and engagement with Breathing Space, a novel, moderated, private, and anonymous peer support platform. Data were analysed using reflective thematic analysis and are discussed with reference to the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology2.ResultsThree themes were constructed to characterize participants' experiences: (i) navigating the complexities of peer‐peer online engagement; (ii) the function of anonymity in online connection; and (iii) experiences of app features and content.ConclusionsFuture development of digital peer support for older adults would benefit from the following: (i) co‐design with older adults; (ii) providing choice over anonymity and increased options for interacting with peers; (iii) streamlining the basic functionality with popular platforms; (iv) providing options for users to curate their digital experience; and (v) providing telephone support for troubleshooting technical difficulties. Future research should explore the use of digital peer support among older adults who experience social exclusion challenges.Keywords: e‐health interventions, peer support, social connectedness, social isolation, technology acceptance, UTAUT2

INTRODUCTION

Older adults are the fastest growing age demographic globally, 1 and ~15% experience a mental illness, including depression and problematic alcohol use. 2 Indeed, depression is more common than dementia in older adulthood (7% vs 5% of the global population, respectively), is associated with adverse health effects including poorer overall functioning, and is often overlooked due to the co‐occurrence of other health conditions. 2 Older adults also have the highest rates of social isolation among all populations, 3 which is linked to higher mortality rates. 4 The health restrictions imposed during the COVID‐19 pandemic were unequally felt by older adults, further exacerbating depression and social isolation in this population. 5 Unfortunately, as people live longer, the above risks to mental well‐being increase. 6 Additionally, older adults are the least likely age group to access mental health services and, when they do, treatment responses are not always effective, 7 highlighting the urgent need for developing accessible mental health solutions and encouraging social connectedness to support the mental well‐being of older adults.

Digital mental health has significant potential to enhance mental health outcomes for older adults, 8 with technology use among this population increasing substantially in recent years. 9 While relatively few published studies have evaluated digital mental health interventions for older adults, those that have provide preliminary evidence for their utility. 10 , 11 In particular, peer support interventions have been found to be acceptable, cost‐effective, and equivalent to individually delivered internet interventions for this population. 12 Benefits of peer support interventions include (i) increased social connectedness and inclusion, 13 , 14 (ii) enhanced mental and physical well‐being, 15 , 16 (iii) development of new coping skills 15 and hobbies, 13 and (iv) improved access to support between clinical appointments. 15

Despite these promising findings, older adults continue to be the largest group facing difficulties accessing and using digital interventions. 17 , 18 , 19 Barriers include a lack of access to or support to use technology, digital health illiteracy, and physical limitations associated with ageing (i.e., poor eyesight, hearing, and memory). 13 , 17 , 18 , 19 These barriers, as well as a fear of technology replacing usual social connection and communication, were identified in a recent study specifically exploring older adults’ use of social technology. 20 Conversely, social encouragement and support, increased social capital, and the ability for the technology to facilitate visual communication were identified as facilitators of social technology use. 20

Despite this evidence and other recent reviews investigating the uptake, adoption, and use of digital health and social technologies by older adults, 21 , 22 , 23 there are no published studies exploring the uptake and engagement of purpose‐designed social support technologies providing a private and anonymous space for older adults with specific well‐being concerns. Therefore, the objective of the current study was to qualitatively explore older adults’ uptake and engagement of a clinically moderated digital peer support platform (Breathing Space) for supporting mental well‐being. We investigated the uptake and engagement of Breathing Space and used the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology2 (UTAUT2) 24 as a framework for discussion of the findings.

METHODS

Study design

The current article reports on a subset of findings from a randomized controlled trial that examined the effectiveness of SHADE Plus, a digital treatment package (digital intervention, digital peer support community, and weekly supportive check‐ins) targeting symptoms of depression and alcohol use in Australian adults aged 60 years and older (https://www.anzctr.org.au/ registration: ACTRN12620000366954). To be eligible for the trial, participants needed to (i) be aged 60 years or over; (ii) have regular access to a tablet, computer, or smartphone with an internet connection; and (iii) be experiencing problematic alcohol use and low mood. This article reports on the qualitative findings of the trial that relate to the uptake and use of the Breathing Space platform (described below) by older adults. While the present study included a subsample of the wider SHADE Plus trial, the findings presented here are not intended to represent those of the larger sample.

Breathing Space digital peer support community

Breathing Space is a private, clinically moderated online social networking platform developed by our research team as an adjunct resource for peer connection provided to participants engaged in various digital intervention trials conducted by our team. Breathing Space is therefore only currently available to the research team's study participants and is accessible by either smartphone application or web browser. Each research study has a private ‘group’ within Breathing Space (the current group was ‘SHADE Plus’), and interaction with members of other groups is not possible.

Breathing Space prompts users to engage and interact with each other by (i) posting into the community using text and images, or purpose‐built emojis to indicate their mood or feelings (i.e., positive: happy, strong, hopeful; neutral: restless, thoughtful, lonely; or negative: craving, depressed, angry); (ii) reacting to posts by other community members using purpose‐built responses (i.e., ‘thank you for sharing’, ‘I feel like that too’, ‘I understand’); (iii) commenting on posts by other community members; and (iv) sending private messages to community members with whom they have established a connection. Clinical moderation of the community is provided by researchers JW, BS, HW, and FKL. JW is a researcher and doctoral social work student, BS is a researcher and clinical psychologist, HW is a researcher with a doctorate in social work, and FKL is a senior research psychologist with a doctorate in psychiatry. Each clinical moderator shared the responsibilities of (i) facilitation of engagement between community members; and (ii) posting about various topics concerning mental health and alcohol use among older adults on a predetermined schedule. Moderators JW and BS completed risk and welfare checks when community members indicated feelings of emotional distress.

To address barriers to accessibility of digital tools by older adults identified in prior research, 19 we included the following additional features and resources in Breathing Space: (i) adjustable text size; (ii) text to speech functionality via Kanzi tool (https://kanzi.io/); and (iii) a weekly 10‐min telephone check‐in with a research clinician for technical and/or emotional support for the duration of the 10‐week trial.

Procedure

A total of 145 Australians participated in the SHADE Plus trial between May 2021 and June 2022 and were recruited via a paid social media campaign conducted by the clinical research recruitment agency TrialFacts (https://trialfacts.com/). All participants were invited to complete the qualitative interview during their 12‐week post‐intervention follow up. Thirty‐five participants consented, and 30 participants completed the interviews, which were conducted between August 2021 and September 2022; three could not be contacted and two had scheduling conflicts that could not be resolved. All participants received an information statement and completed electronic informed consent via REDCap data collection software (https://www.project-redcap.org/), and consenting participants were contacted via telephone or email (contact preference elicited at baseline data collection) to schedule a time for the interview.

Data collection

Interviews were completed by telephone (with the exception of one interview that was completed via Zoom at the participant's request), averaged 30 min in duration, and were digitally recorded and professionally transcribed (https://www.outscribetranscription.com.au/). During the interviews, we asked participants about their experiences, barriers to engagement and use, benefits of use, and whether and for what reason they ceased use, of Breathing Space. Interviews were conducted by the first and fourth authors (project manager and research assistant, respectively). Participants were given the opportunity to review and amend their interview transcripts and were offered a $20 digital shopping voucher as reimbursement for their time. Each participant was asked to confirm their consent to participate at the beginning of each interview and was informed that they could stop the interview at any time without having to provide a reason. No participant chose to amend their transcript or ceased their interview. Demographic information collected included age, gender, sexual identity, education, employment status, relationship status, and number of children.

Analytic approach

Qualitative data were analysed using Braun and Clarke's six stage reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) approach. 25 , 26 RTA enables researchers to construct meaning‐based patterns and themes, while acknowledging both the theoretical underpinnings and the centrality of the researcher as an analytic resource. 25 , 26 This analytic approach was selected as it encourages open acknowledgement of the researchers’ influence over the interpretation of data and facilitates a more comprehensive and unrestrained analysis of the participants' experiences.

As any analytic method is grounded by theoretical assumptions, it is crucial to articulate our own. 24 Our analysis was underscored by social constructionism, which posits that knowledge and meaning are constructed through social interchange. 27 As such, we acknowledge that the interpretation of meaning stems from interaction between researchers and participants, and is influenced by their respective backgrounds and worldview. 28 , 29 This perspective views research not as a singular truth, but as something that has been co‐created. 30 Rather than conducting a theoretically‐informed deductive analysis, we used social constructionism as a guiding lens throughout the analytic process.

Data analysis was led by the first author, with support and guidance provided by the third and fourth authors. We began by familiarizing ourselves with the data by reading and re‐reading the transcripts and making preliminary notes. We then progressed to initial coding using Microsoft Word, ascribing pithy labels to each relevant segment of data using the comment function in Word. Each transcript was then reviewed again to refine codes to clarify intended meaning, and these codes and associated quotes were added to the digital tool Miro (https://miro.com/), which acted as an online whiteboard where codes could be arranged and clustered as needed. In Miro, initial theme generation was an iterative process whereby codes were clustered together based on the similarity of an idea or concept and given descriptive names. The finalization and naming of themes was an ongoing process throughout the write‐up phase, guided by familiarization notes recorded during analysis.

Qualitative findings

Navigating the complexities of peer‐peer online engagement

Participants reported varied experiences of peer‐peer engagement with Breathing Space. The analysis revealed nuanced aspects of participant interaction with the platform, implicit barriers to engagement, and contextual factors that influenced the platform's perceived usefulness to the community.

Both active participants (those who posted or responded) and passive participants (also known as ‘lurkers’, who only read the content but did not interact) perceived Breathing Space as a safe and empathetic environment where support was given and received:

Members were empathetic to others. I would see some of the comments and if someone's had a bad day people would come in and support them and, you know, wish them a good day and do all that. So it appeared to be a very supportive site for some people. So, I guess it was a place where people could go and say hey, you know, the little face, not having a good day and people, you know, there would be somebody there, people would respond to that. (Female, age 65)

Participants reported that they primarily rely on friends and family for support. However, for those who lacked social connections, the supportive digital community was crucial, offering empathetic support and a connection to peers:

Being able to have an electronic means and particularly if you're housebound like I am. Knowing that there's somebody at the end of the iPad or the computer or the phone or whatever that you can tap into, without actually having to … because I wouldn't get up and go to a group meeting.… If you're socially isolated and it can often feel like it's you against the world, and my goodness … even just to read you know, I'm feeling distressed or I'm angry, or I'm feeling hopeful. (Female, age 64)

Participants highlighted the contrast between Breathing Space and larger online forums. They appreciated that Breathing Space offered a more personal and intimate atmosphere where individual voices were not lost, and the online connection and relatability was more profound:

I've used [larger forums] a couple of times but it's never been really helpful. The age group's so broad, the people that read it at that time might not relate to specific issues that you might be having and so it just gets lost. And then I'm just being swallowed up. I'm a little fish in a great, big, massive ocean. And nobody really … I don't think anybody really hears this. A lot of forums are generic people and going ‘I get that, yes, I went through …’ but they could be 22 and I'm 64.… So, it's been good to have. Not just your peers but people that have been through life. On Breathing Space you're actually heard. (Female, age 64)

While some participants had positive experiences with Breathing Space, others preferred more traditional and direct methods of communication, noting the lack of immediate feedback and validation of their experience on Breathing Space:

And that's the thing with the app, you can put something on there, but you don't get feedback straightaway. You've got to go back to it later. (Male, age 60)

There was no real person at the other end. Whereas with you [on the phone], we can connect on all sorts of levels and communicate as such. Frankly, talking is easier. (Female, age 71)

Some participants reported difficulty connecting or relating to their peer group on Breathing Space. One participant was uncomfortable with the group dynamic, while others were discouraged by the low level of engagement among members, resulting in a general reluctance to engage and share with the community:

I had a conversation with [research clinician] around the non‐alcoholic drinks and the ones that are coming out on the market. She said, ‘oh look that would be a great post, why don't you think about starting a thread on that’. I don't know why I didn't do it, I'm not sure why I didn't, but anyway. (Female, age 60)

For some, there was a perception that their contributions would not be seen as interesting, valuable, or meaningful, or that their problems were not significant enough to warrant sharing:

I mean, these people have much worse issues than I've got. You know, there's no point me posting anything, because they've got much worse problems, you know, etc., etc. So, it was sort of that thing of comparing, but in a different way. (Female, age 68)

The function of anonymity in online connection

The theme of anonymity emerged as a key topic among participants in the Breathing Space community. Older adults with addiction‐related issues found Breathing Space to be a particularly helpful option because it allowed them to seek support without revealing their struggles to their social or professional networks:

If someone had a really bad drinking problem, and they didn't want to have to embarrass themselves, if they knew how they felt about it themselves. The anonymity would really work well. (Female, aged 71)

Indeed, for one person, this was their motivation for joining the study:

I've sort of been through two phases of my life, one being drugs and the other being alcohol. So I could really fall back into that trap, basically. And I wanted strategies to stay away from that. Mainly because I'm on work cover now. I had to see a psychiatrist. I had to kind of keep mum on difficulties I've had with addiction. (Female, age 64)

However, the anonymity provided to participants was also seen by some as depersonalizing the experience of interacting with others:

I did find a bit of a disconnect between it being like an anonymized space and being supportive as well. We are given our usernames that are sort of not our own names. So, it's this anonymous space, but when it hasn't come necessarily from us, it's externally provided … it's possibly a bit performative. (Female, age 68)

One participant suggested adding the option to share non‐identifying personal information, such as location, to promote connection. Another recommended adding videoconferencing capabilities for members who prefer a less anonymous interaction:

When I got back after the cruises I opened it [Breathing Space] up a couple of times but I haven't felt inspired to engage with the app. And I think the reason for that is it's two dimensional. I do a lot of online meetings and you can see the person. Are they happy? Are they sad? Are they animated? Are they pleased to be talking to me? What I would love to see in Breathing Space is, if it were possible to allow that video conference capacity, whether it's just once a week, we're gonna open it up and for an hour, and anyone who wants to come in and can come in, and you know, sort of get to learn who the other people are a bit better. That would make the Breathing Space something I would want to engage with. (Male, age 60)

Experiences of app features and content

Participants who reported limited engagement with, or benefit from using, Breathing Space identified opportunities for improving the app design and content. Some expressed frustration that the app is not similar enough to popular social media platforms like Facebook, underscoring the importance of incorporating familiar features to increase acceptance of new digital technologies. In particular, there was a desire for a wider selection of emojis to discreetly display emotions, consistent features across devices (smartphone and tablet), and notifications that clearly indicate the type of activity within the app:

Or even the little reminders, I mean I know it used to say so and so has posted but I mean maybe even a little thing that said, new article posted and I'd probably go, oh, well I might just go in and have a bit of a look at that. That'd probably be a bit of a motivator, actually, rather than the person [member] posting. (Female, age 60)

Others indicated that the level of digital literacy needed to use the app might be too advanced for some older adults. This, however, was not expressed by anyone who had difficulties themselves:

I'm pretty okay with technology. So some old people may not have that same sort of ability. So that may be detrimental to them. (Female, age 71)

Regarding app content, participants shared their personal challenges with the Breathing Space community and received support and validation in return. One person highlighted the significance of having a space to ‘feel down’ and receive support during such times:

[I'm] sick of hearing about resilience, do we all have to be resilient? Sometimes you just feel down and you want to feel down and you want someone to sit with you in that place. (Female, age 60)

However, some participants perceived the sharing of struggles as negative and depressing, creating a community that was not seen as a source of upliftment during low moods. One participant felt a sense of obligation towards other members that was overwhelming, hindering their engagement. Despite this, others found a sense of balance through the clinical moderation:

Various people were saying, I've had a terrible day, and it's been a real struggle. I'd read it, and I think it brought me down. It depressed me. I thought, I've had a struggle, but I don't really want to know about yours. It's making me feel worse. The problem was, you've got to go through the negative comments to find the positive ones. The comments from you guys, the moderators, were always very positive and uplifting. But to get through to them, I had to go through the negative ones as well. (Female, age 65)

DISCUSSION

This article explored uptake and engagement with an innovative, clinically moderated digital peer support community specifically designed for older adults with depressive symptoms and alcohol‐related concerns. In total, three themes were constructed to characterize participants' experiences: (i) navigating the complexities of peer‐peer online engagement; (ii) the function of anonymity in online connection; and (iii) experiences of app features and content. The findings are discussed with reference to the UTAUT2 24 and recommendations are provided for the future development of digital peer support for older adults. The UTAUT2 24 models consumer technology use and includes seven constructs: (i) Performance Expectancy (perceived utility), (ii) Effort Expectancy (ease of use), (iii) Social Influence (perception that close others believe the person should use the technology), (iv) Facilitating Conditions (resources and support), (v) Hedonic Motivation (enjoyment and pleasure), (vi) Price Value (benefits versus monetary cost), and (vii) Habit (use of technology is automatic). The UTAUT2 was chosen for this study as it has been applied in various research contexts, 31 including qualitatively evaluating the use of social communication technologies among older adults. 32 The UTAUT2 was not used as an analytical framework. Rather, it was applied post‐analysis to enhance the interpretation of older adults' uptake of, and engagement with, Breathing Space.

Breathing Space was perceived as an empathetic and supportive environment by participants, whether or not they actively contributed. Some participants did not see a need for additional peer support, and thus did not use the app. This finding contradicts the findings of Andrews et al., 33 who found that older adults preferred supportive technology for their mental health to relieve the burden on others. In the current study, socially isolated participants reported more app use and more positive experiences. The exclusive nature of Breathing Space as a platform for older adults with similar experiences (low mood and alcohol concerns) was welcomed by participants, as it differentiated the app from larger online forums where their voices may go unheard. These findings support the importance of co‐design for end‐user utility. They suggest that high perceived usefulness of technology enhances adoption and engagement with digital support, supporting the Performance Expectancy construct of the UTAUT2. 24

Participants also discussed the impact of the non‐optional anonymity of the Breathing Space community. Some saw it as valuable for addressing stigmatized issues like mental health and alcohol concerns, while others found it depersonalizing and hindering interpersonal connection and support. These findings support Wang et al. 34 who found that anonymity resulted in weak ties and limited long‐term engagement of an anonymous social networking app. Future digital peer support may therefore benefit from offering the option of non‐anonymous engagement, such as video‐chat. This supports Roberts et al. 16 who found that a video‐facilitated peer‐led mood support program was highly acceptable among older adults, and Wilson et al. 20 who described the capacity for video as facilitating face‐to‐face connection. Additionally, Bixter et al. 32 found that social influence was important for the use of social communication technologies. Thus, for some, the anonymity of Breathing Space may have hindered the social influence aspect of the UTAUT2, which is critical in influencing older adults' use of communication technology. This finding is also in line with Wilson et al., 20 who found social encouragement and support were facilitators of social technology use.

In the final theme, participants suggested features to increase useability and uptake of Breathing Space by older adults, including functionalities similar to popular social media platforms (e.g., Facebook), interactive features (e.g., expanded emoji options), tailored notifications, and seamless device integration (e.g., smartphone and tablet). These suggestions align with the Effort Expectancy construct of the UTAUT2, 24 which posits that ease of use leads to increased technology adoption, and supports Wilson et al., 19 who found that incorporating user preferences in digital tool design improves useability for older adults. Additionally, some participants sought primarily positive content on the Breathing Space app, expressing frustration with an overabundance of negativity, aligning with the Hedonic Motivation construct of the UTAUT2, 24 which links pleasure and enjoyment with the intention to use technology. In line with Andrews et al., 33 this suggests that digital tools should allow users to customize and control their content exposure.

Overall, our findings support the Performance and Effort Expectancy, and Hedonic Motivation constructs of the UTAUT2. 24 Regarding Performance Expectancy, participants used and benefited from Breathing Space when there was perceived need (social isolation) and utility (social connection and support). Regarding Effort Expectancy, which is moderated by age and thus more important for older adults, participants made useful suggestions to increase ease of use of Breathing Space. Additionally, participants disengaged from Breathing Space where they encountered negative content from other members within the group, aligning with the Hedonic Motivation construct. Regarding Social Influence (also moderated by age) 24 the anonymous, private, and mental well‐being focus of Breathing Space may have hindered participants from connecting with other group members or discussing their use of the platform with their social circles, potentially acting as a barrier to uptake and engagement. Further, there were no explicit qualitative outcomes aligning with the Facilitating Conditions construct of the UTAUT2, 24 referring to the availability of resources and support to use technology and is also moderated by age. Importantly, in response to research exploring barriers and facilitators to the use of digital health technologies among older adults, 19 text and speech functionality in consideration of some physical limitations was added to Breathing Space, and each participant received one‐on‐one digital telephone support. Therefore, the Facilitating Conditions construct may have been supported by these considerations. Finally, as the study only included older adults with access to digital devices and the internet, this may have inhibited outcomes aligning with the Habit and Price Value constructs of the UTAUT2. 24

Limitations and future directions

The qualitative findings in this article should be considered in light of the following limitations. First, the participants were mostly highly educated (53% held a tertiary degree) and relatively young (mean age of 66 years), which may have influenced their experience of the Breathing Space technology. Further research is needed to examine the adoption of digital peer support in more diverse groups of older adults. Second, the interviews were conducted by the same research team members who conducted the supportive telephone check‐ins, which may have influenced participant responses. Finally, the older adults in this study needed to have access to technology and internet to participate, and as a result, the findings may not represent the experiences of older adults without digital accessibility. Future studies should focus on exploring digital access challenges faced by older adults experiencing social exclusion.

CONCLUSIONS

The findings reported in this article highlight the potential mental well‐being benefits of the Breathing Space platform for older adults. Three themes were constructed to characterize participants’ experiences using Breathing Space, showing that those who were lacking social support or engagement were most likely to benefit, while enforced anonymity, novel functionality, and non‐customizable features were barriers to uptake and engagement. The following recommendations are suggested for future development of digital peer support for older adults: (i) involve end‐users in the design process to ensure the platform is user‐friendly; (ii) offer end‐users control over anonymity and expand communication options, such as video chat; (iii) make the platform's functionality similar to popular platforms and provide options for customizing the experience, such as adjustable notifications and the ability to filter posts or topics; and (iv) provide telephone support for technical assistance.
