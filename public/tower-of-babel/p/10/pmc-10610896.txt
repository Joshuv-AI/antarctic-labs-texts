Regulating reconstruction of oxide-derived Cu for electrochemical CO2 reduction toward n-propanol
Authors: Chang Long, Xiaolong Liu, Kaiwei Wan, Yuheng Jiang, Pengfei An, Caoyu Yang, Guoling Wu, Wenyang Wang, Jun Guo, Lei Li, Kanglei Pang, Qun Li, Chunhua Cui, Shaoqin Liu, Ting Tan, Zhiyong Tang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Oxide-derived copper (OD-Cu) is the most efficient and likely practical electrocatalyst for CO2 reduction toward multicarbon products. However, the inevitable but poorly understood reconstruction from the pristine state to the working state of OD-Cu under strong reduction conditions largely hinders the rational construction of catalysts toward multicarbon products, especially C3 products like n-propanol. Here, we simulate the reconstruction of CuO and Cu2O into their derived Cu by molecular dynamics, revealing that CuO-derived Cu (CuOD-Cu) intrinsically has a richer population of undercoordinated Cu sites and higher surficial Cu atom density than the counterpart Cu2O-derived Cu (Cu2OD-Cu) because of the vigorous oxygen removal. In situ spectroscopes disclose that the coordination number of CuOD-Cu is considerably lower than that of Cu2OD-Cu, enabling the fast kinetics of CO2 reaction and strengthened binding of *C2 intermediate(s). Benefiting from the rich undercoordinated Cu sites, CuOD-Cu achieves remarkable n-propanol faradaic efficiency up to ~17.9%, whereas the Cu2OD-Cu dominantly generates formate.

INTRODUCTION

Renewable electricity–driven electrochemical reduction reaction of CO2 (CO2RR) to upgraded chemicals can simultaneously mitigate the overwhelming carbon footprint and store intermittent energy for use on demand (1–4). In the past decade, continuous efforts have been devoted to pushing the performance boundary toward the critical point of economic benefit to meet the commercialization request, especially C1 and C2 products, like CO (5, 6), formic acid (7, 8), and ethylene (9). Nevertheless, high-order alcohol, typically n-propanol, has remained a minor product with faradaic efficiency (F.E.) usually less than 10%, regardless of its high-octane number and portable liquid state as an ideal engine fuel with high market value (10, 11).

By far, Cu is the only metallic catalyst promising for C2+ products in CO2RR, thanks to its suitable binding strength toward *CO (asterisk denotes the absorbed state) and sequential *C2 key intermediates (12–14). Specifically, previous experimental and theoretical observations on Cu surfaces have disclosed that the rate-determining step for C2 products, such as ethylene and ethanol, is the *CO dimerization (12, 14–16). Furthermore, the n-propanol as a C3 alcohol shares the identical initial *CO dimerization pathway with C2 products followed by the C-C coupling between *CO and *C2 intermediates, which are mostly referred to *OCCO, *OCCOH, *HOCCOH, and/or *CCH2 (12–14, 17, 18).

Among various Cu materials, the oxide-derived (OD) Cu has received the most attention for C2+ production due to its high electrocatalytic activity, easy preparation, and low cost (10, 19, 20). We notice that during oxygen removal under reductive electrolysis, the pristine Cu oxides will inevitably reconstruct and yield its OD-Cu with metastable defective grain boundaries, where the diverse types of active facets/sites are responsible for CO2RR to C2+ products (20–22). Recent works have further provided valuable insight that the undercoordinated Cu sites play a vital role in *CO dimerization and *C2 stabilization, promoting C-C coupling and final n-propanol formation (17, 18, 23). Therefore, there comes an open and crucial question: How can one design the OD-Cu catalyst bearing richer undercoordinated sites via reconstruction under reductive working conditions? Different Cu oxides have been reported to exhibit varied performance regarding the selectivity of the final product(s), ranging from C1 to C2+ products (19, 24–27), suggesting that one cannot overlook the importance of their pristine state on the electrocatalytic products.

Here, we assume that the two typical Cu oxides, CuO and Cu2O, will be of distinct reconstruction behavior under reductive conditions. On the basis of molecular dynamic (MD) simulation, we first envision the reconstruction process of CuO and Cu2O toward their respective derived Cu, namely, CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu. Because of the vigorous oxygen removal from the parent lattice structure, CuOD-Cu intrinsically owns a rougher surface and richer population of undercoordinated Cu sites than the Cu2OD-Cu counterpart. Then, we prepare CuO and Cu2O and comprehensively study their structure and performance in CO2RR. In situ Fourier-transformed extended x-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) reveals that both CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu are in metallic state under reductive potential, while the average coordination number (CN) of CuOD-Cu is 9.1, 11% lower than that of Cu2OD-Cu. Electrochemical surface analyses further disclose that the roughness of CuOD-Cu is around 32% higher than that of Cu2OD-Cu. Both measurements are in good agreement with the MD simulations. Benefiting from its intrinsic reconstructed surface, CuOD-Cu achieves impressive n-propanol F.E. up to 17.9% at −0.94 V (all potentials refer to reversible hydrogen electrode, abbreviated as RHE unless special note). Not only the materials that we synthesize but also the commercial CuO and Cu2O work well with this theory and experimental finding.

RESULTS

MD simulation of reconstruction from Cu oxides to derived Cu

To visualize the surface reconstruction behavior from CuO and Cu2O to their respective derived Cu, we resort to neural network potential–based MD (NN-MD) simulation of the reduction process of seven-layered CuO and Cu2O models, which involves gradual oxygen removal (see Materials and Methods for details) (28). Figure 1A shows the snapshots of the initial models and their final configurations after oxygen removal, characteristic of pure metallic Cu surfaces. Evidently, the CuOD-Cu surface tends to collapse after reduction MD cycles, while Cu2OD-Cu maintains relatively flat, originating from the vigorous reconstruction of Cu atoms after a higher amount of oxygen removal on CuOD-Cu compared with Cu2OD-Cu (movies S1 and S2). Specifically, Fig. 1B plots the statistics of surface Cu atom density in the supercell, which is defined and calculated by dividing the number of Cu atoms on the surface by the area of the cell XY plane, and points out that the surface Cu atom density is 19.47 and 17.69 atoms/nm2 for CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu, respectively. As a comparison, the value of the most common lowest energy Cu(111) surface is 17.68 atoms/nm2, which is close to Cu2OD-Cu but notably less than CuOD-Cu. This result highlights that the CuOD-Cu tends to have a rougher surface than Cu2OD-Cu during reconstruction. Meanwhile, to investigate the structural difference between CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu, the surface Cu atoms of varied CNs are carefully counted (see Materials and Methods for details). As Fig. 1C depicted, the density of surface Cu sites for most types of Cu sites on CuOD-Cu is higher than that on Cu2OD-Cu, particularly the large population of Cu sites with CN ranging from 6 to 9. Thus, a reasonable conclusion is drawn that the major difference between CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu should be attributed to the higher population of Cu with CN ranging from 6 to 9, which has been theoretically demonstrated efficient for C-C coupling in previous studies (17, 18, 23). Briefly, the MD simulations reveal that CuOD-Cu has a rougher surface with richer undercoordinated Cu sites than Cu2O.

Synthesis and characterization of Cu oxides and derived Cu

Armed with the simulations, we investigated CuO and Cu2O, as well as their OD-Cu in the experiment (fig. S1). The CuO and Cu2O samples were synthesized by treating the freshly acid-cleaned Cu foil with dilute and condensed NaOH aqueous solution, respectively (see Materials and Methods for details). After NaOH was etched, the color of parent Cu foils changed into black or red, indicating the formation of CuO or Cu2O on the surface, respectively. The scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images manifest that the as-prepared black sample appears with a stacking belt-like morphology, while the red counterpart shows a particle-like shape (Fig. 2A and figs. S2 and S3). High-resolution transmission electron microscope (HR-TEM) and high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images clearly display the lattice fringes of CuO d<111> = 0.230 nm and Cu2O d<110> = 0.301 nm for the black and red samples, respectively (Fig. 2B and figs. S4 and S5). These distinct oxide phases are supported by the grazing incident x-ray diffraction (GI-XRD) patterns (fig. S6). The SEM observations uncover that similarly to previous studies (19, 29), both CuO and Cu2O undergo notable reconstruction and yield the corresponding OD-Cu under reduction conditions (Fig. 2C). It is noticed that both CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu lose their parent morphology and emerge in the shape of a polygon. To check the detailed difference between CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu, the localized structures are discerned by HR-TEM characterization. As illustrated in Fig. 1D, CuOD-Cu has much more fragments than Cu2OD-Cu does. Besides, HAADF-STEM images reveal that CuOD-Cu experiences more pit-like collapses at the microscale, while Cu2OD-Cu is rather flattened without pits, resulting in a larger population of irregular nanograins of Cu species characteristic of defective undercoordinated Cu on CuOD-Cu than those on Cu2OD-Cu (fig. S7).

To ascertain whether Cu oxides are completely reduced, in situ Raman measurements were performed to monitor the surface chemicals. As shown in Fig. 2E, the characteristic CuO signals at 290, 327, and 609 cm−1 totally vanish within a few minutes once the reductive potential is applied. Such complete reduction behavior is also observed for Cu2OD-Cu. We find that the oxides reappear on both CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu after deploying the negative potential in several seconds, suggesting the rapid reoxidation nature of OD-Cu samples (fig. S8). This result explains the observation of oxides in HR-TEM images for both CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu because of the inevitable oxidation in electrolytes or air without reducing potential (19, 30). Concurrently, to figure out that CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu are metallic in bulk phase or merely the surficial phase, in situ x-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) measurements were used to supplement the in situ Raman tests. Both CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu showcase their absorbing edge at 8979 eV, clearly distinct from either CuO (ca. 8984 eV) or Cu2O (ca. 8982 eV) standard samples and consistently overlapping with the Cu foil standard (Fig. 2F). Therefore, it is deduced that both CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu are reduced to be metallic in bulk phase instead of merely the surficial part, agreeing with the previous report (20). Last, to investigate the exposed facets of metallic CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu, the electrochemical OH adsorption (OHad) measurements were carried out. The voltammograms exhibit a series of facet-dependent peaks in the potential region of 0.35 to 0.50 V (Fig. 2G and fig. S9), which are assigned to Cu(100), Cu(110), and Cu(111) at potentials of 0.38, 0.42, and 0.48 V, respectively (31). For both CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu, all three facets explicitly coexist with neglectable differences, likely because that the CO intermediates generated in CO2RR bind on the reconstructed surfaces of two OD-Cu samples and guide their facet growth (32). This result excludes the facet selectivity as the major cause for the difference in performance.

In situ XAS study on reconstruction from Cu oxides to derived Cu

To understand the evolution of active sites, in situ x-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) measurements were performed to probe the localized structure (33, 34). The linear component fitting for XANES during the reconstruction process from CuO to CuOD-Cu discloses that its initial oxidation state is +2 and rapidly converts to metallic within 20 min (Fig. 3A and figs. S10 to S12). A similar chemical state change is found on Cu2OD-Cu from Cu2O, and the +1 oxidation state is reduced into a metallic state within 20 min (Fig. 3A and figs. S10 to S12). Accordingly, as revealed by the R space of FT-EXAFS, the characteristic signals of CuO (Cu-O scattering at 1.56 Å and Cu-Cu scattering at 2.51 Å before correction) and Cu2O (Cu-O scattering at 1.50 Å and Cu-Cu scattering at 2.80 Å before correction) disappear gradually, while those of metallic Cu-Cu bond (Cu-Cu scattering at 2.24 Å before correction) simultaneously increase during CuO-to-CuOD-Cu and Cu2O-to-Cu2OD-Cu processes (Fig. 3B). The wavelet transformation of EXAFS at Cu K-edge also suggests that as-formed CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu have the identical features with Cu foil standard (fig. S13). It needs to be stressed that despite the metallic state of both CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu, the localized structure obviously differs from each other. The fitting result of FT-EXAFS uncovers that the CN of Cu-Cu first shell for CuOD-Cu is 9.1, 11% lower than that of the counterpart Cu2OD-Cu with CN being 10.2 (Fig. 3, C and D, figs. S14 to S17, and table S1). Since the EXAFS reflects the average localized information of the detected sample, this CN difference highlights that CuOD-Cu has a substantially larger population of undercoordinated Cu sites than Cu2OD-Cu, accounting for the more efficient multicarbon selectivity based on previous reports (17, 23). Furthermore, the surface roughness factor (RF) supports the EXAFS result. Specifically, the RF of CuOD-Cu obtained from electrochemical measurement is approximately 32% higher than that of Cu2OD-Cu (Fig. 3, E and F, and figs. S18 to S20), indicating that the exposure of undercoordinated sites is much richer on CuOD-Cu. These results are well consistent with the above MD simulations that CuOD-Cu has higher surface Cu density and more undercoordinated Cu than Cu2OD-Cu.

Understanding of electrocatalytic routes on derived catalysts

To explore how the reconstructed surface with varied coordinated sites and surface roughness affects the reaction pathways during CO2RR, in situ surface-enhanced Raman measurements were taken to monitor the surface-adsorbed intermediates. Noteworthily, the fragmented feature of derived Cu catalysts readily improves the Raman signals, alleviating the necessity to introduce surface-enhancement particles (35, 36). As depicted in Fig. 4 (A and B) and table S2, Raman spectra on both CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu are recorded at a range of applied potential from −0.24 to −0.79 V. Note that no reliable Raman signals can be obtained at potential negative than −0.79 V owing to excessive bubble generation at high current density. Under all the negative potentials applied, the peaks associated with surface copper oxides (CuO and Cu2O) do not exist, further validating the reduction of oxides to metallic Cu.

Reaction mechanism study.

(A and B) In situ Raman spectra for adsorbed intermediates on (A) CuOD-Cu and (B) Cu2OD-Cu. The laser source is 638 nm. (C) Theoretical calculation for CO coupling energy with different intermediates. (D) Empirical and theoretical suggestions about the key C2 intermediates.

Noteworthily, the adsorbed intermediates and their evolution are different on CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu electrodes. At the low wave number range from 200 to 600 cm−1, the typical *CO paired peaks at 281 and 360 cm−1 show up on CuOD-Cu at −0.39 V (37, 38). Meanwhile, on Cu2OD-Cu, a single broad peak at 367 cm−1 first emerges in the range of −0.34 to −0.49 V, followed by disappearance until a pair of peaks at 285 and 358 cm−1 show up at −0.64 V. On the basis of the previous theoretical calculations and experimental works, the single broad peak at 367 cm−1 along with two peaks at 1308 and 1589 cm−1 is attributed to surface-binding *CO2− intermediate (37, 39, 40). This observation manifests that the activation of CO2 through *CO2− toward *CO on CuOD-Cu is much faster than that on Cu2OD-Cu; otherwise, the *CO2− species should be discerned on CuOD-Cu as well. At the medium-wave number region from 900 to 2200 cm−1, upon the potentials negative than −0.54 V, growing group peaks are observed at 1188 and 1381 cm−1 on CuOD-Cu, which are attributed to *OCCOH and *HOCCOH (39). Instead, none of the peaks involving these C2 intermediates (*OCCOH or *HOCCOH) are detected on the Cu2OD-Cu surface at any applied potential. At the high wave number region from 2700 to 3200 cm−1, the C-H signals are observed on both OD-Cu surfaces, but no reliable conclusion may be drawn out due to the C-H complexity (38). Nevertheless, the information provided by low– and medium–wave number regions reveals the fact that CuOD-Cu with a rich population of undercoordinated Cu sites promotes the generation of *CO and *(H)OCCOH species compared with the counterpart Cu2OD-Cu with less undercoordinated Cu sites population.

Meanwhile, the representative undercoordinated Cu site with seven-coordination (denoted as Cu7c) is selected to carry out additional density functional theory (DFT) calculation for an in-depth understanding of the mechanism because the Cu7c is the most distinct site between CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu. As shown in fig. S21, the CO coupling with the most reported C2 intermediates is studied, namely, *OCCO, *OCCOH, *HOCCOH, and *CCH2. Specifically, the *CO coupling energy with *C2 intermediates is −0.83, −0.79, −1.13, and − 1.13 eV for *OCCO, *OCCOH, *HOCCOH, and *CCH2, respectively (Fig. 4C). The results indicate that *HOCCOH and *CCH2 exhibit the most negative CO coupling energy. And *CCH2 is formed via multiple-step reduction of *HOCCOH (fig. S22). However, *HOCCOH is thermodynamically more favored to coupling with CO rather than further reduced to *CCH2, which is consistent with the absence of *CCH2 in Raman spectra. Therefore, *HOCCOH is the most probable C2 intermediate on CuOD-Cu (Fig. 4D). This favored the formation of *C1 and *C2 intermediate on CuOD-Cu making it capable of C-C coupling not only between *CO and *CO but also between *CO and *C2 species (most probably *HOCCOH) (14, 41), leading to identified C2+ products including n-propanol. On the contrary, only varied *C1 intermediates on Cu2OD-Cu imply that the majority of products should be C1 chemicals.

Electrocatalytic CO2 reduction performance

To evaluate the electrocatalytic performance of CO2RR on CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu, electrochemical measurements were first accomplished in 0.1 M KHCO3 aqueous electrolyte with an airtight custom-tailored H cell equipped with a three-electrode system. As plotted in Fig. 5A and fig. S23, linear sweep voltammograms (LSVs) are obtained in CO2- or N2-saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 aqueous electrolyte. Notably, the LSV curve of CuOD-Cu onsets at −0.47 V and achieves 50 mA/cm2 at −1.14 V, essentially outperforming its counterpart Cu2OD-Cu. The gaseous and liquid products are further quantitatively identified by gas chromatography and 1H-NMR spectroscopy, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5B and table S3, multiple CO2RR products are observed on CuOD-Cu throughout the examined potential range, including major ethylene, ethanol, and n-propanol as well as minor CO, ethane, and acetate. Specifically, C2+ products F.E. on CuOD-Cu exceeds 10% at −0.74 V and increases with more negative potentials, approaching 48% at −1.14 V. Among those, the F.E. of n-propanol on CuOD-Cu is up to a remarkable 17.9% at −0.94 V. In sharp contrast, C2+ products on Cu2OD-Cu are identified as ethylene and ethanol with negligible n-propanol, and their F.E. is only 9% at −1.14 V. The F.E. value indicates that the CO2RR activity of Cu2OD-Cu is mainly toward the selective production of formate, which is well consistent with the Raman spectra (Fig. 4) and previous observations on Cu2O-based OD-Cu (19, 42). We note that the hydrogen evolution is relatively high on both CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu (Fig. 5B), which needs to be further restrained or efficiently used in the future to make it closer to the practical application of CO2RR or along with green hydrogen production. Impressively, compared with the state-of-the-art CO2RR reports, the CuOD-Cu exhibits the top performance with high F.E. and low overpotential (Fig. 5C and table S4). Moreover, after 150-hour continuous CO2RR electrolysis on CuOD-Cu at −0.94 V, the n-propanol F.E. is maintained at 15%, indicating 84% retention and excellent stability (Fig. 5D). Considering the state-of-the-art membrane-based separation technologies enable efficiently purifying the liquid products from aqueous solution at low energy cost (43, 44), this selective production of n-propanol shows great value for future practical application.

To promote the yield of n-propanol on CuOD-Cu, the electrochemical performance of CO2RR was further conducted in a flow cell containing 1 M KMCO3 aqueous electrolyte (see Materials and Methods for details). Benefited from the efficient mass transport in a flow cell (9, 45), the total current density of CuOD-Cu markedly increases from 50 mA/cm2 to 240 mA/cm2 at the same potential of −1.14 V (Fig. 5E), namely, an approximately 4.8-fold enhancement. In addition, a maximum n-propanol F.E. of 6.96% is observed at −0.94 V, corresponding to a partial n-propanol current density of 8.51 mA/cm2 (Fig. 5F and fig. S24). Again, this partial n-propanol current density and its corresponding driven potential represent one of the best performances in the state-of-the-art CO2RR works (table S4). This good performance of selective n-propanol production is also attained in membrane-electrode assembly cells (fig. S25).

Last, we tried the commercial CuO and Cu2O samples to verify our findings. The commercial CuOD-Cu also outperforms the commercial Cu2OD-Cu regarding the selective production of multi-carbon products including n-propanol. The morphology and crystalline structure of commercial CuO and Cu2O are proved by SEM, TEM, and XRD, respectively (figs. S26 to S30). In situ XAS results disclose that the commercial CuOD-Cu has an averaged first shell CN of 9.7, considerably lower than that of commercial Cu2OD-Cu(10.4) (figs. S31 and S32 and table S5). Electrochemical performance indicates that commercial CuOD-Cu has a high F.E. of 11.4% n-propanol at −0.94 V, while the commercial Cu2OD-Cu generates formate as a major product with F.E. of 51.3% at −0.84 V (fig. S33 and table S6). The observation on commercial samples highlights that the pristine state can fundamentally affect the reconstruction during CO2RR and the corresponding electrochemical performance.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we have demonstrated that CuO and Cu2O exhibit notable different behaviors during oxygen removal reconstruction toward their derived catalysts under CO2RR. As a result, the outstanding and stable generation of n-propanol at low reduction potential is attained on CuOD-Cu, whereas formate is a dominant product on Cu2OD-Cu. This work not only offers fundamental insight into the structural transformation of electrocatalysts (such as Cu-based alloys, Cu-sulfides, and Cu-oxide–based hybrid materials) under reactions but also enables rational design of efficient and low-cost catalysts toward important but challenging electrochemical conversions including but not limited to CO2RR.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Reagents

High-pure Cu foil (99.9999%) was bought from Alfa Aesar Reagent Co. Ltd. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (99.98%) and potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3) (99.5%) were purchased from J&K Scientific Co. Ltd. Hydrochloric acid (HCl) (36 to 38%, GR) and anhydrous methanol (99.8%) were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co. Ltd. Commercial CuO and Cu2O powders (99.9%) were purchased from Macklin Co. Ltd. All the chemicals were used without further purification. Milli-Q ultrapure water with a resistivity of 18 kilohms was used for all the experiments.

Characterization

SEM was performed on a Hitachi S-8220 Scanning Electron Microscope operated at 5 to 10 kV. The transmission electron microscope was carried out on JEOL JEM-ARM300F Spherical Aberration Electron Microscopy and FEI Tecnai G2 F20 U-TWIN Electron Microscopy. GI-XRD patterns were recorded on Bruker D8 Advance with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.542 Å). The incidence angle was used as 0.2° and the scan rate was 5 degrees/min. Raman spectroscopy was performed on Horiba Jobin Yvon XploRA PLUS Raman spectrometer equipped with a 50× objective and He-Ne laser with 532 and/or 638 nm.

Synthesis procedures

Synthesis of copper oxides

Typically, to prepare CuO, the copper foil was first cut into a slice with the size of 1 cm by 5 cm. The small copper piece was thoroughly washed in anhydrous methanol and 2 M HCl aqueous solution successively under sonication for approximately 3 hours to ensure the removal of surface organic impurities and oxides. Subsequently, the freshly cleaned copper slice was rinsed with Milli-Q water thoroughly and transferred into a plastic Corning tube. Then, a mixture containing 200 μl of 1 M NaOH and 10 ml of H2O was added into the Corning tube. The Corning tube was then sealed and kept in a heat oven under 60°C overnight. The black CuO formed the surface of the parent Cu foil. Last, the yield CuO was cooled to room temperature, washed with flowing Milli-Q ultrapure water, and dried under argon flow. The slice was further cut into smaller ones for testing. To prepare the Cu2O, the same protocol was used, except for the mixture containing 200 μl of 1 M NaOH and 10 ml of H2O being replaced with 10 ml of 1 M NaOH.

Synthesis of oxide derived catalysts

To activate the pristine oxide of CuO or Cu2O, a classical three-electrode system was used to prepare the oxide-derived catalyst under CO2 electroreduction. Specifically, the pristine Cu oxide, Ag/AgCl (3.5 M KCl), and Pt net were used as the working, reference, and counter electrode, respectively. CO2-saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 aqueous solution was adopted as the electrolyte. A potential of −0.94 V was applied for 20 min, followed by drying under Ar flow to prepare the corresponding OD-Cu for immediate characterization and test.

Electrochemical OHad measurement

Before OHad measurement, the OD-Cu sample was activated at −0.94 V for 20 min in an H cell containing CO2-saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 with CO2 continuous feed. Then, OD-Cu samples were transferred to a three-electrode cell. Electrochemical OHad was measured in the Ar-saturated 1 M KOH electrolyte with a cyclic voltammetry (CV) method at a scan rate of 100 mV/s.

Electrochemical measurement

All electrochemical measurements were carried out in either CHI-760 or BioLogic electrochemical station. All potentials were measured versus an Ag/AgCl (3.5 M KCl) reference electrode and calculated into the RHE scale by usingE(vs.RHE)=E(vs.Ag/AgCl)+0.204V+0.0591×pHwhere E represented the potential and V denoted the potential.

In the H cell, the airtight two compartments were separated by an ion exchange membrane (Nafion 117). Both anodic and cathodic chambers were charged with 0.1 M KHCO3 as electrolyte. OD-Cu catalysts (we synthesized or commercial samples), Ag/AgCl (3.5 M KCl), and a Pt net were used as working, reference, and counter electrode, respectively. CO2 or N2 was fed at a constant rate of 10 ml/min into the cathodic chamber using a mass flow controller.

For the flow cell consisting of a gas chamber, a cathodic chamber, and an anodic chamber, 1.0 M KHCO3 was used as an electrolyte and circulated through the electrochemical cell at 50 ml/min using a peristaltic pump. The OD catalyst as a working electrode was pierced and fixed between gas and cathodic chambers, with an additional hydrophobic carbon paper attached on the side of the gas chamber (geometric active surface area of 1 cm2). An anion exchange membrane (Fumasep FAA-3-PK-130) was used to separate the anodic and cathodic chambers. Ag/AgCl (3.5 M KCl) and commercial RuO2 supported by carbon paper were used as the reference electrode and counter electrode, respectively. The CO2 inlet flow was kept constant rate of 50 ml/min using a mass flow controller.

The surface RF of samples was determined by measuring the electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl) referenced to previous protocols (19, 46). CV was performed in 0.1 M HClO4 electrolyte. CVs were obtained for a potential range in which no faradaic processes occurred, and the geometric current density was plotted against the scan rate of the CV. The slope of the linear regression gave the capacitance.

Product analysis

The evolved gas products were analyzed by a gas chromatograph (GC; Shimadzu GC-2014C) equipped with a packed Molecular Sieve 5A capillary column (Agilent) and a Porapak Q column (Agilent, 80 to 100 mesh). Both thermal conductivity detector and flame ionization detector were installed on the GC to detect H2 and gaseous CO2-reduced products, respectively. The liquid product was determined by 1H-NMR spectroscopy (Bruker Advance, 400 MHz) with a water suppression technique. D2O and dimethyl sulfoxide were adopted as the lock solvent and the internal reference, respectively.

In situ XAFS measurement

XAS experiments were performed at the 1W1B beamline of the Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility. The storage ring runs at 2.5 GeV with a maximum electron current of about 250 mA. The energy range of the incident x-ray was tunable from 4.8 to 22.8 keV by fix-exit Si(111) double crystal monochromators. The absorption edge of standard metal foils was used to calibrate the x-ray energy. The surficial pristine Cu oxide was transferred onto the carbon paper support to exclude the signal interference of parent Cu foil. Commercial Cu foil, Cu2O powder, and CuO powder were used as standard reference samples. Cu K-edge spectra were collected at room temperature in transmission mode for ex situ tests and fluorescence mode for in situ tests. The acquired XAFS data were processed according to the standard procedures using the ATHENA module implemented in the IFEFFIT software packages (47). The k3-weighted EXAFS spectra were obtained by subtracting the post-edge background from the overall absorption and then normalizing regarding the edge-jump step. Subsequently, k3-weighted χ(k) data of Cu K-edge were Fourier-transformed to real (R) space using a Hanning windows (dk = 1.0 Å−1) to separate the EXAFS contributions from different coordination shells. To obtain the quantitative structural parameters around central atoms, least-squares curve parameter fitting was performed using the ARTEMIS module of the IFEFFIT software package. The wavelet transformation of χ(k) was conducted using the HAMA Fortune software package (48, 49).

Theoretical calculation

The DFT method implemented in the Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package (VASP) was adopted to obtain the structure of the primitive cell of CuO and Cu2O (50–52). The projected augmented wave (PAW) basis and Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional were used (53). The Hubbard U correction of 4.0 eV was applied to the Cu atoms to describe the exchange interaction of their 3d orbitals (54). The 600-eV energy cutoff was adopted during the computation. The convergence criteria of the electronic step were set to 10−7 eV. A 6 × 8 × 5 MP k-point mesh was adopted for the calculations of CuO and a 6 × 6 × 6 k-point mesh was applied for Cu2O. The atomic coordinates and the lattice parameters of both systems were optimized until the residual force was less than 0.01 eV/Å.

Seven-layered realistic CuO and Cu2O surface models were built by extending the primitive cell to 5 × 5 supercells. Here, the most stable (111) surface was chosen as reported in the literature (55, 56) and observed in our experimental GI-XRD results. The seven-layered model was inspired by the full MD simulations carried out on a five-layer model by Cheng et al. (28). Additional fixed two layers were added to represent the bulk phase (57, 58). A neutral network potential was adopted to represent the interactions between the atom pairs (59, 60). Such a method was proven to accurately simulate the long-term evolution of a large-scale reactive system. The constant temperature and constant volume MD (NVT-MD) simulations were then carried out using the LASP code (59, 61). The time step of MD simulations was set to 1 fs and one cycle of simulation lasted for 0.5 ns at the temperature of 300 K. The bottom two layers of CuO and Cu2O slabs were fixed because of the negligible impact of the bottom layers on the surface (28). Before of each MD cycle, the weakly bound O atoms (CN < 4) on the H atom accessible surface were removed to represent the reduction process, until no oxygen was left on the surface. Last, an extra 0.5-ns MD simulation was conducted, and a total of 51 frames of atomic trajectory were generated for statistics of surface Cu. To study the exceeding proportion of the surface Cu atoms on the CuOD-Cu surface over that of Cu2OD-Cu, the following equation was adopted to calculate the composition of the Cu atoms with different CNCi=NCuOi−NCu2OiNCuO−NCu2O×100%where Ci was the contribution of Cu with CN of i. NCuOi and NCu2Oi stood for the number of surface Cu with CN = i on CuOD-Cu and Cu2OD-Cu. NCuO and NCu2O were the total surface atom number on CuOD-Cu(169.14) and Cu2OD-Cu(141.24).

The reaction energetics of CO coupling and proton addition for possible C2 intermediates were calculated with the spin-polarized DFT method implemented by the VASP (50–52). The PAW basis and PBE exchange-correlation functional were used (53). The long-range van der Waals interactions were accounted for using Grimme’s DFT-D3 method (62). A four-layered 3*1 Cu(332) slab was constructed and the step-square site was used to represent the most characteristic Cu7c active sites on CuOD-Cu. An additional 20-Å vacuum space was added along the z direction to avoid interaction between the adjacent slabs. The 400-eV energy cutoff was adopted during the optimization. A 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh was used for the calculation of the reactions on the metal surfaces. The structures of all reaction intermediates were optimized until the residual force was less than 0.03 eV/Å. The bottom two Cu layers were fixed to represent the bulk phase, and other atoms were fully relaxed. The energetics were further refined considering the solvation effect of water using the VASPsol module.
