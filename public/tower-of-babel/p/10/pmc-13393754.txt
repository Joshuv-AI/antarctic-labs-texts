A survey of primary care physicians' views on supporting patients who lose insurance coverage for Glucagon-Like Peptide-1-based drugs for weight management
Authors: Lauren Oshman, Amy Runyon, Alexis Vicenzi Bal, Patricia Arizaca-Dileo, Ananda Sen, Amal Othman, Jonathan Gabison, Amy Rothberg, Andrew Kraftson, Dina Griauzde, A. Mark Fendrick
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1)-based drugs for weight management are commonly prescribed by primary care physicians (PCPs), many of whom have limited familiarity with alternative weight management treatments. This knowledge gap may be especially problematic when patients lose insurance coverage for – and therefore access to – GLP-1-based drugs after initial weight loss, as these patients may require alternative treatments to sustain weight loss or maintenance. To date, little is known about how prepared PCPs feel to manage this increasingly common clinical scenario.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional web-based survey of PCPs at an academic medical center in the Midwestern United States who provide care to patients who lost coverage for GLP-1-based drugs for weight management from a large statewide insurer in 2025. Quantitative responses were analyzed descriptively and with chi-square tests; open-ended responses were analyzed with directed content analysis.ResultsAmong surveyed PCPs, the response rate was 51% (111/216). Approximately half (53/109, 49%) felt uncomfortable helping patients manage their weight following loss of coverage for GLP-1-based drugs for weight management. Greater comfort was associated with past experiences prescribing phentermine (aOR 4.14, 95% CI 1.69–10.18) and prescribing liraglutide (aOR 2.34, 95% CI 1.05–5.24). Most (82/110, 75%) felt that less than a quarter of their patients would be able to maintain their weight loss after losing insurance coverage and access to GLP-1-based drugs. Referral to American Board of Obesity Medicine (ABOM) board-certified physicians was preferred to support ongoing weight management of these patients (46/96, 48%).ConclusionsHalf of all PCPs at an academic medical center are uncomfortable supporting patients’ transition from GLP-1-based drugs to alternative weight management treatments following loss of insurance for these agents. Additional resources, including access to ABOM-certified physicians, are needed to support patients facing this common clinical scenario.

Introduction

GLP-1-based drugs have far-reaching benefits in the treatment of obesity and weight-related conditions, but their widespread use has led to unsustainable financial costs. Recent estimates report that 40% of US adults have obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) and almost 10% have severe obesity (BMI ≥40 kg/m2) [1]. Semaglutide, a glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) and tirzepatide, a dual GLP-1 RA/gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP) agonist, are effective in weight reduction of approximately 15% and 20%, respectively, for people with overweight or obesity based on clinical trial data [2,3]. In people with obesity but without type 2 diabetes (T2D), GLP-1-based drugs can prevent new-onset diabetes, reduce the risk of major cardiovascular events in high-risk populations, and control or remit weight-related chronic conditions such as obstructive sleep apnea and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [4]. While GLP-1-based drugs represent just one aspect of comprehensive weight management, their popularity has skyrocketed. This is due not only to their efficacy, tolerability, and PCP familiarity with their use for type 2 diabetes and cardio-renal-metabolic risk reduction [5], but also to direct-to-consumer advertising and booming compounding and cash-pay options. The list price of GLP-1-based drugs in the United States ranges between $936 and $1349 per month before insurance, rebate, direct-to-consumer or other discounts [6]. The Center for Medicare & Medicaid Services BRIDGE demonstration project temporarily offers GLP-1-based drugs to selected Medicare Part D beneficiaries for weight management for a $50 monthly copayment [7]. The future BALANCE Model proposes to provide access to GLP-1-based drugs and lifestyle support to some Medicare beneficiaries for weight management [8]. Separately, the Inflation Reduction Act may negotiate the price of semaglutide (Wegovy) to $386 [9].

In real-world practice, initiating GLP-1-based drugs is often restricted by complex prior authorization, cost control requirements, and formulary exclusions that may apply to some or all medications in this class. GLP-1-based drugs for weight management are more likely to be prescribed for non-Hispanic white individuals, women, and less socially vulnerable individuals [10]. Long term efficacy is further compromised by inadequate maintenance dosing, poor persistence with treatment, and discontinuation driven by high costs [11,12]. The high cost of these medications has prompted many health insurers to eliminate coverage or impose restrictive cost control measures, such as lifetime coverage limits, high cost sharing, step therapy, and prior authorization requirements [13]. For example, Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan cited a cost of $1.1 billion in claims due to GLP-1-based drugs in 2024, prompting the decision to discontinue coverage of this class of medications for the indication of obesity or overweight in 2025 [14]. Several states including California, New Hampshire and North Carolina, recently restricted Medicaid coverage of GLP-1-based drugs [15]. Michigan Medicaid limited coverage to individuals with a BMI ≥40 kg/m2 with strict prior authorization criteria in 2026 [16].

Primary care physicians and advanced practice providers (PCPs) prescribe 88.7% of all weight management medications [17]. Consequently, they are also tasked with supporting weight maintenance among patients who lose coverage for – and therefore access to – these therapies. Clinical efficacy trials demonstrate weight regain with loss of cardiometabolic health benefits following discontinuation of GLP-1-based drugs [18]. In contrast, limited data suggest that gradual discontinuation, transition to alternative weight management medications, or certain lifestyle interventions might support weight maintenance following initial weight loss with GLP-1-based drugs [[19], [20], [21], [22]]. Fewer than 10% of PCPs in a recent study used evidence-based guidelines in weight management and identified lack of time and reimbursement for counseling as key barriers [23]. Thus, PCPs may not be equipped to support patients’ weight management with non-GLP-1-based therapies.

To date, no published guidelines or recommendations exist to guide PCPs in supporting patients who lose insurance coverage for GLP-1-based drugs, and little is known about their preparedness for this common clinical scenario. To address this gap, we surveyed PCPs with the aim of understanding their comfort in caring for patients and their perceptions of how the health system could better support clinicians and their patients in maintaining weight loss outcomes after insurance coverage loss for GLP-1-based drugs.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a cross-sectional survey of PCPs employed at a large academic medical center in the midwestern United States. The University of Michigan Health Sciences and Behavioral Sciences Institutional Review Board (IRB-HSBS) determined this study exempt status because it involved educational testing only and survey data were de-identified (IRB protocol number: HUM00256866). The description of this study follows the CROSS guidelines [24].

Setting

The academic health system offers a variety of resources for weight management, including a primary care based weight navigation program (WNP), consisting of weight-focused visits with American Board of Obesity Medicine (ABOM) certified physicians who help patients develop preference-aligned weight-loss treatment and follow-up plans. Additional health system resources include registered dietitian services, a medically supervised intensive behavioral and meal replacement program, endoscopic and bariatric surgical programs, and subspecialty clinics addressing obesity-related conditions such as fatty liver disease, lipodystrophy, and obesity hypoventilation [25].

Participants and recruitment

Eligible physicians included all primary care general internal medicine and medicine-pediatrics physicians (n = 116) and family medicine physicians (n = 100) with a clinical practice in the health system. Recruitment occurred via email list-serve and announcement during departmental and divisional meetings. The survey was deployed via Qualtrics between 08/22/24-11/3/24. Participants provided electronic informed consent prior to completing the survey.

Quantitative data collection

Survey questions

The survey was developed based on prior literature on PCP responses to medication access disruptions [26] and the authors' prior work assessing PCPs’ use of weight management treatments and barriers to high quality comprehensive treatment [23]. The survey was pre-tested by physicians who specialize in primary care and obesity medicine, and subsequently refined based on their feedback. The survey included twenty-three questions and covered current weight management medication prescribing practices, comfort with managing patients who lose insurance coverage for GLP-1-based drugs, use of alternative strategies, and desired health care system support and resources to help these patients (Supplementary Data). We collected demographic variables including age, gender, specialty, number of years in practice, and clinical practice characteristics. In this survey we used the term “incretin mimetics” to denote the medications liraglutide, semaglutide and tirzepatide prescribed for weight management.

Quantitative data analysis

We performed descriptive statistical analysis using IBM SPSS v30 [27]. Analyses were calculated using the number of respondents who answered the question rather than the total number of survey participants, as respondents were not required to answer all of the questions in the survey, leading to variable numbers of skipped responses. We dichotomized five-point Likert-style survey questions assessing the extent of agreement into two categories: extremely or somewhat likely versus extremely or somewhat unlikely and neutral. We combined neutral responses with uncomfortable responses under the assumption that neutrality is more likely to reflect the experience of not being comfortable. We evaluated associations between physician comfort helping patients who lose coverage for GLP-1-based drugs and categorical variables of interest (volume of GLP-1-based drug prescribing and alternative weight management medication prescribing, likelihood to recommend compounded GLP-1-based drugs, and likelihood to recommend bariatric surgery) using chi-square (χ2) tests of independence.

Qualitative data collection

Survey questions

The survey included two open-ended questions: “What additional resources would help your patients with overweight or obesity when they lose access to insurance coverage for incretin mimetics for weight loss?” and “Is there anything else you want to share with us about your perspectives on insurance policy decision to limit incretin mimetics?”

Qualitative data analysis

Qualitative responses to the two open-ended questions were analyzed using directed content analysis, in which existing deductive codes derived from the quantitative data were complemented with new inductive codes from the qualitative data and then compiled into larger categories [28]. One author (LO) created an initial codebook from the qualitative data. UMGPT, an artificial intelligence program, was then used to create a preliminary summary of the data and codes and suggest additional codes [29]. One author (LO) then reviewed, revised, and refined the codebook. Two authors (LO and AVB) then manually recoded the qualitative responses. A third author (PAD) resolved any coding discrepancies using consensus conference. Then, authors (LO and AVB) mapped each code to larger organizational categories within question one (desired resources) and question 2 (additional PCP perspectives) and subsequently organized those categories into major themes and subthemes.

Qualitative results

Among 37 individuals who provided open-ended responses, we identified three major themes describing respondent perspectives on loss of insurance coverage for GLP-1-based drugs.

The benefits of GLP-1-based drugs support continued coverage and access (10/37, 27%). One respondent noted that they recommend lifestyle change, but that when patients do not achieve their health goals, GLP-1-based therapies have a role in care, noting “These medications WORK and RESOLVE issues like pre-diabetes, hyperlipidemia, hypertension, OSA. I know that they are incredibly expensive, but I feel like the long-term benefit AND patient satisfaction are worth it. The vast majority of patients are unable to budge their weight in a significant way with [lifestyle] - even with support groups/apps.” Of note, conditions such as sleep apnea are controlled rather than resolved with appropriate weight management treatment. Respondents appreciated the benefits of weight loss on cardiometabolic and physical health outcomes. One respondent commented, “… I have seen patients start taking these medications and lose weight to the point where they may qualify for knee replacement surgery where they had not previously.”

PCPs lack the resources needed to develop comprehensive weight management plans (17/37, 46%). Respondents described feeling overwhelmed by opaque insurance coverage criteria, especially in between face-to-face visits, noting “The hardest thing is not knowing how to know what is and isn't going to be covered and when. This wastes our time with counseling on and prescribing medications and then having to change the whole plan asynchronously.” One respondent described the workload as unsustainable, noting “… I sometimes feel we have become a prior auth factory for these medications and would be happy to send patients elsewhere as it seems to burden clinicians and staff significantly.” This PCP elaborated on the ethical discomfort and moral injury that makes it increasingly challenging to treat obesity in their practice, “It is also mind boggling and ethically questionable to prescribe a medication that requires counseling that coming off it may reverse all its effectiveness and also that their insurance will only cover it for a certain amount of time or that their insurance may stop covering it at some unknown time.”

Advocacy on multiple levels is needed to ensure continued access to GLP-1-based drugs for weight management (19/37, 51%). One respondent identified the need for advocacy at the federal policy level, noting “… yet another argument in favor of Federal policy changes to cap medication pricing. There is no reason for the current prices for these medications other than pure greed.” Another respondent advocated for pharmaceutical companies lowering costs, “It is my hope that the decision by [insurer] prescription drug plan and Michigan Medicaid to not cover incretin mimetics means that this will drive [manufacturers] to lower costs, make more accessible dosing regimens such as pens that can have doses dialed in, and/or consider accepting insurance coverage for vials of their medication.” Health systems were identified as an ally in placing more pressure on statewide insurers, as one respondent identified “[I] feel that [health system] leadership should try to use health care system's leverage to potentially impact insurer's coverage decisions.”

Discussion

This cross-sectional survey found that half of primary care physicians are uncomfortable helping patients manage their weight following loss of coverage for—and therefore access to—GLP-1-based drugs for weight management. Our results suggest the need for best-practice guidelines, team-based care, and obesity-medicine consultants to help PCPs navigate opaque insurance coverage policies and lack of experience with first-generation weight management medications and non-pharmacologic treatments.

Our results extend prior literature on medication access disruptions, which largely focuses on short-term shortages or formulary changes by dose rationing, finding therapeutic alternatives, and using alternative preparations or compounded substitutes [31]. Population-wide, insurer-driven elimination of the entire class of GLP-1-based drugs represents a structurally distinct challenge for which these strategies may be insufficient. Prior studies demonstrate that PCPs often under-utilize effective treatments for obesity due to limited training in obesity medicine and clinical time constraints [23]. Our findings suggest that many PCPs have some experience prescribing first-generation weight management medications and thus transitioning patients from GLP-1-based therapy to these medications but lack the practical expertise to manage this complex transition confidently. This comfort gap may contribute to clinician frustration and diminished engagement with comprehensive weight management care, potentially driving patients to seek treatment from commercial GLP-1 prescribers — where longitudinal oversight, comorbidity management, and medication monitoring may be limited.

Guidelines on use of pharmacotherapy for weight management commonly offer approaches to step-up therapy and consider cost and coverage barriers to determine a treatment strategy but lack recommendations to step down [32,33]. The 2025 update to the American Association of Clinical Endocrinology Consensus Statement: Algorithm for the Evaluation and Treatment of Adults with Obesity/Adiposity-Based Chronic Disease recommends considering a trial of a first-generation weight management medication after a trial of a second-generation medication, such as GLP-1-based drugs, to maintain weight loss [34]. Another recent proposed clinical pathway focuses on close clinical monitoring for weight recurrence and intensive lifestyle and behavioral management [35]. Clinicians can use a personalized approach in which benefits of medications and patient-specific contraindications and precautions are matched to identify appropriate treatment options. For example, phentermine-topiramate may benefit sleep apnea though there is no FDA approval specifically for this indication [36]. Alternatively, phentermine-topiramate may exacerbate certain types of kidney stones and bupropion-naltrexone may be selected for patients with this condition. These approaches do not resolve concerns about pharmacoequity. As individuals who are able to continue cash pay for GLP-1-based drugs persist in receiving treatment and health benefits from these medications, loss of coverage may contribute to a growing gap in health outcomes among minoritized and socially and financially vulnerable populations with overweight or obesity [37]. Our study demonstrates the need for practical tools and resources to assist clinicians and patients in the medical decision making and practical implementation of alternative pharmacotherapy.

Our qualitative results suggest that loss of coverage and access to GLP-1-based drugs may substantially increase physician workload. Given that 40% of adults are affected by obesity and that respondents to our study identified 14% of their clinical encounters as involving GLP-1-based drugs, primary care clinicians are burdened with “guessing” which medications may be covered and managing utilization management requests from insurers. Guiding patients through cash-pay GLP-1-based drug prices and options and prescribing equivalent doses requires significant time and expertise. Utilization measures including prior authorization and step therapy are significant contributors to burnout [38]. Further, loss of autonomy and authority in treatment decisions, otherwise termed “professional dissonance,” contributes to burnout and risk of leaving clinical practice in primary care [39,40]. Respondents to our study described the frustration of seeing life-changing benefits of GLP-1-based drugs in their patients’ lives and advocating against insurance coverage limitations to prescribing the medication that they feel will be most beneficial for their patients. Tools for patients to use to check insurance coverage and cash-pay prices and delegation of these tasks to non-physician team members relieve some of the burden of utilization review management in weight management.

Moving forward, the federal government has suggested that it will lower prices of GLP-1-based drugs and make them more widely available to Medicare and Medicaid beneficiaries [41]. Price reductions may increase the likelihood of insurance coverage for obesity [42,43]. Health care systems and insurers can monitor pharmacoequity and take action to ensure access to high-quality weight management treatment [44]. Primary care physicians can play an important role in advocating for less burdensome utilization management policies and prior authorization criteria, reduction in pharmaceutical company prices for GLP-1-based drugs, and coverage of comprehensive long-term intensive behavioral management and lifestyle therapy by insurers. In the short term, physicians can transition patients to alternative medications for weight management. Small studies demonstrate alternative dosing (such as slow tapering) or skipped dose regimens as ways to better allocate the high cost of GLP-1-based drugs in weight maintenance after weight loss [21,22]. Another study from our institution demonstrates that ABOM-certified physicians achieve higher levels of weight loss using non-GLP-1-based drugs than other primary care clinicians [45]. Additional education continues to be needed in order to assist primary care clinicians in utilizing older medications for uncomplicated, mild obesity in order to maximize population health and cost effectiveness of treatment [46].

Limitations

This survey has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single academic health center, and the results may not reflect the experiences of clinicians in other practice settings or in other states. Second, this survey may be subject to selection bias and nonresponse bias and does not address the experiences of PCPs who did not respond to the survey. Third, the specific resources available to respondents in our health system are likely not available to clinicians in other systems, though access to weight management specialty physicians may be more available with increasing numbers of ABOM diplomates in practice [47]. Fourth, we did not collect data on other factors that may have influenced PCP comfort with managing loss of coverage, which could include panel size and quality and quantity of support staff, including nurses, pharmacists, medical assistant and prior authorization team members. Fifth, the survey did not ask about connecting patients to additional weight management treatments before and during GLP-1-based medication treatment. Sixth, we assumed that lack of insurance coverage implies lack of access for most patients, which excluded compounded GLP-1-based drugs. This survey was administered prior to recent cash-pay availability of semaglutide (Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Zepbound) direct from the manufacturer; more patients may select to pay for medications outside of insurance and retain access.

Conclusion

This study found that half of all PCPs at an academic medical center are not comfortable supporting weight management following loss of insurance coverage and access to GLP-1-based drugs. A comprehensive approach including education, team-based support, and access to programs like the Weight Navigation Program that integrate ABOM diplomate physicians into primary care settings, may benefit patients who lose coverage for GLP-1-based medications for weight management to avoid weight regain and maintain health benefits of weight loss.

Key takeaways:•Half of all PCPs at an academic medical center are not comfortable supporting weight management following loss of insurance coverage and access to GLP-1-based drugs for weight management.•Referral to American Board of Obesity Medicine (ABOM)-certified physicians embedded in primary care practice (Weight Navigation Program) for weight management was preferred to support ongoing weight management.•Practical tools and resources are urgently needed to assist clinicians and patients in the medical decision making and practical implementation of alternative pharmacotherapy and intensive behavioral and lifestyle treatment.
