Artificial Intelligence in Dermatology: New Regulatory Aspects After the European AI Act
Authors: Marco Manfredini, Chiara Ciccia Romito, Susanna Viggiani, Caterina Longo
Published: 2026

To the Editor:

The development and regulation of tools based on artificial intelligence (AI) is a pivotal challenge for modern healthcare. Political, jurisdictional, and scientific counterparts are called to shape the future progress and implementations of one of the most promising and yet controversial tools that human race has ever developed. In dermatology, AI's capability to analyze vast datasets of medical images has fostered its integration into the clinical diagnosis of conditions like melanoma or psoriasis [1, 2]. In addition to clinical image analysis, several AI applications have been developed for dermoscopic image classification [1, 2, 3]. Dermoscopy significantly improves the accuracy of the diagnosis of pigmented and non‐pigmented skin lesions compared to naked‐eye examination [1, 3]. Recently it was demonstrated that neural networks can classify dermoscopic images as accurately as human experts in several experimental settings [1, 3]. High quality AI‐based clinical support systems positively outperform the diagnostic accuracy of either AI or physicians alone [1, 3, 4]. In addition, the least experienced clinicians may gain the greatest benefits from AI‐based support.

In April 2021, the European Commission published its first AI Act proposal, and in December 2023, the European Commissioner for Internal Market announced that a deal had been reached. On March 13, 2024, the European Union introduced the AI Act, the first global cross‐party law on AI. This comprehensive regulation categorizes AI applications into various risk levels. We analyzed the AI Act to define how it may affect the development of dermatological AI‐based applications.

The Regulation consists of 180 considerando, 113 articles, and 13 annexes. The AI Act applies to providers and users of AI systems in all industries in the European Union. Its content includes ethical principles such as human intervention and surveillance; cybersecurity; privacy and data governance; transparency; diversity, non‐discrimination and fairness. Even though all these principles need to be equally addressed, a peculiar aspect of dermatology‐based applications is the frequent need for image processing for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes. AI‐based image analysis harbors two primary privacy concerns: (1) the potential linkage of personal data to disease diagnosis, and (2) the inherent sensitivity of clinical images, which might permit patient identification even after anonymization. The AI Act adopts the already defined risk‐based approach of the Regulation (EU) 2016/679 on personal data protection. Four risk levels (from unacceptable to minimal) are identified, and different requirements are associated with each one of these levels. The risk is based on the use and purpose of the processing. Article 9(2)(h) defines the limitations that healthcare devices and software should comply with. These are cases where the processing is necessary for the purposes of preventive medicine, medical diagnosis, the provision of health care or treatment, subject to the conditions and safeguards set out in paragraph 3. For instance, if the data being processed by the AI system are completely not associated with the patient, the level of risk is considered low. In detail, the treatment of special categories of personal data (e.g., health data) may take place if it is necessary for the purpose of a relevant public interest, such as the management of health services, preventive medicine, disease treatment, diagnostics, and other general health activities. The processing of special categories of personal data for these purposes is allowed if there are adequate legal precautions in place to protect the privacy of individuals. To avoid regulatory ambiguity, it is important to distinguish that the GDPR defines the legal bases that make the processing of health data lawful, whereas the AI Act classifies AI systems according to their risk level and imposes technical and governance requirements that are independent of the GDPR's data‑processing permissions.

According to the AI Act classification, if the processed data cannot be definitively linked to an individual, the associated risk level is categorized as limited or minimal.

To provide a clear conceptual framework, it is essential to outline the risk‐based classification presented by the AI Act, which forms the foundation of the European approach to AI governance. The regulation identifies four categories of risk—unacceptable, high, limited, and minimal—each associated with specific obligations and safeguards. Systems falling under the category of “unacceptable risk” include those capable of exploiting cognitive or clinical vulnerabilities of individuals; these systems are prohibited entirely due to their potential to undermine human dignity, autonomy, or safety. An AI tool that analyzes skin‑lesion photos to infer a patient's psychological state or treatment compliance—without their awareness and with the aim of influencing their decisions—would be classified as an unacceptable‑risk system because it exploits clinical and cognitive vulnerabilities. High‐risk systems, which are particularly relevant to dermatology, encompass AI tools used for diagnostic purposes or clinical decision‐making based on automated analysis of medical images. These applications are subject to stringent technical, documentation, and human‐oversight requirements. A deep understanding of each application's functions and a case‐by‐case evaluation, should be performed to define the risks of any AI tool analyzed. Limited‐risk systems must comply with transparency obligations, such as informing users about the involvement of automated processes, while minimal‐risk systems carry no specific regulatory burdens. Both the aim of the AI tool and its technical structure contribute to defining different risk levels with their inherent implications. This structured classification helps position dermatology‐related AI tools within a coherent regulatory landscape and clarifies the medico‐legal consequences associated with their adoption. Within the European context, the AI Act interacts with existing medical device frameworks, which share important parallels with FDA approval pathways. AI systems intended for clinical use must undergo rigorous validation processes, including performance verification, risk assessment, and post‐market monitoring. These procedures, combined with mandatory human oversight, algorithmic transparency, and well‐defined accountability structures, are essential to ensuring that AI technologies are introduced into dermatological practice in an ethical, reliable, and patient‐centered manner. In particular, the implementation of well‐defined accountability frameworks is essential to ensure a clear delineation of roles and responsibilities in the event of errors or failures within the AI system. The AI Act highlights the necessity for a framework of accountability covering every step of the process, from design and development to deployment and daily clinical use, ensuring patient protection.

The goal is not merely regulatory compliance, but the establishment of trust—trust in the technology, in the clinical process that integrates it, and in the physician who interprets and contextualizes its outputs.

On the other hand, if the system is used to predict a diagnosis or a medical condition, directly or indirectly allowing harmful AI‐based exploitation of patient vulnerabilities, the risk rises to an unacceptable level. It is important to always keep in mind that AI Act considers the use of the AI system in medical diagnosis or health management as a high‐risk factor. For example, if AI is used to scan medical images for diagnosing diseases or to support clinical decisions, it may be ranked as high risk, even if no user‐identifiable data are directly paired with the patient. This is because the use of AI in clinical settings has direct health impacts and can influence medical decisions, such as diagnosing critical illnesses or treating diseases (Figure 1). The use of AI, even in applications for dermoscopic imaging, therefore, requires system supervision and validation because of the implications for patients’ health [3, 4, 5, 6]. The inclusion of regulatory frameworks, such as FDA certification in the United States and the CE Marking process under the EU's Medical Device Regulation/In‐Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (MDR/IVDR) in Europe, is critical for establishing trust in clinical AI. These processes provide a structured, evidence‐based pathway to ensure that AI systems are safe, effective, and reliable before they can be used in patient care.

In conclusion, the introduction of the AI Act represents a pivotal moment for the application of AI in dermatology. It is crucial to recognize that, while AI holds advanced capabilities, its role should remain supportive rather than substitutive for human clinical judgment, meaning that AI will not replace humans in diagnostic and clinical decisions. According to Gödel's theorem, which provide a foundational philosophical framework for AI software evaluation, there remains a substantial difference between human thinking, which is non‐algorithmic, and AI‐based decision‐making process [8]. Although these arguments stem from outside the biomedical domain, they offer an important conceptual reminder: AI, however, sophisticated, operates within inherent structural limits. Clinical reasoning, intuitive pattern recognition, and the ability to integrate emotional, contextual, and experiential cues—all of which play a central role in dermatological assessment—lie beyond the reach of computational systems. For this reason, AI should be regarded as an instrument that augments, rather than replaces, the clinician's expertise. Ultimately, technological innovation becomes meaningful only when placed in the hands of professionals who understand both its power and its limitations. Effective integration will depend on fostering robust human‐AI collaboration. AI can significantly enhance the accuracy of dermatological diagnoses and optimize therapeutic pathways, but the final decision must remain in the hands of the physician. Therefore, the AI Act not only promotes the safe adoption of AI but also emphasizes the importance of human‐machine collaboration.
