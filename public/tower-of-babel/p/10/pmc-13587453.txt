Meningococcal serogroup W ST-11 causing meningitis, endogenous endophthalmitis, and septic arthritis in a child
Authors: Eylem Kiral, Omer Kilic, Gurkan Bozan, Gizem Mardinoglu, Tuba Erem, Ayşenur Bostan, Mustafa Deger Bilgec, Yasemin Oz, Ener Cagri Dinleyici
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInvasive meningococcal disease (IMD) remains a major global health concern because of its epidemic potential, unpredictable presentation, and rapid progression. Although IMD classically presents as meningitis, meningococcemia, or both, Neisseria meningitidis can also cause extra-meningeal manifestations. Serogroup W (MenW), particularly the hyperinvasive ST-11 lineage, has increasingly been associated with atypical and invasive clinical phenotypes. We report an 11-year-old previously healthy boy with IMD due to MenW ST-11 who developed the exceptional triad of meningitis, endogenous endophthalmitis, and septic arthritis.Case reportAn 11-year-old boy residing in a school dormitory presented with fever, rhinorrhea, cough, generalized erythematous rash, and conjunctival redness. Because of altered consciousness and seizures, he was transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit after receiving initial antibiotic therapy. He had not received meningococcal vaccines. On admission, he was lethargic, with anisocoria, marked right conjunctival hyperemia, neck stiffness, and a diffuse non-blanching petechial and purpuric rash. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was consistent with purulent meningitis; Gram stain showed rare Gram-negative diplococci, and CSF PCR identified N. meningitidis serogroup W, ST-11. Ophthalmologic examination confirmed severe endogenous endophthalmitis with hypopyon, and vitreous PCR also detected N. meningitidis. Because of clinical non-response with progressive ocular and neurologic involvement, therapy was escalated to meropenem. On hospital day 7, acute right knee swelling developed, and synovial fluid PCR confirmed MenW. At 6-week follow-up, the patient had normal vision, normal hearing, no neurologic deficits, and no joint sequelae.ConclusionMeningococcal endophthalmitis is rare and usually associated with poor visual outcomes; only one adult MenW case has been reported previously. This case demonstrates that MenW ST-11 can cause aggressive multisystem IMD in children and underscores the importance of molecular diagnosis, early multidisciplinary care, and MenACWY vaccination.

Introduction

Invasive meningococcal disease (IMD) can progress rapidly and unpredictably, so timely and precise microbiological diagnosis, including confirmation and characterization of Neisseria meningitidis, is essential, both to guide treatment and to inform the public-health response. Although IMD classically presents as meningitis, meningococcemia, or both, N. meningitidis can also cause extra-meningeal manifestations including septic arthritis, pneumonia, myocarditis, pericarditis, conjunctivitis, and rarely endophthalmitis [1, 2]. Serogroup-specific differences in clinical phenotype have been increasingly recognized, particularly for serogroup W (MenW), which is associated with atypical and invasive presentations [3–5]. We report an 11-year-old previously healthy boy with N. meningitidis serogroup W sequence type 11 (MenW ST-11) who developed the exceptional triad of meningitis, endogenous endophthalmitis, and septic arthritis.

Case report

An 11-year-old boy residing in a school dormitory, developed fever, rhinorrhea and cough. The following day he developed a generalized erythematous rash and conjunctival redness and was assessed at an external emergency department, where he was discharged on oral amoxicillin-clavulanic acid due to upper respiratory tract infection. Because of worsening lethargy, persistent rash, and poor oral intake, he re-presented to a second emergency department, where meningitis was suspected and he received one dose of intravenous ceftriaxone before being transferred to our pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) for altered consciousness and seizures. The entire course from the first symptoms to PICU admission occurred within approximately 36 h. His routine childhood immunizations were complete according to the National Immunization Program in Türkiye; however, he had not received meningococcal vaccination (neither meningococcal B nor meningococcal ACWY vaccine), as these vaccines are not included in the national program.

On admission, he was confused and lethargic, with anisocoria, marked right conjunctival hyperemia with hypopyon, neck stiffness and a diffuse non-blanching petechial and purpuric rash over the trunk and extremities. Vital signs were stable, with no hypotension or respiratory distress. An urgent cranial computed tomography scan was performed prior to lumbar puncture and demonstrated no intracranial hemorrhage, mass lesion, or hydrocephalus, but showed mucosal thickening in the paranasal sinuses. Empirical cefotaxime and vancomycin were started. The cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis was cloudy, with 230 leukocytes/mm³ (neutrophil predominant, 90%), a protein concentration of 199 mg/dL, and glucose of 13.4 mg/dL. Gram stain demonstrated rare Gram-negative diplococci, and CSF PCR was positive for N. meningitidis and confirmed by National Public Health Reference Laboratory. The case was reported to the local health authority and close contacts received chemoprophylaxis with single-dose ciprofloxacin (500 mg for adult contact) and rifampicin for children. Because all sterile-site samples were obtained after the patient had already received antibiotics (a single oral dose of amoxicillin-clavulanic acid followed by one dose of intravenous ceftriaxone), the cultures were negative despite a positive Gram stain. Repeated blood cultures, as well as CSF cultures, remained negative. We also performed a single-tube multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) analysis for the simultaneous detection of N.meningitidis. We performed a general screening using sodC, CtrA, PorA, tauE and metA. A (Orf-2), B (Sia D), C (Sia D), Y (Sia D), X (CtrA), W (Sia D), E (cseE), Z (cszC), and H (cshC) serogrouping was performed on the samples that tested positive for N. meningitidis. Serogrouping and multilocus sequence typing (MLST) were performed directly from CSF DNA by Sanger sequencing, identifying serogroup W, ST-11.

Because of the ocular findings, the patient was urgently evaluated by ophthalmology and diagnosed with severe endogenous endophthalmitis with hypopyon; vitreous PCR detected N. meningitidis. After vitreous PCR had identified N. meningitidis, intravitreal vancomycin and ceftazidime were administered, together with intensive ocular topical therapy. Given the fulminant, multisystem serious bacterial infection, adjunctive IgM-enriched immunoglobulin was administered, an approach used in our PICU for severe bacterial infections. Echocardiography was normal, and there were no clinical features of an endovascular focus. Because there was no clinical improvement during the first three days despite high-dose cefotaxime, and because of progressive ocular and neurologic involvement (increased seizure activity), therapy was escalated to meropenem; this decision was based on clinical non-response rather than on any evidence of cephalosporin resistance, while source control of the control focus addressed with intravitreal therapy.

The patient’s level of consciousness gradually improved, and no new cutaneous lesions developed. Neck stiffness progressively decreased, and no seizures were observed. He was transferred to the pediatric infectious diseases ward. On hospital day 7, he developed acute right knee swelling and pain. Synovial fluid contained 95,000 polymorphonuclear leukocytes/mm³, and PCR confirmed N. meningitidis serogroup W, establishing septic arthritis. Surgical drainage with hemovac placement was performed.

He completed a total of 14 days of parenteral antibiotic therapy (initial empirical cefotaxime and vancomycin, followed by meropenem after escalation); given the multisystem invasive disease involving three sterile sites, an intravenous-to-oral switch was not considered appropriate, and parenteral therapy was continued throughout. Because invasive, multisystem meningococcal disease in a previously healthy child can be associated with an underlying inborn error of immunity, particularly complement pathway deficiencies that predispose to meningococcal infection, the patient was evaluated by pediatric immunology; baseline C3 and C4 complement levels were normal, and no underlying immunodeficiency was identified on this baseline assessment. At discharge, he was alert, oriented, and ambulatory with physiotherapy support. The right pupil remained fixed and dilated, attributed to iris sphincter injury secondary to intraocular inflammation, but visual acuity was preserved. The knee showed an improving range of motion. At the 6-week follow-up, the patient had normal vision, normal hearing on audiometric testing, no neurologic deficits, and no joint sequelae.

Discussion

Our case represents the first pediatric MenW ST-11 case with simultaneous fulminant CNS, ocular, and joint involvement. Meningococcal endophthalmitis is uncommon and associated with poor visual outcomes. Paez Guillan et al. [6] reviewed 38 cases with meningococcal endophthalmitis, spanning infants to older adults (3 months–58 years), pediatric patients predominate, and showed predominance of MenC and MenB, with no MenW. Endophthalmitis was unilateral and approximately 60–70% of reported cases occurred in the setting of meningitis like our patient. Ocular findings were typically acute and severe, including hypopyon, panuveitis, vitreitis, retinal necrosis, and papilledema, often progressing rapidly to structural damage. Visual outcomes were poor (≈ 60% permanent visual loss, ≈ 25–30% retinal detachment, and enucleation in severe cases) [6]. Lo et al. [7] further demonstrated that even isolated meningococcal endophthalmitis without meningitis or bacteremia in 25 patients (80% are unilateral, 52% are children), leads to devastating visual outcomes. Only one MenW endophthalmitis has been previously reported. Shah et al. [8] reported a 22-year-old woman with diabetes who developed endogenous MenW endophthalmitis (confirmed with PCR analysis of vitreous sampling) with septic arthritis, but no meningitis. Early ophthalmologic intervention and intravitreal antibiotics likely contributed to stabilization and in our case, while the patient has severe endophthalmitis, with intensive systemic treatment, patient visual functions recovered.

In Paez Guillan et al. [6] series, articular pain or arthritis was reported in at least one third of cases with endophthalmitis, Lo et al. [7] showed that 48% of patients with endophthalmitis have myalgia and arthralgia and 8% have septic arthritis. These findings underscoring hematogenous dissemination to joints and eyes. However, in our case clinical course of joint involvement are quite different, not available at admission, has been observed at 7th days of admission, despite broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. Arthritis, while reported in IMD, typically occurs in late immune-mediated phases; however, our patient demonstrated acute purulent septic arthritis. In the literature, arthritis in MenW disease — including septic arthritis confirmed by culture or PCR — has been described as an established but relatively infrequent manifestation (reported in up to ~ 15% of some cohorts) [3]. In our patient, septic arthritis was confirmed by PCR detection of MenW in synovial fluid, the synovial fluid culture, like the blood, CSF, and vitreous cultures, was negative, so phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing could not be performed. Population surveillance data from England and Wales showed that MenW accounted for ~ 40.9% of meningococcal septic arthritis cases between 2010 and 2020 in children and adolescents [9].

MenW ST-11 (cc11) is a hypervirulent lineage responsible for global waves of serogroup W disease since the Hajj-associated outbreaks of 2000–2001, in worldwide including Türkiye [2, 5]. This lineage (specifically the South American/UK W-135 clone, ST-11) is associated with atypical extra meningeal presentations including pneumonia, septic arthritis, abdominal symptoms, endophthalmitis, epiglottitis, with greater severity and mortality [3, 4]. Although penicillin reduced susceptibility has been described in cc11 strains [10], third-generation cephalosporins remain first-line; and we have no evidence that the infecting strain was cephalosporin resistant; indeed, the blood and CSF cultures were rapidly sterile and N. meningitidis was detectable only by PCR. The escalation to meropenem in our patient was therefore driven by ongoing clinical deterioration (persistent fever, elevated acute-phase reactants, continued meningeal signs, and seizures) rather than by microbiological treatment failure, and most likely reflected the severity of the host inflammatory response and the evolving metastatic foci rather than antimicrobial inadequacy. This course nonetheless underscores the need for close clinical monitoring and intensive supportive care in complicated MenW disease. Given the fulminant, multisystem bacterial infection, adjunctive IgM-enriched immunoglobulin was administered, an approach used in our unit for severe bacterial infection and supported by evidence for IgM-enriched immunoglobulin as adjunctive therapy in pediatric sepsis and in invasive meningococcal disease [11–13].

Our report has some important limitations. First, although N. meningitidis was confirmed by PCR at three sterile sites (CSF, vitreous, and synovial fluid), all bacterial cultures — blood, CSF, vitreous, and synovial fluid — remained negative. Septic arthritis was therefore confirmed molecularly, by synovial fluid PCR, rather than by culture, and the uniformly negative cultures precluded phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing and whole-genome sequencing. Second, although baseline C3 and C4 levels were normal, comprehensive complement pathway testing and genetic analysis were not performed. Despite these constraints, the diagnosis was robustly supported by molecular detection at three sterile sites and Sanger-based MLST, which established MenW ST-11 as the causative strain .

Previous reports describe endophthalmitis with meningococcemia or meningitis, and rare cases of meningococcal arthritis, but not all three manifestations concurrently. This case shows that MenW ST-11 can cause aggressive, multisystem IMD with meningitis, endogenous endophthalmitis, and septic arthritis in a previously healthy child. It highlights three important points: (1) ocular and joint symptoms in meningococcal disease should prompt urgent evaluation; (2) molecular diagnosis from CSF, vitreous, and synovial fluid can provide critical epidemiologic and clinical insight; and (3) hyperinvasive MenW ST-11 may cause aggressive, multisystem disease, underscoring the importance of MenACWY vaccination and rapid public-health interventions. This message is particularly relevant to the Turkish context: meningococcal vaccines (both MenACWY and MenB) are not included in the national immunization program and are available only on the private market, and our patient was an unvaccinated adolescent living in a school dormitory. In Türkiye, serogroup B is the predominant cause of childhood IMD, followed by serogroup W [14]. MenW has previously caused outbreak-like activity but has been largely controlled through targeted vaccination of military conscripts and Hajj/Umrah pilgrims; this targeted strategy, however, leaves the general pediatric and adolescent population unprotected against a MenACWY-preventable serogroup, as illustrated by the present case. While a single case cannot establish population-level policy, it supports consideration of broader MenACWY vaccination. Molecular diagnosis, early multidisciplinary care, and close clinical monitoring with intensive supportive management were crucial to a favorable systemic outcome.
