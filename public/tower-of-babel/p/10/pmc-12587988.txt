Role development needs of stroke advanced practice nurses in mainland China hospital: a participatory qualitative study
Authors: Chujin Qiu, Lijuan Duan, Chaofeng Fan, Yan Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo identify challenges in stroke care services and stakeholders’ expectations for the role of stroke advanced practice nurses (APNs) in the neurology department of a tertiary hospital in ainland China.DesignA field study using participatory observation and in-depth interviews.SettingThe neurology department of a tertiary hospital in Sichuan Province, China.ParticipantsThe principal researcher conducted participant observation in the capacity of a clinical nurse. In-depth individual interviews were conducted within 7 stroke patients and their carers, 4 doctors and 10 nurses.ResultsKey challenges included a shortage of specialised professionals, imprecise, untimely and discontinuous care, poor medical–nursing collaboration, limited nursing expertise and unmet patient needs. Stakeholders expected stroke APNs to fulfil six roles: stroke care expert, advanced clinical practitioner, collaborative coordinator, specialist care innovator, specialised educator and role model.ConclusionsStroke APNs should provide precise, timely and continuous care for patients while enhancing team cohesion, overall competence and service quality.

Introduction

Stroke is a significant global public health issue, ranking as the second leading cause of death worldwide and the leading cause of adult mortality and disability in China.1 The rising incidence and burden of stroke have strained Chinese healthcare systems, with challenges including increasing complexity of stroke cases,2 demand for multidisciplinary symptom management3 4 and the growing demand for clinical stroke care.5 6 To mitigate healthcare resource shortages and improve service quality, many developed countries have expanded the role of advanced practice nurses (APNs).7 8 APNs are registered nurses with advanced clinical expertise, complex decision-making skills and competencies in leadership, management, education, research, consultation and interprofessional collaboration.9 The role of stroke APNs has been explored in countries such as the United Kingdom, the USA and Canada, with significant improvements in stroke management, patient outcomes, service quality and cost-effectiveness.10 11

In mainland China, the APN role remains in the early exploratory stage, with no structured APN role established. Currently, the stroke nursing workforce operates at specialist nurse level. Limited by gaps in multidisciplinary knowledge and core clinical competencies, these stroke specialist nurses often struggle to manage complex stroke cases or coordinate multidisciplinary care effectively.12 Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop advanced nursing roles to address the care needs of patients with stroke and bridge professional care gaps. The International Council of Nurses (ICN) recommends that APN role development must adapt to local healthcare systems and societal needs to ensure that APNs can practice legally and meet patients/stakeholders’ expectations.9 Given differences in cultural contexts and healthcare structures, Western APN models cannot be directly replicated in mainland China. Therefore, we aimed to investigate stroke APN role development needs within the Chinese clinical context.

To guide this study, we drew on Brown’s Framework for Advanced Practice Nursing as a conceptual foundation.13 This model, widely used in APN role development, comprises four key components: context, role requirements, advanced nursing practice and outcomes (as shown in figure 1). We applied this framework to systematically analyse the current challenges in stroke care and stakeholders’ expectations for the APN role. Specifically, the context and role requirements components informed our exploration of institutional conditions and professional legitimacy, while the advanced nursing practice domain guided the identification of core role functions—such as clinical expertise, coordination, leadership and education—expected of stroke APNs. The outcome dimension helped us assess the anticipated impact across patient, system and professional levels. By aligning our analysis with this established framework, we ensure conceptual coherence in characterising the developmental needs of stroke APNs and strengthen the link between observed care gaps and proposed role expectations.

Based on the research background and theoretical foundation, the following main research questions were formulated: (1) What are the perceived role expectations and practice gaps of current stroke nursing professionals in mainland China? (2) How do stakeholders envision the integration and responsibilities of an APN role within the stroke care pathway? (3) What factors facilitate or hinder the development of a contextually appropriate stroke APN role in mainland China? These questions aim to explore the multifaceted dimensions of role development and inform the design of a culturally and systemically responsive APN framework for stroke care in China.

Methods

Study design

This study adopted a field study design, using participatory observation and in-depth interviews in a real-world clinical setting. This approach was chosen because it enables direct investigation of complex, context-dependent phenomena—such as role expectations and system-level challenges in hospital care—that are shaped by daily practices, team dynamics and organisational structures. As a clinical nurse, the researcher conducted participatory observation within the stroke unit, allowing firsthand insight into care processes, workflow barriers and interdisciplinary interactions. In-depth interviews with key stakeholders complemented these observations by capturing perspectives on current gaps and future role expectations for stroke APN. The integration of observation and interview data enhanced credibility and contextual depth, supporting the identification of both visible and latent system challenges. The field study design thus provided a naturalistic, holistic foundation for understanding how clinical realities and stakeholder expectations intersect—essential for developing a feasible and contextually relevant APN role.

Study setting and participants

The study was conducted in the neurology department of a tertiary hospital in Sichuan Province, China. Participants were selected to address issues in stroke care services, and interviewees included department managers, doctors, nurses, patients with stroke and their carers. To explore expectations for stroke APN, interviews focused on department managers and clinical staff. The specific inclusion criteria for each participant group are listed in table 1. Data collection

Participatory observation

Between July and September 2021, the researcher participated as a clinical nurse in the neurology department, observing the daily workflow, care processes and multidisciplinary interactions. Data collection incorporated multiple methods: direct observation, audio recordings, document analysis and reflective journaling. The key aspects of the observation included (a) understanding the clinical environment, work protocols and nursing procedures, (b) assisting nurses in daily care tasks and engaging in patient care activities, (c) participating in physician rounds, case discussions and staff training sessions and (d) conducting patient education and discharge follow-ups to assess the needs of patients and their carers.

In-depth interviews

Semistructured individual interviews were conducted with participants between October and November 2021. Each interview lasted 30–40 min and was conducted in a private setting. Tailored interview guides for each participant group were developed (table 2). Prior to formal data collection, a pilot phase was conducted, consisting of cognitive preinterviews with four individuals (one patient, one carer, one doctor and one nurse) who met the inclusion criteria but were not included in the final sample. These preinterviews aimed to assess the clarity, relevance and flow of the interview questions.

Based on feedback from the preinterviews, minor revisions were made to the interview guides: certain questions were rephrased for improved clarity and cultural appropriateness, and two additional questions were added to better capture expectations regarding preparation for the stroke APN role and improvements to advanced stroke nursing practice. These refinements ensured that the final interview guides were comprehensible, comprehensive and aligned with participants’ perspectives. Data collection proceeded only after finalising the revised guides, and recruitment continued until thematic saturation was achieved (when no new themes emerged from subsequent interviews).

Researcher reflexivity and bias mitigation

The researcher, as a clinical nurse participating in the stroke unit, held a dual role as both a care provider and an observer. While this insider position facilitated access to the clinical environment, enhanced trust with participants and enabled deep contextual understanding, it also carried the potential for bias—such as preconceptions about nursing practices or unconscious alignment with professional norms. To address this, two strategies were employed to ensure methodological rigour and reflexivity. First, reflective journaling was conducted throughout the observation and interview phases, in which the researcher documented personal reactions, assumptions and evolving interpretations. These journals were later reviewed during data analysis to identify and bracket potential biases. Second, member checking was used during data analysis: preliminary findings were shared with a subset of nurse participants to confirm accuracy and resonance with their experiences. Together, these strategies strengthened the credibility, transferability and dependability of the findings, while promoting transparency in how the researcher’s dual role was acknowledged and managed.

Data analysis

For participatory observation data, the researcher conducted a comprehensive review of all observational materials, followed by thematic analysis to establish preliminary codes. These codes were systematically categorised to summarise both current practices and existing challenges in stroke clinical care at the study site.

Regarding interview data, qualitative content analysis was performed using NVivo V.12.0 software, following Graneheim and Lundman’s15 inductive approach. The analysis involved: (a) reading transcripts to gain comprehensive understanding, (b) identifying meaningful units and coding them into subcategories, (c) grouping-related subcategories into broader categories, (d) synthesising interconnected categories into overarching themes. This iterative process continued until reaching data saturation. To ensure analytical rigour and trustworthiness, two researchers (the first author and the second author) independently conducted initial coding of the interview transcripts. Both coders were trained in qualitative methodology and familiar with the study context. After independent coding, they met regularly to compare codes, discuss discrepancies and refine preliminary categories. When disagreements arose in coding or theme development, a third senior researcher (the corresponding author), who was not directly involved in the initial coding, was consulted to facilitate consensus through discussion and resolution of divergent interpretations.

Besides, to enhance the level of transferability, patients with stroke patients were allocated ID codes (P1, P2, etc), carer participants (C1, C2, etc), doctors (D1, D2, etc), nursing managers (NM1, NM2, etc) and clinical nurses (N1, N2, etc). Besides, our study enhances the credibility and reliability of data analysis through member checking.

Patient and public involvement

During the recruitment phase, patients and their carers were engaged through direct communication with healthcare professionals who introduced them to the study. They were given detailed information about the study’s purpose, procedures and their rights as participants. Throughout the conduct of the study, they were actively involved through semistructured interviews that explored their experiences, expectations and suggestions for improvement in stroke care. Their involvement ensured that the data collected reflected real-world issues and solutions from the perspective of those receiving care.

Results

Participatory observation findings

The observational data revealed that while the department maintained clearly defined stroke care processes and well-organised divisions of labour, several systemic challenges persist in stroke care services, including: (a) shortage of stroke specialists: the number of medical staff was insufficient to meet the needs of patients with stroke, causing delays in addressing patient and caregiver demands, which compromised care quality. (b) Communication deficits: ineffective communication among healthcare teams (particularly between doctors and nurses) led to poor patient understanding of treatment plans and misalignment in care delivery, affecting outcomes. (c) Rehabilitation gaps: the lack of routinely integrated rehabilitation services resulted in delayed or missed interventions for some patients, potentially hindering recovery. (d) Care continuity issues: despite nurses’ follow-up efforts, limited resources created postdischarge management gaps, elevating risks of complications and hospital readmission.

The stakeholder perspectives revealed both convergence and divergence in expectations for the stroke APN role. Patients and carers primarily emphasised the APN’s role in enhancing communication, providing continuous emotional support and delivering accessible health education—highlighting needs for empathy, clarity and personal connection throughout the care journey. In contrast, healthcare professionals placed greater emphasis on the APN as a clinical leader, coordinator of multidisciplinary care and innovator in practice and research, with expectations centred on improving team efficiency, clinical decision-making and service integration. Notably, strong convergence was observed in the shared expectation that APNs should act as collaborative coordinators—both groups valued the APN’s potential to bridge communication gaps between medical teams and patients/families. Similarly, both patients/carers and professionals anticipated that APNs would deliver more precise, timely and continuous care, particularly in rehabilitation and discharge planning.

Discussions

This study provides a comprehensive exploration of stroke APN role development needs in mainland China, highlighting existing challenges in stroke care and potential APN integration pathways. The findings underscore the critical need for specialised nursing roles to address the growing burden of stroke and improve patient outcomes. Below, we discuss these insights with reference to global literature while refining the analysis.

Addressing gaps in stroke care through APN’s integration

In the study, we identified significant stroke care gaps, including delayed response to patient needs, inconsistent care continuity and limited rehabilitation services. These findings align with global stroke management challenges where APNs have proven effective in bridging such gaps. In the USA, stroke unit APNs have reduced hospital readmission rates by 20% through improved care coordination and patient education.11 Similarly, UK APN-led stroke clinics improved rehabilitation outcomes via timely interventions.16 Chinese current reliance on specialist nurses—who lack advanced clinical decision-making authority—limits complex stroke case management. Introducing APNs could mitigate these challenges by (a) providing continuous care: APNs can oversee the entire stroke care pathway from acute treatment to postdischarge follow-up, ensuring seamless transitions.8 (b) Enhancing rehabilitation: while critical for stroke recovery, rehabilitation remains underprioritised in mainland China. APNs could lead rehabilitation initiatives, as seen in Canada, where APNs reduced disability rates by 15% through early mobilisation.17 These findings position APNs as potential linchpins in Chinese stroke care system, capable of addressing both clinical and operational inefficiencies.

Comparative positioning of the stroke APN role

The six stroke APN roles identified in our study can be better understood through two comparative lenses: specialisation within nursing and adaptation to local healthcare systems. Compared with generalist APN (eg, in primary or chronic care), the stroke APN role places stronger emphasis on acute neurological assessment, time-sensitive interventions (eg, airway management, early mobilisation) and seamless transition from acute to rehabilitation care. This reflects the unique demands of stroke—a sudden, disabling condition requiring protocol-driven, expert nursing within critical time windows.11

When contrasted with stroke APNs in high-income countries, a key difference emerges: contextual function. In the UK, USA or Canada, where multidisciplinary stroke units are well standard, APNs often lead within established teams.16 17 In contrast, our findings show that in Chinese fragmented and hierarchical hospitals, the collaborative coordinator role is not supplementary but foundational—APNs are expected to actively bridge communication gaps, lead team coordination and ensure care continuity in the absence of integrated systems. Moreover, while stroke APNs in countries like Australia and Singapore operate under formal regulatory frameworks,18 19 their Chinese counterparts must first establish professional legitimacy. Thus, the emphasis on role model and innovator also reflects a preinstitutional phase of role development.

In sum, the stroke APN model in mainland China integrates global competencies but is distinct in its focus on acute expertise, system coordination and role legitimisation—shaped by both the nature of stroke and the realities of China’s healthcare delivery.

Education and training: building a sustainable APN workforce

This study highlights that systematic training is essential for stroke APN role development. Early challenges in the USA demonstrated that ad hoc training programmes resulted in inconsistent APN competencies.20 The importance of structured education is evidenced by the American Association of Colleges of Nursing’s competency-based curricula.21 For mainland China, we recommend: (a) Developing standardised curricula: Programmes should combine stroke-specific modules (eg, neurocritical care, rehabilitation) with leadership training. (b) Implementing mentorship: Canada’s model pairing novice APNs with experienced mentors achieved 30% faster competency acquisition,22 which China could adapt. Continuing professional development (CPD) remains crucial. Singapore’s research established that CPD requirements for APNs positively correlate with patient outcomes.23 The Chinese National Health Commission could implement similar mandatory CPD benchmarks to facilitate APNs’ ongoing professional growth.

The need for role clarity, professional recognition and contextual adaptation

This study identifies role ambiguity as a recurring theme, with stakeholders linking it to care delivery inefficiencies. Such confusion is not unique to China, having been documented in other countries’ early APN implementation phases. For example, in the USA, initial lack of clear APN scope created overlaps with physician responsibilities, delaying care.24 However, in mainland China, the feasibility of establishing a clear and effective APN role is further constrained by three inter-related contextual factors: regulatory frameworks, hospital hierarchies and resource limitations. First, the absence of a formal regulatory framework for APNs in China means there is no nationally recognised certification, defined scope of practice or legal authority for advanced nursing functions (eg, autonomous clinical decision-making, diagnostic ordering).25 Without policy endorsement from the National Health Commission, the APN risks being perceived as an honorary title rather than a functional clinical role, undermining professional legitimacy and accountability. Second, deeply entrenched hospital hierarchies and physician-dominated care models present cultural barriers. As highlighted in our data, nurses often delay interventions pending physician orders, reflecting limited autonomy. Introducing APNs as advanced clinical practitioners or collaborative coordinators challenges this traditional structure, necessitating cultural shifts and active physician engagement to redefine interprofessional roles and build trust. Third, resource constraints—including nursing shortages and limited funding—threaten the sustainability of expanded roles. Stakeholders expect APNs to lead rehabilitation, coordinate multidisciplinary teams and ensure postdischarge continuity, yet these responsibilities require dedicated time and institutional support. Without additional staffing or financial investment, APN duties may be added to existing workloads, increasing burnout risk.

Nonetheless, growing national priorities in specialist nursing development and demands for high-quality, patient-centred care offer a strategic opportunity.26 Pilot programmes in tertiary hospitals can serve as testbeds to define role clarity, demonstrate value and advocate for policy reform. A contextually adapted APN model—one that balances advanced practice with China’s healthcare realities—is essential for successful implementation.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the relatively small sample size may limit the generalisability of findings across all Chinese healthcare settings. Second, while this qualitative research provides in-depth insights, additional quantitative validation would strengthen the results. Future studies should employ larger, more diverse samples and mixed-method approaches to strengthen the evidence base for stroke APN role development.

Conclusions

This study highlights critical gaps in stroke care services and stakeholders’ expectations for stroke APNs in China. Our findings demonstrate that stroke APNs should deliver high-quality, continuous care while fulfilling key roles across clinical practice, care coordination, education, management and research. Implementing structured training programmes, establishing a clear role framework and promoting professional certification are essential to support the development of stroke APN in mainland China.
