Ramadan fasting and adverse outcomes in cirrhosis: primary risk estimates with associated independent predictors
Authors: Ignatius Bima Prasetya, Irsan Hasan, Rabbinu Rangga Pribadi, Dicky Levenus Tahapary, Saut Horas Hatoguan Nababan, Hamzah Shatri, Imam Subekti, Sarah Firdausa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRamadan fasting is observed annually by millions of Muslims worldwide. Although generally considered safe, its clinical impact on patients with cirrhosis remains insufficiently defined. To evaluate the impact of Ramadan fasting on hospitalization, hypoglycaemia, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, Child-Pugh score, and quality-of-life in patients with cirrhosis. This prospective cohort study was conducted at Cipto-Mangunkusumo Hospital and included adult patients with liver cirrhosis without hepatocellular carcinoma who intended to fast during Ramadan. Patients were evaluated during non-fasting and Ramadan periods for hospitalization, hypoglycaemia, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, Child-Pugh score, and quality of life using the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire. Hypoglycaemia was assessed in a subgroup using continuous glucose monitoring. Of 156 participants (35.9% decompensated; 52 with continuous glucose monitoring), Ramadan fasting was associated with higher rates of hospitalization (12.2% vs. 3.2%; p = 0.003), upper gastrointestinal bleeding (10.9% vs. 1.3%; p < 0.001), and hypoglycaemia (13.5% vs. 0%; p = 0.013), but not with deterioration of Child-Pugh category (14.1% vs. 9.0%; p = 0.156) or quality-of-life scores (9.0% vs. 6.4%; p = 0.395). Ramadan fasting in cirrhosis increases the risk of hospitalization, upper gastrointestinal bleeding, and hypoglycaemia. Careful monitoring and individualized fasting guidance are recommended.Keywords: Cirrhosis, Ramadan, Hospitalization, Hypoglycaemia, Upper gastrointestinal bleeding, Child-Pugh, Quality-of-lifeSubject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Health care, Medical research

Introduction

Liver cirrhosis remains a major global health burden, with an estimated 58.4 million incident cases and approximately 1.43 million deaths in 2021, making it a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide1. Cirrhosis is characterized by progressive hepatic fibrosis and impaired liver function, predisposing patients to serious complications such as ascites, variceal bleeding, and hepatic encephalopathy1,2. These complications might be exacerbated by the dehydration and metabolic stress during Ramadan fasting, leading to adverse clinical events.

In Islamic teaching, Ramadan Fasting is a compulsory act of abstaining from consuming any kind of food and drink as well as several other actions from dawn to sunset during the Holy Month of Ramadan. This spiritual practice is observed annually by most of the 1.2–1.5 billion Muslims worldwide. Refraining from this practice is forbidden for all adult Muslims, but some populations such as women during their menstrual period, the elderly and those with acute diseases in whom fasting would be harmful are exempted from this obligation2–5. Physiologically, prolonged fasting may lead to dehydration and metabolic stress. While these changes are generally well tolerated in healthy individuals, compensatory mechanisms may be insufficient in elderly patients or those with chronic medical conditions6. Yet, the regulation is still vague regarding those with chronic medical conditions, leading many cases to intuitions of doctors and patients of whether a chronic patient is eligible to fast or not. Without proper research and evidence, this might lead to harmful consequences since patients with chronic medical conditions, including liver cirrhosis, are more prone to metabolic stresses due to prolonged fasting2,3. For some chronic conditions such as diabetes, experts have conducted numerous studies and came up with several consensuses on the practice of Ramadan Fasting in that specific populations4,7. In the case of Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD), experts have also published several recommendations on the safety of Ramadan Fasting in this population5,8.

In cirrhosis, the long period of fasting followed by the typical heavy iftar meal to break the fast during Ramadan may lead to alterations in the portal vein pressure and this in turn might cause increased risk of Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding (UGIB)9,10. In addition, impaired hepatic gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis in cirrhosis may predispose patients to hypoglycaemia during fasting2,10,11. All these complications might then increase the risk of hospitalization and decrease the overall quality-of-life. Despite all these theoretical hazards of fasting in cirrhosis patients, limited evidence is currently available to formulate specific recommendations on this issue. Available published clinical trials on Ramadan Fasting in cirrhosis populations are limited in number and in the quality of the study12–16. Results of these trials were also conflicted, especially in terms of the impact of Ramadan Fasting on portal vein parameters and the Child-Pugh category as indicators of cirrhosis severity15,16. None of the studies directly assessed the impact of Ramadan fasting on the risk of hospitalization, hypoglycaemia, and quality-of-life of cirrhosis patients. Considering the highly religious aspect of Ramadan Fasting and the high number of observing cirrhosis population annually, further evidence is needed to formulate further guidelines on this specific population. In this study, we aim to evaluate the impact of Ramadan Fasting on hospitalization, hypoglycaemia, Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding (UGIB), deterioration of Child-Pugh score, and the quality-of-life of cirrhosis patients. Findings from this study might provide millions of Muslim cirrhosis patients and their healthcare practitioners with specific knowledge to navigate the challenges of fasting during Ramadan.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a prospective cohort study conducted at the Hepatology and Gastroenterology Outpatient Clinic of the Cipto Mangunkusumo National General Hospital (RSCM), Jakarta, Indonesia. Patient recruitment took place in February 2025, one month prior to the start of Ramadan that year.

Study population

All adult patients with a confirmed diagnosis of liver cirrhosis who visited the clinic during the recruitment period were screened for eligibility. The inclusion criteria were: aged 18 years or older, intended voluntarily to observe fasting during the upcoming Ramadan, able to undergo a clinical interview, and willing to provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: diagnosis of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and no plan for follow-up visits to RSCM during the study period.

Data collection

After obtaining informed consent, all patients underwent structured interviews and physical examinations. Medical records were examined for relevant data and further laboratory or radiology examinations were done to complete any missing data. The following baseline data were collected: age, etiology and onset of cirrhosis, history of diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic kidney disease (CKD), prior history of oesophageal varices, prior episodes of Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding (UGIB), serum albumin, total bilirubin, prothrombin time (PT), random blood glucose, blood urea, and serum creatinine. History of oesophageal varices was defined as the documented presence of oesophageal varices on prior endoscopy, including cases without a history of bleeding, whereas previous UGIB referred specifically to prior episodes of any hematemesis or melena events. Liver stiffness was assessed by transient elastography when recent (within 6 month) results were unavailable.

In this study, Patients were considered to have observed Ramadan Fasting if they successfully completed at least 20 days of fasting with full adherence. Hospitalization was defined as any episode of inpatient care due to an acute clinical condition. Hypoglycaemia was defined as a capillary blood glucose value < 70 mg/dL detected by continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) and confirmed by point-of-care testing (POCT). Upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) was defined as the occurrence of hematemesis or melena. A poor Child-Pugh classification was defined as a Child-Pugh score ≥ 9. Poor quality of life was defined as a Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ) score ≥ 5. CLDQ was used as the preferred tool to assess quality-of-life in this study due to its ease of use and the fact that it had been validated in Indonesian population with good validity and reliability.

During one month prior of Ramadan Fasting (February 2025), patients were assessed to evaluate clinical outcomes during the pre-Ramadan (non-fasting) month. The following data were collected: history of hospitalization, hypoglycaemia, Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding (UGIB), hepatic encephalopathy grade, ascites, serum albumin, total bilirubin, prothrombin time (PT) and quality-of-life using the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ). Child-Pugh Score was assessed using the collected data. Assessment of hypoglycaemia was done only in subgroup of 52 patients that were randomly selected and provided consent to undergo continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) for two weeks before Ramadan using the Sinocare I3 CGM device. Additionally, capillary random blood glucose was measured at 06:00 and 09:00 AM daily. Patients were instructed to perform point-of-care capillary glucose testing (POCT) if CGM alerted them to hypoglycaemia or if they experienced clinical symptoms such as diaphoresis or visual disturbances.

A follow-up evaluation was conducted during month of Ramadan (March 2025) to assess outcomes during the fasting period. Similar to the pre-Ramadan assessment, the following were recorded: history of hospitalization, hypoglycaemia, Upper Gastrointestinal Bleeding (UGIB), hepatic encephalopathy grade, ascites, serum albumin, total bilirubin, prothrombin time (PT) and quality-of-life using the Chronic Liver Disease Questionnaire (CLDQ). The same subgroup of 52 patients underwent CGM for two weeks during Ramadan, with daily capillary glucose checks at 15:00 and 18:00. Hypoglycaemia alerts or symptoms prompted immediate POCT glucose testing.

Outcome measures and statistical analysis

Primary outcomes included hospitalization, hypoglycaemia events, UGIB episodes, Child-Pugh classification, and quality-of-life, categorized into two periods: non-fasting (pre-Ramadan) and fasting (during Ramadan). Paired statistical analyses were used to assess differences between these periods. All patients dropping out of the observation will be accounted for the final analysis in accordance with intention-to-treat principle.

Sensitivity analysis was conducted by repeating the analysis of primary outcomes in a narrower population, in this case patients with decompensated cirrhosis (defined as presence of active/prior ascites, variceal bleeding, or encephalopathy). Similar results in the sensitivity analysis would mean the main findings were robust.

Additional analyses were conducted to explore associations between suspected risk factors and the incidence of significant study outcomes during fasting period. Bivariate analyses were followed by multivariate logistic regression to identify independent predictors of adverse outcomes. All statistical analysis were done using SPSS for Windows v24.0. A significant difference was considered when p value was < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

All participants were provided with detailed information regarding the study’s objectives, procedures, and potential risks or complications. Patients were specifically counselled about the possible dangers associated with observing Ramadan Fasting in cirrhosis. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary and only permitted after obtaining written informed consent. Patients who chose to fast did so willingly, having acknowledged and accepted the associated risks. This study protocol had been reviewed and approved by the Health Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Indonesia – Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital (FKUI–RSCM), with ethical clearance number KET 1634/UN2.F1/ETIK/PPM.00.02/2024.

Results

Recruitment process

Recruitment process for the study subjects was carried out during mid-January to early-February 2025. A total of 370 cirrhosis patients visited the Hepatology and gastroenterology Outpatient Clinic of RSCM during this period, among which 249 were practicing Muslims. From these 249 patients, 13 patients decided not to participate in the Ramadan fasting, 35 patients had history of HCC, 2 patients planned on not going back to RSCM, and 43 patients had incomplete data. Therefore, on February 2025, study observation was started for the non-fasting period with 156 cirrhosis patients. From this total, 52 patients were randomly picked for CGM procedure for hypoglycaemia evaluation. During February 2025, none of the observed patients changed their decisions to observe this year’s Ramadan Fasting, so at the start of March 2025, observation on the fasting group was started with the same 156 cirrhosis patients. During the Ramadan Fasting period, 14 of 156 patients failed to complete at least 20 days of fasting (1 patient had UGIB, 4 patients were hospitalized for non-UGIB cases, 2 patients died, and 7 patients stopped fasting due to fatigue), setting the total number of patients who finished the Ramadan Fasting to 142. Among the 52 patients with CGM, 49 patients managed to fast at least 20 days during Ramadan. Illustration of the recruitment and observation process can be seen in Fig. 1 below.

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of the subjects can be seen in Table 1 below. Most patients in our study were male (72.4%) and the largest age group was 51–60 year-old (38.5%) with median age of 54. The most common etiology of cirrhosis in our study was chronic hepatitis B (72.4%). A total of 26.9% patients had diabetes and 15.4% had chronic kidney disease. Previous oesophagogastroduodenoscopy data suggested 56.4% of the study participants already developed oesophageal varices and 30.8% had history of previous UGIB due to ruptured varices. Most patients in our study were in the Child-Pugh class A category (71.8%), followed by B (25.6%), and C (2.6%). Using the decompensation criteria of presence of active/prior ascites, variceal bleeding, or encephalopathy, 37.2% patients had already had decompensated cirrhosis. From the CLDQ results, most patients had a good baseline quality of life (93.6%) with median CLDQ score of 6.5.

Baseline characteristics of the study subjects.

Baseline characteristics of the 52 patients with CGM were similar to those of the overall subjects. Most patients were male (78.8%) and had hepatitis B as cirrhosis etiology (63.5%). The proportion CKD in this subgroup was 15.4% and 36.5% had prior history of UGIB. Oesophageal varices were detected in 59.6% patients, 67.3% patients were in the Child-Pugh A category, and 35.8% had decompensated cirrhosis. The only significantly different characteristics was history of diabetes which was reported in 71.2% patients with CGM. This possibly results from the increased awareness of hypoglycaemia among patients with history of diabetes, making them more likely to give consent for CGM procedure while non-diabetic patients might be more reluctant to this procedure and therefore more likely to not give their consent. It should be noted that the higher proportion of diabetes in the CGM group may also cause overestimation of the risk of hypoglycaemia compared to general cirrhosis population.

During the Month of Ramadan, 14 of the 156 patients failed to observe at least 20 days of fasting. The median duration of fasting in this population was 3.5 days (min-max 1–10 days), and most stopped their Ramadan Fasting due to fatigue (50%). Baseline characteristics of this population were similar to the overall population in terms of age, sex, and duration of cirrhosis, but showed higher proportions of history of previous UGIB (42.9%), history of oesophageal varices (71.4%), Child-Pugh score ≥ 9 (42.9%) and poor quality-of-life (21.4%).

Associations between Ramadan fasting and the study outcomes

During the Ramadan fasting period, 14 patients failed to observe at least 20 days of fasting due to various reasons. By means of intention-to-treat analysis, all these patients were considered to have had bad outcomes during the Ramadan Fasting. A total of 19 from 156 patients were hospitalized during Ramadan Fasting, significantly higher than the 5 of 156 hospitalized during the non-fasting period (12.2% vs. 3.2%; RR = 3.80; 95% CI = 1.45–9.92; p = 0.003). Significant association was also noted for UGIB variables, in which 17 of 156 patients had UGIB during Ramadan compared to only 2 of 156 during non-fasting period (10.9% vs. 1.3%; RR = 8.50; 95% CI = 1.99–36.17; p < 0.001). No significant associations were noted between Ramadan Fasting and Child-Pugh Category (14.1% vs. 9.0%, RR = 1.57; 95% CI = 0.83–2.96, p = 0.156), as well as with quality of life (9.0% vs. 6.4%; RR = 1.40; 95% CI = 0.64–3.06, p = 0.395).

Among the 19 hospitalized fasting patients, 6 were admitted due to UGIB alone, 3 due to UGIB and hepatic encephalopathy, 1 due to acute myocardial infarction, 1 due to cerebrovascular infarction, and the rest were counted as events under the intention-to-treat principle due to early discontinuation of fasting due to fatigue. As for UGIB, among the 17 UGIB events observed during Ramadan, 6 cases were endoscopically confirmed as variceal bleeding, 3 patients experienced UGIB events managed at outside facilities without available endoscopic data, while the rest were counted as events under the intention-to-treat principle due to early discontinuation of fasting. Both cases of UGIB in the non-fasting period were variceal bleedings.

Analysis on the hypoglycaemia outcome was carried out on the subgroup of 52 patients with CGM. None of these patients had any episode of hypoglycaemia during the non-fasting period, while 7 (13.5%) experienced hypoglycaemias during Ramadan Fasting. Due to the absolute 0 value of one cell in the 2 × 2 tables, calculations of RR and p value cannot be carried out using conventional method. Instead, p value was calculated using Fischer-Exact method and Haldane-Anscombe correction was done to calculate the RR and 95% CI. The result of this analysis showed significant association between Ramadan Fasting and hypoglycaemia (13.5% vs. 0%; RR = 15.00; 95% CI = 0.88–256.0; p = 0.013). Subgroup analysis on 56 patients with decompensated cirrhosis observing Ramadan Fasting yielded similar results with the overall population. Significant associations were found between Ramadan Fasting and hospitalization (25.0% vs. 8.9%; RR 2.8; 95% CI = 1.08–7.25; p = 0.042) and UGIB (23.2% vs. 3.6%; RR = 6.5; 95% CI = 1.54–27.49; p = 0.004). No significant association was found between Ramadan Fasting and hypoglycaemia (17.4% vs. 0%; RR = 9.375; 95% CI = 0.51–158.6; p = 0.109). This difference is probably due to the small sample size of subjects with decompensated cirrhosis in the CGM group (total 23 subjects). Similar to the analysis on the overall subjects, analysis on the hypoglycaemia variable was carried out using Fischer-Exact and Haldane-Anscombe correction. There were no significant associations between Ramadan Fasting and Child-Pugh Category (30.4% vs. 19.6%; RR = 1.54; 95% CI = 0.79–2.99; p = 0.190) or quality of life (17.9% vs. 12.5%; RR = 1.43; 95% CI = 0.58–3.49; p = 0.430) in the subgroup of decompensated cirrhosis.

Multivariate analysis on factors associated with adverse outcomes during Ramadan

Further analysis was conducted using multivariate analysis to evaluate associated factors for the 3 positive outcomes of morbidity during Ramadan Fasting. Bivariate analysis was first carried out between the incidence of hospitalization, hypoglycaemia, and UGIB during Ramadan Fasting and each corresponding suspected risk factors. For hospitalization, the suspected risk factors were sex, age, onset of cirrhosis, history of diabetes, history of chronic kidney disease (CKD), history of prior UGIB, documented oesophageal varices, Child-Pugh score ≥ 9, and the presence of decompensated cirrhosis. Meanwhile for hypoglycaemia, the included risk factors were sex, age, onset of cirrhosis, history of diabetes, history of chronic kidney disease (CKD), Child-Pugh score ≥ 9, and the presence of decompensated cirrhosis. Lastly for UGIB, risk factors such as sex, age, onset of cirrhosis, history of prior UGIB, documented oesophageal varices, Child-Pugh score ≥ 9, Liver Stiffness Measurement (LSM), and the presence of decompensated cirrhosis. For the LSM variable, a cut off value of 17.2 kPa was determined as the optimal cut-off for the bivariate analysis using ROC curve analysis to predict UGIB event (sensitivity 76.5% and specificity 51.9%, r = 0.725; 95% CI = 0.59–0.85; p = 0.003). Multivariate analysis using logistical regression were then carried out for each outcome by including every risk factor that had p value of < 0.25 from the bivariate analysis. For the hospitalization variable, these included history of prior UGIB, documented oesophageal varices, Child-Pugh score ≥ 9, and the presence of decompensated cirrhosis. For hypoglycaemia, only Child-Pugh score ≥ 9 and the presence of decompensated cirrhosis were eligible for multivariate analysis. For UGIB, the included risk factors were history of prior UGIB, documented oesophageal varices, Child-Pugh score ≥ 9, LSM ≥ 17.2, and the presence of decompensated cirrhosis. We analysed the performance of CP score in predicting hospitalization, hypoglycaemia, and UGIB events in fasting cirrhosis subjects using ROC curve. The performance of CP score was good in predicting hospitalization (AUROC = 0.85; 95% CI = 0.74–0.97; p < 0.01), hypoglycaemia (AUROC = 0.89; 95% CI = 0.79–0.99; p < 0.01) and UGIB (AUROC = 0.89; 95% CI = 0.79–0.99; p < 0.01). Discussion

During the 2 observation periods, a total of 156 cirrhosis patients decided to observe Ramadan Fasting. Most subjects were male with median age of 46 year old and the most common cause of cirrhosis was chronic hepatitis B, which is consistent with patterns reported in Asian populations1,17–20. The proportion of Child-Pugh class A in our study was higher from data of previous studies, which likely reflects the outpatient-based recruitment strategy and the exclusion of patients who chose not to fast (who tended to had higher Child-Pugh score)17,20.

Our study concluded that Ramadan Fasting was associated with increased risk of hospitalization, UGIB and hypoglycaemia among cirrhosis patients. There were scarce data on the rate of hospitalization of cirrhosis patients during Ramadan Fasting, but a study by Elfert et al. showed that out of the initial 300 cirrhosis patients that voluntarily fasted during Ramadan, 8 patients (2.7%) had variceal bleeding and 6 patients (2.0%) had clinically significant hepatic encephalopathy, all which presumably required hospitalization. It was also stated that 3 patients (1%) died due to cirrhosis related complications. Furthermore another 27 patients decided to stop their Ramadan Fasting due to fatigue. Using intention-to-treat principles, we can concur that the rate of hospitalization from this study was 44 of 300 patients (14.7%), which was quite similar to our finding. The mortality rate from that study (3 out of 300 patients, 1.0%) is similar to our data (2 out of 156 patients, 1.3%)13.

It was also found in our observation that the rate of hypoglycaemia increased significantly during Ramadan among cirrhosis patients. To our knowledge, this study was the first study that was specifically designed to assess the effect of Ramadan fasting on Hypoglycaemia in cirrhosis2. Liver is one of the main organs involved in regulating blood glucose level during fasting and cirrhosis means that this mechanism would be impaired. Experimental and clinical studies have shown reduced hepatic glucose production, diminished glycogen stores, and impaired gluconeogenesis in cirrhotic patients, as well as a higher risk of hypoglycaemia in those with concomitant diabetes. It should be noted that these studies did not evaluate the patients in Ramadan fasting setting and differed substantially from the methods used in our study9,10,20. These findings were cited to support the biological plausibility of hypoglycaemia in cirrhosis during prolonged fasting states rather than as direct comparators to Ramadan fasting. Another caveat is that the CGM subgroup in our study was not representative of the overall cohort, with a high prevalence of diabetes driven by clinical indication and consent patterns, which limits generalizability of CGM-related findings to broader cirrhosis population.

The effect of fasting on portal hypertension parameters in cirrhosis had already been assessed in several studies before, however no definitive conclusions could be made from these results. Owen et al. showed that longer duration of fasting is corelated with lower portal vein blood flow in cirrhosis patients10. Another study by Lee et al. showed that post-prandial hyperaemia associated increase in portal vein pressure occurred faster in cirrhosis patients.9 These two observations provided a plausible mechanism by which Ramadan fasting could influence the risk of upper gastrointestinal bleeding due to sudden increase in portal pressure in the Iftar meal. Previous Ramadan-focused studies have reported conflicting findings, with some demonstrating transient increases in portal hemodynamic parameters after breaking the fast and others showing no significant differences between fasting and non-fasting periods15,16. Our analysis demonstrated a significantly higher incidence of UGIB during Ramadan compared with the pre-Ramadan period. This result is similar to those reported by Mohamed et al. and Elnadry et al., who also observed increased rates of UGIB among fasting cirrhosis patients14,16. Interestingly, Elnadry et al. reported that the rate of variceal bleeding is lower in the Ramadan Fasting group compared to the non-fasting group (1.0% vs. 9.1%, p = 0.004), which means most of the UGIB occurred due to non-variceal cause such as peptic ulcer14.

The impact of Ramadan Fasting on liver function was also one of the more popular topics in previous studies. Deprivation of fluid and nutritional intake during Ramadan Fasting may cause dehydration and metabolic stress which might affect liver functions. Although some studies have reported statistically significant changes in biochemical parameters (most notably small increases in serum bilirubin) these changes were generally of limited clinical significance, with no consistent effects on serum albumin or coagulation parameters. Data on hepatic encephalopathy during Ramadan fasting remain sparse12–14,16. Despite these theoretical concerns, our study found no association between Ramadan fasting and worsening hepatic reserve as reflected by Child-Pugh score ≥ 9. This result is similar to the findings by Abubakar et al., but contradict those by Elnadry et al. and Mohamed et al.12,14,16. This discrepancy might be attributed to different analytical approach in which the two studies that mentioned association between Ramadan Fasting and Child-Pugh score were not analysed using intention-to treat principle.

Our study did not find any association between Ramadan Fasting and quality-of-life of cirrhosis patients. To our knowledge this is the first study specifically designed to assess quality of life in fasting cirrhosis patients. Although fasting was associated with increased risks of hospitalization, hypoglycaemia, and UGIB, these hard clinical outcomes do not necessarily translate into a measurable decline in overall quality-of-life over a short observation period. Quality-of-life is a multidimensional construct encompassing physical, emotional, and psychosocial domains. Previous studies have shown that Ramadan fasting may be associated with preserved or even improved quality of life among patients with chronic diseases21–23. CLDQ, the tool we used to assess quality-of-life in our study, was also designed with several domains in mind, and improvement in the emotional or worry domains due to Ramadan Fasting might offset the worsening quality-of-life in physical health domains caused by increased risk of morbidity, resulting in no net change in overall quality-of-life scores.

In multivariate analysis, a Child–Pugh score ≥ 9 emerged as the only independent risk factor for hospitalization, UGIB, and hypoglycaemia during Ramadan fasting in patients with cirrhosis. The Child-Pugh score integrates key components of hepatic function and portal hypertension, thereby reflecting the liver’s capacity to tolerate metabolic stress. Although Ramadan fasting was not associated with a significant deterioration in Child-Pugh category or quality-of-life over the observation period, baseline hepatic reserve emerged as the dominant determinant of adverse outcomes. This finding suggests that acute clinical events during Ramadan may occur in vulnerable patients without necessarily being preceded by measurable short-term worsening of liver function scores. Given the established prognostic role of the Child-Pugh score and prior recommendations proposing its use in assessing fasting safety among cirrhosis patients, our findings provide supportive evidence for risk stratification based on baseline disease severity2,3. Further research is still needed to assess specific criteria for safe Ramadan Fasting in cirrhosis cases.

This study is one of the few studies assessing the impact of Ramadan Fasting on cirrhosis. To our knowledge, this is the first study that was specifically designed to assess various clinical outcomes including hospitalization, hypoglycaemia, liver function, and quality-of-life using intention-to-treat analysis principle. The use of CGM to measure hypoglycaemia parameter was also a novelty. However, we acknowledge that this study has several limitations. First, the observational design included only patients who voluntarily chose to fast during Ramadan, and the absence of a non-fasting control group limits causal inference and raises the possibility that observed differences may partly reflect time-related or seasonal factors associated with the Ramadan period. This might include changes in dietary habits, healthcare utilization, or participant behaviour related to study awareness (Hawthorne effect). Second, our subjects were predominantly composed of patients with Child-Pugh class A disease which may limit generalizability to patients with more advanced cirrhosis. Hypoglycaemia assessment was also restricted to a relatively small CGM subgroup with a high prevalence of diabetes, driven by clinical indication and consent, which may overestimate hypoglycaemia risk and further limits generalizability. Third, the observation period was limited to one month before and during Ramadan, which may be insufficient to capture delayed hospitalizations or longer-term changes in liver function and quality-of-life. Lastly, complete etiological characterization of UGIB events was not possible for episodes managed outside our institution, limiting interpretation regarding portal hypertension-related bleeding.

Conclusion

Ramadan Fasting is associated with increased risk of hospitalization, hypoglycaemia, and UGIB in liver cirrhosis. No association was noted between Ramadan Fasting and Child-Pugh category and quality-of-life of cirrhosis patients.
