Diabetic health literacy and associated factors among patients with diabetes attending follow-up in public hospitals of Northeastern Ethiopia: a multicentre cross-sectional study
Authors: Elda Mekonnen Nigussie, Mekasha Getnet Demeke, Tigist Demssew Adane, Bethelehem Taye Mengistu, Abel Tibebu Goshu, Yihenew Ayehu Dessie, Birhan Getye Worku, Eyosiyas Yeshialem Asefa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo assess the magnitude of diabetic health literacy (DHL) and associated factors among diabetes patients attending follow-up at public hospitals in Northeastern Ethiopia.DesignAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from 24 May to 24 June 2022.SettingDiabetic clinics of four public hospitals in Northeastern Ethiopia.Participants611 adult diabetes patients who have been undergoing follow-up care for at least 6 months were systematically selected. Patients who were unable to communicate, were critically ill and with gestational diabetes mellitus were excluded from the study.OutcomesDHL was assessed using a 15-item tool developed from existing literature.ResultsOf all the participants, 300 (49.1%) had low, 174 (28.5%) moderate and 137 (22.4%) had high DHL levels. The significant factors impacting DHL included age (<40 years old: AOR=3.48, 95% CI=2.11–5.77; 40–60 years: AOR=2.79, 95% CI=1.75–4.46), low education (AOR=0.29, 95% CI=0.16–0.56 for those who can’t read and write; AOR=0.41, 95% CI=0.23–0.74 for those who can read and write; AOR=0.45, 95% CI=0.27–0.76 for primary education), exposure to diabetic education (AOR=1.84, 95% CI=1.31–2.58), having ≥2 information sources (AOR=2.28, 95% CI=1.57–3.32), moderate social support (AOR=1.94, 95% CI=1.32–2.87), strong social support (AOR=2.73, 95% CI=1.75–4.26) and urban residence (AOR=1.65, 95% CI=1.14–2.39).ConclusionThe study showed that less than a quarter of the patients have high DHL, with almost half having low levels of DHL. Tailoring health education programmes to diverse educational levels, incorporating multiple information sources and fostering social support networks could enhance DHL.Keywords: diabetes & endocrinology, health literacy, self care, social support

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a global health issue with rapidly increasing prevalence, projected to approach a billion cases in the next 20 years.1 In Africa, DM affects approximately 24 million adults, with Ethiopia being one of the top five countries bearing the highest burden, affecting about 2 million adults.1 2

Health literacy, particularly diabetic health literacy (DHL), is crucial for managing DM. DHL encompasses the ability of patients with DM to access, understand, evaluate and use health information effectively for self-management and treatment.3 4 This ability is particularly vital for patients with DM, who need to engage in continuous self-care to manage their condition.4

Low DHL is associated with poor glycaemic control, increased risk of complications and poor adherence to medication and lifestyle modifications.5 Studies show that inadequate DHL leads to adverse health outcomes and is linked to severe DM-related complications.6 7 Health literacy also significantly impacts the quality of life and health-promoting behaviours among patients with DM, highlighting the importance of DHL in effective DM management.8 9

The prevalence of limited DHL is significant globally, with recent reviews indicating a pooled prevalence of 34.3%.10 While studies from Asia report inadequate DHL ranging from 31.3% in Saudi Arabia to 47.1% in China,11 12 Ethiopian studies report prevalences of 41.8% in Bale and 17.3% in Gondar.13 14

Despite the recognised importance of DHL, there is a paucity of evidence in Ethiopia regarding factors associated with it. Only a single study has explored this association, which is insufficient for designing effective health intervention programmes and generalising the DHL level. This gap in research is particularly problematic given the high burden of DM in Ethiopia and the critical role of health literacy in managing chronic diseases.

This study aimed to assess the extent of DHL and its associated factors among patients with DM in public hospitals in the North Shewa zone, Amhara Region, Northeastern Ethiopia. By addressing this gap, the study seeks to provide insights that can inform targeted interventions to improve DHL and enhance DM management and outcomes in this region.

Methods

Study area, design and period

The study was conducted among public hospitals found in North Shewa zone, Amhara region, Ethiopia. The head office of the North Shewa zone is located in Debre Berhan, which is 130 km away from the capital city of Ethiopia, Addis Ababa, and 682 km from the capital city of the Amhara regional state, Bahir Dar. According to the 2022 Central Statistics Agency report, the zone has 23 districts and a total population of 2 429 108, with 1 225 742 males and 1 203 366 females.15 North Shewa zone has 10 public hospitals, 97 health centres, 391 health posts and 164 private clinics. Among all public hospitals, seven of them are primary hospitals, two are general hospitals and one is a comprehensive specialised hospital. An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted in four of the ten public hospitals, namely, Debre Berhan Comprehensive Specialised Hospital, Alem Ketema Enat General Hospital, Deneba Primary Hospital and Shewarobit Primary Hospital from 24 May to 24 June 2022.

Population and eligibility criteria

The source population for this study consisted of all patients with DM who visited the outpatient department in public hospitals located in the North Shewa zone. The study population was systematically selected from patients with DM attending outpatient departments in selected public hospitals in the North Shewa zone. The study included patients with DM aged 18 years or above who had been undergoing follow-up care for a duration of at least 6 months. Patients were excluded if they were unable to communicate effectively, including those with severe hearing or speech impairments or cognitive disorders, critically ill patients whose immediate health concerns could interfere with their participation and those with gestational DM.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

The sample size was determined using the single population proportion formula, assuming a 95% CL, a marginal error (d) of 0.05, a design effect of 1.5 and a 56.5% proportion of high health literacy level obtained from a similar study conducted in Gondar.14 In the North Shewa zone, there are a total of ten public hospitals, and four were selected, namely, Alem Ketema Enat General Hospital, Debre Berhan Comprehensive Specialised Hospital, Deneba Primary Hospital and Shewarobit Primary Hospital, using a simple random sampling technique. The sample was proportionally allocated based on the number of patients undergoing follow-up visits at each hospital during the data collection period. The participants were patients with DM arriving at each healthcare facility for their diabetic follow-ups. The appointment registration lists of the participants were used as sampling frames. A systematic random sampling technique was applied after calculating a constant k value, determined by dividing the total number of DM patients (1270) by the desired sample size (n=623). The first participant was selected through a pooling method, and subsequently, every kth patient (k=2) was included in the study. Every selected participant was contacted by a supervised data collector just before their follow-up visit of the day.

Study variables

The outcome of interest for this study was diabetic health literacy. The independent variables which were assumed to influence the dependent variable and were tested were sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, educational status, monthly income, occupation and residence), clinical factors (type of DM, duration of DM, type of treatment, family history of DM, complications, comorbidities, fasting blood glucose), information accessibility factors (sources of information, exposure to diabetic education) and others behavioural factors (medication adherence) were also incorporated in the questionnaire.

Measurement of variables

The data collection tool comprised parts that assessed sociodemographic characteristics, clinical factors, information accessibility factors and other behavioural factors. The study used a 15-item tool with a 5-point Likert scale, derived from existing literature, to evaluate the DHL level. Each item was scored out of 5 points, and the mean score of these was used after converting it into a percentage.13 14 16 The tool demonstrated high reliability, evidenced by a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.91. Experts assessed the tool’s content validity, marking all items as pertinent, with item-level content validity index scores ranging from 0.8 to 1. Patients with a DHL assessment score greater than or equal to 75% were considered to have high DHL; those who scored between 60% and 74% were considered to have moderate DHL, while the rest were considered to have low DHL.13 14 The Oslo Social Support Scale (OSSS-3) was used to measure the level of social support among patients. The total score was determined by summing up raw scores for each item. The combined raw scores ranged from 3 to 14. A score between 3 and 8 was categorised as poor social support, a score between 9 and 11 indicated moderate social support and a score of 12 or higher indicated strong social support.17 Three consecutive measurements of fasting blood glucose (FBG) were taken from patients’ charts, and the average number was used to determine glycaemic status. Patients with an average FBG of 80–130 mg/dL were considered to have controlled glycaemic targets.18 19 Patients who took all their anti-diabetic medications in the previous 7 days were assumed to be adherent.19

Data collection procedure and quality assurance

The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, translated into the local language Amharic and then back-translated into English to check for any inconsistencies in the meaning of words. The data were collected by four bachelor-level nurses using face-to-face interviews and chart reviews, supervised by two master’s level nurses and the principal investigators. Before the actual data collection, a pre-test was conducted at Molale Primary Hospital among 31 patients with DM (5% of the sample). Based on the results of the pretest, necessary edits were made to the items. Data collectors and supervisors underwent a 3 day training session from the principal investigators before the actual data collection. The data were thoroughly checked for completeness, accuracy, clarity and appropriateness throughout the entire data collection process.

Data processing and analysis

The data were checked for completeness, coded and entered into Epi Data software version 4.6. Data cleaning and analysis were performed using STATA software version 14.1. Descriptive statistics, including the proportion, percentage, frequency distribution, mean and SD, were used to describe the data. Ordinal logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the association between DHL and each independent variable. Variables that were significant at a p value<0.25 in bivariate analysis were candidates for multivariate analysis. Variables with a p value of <0.05, along with their respective AOR and 95% CI, were considered significantly associated with DHL. Model goodness of fit and assumption tests were checked. The test of parallel lines was examined, and the p value was found to be 0.111.

Patient and public involvement

The study participants were not involved in the development of the research question, outcome measures, design, recruitment or conduct of the study.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

Of the total sample size of 623, 611 study participants completed the interview, resulting in a response rate of 98.1%. Within this sample, 330 (54%) of the respondents were males. The mean age of the respondents was 44±15.5 years. About 25.4% of the respondents were farmers, and 25.7% had their own private work. The majority (61%) of the participants were married. More than three-quarters (79.9%) had a monthly income lower than 5000 Ethiopian Birr. Regarding educational level, 159 (26%) of the participants had attained secondary school education. Participants who lived in urban areas accounted for 345 (56.5%) of the total (table 1).

DHL

Of the total 611 participants, almost half (49.1%) had poor, about 174 (28.5%) had moderate and 137 (22.4%) had high DHL levels. About 257 (42.1%) disagreed that they could convey the reason why they should have a diabetic diet, and another 41.6% disagreed with the item mentioning whether they could judge if diabetic information is reliable. Only 27 participants (4.4%) strongly agreed with the item stating if they understand all the information related to diabetic medication. About 36% and 42% of the participants agreed and strongly agreed, respectively, that they could alter the appointment date or time for a medical check-up. About 24.5% strongly disagreed with the item stating if they ask healthcare providers a question, 20.8% disagreed and 20.8% were uncertain about it. The majority (72.5%) of the participants strongly disagreed if they could determine the carbohydrate content per serving. Most participants agreed or strongly disagreed with being able to calculate (determine) the next time to take their diabetic medication. A combined 44.2% strongly agreed or agreed that they could interpret whether their blood glucose level is within the normal range or not, and the rest either strongly disagreed, disagreed or were uncertain (table 3).

Factors associated with DHL

To identify predictors of DHL, all factors with a p value of <0.25 on bivariate analysis (age, educational status, type of DM, type of treatment, family history of DM, exposure to diabetic education, social support, number of information source and residence) were included in multivariate ordinal logistic regression model. Accordingly, age, educational status, social support, residence, exposure to diabetic education and number of sources were factors significantly associated with DHL.

Holding other variables constant, the odds of being in combined categories of moderate and high DHL was found to be about 3.5 times more likely for those aged 40 years or less (AOR=3.48; 95%CI (2.11, 5.77) and about 2.8 times more likely for those participants whose age was 40–60 years (AOR=2.79; 95%CI (1.75, 4.46) compared with those participants who were 60 years and above. The combined effect of moderate and high DHL is 71% less likely (AOR=0.29; 95%CI (0.16, 0.56) for those who can’t read and write, 59% less likely (AOR=0.41; 95%CI (0.23, 0.74) for those who can read and write and 55% less likely (AOR=0.45; 95%CI (0.27, 0.76) for participants who completed primary school when compared with those participants whose educational level was college and above.

The odds of having moderate or high DHL were 1.8 times more likely for those who had exposure to diabetic education (AOR=1.84; 95% CI (1.31, 2.58)) compared with those who weren’t exposed. The probability of having higher level of DHL was 2.3 times more likely for those who had two or more sources of information compared with participants who had only one source of information (AOR=2.28; 95% CI (1.57,3.32)).The odds of having moderate or high DHL were about two times more likely for those who had moderate social support (AOR=1.94; 95% CI (1.32,2.87)) and 2.7 times more likely for those who had strong social support (AOR=2.73; 95% CI (1.75,4.26)). Being an urban resident was associated with 1.6 times higher odds of having higher diabetic health literacy (AOR=1.65; 95% CI (1.14, 2.39)) compared with being a rural resident (table 4).

Discussion

DM management requires active patient involvement in self-management practices, which is influenced by the patient’s capacity to obtain, comprehend, evaluate and apply diabetic-related information, collectively known as DHL.20 The aim of this study was to assess the magnitude of DHL and its related factors among patients with DM at the outpatient department of public hospitals in North Shewa zone, Amhara, Ethiopia.

The study findings revealed that only 22.4% (95% CI 19.2, 25.9) of the participants had a high DHL level. This finding is higher than studies in Iran (18.2%),21 Mexico (17.6%)22 and Kigali, Rwanda (14.3%).23 The discrepancy might be attributed to differences in the tools used. Additionally, the source of variation can be traced to most other studies recruiting type 2 patients with DM and sociocultural variations. On the other hand, this finding was lower than a study conducted in Saudi Arabia (37.1%),24 possibly due to the advanced healthcare delivery system and socioeconomic status of the participants in the comparison study.

There are limited studies primarily assessing DHL in Ethiopia. In comparison to previous local studies conducted in Gondar11 and Bale,12 Ethiopia, the magnitude of patients who have high DHL was lower in this study.13 14 The possible explanation for this discrepancy might be related to the methodological differences across studies. In addition, the lower results in this study might be related to the high number of participants who did not attain formal education.

The relationship between low health literacy and adverse health outcomes is well-documented in the literature, where insufficient health literacy is associated with poorer disease management, increased hospitalisations and higher mortality rates among patients with DM. The findings indicate that patients in the northeastern Ethiopian region may face a higher risk of poor health outcomes due to inadequate health literacy.

In the present study, being younger than 60 years of age was significantly associated with having high or medium DHL level. This result is comparable to the findings of studies conducted in Iran,21 Rwanda23 and Singapore.25 Cognitive abilities and social capital tend to decline with ageing, which may hinder the ability to access knowledge regarding health-related concerns.26 27 In addition to the negative impacts on the disease prognosis, poor DHL can also be related to detrimental effects on the ageing process itself.28

Participants who have completed higher levels of education were found to have better odds of having higher DHL. This finding is comparable with those of other studies from different parts of the world.21 23 29 The result is also consistent with a study from Gondar, Ethiopia, in which participants with greater educational status had a higher mean score of DHL.14 Higher educational attainment likely enhances the ability to access, comprehend and use health information effectively. Educated individuals are more likely to use diverse information sources such as the internet, books and health magazines, and they may be more receptive to health education initiatives.30

Urban residency was positively associated with moderate or high DHL, similar to findings from Gondar.14 Patients with DM living in the urban settings of Ethiopia have better access to educational resources, healthcare facilities and diverse information sources. They may also benefit from greater exposure to health education campaigns and community health programmes.31

Each patient with DM receives both the mass and client centred DM-related education at healthcare facilities. However, the extent and depth of education varied across different settings, healthcare providers and regions. Patients who receive diabetic education would know more about their illness conditions, prevention strategies and confidence level needed to interact with healthcare providers. The results of the present study and a Malaysian5 study also demonstrated this positive relationship between DM education and DHL. This study and a finding from another local study13 also depicted that multiple sources of information regarding DM is associated with a higher DHL level.

Social support was another significant factor associated with higher DHL, corroborating findings from China29 and Bale, Ethiopia.13 Strong social support networks may facilitate the sharing of health information and provide emotional and practical assistance, contributing to better health literacy. Additionally, social support can enhance mental and physical well-being, motivating patients to engage in health-promoting behaviours and manage their condition effectively.32

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study is unique to the northeastern Ethiopian region and is one of the few studies conducted within the nation and the sub-Saharan African region. It used a relatively larger sample size and representative health facilities to assess the problem under study. However, the study may have limitations in showing a cause-and-effect relationship across the variables studied. In addition, some responses by the participants could be subject to recall bias.

Conclusion

The study showed that less than a quarter of the patients have high DHL, with almost half having low DHL levels. This study showed that being younger, having higher educational status, having more than one source of information, having strong social support, being urban dweller and exposure to diabetic education were factors associated with high DHL level. Addressing the factors influencing DHL is crucial for developing effective interventions aimed at improving health outcomes among patients with DM. Tailoring health educational programmes to diverse educational levels, incorporating multiple information sources and fostering social support networks could contribute to enhancing DHL and, consequently, promoting better self-management practices among individuals with DM in the studied region. Further research is also needed to accurately capture the epidemiology of the different types of DM in the region.
