Concise construction of β-branched aromatic D-amino acids via transaminase-catalyzed dynamic kinetic resolution
Authors: Zhenling Liu, Wei Zhai, Zhihua Zeng, Xinxin Meng, Yilin Shi, Jing Huang, Ping Su, Xiaohui Yan, Li-Cheng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The synthesis of non-canonical amino acids (ncAAs) has recently made tremendous progress in the field of biocatalysis, with enzymes such as transaminases, dehydrogenases, and ammonia lyases being used. Frameworks of β-branched aromatic D-amino acids are crucial for the efficacy of pharmaceuticals, influencing spatial conformation, binding modes with receptors and pharmacokinetic properties. However, there is a scarcity of studies on the efficient synthesis of β-branched D-amino acids, which has significantly hindered the advancement of structure-activity relationship (SAR) studies. Here, we present the asymmetric synthesis of β-branched aromatic D-amino acid analogues catalyzed by PLP-dependent transaminase BsDAAT (from Bacillus sp. strain YM-1), an efficient transamination system that relies on the dynamic kinetic resolution (DKR) process. Through docking analysis and concise enzyme engineering, we successfully obtain various aromatic D-amino acids with β-substitution in high yields (up to 99%), dr ratios (>20:1) and ee values (up to 99%). These β-branched aromatic D-amino acids are then successfully introduced into the modifications of the antineoplastic reagent Lanreotide.

Introduction

D-amino acids (DAAs), enantiomers of the naturally occurring L-amino acids (LAAs), have attracted significant interest in the fields of medicinal chemistry, material science, and biotechnology due to their distinctive biological functions and broad range of applications1–4. Although they are less prevalent in nature than LAAs, DAAs with biofunctions may not be easily replaced by their enantiomers. For instance, D-serine (D-Ser) and D-aspartic acid (D-Asp) are prevalent in tissues and bodily fluids of vertebrates. Notably, D-Asp is prevalent in the central nervous systems and endocrine peripheral organs of humans and rats5,6. DAAs also constitute various active natural products and drugs, such as antibiotics, antidiabetics and chemotherapeutic agents. Examples include Nateglinide (D-Phe), Cefadroxil (D-Hpg), Fluvalinate (D-Val), Octreotide (D-Trp, D-Phe) and Lanreotide (D-Napa, D-Trp) (Fig. 1A)7–10. Structure-activity relationship studies have indicated that LAAs with β-modifications exhibit superior pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties compared with natural LAAs11,12. The emergence of biocatalysis has led to substantial advancements in the asymmetric synthesis of β-substituted L-amino acids, such as lyases13–15, transaminases16–18, and artificial photoenzymatic strategies19,20. For example, a notable study by Renata et al. achieved the stereoselective synthesis of β-substituted L-amino acids via enzyme-catalyzed dynamic kinetic resolution (DKR). Their work detailed the DKR process of ketoacids and the selectivity of TtArAT16. In another innovative research by Merck, the corresponding bulky β-substituted L-amino acid isomers were synthesized through a dynamic equilibrium driven by the key side reaction of the amine donor17.

In recent years, increasing efforts have been devoted to the synthesis of D-amino acids and their derivatives in accordance with the principles of green chemistry and environmentally sustainable solutions21,22. Effective biocatalysts include transaminases and dehydrogenases, which have the potential to produce DAAs with a single enantiomer from achiral ketoacids (Fig. 1B)23–27. A recent example of the enzymatic synthesis of β-branched D-amino acids was presented by Seebeck et al., who achieved transformation via a cascading process involving transaminases (TAs) and methyltransferases (MTs) (Fig. 1C)28. Multiple aliphatic β-Me L-/D-amino acids were generated with high stereoselectivity under the catalysis of selected MTs and TAs. However, the enzymatic synthesis of aromatic DAAs with β-branches has not been widely demonstrated in published research.

Herein, we report the enzymatic synthesis of β-substituted aromatic D-amino acids via a DKR process mediated by transaminase BsDAAT. DKR is a powerful enantioselective methodology that converts both enantiomers of a racemic substrate into a single enantiomeric product. In our DKR process of the BsDAAT system, two ketoacid enantiomers could be interconverted rapidly and spontaneously, but only the fast-reacting (R)-enantiomer could be selected to undergo enzymatic transformation into the β-substituted D-amino acids. This innovative biocatalytic system yields a diverse array of DAAs with two adjacent chiral centers from the appropriate achiral ketoacids with high efficiencies (up to 99% yield) and stereoselectivities (up to >20:1 dr, >99% ee) (Fig. 1D).

Results and discussion

Inspired by the capability of enzyme-catalyzed DKR to establish two consecutive chiral centers, we sought to exploit this strategy to construct aromatic β-substituted D-amino acids. Following recent investigations on the enzymatic synthesis of DAAs, D-phenylalanine aminotransferase (DAAT) from Bacillus sp. YM-1, which exhibits excellent natural activity and stereoselectivity in the production of D-Phe, was selected for our study27,29. Initially, we evaluated the activity of BsDAAT-catalyzed reaction on racemic 2-oxo-3-phenylbutanoic acid (1 s) under conditions: PLP (10 mol%), D-Glu (4 equiv.), and 0.6 mol% of the purified BsDAAT-WT enzyme in a KPi buffer solution (100 mM, pH 8.0) containing 1% (v/v) DMSO, at 37 °C for 18 hours. Although the desired product with high stereoselectivity could be identified by TLC and LCMS analysis, only a small amount of product was obtained from a 0.06 mmol-scale reaction (Fig. 2A, Entry 1 and Supplementary Table 2). To evaluate simpler reaction protocols, we explored transamination reactions catalyzed by whole cells or lysates expressing BsDAAT. However, non-stereospecific product 1 was observed, albeit with increased yields. We hypothesized that the non-native substrate 1 s was also bio-transformed by the endogenous enzymes in E. coli, such as transaminase and dehydrogenase27,30–32. This hypothesis was confirmed by the detection of product 1 catalyzed by cultivated E. coli BL21 (DE3) cells without any exogenous enzymes (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Table 4). Following the positive reaction results driven by BsDAAT, we optimized the reaction conditions for substrate 1 s by screening the alterable elements (Supplementary Table 3). Screening alternative pH levels, temperatures, and enzyme or amino donor loadings did not lead to an obvious improvement in reaction efficiency, but the type of amino donor was verified to be the key factor in this reaction. Under the guidance of previous studies, several D-amino donors with different affinities to wild-type BsDAAT were tested, including D-Ala, D-Gln, D-Glu, D-Asp, and D-Asn29,33. However, it was observed that D-Glu and D-Ala, with higher specific activity in transamination toward pyruvate and other amino receptors, only provided a 14% yield. In contrast, D-Lys with weak enzyme affinity showed a significant improvement in compound 1 production (Fig. 2A). This finding was supported by the study on transamination equilibrium, in which the deamination product of lysine was found to convert into the cyclized by-product 6-amino-2-oxohexanoic acid to drive the reaction forward17. Based on this, a lower equivalent of D-Lys, a suitable temperature, and a lower loading of the purified enzyme were found to be more conducive to the production of target compounds. The final conditions after rational optimization were as follows: substrate (1 equiv.), D-Lys (3 equiv.), PLP (10 mol%), purified enzyme (0.6 mol%) in KPi buffer (100 mM, pH 8.0) containing 1% (v/v) DMSO, at 37 °C for 18 hours. Under these conditions, compound 1 could be obtained with excellent yield (>99%) and stereoselectivity (>20:1 dr), as shown in Fig. 2A (entry 5).

Following the successful dynamic kinetic amination of the model substrate, we conducted comprehensive investigations into the capabilities of BsDAAT-WT. β-Branched α-ketoacids with various substituents were subjected to optimized enzymatic processes. However, most aryl substrates could not be ideally transformed compared with 1 s (Fig. 3). Target compounds were effectively synthesized with high stereoselectivity (>20:1 dr) and varied yields for o-substituted substrates with different halogens (2s–5 s), except for 6 s. When a -CF3 group was incorporated at the m- (12 s) or p- (20 s) site, substrates were effectively utilized by BsDAAT to yield compounds 12 and 20 with 14:1 and 9:1 dr, respectively. Although substrates with m- (7s–9 s) and p- (16s–19 s) halogen substitutions could be recognized, a discernible decrease in yields and dr ratios was evident. Exceptional production efficiency was also demonstrated with substrates 13 s and 15 s, which have potent electron-withdrawing substituents; however, the capacity for chiral control still needs to be improved. Additionally, substrates modified by alkyl groups such as methyl (10 s, 14 s), isopropyl (22 s), tert-butyl (23 s), and acetyl (24 s) were effectively converted into unnatural amino acids with yields exceeding 60%. The yields declined when the methyl group was replaced by a methoxy group (11 s and 21 s). In addition to single-substituted ketoacids, substrates with other frameworks were tested using BsDAAT-catalyzed system. These included difluorides 25 s and 26 s, ketobutyric acid 27 s, bulky naphthyl-type substrate 28 s and heterocyclic compound 29 s. However, the results did not meet the expected level. Broad screening revealed that only 30% of products exhibited optimal stereoselectivity (>20:1 dr), while most substrates delivered dr ratios ranging from 5:1 to 20:1. At this stage, we decided to conduct an enzyme engineering program to achieve universally satisfactory transformation of the desired β-Me-D-Phe analogues. Based on docking analysis of the BsDAAT-WT crystal structure and substrate 1 s, 16 amino acids within 5 Å of the binding model were selected for site mutation (Fig. 4A). Focused rational iterative site-specific mutagenesis (FRISM) was applied to evolutionary studies at sites lining the enzyme’s binding pocket34. Three amino acids with different steric properties were chosen to replace the amino acids individually at the selected residue sites. Alanine (A), leucine (L), and phenylalanine (F) were chosen as small, medium, and large amino acids, respectively, and were used to modify the space available in BsDAAT binding pockets. The obtained mutants were then tested in reactions with three substrates: 6 s (a special non-working substrate), 17 s (representing most substrates), and 28 s (a substrate type with greater steric hindrance). This identified the ‘hotspots’ for manipulating stereoselectivity and reactivity. Regarding substrates 17 s and 28 s, both V33L and V33F exhibited greater stereoselectivity. Notably, V33F displayed a significantly higher activity (>20:1 dr for both 17 s and 28 s) than V33L (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 5). Variant V33F was then tested on all the remaining substrates. The stereoselectivity of most products was greatly improved, achieving a ratio of over 20:1 dr, with over ten compounds reaching an isolated yield of over 99%, which distinguishes V33F from other mutants. However, V33F was incompatible with o-substituted substrates (3s–6 s). Among the mutants that were positive towards substrate 6s (V33A, T242A/F, S240F/L, and V33G with an isolated yield of 18%), V33A exhibited superior reaction efficiency and remarkable stereoselectivity (>20:1 dr). Similar results were obtained when V33A was evaluated on substrates 3s–5 s, where sustained stereoselectivity was associated with significantly higher yields (Fig. 3, compounds 3–5). V33F and V33A were identified as prospective mutants for substrate scope investigations. Overall, all primary substrates with low dr ratios were favorably accepted by V33F or V33A, exhibiting increased stereoselectivities (>20:1 dr, >99% ee) and isolated yields (Fig. 3, compounds 1–29; Supplementary Fig. 9). The absolute configuration of (2 R,3 R)-8 was verified by X-ray crystallography, and those of the other products were assigned by analogy.

Owing to the successful acceptance of various substituted β-Me ketoacids, larger groups in the β-position were investigated within the BsDAAT-WT system. However, no products were detected in reactions involving β-Et (30 s), β-nPr (31 s), β-cPr, or β-iPr. Then we attempted a new round of mutations involving S240, T241, and T242 using a minimization strategy (Supplementary Table 6). These three residues were positioned within the β-chain orientation of the binding pocket in BsDAAT (Fig. 5E). The results indicated that substrates with ethyl or n-propyl were not well tolerated by any single-site or double-site mutant containing glycine. However, they could be successfully converted into the desired product by the triple mutant variant S240G/T241G/T242G, albeit with yields of only 4% and 6%, respectively. Excellent stereoselectivity was only demonstrated with compound 31, with a dr ratio of >20:1, whereas compound 30 displayed suboptimal stereocontrol with a dr ratio of 6:1. Only trace amounts of the product from β-cPr ketoacid could be detected by LCMS. The anticipated product from the β-iPr substrate was undetectable in any of the variation reactions, as confirmed by TLC and LCMS. For substrates with larger groups at the β-site, the S240G/T241G/T242G variant, featuring minimized amino acids, demonstrated improved receptivity to substrates 30 s and 31 s. However, it remained suboptimal for larger side chains, such as cPr and iPr. Following our exploration of the scope of aromatic substrates, we sought to investigate the efficacy of BsDAAT on aliphatic substrates. Replacing the phenyl group with a cyclohexyl group dramatically decreased activity, resulting in no derivatization of the α-ketoacid (Fig. 3). However, the extended backbone cyclohexylbutanone acid (32 s) could be catalyzed with a low yield of 28% and an original dr ratio of >20:1. When the carbon cycle transitioned from a cyclohexyl group to a cyclopentyl group, only minimal product could be detected by LCMS. Moderate reactivity was noted for other substrates featuring distinct straight or branched chains with reduced steric hindrance (33s–36 s), while no chiral selectivity was observed for β-branched groups (1:1–2:1 dr ratios for compounds 33, 34, 35, and 36). Further innovative evolutionary techniques are required for the stereoselective production of D-amino acids from aliphatic α-ketoacids via the BsDAAT system35. Research on the potential of BsDAAT for producing aliphatic DAAs as single isomers is currently in progress.

Based on the scope results, it could be observed that in the biocatalytic system of wild-type BsDAAT, most aromatic substrates bearing electron-withdrawing substituents were converted into the corresponding products with high yields, such as -F (2 s, 7 s, 16 s), -Cl (3 s, 17 s), and -NO2 (13 s, 15 s). Hydrophobic alkyl chain-substituted substrates also exhibited favorable reactivity, including -Me (10 s, 14 s), -iPr (22 s), and -tBu (23 s). Substrates 11 s and 21 s showed reduced yields due to the electron-donating effect from -OMe group. However, substrate 6 s with o-CF3 showed significantly reduced reactivity compared to 12 s and 20 s. This diminished activity likely stemmed from the combined influence of electronic and steric factors, along with suboptimal enzyme-substrate binding. The -CF3 group in substrate 6 s was positioned closer to the reaction center, exerting a more potent electron-withdrawing effect. Reduced electron density around the reactive site likely weakens the substrate reactivity and interactions between carboxyl groups and nearby residues, leading to an undesired result. Moreover, -CF3 group at the o-position might compress the key regions required for catalytic or positioning residues. The tetrahedral geometry of group -CF3 could generate strong steric repulsion with surrounding residues, preventing the substrate from achieving the optimal alignment necessary for catalysis, resulting in poor reactivity. But substrates with -Br and -I exhibited limited electronic effects. Their approximately spherical atomic shapes, symmetry, and high polarizability enabled a favorable fit within active site. The resulting low steric barrier overcame any minor electronic perturbations, allowing reactions to proceed successfully.

Compared with the wild enzyme, mutants V33F and V33A exhibited enhanced reactivity toward most substrates. This improvement was attributed to both higher enzyme-substrate affinity and increased transamination rates (comparison of kinetic parameters was shown in Supplementary Fig. 8). To gain a better understanding of these optimized results, we conducted molecular docking using BsDAAT and substrates 6 s and 28 s, matching imines PLP-6s and PLP-28s with BsDAAT-WT and BsDAAT-V33F or V33A, respectively (Fig. 5). As shown in Fig. 5A and B, V33F exhibited enhanced π-π interaction with the benzene ring within substrate 28 s, making it superior for this biocatalysis. Increased π-π interactions may further constrain substrate conformation, confining the methyl group to a small region and yielding desirable products with higher stereoselectivity. In contrast, molecular docking between BsDAAT and substrate 6 s revealed that BsDAAT with the small-site mutation V33A exhibited greater accommodation for the o-substitution CF₃ (Fig. 5C and D). When valine was mutated to alanine, the difference in electronic properties of alkyl side chains was so negligible that it could not influence the electrostatic potential of the active site. Therefore, electronic effects were not the main driving force behind this mutation. With a smaller side chain, alanine provides additional space and greater conformational flexibility, enabling subtle “induced-fit” adjustments of the active site. This enhanced the ability of the pocket to accommodate o-substituted substrates with substantial steric bulk. However, minimizing residues does not always enhance enzyme activity, and optimal catalytic efficiency requires a precise balance. Similar results were not observed when the residue was minimized to glycine (V33G). Although V33G provided maximal space, the excessive flexibility introduced by glycine disrupted the precise preorganization and stability of the active site, leading to a reduction in enzyme’s intrinsic catalytic capability. For other single-point mutants, they either failed to effectively optimize the binding conformation and stereo-control between substrates and enzyme, or disrupted key interactions between residues and substrates, impairing substrate recognition and catalytic efficiency. The phenol hydroxyl group of Y31, the guanidinium group of R98, and the amino group of K145 side chain played critical roles in recognizing and binding carboxyl groups of the substrates. Substitution of these residues disrupted enzyme-substrate recognition and binding, resulting in interrupted reactions. The hydrophobic interactions network among residues S240, T241, T242, and S243 provided sufficient space to accommodate β-substituted substrates but lacked the ability to control methyl conformation, resulting in the poor performance of corresponding mutants. Residues A99, H100, and others likely served only as structural scaffolds but were not directly involved in transamination reactions, explaining the lack of significant change upon mutations.

Beyond these, we explored the relationships between the active pocket and aromatic substrates with larger β-groups and aliphatic substrates. The binding pocket of BsDAAT was deemed too narrow to accommodate the extension of β-chain (aromatic ketoacids), resulting in undesirable reaction outcomes (Fig. 5E and F). Although the triple mutant S240G/T241G/T242G offered more space for β-chains, it was still not fully adaptable. For aliphatic ketoacids, insufficient steric hindrance disrupts the critical capacity for chiral control over most aliphatic substrates in our study. For 32 s, limited space restricts the free movement of the side chain of 32 s, stabilizing the spatial conformation of the methyl group and resulting in the formation of a single isomer. However, as shown in Fig. 5H, the spacious active pocket provides sufficient room for free rotation of the side chain, ultimately leading to a dr 1:1 mixture of compound 33 isomers. These findings suggest that substrates featuring π-π interaction groups or steric hindrance may be more advantageous for the current transamination system to achieve the expected yields and configurations.

In order to show the usage of the β-branched DAAs, substrates 1 s and 28 s were synthesized using the BsDAAT system on a 1-mmol scale, yielding 84% and 48%, respectively. Further scale-up reaction of 1 s at 1 gram resulted in 55% yield (Supplementary Fig. 11). Both products exhibited exceptional stereoselectivity (>20:1 dr and >99% ee). The products were then applied in the solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) of Lanreotide derivatives, with D-naphthylalanine (D-Napa) being replaced by β-Me-D-Phe and β-Me-D-Napa (Fig. 6). Lanreotide, a somatostatin analogue with antineoplastic properties, possesses a stable octa-membered cyclic structure (D-Napa-L-Cys-L-Tyr-D-Trp-L-Lys-L-Val-L-Cys-L-Thr, disulfide bridge: Cys2-Cys7), containing two essential unnatural amino acids, D-Trp and D-Napa36,37. With extensive research into structural modifications and variations in drug indications, Lanreotide analogues were synthesized on Rink Amide AM resin using a HATU/DIEA condensation system. The final cyclic products were obtained via cyclization, deprotection, and resin cleavage, in accordance with standard procedures38. In summary, we have developed an unprecedentedly efficient biocatalytic approach to the asymmetric synthesis of β-branched aromatic D-amino acids via the DKR process. Variants V33F and V33A from the BsDAAT were identified and characterized in the synthesis of β-Me-D-Phe analogues bearing various substituents, leading to good yields and exceptional stereoselectivities (>20:1 dr, >99% ee for 30 examples). This reaction system could be easily scaled up and incorporated to produce Lanreotide derivatives. This efficient, environmentally friendly approach greatly broadens the range of biocatalytic reactions for aromatic D-amino acids with β-chirality. Future research may focus on the directed evolution of BsDAAT to generate β-methyl aliphatic D-amino acids with higher yields and stereoselectivities via enzymatic DKR. Furthermore, large-scale production using more efficient protocols, such as a multi-enzyme cascade in a whole-cell system, will be explored to produce β-branched aromatic D-amino acid analogues for industrial manufacturing and the discovery of innovative medications.

Methods

Construction of mutagenesis libraries

The mutant library was constructed using the FRISM strategy for the amino acid residues within the 5 Å range near the BsDAAT active site.

PCR was performed using BsDAAT-WT plasmid (pET-22b) as the template DNA. The reaction (50 μL final volume) contained ddH2O (33 μL), KOD 10X buffer (5 μL), dNTP (5 μL, 2 mM each), forward primers (1.5 μL, 10 μM each), silent reverse primer (1.5 μL, 10 μM each), template plasmid (1.0 μL, 100 ng/μL each), MgSO4 (2.0 μL, 25 μM each), and 1.0 μL of KOD plus. PCR conditions used were 94 °C, 2 min; 30 cycles of (94 °C, 15 sec; 61 °C, 30 sec; 68 °C, 10 min) and final extension at 68 °C, 10 min. To ensure the elimination of the template plasmid, 50 μL of PCR mixture were mixed with 2 μL DpnI (10 U/μL) and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Upon purification of the DpnI-digested product with a GeneJET PCR purification kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), an aliquot of 15 μL was used to transform the E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells by the chemical transformation method. The transformation mixture was incubated with 900 μL of LB medium at 37 °C with shaking and spread on LB-agar plates containing 100 µg/mL carbenicillin. A single colony from a plate was incubated in 5 mL of LB medium at 37 °C overnight, and the plasmid was extracted with an EasyPure® Plasmid MiniPrep Kit (TransGen Biotech Co., Beijing, China). DNA sequencing was performed by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).

Enzymatic synthesis of β-branched aromatic D-amino acids

The suitable α-ketoacids (0.07 mmol) and PLP (10%, 0.007 mmol) were added to potassium phosphate buffer (5 mL, 100 mM, pH 8.0) containing D-Lys (3 equiv., 0.21 mmol). Purified BsDAAT-WT or mutant enzyme (0.6% mmol) was added as a final, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C overnight in a shaking incubator at 280 rpm. Later, the samples were adjusted to pH = 2.0 with 1 M HCl, then the mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 × g and 4 °C for 30 min. Supernatant was filtered by a 0.22 μm PES filter and purified by a reverse C18 silica column using acetonitrile/water: 100% water for 15–20 min, 0–16% acetonitrile over 5 min, 16–70% acetonitrile over 40 min, 65–95% acetonitrile over 5 min under a flow rate of 5mL/min. Structures and diastereomeric ratios (dr) of purified compounds were confirmed by 1H NMR and 13C NMR.
