A hypotensive protocol of inspiratory muscle strength training: Systematic review and meta‐analysis with trial sequential analysis
Authors: Wenjing Li, Xiaoping Zhu, Xinai Wang, Hao Liu, Jingying Liu, Hongling Xiao, Liang Dong, Chenqi Wang, Yaxuan Wu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe aim of this study was to evaluate the hypotensive effect and optimal protocol of inspiratory muscle resistance training (IMST). Randomized controlled trials using IMST to lower blood pressure (BP) were retrieved from 12 databases as of July 2022. A meta‐analysis of BP and heart rate variability (HRV) was performed and a trial sequence analysis was performed using trial sequential analysis (TSA) software. Twelve articles (n = 386 participants) from five countries were included, with a mean quality score of 5.83. IMST achieved significant results in reducing systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressure (−7.93 [−12.08, −3.78]; −3.80 [−6.08, −1.53]; −4.90 [−13.76, 3.96]). Furthermore, TSA has shown that the findings for systolic and diastolic BP are conclusive. Finally, considerable variation remained between studies when analyzing HRV. The overall hypotensive effect of IMST was demonstrated by the TSA and was well tolerated in different populations. Of these, two interventions, high resistance or low resistance combined with slow breathing, showed the best efficacy under an 8‐week exercise intervention. In addition, the process of lowering BP by modulating sympathetic vagal activity has not been further confirmed in this study. Future long‐term interventions, especially those over 3 months, are needed to observe the prolonged antihypertensive effects and modulatory mechanisms; controlling for variables such as respiratory rate and executing more rigorous studies to further explore antihypertensive options.Keywords: exercise protocol, hypertension, inspiratory muscle strength training, mechanism, meta‐analysis

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension is one of the common chronic diseases that endanger human health. 1 Its prevalence is gradually increasing, and the number of people with hypertension is expected to reach 1.5 billion by 2025. 2 Although the number of people affected increases yearly, the control rate is only 15.3% with medication. 3

The benefits of exercise therapy in controlling blood pressure (BP) are gradually being discovered and have demonstrated similar antihypertensive efficacy to first‐line drugs in some studies. 4 As research into the relationship between BP and breathing has advanced, inspiratory muscle strength training (IMST), an exercise program that uses inspiratory resistance to lower BP, has been explored. Recently, it has been used for BP control in different populations. 5 , 6 , 7

In addition, IMST as an exercise program can reduce medical costs; it causes gentler BP fluctuations after exercise than, for example, aerobic exercises; and, being less demanding on trunk muscle capacity, it is more suitable for people who are bedridden and have limited physical activity. 8 The safety of the exercise has also been proven. 9 , 10

As research has progressed, the strategies for implementing IMST have gradually diversified. Currently, they are classified mainly by maximum inspiratory pressure (MIP) into low resistance protocols (30 min/day, 30%−50% MIP) and high resistance protocols (5 min/day, 50%−75% MIP). 11 Some regimens are combined with respiratory rate to promote the hypotensive effect of IMST by combining resistance inspiration with deep slow breathing (6, 8, 12 breaths/min) from the point of view of influencing respiratory rate.

This paper will conduct a broad search for the antihypertensive effect of IMST, without limiting the population included, to obtain more data to analyze the antihypertensive effect of IMST and the best options.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

The protocol for this systematic review has been registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), registration number CRD42022345353.

Data sources and search strategy

Search the following electronic databases for original articles published in various languages before July 9, 2022: PubMed, The Cochrane Library, SCOPUS, PEDro, Web of Science, Clinical Trials, EmBase, ChiCTR, CNKI, Wanfang Data, VIP, CBM. The search strategy can be found in Table S1, including mesh words such as “respiratory training, blood pressure” and keywords such as “ inspiratory muscle training,” which are combined by Boolean logic. Moreover, the list of cited literature for the articles reviewed for omissions.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This paper screened the literature based on the following criteria: (a) Inclusion criteria: the test group underwent loaded inspiratory muscle strength training; the primary outcome measure was SBP. (b) Exclusion criteria: the presence of non‐pharmacological interventions other than IMST; incomplete or unavailable extraction of primary outcome indicators; single‐arm trials, animal trials; duplicate articles, systematic reviews, reviews, and conference papers; articles of poor quality after PEDro evaluation. This article was more expansive than the population included, and we hoped that additional articles could be obtained to comprehensively analyze the use of IMST interventions for BP.

Two researchers (Wenjing Li, Xinai Wang) assessed independently. If disagreements arose, a third researcher (Hao Liu) was consulted.

Data extraction

Two researchers (Wenjing Li, Xinai Wang) independently completed data extraction using data tables. Disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third researcher (Hao Liu). The extracted information mainly included author information (author, publication time), demographic and clinical information of the research population (sample size, age, sex, disease types, and treatment measures), IMST research design (training frequency, duration, time, and patient compliance), primary and secondary outcome indicators (SBP, DBP, mean arterial pressure [MAP], heart rate variability [HRV]).

Quality assessment

Two investigators (Wenjing Li, Xinai Wang) used the Physical Therapy Evidence Database (PEDro) scale 12 to assess the methodological quality of the evaluated studies. The scale scored with 9 points above being “excellent,” 6−8 points being “good,” 4−5 points being considered “fair,” and <4 points being considered “poor. 12 ” This paper included the literature with four grades or above for analysis.

Estimates were made using funnel plots and Egger tests to determine whether there was potential publication bias and minor sample effects in the articles.

Data analysis

Continuous outcomes were analyzed using RevMan 5.4 software. The pre‐ and post‐treatment differences were expressed as mean ± standard deviation [SD]. When the heterogeneity test results in p > .1 or I2≤50%, we employed a fixed‐effects model for meta‐analysis. Otherwise, a random effects model was used. Subgroup analyses were also conducted with different population and intervention program divisions.

Publication bias was examined using STATA 17. A trial sequential analysis (TSA) was performed using TSA 0.9 software to assess random errors and sample size, 13 , 14 including all trials with SBP and DBP outcomes, with type I risk set at 5% and power set at 95%.

RESULT

A total of 4594 articles were searched, leaving 3738 articles after removing duplicates. Fifty‐four articles were included after reading the titles and abstracts, and 13 papers were retained after the final screening of the full text. One of the research was finally rejected because of its low quality. 15 Twelve articles met the criteria and were included in this paper, including 11 English and one Chinese 16 (Figure 1).

Database search and selection process using PRISMA.

Characteristics of participants and groups

The 13 studies included five trials (Table 1) focusing on healthy adults, 8 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 five trials focusing on patients with hypertension (simple systolic hypertension, 21 , 22 , 23 Grade I and II hypertension 24 and essential hypertension 16 ) and two tests on patients with OSA 25 , 26 from five countries including the USA, China, Brazil, Thailand, and Turkey. A total of 386 participants were enrolled in the study.

Quality assessment

The quality of the included literature was evaluated using the PEDro physical test scale (Table 1). After independent evaluation by two individuals, seven articles were rated as “good” quality and five as “fair” quality, with an average score of 5.83 (Table S2). One article was rated as two and excluded due to poor quality.

There were more than ten studies that included SBP and DBP outcome indicators. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots, and the results showed more symmetry (Figure S1). Further quantitative analysis using Egger's test showed better results without significant publication bias (Table S3).

Characteristics of the interventions

Of the 12 articles included in this paper (Table 2), nine studies 8 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 25 , 26 used threshold devices and three studies 21 , 23 , 24 used flow resistance loads with MIP ranging from 15% to 75% of MIP and water column fluctuations in the range of 18−20 mm H2O. There are two studies 18 , 20 in which several groups of different resistances were compared. In addition, there were two studies 21 , 24 in which respiratory rate was used as an essential variable by grouping it separately for control.

Regarding training duration and frequency, three studies 18 , 19 , 25 performed a single session of breathing training to observe immediate BP changes after training; four studies 8 , 17 , 20 , 26 conducted 6 weeks of training, and six studies 16 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 conducted an 8‐week controlled intervention. Furthermore, to observe the continuing effect of the exercise, 16 , 21 BP fluctuations were followed up for 6–8 weeks post‐intervention.

Meta‐analysis of included studies

Systolic blood pressure

A meta‐analysis of resting SBP (Figure 2A demonstrated that IMST had a significant reduction in SBP compared to no training, sham training or low resistance training in people of all health conditions (n = 12, MD = −7.93, 95% CrI[−12.08, −3.78]).

Meta‐analysis of systolic blood pressure after IMST intervention total effect (A) and subgroup analysis by duration (B).

In a subgroup analysis of the duration of the intervention (Figure 2B), trials with an 8‐week intervention achieved a better reduction in SBP (n = 5, MD = −11.46, 95% CrI[−16.19, −6.74]). Trials with only a single session did not lower SBP but raised it (n = 3, MD = 6.34, 95% CrI[1.06, 11.63]).

In addition, articles with different resistance were analyzed by subgroups after exclusion of a single treatment (Figure 3).Using 50% MIP as the cut‐off for high and low load training, 20 both high and low resistances produced significant efficacy in different populations (n = 4, MD = −11.79, 95% CrI[−17.16, −6.42]; n = 4, MD = −12.45, 95% CrI[−14.65, −10.24]).

Antihypertensive effect of IMST under inspiratory resistance subgroup analysis.

A subgroup analysis of the population was performed after removing single‐treatment articles (Figure 4). IMST had a more significant effect on lowering BP in healthy adults, hypertensive, and OSA patients (n = 2, MD = −10.75, 95% CrI[−16.16, −5.34]; n = 6, MD = −9.95, 95% CrI[−14.69, −5.21]; n = 1, MD = −13.50, 95% CrI[−16.70, −10.30]).

Antihypertensive effect of IMST under population subgroup analysis.

Diastolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure

The results demonstrate the effect of the IMST intervention on resting DBP (Figure S2) and MAP (Figure S3). Overall, IMST reduced resting DBP in participants (n = 12, MD = −3.80, 95% CrI[−6.08, −1.53]), with 8 weeks of training being more effective. For MAP, 6−8 weeks of training produced a similar effect (n = 3, MD = −8.64, 95% CrI[−13.34, −3.93]), with a single training session having the opposite effect.

Heart rate variability

HRV was analyzed across the three articles (Figure S4), and the index of HRV varied considerably in the conditions where a single training session was performed. Low resistance training altered parasympathetic regulation in older healthy women, but the difference was insignificant in healthy young adults or patients with OSA.

Heterogeneity

There was significant heterogeneity between studies in this paper due to the different inclusion populations and intervention protocols, but the results were not significant by subgroup analysis.

Trial sequential analysis

Analysis of SBP showed (Figure 5A) that the sample size included in study 1 had exceeded the conventional alpha boundaries (Z = 1.96) and TSA cut‐off and reached the required sample size after study 3 (RIS = 42); the curve representing DBP (Figure 5B) crossed both the traditional and TSA cut‐offs and reached the cumulative information set (RIS = 143). Suggesting that the observed effect of IMST in reducing SBP and DBP in the current information set is conclusive.

DISCUSSION

IMST has been shown to have a beneficial effect in reducing SBP, DBP, and MAP. This beneficial effect has been demonstrated in healthy individuals, hypertensive patients, and patients with OSA, and TSA has shown some certainty in the overall antihypertensive effect of IMST. In some studies, it has also effectively reduced 24 h BP, nocturnal SBP, 8 and pulse pressure, 21 , 23 , 24 which are essential in preventing myocardial infarction and cardiovascular risk. 27

Current research suggests that IMST affects BP through increased negative intrathoracic pressures rather than significant changes in lung volume, 8 , 20 which in turn affects peripheral hemodynamics in other non‐exercise areas to improve vascular health. Loaded inspiratory exercise directly stimulates cardiopulmonary receptors 17 and indirectly stimulates arterial receptors 28 through aortic dilation, while respiratory exercise 29 affects sympathetic vagal activity through centrally driven radiation. 21 Both synergistically lead to changes in cardiac output and peripheral vascular resistance that regulate BP.

IMST was first used to improve athletic 30 , 31 and address lung‐related diseases. Most exercise protocols designed to improve respiratory muscle strength and endurance, 32 more recently, to improve structural cardiovascular function. The main types of IMST interventions are resistance threshold devices and flow resistance loading, which varies in terms of resistance level, intervention time, respiratory rate, and respiratory ratio. 33

Response to increased BP after a single intervention compared to a prolonged intervention, but the BP fluctuations it caused were more subdued compared to aerobic exercise, 23 preventing adverse cardiovascular effects caused by rapid changes in BP after exercise. When comparing the effects of prolonged interventions, the 8‐week intervention had a better BP lowering effect than the 6‐week intervention, which is somewhat different from the most common IMST intervention performed for 6 weeks. 34 In addition, other IMST programmes 35 have shown that maximum cardiovascular and respiratory muscle benefits can be achieved with more than 12 weeks of exercise. It is recommended that the next step is to explore whether more cardiovascular gains can be obtained, such as arterial wall remodeling that is not significantly altered until after more than 3 months of intervention, 26 and that the length of the intervention program also needs to be extended.

After excluding the adverse effects of short‐term interventions, IMST achieved significant efficacy in healthy adults, hypertensive, and OSA patients. Furthermore, IMST positively affected cardiac vagal‐mediated HRV and BP in an elderly female population 18 under conditions where aerobic exercise did not provide reasonable control of BP in menopausal women. 36

This area has been studied with low‐resistance IMST, but in recent years high‐resistance IMST has come to the forefront of researchers' attention. The effect of different resistance on SBP was similar, while high‐resistance was more effective than low‐resistance in reducing DBP. However, in the studies included here, high‐resistance IMST was usually accompanied by 12 breaths/min, and low‐resistance training was mainly accompanied by 6 breaths/min. In conditions where slow breathing alone already has a clear hypotensive effect, 37 the low‐resistance group cannot exclude a reduction in BP due to the low respiratory rate. It can only demonstrate that high‐resistance IMST achieves a similar SBP lowering effect as low‐resistance IMT combined with slow breathing. In addition to a fixed training intensity, when participants are unable to perform a high‐resistance exercise. This can gradually increase the training intensity 8 (from 55% to 75%) to improve patient tolerance and achieve a better exercise effect. This progressive loading has also improved apnea hypoventilation index for respiratory muscle strength 35 and OSA. 38

With the data available to date, 75% MIP training can be preferred when the population has a good exercise tolerance, achieving better effects and having high adherence rates. Progressive load training or low‐resistance training combined with slow breathing was also chosen to achieve a hypotensive effect when a population was intolerant to high‐resistance IMST. Regarding intervention timing, better BP lowering is often achieved with 8 weeks of training.

Factors affecting the BP lowering effect are not only influenced by the aspects mentioned above of intervention duration, resistance, population, and breathing frequency but also by unmentioned factors such as breathing ratio, supervision of the training process, and total intervention frequency. All of these variables should also be considered in future intervention programs.

Strengths and limitations

This article is the first to discuss the effectiveness of IMST in reducing BP and its intervention protocol using Meta‐analysis. In addition, a subgroup analysis was performed to derive the most appropriate intervention protocol, with less heterogeneity between the groups.

This paper also has some limitations in that although reductions were obtained after the IMST intervention in this paper, the sample size of the studies included in this paper was small. Also, this paper is a secondary study based on the literature and cannot dynamically interpret indicators of BP change, such as 24‐h ambulatory BP. A more significant number of articles were obtained without restricting the population, but at the same time, it failed to target the hypertensive population for in‐depth study strongly.

CONCLUSIONS

The effectiveness of IMST in reducing SBP and DBP after long‐term intervention in different populations has been confirmed, and TSA has shown that its cumulative data volume meets the target under conditions controlling for type I and II errors. The protocol for achieving optimal pressure reduction is 75% MIP or 30% MIP combined with 6 breaths/min for 8 weeks. There are some effects of intrathoracic pressure on cardiovascular function following the effects of IMST, which in turn allow favorable changes in sympathetic control, endothelial cell resistance, chronic low‐grade inflammation and oxidative stress.
