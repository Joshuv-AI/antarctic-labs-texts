Use of Angiotensin‐Converting Enzyme Inhibitors and Angiotensin Receptor Blockers Is Associated With a Reduced Risk of Poststroke Epilepsy in Patients With Ischemic Stroke
Authors: Kuo‐Cheng Chang, Ching‐Heng Lin, Jin‐An Huang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPoststroke epilepsy (PSE) is a common complication after ischemic stroke. This study investigates the association between the use of angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) and the risk of PSE in patients with ischemic stroke.Methods and ResultsA population‐based retrospective cohort study was conducted using Taiwan's National Health Insurance Research Database between 2000 and 2015. Patients with hypertension with a history of ischemic stroke were classified into prevalent, new, and nonusers according to their use of ACEIs/ARBs. Prevalent ACEI/ARB users were further classified into continuing or discontinued users, based on their poststroke medication adherence. We used multivariate Cox regression models, adjusted for demographics and comorbidities, to assess the risk of PSE among different ACEI/ARB user groups. There were 182 983 ACEI/ARB users and 38 365 nonusers included. There were 7387 patients diagnosed with PSE, whereas 213 961 were not. Nonusers exhibited a higher risk of PSE (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.72 [95% CI, 1.63–1.82]). Both prevalent and nonusers had higher risks compared with new ACEI/ARB users, with respective aHRs of 1.33 (95% CI, 1.25–1.41) and 2.00 (95% CI, 1.87–2.14). Discontinued ACEI/ARB users showed the highest risk of PSE (aHR, 2.34 [95% CI, 2.15–2.54]), suggesting the importance of continuing ACEI/ARB use after stroke. Treatment‐by‐age interaction was significant among patients with or without ACEI/ARB use before stroke (P value for interaction 0.004 and <0.001, respectively), suggesting a stronger beneficial association in younger patients.ConclusionsThe use of ACEIs/ARBs after ischemic stroke in patients with hypertension is associated with a reduced risk of PSE, especially among younger patients.

Stroke is the leading cause of acquired adult‐onset epilepsy, particularly among people >60 years of age, with cerebrovascular diseases accounting for up to 50% of newly diagnosed epilepsy. 1 The incidence of stroke‐related seizures ranges from 2% to 20%, primarily due to the different follow‐up time of seizure onset after stroke. 2 According to guidelines recommended by the International League Against Epilepsy, poststroke seizures are classified as early onset or late onset based on their occurrence within or after 7 days of a stroke. 3 A single late‐onset poststroke seizure implies a 71.5% risk of recurrent unprovoked seizures over the following 10 years 4 ; therefore, according to the definition of epilepsy by the International League Against Epilepsy, a diagnosis of poststroke epilepsy (PSE) can be established. Approximately 2% to 6% of patients with stroke are diagnosed with PSE, which can adversely affect poststroke recovery, potentially resulting in a worse prognosis and increased risks of disability, mortality, and cognitive impairment. 5 , 6 , 7

Arterial hypertension has been reported to be a risk factor for stroke and late‐onset epilepsy. 8 , 9 Hypertension can cause ischemic or hemorrhagic strokes and cerebral small vessel disease, indirectly contributing to epileptogenesis. However, recent preclinical studies suggest that hypertension also directly influences the development of epilepsy, even in the absence of overt brain lesions, possibly through central dysregulation of the renin‐angiotensin system (RAS). 10 Research in hypertensive rat models has shown an increase in protein expression of angiotensin 1 receptors in the hippocampal region of rats with epilepsy. 11 Angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs) and angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) are antihypertensive drugs that act primarily on RAS. Many animal studies have shown that the use of ARBs, such as losartan, olmesartan, and telmisartan, can reduce epileptogenesis and offer neuroprotective properties. 11 , 12 , 13 A recent study found that patients with hypertension who used ARBs exhibited a significantly lower incidence of subsequent epilepsy compared with those who received other types of antihypertensive medications. 14 However, to date, no studies have evaluated the protective effects of RAS antagonists, including ARBs or ACEIs, on PSE in patients with ischemic stroke.

This population‐based retrospective cohort study aimed to investigate whether the use of ARBs or ACEIs in patients with a history of ischemic stroke and concurrent hypertension is associated with the risk of PSE compared with other antihypertensive medications. We also assessed the impact of different pre‐ and poststroke ARB/AECI usage patterns on the risk of PSE.

METHODS

Data Transparency

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Data Source

Taiwan's National Health Insurance program, initiated in March 1995, is a compulsory health insurance scheme that encompasses 99.99% of the population, comprising 23.86 million individuals in Taiwan. To facilitate scientific and public health research, Taiwan's National Health Research Institutes collects and organizes all National Health Insurance claims data, including patient demographics, dates of medical visits, medications, procedures, and surgical claims; outpatient and inpatient disease diagnoses; and claimed costs. These records are stored in the National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD). Upon inclusion in the NHIRD, all identifiable personal information of each patient is anonymized and replaced with a unique, confidential identifier to protect patient privacy. The diagnosis of disease in the NHIRD is based on the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐9‐CM) and the Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) diagnostic codes, used before and after 2016, respectively. The database has been widely used in research related to stroke and epilepsy. 15 , 16 , 17

Ethical Statement

All identifiable data from NHIRD participants have been anonymized to ensure privacy and confidentiality. Because the data are anonymized before analysis, informed consent from the subjects is not required. This study was reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of Taichung Veterans General Hospital (CE13152B‐4).

Selection Criteria for Study Enrollees

From the NHIRD, we identified adult patients diagnosed with hypertension and ischemic stroke between January 1, 2000 and December 31, 2015. The diagnosis of ischemic stroke was based on ICD codes recorded during hospitalization, including ICD‐9‐CM: 433 and 434 or ICD‐10: I63, I65, and I66. These codes were required to be among the top 3 listed diagnoses to ensure that stroke was 1 of the primary diagnoses for admission. Furthermore, in Taiwan, patients diagnosed with ischemic stroke are eligible for catastrophic illness certification, which exempts them from a medical copayment. This certification is valid from the date of stroke onset until 1 month afterward. By using both ICD codes and catastrophic illness certification, we improved the precision of stroke diagnosis identification and confirmed the index date of stroke. The diagnosis of hypertension was based on at least 1 inpatient diagnosis or 3 outpatient diagnoses with any of the following ICD codes during the enrollment period: ICD‐9‐CM: 401–405 or ICD‐10: I10–I16.

We excluded patients who were <18 years of age, had a history of hemorrhagic stroke, were followed for <30 days, or had preexisting epilepsy before the index date of ischemic stroke. Furthermore, patients who had used antiseizure medications (ASMs) for >1 month before ischemic stroke were also excluded. All enrollees were followed for medication use and comorbidities assessment until the occurrence of PSE, withdrawal from the National Health Insurance scheme, or until December 31, 2020, whichever came first. Those who had never used ARBs, ACEIs, or any other type of antihypertensive medication during the follow‐up period were excluded as well.

Identification of the Use of Antihypertensive Drugs

The use of medications was based on the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) code. During the follow‐up period, patients who had received an ACEI (ATC codes C09A, C09B) or ARB (ATC codes C09C, C09D) for at least 90 cumulative days, before or after ischemic stroke, were classified as ACEI/ARB users. Those who did not meet the criteria for ACEI/ARB users but had taken calcium channel blockers (ATC code C08CA), β blockers (ATC code C07), or α blockers (ATC code C02CA) were defined as non‐ACEI/ARB users.

Patients who were already on ACEI/ARB therapy before the index ischemic stroke were defined as prevalent ACEI/ARB users, whereas those who started ACEI/ARB treatment after the index stroke were classified as new ACEI/ARB users. Among the prevalent ACEI/ARB users, we further differentiated 2 subgroups based on the continuity of ACEI/ARB therapy after stroke; those who continued their ACEI/ARB therapy were termed prevalent and continuing ACEI/ARB users (hereafter also termed continuing ACEI/ARB users), and those who stopped ACEI/ARB treatment after stroke were termed prevalent and discontinued ACEI/ARB users (hereafter also termed discontinued ACEI/ARB users).

Outcomes and Comorbidities

The diagnosis of PSE required an ICD code (ICD‐9‐CM: 345; ICD‐10: G40 and G41) in conjunction with records of consecutive outpatient prescriptions of ASMs (ATC code N03A) during the follow‐up period from 2000 to 2020. Moreover, the following ICD‐coded inpatient or outpatient diagnoses were considered comorbidities: diabetes, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, liver disease, chronic kidney disease, central nervous system infection and inflammation, head injury, brain tumor, dementia, depression, fibromyalgia, peripheral neuropathy, anxiety, migraine, and malignant neoplasm. The ICD codes for these comorbidities are listed in Table S1.

The severity of ischemic stroke was assessed using the claims‐based Stroke Severity Index (SSI). SSI is calculated using multiple linear regression equations based on in‐hospital therapeutic and procedural claims data, which predict stroke severity. 18 These include airway suctioning, bacterial sensitivity tests, stay in intensive care unit, placement of nasogastric tube, osmotherapy, urinary catheterization, and stay in a general ward. The SSI enables the calculation of an estimated National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (eNIHSS) score, whose application in ischemic stroke‐related NHIRD studies has been validated. 19 , 20

Statistical Analysis

We compared sociodemographic characteristics, SSI, and comorbidities between ACEI/ARB users and nonusers using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables. For continuous variables, normality of distribution was assessed before analysis. If met, the Student t test was used; otherwise, the Mann‐Whitney U test was used for analysis. Adjusting for age, sex, SSI, and all comorbidities, we evaluated the risk of PSE among patients with different ACEI/ARB usage patterns using multivariate Cox regression models, presented with adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) and 95% CIs. Subgroup analyses were performed with stratification by age (<65 years, 65–84 years, ≥85 years) and sex and stroke severity (eNIHSS <8, 9–15, ≥16) to assess whether the use of ACEIs/ARBs after stroke is consistently associated with a lower risk of PSE in each specific subgroup. The Kaplan‐Meier method was used for survival analysis of PSE, with the log‐rank test comparing differences between groups. All data analyses were performed with SAS software version 9.4 for Windows. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant, and all tests were 2‐tailed.

RESULTS

We initially identified 728 113 patients hospitalized for the first time with ischemic stroke between 2000 and 2015. Following exclusion of those without hypertension, those <18 years of age, those with hemorrhagic stroke, those with epilepsy before ischemic stroke, those with <30 days of follow‐up, or those with ASM use before diagnosis of ischemic stroke, 223 379 patients remained. Further exclusions for not receiving ACEIs/ARBs or other antihypertensive medications left 182 983 ACEI/ARB users and 38 365 nonusers. The users were subdivided into 106 669 prevalent and 76 314 new ACEI/ARB users, according to the use of medications before or after the index stroke. Among the total ACEI/ARB users, 5802 patients were diagnosed with PSE, including 3466 prevalent ACEI/ARB users and 2336 new ACEI/ARB users. Additionally, 1585 nonusers were diagnosed with PSE. Clinical and Demographic Characteristics of ACEI/ARB Users and Nonusers

The mean age of all ACEI/ARB users was 68.80±11.96 years, with new ACEI/ARB users averaging 65.73±11.99 years of age and prevalent ACEI/ARB users averaging 70.99±11.45 years of age. In regard to the prevalence of comorbidities, ACEI/ARB users exhibited overall higher rates of diabetes, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, anxiety, liver disease, and chronic kidney disease, but there were lower proportions of ACEI/ARB users with dementia, malignant neoplasm, and head injury compared with nonusers. Additionally, the prevalent ACEI/ARB users had the highest rates of comorbidity for diabetes, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, atrial fibrillation, liver disease, and chronic kidney disease among all groups. Risks of PSE Among Patients With Different Patterns of ACEI/ARB Use

Multivariate Cox regression models were used, adjusted for age, sex, eNIHSS, and all comorbidities, to compare the risk of developing PSE in different patterns of ACEI/ARB use. The results are presented in Table 2. Initially, all ACEI/ARB users were compared with nonusers, revealing that nonusers had a higher risk of PSE (aHR, 1.72 [95% CI, 1.63–1.82]). Subsequently, we compared new ACEI/ARB users, prevalent ACEI/ARB users, and nonusers to assess the impact of ACEI/ARB use before or after stroke. We found that both prevalent and nonusers had a higher risk of PSE compared with new ACEI/ARB users, with aHRs of 1.33 (95% CI, 1.25–1.41) and 2.00 (95% CI, 1.87–2.14), respectively. Further analysis was conducted to examine the effect of continuity of ACEI/ARB use after stroke among prevalent ACEI/ARB users by comparing new users, continuing users, discontinued users, and nonusers. The results demonstrated that discontinued users had the highest risk of PSE (aHR, 2.34 [95% CI, 2.15–2.54]), followed by nonusers (aHR, 2.06 [95% CI, 1.93–2.20]), with continuing users also showing a slightly elevated risk of PSE (aHR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.09–1.24]).

In Table 3, continuing ACEI/ARB users >85 years of age and those with eNIHSS scores >16 showed a higher prevalence of PSE compared with discontinued ACEI/ARB users, yet they exhibited lower hazard ratios for PSE. A similar trend is also observed in Table 4 between new ACEI/ARB users and nonusers. This disparity is likely attributed to the shorter follow‐up durations observed in discontinued ACEI/ARB users and nonusers within these subgroups, as indicated in Tables S2 through S5, which suggests higher mortality rates among older patients or those with severe strokes. Importantly, unlike prevalence rates, the calculation of hazard ratios considers follow‐up duration, thereby providing a more accurate representation of the protective association between poststroke ACEI/ARB use and the reduced risk of PSE.

DISCUSSION

In our population‐based cohort study of a large Taiwanese cohort, we analyzed the association between the use of ACEIs/ARBs for hypertension treatment and the risk of PSE in patients with ischemic stroke. Our findings indicate that patients with stroke treated with ACEIs/ARBs had a significantly lower incidence of PSE compared with those treated with other types of antihypertensive medications. Further analysis of different ACEI/ARB usage patterns before and after stroke revealed that patients not using ACEIs/ARBs after stroke, including discontinued ACEI/ARB users and nonusers, exhibited the highest incidence of PSE, followed by continuing ACEI/ARB users, with new ACEI/ARB users experiencing the lowest incidence of PSE.

In our study, we observed that prevalent ACEI/ARB users had a significantly higher risk of PSE compared with new ACEI/ARB users, although the increase was not as pronounced as that of nonusers. A possible explanation is that our prevalent ACEI/ARB user group inherently carried a higher risk of epilepsy due to preexisting comorbidities. According to Taiwan's hypertension treatment guidelines, RAS antagonists are recommended as first‐line therapy for patients with comorbid diabetes, chronic kidney disease, or coronary artery disease, leading to earlier initiation of ACEIs/ARBs in these patients. 21 Patients in our prevalent ACEI/ARB cohort not only had higher baseline rates of diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and coronary artery disease, but also a greater prevalence of hyperlipidemia and atrial fibrillation, all of which are known epilepsy risk factors. 22 , 23 To further investigate the benefits of RAS antagonists in our prevalent ACEI/ARB cohort, we divided them into continuing and discontinued users. Our findings revealed that within all groups examined, discontinued ACEI/ARB users faced the highest risk of PSE, surpassing even that of nonusers, whereas continuing ACEI/ARB users demonstrated a lower risk of PSE compared with nonusers. A plausible interpretation of these findings is as follows: Our prevalent ACEI/ARB users exhibited the highest comorbidity rates, even higher than those of nonusers, thereby increasing the risk of developing epilepsy. When prevalent ACEI/ARB users discontinued treatment after stroke, the consequent loss of the protective effect from RAS antagonists resulted in discontinued ACEI/ARB users having the highest risk of PSE among all patient groups. This underscores the critical importance of continuous ACEI/ARB administration, particularly in patients with high baseline epilepsy risk due to substantial comorbidities, because cessation of ACEI/ARB is markedly associated with increased susceptibility to PSE.

Arterial hypertension is a known risk factor for epilepsy. A previous single‐center study indicated that patients taking antihypertensive medications had a lower risk of PSE, with protective effects observed only with calcium channel blockers, ACEIs, and ARBs. 24 A recent, larger cohort study has shown that among general patients with arterial hypertension, those treated with ARBs have a lower incidence of epilepsy compared with those treated with calcium channel blockers and β blockers. 14 These findings corroborate our results, suggesting that RAS antagonists may confer poststroke neuroprotection against epileptogenesis through mechanisms beyond blood pressure reduction.

Although human studies are scarce, current preclinical animal research suggests several possible mechanisms by which RAS antagonists could prevent PSE. First, they aid in maintaining the integrity of the blood–brain barrier (BBB). After stroke or other brain injuries, the BBB can become compromised, leading to increased permeability to plasma proteins and other substances, a consequent inflammatory process, and hyperexcitability, thus exacerbating epileptogenesis. 25 Previous studies showed that the use of losartan helped prevent increased BBB permeability in hypertensive rats, indicating its potential protective effect on BBB integrity. 26 , 27 Second, BBB dysfunction can lead to the entry of blood proteins into the brain, stimulating TGF (transforming growth factor)‐β‐mediated astrocyte activation and resulting in chronic neuroinflammation that contributes to epilepsy. Past studies found that ARBs possessed anti‐inflammatory effects capable of inhibiting TGF‐β‐related pathways and preventing the occurrence of delayed spontaneous seizures. 28 , 29 Third, RAS antagonists were reported to exhibit antioxidative and antineurotoxic effects in rats following various brain injuries, which reduced the frequency of spontaneous motor seizure occurrences. 30 Last, angiotensin peptides are also considered to act as neurotransmitters or neuromodulators, particularly in the brainstem, hypothalamus, and forebrain. 31 Angiotensin can influence the epilepsy threshold by binding directly to its receptors or indirectly as modulators that affect the balance of other typical neurotransmitters, such as dopamine, γ‐aminobutyric acid, and adenosine. 31 , 32 A previous study showed that the use of telmisartan and olmesartan had a dose‐dependent anticonvulsant effect in a mouse model. 12 In summary, the protective effects of ACEIs/ARBs on epileptogenesis are multifaceted, encompassing at least the preservation of BBB integrity, anti‐inflammatory, antioxidative, antineurotoxic properties, as well as actions in neurotransmitters and neuromodulators.

In our subgroup analyses, we found that although ACEI/ARB therapy was associated with a significantly lower risk of PSE across all subgroups, this association was more pronounced in younger age groups and in those with eNIHSS scores of <16. The previous literature has indicated that being <65 years of age is a risk factor for PSE, whereas being >85 years is protective against seizure recurrence after stroke. 5 , 33 This can be attributed to younger individuals being more prone to cortical infarcts rather than small vessel disease, whereas older individuals typically have thinner cerebral cortices and reduced cortical excitability due to aging and neurodegeneration. The aforementioned potential mechanisms of ACEIs/ARBs in preventing epileptogenesis occur predominantly in the cortical regions, which may explain why there is a stronger association between ACEI/ARB use after stroke and a lower risk of PSE in younger patients. Furthermore, we found that in patients with higher stroke severity, the association of ACEI/ARB therapy as a protective factor for PSE was weaker. Many studies have identified stroke severity as the most consistent and robust risk factor for PSE. 34 , 35 Our research also indicates that subgroups with an eNIHSS score of ≥16 have the highest prevalence of PSE, and our findings suggest that the beneficial association of ACEI/ARB therapy for PSE can be attenuated in these patients.

To our knowledge, we present the first study primarily examining the association between the use of ACEIs/ARBs as antihypertensive therapy and the risk of PSE in patients with ischemic stroke. One of the strengths of our research was the use of a large‐scale population‐based cohort. Additionally, we classified the subjects according to different patterns of ACEI/ARB use before and after stroke, allowing for an analysis of the differential risks of PSE between various medication usage patterns. Our study also has limitations. First, the diagnoses of ischemic stroke, PSE, and other comorbidities were based on ICD codes from the NHIRD, and therefore the accuracy of these diagnoses is of concern. However, previous studies have successfully validated the accuracy of using NHIRD ICD codes for the diagnosis of ischemic stroke, reporting a high sensitivity and positive predictive value of 97.3% and 98%, respectively. 20 , 36 In our study, the requirement of catastrophic illness certification for stroke diagnosis further improved the reliability of identifying stroke diagnoses and onset dates. Furthermore, in terms of epilepsy diagnosis, previous research has also validated its accuracy in the NHIRD, with a sensitivity and specificity of 81.4% and 99.8%, respectively. 37 Second, because the NHIRD consists of claims‐based data, it does not capture data on smoking and alcohol use, blood pressure control status, and the Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) classification of stroke. Additionally, our study could not consider important factors associated with PSE, such as cortical superficial siderosis, cortical lesions, and territory of middle cerebral artery involvement, as indicated by the SeLECT score. 38 Furthermore, the data set lacks information on the semiology, frequency, and control status of PSE. Future prospective randomized controlled trials are warranted to further analyze the impact of these factors on the reduction of PSE risk in patients with ischemic stroke using RAS antagonists. Third, our study did not include poststroke ASM use as a potential confounder, primarily due to insufficient evidence on specific dosages and treatment durations for each ASM that can influence PSE occurrence. Instead, we incorporated nonepileptic conditions commonly treated with ASMs as confounders to assess the likelihood of ASM use among each patient group and adjusted with a Cox regression model. Fourth, our study did not analyze the occurrence of early poststroke seizure and seizure recurrence. Due to the inaccessibility of detailed inpatient and outpatient medical records from the NHIRD, it is challenging to identify early PSE during hospitalization or determine seizure recurrence at outpatient visits. Further research is needed to explore whether ACEI/ARB use also affects the incidence of early poststroke seizure or seizure recurrence.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study revealed a negative correlation between the use of ACEIs or ARBs and PSE in patients with hypertension following ischemic stroke. Patients with ACEI/ARB use before stroke often had more comorbidities and exhibited the highest risk of PSE if they discontinued ACEIs/ARBs after stroke. Additionally, the association of ACEI/ARB treatment with a reduced risk of PSE was more pronounced in younger patients or those with mild‐to‐moderate strokes. Our findings support the potential of ACEIs/ARBs in lowering the risk of PSE in patients with ischemic stroke.
