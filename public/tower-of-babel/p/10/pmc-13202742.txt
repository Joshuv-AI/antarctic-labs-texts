PRKCQ-TRIM22 axis promotes proliferation and metastasis of oral squamous cell carcinoma via autophagosome‒lysosome pathway activation
Authors: Jinghong Liu, Jianbo Zhang, Qunxiang Chen, Weijia Qing, Yongchun Peng, Zhijing He, Xi Zhou, Huifeng Pi, Bo Li, Qingyun Lin, Junxin Liu, Fan Zhang, Sheng Zhang, Tengfei Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral squamous cell carcinoma(OSCC)is the most common oral cancer, featuring aggressive growth and high metastatic potential. Autophagy, the main cellular degradation mechanism, is closely linked to the progression of this cancer, but its specific role and related molecular mechanisms remain unclear. This study aimed to clarify the function of PRKCQ (protein kinase Cθ) in oral squamous cell carcinoma and explore how it affects cancer progression by regulating autophagy.MethodsTissue microarray, animal experiments and cell assays were used to detect the effect of PRKCQ knockdown on the proliferation and metastasis of OSCC. Transcriptomic analysis of cancer tissues was combined with data from public databases to analyze the correlation between PRKCQ and autophagy, and their association with patient prognosis. Cell experiments verified the effect of PRKCQ knockdown on autophagic flux and related functions. Cell transcriptomic analysis screened downstream target genes and verified the mechanism.ResultsThe tissue microarray (TMA) revealed that PRKCQ is highly expressed in OSCC tissues and is negatively correlated with survival rate. Animal and cell experiments demonstrated that PRKCQ knockdown (KD) inhibited the proliferation and metastasis of OSCC cells. Through transcriptome analysis of OSCC tissues combined with data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database and the Human Autophagy Database (HADb), we revealed a close association between PRKCQ and autophagy, which involved in OSCC progression. Subsequent functional experiments demonstrated that PRKCQ KD disrupts autophagic flux by impairing lysosomal function and blocking autophagosome‒lysosome fusion. Furthermore, we found that knockdown of PRKCQ significantly suppressed the expression of TRIM22 (tripartite motif containing 22) through transcriptome analysis of PRKCQ KD cells. The overexpression of TRIM22 restored lysosomal function and autophagosome‒lysosome fusion, thereby restoring the progression of OSCC cells impaired by PRKCQ knockdown.ConclusionThe PRKCQ-TRIM22 autophagic regulatory axis plays a key role in oral squamous cell carcinoma progression. This study clarifies the molecular mechanism by which PRKCQ promotes cancer development via autophagy regulation, providing potential new targets for targeted therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04232-3.Keywords: Autophagy, PRKCQ, Oral squamous cell carcinoma, TRIM22, Therapeutic target

Introduction

In 2020, there were approximately 377,731 new cases of oral cancer globally [1], with high incidence and mortality rates in Asia [2]. More than 90% of oral cancers are OSCC, with cervical lymph node metastasis being a key survival factor; after metastasis, survival rates drop to 50% [3], with an annual secondary tumor incidence of 3%−7% [4]. The mechanisms of OSCC invasion and metastasis remain incompletely understood. Despite advancements in medical care, the survival rates of OSCC patients have improved little in recent decades, underscoring the urgent need to explore the intrinsic molecular mechanisms of OSCC invasion and metastasis.

Autophagy is a deeply preserved mechanism of cellular degradation in eukaryotes, playing key roles in maintaining intracellular homeostasis, responding to nutrient deficiency, and clearing damaged organelles and protein aggregates [5]. In the late stage of tumor progression, autophagy can promote tumor growth, invasion, and metastasis through various mechanisms. For instance, a study showed that suppressing SRSF1 markedly reduces OSCC progression by disrupting lysosome formation and autophagy [6]. Studies have revealed that MAP3K11 can activate ATG4B and promote autophagy, which may increase the malignancy of OSCC cells [7]. Our prior studies demonstrated that increased autophagy mediated by the NUPR1/TFE3 axis enhances the proliferation and metastasis of OSCC cells [8] and that EPHB2 facilitates the progression and metastasis of OSCC by activating VPS4A to promote autophagy [9]. Therefore, identifying targets that inhibit autophagy in OSCC may provide new strategies for the treatment of OSCC.

PRKCQ (protein kinase Cθ, abbreviated as PKCθ), a member of the protein kinase C (PKC) group, is strongly correlated with tumor proliferation and invasion. For example, PRKCQ expression in breast cancer cells enhances their proliferative capacity, whereas inhibiting its expression suppresses cell proliferation [10]. Studies have highlighted the close relationship between PRKCQ and autophagy [11]. Previous investigations have indicated that elevated expression of the PKC family in OSCC cells is closely correlated with low patient survival and high recurrence rates [12–14]. However, the precise molecular mechanism, particularly the regulatory interplay between PRKCQ and autophagy, remains unclear in OSCC.

TRIM22, a TRIM protein family E3 ubiquitin ligase, has a standard N-terminal RING, B-box, coiled-coil architecture and modulates autophagy, apoptosis, and innate immune responses [15]. TRIM proteins modulate autophagy by binding to autophagy-related proteins and acting as receptors or scaffolds for substrate degradation [16]. TRIM proteins bind the BECN1-ULK1 complex, assembling TRIMosomes that direct substrates to autophagosomes [17]. Studies link TRIM22 dysregulation to several diseases, such as cancer, immune disorders, and developmental anomalies [15, 18].

In this study, through the use of TMA analysis and in vivo and in vitro studies, we demonstrated that PRKCQ expression is correlated with poor prognosis. Furthermore, analysis of OSCC data from the TCGA database and HADb and transcriptome sequencing data revealed that the autophagy-related gene PRKCQ plays an important role in OSCC progression. Additionally, via cell transcriptome sequencing and in vivo and in vitro studies, we revealed that PRKCQ affects autophagic flux through altering TRIM22 expression, thereby increasing OSCC cell proliferation and metastasis. In summary, this study identifies the PRKCQ–TRIM22 axis as a modulator of autophagic flux and proposes a novel OSCC treatment strategy.

Materials and methods

Ethics statement

After authorization from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee and the Oral & Maxillofacial Surgery Department at the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University (w20243206), 88 OSCC samples and 31 oral mucosal tissue samples were obtained from patients at this hospital. Before study commencement [9], the subjects provided documented consent.

Tissue microarray (TMA)

Tissues were obtained from patients at the undergraduate level. The collected tissue samples were immediately placed in a fixative solution. The standard fixative was 10% neutral buffered formalin, and the samples were immersed for 24 h, sectioned, and subjected to tissue microarray (TMA) analysis and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining by Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. The samples were evaluated independently by pathologists in a blinded manner.

Animal experiments

This study’s animal experiments adhered to the protocols established by the Army Medical University’s Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee, Chongqing, China (AMUWEC 20250045). To evaluate the effect of PRKCQ on SCC9 cell growth in vivo, we conducted experiments using nude mice (obtained from Sichuan Weitong Lihua Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd.) The animals were allowed to adapt for one week, with continuous availability of food and water. The first batch consisted of 28-day-old, male BALB/c nude mice, which were divided into two cohorts: (i) the control group, in which mice were subcutaneously injected with SCC9 cells stably transduced with an empty vector (n = 5), and (ii) the PRKCQ KD group, in which mice were injected with SCC9 cells stably transduced with the PRKCQ KD virus (n = 5). The second batch comprised another set of 28-day-old, male BALB/c nude mice, which were divided into four groups: (i) the control group, in which mice were injected with empty vector-transduced SCC9 cells (n = 5); (ii) the TRIM22 OE group, in which mice were injected with TRIM22-overexpressing (OE) SCC9 cells (n = 5); (iii) the PRKCQ KD group, in which mice were injected with PRKCQ KD SCC9 cells (n = 5); and (iv) the PRKCQ KD + TRIM22 OE group, in which mice were injected with SCC9 cells co-expressing the PRKCQ KD and TRIM22 OE sequences (n = 5). Tumor growth was monitored by palpation and measurement. Each mouse was injected with 1 × 10⁶ SCC9 cells. The mice were euthanized prior to the tumor diameter exceeding 1.5 cm.

Culturing and treatment of cells

The SCC9 and CAL27 cell lines were obtained from ATCC (USA). These cells were cultured in DMEM (11965092; Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (A5256701; Gibco) and a 1% antibiotic solution containing penicillin and streptomycin (15070063; Gibco), adhering to established laboratory protocols. The cells were incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂-regulated setting, with the CAL27 and SCC9 cell lines preserved under 37 °C conditions and a 5% CO₂-enriched, humidified atmosphere.

Plasmid transfection and construction of stable cell lines

shRNA plasmids targeting PRKCQ and TRIM22 were purchased from GenePharma (GenePharma, China). All plasmid transfections were performed using Lipo8000 DNA Transfection Reagent (Beyotime, Cat. No. C0533). For lentivirus production in 293 T cells, DNA mixtures (diluted in Opti-MEM) were prepared in 6-well plates containing the transfer vector (8 µg), the envelope plasmid pMD2.G (4 µg) packaging and the psPAX2 plasmid (6 µg). After the cells were harvested at 48 h and 72 h after transfection, viral media were concentrated using the Universal Virus Concentration Kit (Beyotime, C2901S). Stable PRKCQ knockdown and TRIM22 overexpression cell lines were established by culturing the transduced cells under puromycin treatment for 14 days. Transfection success was confirmed via WB analysis (Fig. 3A and B and S2A and B).

Colony formation assay

Following resuspension, OSCC cells were incubated in enriched media at 37 °C for one week. Six-well plates were seeded at a density of 1,000 cells/well, and the culture medium was replaced every three days. Once the samples were properly fixed and washed, they were subjected to crystal violet staining. The resulting data were then meticulously analyzed using the ImageJ program.

Cell migration and invasion assays

For the Transwell assay, we used 24-well plates paired with 8.0-µm pore size inserts (#3422, Corning, USA). For migration, cells were resuspended in serum-free DMEM, and 100 µL of the cell (2 × 10⁴ cells) was seeded into the upper chamber. The lower chamber was added with 600 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS. For invasion, the upper chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel (Servicebio, G4131-5ML) at a dilution ratio of 1:8, and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h for solidification. Cells were resuspended in serum-free medium, and 100 µL of the cell suspension (4 × 10⁴ cells) was added to the pre-coated upper chambers. The lower chamber was supplemented with 600 µL of complete medium containing 10% FBS.After being incubated 24 h, the cells were fixed and stained with 0.5% crystal violet in methanol (Beyotime, C0121-100 ml). We carefully removed any cells clinging to the polycarbonate membrane with a cotton swab, recorded images, and then, as we previously described, we tallied up the cell count [19–21] [.

Sample and data collection

OSCC sample mRNA data were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA). The dataset included 340 tumor specimens with RNA-seq data and 32 matched normal controls. To ensure data reliability, we excluded cases in which the patient follow-up period was shorter than 30 days. Among the initial 333 samples with survival data, those lacking complete clinical details—such as missing TNM staging data—were filtered out, leaving 275 cases for further study. Additionally, the research included 10 oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) samples and 10 healthy control samples, both of which were collected from the Oral & Maxillofacial Surgery Department at the Second Xiangya Hospital of Central South University [9].

Bioinformatic analysis

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between samples from the HNSC TCGA cohort and our OSCC samples were identified using the DESeq2 package (version 1.40.1) in R (version 4.4.0). With the screening criteria of padj < 0.05 and |logFC| > 1, 2212 genes were identified [9]. Moreover, the gene expression data were normalized. A total of 232 autophagy-associated genes were sourced from the HADb. A total of 22 DEGs related to autophagy were jointly screened, and then we used the clinical data from the 333 OSCC patients to perform univariate Cox and LASSO regression analyses. Finally, we identified four autophagy-related genes strongly significantly related to OSCC patient prognosis.

Western blot analysis

Cell or tissue samples were collected, mixed with lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0011) containing protease inhibitors (Roche, USA), and fully lysed on ice with ultrasonic disruption to release the proteins into the solution. The samples were subsequently centrifuged at high speed (12,000×g, 30 min) at 4 °C. The protein content of the supernatant was assessed using a BCA assay. The separating gel was prepared, and the samples were loaded and subjected to electrophoresis; then, the proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane, blocked for 15 min, exposed to primary antibody for an 8-hour period, and then subjected to three washes with PBST for 5 min each, followed by a 1-hour exposure to secondary antibodies and a final three washes of 10 min each. Afterward, the membrane was developed and analyzed using a chemiluminescence imager (Bio-Rad). The details of the antibodies utilized are listed in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence analysis

OSCC cells were grown on coverslips and fixed for 20 min with 4% paraformaldehyde. The cells were subjected to permeabilization and blocking via incubation in PBS supplemented with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min. The cells were then incubated for 12 h with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by three 5-minute washes with PBST. The cells were subsequently incubated with dilute fluorophore-labeled secondary antibodies in blocking agent for one hour, followed by three 5-minute washes in PBST. For nuclear counterstaining, the cells were treated with DAPI (Abcam, B1098). Slides were prepared by affixing coverslips with anti-fade solution (Beyotime, P0131). Fluorescence images were obtained via a Zeiss 800 confocal microscope. The details of the antibodies utilized are listed in Table S2.

Quantitative realtime PCR

We utilized the RNAiso Plus reagent from TaKaRa (catalog number 9109) for total RNA extraction and converted it into complementary DNA (cDNA) utilizing the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (catalog number RR047A, also from TaKaRa). For quantitative analysis, we utilized SYBR Green Fast Mix (catalog number RK21203 from ABclonal) as our fluorescent dye on a state-of-the-art real-time PCR system by Bio-Rad. The specific primer sequences are detailed in Table S3.

RFP-GFP-LC3B analysis

Twenty-four hours prior to infection, OSCC cells were seeded at 60,000 cells/well in each well containing 1 mL of full-growth culture medium. The viral dosage was determined on the basis of the specifications of the Premo Autophagy Tandem Sensor RFP-GFP-LC3B kit (Invitrogen, P36239) [8]. The virus was thawed on ice, mixed, and then left to sit. The confocal dishes were removed from the cell incubator, the old medium was removed, and one-half milliliter of fresh medium was added to each well. Approximately one day after infection, the viral medium was removed, two milliliters of fresh, complete medium was added to each well, and the cells were cultured for another day. Images were subsequently captured using a confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8, Germany), and statistical analysis was performed using ImageJ.

GFP-LC3-LysoTracker analysis

In accordance with established protocols, OSCC cells were transfected with the GFP-LC3B plasmid using Lipo8000 DNA Transfection Reagent (Beyotime, Cat. No. C0533) [22]. After the cells were allowed to sit for 48 h, they were treated with LysoTracker Red DND-99 (Invitrogen, L7528) at 200 ng/ml, followed by a 30-minute incubation at 37 °C. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed three times with PBS. Finally, anti-fade mounting medium (Beyotime, P0131) was used for coverslip mounting. Images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal laser scanning microscope with 63× and 40× oil immersion objectives; 5 nonoverlapping high-power fields (HPFs) were randomly selected per sample. Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) analysis was performed via ImageJ software (v1.8.0) with the “Coloc 2” plugin. Regions of interest (ROIs) were manually defined to cover the cytoplasm of target cells, avoiding nonspecific background interference. The average PCC value of the 5 HPFs was calculated for each sample, and intergroup statistical comparisons were conducted on the basis of these averages.

DQ-BSA proteolytic activity assay

Cells were plated in confocal dishes at 5 × 10⁴ cells/96-well plate. After the old medium was removed, 20 µg/mL DQ-BSA solution was added. The cells were left to incubate in the dark at 37 °C for six hours. After they were incubated, they were subjected to three washes with PBS. The fluorescence was subsequently measured via an Infinite M200 reader (excitation: 590 nm; emission: 620 nm).

LysoSensor green DND189 staining

OSCC cells (1 × 10⁴/well) were added to 96-well plates and incubated to 70–80% density. The old medium was aspirated, and LysoSensor working solution (1 µM; Invitrogen, Cat. No. L7535) was added. The cells were kept under light-proof conditions at 37 °C for 30 min and then washed off with PBS. The fluorescence intensity was measured using an Infinite™ M200 microplate reader (excitation wavelength: 485 nm; emission wavelength: 530 nm).

Transcriptomic analysis

The OSCC cell (SCC9) from the control and PRKCQ KD groups were completely homogenized, and total RNA was isolated with TRIzol (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA; Cat. No. 15596026). Transcriptome analysis was subsequently performed by Shanghai Applied Protein Technology using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000/MGISEQ-T7 instrument. Using reference gene sequences, Illumina/BGI software was used to quantify protein-coding gene read counts per sample. The resulting data were subsequently utilized to determine the gene expression values for every individual sample. To standardize the gene counts across samples, the DESeq2 tool was used, leveraging the base mean to estimate relative expression levels. Fold changes in expression were evaluated using negative binomial distribution-based differential significance analyses. Protein-coding genes with differential expression were determined from the results of expression fold changes and differential significance tests, with the screening criteria of padj < 0.05 and |logFC| > 0.585.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

OSCC tumor tissues obtained from subcutaneous xenografts in nude mice were first preserved in 4% paraformaldehyde. The samples were then dehydrated with 90% ethanol and embedded in paraffin. Sections were sliced to 5 μm thickness under a microtome, baked at 60 °C for 1 h to ensure firm adherence to the slides, and then deparaffinized and rehydrated. After blocking with serum, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies for 12 h and then washed with PBS. Secondary antibodies were then applied for 1 h, after which the cells were washed three times with PBS. The antibody specifications are listed in Table S1. Ultimately, the samples were covered with glass slides, digitized, and processed through ImageJ to determine the proportion of positive cells. The details of the antibodies utilized are listed in Table S1.

Detection of CTSB/CTSD enzyme activity

First, 500 mg of mouse tumor tissue was removed and mixed with 5 times the volume of lysis buffer; then, the samples were centrifuged at high speed (12,000×g, 30 min) at 4 °C, and the supernatant was subjected to quantitative analysis. In a 96-well plate, the following components were added: 50 µL of crude enzyme extract + 50 µL of reaction buffer + 10 µL of substrate solution. For the negative control, an equal amount of inactivated sample (heated at 95 °C for 15 min to deactivate the enzyme) was added; for the blank control, the sample was replaced with lysis buffer. The samples were kept in the dark at 37 °C and processed for 30 min. The fluorescence was then measured via a microplate reader using a Cathepsin B/D Activity Assay Kit (Abcam, ab65300/ab65302) [23].

Statistical analysis

R (v4.4.0) and GraphPad Prism 10.0 were used to examine missing values and outliers and for the subsequent data analysis. The sample size for each group was at least n ≥ 3. Quantitative results are presented as the mean ± SEM for within-group and between-group analyses, and suitable statistical approaches were chosen according to the data distribution pattern and grouping design: for data that were normally distributed, we used independent t tests and one-way ANOVA. But when the data was not normally distributed, we employed the Mann‒Whitney U test and Kruskal‒Wallis test. In correlation studies, the Pearson coefficient was used for data with a normal distribution, whereas the Spearman rank correlation was employed for data with a skewed distribution. The Kaplan‒Meier method was used to determine survival probabilities. All trials were repeated at least three times. The significance thresholds were *p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01.

Results

PRKCQ is highly expressed in OSCC tissues and is negatively correlated with survival rate

The PRKCQ protein is intricately linked to various tumors. For example, it is associated with malignant neoplasms such as rectal cancer, breast cancer, and lymphoma [13, 24, 25]. To further confirm the expression of PRKCQ in OSCC, we constructed an OSCC TMA using 88 OSCC tissue samples and 31 normal samples collected from our hospital. Our findings revealed that PRKCQ was distinctly overexpressed in oral cancer tissues compared with normal tissues (Fig. 1A and D). Furthermore, the PRKCQ level was directly related to lymph node metastasis in patients with OSCC (Fig. 1B, C and E). Through Kaplan‒Meier (K‒M) analysis of patients admitted to our department, we found that the PRKCQ expression level served as a negative prognostic indicator for patient outcomes at the 5-year time point (Fig. 1F).

PRKCQ knockdown inhibited the proliferation and metastasis of OSCC cells in vitro and in vivo

We generated OSCC cell lines with stable PRKCQ knockdown (KD). In the colony formation assay, compared with that in the control group, the colony formation rate of OSCC cells in the PRKCQ-KD group was significantly lower (Fig. 2A and B). The results of the Transwell assay revealed that compared with those in the control group, the migration and invasion abilities in the PRKCQ-KD group were significantly lower (Fig. 2C and D). For animal experiments, we established a stable SCC9 cell line in which PRKCQ was knocked down. Subsequently, xenograft tumor models were constructed by subcutaneous injection of control and PRKCQ-KD SCC9 cells into nude mice. Our findings demonstrated that compared with those in the control group, the tumor growth rate, tumor volume, and tumor weight were lower in the PRKCQ-KD group (Fig. 2E and F). These results indicate that PRKCQ promotes the progression of OSCC in vitro and in vivo.

PRKCQ leads to a poor prognosis in patients with OSCC by promoting autophagic flux

To clarify how OSCC invades and spreads, we analyzed RNA sequencing data from both TCGA and our own studies, pinpointing 2,212 differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Among these genes, 232 were linked to autophagy, as identified in the HADb. Through univariate Cox regression and LASSO analyses, we focused on four autophagy-related genes that were significantly associated with OSCC progression (Fig. S1A–C). To further investigate whether PRKCQ promotes the progression of OSCC through autophagy, we next aimed to clarify the relationship between PRKCQ and autophagy by comparing the PRKCQ-KD group with the control group. LC3B-II, one of the most vital marker proteins of autophagy, is widely used in the detection and analysis of autophagic activity. P62, an important autophagic receptor, can recruit ubiquitinated protein aggregates to autophagosomes to promote their degradation. When autophagic function is impaired, P62 protein accumulates within cells. The Western blot and immunofluorescence results indicated that compared with those in the control group, autophagic substrates significantly accumulated in the PRKCQ-KD group (Fig. 3A–E). The simultaneous increase in LC3B and P62 often indicates complex alterations in the autophagic process. To clarify whether P62 protein accumulation induced by PRKCQ KD results from transcriptional upregulation [26], we carried out real-time PCR to measure P62 mRNA levels in samples with and without PRKCQ KD. As shown in (Fig. 3F), PRKCQ KD did not affect P62 mRNA levels in OSCC cells. When autophagosome‒lysosome fusion is impaired or lysosomal function is defective, LC3B-II and P62 accumulate because of the failure of normal degradation [27]. Chloroquine (CQ) inhibits lysosomal activity and autophagosome‒lysosome fusion, thereby suppressing autophagy [28]. In this study, LC3B-II expression did not significantly differ between the PRKCQ-KD groups with and without CQ treatment, and the differences among the PRKCQ-KD group, CQ-only group and control group were similar (Fig. 3G and H). In conclusion, PRKCQ leads to poor prognosis in OSCC through the promotion of autophagic flux.

PRKCQ knockdown blocks autophagosome–lysosome fusion and inhibits lysosomal function in OSCC

The mechanism underlying the relationship between PRKCQ and autophagy was investigated. The fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes is essential for autophagy, and blocking this step disrupts degradation [29], leading to the accumulation of LC3B-II and P62. To further elucidate the underlying molecular mechanism through which PRKCQ modulates autophagy, we evaluated the effects of PRKCQ knockdown on autophagosome‒lysosome fusion and autophagic flux. Costaining with GFP-LC3B and LysoTracker Red is a classic fluorescence technique for studying autophagosome–lysosome fusion, a key step in autophagic flux. Under normal autophagic flux, autophagosomes (green puncta) fuse with lysosomes (red puncta), resulting in the formation of yellow colocalized puncta (overlapping green–red fluorescence). When autophagic flux is impaired, green and red puncta remain separated, with significantly reduced colocalization signals. We examined the colocalization of GFP-LC3B with LysoTracker Red, a lysosome-specific vital stain [30]. We observed significant separation between GFP-LC3B (green) and LysoTracker Red (red) in the PRKCQ-KD group compared with the control group. The results of the analysis revealed markedly lower Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) values in the PRKCQ knockdown group than in the control group (Fig. 4A), indicating that PRKCQ KD indeed disrupted autophagosome‒lysosome fusion. To further confirm that PRKCQ KD disrupts the fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes, we applied RFP-GFP-LC3B, a dual-fluorescence reporter system widely used in autophagy research [31]. The green fluorescence emitted by GFP is quenched in an acidic environment, whereas the fluorescence of RFP remains unaltered. Therefore, when we merge the images, a higher ratio of yellow fluorescence to red fluorescence indicates lower autophagosome-lysosome fusion efficiency. In contrast to that in the control group, the ratio of autophagosomes (yellow) to autolysosomes (red) was notably higher in the PRKCQ-KD group (Fig. 4B). The function of lysosomes is a key factor influencing their fusion with autophagosomes [27, 28]. To further verify the effect on lysosomal function, we measured the levels of the lysosomal membrane markers LAMP1 and LAMP2. Western blot analysis revealed significantly decreased LAMP1 and LAMP2 expression following PRKCQ KD (Fig.4C and D). To assess the influence of PRKCQ KD on lysosomal activity, we detected the fluorescence intensity via a DQ-BSA assay, which revealed lysosomal proteolytic activity [32]. LysoSensor Green DND-189 is highly sensitive to changes in the pH of lysosomes. In an acidic environment, the fluorescence intensity increases, and there is a certain positive correlation between the fluorescence intensity and the degree of acidity of lysosomes [8]. The fluorescence intensities of both LysoSensor Green DND-189 and DQ-BSA decreased significantly in the PRKCQ KD group compared with those in the control group (Fig. 4E and f). These findings suggest that PRKCQ knockdown impairs lysosomal function by altering the lysosomal pH and decreasing the expression abundance of lysosomal cathepsins. In summary, PRKCQ knockdown suppresses autophagic flux by concurrently influencing both fusion efficiency and lysosomal function.

Transcriptomic analysis identified TRIM22 as a potential target gene involved in PRKCQ-mediated OSCC progression

We explored the molecular mechanisms through which PRKCQ promotes the proliferation of OSCC cells. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) revealed significant changes in the transcriptional profiles of SCC9 cells following PRKCQ knockdown. On the basis of the fold change (FC) and p value thresholds, differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq. A total of 177 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) emerged—138 upregulated and 39 downregulated genes—after PRKCQ knockdown (Fig. 5A). A heatmap was generated to visualize the clustering analysis results of these DEGs (Fig. 5B). In the volcano plot and KEGG pathway, It can be observed that after PRKCQ knockdown, both the autophagy animal pathway and tumor-related pathways are enriched, and the autophagy pathway includes the TRIM22 gene. Therefore, we have selected the TRIM22 gene as the key gene that mediates PRKCQ’s promotion of autophagic flux (Fig. S3). Additionally, real-time PCR, Western blot, and immunofluorescence analyses confirmed that TRIM22 expression was lower in the PRKCQ-KD group than in the control group (Fig. 5C–H). To investigate the roles of PRKCQ and TRIM22 in the progression and invasion of OSCC, we overexpressed TRIM22 in both the NC group and PRKCQ KD group via cell transfection. The overexpression efficiency is shown (Fig. S2). In subsequent colony formation assays, we observed that the overexpression of TRIM22 attenuated the inhibitory effect of PRKCQ knockdown on the proliferation of OSCC cells (Fig. 6A and B). Similarly, according to the results of the Transwell assays, TRIM22 overexpression reversed the decrease in the invasion and migration of OSCC cells caused by PRKCQ KD. Additionally, TRIM22 overexpression alone enhanced OSCC cell invasion and migration (Fig. 6C and D). These findings demonstrate that PRKCQ promotes the proliferation and aggression of OSCC cells through TRIM22-mediated mechanisms.

PRKCQ enhances TRIM22 expression to activate lysosomal function and facilitate the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes

Numerous investigations have confirmed a robust association between the TRIM family and autophagy. Notably, TRIM22 has been shown to increase autophagic flux by enhancing autophagosome‒lysosome fusion [19]. Our previous studies revealed that autophagic flux promotes the development and metastasis of OSCC cells [8, 9]. Both PRKCQ and TRIM22 are autophagy-related genes, and their potential association is likely closely linked to the progression of OSCC.

To clarify the potential association between PRKCQ and TRIM22 in terms of autophagy, we overexpressed TRIM22 in the control group and the PRKCQ-KD group. Western blot analysis revealed that the PRKCQ KD-induced accumulation of LC3B-II and P62 was attenuated by the overexpression of TRIM22, indicating that TRIM22 overexpression can significantly reverse the blockade of autophagic flux caused by PRKCQ KD. Furthermore, we found that the expression of LC3B-II and P62 decreased after TRIM22 overexpression, indicating that TRIM22 overexpression can promote autophagic flux to a certain extent (Fig. 7A and B). In our previous study, we found that PRKCQ KD impaired lysosomal function. However, Western blot analysis demonstrated that the PRKCQ KD-induced downregulation of LAMP1 and LAMP2 expression remained unchanged following TRIM22 overexpression (Fig. 7C and D). Furthermore, the results of the LysoSensor Green DND-189 assay revealed that TRIM22 OE did not reverse the lysosomal activity induced by PRKCQ KD (Fig. 7E). Interestingly, the decrease in lysosomal proteolytic function induced by PRKCQ KD, as indicated by the results of the DQ-BSA assay, was reversed by TRIM22 OE (Fig. 7F). Although TRIM22 reversed the autophagic block caused by PRKCQ KD and increased the activity of proteolytic enzymes, it did not restore the pH changes induced by PRKCQ KD. These findings indicate that PRKCQ affects the proteolytic enzyme function of lysosomes through TRIM22. We found that autophagosome‒lysosome fusion is impaired after PRKCQ KD. To explore the function of TRIM22 in modulating the fusion between autophagosomes and lysosomes triggered by PRKCQ, we examined the colocalization of GFP-LC3B and LysoTracker Red. We found that TRIM22 overexpression reversed the decrease in the PCC between GFP-LC3B and LysoTracker Red induced by PRKCQ knockdown. Furthermore, compared with the control treatment, the overexpression of TRIM22 increased the PCC between GFP-LC3B and LysoTracker Red (Fig. 8A–C). Similarly, by analyzing the fluorescence intensity of RFP-GFP-LC3B and the ratio of autolysosomes in TRIM22-expressing cells (Fig. 8D–F), we confirmed that TRIM22 overexpression reversed the reduction in autophagosome‒lysosome fusion caused by PRKCQ KD. TRIM22 overexpression enhanced autophagosome-lysosome fusion efficiency beyond that observed in the control group. In summary, TRIM22 overexpression reversed the PRKCQ KD-mediated impairment of lysosomal function and inhibition of autophagosome‒lysosome fusion, rescuing autophagic flux blockage caused by PRKCQ KD in OSCC cells.

PRKCQ promotes the growth of OSCC xenograft models by increasing autophagic flux through the promotion of TRIM22 expression

In the nude mouse subcutaneous xenograft experiments, the tumor growth rate and tumor volume were notably lower in the PRKCQ KD group than in the control group, and these effects were significantly reversed by the overexpression of TRIM22 (Fig.9A–C). Additionally, compared with those in the control group, LC3B and P62 expression in the PRKCQ-KD group was significantly upregulated, whereas TRIM22, LAMP1, and LAMP2 expression was significantly downregulated, as determined by immunohistochemical analysis. Meanwhile, TRIM22 overexpression during PRKCQ KD decreased the expression of LC3B and P62 further and increased the downregulation of TRIM22 expression caused by PRKCQ KD alone but had no significant effect on the expression of LAMP1 or LAMP2 (Fig. 9D). Cathepsin B (CTSB) and cathepsin D (CTSD) are key proteolytic enzymes in lysosomes, and their activity and expression levels are important indicators for evaluating lysosomal degradation function. In our subsequent studies, we found that in mouse tumor tissues, the decrease in CTSB and CTSD expression caused by PRKCQ KD could be elevated by OE of TRIM22 (Fig. 9E and F). These results also demonstrate that the ectopic overexpression of TRIM22 rescues the inhibition of tumor growth and the blockade of autophagic flux caused by PRKCQ KD.

Discussion

Globally, the incidence of OSCC is increasing annually. Owing to its nonspecific early symptoms, it is easily overlooked or misdiagnosed [33, 34]. However, the treatment of advanced OSCC remains extremely challenging; thus, new therapeutic targets are urgently needed. We identified a new role for PRKCQ in controlling autophagy, further validating the critical role of the PKC family in autophagy. PRKCQ promotes autophagosome‒lysosome fusion and enhances lysosomal activity by mediating the activity of TRIM22, thereby facilitating the proliferation, migration, and invasion of OSCC cells through increasing autophagic flux.

Autophagy can promote the development and metastasis of OSCC. MAP3K11 directly phosphorylates the S383/S392 site of the core autophagy protein ATG4B, increasing its proteolytic activity and thereby promoting autophagosome formation and the activation of autophagic flux to facilitate the malignant progression of OSCC [7]. Autophagy-related biomarkers, including autophagy-associated genes and noncoding RNAs such as long noncoding RNAs, are associated with the diagnosis, clinical treatment, and prognosis prediction of OSCC patients [35]. Similarly, autophagy can reduce the sensitivity of OSCC to chemotherapeutic drugs such as cisplatin, increasing drug resistance. Furthermore, our previous studies revealed that autophagy promotes OSCC cell expansion and metastasis [8, 12, 36]. In line with these previous studies, we confirmed that autophagy promotes OSCC progression. Moreover, we further clarified the effects of lysosomal function on autophagy and autophagosome–lysosome fusion efficiency on the proliferation and invasion of OSCC cells.

The notable relationship between PRKCQ gene expression and malignant tumors has been fully confirmed in numerous studies. Several studies have indicated that PRKCQ also promotes tumor progression [24, 25, 37]. For example, some studies have explored the role of the PKC family in OSCC. One study revealed that high PKCβII expression was significantly correlated with betel nut consumption and alcohol intake in OSCC patients and that the time to recurrence was shorter in these patients [17]. Similarly, another study detected changes in the expression levels of different PKC isoforms in tissue extracts from 29 OSCC patients [38]. Our tissue microarray (TMA) analysis of samples from patients in our department revealed markedly higher PRKCQ expression in tumor tissues than in normal mucosal tissues; additionally, its expression was significantly increased in patients with lymph node metastasis (LNM) and was negatively correlated with the 5-year survival rate of OSCC patients. Through cellular and animal experiments, we found that PRKCQ can increase the proliferation and invasion of OSCC cells.

PRKCQ has a close and complex connection with autophagy. For example, PRKCQ can mediate the phosphorylation of Hippocalcin-like 1 (HPCAL1) at the Thr149 site, and its substrate selectivity is involved in ferroptosis-related autophagy [14]. In addition, from the perspective of regulatory pathways, in triple-negative breast cancer cells, the long noncoding RNA PRKCQ-AS1 can mediate autophagy through the miR-361-5p/PIK3C3 pathway [39]. However, these studies do not clarify the stage at which autophagy is influenced by PRKCQ to promote autophagy.

As a TRIM family protein, TRIM22 significantly influences the regulation of autophagy. Several roles of PRKCQ in autophagy, including the promotion of autophagy initiation and the fusion of autophagosomes and lysosomes, have been suggested. TRIM22 interacts with components of the ULK1 complex and class III PI3K complex through its different domains, recruiting them to autophagosome formation sites and thereby facilitating autophagy initiation [40]. TRIM22 enhances autophagosome‒lysosome integration via the GABARAP–PLEKHM1 interaction. Furthermore, TRIM22 mutants associated with early-onset Alzheimer’s disease can impede autophagosome‒lysosome fusion and affect intracellular clearance [41]. Similarly, TRIM22 enhances the proliferation and invasion abilities of many tumors. Some studies have reported that PKC induces the expression of TRIM22 through IFN-γ [42, 43]. Our transcriptomic study revealed that the expression of PRKCQ, an autophagy-linked gene, is strongly correlated with that of TRIM22. TRIM22 is crucial for autophagy regulation, and our findings revealed that its expression markedly decreased following PRKCQ knockdown in OSCC cells. To clarify the mechanisms of TRIM22 autophagy-promoting, we examined its effects on lysosomal proteolytic enzymes. In our experiment, we found that overexpression of TRIM22 did not exert a significant effect on lysosomal pH, yet still activated proteolytic enzymes. The pH of lysosomes is mainly determined by the active transport of proton pumps on the lysosomal membrane and is simultaneously regulated by the coordination of ion channels/transporters on the membrane [28, 44]. In contrast, the type, quantity, and function of lysosomal proteolytic enzymes are primarily governed by gene regulation within the cell nucleus [45]. The core reason for the increased activity of proteolytic enzymes without changes in lysosomal pH lies in the fact that the regulation of enzyme activity does not depend on the single factor of pH [46]. In fact, lysosomal pH mainly provides a suitable microenvironment for proteolytic enzymes, but it is not the decisive factor for triggering or enhancing their catalytic activity [47–49] Instead, it may occur through the proteolytic activation of zymogens or via post-translational modifications [17]. The underlying mechanism for this phenomenon will be further investigated in our future work. Additionally, TRIM22 upregulation alleviated the effects of PRKCQ knockdown on cell proliferation, invasive capacity, and migratory potential in OSCC cells. These results suggest that PRKCQ may regulate the progression of OSCC by modulating autophagic flux through TRIM22.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrated that the PRKCQ/TRIM22 axis regulates the progression of OSCC by modulating lysosomal function and promoting autophagosome‒lysosome fusion (Fig. 10). This research provides a novel molecular mechanism clarifying the role of autophagy in OSCC and identifies potential biomarkers for the diagnosis and treatment of OSCC, thereby contributing to the prevention and management of this disease.
