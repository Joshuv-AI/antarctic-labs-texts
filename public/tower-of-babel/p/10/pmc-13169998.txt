A finite element–based biomechanical analysis of fixation systems used in advancement genioplasty
Authors: Kadriye Ayça Dere, Yavuz Fındık
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to investigate the biomechanical behavior of different fixation systems used in advancement genioplasty using finite element analysis (FEA). The study aimed to provide numerical insight into stress distribution and displacement patterns under controlled loading conditions, rather than to evaluate clinical performance or long-term stability.MethodsA three-dimensional finite element model of a human mandible was reconstructed from the Visible Human Project dataset. Cortical and trabecular bone, fixation plates, and screws were modeled as homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic materials. Advancement genioplasty was simulated at different advancement distances, and fixation was achieved using various plate configurations. A static horizontal load of 100 N was applied to the distal segment. Stress distribution and displacement were evaluated for the fixation devices and surrounding bone structures.ResultsAs advancement increased, principal stress values in cortical and trabecular bone decreased in both L-plate and X-plate models due to increased plate length and material volume. X-plate models consistently produced higher stresses on bone, plates, and screws compared with L-plates. Bicortical screw fixation generated the lowest stress values in bone; however, increasing advancement reduced screw penetration into the proximal segment, increasing local stress concentrations. All plates stresses remained below the material’s yield strength.ConclusionsWithin the limitations of a simplified, static finite element model, the findings provide comparative biomechanical insight into the mechanical response of different fixation systems used in advancement genioplasty. The results should be interpreted as numerical trends rather than indicators of clinical superiority or long-term stability.Keywords: Genioplasty, Orthognathic surgery, Chin surgery, Bone plates, Bone screws, Finite element analysis

Introduction

The chin is a vital anatomical structure that plays a key role in the overall harmony and aesthetic appearance of the facial profile, contributing significantly to the uniqueness of an individual’s face [1]. Genioplasty is a common surgical procedure performed on the chin to correct dentofacial deformities. This technique enables three-dimensional control of chin positioning and enhances facial aesthetics by correcting abnormalities in the anterior region and body of the mandible across vertical, transverse, and anteroposterior dimensions [2]. Advancement genioplasty is one of the most commonly performed genioplasty techniques [3]. It is often performed simultaneously with other orthognathic surgical procedures to correct facial asymmetry or deformities [2].

In genioplasty procedures, ensuring the permanent stability of bone segments in their new positions following incision is of paramount importance. While wire osteosynthesis was initially widely used for fixation, it has been largely replaced by rigid fixation techniques because of its insufficient rigidity and the associated risk of relapse [1, 3–5]. Rigid fixation involves metal plates, screw systems, and various combinations thereof, and has been used reliably for many years [5]. Rigid internal fixation (RIF) is particularly recommended in cases where the inferior bone segment is advanced beyond 8–10 mm, as complication rates tend to increase with larger advancements. Postoperatively, the advanced genial segment is subjected to retraction forces from the suprahyoid muscles, including the anterior belly of the digastric muscle, the mylohyoid muscle, and the geniohyoid muscle, making adequate stabilization critical. Despite the widespread use of rigid fixation, debate continues regarding the optimal fixation material and configuration for genioplasty [1, 3]. The wide variety of plate and screw sizes and configurations make it challenging for surgeons to select the most appropriate system.

Since analyzing the complex biomechanical behavior of living tissues in vitro or in vivo is limited by ethical constraints, high costs, and practical challenges, Finite Element Analysis (FEA) is widely used in dentistry and maxillofacial surgery [5, 6]. FEA is a reliable method that transforms complex physical structures into simplified algebraic models and mathematically determines the stresses, displacements, and constraints within these models [7]. Recent FEA studies in dental and maxillofacial biomechanics have extensively examined how implant geometry, material properties, and loading conditions influence stress distribution within cortical and trabecular bone. Three-dimensional FEA investigations evaluating angled abutments, implant insertion mechanics, pilot drilling procedures, air abrasion techniques, and prosthetic material selection have consistently demonstrated that changes in design parameters and contact conditions significantly alter local stress concentrations in surrounding bone tissue [8–11]. Although these studies primarily focus on dental implantology rather than genioplasty, they provide valuable methodological insight into bone–implant interaction, load transfer mechanisms, and the biomechanical consequences of design optimization. FEA is widely used to address patient-specific modeling challenges associated with complex anatomical structures. Its capacity to customize computational simulations for individual patients by accounting for anatomical variations and diverse tissue properties makes it a highly effective tool for virtual surgical planning [12]. Recently, FEA has been used in conjunction with Virtual Surgical Planning (VSP) to maximize both surgical precision and biomechanical safety, particularly in complex jaw reconstructions. VSP employs computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD/CAM) technologies, enabling surgeons to simulate surgery on 3D models and prepare personalized cutting guides and plates [13]. Kim et al. utilized FEA to determine the optimal osteotomy geometry for improving stress distribution in reconstructed mandibles [14]. Wan et al. investigated intraoperative geometric errors during the reconstruction of large segmental bone defects in the maxillofacial region and examined the effects of loading patterns on the biomechanical behavior of reconstructed mandibles using FEA. The integration of these two technologies offers clinical benefits that enhance procedural success, including biomechanical risk analysis, hardware safety, personalized rehabilitation protocols, optimized bone healing, and improved process efficiency and accuracy [15]. The principles derived from these investigations form a relevant biomechanical framework for evaluating fixation systems used to stabilize osteotomized mandibular segments following genioplasty.

Although numerous studies have examined advancement genioplasty using finite element analysis (FEA), most have evaluated stress values in bone and fixation systems at only a single advancement distance. Since the advancement distance varies in each case and surgeons select different fixation materials, this study was designed to address this gap by analyzing three commonly used fixation systems across six different advancement distances. The aim of this study was to compare stress distribution induced by different types of fixation devices (plate and screw systems) used to stabilize bone segments repositioned after incisions at various advancement levels (3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 mm) during advancement genioplasty surgery, using FEA. This finite element study aims to provide biomechanical and numerical insight into the stress distribution of different fixation devices used in advancement genioplasty.

Materials and methods

Cortical and trabecular bone modeling

A generic mandible model derived from the Visible Human Project(National Library of Medicine, Fact Sheets Office of Communications and Public Liaison National Library of Medicine, Maryland, USA) was used to ensure geometric consistency, while acknowledging that patient-specific variability was not represented.To create the mandible bone model, tomography data from the Visible Human Project was reconstructed with a slice thickness of 0.33 mm. The CT dataset had a voxel size of 0.2 mm, providing sufficient spatial resolution for accurate three-dimensional reconstruction. The reconstructed tomography data were imported into 3DSlicer v5.0.3 (https://www.slicer.org/, Slicer Community, Boston, MA, USA) in DICOM (.dcm) format. The DICOM CT data were segmented using appropriate Hounsfield unit values in 3DSlicer v5.0.3 (threshold range: 426.50–3193.04). Unwanted regions and artifacts were removed using the ‘Erase’ and ‘Scissors’ parameters. The most challenging aspect of processing DICOM data was determining the appropriate threshold range and eliminating unwanted regions. The data, divided into appropriate segments, was converted into three-dimensional models through the segmentation process, and the models were exported in stl format. The three-dimensional models were imported into Blender v3.6 (Blender Foundation, Amsterdam, Netherlands), where the mandible bone model was first offset inward by 2 mm to create a cortical bone layer with a thickness of 2 mm [16]. The trabecular bone was obtained by referencing the inner surface of the three-dimensional mandibular cortical bone, with its thickness adjusted accordingly. The cortical bone was modeled as an outer shell surrounding the trabecular core, sharing common nodes at the interface to ensure load transfer continuity. The cut mandibular chin was then advanced anteriorly by 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 mm. All prepared models were positioned at the correct coordinates in 3D space within Blender v3.6, completing the modeling process.

Modeling and creating functional models of the l plate, x plate, bicortical screw, and fixation screw

The L and X plates were modeled using Blender v3.6, featuring a thickness of 2 mm and a hole diameter of 2 mm, based on data from the KLS Martin catalog (Gebrüder Martin GmbH & Co., Tuttlingen, Germany). The L plates were designed in small sizes with 3–4 mm advancement, medium sizes with 4–5 mm advancement, and large sizes with 7–8 mm advancement. X plates were designed with a step length equal to each advancement increment. Two L-shaped plates were placed to the right and left of the midline, and one X-shaped plate was positioned along the midline. The bicortical screw was modeled in Blender v3.6 with a length of 15 mm and a diameter of 2 mm. The fixation screws were modeled in Blender v3.6 with diameters of 7 mm and 2 mm (Fig. 1). To ensure effective force transfer between the models, the mesh structures were aligned using Altair HyperMesh v2023.2 (Altair Engineering, Troy, MI, USA). During the modeling phase, this software was used to align element sizes, element densities, and geometric continuity in the interface regions of adjacent components (for example, when the plate and screw have the same mesh size). This process aimed to prevent numerical discrepancies and artificial stress concentrations at the interfaces.

Material definitions

Cortical and trabecular bone were modeled as separate materials. All materials were assumed to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic. The elastic modulus and Poisson’s ratio of cortical bone, trabecular bone, titanium plates, and screws were adopted from previously published studies [17]. Titanium fixation devices were modeled using material properties corresponding to Ti-6Al-4 V alloy. The following assumptions were applied: (1) all materials behaved as linearly elastic; (2) perfect bonding was assumed between bone and fixation devices; (3) biological remodeling and time-dependent healing processes were not considered. These assumptions are commonly employed in comparative finite element analyses and allow isolation of the mechanical influence of fixation design while limiting model complexity. Linear material properties, including the elastic modulus and Poisson’s ratio, were used in the analyses [17]. The material properties used in the analysis sources are summarized in Table 2.

Obtaining mathematical models

After completing the modeling process in Blender v3.6, the models were mathematically generated using ALTAIR Hypermesh v2023.2 and prepared for analysis. Finite element discretization involves dividing geometric models into small, simple units called elements. The collection of these elements and nodes, including their connectivity, is commonly referred to as the mesh in finite element software. To achieve high accuracy and stability in the simulations, each surface was meshed using triangular (tria) elements with a refined element size ranging between 0.1 and 0.25 mm, enabling the accurate representation of complex anatomical structures. Triangular elements were selected due to their ability to accurately represent complex anatomical contours and irregular geometries of the mandible and fixation components. For volumetric meshing, linear tetrahedral (4-node) elements were used to discretize the mandible and fixation devices. Linear tetrahedral elements were selected to provide a balance between computational efficiency and geometric fidelity, particularly given the complex anatomy of the mandible and the high number of models analyzed. A mesh convergence study was conducted to ensure that this choice of element type and size produced numerically stable and accurate stress distributions at critical regions, including osteotomy lines and screw–bone interfaces.

Mesh convergence study

To ensure the reliability and accuracy of the finite element results, a mesh convergence study was conducted on a representative model. The mesh was progressively refined, and the resulting stress values at critical regions, including the osteotomy line and screw–bone interfaces, were monitored. Convergence was considered achieved when further refinement produced changes of less than 2% in maximum principal stress, minimum principal stress, and von Mises stress values. This procedure confirmed that the chosen element size (0.1–0.25 mm) provided sufficient resolution for all models without unnecessary computational cost, ensuring numerically stable and reliable stress distributions [18].

Once the surface meshes of all models were generated with triangular elements (tria), the solid meshes were created using tetrahedral (regular tetrahedral) elements. Both triangular surface meshes and tetrahedral volumetric elements were selected due to their suitability for complex anatomical geometries. To perform the analyses, the mathematical models created in ALTAIR HyperMesh were imported into the ALTAIR OptiStruct solver (Altair Engineering, Michigan, USA).

Loading scenarios and boundary conditions

In all models, a total horizontal force of 100 N was applied to the mandibular region. This force was evenly distributed across the entire surface, with 50 N applied from the right side and 50 N from the left side of the midline, directed toward the tongue. Although this loading condition does not represent physiological chewing, it was simulated to mimic the action of the suprahyoid muscle group [16]. Fully constrained boundary conditions were applied at the condyles to ensure numerical stability and prevent rigid body movement; however, this simplification does not replicate the physiological mechanics of the temporomandibular joint. To simulate postoperative functional loading, a horizontal force of 100 N was applied to the anterior mandibular segment, consisting of 50 N from the right side of the midline and 50 N from the left side, directed lingually and parallel to the occlusal plane. (Fig. 3). The direction and magnitude of the applied load were selected based on values reported in previous experimental and numerical studies on mandibular biomechanics. Although simplified, these boundary and loading conditions provide a controlled framework for the comparative evaluation of fixation configurations under consistent mechanical environments. System integration and intercomponent connections

To conduct analyses using the developed mathematical models and obtain accurate results, the surface interactions of the components must be defined within the analysis program. A slide-type contact definition was implemented at the interface between the proximal and distal segments. In all analyses, a freeze-type contact was defined between the contacting components. This assumption was adopted to represent an idealized state of complete osseointegration and to ensure numerical stability of the finite element model.

In all models, the contact between screws and bone was defined as bonded, assuming full osseointegration between the fixation devices and the underlying bone, consistent with previous finite element studies that adopt a fully osseointegrated interface to represent ideal load transfer conditions [19].

The horizontal force, representing the muscle force applied at the pogonion point, did not induce bending at the bending point of the distal segment and plates. The distal segment is forced to slide horizontally over the proximal segment. Therefore, increasing the plate length due to advancement resulted in a more uniform distribution of stress across the extended plate and a reduction in peak stress values. Figures 4 and 5 shows the stress distributions in 2 L plates and screws, while Figs. 6 and 7 shows the stress distributions in X plates and screws. It was observed that the stresses generated in the L-plate model, positioned bilaterally on the right and left sides of the mandible, were lower than those in the X-plate model. With a 3 mm advancement, the highest von Mises stress value generated in the X plate was 248.088 MPa, and the maximum principal stress value in the proximal segment cortical bone was 42.132 MPa, while the highest von Mises stress value generated in the L plate was 185.974 MPa, and the maximum principal stress value in the proximal segment cortical bone was 18.659 MPa. With an 8 mm advancement, the highest von Mises stress value generated in the X plate was 189.091 MPa, and the maximum principal stress value in the proximal segment cortical bone was 31.458 MPa, while the highest von Mises stress value generated in the L plate was 150.478 MPa, and the maximum principal stress value in the proximal segment cortical bone was 16.644 MPa. The L-plates, positioned on the right and left sides, distributed stress over a wider area but provided greater resistance to jaw opening than the X-plate. In the models with plate application, the general trend in stress distribution on the plate was similar to that observed on the screws. The maximum stress values on the screws were found near the incision line, with a particular concentration in the neck region of the screw. It was determined that the maximum stress values on the plates used in the study did not exceed the yield strength of Ti-6Al-4 V alloy, approximately 880 MPa (Table 3). The maximum and minimum principal stresses observed in the bone tissue are expected to be concentrated around the screw hole, which is consistent with the analysis results. When examining the maximum and minimum principal stresses in the proximal segment cortical bone of the L and X plate models, a decrease in stress values was observed as the distal segment advanced anteriorly. A similar trend was observed in the distal segment. Stress levels were observed to be lower in trabecular bone compared to cortical bone. Furthermore, it was observed that the maximum and minimum principal stresses in the trabecular structure decreased as the advancement increased (Figs. 4, 5 and 6, and 7). The use of bicortical screws was found to significantly reduce stress levels compared to both the L and X plate models. However, in the bicortical screw model, moving the incision line away from the proximal region resulted in an increased local stress distribution in the bone. In screw fixation models, the maximum principal stress value in the cortical bone of the proximal segment was found to be 1.985 MPa with a 3 mm advancement, 3.039 MPa with a 5 mm advancement, and 5.409 MPa with an 8 mm advancement. This increase is attributed to stress distributions arising from geometric changes in the contact area of the proximal segment. In the bicortical screw model, advancement resulted in significant changes in the screw contact area, particularly within the proximal cortical and trabecular bone. Table 3; Figs. 8 and 9 shows the stress distributions in screws. This resulted in stress singularities in certain advancement scenarios. Therefore, the stress sequences and trends in the proximal regions of this model differ from those in the plate models. In bicortical screws, stress levels gradually increased because of reduced penetration into the proximal segment as a result of advancement.

From a biomechanical standpoint, the magnitude of the calculated stress values remained within ranges commonly considered acceptable for both bone tissue and titanium fixation systems. The maximum principal stresses observed in cortical bone and the compressive stresses in trabecular bone did not exceed reported physiological or failure-related thresholds for mandibular bone under static loading conditions. Similarly, the von Mises stresses calculated for all plates and screws remained well below the reported yield strength of Ti-6Al-4 V alloy. These findings indicate that, under the applied loading scenario, none of the fixation configurations reached stress levels associated with immediate material or bone failure.

These findings describe relative biomechanical behavior under standardized loading conditions and do not imply clinical superiority of any fixation system.

Discussion

A balanced facial appearance has long been recognized as a key determinant of facial harmony, and the morphology of the chin plays an essential role in this balance. Any discrepancies in this area can be corrected through orthognathic surgery procedures. One such procedure is genioplasty, which can be performed alone or in combination with other surgical techniques to modify the size, shape, and position of the chin. It is performed to correct dentofacial deformities in the sagittal, vertical, and anteroposterior planes of the symphysis region, enhance facial balance, improve the profile, treat respiratory disorders such as obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, and solve psychosocial issues related to the perception of gender and character based on facial profile [1, 2, 20, 21]. After determining the planes and the extent of positional change to be made at the chin tip during genioplasty, various fixation systems can be selected to stabilize the bone segment in its new position. For the procedure to be successful, these systems must prevent movement of the bone segments caused by muscle pull, provide stability, resist applied forces and deformation, and avoid generating high stress levels on the bone segments [17, 22]. In light of this information, this study aimed to compare the various fixation devices used to secure bone segments repositioned after incisions at different advancement levels during advancement genioplasty surgery, as well as to analyze the stress distribution they generate on the fixation devices and bone using FEA. The biomechanical approach adopted in the present study shares a common methodological foundation with recent finite element investigations conducted in implant dentistry, including analyses of angled abutments, implant insertion dynamics, drilling protocols, and air abrasion–induced stress distributions [8–11]. These studies emphasize that implant geometry, material stiffness, and bone–implant contact conditions play a critical role in determining stress concentration patterns and load transmission pathways within cortical and trabecular bone. Similarly, in advancement genioplasty, fixation plates and screws function as load-bearing implants that mediate force transfer between repositioned bone segments and the native mandible under postoperative muscular loading. In present study, FEA was chosen because its results closely align with biomechanical test data and because it enables researchers to obtain more accurate outcomes by simplifying complex boundary conditions when comparing different fixation systems [23].

Recent advances in computational design suggest that fixation plates used in maxillofacial surgery can be further optimized through modern biomechanical modeling techniques. In particular, topology optimization has been applied to the design of patient-specific osteosynthesis plates in order to redistribute material within a predefined design space and improve mechanical efficiency. Park et al. demonstrated that topology-based optimization enables the development of customized fixation plates with improved stiffness-to-weight ratios and more homogeneous stress distribution compared with conventional plate geometries. Such approaches allow the implant design to be adapted to patient-specific anatomical and mechanical requirements, potentially reducing localized stress concentrations around screw holes and osteotomy lines. In addition to topology-based strategies, recent studies have also explored the use of porous or structurally optimized fixation plates that account for the biological aspects of bone healing [24]. Using computational modeling and finite element–based optimization, Mehboob et al. reported that porous mandibular fixation plates can be designed to achieve improved load transfer while simultaneously promoting favorable mechanical conditions for bone regeneration. Their results indicated that optimized porous architectures may reduce stress shielding and create a more physiologically relevant mechanical environment at the bone–implant interface [25]. Taken together, these findings indicate that fixation plates used in mandibular procedures are amenable to advanced computational optimization strategies. Although the present study evaluated commonly used commercially available plate systems, future investigations may benefit from incorporating topology optimization and porous structural design approaches to develop patient-specific fixation plates with improved biomechanical performance and potentially enhanced clinical outcomes.

Although advancement genioplasty is considered a reliable surgical procedure with only minor recurrence rates, factors such as the method of fixation, the magnitude of surgical movement, age, gender, and the occurrence of complications may influence the postoperative recurrence rate [26, 27]. The tendency of the distal segment to migrate downward and posteriorly due to the pull of the suprahyoid muscles and peri mandibular connective tissue can result in potential bony instability and undesirable resorption when rigid fixation is not employed [1, 2, 28–30]. Maximum stabilization is essential for the successful healing of the separated segments. Numerous fixation techniques for these segments have been described in the literature and compared in various studies. Polido et al., Shaughnessy et al., and Shaik et al. compared wire and plate systems; Reyneke et al. compared wire and screw systems; Ramos et al. compared different plate systems; Lee et al. compared metal plates and resorbable plate systems; Yurtyapan et al. compared metal and resorbable screw systems; Aktı et al. and Gülsever et al. compared various plate and screw systems; and Akkoyun et al. compared custom plates [1, 3, 16, 17, 26, 29, 31–34]. However, all these studies evaluated fixation systems at a single advancement value. Addressing this gap in the literature, the most commonly used X-plate, L-plate, and screw systems in surgical practice were assessed through 18 separate analyses across six different advancement levels to identify the most effective fixation technique for genioplasty procedures requiring rigid fixation and stabilization.

In the present study, the advancement amounts were set at 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 mm. A review of genioplasty studies in the literature revealed that they typically used a single advancement value, most commonly 5–8 mm. These values may be selected because advancements exceeding 5 mm have been shown to increase relapse rates, while complication rates and the risk of bone resorption tend to rise with advancements beyond 8–10 mm [1, 3, 21]. The present study demonstrated that in models using 2 L and X-plates, as the advancement increased from 3 mm to 8 mm, the maximum and minimum principal stress values on the cortical and trabecular bone of the proximal and distal segments, as well as on the plate and screw surfaces, decreased. The decrease in bone stress observed in plate models with increasing advancement appears to contradict clinical studies reporting higher recurrence rates with larger advancements. This discrepancy may be explained by the difference between instantaneous mechanical stress and the multifactorial biological mechanisms responsible for relapse. However, this discrepancy may arise from the difference between the biomechanical interpretation of stress measurements and the multifactorial nature of clinical recurrence. This study evaluated mechanical stress distribution during short-term static loading under rigid fixation. The use of longer and bulkier plates in cases of greater advancement, which distribute the load over a wider area, resulted in reduced stresses. In contrast, clinical recurrence is influenced by dynamic factors such as soft tissue tensile forces, muscle adaptation, bone resorption, the quality of contact at osteotomy surfaces, micromobility during the healing process, and patient-specific biological responses. Therefore, the increased recurrence observed with greater advancement cannot be explained solely by instantaneous mechanical stresses. Our findings demonstrate that biomechanically optimized fixation design can reduce stresses even with larger advancements, but this does not entirely eliminate the risk of recurrence. Consequently, we believe that mechanical optimization should be considered alongside biological healing and soft tissue strength. In all advancement values, the maximum principal stress values in the tensile direction and the minimum principal stress values in the compressive direction were highest in the cortical bones of the proximal and distal segments in the study models equipped with an X-plate. Similarly, the highest von Mises stress values on the plate and screws were observed in the X-plate and the 2.0-mm mini screws securing it to the segments. These results may be attributed to the greater contact between the bone and fixation materials in the two L-plate and two-screw models compared to the X-plate models, as well as the distribution of muscle traction forces across these materials. Specifically, the X-plate was secured with four screws along the midline, whereas the L-plate was fixed with four screws positioned bilaterally, one on each side of the midline. This arrangement allowed for a more effective distribution of forces across the fixation elements. Similarly, in models utilizing screws, the use of double-sided 15 mm screws, increased surface contact, and bicortical engagement contributed to reduced stress values. In all study models, the maximum and minimum principal stress values in the compression direction were higher in cortical bone than in trabecular bone. This difference can be attributed to the fact that the two types of bone have different elastic moduli and offer varying resistance during screw placement. With respect to fixation materials, the von Mises stress values calculated for titanium alloy plates and screws were well below the reported yield strength of Ti-6Al-4 V (approximately 880–900 MPa), indicating a low risk of acute material failure under static loading. However, localized regions of stress concentration, especially around screw necks and adjacent to the osteotomy line, are clinically significant because they are recognized as initiation sites for fatigue-related failures under cyclic functional loading. Experimental and clinical studies have shown that even when peak stresses remain below yield strength, repeated masticatory loading can lead to progressive microcrack formation and eventual plate or screw fracture, especially in designs with unfavorable stress distribution [35, 36]. Accordingly, fixation systems that demonstrated lower and more uniformly distributed stress patterns in this study may be expected to exhibit improved long-term mechanical performance. From a clinical perspective, the numerical stress values obtained in this study should be interpreted in relation to established biomechanical thresholds reported for bone tissue and commonly used titanium fixation systems. Cortical bone is known to tolerate compressive stresses on the order of 130–200 MPa and tensile stresses approximately between 80 and 120 MPa before microdamage accumulation and failure become likely, whereas trabecular bone exhibits substantially lower tolerance limits [37, 38]. In the present analyses, the maximum and minimum principal stresses observed in both cortical and trabecular bone remained below these commonly cited physiological and failure-related thresholds across all fixation configurations, suggesting that none of the evaluated systems would be expected to induce immediate bone failure under the applied loading conditions.

In all fixation configurations, stress concentrations were primarily located around the osteotomy line and the screw–bone interfaces, particularly near the screw neck regions and the cortical bone surrounding the screw holes. These regions represent structural discontinuities where load transfer between the fixation system and the mandibular bone occurs. From a mechanical standpoint, rigid fixation components act as load-bearing implants that transmit muscular forces applied to the distal segment toward the proximal mandibular body. Consequently, localized stress concentrations are expected at the plate–screw junctions and adjacent cortical bone due to abrupt changes in geometry, stiffness mismatch between titanium and bone, and the limited contact area around the fixation elements. Similar stress concentration patterns around screw interfaces and osteotomy borders have been reported in previous finite element analyses of orthognathic surgery and mandibular fixation systems. To ensure that these stress peaks did not arise from numerical artifacts, mesh quality was carefully evaluated during model preparation. Particular attention was given to the element geometry in regions where maximum stresses were detected. The aspect ratios of the tetrahedral elements in these critical areas remained within commonly accepted ranges for biomechanical finite element analyses, indicating that the observed stress concentrations were not caused by excessively distorted elements but instead reflect realistic mechanical responses of the modeled structures. Another important observation is that the location of the highest stress values remained generally consistent across the different advancement magnitudes investigated in this study. Although the absolute magnitude of stresses varied depending on the advancement distance and fixation configuration, the peak stresses were consistently located near the fixation interfaces and the osteotomy margins. This finding suggests that these anatomical regions represent intrinsic biomechanical weak points of the system, where load transfer is concentrated regardless of the magnitude of anterior displacement. Different fixation configurations influenced primarily the magnitude and distribution pattern of stresses rather than completely changing the critical stress locations. Plate systems tended to distribute stresses over a wider surface area due to the extended contact between the plate and the cortical bone, whereas screw-only fixation concentrated stresses more locally around the screw–bone interface. Consequently, although the highest stress regions were generally similar across configurations, the degree of stress concentration varied depending on fixation rigidity, screw positioning, and load-sharing characteristics of each system.

While preoperative 3D planning is highly beneficial, the final decision to use plate and/or screw osteosynthesis is typically made intraoperatively in the operating room. Pre-bent chin plates have become increasingly popular due to their ease of use, and fixation with a single chin plate placed at the midline of the symphysis is generally preferred [1, 27, 31]. However, the results of the present study demonstrated that while single plate placement in the midline is an easy and rapid intraoperative solution, it places greater stress on the bone, plate, and screws at all advancement rates than other fixation options. This situation can lead to long-term stress accumulation and deformation in the plates and screws, causing a risk such as displacement of the distal segment. Screw osteosynthesis is also an option used in genioplasty; however, the applicability of this technique depends on the type of osteotomy and the direction of movement of the genial segment. Once the distal segment is mobilized, preparing the screw holes for the two screws and placing them while maintaining intraoperative segmental stability can be relatively challenging [31]. The results of the present study showed that, despite placement difficulties, the fixation system causing the least stress on the bone was two-screw fixation. However, increasing advancement distances resulted in reduced screw engagement within the proximal segment, leading to localized stress amplification. From a clinical standpoint, such stress concentration may predispose the bone–screw interface to microdamage, screw loosening, or early fixation instability, particularly during the initial postoperative period before complete bone healing has occurred. These findings emphasize the importance of maintaining sufficient bicortical purchase and appropriate screw length selection when screw-only fixation strategies are employed. When adequate bicortical contact in both the proximal and distal segments was achieved using appropriately sized screws, this fixation system transmitted the least stress to the surrounding bone. In the present study, the L and X plates used were not manually bent to match the amount of advancement; instead, pre-bent plate systems from KLS Martin, designed to correspond to the advancement amounts, were utilized. Although these plates fit the chin anatomy perfectly in the study, their fit in clinical practice with commercially available plates remains uncertain. In most cases, contouring and bending of mandibular reconstruction and mini plates are unavoidable during surgery to adapt them to the patient’s specific mandibular and maxillary geometry. During this bending process, elastoplastic deformation occurs in the plate, resulting in irreversible residual stress areas after the load is removed. Following surgical placement, tightening the screws presses the plate against the bone surface, causing additional stress concentrations, particularly around the screw holes. Consequently, the stress areas generated by bending and screwing overlap, producing clinically significant residual stress within the system. Studies have shown that, in most cases, not only bending-related stresses but also preloads generated during screwing are more critical. The superposition of stresses from these two sources causes the maximum principal stress to approach critical thresholds in certain regions, even when functional loads such as mastication remain below the material’s yield strength. Zones favorable for crack initiation form, especially in segments adjacent to defect boundaries and around screw holes, thereby facilitating the earlier onset of plate fractures through fatigue mechanisms [35, 36]. Modeling results indicate that ignoring residual stress leads to systematic overestimation of plate strength, whereas accounting for residual stress significantly lowers the crack initiation load and considerably reduces fatigue life. These findings demonstrate that the combined evaluation of elastoplastic deformation from manual contouring and preloads caused by excessive screwing is essential for explaining plate fractures observed in clinical practice. Therefore, minimizing residual stress through patient-specific plate design, virtual surgical planning, and controlled screwing strategies can provide a safer biomechanical environment with respect to both static fracture and fatigue failure [39].

Biomechanical modeling of genioplasty procedures is inherently complex due to anatomical variations, the dynamic nature of muscle forces, and the time-dependent properties of living tissues. Therefore, certain simplifications were applied in our finite element model to reliably represent the fundamental load-bearing behavior of the system and to enable comparison of the relative effects of different fixation options. Loading conditions were standardized based on horizontal functional forces commonly reported in the literature for genioplasty and mandibular reconstruction. The condylar regions were fixed with rigid boundary conditions, and fixation system–bone interfaces were modeled using contact-dependent (slide/freeze) interactions to simulate the stable post-healing state. Although this approach does not fully capture inter-individual biological variability, it provides a reproducible and comparable mechanical environment representative of the masticatory forces encountered clinically [5, 6, 16]. As part of model validation, the maximum and minimum principal stress distributions and von Mises stress fields in the plates and screws were analyzed. The high-stress regions, especially around the screws and adjacent to the osteotomy line, corresponded with trends observed in previously published experimental, computational, and finite element studies [7, 15, 17]. Parametric sensitivity analyses demonstrated that reasonable variations in contact conditions and load magnitudes did not alter the qualitative trends of the results, only causing predictable differences in absolute values. However, soft tissue effects, the dynamic nature of muscle activation, the time-dependent biomechanical response during healing, and bone remodeling could not be fully incorporated into the model. Furthermore, the loading scenario represents a simplified approximation of functional forces and may not fully reflect individual occlusal habits. Therefore, rather than providing absolute predictions of fracture or relapse, the model offers an appropriate framework for identifying risk zones and comparing different fixation configurations [6, 23, 31]. Consequently, the simplifications employed constitute a rational modeling strategy that isolates the primary mechanical factors affecting segment stability after genioplasty and facilitates comparison of fixation systems. Nevertheless, the limitations inherent in these assumptions are clearly acknowledged. Future studies are planned to incorporate patient-specific loading data, experimental validation, and time-dependent biological processes.

From a biomechanical perspective, the stress distribution patterns observed in the present simulations provide insight into regions of mechanical vulnerability associated with different fixation configurations. Localized stress concentrations were consistently observed around the osteotomy line and the screw–bone interfaces, identifying these regions as critical zones that may influence mechanical behavior under loading conditions. The primary contribution of this study lies in the systematic comparison of multiple commonly used fixation designs under identical loading and boundary conditions while accounting for varying advancement magnitudes. By quantifying stress patterns within both the fixation components and the mandibular bone, the present finite element analysis establishes a comparative mechanical framework that may assist in fixation strategy selection by highlighting configurations associated with lower stress concentrations, rather than implying clinical superiority or long-term stability.

This study has several limitations. A major limitation of this study is the use of a generic mandible model derived from the Visible Human Project. While this model provides standardized anatomy and high geometric fidelity, it represents a normal mandible without clinical indication for genioplasty. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as theoretical biomechanical guidance rather than clinical evidence. Patient-specific anatomical variations, which are increasingly emphasized in virtual surgical planning and individualized medicine, were not captured in this model. Second, real bone tissue is dynamic, living, heterogeneous, and anisotropic, meaning that its elastic properties vary depending on the direction. To simplify the analysis, all materials in this study (bone, plates, and screws) were assumed to be homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic. These assumptions do not fully capture the mechanical behavior of actual bone. Third, under clinical conditions, contact between bone and fixation materials (plates and screws) is never complete. However, consistent with previous studies, and to simplify the analyses while facilitating comparison, this study assumed full compatibility, meaning 100% contact, between the materials and the bone. Fourth, because the primary purpose of the study was to compare fixation systems, other muscle attachments and ligament structures were omitted to simplify the complex boundary conditions. For example, the movement of the hyoid bone, which is connected to the suprahyoid muscles during swallowing, chewing, and mouth opening, was not considered due to its distinct and complex mechanics. Fifth, individual anatomical variations, including race, gender, bone quality, and mandibular plane angle, can influence outcomes. Conducting the study using a single standardized model overlooks these variations, which can cause data to differ significantly between patients. Therefore, the generalizability of the results may be limited. Future investigations incorporating patient-specific geometries, dynamic loading protocols, nonlinear and anisotropic material models, realistic contact definitions, and experimental or clinical validation are necessary to further substantiate the biomechanical relevance of different fixation strategies in advancement genioplasty.

Conclusion

This finite element study comparatively evaluated the mechanical behavior of different fixation strategies used in advancement genioplasty across increasing advancement distances. The results demonstrated that fixation configurations incorporating plate-based systems exhibited more favorable stress distribution patterns within both the fixation devices and the surrounding bone when compared with screw-only fixation, particularly at larger advancement distances.

Several important limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the finite element analysis was performed using a single generic mandibular model derived from reference anatomical data, which does not capture inter-individual variability in mandibular morphology, bone density, age, or sex. Therefore, the results cannot be directly extrapolated to patient-specific clinical scenarios. Second, the simulations were conducted under static, linear loading conditions with a simplified force vector, which does not represent the complex, time-dependent, and multidirectional forces generated by masticatory muscles or functional loading during postoperative healing. Third, bone tissue was modeled as homogeneous, isotropic, and linearly elastic, and perfect bonded contact was assumed at the bone–fixation interfaces, neglecting potential micromotion, interface compliance, and viscoelastic behavior observed in vivo. In addition, no experimental, clinical, or literature-based validation of the numerical results was performed. Consequently, the reported stress and displacement values should be interpreted as relative, comparative biomechanical indicators under controlled conditions rather than absolute predictors of clinical stability, relapse risk, or long-term outcomes.
