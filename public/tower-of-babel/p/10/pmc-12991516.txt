Analysis of the Effect of Dental Implant Therapy on Serum Metal Ion Levels: An Observational Study
Authors: Moez I. Khakiani, Ruby Bhatti, Anshul Gangwar, Ravneet K. Dhaliwal, Agamgeet K. Maan, Sibgutulah Rashid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The research was carried out for the evaluation of serum metal ion concentrations in dental implant subjects.Material and Methods:The study comprised 50 individuals, with 26 being male and 24 being female. Before inserting the implant, serum samples had been taken from every individual to determine the initial amounts of titanium and aluminum ions. Each patient had 10 milliliters of blood extracted into sterile vacutainers, without the addition of any anticoagulant. The blood had been permitted to coagulate precisely for 20 minutes at room temperature, and thereafter subjected to centrifugation to get serum. Prolonged incubation could potentially lead to the introduction of external contaminants. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) was employed to evaluate the samples for titanium and aluminum. Subsequently, implants were inserted via a flapless approach. Serum samples were obtained on follow-up and results were assessed.Results:Mean titanium amounts prior to placement of dental implants were 3.24 mg/dl. The mean titanium levels at six weeks, three months, and six months following implant placement were 3.26 mg/dl, 3.29 mg/dl, and 3.31 mg/dl, respectively. Mean aluminum levels before placement of dental implants were 4.21 mg/dl. The mean titanium levels at six weeks, three months, and six months following implant placement were 4.25 mg/dl, 4.27 mg/dl, and 4.3 mg/dl, respectively.Conclusion:No effect in serum titanium and aluminum levels occurs after dental implant placement.

INTRODUCTION

Dental implantation refers to a surgical procedure involving the jawbone, aimed at offering support for various dental prosthetics, such as crowns, bridges, dentures, and facial prostheses. The cornerstone of contemporary dental implantation is osseointegration, which denotes the direct structural as well as functional relationship between living bone and the surface of a load-bearing implant. Implants play a crucial role in securing auricular, mandibular, maxillary, nasal, and orbital prosthetics, as well as in the application of bone-anchored hearing aids. Initially, the implant fixture is positioned to promote osseointegration, after which a dental prosthesis is subsequently affixed. The process of osseointegration necessitates a variable healing period prior to the placement of a crown, denture, or abutment that will support the dental prosthesis. Traditional implant protocols typically recommend a waiting period between tooth extraction and the placement of the implant, thereby segmenting the treatment into two distinct phases.[1]

The utilization of dental implants for the replacement of prosthetic teeth has become increasingly significant over the past 20 years, with implants now being the primary treatment method for edentulism.[2] The majority of implants are constructed from titanium or titanium alloys, chosen for their low density, favorable mechanical properties, and ability to osseointegrate.[3] Nevertheless, concerns have been raised regarding the release of metal ions from implants and its potential clinical implications. Various researchers have investigated the release of titanium from different orthopedic and dental implants. For instance, Levine et al.[4] conducted a decade-long follow-up study on subjects who had undergone total hip arthroplasty, finding notably elevated levels of cobalt, chromium, and titanium in these patients contrasted to controls. Conversely, Bianco et al.[5] examined the variance in serum and urine titanium levels prior to as well as following implant positioning in rabbits, ultimately determining that there had been no considerable increase.[5]

MATERIAL AND METHODS

The study comprised 50 individuals, with 26 being male and 24 being female. Before inserting the implant, serum samples had been taken from every individual to determine the initial amounts of titanium and aluminum ions. Each patient had 10 milliliters of blood extracted into sterile vacutainers, without the addition of any anticoagulant. The blood had been permitted to coagulate precisely for 20 minutes at room temperature, and thereafter subjected to centrifugation to get serum. Prolonged incubation could potentially lead to the introduction of external contaminants. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) was employed to evaluate the samples for titanium and aluminum. Subsequently, implants were inserted via a flapless approach. Serum samples were obtained on follow-up and results were assessed.

DISCUSSION

The utilization of titanium implants in dentistry has significantly increased as a means to substitute natural dentition, primarily because of their exceptional biocompatibility. Typically, titanium or titanium mixtures are utilized in the composition of these implants, owing to their mechanical properties and biocompatibility.[6] Research suggests that titanium dental implants have the ability to withstand exposure to the oral environment for over 20 years.

The biocompatibility demonstrated by dental implants is believed to be a result of the formation of a titanium dioxide layer, which acts as a barrier preventing direct contact between the implant and the biological surroundings.[7] Consequently, this minimizes the likelihood of metal reactivity. The success and longevity of dental implants are reliant on their ability to integrate with both hard and soft tissues. Presently, researchers are exploring various methods to accelerate the osseointegration process of dental implants and enhance the surface area of contact between the implant and bone. This is achieved by increasing the osseointegration surface areas through the creation of irregularities on the surfaces of materials employed for dental implants.[8] To investigate the interactions between dental implants and biological tissues, it is necessary to apply a layer of titanium dioxide (TiO2). During periods of increased stress, the TiO2 layer is susceptible to harm caused by the motion between the implant and bone tissue, resulting in corrosion of the implant and consequent loss of strength. Moreover, corrosion can cause the liberation of minute metallic particles or ions into the adjacent biological tissues.[9]

In this research, mean titanium amounts prior to placement of dental implants were 3.24 mg/dl. The mean titanium levels at six weeks, three months, and six months following implant placement were 3.26 mg/dl, 3.29 mg/dl, and 3.31 mg/dl, respectively. Mean aluminum levels before placement of dental implants were 4.21 mg/dl. The mean titanium levels at six weeks, three months, and six months following implant placement were 4.25 mg/dl, 4.27 mg/dl, and 4.3 mg/dl, respectively. Gopi G et al.[10] conducted a study to assess the secretion of titanium, aluminum, and vanadium from dental implants by comparing serum ion levels before and after the procedure. Serum samples had been obtained from 30 subjects at various intervals: preoperatively and postoperatively at six weeks, three months, six months, as well as 12 months after dental implant placement. The results indicated a minor rise in postoperative titanium and aluminum concentrations (2.30 and 4.07 mg/dl, respectively) contrasted to preoperative amounts (2.28 and 2.30 mg/dl), although these differences were not statistically significant. Furthermore, the serum amounts of vanadium had been found to be quite low for detection by the analytical instrument. While a slight elevation in titanium and aluminum amounts in serum had been observed, the authors suggest that further clinical investigations with larger cohorts as well as extended follow-up periods are crucial to evaluate the clinical implications of metallic ion release from dental implants. Overall, the study concluded that there is no substantial variation in serum metal ion quantities prior to as well as following implant positioning, despite a minor rise in aluminum ion concentrations postoperatively. Sharanappa M et al.[11] conducted a study comparing titanium serum levels pre- and postdental implant placement, as well as between subjects with healthy implants and those with peri-implantitis. The research involved two groups: group 1, consisting of patients undergoing dental implant surgery, and group 2, comprising patients diagnosed with peri-implantitis, with every consisting of 60 subjects. Blood samples had been collected from group 1 patients at three distinct time points, while samples were obtained from group 2 patients during peri-implantitis using inductively coupled plasma mass spectroscopy to measure serum titanium levels. Statistical analysis was conducted on the data collected. The results showed an increase in serum titanium concentrations at the fourth month after the implant was placed (2.39 mg/dl) and among subjects with peri-implantitis (2.94 mg/dl), both of which were substantially distinct from the serum titanium levels before surgery (1.79 mg/dl). Having a comprehensive grasp of the connection between titanium corrosion and peri-implantitis is essential for enhancing the durability and safety of dental implants in the long run. Additional investigation is required to examine these associations and formulate appropriate tactics to protect the welfare of individuals with dental implants.

CONCLUSION

No effect in serum titanium and aluminum levels occurs after dental implant placement.
