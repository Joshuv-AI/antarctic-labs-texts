Background

The spleen is the most frequently injured solid organ in the abdomen, accounting for approximately 20% to 40% of closed abdominal injuries and about 10% of open abdominal injuries. Common causes include traffic accidents, falls from heights, and crush injuries. Due to its rich vascularization and delicate tissue structure, traumatic splenic rupture often results in hemorrhagic shock, which can be life-threatening. [1]

Current treatment options for traumatic splenic rupture (TSR) encompass surgical intervention and non-operative splenic preservation therapy. The severity of TSR is influenced by factors such as the mechanism of injury, bleeding rate, volume of blood loss, and the body's stress response. Hemodynamically stable patients typically present with mild injuries, while severe cases may manifest as hypotension due to significant blood loss, along with multi-organ damage or peritonitis. Traditionally, splenectomy via laparotomy has been the standard treatment; however, due to extended recovery periods and potential impacts on immune function, conservative non-operative management is increasingly recommended to preserve splenic function. Nonetheless, in instances of hemorrhagic shock where non-operative treatment proves ineffective, prompt surgical intervention is essential to ensure patient safety. [2], [3], [4]

To date, there have been no reports of a complete cure for hemorrhage caused by traumatic rupture of the spleen using Chinese herbal medicine. In this case report, we present a patient who experienced hemorrhage due to splenic rupture and achieved a full recovery through self-administration of oral and topical Chinese herbs following a collision injury. The patient provided written informed consent for the publication of this report and the accompanying images.

Case report

A 54-year-old male patient, Han nationality, accidentally fell from a height of about 3 m while collecting herbs on a mountain. The patient landed on the left side of his chest and abdomen, leading to persistent pain and discomfort. He self-administered oral herbal soup and topical Chinese herbal medicine initially, but his symptoms worsened, including bloating, general weakness, dizziness, chest tightness, and fever (maximum temperature of 38.5 °C). Two days after the injury, the patient underwent chest and upper and lower abdominal computed tomography scans, which revealed multiple rib fractures on the left side, splenic contusions, and a significant accumulation of blood and fluid in the abdominal and pelvic cavities (Fig. 1). Despite the severity of the injuries, the patient opted not to be hospitalized and continued to manage his condition at home with oral and topical Chinese herbal medicine. Five days later, due to persistent symptoms, the patient's family insisted on hospitalization for a comprehensive examination. At this point, the patient reported no significant abdominal pain or bloating, but he experienced pain when changing position. Upon physical examination, the patient appeared anemic, exhibited pale nail beds on the lips and bilateral nails, and had visible Chinese herbs on the left side of the chest. The continuous decrease in red blood cells. Chest squeeze test results were positive, and there was significant tenderness when palpating the abdomen. However, there was no rebound pain or muscle tightness. Mobile opacities and fluid wave oscillations were detected. The patient underwent multiple surgeries, blood draws, and imaging procedures and a diagnostic laparotomy revealed non-clotted blood. Blood sampling indicated inflammation, with a hemoglobin concentration of only 78 g/L. Abdominal CT still showed a considerable accumulation of blood/fluid in the abdominal and pelvic cavities (Fig. 2). The patient's vital signs remained stable, and he was provided with the general treatment, including symptomatic support and nutrition, while continuing to take oral and topical Chinese herbal medicine. After a four-day hospitalization, the patient's condition showed marked improvement, prompting consideration for discharge. Upon discharge, the patient's vital signs were stable. A physical examination revealed mild pain in the left chest wall, slight pallor of the lips and bilateral nail beds, and a possibly positive chest compression test, while the abdomen was non-tender with no shifting dullness noted. The patient was subsequently monitored through follow-up visits at two and six months post-discharge. Abdominal CT scans demonstrated a reduction in the volume of blood and fluid accumulation in the abdominal and pelvic cavities compared to earlier scans, with no abnormalities detected (Fig. 3). After a year of follow-up, the patient achieved full recovery.

Discussion

Traumatic rupture of the spleen is a common occurrence in surgical trauma due to its rich blood stores, making it susceptible to rupture and hemorrhage when exposed to external forces. Current clinical management of traumatic splenic rupture primarily involves conservative and surgical treatment options. Previously, total splenectomy was the preferred treatment approach among surgeons. However, there is a growing emphasis on preserving the spleen, given its unique functions as a blood storage, hematopoietic, immunological, and endocrine organ. [5], [6] The most recent classification by AAST categorizes splenic injuries into five grades. Grade I splenic injuries primarily affect the inner peritoneum of the spleen. In these cases, the area of subperitoneal hematoma is less than 10%, and there is only slight rupture upon palpation, with parenchymal laceration not exceeding 1 cm in depth. Typically, Grade I splenic injuries are managed through bed rest, fluid replacement, and hemostasis. [7] F. Vyhnánek et. Al [8] noted that a non-surgical approach is appropriate for patients with Grade I splenic injuries who are hemodynamically stable, have minimal hemoperitoneum (up to 300 mL), and are under continuous intensive care. Conservative treatment may be considered in patients with stable vital signs, overall good health, absence of additional organ injuries, and bleeding volume not exceeding 30% of the total body blood volume. However, in this case, imaging studies revealed a significant accumulation of blood within the abdominal cavity, with the volume exceeding 30% of the total blood volume. Although the patient's vital signs remained stable, it is important to consider that this may be a temporary compensatory mechanism maintaining circulatory stability. Nonetheless, circulatory function could be impaired, necessitating a high level of vigilance for potential concealed shock, close monitoring, and timely surgical intervention. The patient was completely cured by the oral and external application of Chinese herbs, which is a rare occurrence.

In Western medicine, the primary focus following spleen rupture is prompt control of bleeding, often requiring blood replacement in cases of excessive blood loss. However, in this patient, a combination of herbs such as Laccifer lacca, Toddalia asiatica Lam, China Fortunearia, and Bauhinia brachycarpa Benth was administered both internally and externally to effectively stop the bleeding. Additionally, Angelica sinensis significantly promoted the body's hematopoietic function, leading to increased red blood cells, white blood cells, and hemoglobin levels, further enhanced by the supplementation of a small amount of blood for better outcomes. [9]

In Chinese medicine, a ruptured spleen falls under the category of “blood stasis signs”. [10] The primary cause of this condition is a traumatic injury to the spleen's veins and ligaments, resulting in the loss of the spleen's regulatory function, leading to blood stasis outside the veins and stagnation of “Qi” and blood, thereby causing abdominal pain and other symptoms. In addition, TCM emphasizes the importance of activating blood circulation, resolving blood stasis, and regulating “Qi” and internal organs in the treatment of traumatic spleen rupture. On the other hand, Western medicine focuses on addressing a large amount of blood in the abdominal and pelvic cavities resulting from the ruptured spleen, necessitating the promotion of blood flow and elimination of blood stasis in the body. The herbs used in this patient's treatment, such as Lysimachia pardiformis, Carthamus tinctorius, Brook Anemone, and Sargentodoxa cuneata, were found to be effective in activating blood circulation and clearing stasis Furthermore, herbs like Toddalia asiatica Lam, Notoginseng Radix et Rhizoma, and Polygonum amplexicaule exhibit both hemostatic and stasis-clearing effects. [11]

Additionally, the patient presented with multiple rib fractures on the left chest. In Chinese medicine, the early stage of a fracture is primarily attributed to “Qi” stagnation and blood stasis. Therefore, the main focus of treatment is to activate blood circulation, eliminate blood stagnation, reduce swelling, and alleviate pain. During the middle and late stages of the fracture, liver and kidney deficiencies, along with cold and dampness stagnation, are the primary causes. In such cases, treatment should involve tonifying the liver and kidneys, dispelling cold, relaxing tendons, and promoting collateral circulation. An effective approach involves using Strychnos nux-vomica, also known as the “bone bonding miracle”, supplemented with internal and external applications of Toddalia asiatica Lam to accelerate bone regeneration and healing. [12] Furthermore, the following herbs, including Achyranthes bidentata, Acanthopanacis Cortex, Centipeda minima, Aucklandiae Radix, and Chinese taxillus herb, have proven to be beneficial in nourishing the liver and kidneys, strengthening tendons and bones, opening meridians, dispersing blood stasis, relieving pain, and treating bruises and injuries. Additionally, Amsacta lactinea, Bamboo leaf, Liparis nervosa, and Trachophyllum have been shown to possess properties that cool the blood and eliminate heat and toxins, thereby exhibiting antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effects.

It is noteworthy that the topically applied medication contained a small amount of menthol, which enhances skin permeability and facilitates improved penetration and absorption of the local drugs. The orally administered medications are absorbed into the bloodstream, undergoing metabolism to exert systemic effects. These effects coordinate and enhance overall physiological functions, thereby expediting the recovery process.

It is noteworthy that a single herb can have several effects. For example, Toddalia asiatica Lam has various effects, such as warming the meridians, relieving swelling and pain, eliminating heat and dampness, stopping bleeding, and promoting the regeneration of broken bones. Moreover, when combined with other herbs, Toddalia asiatica Lam can exhibit different, and even opposing effects. For example, while the herb on its own can stop bleeding, its combination with specific herbs may activate blood circulation and resolve stasis. The theories of Chinese medicine are highly intricate, highlighting the need for continuous learning and practical application in clinical practice. [13] In summary, traditional Chinese medicine treatment for splenic rupture hemorrhage highlights the synergistic effects of multiple herbs and their complexity, demonstrating the unique advantages of traditional medicine in addressing traumatic injuries.

Limitations

Although this case report presents a successful instance of traditional Chinese medicine treating splenic rupture and hemorrhage, several limitations remain. Firstly, the report is based on a single patient, lacking support from larger clinical studies, which limits its generalizability to a broader patient population. Secondly, without a direct comparison to Western medical treatments, the specific advantages and efficacy of traditional Chinese medicine in managing splenic rupture and hemorrhage cannot be clearly established. Finally, the absence of long-term systematic follow-up data and analysis of long-term outcomes necessitates further evidence to support the safety and effectiveness of traditional Chinese medicine therapies.

Conclusion

This case report highlights a rare occurrence wherein the bleeding caused by a ruptured spleen was completely cured through the oral and external application of Chinese herbs. The patient's use of Chinese herbal medicine, both orally and topically, demonstrated a highly effective therapeutic outcome, warranting acknowledgment and further exploration.
