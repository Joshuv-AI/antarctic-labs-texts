Colorectal cancer incidence in Saudi Arabia, 2002–2023: Age-specific trends and implications for screening and prevention
Authors: Bader Alshamsan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine trends in colorectal cancer incidence in Saudi Arabia and assess implications for screening and prevention.MethodsA population-based analysis of Saudi Cancer Registry data (2002−2023) was conducted. Annual percent changes in age-standardized incidence rates were estimated using Joinpoint regression. Interrupted time-series and regression analyses assessed post-2020 and age-specific changes.ResultsA total of 30,346 colorectal cancer cases were reported. Incidence increased in both sexes, with average annual percent changes of 5.33% in males and 4.28% in females. Colorectal cancer accounted for an increasing share of all cancers, rising from 8.5% to 13.1% overall and from 9.0% to 16.7% in males. Annual percent changes were higher in adults aged ≥50 years than <50 years (males: 5.39% vs 3.69%; females: 5.28% vs 2.60%). Colon cancer increased more rapidly than rectal cancer in both sexes (males: 5.99% vs 3.86%, interaction p = 0.01; females: 5.65% vs 3.14%, interaction p < 0.01). The odds of localized-stage diagnosis increased by 5.8% annually. Post-2020 slope increases were 17.6% in males and 18.4% in females.ConclusionsColorectal cancer incidence in Saudi Arabia increased substantially, particularly among adults aged ≥50 years, supporting age-targeted screening, stronger early detection pathways, and continuity of diagnostic services.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, Incidence trends, Early-onset colorectal cancer, colon cancer, Rectal cancer, cancer screening, cancer epidemiology, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Colorectal cancer is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide and shows marked geographic variation in incidence and mortality (Arnold et al., 2017; Bray et al., 2024). In Saudi Arabia, colorectal cancer is among the most frequently diagnosed cancers, ranking first among men and second among women, underscoring its public health importance (International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), 2026a). Although historically concentrated in high-income Western countries, colorectal cancer incidence has been rising in rapidly transitioning settings undergoing demographic, lifestyle, and health-system changes (Arnold et al., 2017; Keum and Giovannucci, 2019). International epidemiologic patterns show two concurrent trends: stabilization or decline in screened Western populations and continued increases in many low- and middle-income countries (Siegel et al., 2023; Siegel et al., 2019; Tian et al., 2025). Growing attention has also focused on early-onset colorectal cancer, defined as diagnosis before age 50 years, with several regions reporting increasing incidence in younger adults (Keum and Giovannucci, 2019; Siegel et al., 2019; Sung et al., 2025; Vuik et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2026).

Within the Gulf Cooperation Council region, colorectal cancer is one of the leading malignancies, particularly among men (Al-Shamsi et al., 2025; Alsanea et al., 2015; Elwali et al., 2023). In Saudi Arabia, national registry data have documented sustained increases in incidence over the past two decades (Alsanea et al., 2015; Alyabsi et al., 2021; Alsadhan et al., 2024a). Earlier analyses covering 1994–2010 showed rising age-standardized incidence rates (Alsanea et al., 2015), and subsequent evaluations through 2016 and 2018 confirmed continued upward trends across age groups (Alyabsi et al., 2021; Alsadhan et al., 2024a; Basudan et al., 2023; Alshamsan, 2022). Additional registry-based studies have described determinants of advanced presentation in Saudi Arabia (Alsadhan et al., 2024a; Alsadhan et al., 2024b).

Despite these advances, important gaps remain. Many previous analyses were limited to earlier time periods, frequently examined incidence, stage, or morphology separately, and did not extend into the post–COVID-19 period (Alsanea et al., 2015; Elwali et al., 2023; Alyabsi et al., 2021; Alsadhan et al., 2024a; Basudan et al., 2023; Alshamsan, 2022; Alsadhan et al., 2024b). In addition, colorectal cancer has often been treated as a single entity, with limited formal evaluation of whether colon and rectal cancers are diverging over time, whether temporal patterns differ by sex, and whether colorectal cancer is accounting for an increasing share of the broader national cancer burden. A more integrated national analysis is therefore needed to clarify how incidence, age structure, and stage at diagnosis are evolving together in Saudi Arabia.

These issues are particularly relevant in Saudi Arabia, where rapid socioeconomic transformation has been accompanied by increasing obesity, sedentary behavior, dietary westernization, and population aging, all of which may influence colorectal cancer risk (Tian et al., 2025; Sung et al., 2025; World Cancer Research Fund, 2018). Expansion of healthcare infrastructure and evolving screening initiatives may also affect diagnostic patterns and stage distribution. In addition, the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted cancer screening and diagnostic pathways internationally, with evidence of delayed detection and recovery effects in colorectal cancer care, highlighting the need to formally assess post-2020 changes rather than rely on descriptive year-to-year comparisons alone.

Accordingly, this study used data from the Saudi Cancer Registry annual reports to examine colorectal cancer incidence in Saudi Arabia from 2002 to 2023 (Saudi Health Council. National Cancer Center, 2026). The analysis evaluated age-standardized incidence trends overall and by sex, compared temporal slopes between colon and rectal cancer and between males and females, assessed age-stratified patterns (<50 vs ≥50 years) and temporal changes in the odds of diagnosis before age 50, examined temporal changes in stage at diagnosis and histologic distribution, and evaluated post-2020 slope changes using interrupted time-series analysis. By integrating these components within a unified framework, this study aimed to provide an updated characterization of colorectal cancer burden dynamics and inform screening, early detection, and cancer-control planning in Saudi Arabia.

Methods

Study design and population

A retrospective population-based analysis of colorectal cancer incidence in Saudi Arabia was conducted using data from the Saudi Cancer Registry (Saudi Health Council. National Cancer Center, 2026). The registry systematically collects data on newly diagnosed malignant neoplasms nationwide. Mandatory cancer reporting supports comprehensive coverage, and registry data undergo quality control before annual reporting (Bray et al., 2015). Cases are abstracted by registrars, deduplicated, and centrally coded.

All newly diagnosed primary invasive colorectal malignancies among Saudi nationals recorded in the Saudi Cancer Registry during 2002–2023 were included in the analysis. Benign, borderline, and in situ tumors were excluded, and each primary malignancy was counted once.

Measures

Extracted variables included age at diagnosis, sex, year of diagnosis, primary tumor site, histologic subtype, and stage at diagnosis. Tumor site and morphology were coded using the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition, and site codes were converted to the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, for reporting. Colon and rectal cancers were defined by primary site codes.

Stage at diagnosis was classified according to Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Summary Stage 2000 as localized, regional, distant, or unknown. Unknown stage was retained; no imputation was performed. Tumor histology was classified into four analytical groups: conventional adenocarcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma, signet ring cell carcinoma, and other histological types.

Early-onset colorectal cancer was defined as colorectal cancer diagnosed before age 50 years, consistent with widely used epidemiologic definitions (Siegel et al., 2019). Age-stratified analyses compared incidence trends in persons younger than 50 years with those 50 years of age or older. For descriptive analyses, age was also grouped into 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, and ≥ 75 years. Age-specific incidence rates were extracted from Saudi Cancer Registry reports, which are provided in standard five-year age groups.

Statistical analysis

Age-standardized incidence rates were calculated by direct standardization using the Segi–Doll world standard population, which is the standard population used in Saudi Cancer Registry reporting (International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), 2026b). This approach maintained consistency with the source registry data and comparability with prior national and international cancer registry studies.

Temporal trends were evaluated using Joinpoint regression with log-linear models of annual age-standardized incidence rates to estimate annual percent changes (Kim et al., 2022). A maximum of two joinpoints was permitted, and model selection was based on Monte Carlo permutation testing with a two-sided α of 0.05. Annual percent changes were calculated for each identified segment; when no joinpoints were detected, a single log-linear model was fitted across the full study period. To compare subgroup trends, interaction analyses used log-linear regression models including calendar year, subgroup, and a year × subgroup interaction term to test differences between colon and rectal cancer and between males and females within each subsite. Interrupted time-series analysis was used to assess post-2020 changes in colorectal cancer incidence, with 2020 prespecified as the interruption point. Segmented regression models of annual age-standardized incidence rates included terms for time, a 2020 indicator, and a post-2020 time interaction to estimate the pre-2020 trend, immediate level change, and post-2020 slope change. Log-transformed models were used to express slope changes as annual percent changes.

Annual median age at diagnosis was analyzed using linear regression. Binary logistic regression models were used to evaluate temporal changes in diagnosis before age 50 years and stage at diagnosis (localized vs non-localized; distant vs non-distant), with calendar year entered as a continuous predictor and sex included where applicable. Models were fitted to annual aggregated counts using frequency weights based on annual case counts. As a sensitivity analysis, stage models were repeated after excluding cases with unknown stage. All tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered significant. Interrupted time-series, linear regression, and logistic regression analyses were performed in IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, version 31. Joinpoint analyses used the Joinpoint Regression Program, version 5.3.0.0, from the National Cancer Institute.

This study used publicly available, anonymized data from the Saudi Cancer Registry and did not involve identifiable human subjects. In accordance with institutional guidelines, the study was exempt from formal ethical approval, and a statement of exemption is on file.

Results

Colorectal cancer burden and proportion of total cancers

Between 2002 and 2023, 30,346 colorectal cancer cases were diagnosed in Saudi Arabia, including 16,631 in males (54.8%) and 13,715 in females. Median age at diagnosis remained stable in males, increasing from 57 years in 2002 to 60 years in 2023 without a significant temporal trend (β = 0.05 years/year, 95% CI, −0.01, 0.12). In females, median age increased significantly from 53 to 59.5 years (β = 0.18 years/year, 95% CI, 0.10, 0.26). Colorectal cancer accounted for an increasing share of all cancers, rising from 8.5% in 2002 to 13.1% in 2023. In males, colorectal cancer cases increased from 265 to 1430, and the proportion of all cancers increased from 9.0% to 16.7%; in females, cases increased from 237 to 1250, and the proportion increased from 8.1% to 10.5% (Supplementary Table S1).

Trends in age-standardized incidence rates

Age-standardized incidence rates increased substantially in both sexes during 2002–2023 (Fig. 1). In males, the age-standardized incidence rate rose from 5.6 to 24.1 per 100,000; in females, it rose from 5.9 to 19.9 per 100,000. A temporary decline was observed in 2020, followed by a marked post-2020 rebound in both sexes. Joinpoint regression identified a statistically significant joinpoint in 2020 in males, while females showed a similar post-2020 acceleration. Over the full study period, average annual percent changes were 5.33% in males (95% CI, 4.21, 6.46) and 4.28% in females (95% CI, 3.14, 5.43), with no significant sex-by-year interaction (p = 0.21). Detailed joinpoint estimates are presented in Supplementary Table S2. Interrupted time-series analysis likewise showed significant pre-2020 increases, no immediate level change in 2020, and significant positive post-2020 slope changes in both sexes (Supplementary Table S3). In log-transformed models, the corresponding post-2020 slope increases were 17.6% in males and 18.4% in females.

Age-specific incidence trends

Colorectal cancer incidence increased across all age groups in both sexes, with larger increases in older adults (Supplementary Table S4). Age-stratified joinpoint analyses likewise showed rising incidence in both early-onset (<50 years) and older-onset (≥50 years) colorectal cancer, with consistently higher long-term annual percent changes in the older group (Fig. 2; Supplementary Table S5). In males aged <50 years, incidence changed little before 2019 and increased sharply thereafter, whereas in females aged <50 years incidence rose steadily across the study period. Overall annual percent changes remained higher in adults aged ≥50 years than in those aged <50 years in both sexes. The odds of diagnosis before age 50 declined over time for colon cancer in both sexes and for rectal cancer in females, but not for rectal cancer in males.

The vertical dotted line marks 2020, the prespecified interruption year used for segmented trend analyses.

Subsite-specific incidence trends

Colon cancer accounted for most colorectal cancer cases throughout the study period and increased faster than rectal cancer in both sexes (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table S6). By 2023, colon cancer comprised approximately two-thirds of colorectal cancer diagnoses nationally. Site-by-year interaction tests showed significantly steeper increases in colon than in rectal cancer in both males (p = 0.01) and females (p < 0.01), whereas sex-by-year interactions were not significant for colon (p = 0.69) or rectal cancer (p = 0.36).

Stage distribution and morphology

Stage distribution shifted toward earlier diagnosis over time in both sexes (Fig. 4). Localized-stage disease increased from 23.4% to 47.9% in males and from 20.7% to 47.4% in females, while regional-stage disease declined from 40.4% to 26.5% in males and from 48.9% to 28.3% in females. The proportional share of distant-stage disease remained relatively stable at approximately 22%–29% despite increasing absolute case counts. Cases with unknown stage declined from 14.3% in 2002 to 0.6% in 2023, consistent with improved staging completeness. In logistic regression models including calendar year and sex, the odds of localized-stage diagnosis increased over time (OR/year 1.06, 95% CI, 1.05, 1.06), equivalent to a 5.8% annual increase. Sex was not significantly associated with localized-stage presentation after adjustment for year (female vs male OR 0.95, 95% CI, 0.90, 1.00). In the corresponding model for distant-stage disease, the adjusted odds decreased over time (OR/year 0.99, 95% CI, 0.98, 0.99), whereas males had higher odds of distant-stage presentation than females (OR 1.10, 95% CI, 1.04, 1.16). Histologic distribution remained stable over time, with conventional adenocarcinoma predominating in both sexes and other subtypes remaining uncommon.

Discussion

This 22-year national analysis showed a substantial increase in colorectal cancer incidence in Saudi Arabia in both sexes, driven predominantly by colon cancer and with greater long-term growth among adults aged ≥50 years. Colorectal cancer also accounted for an increasing share of all cancers, underscoring its growing public health importance. Although incidence increased in adults aged <50 years, the larger and more sustained rise occurred in the screening-eligible population, highlighting the relevance of these trends for screening strategy, diagnostic capacity, and preventive service planning.

The higher long-term annual percent changes observed among adults aged ≥50 years are notable in the context of Saudi Arabia's relatively young population structure (median age 29 years overall; 22 among Saudis and 34 among non-Saudis), and suggest increasing future demand for screening and colorectal cancer services (General Authority for Statistics (GASTAT), 2026). Rather than indicating a shift confined to younger cohorts, the observed pattern suggests broad population-level expansion with greater long-term growth in older adults (Sung et al., 2025; Vuik et al., 2019; Shen et al., 2026). In contrast to higher-income settings where screening has moderated colorectal cancer incidence in older adults (Siegel et al., 2023; Sung et al., 2025), the Saudi pattern suggests continued expansion within a screening-eligible population during health-system maturation. Sustained increases over more than two decades, without evidence of plateauing, are consistent with continued colorectal cancer expansion in a high-Human Development Index setting undergoing rapid socioeconomic and lifestyle change (Worthington et al., 2024; Walker et al., 2024). Similar to recent national breast cancer patterns in Saudi Arabia, these findings suggest a broader cancer-control context in which screen-detectable cancers are increasing and advanced-stage disease remains substantial, underscoring the need for more resilient early-detection pathways (Alshamsan, 2026).

Colorectal cancer also accounted for an increasing share of the national cancer burden, indicating that the observed rise reflects not only higher case counts but also a growing contribution to the overall cancer profile. This increase was not uniform across subsites: colon cancer increased significantly faster than rectal cancer in both sexes and was the principal driver of the rising burden. Similar colon-dominant patterns have been linked to obesity, metabolic dysfunction, and dietary transition, exposures that are plausible in the Saudi context (Tian et al., 2025; Shen et al., 2026; World Cancer Research Fund, 2018; Walker et al., 2024). Although causal inference is not possible from registry data, continued incidence growth despite organized fecal immunochemical test-based screening suggests that underlying risk trends may currently be outpacing preventive impact (Awaf and Abd-Ellatif, 2025; Ministry of Health (MOH), Saudi Arabia, 2026). These findings therefore have implications not only for risk reduction but also for the scale and organization of diagnostic and follow-up services required to support effective screening pathways.

Stage distribution shifted substantially over time. Localized-stage diagnoses approximately doubled, accompanied by a marked decline in unknown-stage cases, consistent with improved staging completeness (Fig. 4). However, the proportional share of distant-stage disease remained relatively stable, and absolute distant-stage case counts increased as total incidence rose, indicating a persistent advanced-stage burden. Together, these findings suggest that gains in earlier diagnosis have not yet translated into clear population-level reductions in late-stage presentation.

The post-2020 increase in incidence slope, superimposed on a long-standing upward trend, raises additional health-service considerations. This pattern may reflect disruption and subsequent recovery in screening, diagnostic evaluation, or care-seeking pathways, although it should not be attributed to COVID-19 alone (Worthington et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026; Kumar et al., 2025; van den Puttelaar et al., 2023). Although the post-2020 period includes only three annual observations, concordant findings across Joinpoint and interrupted time-series analyses, together with similar patterns across sexes, support treating this increase as an important observed change in incidence trends. From a health-services perspective, this finding highlights the importance of maintaining continuity in preventive and diagnostic care during periods of system stress, as interruptions may delay detection and increase downstream service demand.

Complementary regional data suggest that although initial acceptance of fecal immunochemical test screening may be high, sustained annual adherence remains suboptimal and knowledge of colorectal cancer is limited (Alshamsan et al., 2026). Lower knowledge is associated with reduced screening uptake, whereas physician engagement may improve participation (Alshamsan et al., 2026). Colonoscopy uptake also remains low, which may limit the effectiveness of fecal immunochemical test-based pathways without strong follow-through (Aljabri and Alharbi, 2026). Taken together with the persistent advanced-stage burden and the post-2020 increase in incidence slope, these findings suggest that colorectal cancer control in Saudi Arabia will require stronger adherence to screening pathways, greater public awareness, stronger primary care engagement, and more resilient diagnostic continuity. These findings have practical implications for prevention policy in Saudi Arabia, including prioritizing age-eligible adults in screening strategy and service planning, strengthening colonoscopy and pathology capacity, improving follow-up after abnormal fecal immunochemical test results, expanding public and primary care engagement, and using national trend data to guide cancer control planning in alignment with Saudi Vision 2030's Health Sector Transformation Program (Saudi Vision 2030, 2021).

This study has several strengths. It is based on a comprehensive national cancer registry with broad institutional coverage and mandatory reporting. The 22-year study period enabled assessment of long-term trend dynamics, and the use of age-standardized incidence rates minimized demographic confounding. Joinpoint regression with interaction testing also allowed objective comparison of temporal trends by subsite, sex, age group, and post-2020 period.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. As a registry-based ecological analysis, the study cannot establish causal relationships for underlying exposures. Individual-level data on obesity, diet, physical activity, screening participation, and comorbidities were unavailable. Mortality and survival outcomes were not examined, limiting assessment of prognostic impact. Although staging completeness improved over time, residual variation in documentation may still have influenced comparisons. The absence of regional and socioeconomic stratification also limited assessment of disparities.

Future work should incorporate age–period–cohort modeling, linkage with mortality data, and regional analyses of stage and access. Evaluation of screening uptake and adherence will also be important as organized programs expand. Further work examining regional capacity, follow-up after abnormal screening, and continuity of diagnostic services would also help translate these national trend findings into more effective preventive health policy and service delivery.

Conclusions

Over 22 years of national data, colorectal cancer incidence in Saudi Arabia increased substantially, with colon-dominant growth, increasing incidence across adult age groups, and a growing contribution to the national cancer burden, particularly among males. The absence of sex-based differences in temporal slopes, together with a persistent advanced-stage burden, suggests that this increase reflects broad population-level dynamics rather than changes confined to specific subgroups. Despite improvements in stage documentation and evolving screening infrastructure, the colorectal cancer burden continues to rise. Colorectal cancer is therefore an increasingly important public health priority in Saudi Arabia. These national trends have important implications for screening strategy, diagnostic service planning, and preventive health policy. Strengthening screening participation, earlier detection pathways, continuity of diagnostic care, and primary prevention will be important to reduce future colorectal cancer burden.
