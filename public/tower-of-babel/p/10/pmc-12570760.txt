Interrelationship of diabetic neuropathy, retinopathy and nephropathy in type 2 diabetes mellitus: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Sara Roostaei, Alireza Ashraf, Mohammadjavad Vahidi Fard, Mohammadkarim Johari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the interrelationships between peripheral neuropathy, diabetic retinopathy (DR), and nephropathy in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), with a specific focus on how DR severity correlates with the presence and severity of diabetic neuropathy and nephropathy.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, adult patients with at least five years’ duration of T2DM and preserved renal function (eGFR ≥ 60 mL/min/1.73 m²) were evaluated. DR was graded using the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy Severity Scale, and DME was assessed by spectral-domain optical coherence tomography. Peripheral neuropathy was quantified via nerve conduction studies (NCS), specifically sural sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude, while nephropathy was evaluated by urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) and eGFR.ResultsA total of 155, 138, and 94 patients completed neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy assessments, respectively. Among the 138 participants who underwent fundus examination, 60% had proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) and 32% had diabetic macular edema (DME). A significant inverse correlation was found between SNAP amplitude and both DR severity (p = 0.001) and DME presence (p = 0.001), indicating worse nerve conduction in advanced retinal disease. Patients with DME had a fourfold higher likelihood of impaired SNAP values (OR = 4.0; 95% CI: 1.849–8.653). Additionally, DME presence was associated with significantly higher UACR (p = 0.011), and nephropathy severity showed a mild but significant association with DR stage (p = 0.047).ConclusionThe severity of DR and the presence of DME correlate strongly with peripheral neuropathy and increased albuminuria in T2DM patients, reflecting shared microvascular pathology. Retinal findings, particularly DME, may serve as accessible clinical markers for identifying individuals at elevated risk of systemic microvascular complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04470-5.Keywords: Diabetic neuropathy, Diabetic retinopathy, Diabetic nephropathy, Diabetic macular edema, Albuminuria

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder with increasing global prevalence and substantial microvascular complications. Among these, diabetic neuropathy, nephropathy, and retinopathy are major contributors to morbidity and diminished quality of life [1–3]. These microvascular complications, though affecting different organ systems, share common pathophysiological mechanisms, such as chronic hyperglycemia-induced endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation [3].

Diabetic neuropathy is among the most frequent chronic microvascular complications of T2DM, affecting approximately 30–50% of patients, while diabetic retinopathy and nephropathy affect about 25–35% and 20–40%, respectively, depending on disease duration and glycemic control. Recent population-based studies have reported that peripheral neuropathy is often the earliest and most prevalent of these complications in long-standing diabetes [4–6]

Despite their distinct clinical manifestations, the shared microvascular etiology suggests a potential interplay between these complications. Growing evidence supports the notion that the severity of one complication may correlate with or predict the presence or progression of others [7, 8]. For example, worsening nephropathy has been linked to more severe diabetic neuropathy [9]. Similarly, advances in retinal imaging, such as optical coherence tomography (OCT), have enabled the detection of subtle structural changes that may mirror systemic microvascular dysfunction, including neuropathic alterations [10, 11].

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the interrelationships among diabetic neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy in patients with T2DM, specifically evaluating how the severity of diabetic retinopathy is associated with the presence and severity of peripheral neuropathy and nephropathy indicators.

Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted at academic and medical centers affiliated with Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Iran, to investigate the correlations between the severity of diabetic neuropathy, nephropathy, and retinopathy in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Study population and sampling

Adults with with a minimum 5-year history of T2DM who attended the endocrinology and diabetes clinics of Imam Reza Subspecialty Clinic (Shiraz University of Medical Sciences) for regular metabolic follow-up and screening or management of diabetes-related complications were consecutively recruited between November 2022 and March 2024.

Patients were recruited consecutively, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Patients with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² were excluded to avoid confounding effects of uremic neuropathy and advanced nephropathy on nerve conduction and retinal outcomes. In contrast, patients with varying degrees of diabetic retinopathy or neuropathy were included, as one of the primaries aims of this study was to evaluate associations across different complication severities.

Data collection and clinical assessments

All assessments were conducted by subspecialists using standardized protocols to ensure consistency and minimize inter-observer variability. T2DM diagnosis was confirmed by an endocrinology subspecialist based on American Diabetes Association criteria [12].

Diabetic neuropathy assessment

Peripheral neuropathy severity was assessed using Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS), performed with the Natus system, which is considered the gold standard for evaluating diabetic neuropathy [13]. Given the lack of universally accepted NCS severity criteria [13] and the predominance of distal lower limb involvement in diabetes, the following parameters were measured:

Tibial motor nerve: compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude, distal latency, and minimum F-wave latency.

Tibial motor nerve: compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude, distal latency, and minimum F-wave latency.

Sural sensory nerve: sensory nerve action potential (SNAP) amplitude (µV) and conduction velocity [14, 15] For statistical comparison, sural SNAP amplitude was also analyzed as a binary variable, defined as normal (≥ 5 µV) or impaired (< 5 µV) according to the Baba classification.

Sural SNAP amplitude was the primary electrophysiological marker for distal symmetric polyneuropathy [17]. All neurological evaluations were conducted by a single physical medicine and rehabilitation subspecialist. Overlapping neurological conditions were excluded through history-taking and the criteria outlined above.

Diabetic retinopathy assessment

Diabetic retinopathy was graded by two retinal specialists(MKJ-MJV) using dilated fundus ophthalmoscopy according to the International Clinical Diabetic Retinopathy (ICDR) scale.

Diabetic macular edema (DME) was evaluated using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (OCT). DR was assessed clinically because ophthalmoscopy remains a validated and widely accepted method for grading retinopathy severity in clinical studies, while DME was evaluated by OCT to ensure precise measurement of macular thickness and fluid accumulation. DME was defined according to the International Clinical Diabetic Macular Edema (ICDME) scale. Center-involved DME (ci-DME) was diagnosed when retinal thickening or cystoid changes involved the central 1-mm subfield on OCT, regardless of total CMT value. Eyes showing localized thickening outside the central subfield but with normal CMT were considered to have non–center-involved DME. This classification was based on previous consensus definitions (ETDRS Research Group, 1991; Diabetic Retinopathy Clinical Research Network, 2010) [19].

The more severely affected eye was included per patient. OCT assessments were conducted by a single retina specialist(MKJ).

Diabetic nephropathy assessment

Nephropathy was evaluated based on albuminuria and eGFR. Albuminuria was assessed from early morning spot urine samples, using the urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR), and classified per KDIGO guidelines [20]:

A1: ACR < 30 mg/g (normal to mildly increased).

A1: ACR < 30 mg/g (normal to mildly increased).

A2: ACR 30–300 mg/g (moderately increased, microalbuminuria).

A2: ACR 30–300 mg/g (moderately increased, microalbuminuria).

A3: ACR > 300 mg/g (severely increased, macroalbuminuria).

A3: ACR > 300 mg/g (severely increased, macroalbuminuria).

Samples were collected in a fasting state (6–7 AM) immediately after waking, prior to physical activity. Participants were instructed to avoid strenuous exercise before collection. All samples were processed within 24 h using immunochemical methods in a certified laboratory.

eGFR was estimated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [21]. To eliminate the confounding effects of reduced renal function on neuropathy evaluations, patients with eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² (KDIGO G3–G5) were excluded [22]. All nephropathy assessments were performed by a single nephrologist.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. Continuous variables with normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and non-normally distributed variables as median (interquartile range, IQR). Between-group comparisons were made using independent t-test or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate. Linear regression analysis was used to examine associations between DR severity and nerve conduction parameters after adjusting for age, diabetes duration, and eGFR.

Correlations among neuropathy (based on NCS and clinical evaluations), retinopathy (ICDR staging and OCT parameters), and nephropathy (ACR and eGFR) were assessed using Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients according to data distribution: Pearson correlationwas used for continuous, normally distributed variables (e.g., SNAP amplitude vs. eGFR), and Spearman correlation for ordinal or non-normally distributed variables (e.g., DR severity, ACR). Multivariable linear regression was performed to adjust for potential confounders, including age, sex, and diabetes duration. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences [IR.SUMS.MED.REC.1402.283]. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Initially, 240 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) were enrolled in the study. Of these, 85 patients were excluded based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ultimately, 155, 138, and 94 patients completed the assessments for neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy, respectively (Fig. 1).

The mean age of the participants (52 males and 103 females) was 60.78 ± 9.17 years, and the mean duration of diabetes at enrollment was 10.21 ± 5.05 years.

Post hoc Tukey HSD tests showed that patients with PDR had the lowest mean SNAP values (M = 1.27), indicating more severe neuropathy. Intermediate SNAP values were observed in patients with severe NPDR (M = 3.46). The highest SNAP amplitudes were seen in patients with no DR (M = 6.67) and mild NPDR (M = 6.48), suggesting better nerve conduction. These findings support a progressive reduction in nerve conduction function with advancing DR severity.

Crosstabulation and odds ratio analyses further explored the association between DME and binary SNAP values. Patients with DME were four times more likely to have impaired SNAP values compared to those without DME (Odds Ratio = 4.0; 95% CI: 1.849–8.653). Among patients with low SNAP binary values (coded as 1.00), the risk of DME was significantly higher (Risk = 2.154; 95% CI: 1.457–3.185), while the risk decreased among those with higher SNAP values (coded as 2.00) (Risk = 0.538; 95% CI: 0.356–0.815). After adjusting for DR severity, DME remained significantly associated with impaired SNAP amplitude (adjusted OR = 3.42; 95% CI: 1.51–7.73; p = 0.003), indicating an independent relationship between macular edema and peripheral neuropathy.

Further analysis (Table 5) examined the association between DR severity and nephropathy stage based on UACR categories. Among patients with no DR and mild–moderate NPDR, microalbuminuria was present in 38% and 22% of patients, respectively. In contrast, macroalbuminuria was found in 45.5% of patients with PDR. The association between DR severity and nephropathy stage was mildly statistically significant (Chi-square test, p = 0.047).

Indeed, not all participants completed nephropathy testing, to assess potential selection bias, baseline characteristics were compared between patients with available nephropathy data (n = 94) and those without (n = 61). No significant differences were found in age (p = 0.42), diabetes duration (p = 0.37), DR severity distribution (p = 0.45), or neuropathy stage (p = 0.53). These findings suggest that the missing nephropathy data were largely random rather than systematically related to disease severity (supplementary Table e1).

Discussion

Diabetic neuropathy, retinopathy, and nephropathy are major microvascular complications of T2DM. Although traditionally managed in isolation, growing evidence suggests that they share common pathological mechanisms, including microvascular ischemia, inflammation, and glycation-related injury. In this context, our study evaluated the interrelationship among these three complications with particular focus on the association between DME and peripheral neuropathy.

Nerve conduction studies (NCS), the gold standard for evaluating diabetic neuropathy, confirmed a wide spectrum of neural impairment in our cohort. Both sural SNAP amplitude and Baba classification stage showed significant inverse correlations with DR severity and the presence of DME, indicating that worsening retinal pathology parallels progressive peripheral nerve dysfunction. This aligns with the observations of Kulkarni et al., who proposed that retinopathy and neuropathy share common pathogenic pathways involving microvascular ischemia, inflammatory injury, and advanced glycation end-product accumulation [23] The high proportion of proliferative DR and center-involved DME in our study likely reflects the tertiary referral nature of our center, where patients with more advanced disease are commonly managed. As expected, the prevalence of DME increased progressively with DR severity, consistent with previous reports highlighting the close relationship between macular edema and retinopathy progression [24].

Nephropathy data were available for 94 patients, among whom a considerable proportion exhibited albuminuria. DME was significantly associated with higher UACR levels, and although mean UACR did not differ substantially across DR severity categories, cross-tabulation of DR staging with UACR staging revealed a modest yet significant trend toward increasing nephropathy burden with advancing DR. These findings align with reports from the SN-DREAMS study and others, which demonstrated that macroalbuminuria strongly associates with sight-threatening DR, including PDR and DME, further supporting the interconnected nature of retinal and renal microvascular injury [25–29]. Variations in correlation strength compared with earlier studies may reflect methodological differences, as previous reports often utilized 24-hour urine collections, whereas our study relied on spot ACR measurements, which may be less sensitive for detecting subtle changes [25].

The relationship between nephropathy and neuropathy observed in our dataset reinforces this shared pathophysiological pathway. Consistent with Xu et al., who reported that elevated urinary albumin excretion correlates with more severe nerve conduction deficits, our post hoc analysis identified a significant association between nephropathy severity and neuropathy stage, despite the smaller nephropathy sample size [30]. Although urinary data were incomplete, comparative assessment suggested no major demographic or disease severity differences between patients with and without available UACR measurements, minimizing the likelihood of systematic selection bias.

A major strength of this study is the incorporation of spectral-domain OCT parameters—specifically center-involved DME—as structural biomarkers within a systemic microvascular context. Unlike earlier studies that relied primarily on fundus examination or clinical staging alone, we integrated high-resolution retinal imaging with electrophysiological neuropathy testing. The observed inverse association between DME and sural SNAP amplitude suggests that OCT-detected neurovascular disruption in the retina may serve as a surrogate marker for broader microvascular dysfunction, including peripheral neuropathy and nephropathy. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to directly link objective OCT features with nerve conduction abnormalities in T2DM, providing new insight into shared disease mechanisms across organ systems and supporting the role of ophthalmic imaging as a window into systemic vascular health.

Limitations of this study include its cross-sectional design, which precludes causal inference, and the tertiary care setting, which likely overrepresents advanced DR and DME. Additionally, nephropathy data were incomplete; however, comparative analysis showed no significant demographic or disease-severity differences between patients with and without urinary data, reducing the likelihood of selection bias. Finally, metabolic confounders such as HbA1c, blood pressure, lipid profile, and smoking status were not consistently available and could not be adjusted for, leaving room for residual confounding.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates a strong interrelationship between diabetic retinopathy, neuropathy, and nephropathy in type 2 diabetes mellitus. Advanced DR stages and center-involved DME were closely associated with greater peripheral neuropathy severity on NCS and higher albuminuria levels, supporting a shared microvascular pathogenesis across organ systems. These results highlight the value of ophthalmic assessment—especially OCT-based markers—as potential clinical indicators of broader systemic risk. Integrated, multidisciplinary screening strategies may therefore be warranted for patients presenting with advanced retinal disease.
