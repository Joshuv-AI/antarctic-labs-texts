Effect of group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring on middle-aged and elderly participants with schizophrenia
Authors: Guiyun Gan, Chenxia Song, Li Zhang, Jin Liu, Juan Chen, Feifei Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo investigate the effectiveness of group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring in the nursing care of middle-aged and elderly people with schizophrenia.MethodsUsing a convenience sampling approach, 70 hospitalized middle-aged and elderly participants with schizophrenia were selected from a tertiary psychiatric hospital in Hefei, Anhui Province, during the period from March 2023 to March 2024. A random assignment placed 35 participants in the control group and 35 in the intervention group. All participants maintained their stable, pre-existing antipsychotic medication regimens throughout the study period, with no changes initiated for research purposes. While the control group received conventional rehabilitation nursing, the intervention group received an additional intervention of group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring on top of routine care. The intervention lasted for 8 weeks. The Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) was used to assess psychiatric symptom severity before and after the intervention. The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was used to assess cognitive function, and the Schizophrenia Quality of Life Scale (SQLS) was employed to evaluate quality of life.ResultsRepeated-measures ANOVA revealed significant Group × Time interaction effects for PANSS total score (F(1,68) = 23.96, p < 0.001), MoCA (F(1,68) = 9.01, p = 0.004), and SQLS (F(1,68) = 15.07, p < 0.001). Simple effects analyses showed that after the 8-week intervention, the intervention group achieved significantly greater reductions in PANSS total score and SQLS score, as well as a significantly greater increase in MoCA score, compared to the control group (all p < 0.05).ConclusionGroup reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring can effectively alleviate psychiatric symptoms, enhance cognitive function, and improve the quality of life in middle-aged and elderly people with schizophrenia. This combined intervention also contributes to psychological activation and facilitates psychiatric rehabilitation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

As a severe mental illness, schizophrenia is defined by disruptions in perception, emotional regulation, behavior, and cognitive processes. People with schizophrenia often experience profound cognitive impairments and persistent negative symptoms [1–2]. Negative symptoms primarily manifest as thought impoverishment, emotional blunting, avolition, and diminished social engagement. Progressive social withdrawal is common, and numerous studies have identified cognitive dysfunction as a core clinical feature of schizophrenia [3], which significantly impairs participants’ quality of life [4–6] and adversely affects long-term prognosis.

Currently, antipsychotic medications remain the mainstay of schizophrenia treatment. However, their efficacy is often limited, particularly in ameliorating cognitive deficits [7]. Moreover, pharmacological interventions are frequently associated with adverse effects, including weight gain, sleep disturbances, and impaired concentration [8], which in turn reduce patient adherence to treatment regimens. Non-pharmacological interventions, designed to enhance patients’ functional capacity in daily activities, offer several advantages, including mitigating side effects and facilitating early prevention. These interventions can effectively improve self-care abilities, cognitive function, and social functioning, thereby enhancing overall quality of life [9–10]. Group reminiscence therapy is a form of non-pharmacological intervention that provides patients with social interaction opportunities, fosters a supportive social network, and alleviates feelings of loneliness [11]. By guiding patients to reframe negative thought patterns into positive and affirming perspectives, it helps alleviate emotional distress and promotes adaptive coping strategies, ultimately contributing to improved clinical outcomes [12]. Cognitive restructuring, on the other hand, is a targeted cognitive intervention aimed at reshaping maladaptive thought processes [13]. The two approaches were combined based on their complementary mechanisms: reminiscence therapy elicits autobiographical material and emotional engagement, while cognitive restructuring provides a structured method to address dysfunctional cognitions that may emerge during reminiscence. This synergistic integration aims to more comprehensively address the cognitive, emotional, and social impairments associated with schizophrenia. Therefore, this study proposes the integration of group reminiscence therapy with cognitive restructuring. The objective is to simultaneously improve cognitive function and emotional responses, alleviate psychiatric symptoms, and enhance social functioning and life quality in schizophrenia patients.

A total of 70 eligible participants were enrolled. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated random number sequence, and assignments were placed in sealed, opaque envelopes. Outcome measures

Primary and secondary outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the total score of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS). Secondary outcomes included scores on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and the Schizophrenia Quality of Life Scale (SQLS). We hypothesized that, compared to the control group, the intervention group would show a significantly greater reduction in PANSS total score, a significantly greater improvement in MoCA score, and a significantly greater improvement (lower score) in SQLS after the 8-week intervention.

Assessment instruments

Positive and negative syndrome scale (PANSS)

The PANSS [14] was used to evaluate the severity of psychiatric symptoms. Widely used for assessing symptom severity and treatment effectiveness in schizophrenia, it comprises three subscales: Positive (7 items), Negative (7 items), and General Psychopathology (16 items). Each of the 30 items is rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = absent to 7 = extreme), with higher scores indicating greater severity. The PANSS has demonstrated excellent reliability and validity [15]. The total score ranges from 30 to 210.

Montreal cognitive assessment (MoCA)

Cognitive function was evaluated using the MoCA [16]. It assesses seven domains: visuospatial/executive function, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction, and orientation. Assessments were conducted one-on-one and lasted approximately 15 min. For participants with ≤ 12 years of education, 1 point was added to the total score to adjust for educational bias, with a maximum possible score of 30. We selected the MoCA based on the specific design and intended mechanisms of our combined intervention. We posited that the cognitive restructuring component would facilitate the identification and modification of dysfunctional thought patterns, while the reminiscence therapy component was designed to actively engage and stimulate episodic and autobiographical memory recall through guided recollection [12]. It was therefore hypothesized that this integrated approach would lead to observable improvements in overall cognitive performance, which the MoCA, as a comprehensive screening tool, is well-suited to capture.

Schizophrenia quality of life scale (SQLS)

Quality of life was assessed using the SQLS [17], a schizophrenia-specific scale comprising 30 items across three dimensions: psychosocial factors, motivation/energy, and symptoms/side effects. Items are scored on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4), with higher scores indicating poorer quality of life.

Intervention methods

Intervention for the control group

Routine nursing interventions was administered to the control group, which included the following measures: providing participants and their families with education on schizophrenia-related knowledge; facilitating participant communication to understand their psychological state and offering psychological support through both verbal and non-verbal means; ensuring medication adherence by supervising and guiding participants in the proper use of prescribed antipsychotic medications; collaborating with the nutrition department to develop personalized and scientifically sound dietary plans; and organizing daily occupational and recreational therapy activities. The overall intervention duration was 8 weeks, with sessions conducted once per week, each lasting 45–60 min.

Intervention for the experimental group

Participants in the experimental group received the same routine nursing care as the control group (described in Sect. 1.2.1). In addition, they received a structured program of group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring. This combined intervention was provided as an adjunct to the routine care. Interventions were conducted in three separate cohorts according to enrollment time, with each group participating in one session per week, totaling eight sessions.

Intervention team composition and training

To ensure standardized delivery and objective evaluation, the research team was structured with distinct roles:

Intervention Delivery Team (Research Therapists): All group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring sessions were directly facilitated by two research therapists. They were licensed clinical psychologists with formal postgraduate training in cognitive-behavioral therapy for psychosis (CBTp) and had over three years of experience in group-based interventions for severe mental illness.

Clinical Management Team: Two consultant psychiatrists were responsible for overall medical oversight and participant safety. Three psychiatric nurses managed routine daily care and monitored participants’ general well-being. This team did not deliver the experimental intervention.

Outcome Assessment Team: To maintain blinding, all research assessments (PANSS, MoCA, SQLS) were conducted by a separate assessor (a research nurse) who was not involved in any intervention activities and was unaware of group allocation.

Training and Fidelity Assurance: Prior to the study, the research therapists completed a structured 16-hour training program. The training covered the manualized protocol, including: (a) the methodology for conducting group reminiscence therapy, and (b) the application of cognitive restructuring techniques within a CBTp framework. Training methods included didactic instruction, supervised role-playing, and review of practice sessions. Adherence to the protocol was monitored throughout the study; therapists followed a structured guide for each session, and 15% of sessions were randomly selected for independent fidelity review by a senior CBTp supervisor using a standardized checklist.

Intervention program development and implementation

The intervention protocol was developed through an extensive literature review, grounded in the fundamental principles of reminiscence therapy and cognitive restructuring. Key references included the Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Schizophrenia (2nd Edition) [18], and the 2024 Chinese Expert Consensus on Maintenance Treatment of Schizophrenia [15], The intervention plan was further refined based on the clinical characteristics of the target participant population. Expert consultations were conducted with three chief psychiatrists (with over 15 years of experience), two senior psychologists (with over 10 years of experience), two chief nursing specialists specializing in palliative care (with over 10 years of experience), and two nursing managers. Their feedback, combined with results from a pilot study, was used to revise and finalize the intervention content. The final program comprised eight thematic sessions (Table 1), with one theme addressed each week. participants were divided into subgroups of 8 to 10 individuals, and the intervention was implemented accordingly, supplementing the routine nursing care received by the control group with group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring.

Implementation of the intervention protocol

The intervention was conducted in group sessions over a period of 8 weeks, with one session held per week, each lasting 45–60 min. All sessions took place in the hospital’s rehabilitation activity hall, a spacious, well-lit, and quiet environment designed to facilitate therapeutic activities. During the sessions, participants were seated in a circular arrangement to encourage open discussion and interaction on the designated themes. Members of the research team, who had undergone standardized training, were responsible for administering the intervention. Additionally, outcome assessments for both groups were conducted by the blinded outcome assessor (as described in section 1.2.2.1) before the intervention commenced and after its completion in week 8. Outcome measures included the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) for cognitive function, and the Schizophrenia Quality of Life Scale (SQLS) to evaluate overall quality of life.

Outcome assessments were conducted using a blinded evaluation method. To maintain blinding, all assessments (PANSS, MoCA, and SQLS) were performed by the dedicated outcome assessor (a research nurse) who was not involved in any intervention delivery or participant allocation, and was unaware of group assignment. This assessor had received standardized training on the assessment tools. All measurements were conducted twice: once before the intervention and once after the 8-week intervention period. To minimize confounding, the study protocol explicitly stipulated that participants should not initiate new individual psychotherapy or other structured psychosocial interventions outside the trial protocol during the 8-week study period. This was communicated to all participants and clinical staff.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated a priori based on the primary outcome (PANSS total score). The calculation was informed by an unpublished pilot study (n = 20) conducted at our center under the same ethical approval (HSY-IRB-PJ-SX202114-GGY), which observed a large effect size (Cohen‘s d ≈ 0.85) on PANSS following a similar intervention.For the main trial, we conservatively estimated a moderate effect size (d = 0.65), which is more typical of non-pharmacological interventions in chronic schizophrenia populations. To detect this effect with 80% power at a two-sided a of 0.05, approximately 38 participants per group were required. Factoring in an estimated 10% attrition rate, the target sample size was 42 per group. However, due to the constrained flow of eligible patients meeting all inclusion criteria within the single-center study period, a final sample of 35 participants per group (total N = 70) was enrolled. A post-hoc analysis indicated that with the achieved sample size and the observed effect size (see Results), the study retained approximately 75% power to detect a significant between-group difference on the primary outcome.

Data collection

All outcome measures (PANSS, MoCA, and SQLS) were assessed by the blinded outcome assessor described in section 1.2.2.1. Assessments were conducted at two time points: at baseline (before the intervention) and immediately after the completion of the 8-week intervention period.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. The effects of the intervention were analyzed using a 2 (Group: Intervention vs. Control) × 2 (Time: Pre- vs. Post-intervention) repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) for the primary outcome (PANSS total score) and secondary outcomes (MoCA and SQLS scores). This model formally tests the Group × Time interaction effect, which indicates whether the change over time differs between groups. Following a significant interaction, simple effects analyses were conducted using paired t-tests (for within-group changes) and independent samples t-tests (for between-group comparisons at each time point). Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Comparison of PANSS scores between the two groups

For the primary outcome (PANSS total score), a 2 (Group: Intervention vs. Control) × 2 (Time: Pre- vs. Post-intervention) repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) revealed a significant Group × Time interaction effect (F(1, 68) = 23.96, p < 0.001). Discussion

Schizophrenia is one of the most severe forms of mental illness, characterized by a protracted disease course with a high propensity for recurrence and exacerbation. Epidemiological data indicate that the global prevalence of schizophrenia is approximately 0.4% [19], In China, schizophrenia represents a major public health challenge due to its typically early onset, chronic course, high degree of disability, and profound impact on social and occupational functioning, which collectively impose a substantial burden on participants, families, and the healthcare system [20]. Middle-aged and elderly people with schizophrenia often experience profound impairments in social functioning and cognitive abilities, severely compromising their quality of life and posing significant risks to their overall well-being and safety. In light of these challenges, the purpose of this study was to evaluate the effectiveness of group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring in this patient population, with the goal of providing evidence-based strategies to support their rehabilitation and recovery.

Group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring improves positive and negative symptoms in middle-aged and elderly individuals with schizophrenia

Findings from this study demonstrated that after the intervention, participants in the intervention group had significantly lower scores in positive symptoms, negative symptoms, general psychopathology, and total PANSS scores compared to the control group. These findings suggest that group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring can effectively alleviate positive symptoms such as hallucinations and delusions, while also mitigating negative symptoms like affective blunting and social withdrawal. Group reminiscence therapy facilitates the reconstruction of self-identity by encouraging participants to share personal histories and meaningful memories, thereby enhancing social interaction and reducing feelings of loneliness [21]. Simultaneously, cognitive restructuring interventions guide individuals in developing positive self-dialogue, helping them reframe maladaptive coping strategies and improving their cognitive function and adaptability [22]. This combined therapeutic approach offers a comprehensive intervention model aimed at enhancing participants’ quality of life and social functioning. For middle-aged and elderly individuals with schizophrenia, the hospitalization period is often prolonged, and the rehabilitation process tends to be slow. To sustain treatment benefits, individuals require long-term, systematic psychological support and social skills training. While reminiscence therapy may sometimes evoke negative emotions due to the nature of recalling past experiences, which could potentially hinder recovery, integrating cognitive restructuring techniques provides participants with tools to analyze, understand, and address these emotions more scientifically. This helps participants develop adaptive coping strategies and fosters more constructive cognitive patterns, thereby maximizing the therapeutic efficacy of reminiscence-based interventions.

Group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring improves cognitive function in middle-aged and elderly individuals with schizophrenia

Cognitive impairment is closely associated with the overall symptomatology, prognosis, and functional recovery of patients with schizophrenia [23, 24]. Despite advances in pharmacological treatments, current medications have limited efficacy in improving cognitive function in these participants. Furthermore, conventional interventions such as pharmacotherapy and electroconvulsive therapy may, in some cases, exacerbate cognitive deficits [25]. Therefore, improving cognitive function in schizophrenia cannot rely only on medication, and non-pharmacological interventions play an equally crucial role. The findings of this study demonstrated that the combination of group reminiscence therapy and cognitive restructuring significantly enhanced patients’ cognitive function. This integrated intervention not only provided participants with a warm, reflective environment to revisit their past but also utilized structured cognitive restructuring techniques to help them rebuild accurate perceptions of reality. During the intervention process, participants were encouraged to share personal experiences and memories, which fostered social interaction, facilitated emotional expression, and reinforced self-identity. Concurrently, cognitive restructuring strategies guide participants in recognizing and challenging illogical or negative thought patterns, thereby promoting improvements in cognitive functioning and enhancing problem-solving abilities. It is important to note that the successful implementation of this combined therapy requires therapists to possess both solid professional expertise and a high degree of empathy. Ensuring that participants receive care within a safe and supportive environment is essential for facilitating the gradual restoration of cognitive health.

This study revealed that, following 8 weeks of group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring, the intervention group exhibited significantly lower SQLS scores compared to the control group, indicating a marked improvement in quality of life. Existing research highlights the pivotal role of social support in enhancing the quality of life of individuals with schizophrenia. Social support mitigates the psychological impact of stressors, promotes emotional well-being, and is essential for maintaining overall health. Higher levels of social support are consistently associated with better quality of life outcomes in this population [26–27]. These findings align with the results of the present study. By integrating group reminiscence therapy with cognitive restructuring, the intervention provided participants with meaningful opportunities to participate in social activities, fostering positive emotional experiences, alleviating negative emotions, and reducing feelings of loneliness. Furthermore, group reminiscence therapy facilitated interpersonal interactions among participants, enhancing peer communication and promoting the development of supportive social networks. This collective support structure not only contributed to emotional resilience but also played a crucial role in improving the overall quality of life of middle-aged and elderly individuals with schizophrenia.

When interpreting the findings, the specific characteristics of our sample must be considered. Our inclusion criteria (age ≥ 55 years) selected for a subset of patients with either later-onset illness or a prolonged but stable chronic course managed in long-term inpatient settings. Consequently, the observed average age of onset in our sample was higher than the typical early-adulthood onset of schizophrenia, and the average illness duration was substantial. These demographic and clinical features of our inpatient cohort may influence the generalizability of the results and should be accounted for when extrapolating the findings to the broader schizophrenia population.

Conclusion

Group reminiscence therapy combined with cognitive restructuring has been shown to effectively alleviate psychiatric symptoms, enhance cognitive function, and improve the well-being in middle-aged and elderly participants with schizophrenia. As a structured and evidence-based nursing intervention model, this combined approach facilitates the gradual improvement of participants’ functional abilities across multiple domains of daily living. This study offers important insights into the clinical treatment, rehabilitation, and nursing care of middle-aged and elderly individuals with schizophrenia, offering a practical reference for integrated psychosocial interventions. However, this study has certain limitations. The insufficient sample size may restrict the generalizability and external validity of the results. Future research is recommended to expand the sample size and conduct multicenter studies to further verify and validate these findings, thereby enhancing the reliability and practical relevance of the conclusions.
