Comparative investigation into the immune efficiency of the single intradermal and intramuscular delivery of foot-and-mouth disease inactivated vaccine in swine
Authors: Weijun Cao, Jingwen Li, Fan Yang, Zixiang Zhu, Wei Zhang, Haixue Zheng, Yanming Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•ID injection of FMD inactivated vaccine induces weaker and less uniform FMDV-specific antibody levels compared to traditional IM injection.•Detection of the expression levels of IL-4, IL-6, and IFN-γ in porcine serum showed that the cellular and humoral immune responses induced by the ID vaccination were weaker than that by the IM injection in the early stages.•The IM injection elicits higher percentages of CD8+ T cells producing IFN-γ than ID injection.•IM injection induces higher expression of transcription factors involved in B cell maturation and differentiation than ID injection.

Introduction

Foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) is an acute and highly contagious viral disease caused by the foot-and-mouth disease virus (FMDV), primarily affecting cloven-hoofed animals. The disease poses a significant threat to global livestock industries and international trade, causing substantial economic losses (de Los Santos et al., 2018; Donaldson et al., 2018). Inactivated FMD vaccines have been instrumental in preventing and controlling the disease worldwide, particularly in the Americas and the European Union, with some regions in China achieving near-eradication. Intramuscular (IM) injection is a widely used method for vaccine administration due to its simplicity and minimal training requirements. However, previous studies have shown that intradermal (ID) administration can elicit stronger immune responses by enhancing the speed and coverage of vaccination (Kim and Prausnitz, 2011).

The skin is a highly immunogenic site for vaccination, containing various antigen-presenting cells (APCs), such as Langerhans cells, dermal dendritic cells, and dermal macrophages (Nguyen and Soulika, 2019). These APCs are critical in capturing, processing, and presenting antigens to T cells. Meanwhile, these APCs release chemokines and cytokines, recruiting additional immune cells and facilitating lymphatic drainage of antigens (Kupper and Fuhlbrigge, 2004; Oreskovic et al., 2019). Importantly, ID immunization offers several advantages over IM injection. It reduces vaccine dosage, minimizes animal discomfort, enhances pork quality, decreases the risk of needle-borne diseases, and avoids interference from maternally derived antibodies (Azad and Rojanasakul, 2006; Chase et al., 2008; Martin-Valls et al., 2023). The route of immunization significantly impacts vaccine performance due to differences in local microenvironment, antigen localization, and presentation, thereby affecting the final immune response (Glenn et al., 2003; Zhao et al., 2023).

With the swine industry in China facing profitability challenges, cost reduction has become a priority. The ID vaccination method, an alternative to IM injection, offers benefits such as reduced vaccine dosage, lower costs, and increased vaccination efficiency (Chase et al., 2008; Hickling et al., 2011). Moreover, advancements in ID injection methods and equipment allow for precise control during the injection process. Given that FMD vaccination is mandatory for most pig breeding enterprises, understanding the immune responses induced by ID versus IM immunization is of paramount importance. However, the differences in immune responses to the commercial inactivated FMD vaccine using the ID and IM immunization routes in pigs remain unclear. Therefore, this study conducted a comparative analysis of the immune responses to the inactivated FMD vaccine rA/P1-FMDV using both immunization routes, providing valuable immunological data to inform clinical decision-making regarding the selection of ID or IM immunization for FMD vaccines.

Materials and methods

Antigen and vaccine preparation

The chimeric vaccine strain of rA/P1-FMDV constructed by our lab previously in 2015 (Zheng et al., 2015), which is one of the recommended FMDV vaccine for livestock in China, and was used for the ID and IM vaccination of pigs in this study. rA/P1-FMDV was propagated in BHK-21 suspension cells and subsequently inactivated with binary ethyleneimine at a final concentration of 2 mM at 31 °C for 30 h. Before emulsification, effective antigens were quantified using size-exclusion high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Yang et al., 2015). The purified antigen was then emulsified with an oil adjuvant (Montanide™ ISA 201VG) in an equal weight ratio. The vaccine used in this study consisted 25 μg/ml of rA/P1-FMDV antigen per pig before immunization.

Animal experiments

Two immunization routes (ID and IM) were carried out and compared in this study. A total of 18 eight-week-old pigs, all negative for FMD antibodies, were randomly divided into three groups (Table 1). The ID and IM groups (6 pigs each) were vaccinated with 0.4 ml of FMD vaccine (rA/P1-FMDV) containing 10 μg of 146S viral antigen. Given that the needleless intradermal injection device can only deliver 0.2 ml of vaccine at a time, we administered the vaccine in 2 separate injections, each consisting of 0.2 ml, for a total volume of 0.4 ml. The control group (6 pigs) received 0.4 ml of PBS. The rectal temperatures were recorded continuously for 5 days post-immunization (dpi), and the adverse reactions at the injection site were observed daily. The ID Immunoinjector, provided by Chengdu Hanslid Medical Technology Company, is a battery-powered device capable of immunizing up to 5000 pigs in a single session. A FMD liquid-phase blocking ELISA (LPBE) was used to screen candidate pigs (FMDV-serological negative pigs) according to the World Organisation for Animal Health (WOAH) standard method (OIE, 2018). FMDV-specific antibody titers were measured weekly using the LPBE method established with the A/WH/CHA/09 strain, following the WOAH standard protocol. Detection of FMDV-specific antibodies in pig serum

Serum samples were collected from individual pigs at 0, 7, 14, 28, 35, and 42 dpi and evaluated for FMDV-specific antibodies using the LPBE kit as described above.

Cytokines quantification

To quantify and compare the relative levels of cytokines in the pigs from each group with the two immunization methods, the expression of four cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-4, and IL-6) in porcine serum samples collected at 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28 dpi was measured using a commercially available ELISA kit (Solarbio). The concentrations of the indicated cytokines were determined by plotting absorbance values against standard curves.

Reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

The expression levels of four genes (BCMA, IRF4, Bach-2, and MCL-1) in the peripheral blood of pigs vaccinated via different routes or inoculated with PBS were measured by qRT-PCR. Total RNA was extracted using TRizol reagents (Omega) and reverse-transcribed using the 2 × M5 HiPer UltraSYBR Mixture (Mei5 Biotech) in the QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR system. The qPCR procedure included reverse transcription at 50 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles at 94 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 20 s. β-actin was used as a reference control. Flow cytometry assay (FCM)

Flow cytometry assay was performed to quantify the immune cell populations. To assess the changes in lymphocytes components following the vaccination with two different immunization routes, anti-coagulant blood was collected aseptically from the anterior vena cava of pigs in each group on 28 dpi. The collected blood was transferred to a centrifuge tube containing anticoagulant. After adding the lymphocyte dissociation solution, the tube was centrifuged once more to eliminate the bottom layer of red blood cells. The layer containing the lymphocytes was carefully aspirated, washed, and resuspended twice to ensure purity. The red blood cells were lysed and removed to isolate the immune cells, and the immune cells were then stimulated with rA/P1-FMDV vaccine antigen and cultured at 37 °C for 36 h with 1 μL of protein transport inhibitor per well (BD Biosciences, USA). The cells were then stained with the following monoclonal antibodies (mAbs): anti-CD3 FITC, anti-CD4 PEcy5, anti-CD8 PE, anti-CD21 Bv421, anti-IFN-γ PEcy7, and anti-IL-2 Alexa Fluor 700. FACS controls included unstained cells or cells stained with individual mAbs and appropriate isotype controls.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8. One-way ANOVA was followed by Tukey's test and Student's t-test. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Significance levels: *, P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant; **, P value < 0.01 was very significant; ***, P value < 0.001 was statistically highly significant; ns, P value ≥ 0.5 was not statistically significant.

Results

Adverse reactions after immunization and impact of two different immunization routes on anti-FMDV specific antibodies in pigs

To characterize the immune responses to two different immunization routes, we collected peripheral blood on days 0, 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, and 42 dpi, measured FMDV-specific antibodies, performed gene expression analyses, and assessed key immunological parameters of B and T cell-specific responses (Fig. 1A). Rectal temperatures of all pigs were monitored for five consecutive days post-immunization. No pigs in the ID or IM groups exhibited elevated body temperatures or obvious injection site injuries (Fig. 1B and 1C). By 42 dpi, in comparison to IM groups, the ID groups showed lower levels of FMDV-specific antibodies, particularly in the pigs 5297 and 5299. Despite all pigs receiving 10 μg of viral antigen, the smaller volume of a single ID injection (two injections of 0.2 mL each time) compared to the IM route (0.4 mL) may have increased variability in the ID vaccination process (Table 1). Cellular and humoral immune responses of FMD vaccine elicited by two different immunization routes in pigs

The levels of IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, and IFN-γ in porcine serum samples were quantified by ELISA. At 7 dpi, the mean concentration of IL-4 in the ID group (1689 pg/mL) was significantly lower than in the IM group (8123 pg/mL) (P < 0.001), with this disparity diminishing over time and no significant differences noted at 14, 21, and 28 dpi (Fig. 2A). Similarly, IL-6 levels in the ID group at 7 dpi (305 pg/mL), 14 dpi (263 pg/mL), 21 dpi (571 pg/mL), and 28 dpi (382 pg/mL) were consistently lower than those in the IM group at 7 dpi (2193 pg/mL) (P < 0.05), 14 dpi (1620 pg/mL), 21 dpi (1430 pg/mL), and 28 dpi (631 pg/mL) (Fig. 2B). IFN-γ levels in the ID group at 7 dpi (148 pg/mL), 14 dpi (242 pg/mL), 21 dpi (94 pg/mL), and 28 dpi (120 pg/mL) were consistently lower than those in the IM group at 7 dpi (382 pg/mL), 14 dpi (779 pg/mL), 21 dpi (487 pg/mL), and 28 dpi (351 pg/mL) as well (Fig. 2C). Although no significant differences were observed in IL-2 levels between the two groups, a slightly higher expression of IL-2 was noted in the IM group (Fig. 2D). These findings suggest that the two distinct immunization routes elicit different cellular immune responses, with IM vaccination inducing a stronger response than ID vaccination in the early stages.

Impact of FMD vaccine on B cell maturation and activation by different immunization routes

BCMA, IRF4, Bach-2, and MCL-1 are involved in B cell maturation and activation. We assessed the impact of the FMD vaccine on the mRNA expression levels of BCMA, IRF4, Bach-2, and MCL-1 in porcine peripheral blood lymphocytes using qPCR. At 28 dpi, the expression levels of these genes in the vaccination groups were upregulated compared with the PBS group. In addition, the expression levels of these genes were lower in the ID group than that in the IM group, although statistical analysis did not yield significant differences between the two groups (Fig. 3). Impact of FMD vaccine on T cells and B cells immune responses via different immunization routes

To further investigate the immunological differences between the two immunization routes, we measured cytokine production at 28 dpi using flow cytometry. The IM group exhibited partly higher percentages of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and B cells producing IFN-γ and IL-2 than the ID group (Fig. 4). IFN-γ, a pivotal cytokine regulating cellular immune responses, plays a substantial role in controlling and eliminating pathogens in various viral infections. CD8+ T cells can effectively clear target cells infected with intracellular pathogens by producing cytokines, with the ID group expressing IFN-γ at significantly lower levels than the IM group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). B cells can promote macrophage activation by secreting IFN-γ, thereby facilitating the innate immune response to intracellular infections. Effector B cells (Be-1 and Be-2) can secrete cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-12, and IL-4, reinforcing and stabilizing the cytokine profile of effector T-helper (Th) 1 and Th2 cells (Lund and Randall, 2010). These data suggest that the IM route can induce a stronger T cells and B cells immune responses compared with the ID route. Discussion

FMD is a highly contagious and economically devastating viral disease affecting livestock globally. FMD vaccines have played a crucial role in the livestock industry (Medina and Diaz San Segundo, 2024), with IM injection remaining the predominant method of administration due to its widespread acceptance, cost-effectiveness, and simplicity. The depot effect and extent of antigen presentation are key factors in the immunogenicity of the IM route, as only a few APCs can infiltrate striated muscle (Powell, 1996; Zuckerman, 2000). In contrast, the skin contains a high concentration of resident and recruited immuno-competent cells specializing in antigen presentation. Furthermore, the skin facilitates rapid trafficking of activated cells to draining lymph nodes, initiating the adaptive immune response (Kupper and Fuhlbrigge, 2004). Various vaccine delivery systems, such as microneedles (MNs), intradermal high-density microarray patches (HD-MAP), and others, have been developed to harness the full potential of vaccines (Choo et al., 2021; Laurent et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2022), with the ID route being evaluated and improved in swine vaccines like PRRSV and PCV2 (Jiang et al., 2021; Martin-Valls et al., 2023).

Vaccination induces persistent humoral immunity by triggering the germinal center (GC) reaction, an intricate immune response that produces memory B cells and plasma cells, which protect against the infection. To meet the antigen dosage required for intradermal immunization, the antigen was highly purified and concentrated during vaccine preparation, followed by emulsification. Typically, when antigen is highly purified and concentrated, a certain concentration of phosphate salts is added to prevent antigen degradation. However, to avoid potential skin damage in pigs caused by phosphate salts during intradermal immunization, phosphate salts were not added to the vaccine preparation in this study. This omission may have led to a certain level of antigen degradation, which in turn could have contributed to the suboptimal immunogenicity of the vaccine. In animal experiments, the ID group produce less uniform levels of FMDV-specific antibodies than IM groups (Table 1). Notably, two pigs (5297 and 5299) in the ID group produced lower antibody levels, likely due to random variation in the ID injection process. ELISA assessments of IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, and IFN-γ secretion levels in porcine serum following ID and IM injections revealed significant differences in IL-4 levels at 7 dpi, with the ID group showing lower levels compared to the IM group (ID group: 1689 pg/mL vs. IM group: 8123 pg/mL), but this difference gradually diminished over time. IL-6 is involved in B-cell differentiation and antibody production, and its levels determine the immunization efficacy and protection provided by vaccines (Cox et al., 2011). IL-6 and IFN-γ levels were consistently lower in the ID group, with no significant differences observed in IL-2 between the two routes.

Differentiation of antibody-secreting cells from B cells is controlled by a network of antagonistic transcription factors. Transcription factors including BCMA, IRF4, Bach-2, and MCL-1 play integral roles in the development of mature B cells and the differentiation or survival of activated B cells (Klein et al., 2006; Lightman et al., 2019; Nutt et al., 2015; O'Connor et al., 2004; Peperzak et al., 2013). At 28 dpi, although statistical analysis did not yield significant differences between the two groups, the expression levels of BCMA, IRF4, Bach-2, and MCL-1 were weaker in the ID injection group than that in the IM group.

CD4+ helper T cells differentiate into Th1 or Th2 effector cells after activation, with distinct cytokine expression patterns and immune functions. Effector Th1 cells produce proinflammatory cytokines such as IFN-γ, IL-2, and TNF-β, which organize inflammatory centers and enhance cellular immune responses. In contrast, effector Th2 cells produce a different profile of cytokines (IL-4, IL-5, IL-9, IL-10, IL-13, etc.) that instruct B cells to proliferate and differentiate into antibody-secreting plasma cells (Croft and Swain, 1995; Dong and Flavell, 2000). IL-6 has a wide variety of functions, including its involvement in B-cell differentiation and antibody production (Van Snick, 1990), and it can polarize naïve CD4+ T cells into Th2 cells by potentiating the initial production of IL-4 by activated CD4+ T cells. The IM injection group exhibited higher percentages of CD8+ T cells producing IFN-γ than the ID group. Although each pig in this comparative evaluation was injected with 10 μg of 146S FMD antigen, and mineral oil-based adjuvants (ISA 201VG) were specifically optimized for IM vaccination, which have been developed for the formulation of Water-in-Oil-in-Water (W/O/W) emulsions. The adjuvant may be better suited for the IM injection route, which could explain why the antibodies produced in the IM injection group are more uniform. We speculated that the optimal dose of adjuvants for the ID route of immunization may require further in-depth discovery. A previous study found that adjuvant ISA 207 is a more effective intradermal immunological adjuvant than ISA 201 (Hwang et al., 2024, 2019), particularly when saponin (Quil-A) and aluminum hydroxide (Al(OH)3) were added to the vaccine at the same time to enhance its immunogenicity (Kim et al., 2023). The immune response is influenced by the emulsion type, particle size, and viscosity of adjuvant emulsions. Administering an oil-in-water adjuvanted FMD vaccine twice at 14-day intervals resulted in specific antibody levels comparable to IM when using 1/10 of the inoculation dose for ID injection (Lee et al., 2024), although this adjuvant type is not used in swine FMD vaccines in China.

The most effective method to assess the efficacy of the two immunization routes is through challenge testing. However, in this study, despite using 10 µg of 146S FMD antigen per pig and the limited of pigs used (6 pigs per group), our experience indicates that the vaccinated pigs developed FMDV-specific antibody levels that were insufficient to 100 % withstand a challenge with 1000 SID50 at 28 dpi. Given the natural variability in pigs, the small sample size limits statistical power. Two low responders in the ID group heavily skewed results. Therefore, challenge testing was not performed. This study aims to investigate the antibody immune response differences between pure FMD vaccines containing only antigen and adjuvant. ID responses may have been weaker despite theoretical advantages. The unclear mechanisms for antigen retention in the skin and distribution of oil adjuvants, as well as the injection techniques and limitations of the injection device, may prevent ID injection from playing a more significant role in the use of FMD vaccines.

Taken together, based on the results of this study, in the case of single-dose immunization, and the FMD vaccines contain only antigens and the currently available commercial adjuvants (ISA 201VG), we found that ID injections of FMD inactivated vaccine induce weaker initial humoral and cellular immune responses compared to IM injections at the early stages. More dose-ranging and adjuvant-optimization studies should be performed in the future. Although ID immunization routes are a viable option for FMDV inactivated vaccines in the future, further in-depth studies on optimal antigenic ratios and adjuvant preferences are needed. Otherwise, there is a less consistent immune response compared to IM injections when using ID immunization with currently used FMD vaccines. ID injection may elicit a weaker immune response compared to IM injection at suboptimal (non-protective) vaccine dose.
