Experiential learning in urologic single-port robotic surgery transition training: a qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Young Gyu Kwon, Se Young Choi, Hye Young Park, Yu Seob Shin, Chan Woong Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transitioning from multi-port to single-port robotic systems in urologic surgery requires significant team adaptation; however, the experience of clinicians during this shift remains underexplored. Therefore, this study aims to examine the effect of single-port transition training on learning experiences and perceived readiness among healthcare providers, using Kolb’s experiential learning theory. Eleven physicians and 22 operating room nurses completed phased single-port transition training at a university hospital in Seoul (February–May 2025) and responded to open-ended questionnaires analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis. Most experiences aligned with Kolb’s learning cycle: concrete experience with single-port articulation, reflective comparison of single- and multi-port routines, abstract conceptualization of single-cannula principles, and active experimentation with collision prevention and workflow strategies. Some experienced physicians reported limited learning gains, suggesting that prior expertise reduces engagement. Nurses emphasized docking efficiency and spatial management, whereas physicians prioritized articulation control and technique refinement. Both groups reported greater confidence and interprofessional coordination. These findings suggest that integrating hands-on practice with structured reflection supports adaptation; however, the relationship between perceived readiness and clinical performance requires further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11701-026-03484-5.

Introduction

Robotic surgery is a key innovation in minimally invasive procedures and a standard treatment modality for major malignancies in urology, gynecology, and general surgery [1, 2]. In the Republic of Korea, its use has expanded rapidly since its introduction in 2005 [3]. Early development focused on multi-port (MP) systems; however, recent advances have shifted attention to single-port (SP) platforms, enabling single-incision surgery with less invasiveness and better cosmetic outcomes [4]. A systematic review shows that SP platforms have safety and efficacy comparable to those of conventional MP approaches, with potential advantages in cosmetic satisfaction and reduced postoperative pain [5].

The da Vinci SP system inserts three robotic arms and a camera through a single cannula, improving access to confined anatomical spaces such as the deep pelvis in radical prostatectomy and the retroperitoneum in partial nephrectomy [6, 7]. Unlike MP systems with independently moving arms, SP systems require articulating instruments to be manipulated in confined spaces, necessitating different spatial perception and instrument-control skills [8]. Additionally, the single-cannula configuration alters team positioning, port-site access, and intraoperative coordination, requiring individual skill adaptation and team-wide adjustments in communication and workflow.

These platform-specific demands create educational challenges, as MP-oriented training may not cover competencies required for SP practice. SP transition training typically combines simulation-based skill drills, hands-on practice with animal tissue models, and team-based rehearsals designed to develop SP-specific competencies in instrument articulation, docking, and workflow coordination. Effective robotic surgery requires advanced spatial perception, refined hand–eye coordination, and coordinated team-based performance [9, 10]—competencies that warrant structured training rather than simple skill transfer [11]. In robotic surgery, perioperative nurses perform critical functions, including system docking and undocking, instrument exchange, intraoperative troubleshooting, and real-time spatial coordination with console surgeons. These responsibilities expand substantially during SP procedures owing to the constrained single-cannula workspace and altered port-site geometry. However, current curricula primarily emphasize simulator-based technical skill acquisition and individual performance metrics [12, 13], with limited emphasis on team adaptation to new platforms. Although emerging evidence suggests that interdisciplinary robotic surgery training improves workflow efficiency and reduces procedural errors [14, 15], systematic reviews report a persistent gap between team-level challenges and available interventions in robotic-assisted surgery [16]. Previous survey-based research identifies barriers in robotic surgery training, including limited hands-on exposure and limited trainee autonomy [17, 18]; however, studies qualitatively examining the transition from the perspective of the entire surgical team remain limited.

Kolb’s experiential learning theory [19] holds that effective learning follows a four-stage cycle—concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation—turning experience into practical wisdom [20]. Robotic surgery education aligns with this framework by integrating simulation-based practice, structured feedback, conceptual learning, and supervised clinical application [21]. Despite this alignment, empirical studies examining the experience of healthcare providers with robotic surgery education remain limited. Existing research primarily focuses on the technical skill acquisition of surgeons [22, 23] rather than that of the entire surgical team, including nurses in essential perioperative roles [24, 25]. Qualitative research on the adaptation of perioperative nurses to robotic surgery highlights challenges, including role ambiguity, insufficient training, and technology-related stress [26, 27], highlighting the need to examine physician and nurse perspectives during platform transitions.

Therefore, this study aims to examine the effect of SP robotic surgery education on the learning experiences of healthcare providers, using Kolb’s experiential learning theory. It addresses three questions: (1) How do SP transition training experiences align with Kolb’s experiential learning cycle? (2) What educational outcomes result from experiential learning? (3) How do learning experiences differ between physicians and nurses?

Addressing these questions is essential for developing evidence-based curricula that support safe and effective team-based adoption of emerging SP platforms, particularly as institutions increasingly transition from established MP systems.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

In this qualitative descriptive study, the learning experiences of healthcare providers during early SP robotic system adoption, grounded in pragmatic epistemology and guided by Kolb’s experiential learning theory, were investigated [28, 29]. A qualitative descriptive design was used to capture the subjective accounts of training experiences of participants in their own terms rather than to generate theory or interpret the essence of lived experience. A single-site design enabled in-depth exploration in a consistent institutional and curricular context during early adoption.

The study was conducted between February and May 2025 at a 700-bed tertiary center in Seoul that performs over 700 robotic surgeries annually. After introducing the da Vinci SP system alongside the existing Xi system, the institution implemented targeted transition training based on the da Vinci Technical Training Pathway (Intuitive Surgical Korea, Inc., Seoul, Republic of Korea). This study focused on initial transition phases—didactic instruction, simulation, and supervised rehearsal—excluding later surgeon-led mentorship stages (TR-200 to TR-500). One investigator (S.Y.C.) was the lead SP surgeon, while two (Y.G.K. and C.W.K.) had no direct program involvement, enabling insider and outsider perspectives.

Participant selection and characteristics

Criterion-based purposive sampling was used to recruit all physicians and operating room (OR) nurses who completed SP transition training during the study period; inclusion criteria were (1) SP training completion during the study period, (2) current or anticipated involvement in robotic surgery workflows, and (3) questionnaire completion. Exclusion criteria included individuals without perioperative roles and questionnaires lacking sufficient content for coding. Of the 38 eligible personnel, 33 returned completed questionnaires (86.8% response rate), including 11 physicians and 22 OR nurses. The sample size was guided by the concept of information power, based on the narrow study aim, specific participant group, and theory-informed analytical framework [29]. All sessions were led by certified clinical application specialists under the supervision of experienced console surgeons. Completion required fulfilling all components sequentially, including simulator proficiency benchmarks, TR-100 completion, and rehearsal participation. No formal pass/fail threshold was set; real-time instructor feedback was the primary method for identifying skill deficits.

Data collection

Data were collected using structured open-ended questionnaires administered in person on paper within the institutional educational quality assurance process. Questionnaires were distributed by two investigators (Y.G.K. and C.W.K.), not involved in training delivery, with voluntary participation and no effect of responses on training evaluation. Written questionnaires were used instead of interviews to accommodate clinical scheduling constraints [30], allow reflective responses [31], and minimize social desirability bias in hierarchical clinical settings [32].

Questionnaire items were developed from Kolb’s four experiential learning stages, supported by a literature review and consultation with three experts in medical education and robotic surgery. Items were refined through pilot testing with three healthcare providers excluded from the main analysis, with feedback leading to the rewording of two items for clarity. Separate questionnaires were developed for physicians and nurses.

The physician questionnaire comprised five open-ended items addressing: (1) memorable learning experiences; (2) knowledge and skills acquired and their clinical application; (3) strengths and limitations of practice-centered training; (4) learning barriers; and (5) suggestions for program improvement. The nurse questionnaire included parallel items adapted to perioperative responsibilities; item 4 was modified to address perceived changes in professional expertise and team collaboration, reflecting the central role of interprofessional coordination in bedside nursing practice during robotic surgery. This role-specific adaptation was informed by literature indicating that the learning needs of perioperative nurses in robotic surgery are closely linked to team dynamics and workflow coordination [24, 27], whereas the learning challenges of physicians are more frequently related to individual skill acquisition and prior expertise [17, 18]. Both questionnaires captured sex and years of clinical experience (Supplementary Material 1).

Questionnaires were distributed within 1 week after training completion. Participants completed them individually in 20–30 min. Responses were collected in Korean and translated into English using a consensus-based process: a bilingual researcher (Y.G.K.) performed the initial forward translation, and a second bilingual team member (S.Y.C.) independently reviewed it against the original Korean text. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved. Although a formal back-translation was not performed, the dual-reviewer process ensured that translated quotations preserved the meaning and nuance of the original responses.

While written questionnaires do not allow real-time probing as interviews, the structured open-ended format with multiple items per domain elicited substantive responses from most participants. Adequacy of data depth was assessed during analysis; responses comprising single-word answers or minimal content (n = 3 items across all participants) were retained for coding but classified as thin data segments.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis [33]: familiarization, initial coding, theme development, theme review, theme definition and naming, and report production. A hybrid inductive–deductive approach was used, with Kolb’s experiential learning cycle serving as a deductive sensitizing framework, while subthemes emerged inductively from the data. Coding was performed manually using Microsoft Word and Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA), selected for accessibility and transparency in maintaining an audit trail of coding decisions.

Three researchers independently reviewed de-identified responses and generated initial codes, yielding 142 codes across the three coders. Codes were compared during iterative meetings (four meetings over 6 weeks); discrepancies were resolved through consensus, resulting in 47 unique codes.

Conceptually related codes were grouped into candidate themes, reviewed for coherence, and defined. While Kolb’s experiential learning cycle guided the overall thematic structure, subthemes were derived inductively, and data not aligning with the framework were not forced into it (Fig. 2).

Methodological rigor

Trustworthiness was ensured using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria [34]. Credibility was established through investigator triangulation: three researchers with complementary expertise—medical education and qualitative methodology (Y.G.K.), clinical urology with SP experience (S.Y.C.), and simulation-based education (C.W.K.)—independently coded the data and refined interpretations through consensus. To reduce social desirability bias, one researcher conducted SP training, while two other researchers distributed and collected anonymous questionnaires. Member checking was not performed because the anonymous questionnaire design precluded respondent re-identification; instead, credibility was strengthened through investigator triangulation and iterative consensus coding. Transferability was supported through a thick description of the training context, participant characteristics, and institutional setting, enabling readers to assess applicability in other contexts.

Results

Experiential learning through single-port robotic transition training

Concrete experience: embodied exposure to unfamiliar technical ecology

Participants consistently identified hands-on engagement with the SP platform as the primary learning entry point. This concrete experience involved SP-specific articulation within the constrained single-cannula workspace, distinct from routine MP practice. Data not aligning with Kolb’s experiential learning cycle

While most participant narratives aligned with Kolb’s four-stage framework, a subset did not clearly fit within the experiential learning cycle. These responses were separately coded to maintain analytical transparency.

First, several experienced physicians (≥ 10 years of robotic surgery experience) reported limited perceived learning gains from the training program. One physician noted: “Since I already had extensive robotic surgery experience, the training served more as a refresher than a significant educational experience” (Physician 11, 10 years). Another stated: “The training content was relatively simple and straightforward; nothing was particularly noteworthy” (Physician 2, 18 years). A third observed: “No substantial difference was perceived compared with other surgical training programs” (Physician 3, 20 years).

Second, one physician explicitly identified prior MP expertise as a barrier to engagement: “The overconfidence from extensive MP experience—assuming SP would not be significantly different—hindered learning” (Physician 5, 10 years).

Third, some responses focused primarily on logistical and practical concerns—including scheduling difficulties, travel burden to training centers, and time constraints for completing required modules—rather than on learning processes.

In summary, three categories of responses fell outside Kolb’s four-stage framework: limited perceived learning gains among experienced physicians, prior expertise as a barrier to engagement, and logistical concerns unrelated to learning processes.

Discussion

Theoretical contributions

This study provides qualitative evidence supporting Kolb’s experiential learning theory as a robust framework for understanding the transition to SP surgery. The findings indicate that this transition extends beyond technical skill acquisition to include unlearning established MP practices and reorganizing sensorimotor coordination for single-cannula geometry. Most learning narratives followed the four-stage experiential cycle.

However, physicians with extensive MP experience report limited engagement, suggesting that Kolb’s experiential cycle is potentially most applicable when learners encounter sufficient novelty. This is consistent with adult learning theory, suggesting that prior knowledge both facilitates and constrains new learning [19]. One physician identified “overconfidence from extensive MP experience” as a barrier, and logistical factors emerged as contextual influences independent of the experiential cycle. Studies report similar findings in robotic surgery education [17, 18]. Collectively, Kolb’s framework is most applicable when training is transformative rather than incremental.

Synergistic effects of profession-specific expertise

Profession-specific learning emphases emerged in response to structural constraints of the SP system. Nurses highlighted workflow coordination, docking precision, and bedside workspace management—collectively conceptualized as “surgical space management.” This finding is consistent with those of qualitative studies showing that perioperative nurses in robotic surgery develop anticipatory coordination and troubleshooting capabilities [27, 35]. It also aligns with studies showing role expansion beyond traditional perioperative functions, with skills often acquired through informal, practice-based learning rather than structured curricula [26, 27].

In contrast, physicians emphasized adaptation to SP-specific technical demands, including triangulation within the single-cannula workspace, instrument collision avoidance, and recalibration of force feedback. These findings are consistent with those of simulation-based studies showing that MP skills do not readily transfer to SP platforms [13].

With increasing system knowledge, nurses reported a shift from peripheral to more active engagement, consistent with situated learning theory [36] and findings that trained robotic surgery nurses exhibit faster adaptation and greater innovativeness [30].

Design principles for robotic surgery education

Participant accounts suggest several practical implications as context-sensitive recommendations for single-site SP training.

First, transition-focused curricula targeting SP-specific “gap areas” among experienced MP users were most effective when highlighting novel system constraints rather than generic robotic fundamentals. This is consistent with previously proposed competency-stratified curricula [37]. The phased training pathway used in this study may serve as a template for similar platform transitions.

Second, immediate feedback facilitated rapid correction of MP-derived habits, consistent with models emphasizing timely coaching [38].

Third, joint training enlightened surgeons about bedside constraints and enabled nurses to anticipate intraoperative needs, consistent with evidence that team training enhances workflow efficiency [14, 15] and systematic reviews highlighting the need for team-based interventions in robotic-assisted surgery [16].

Fourth, limited learning gain reported by some experienced physicians suggests that transition curricula may benefit from differentiated, experience-based pathways, including abbreviated foundational modules and extended advanced application sessions for experienced practitioners.

Limitations

This study has some limitations.

First, this single-institution study was conducted at a high-volume tertiary center with established Xi-based practice, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings. Institutions with differing surgical volumes, baseline robotic experience, or organizational cultures may exhibit different learning trajectories. Additionally, the homogeneous context—shared protocols, a consistent training team, and a single specialty—further constrains transferability. Second, reliance on self-reported data reflects perceived rather than objectively measured skills; therefore, readiness and confidence should be interpreted as subjective improvements. Third, the cross-sectional design precluded assessment of long-term retention. Fourth, subsequent mentorship stages (TR-200 to TR-500) were not captured in this study. Fifth, the use of written questionnaires, rather than face-to-face interviews, may have limited response depth by precluding real-time probing and follow-up. This approach was adopted to reduce social desirability bias in a hierarchical clinical setting and accommodate clinical scheduling constraints; nevertheless, future studies could use semi-structured interviews to generate richer qualitative data. Sixth, demographic data were limited to sex and years of clinical experience, as the questionnaires were originally designed for educational quality assurance rather than research purposes. The lack of data on professional level, subspecialty, prior MP experience, and SP exposure limits the interpretation of between-participant differences. Seventh, translation of responses from Korean to English may introduce interpretative bias. Although a structured dual-reviewer consensus process was applied, a formal back-translation procedure was not conducted, potentially limiting the verification of semantic equivalence. Eighth, as most participants had prior MP experience, the findings may not reflect the learning trajectories of novice users.

Conclusions

Hands-on exposure to SP-specific spatial constraints, combined with structured reflection, facilitated reorganization of established practices and increased perceived confidence. Nurses focused on workflow reliability, while physicians emphasized articulation control within confined operative fields. Both groups reported improved readiness and interprofessional coordination; however, experienced physicians reported limited learning gain, suggesting that prior expertise may moderate engagement.

As a single-institution study based on self-reported perceptions, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary insights. The phased training structure provides a curricular framework adaptable to other contexts, though further validation is required. Future research should include longitudinal studies of skill retention and clinical outcomes across the full training pathway (TR-200 to TR-500), multi-institutional comparisons of transition experiences, and objective performance metrics to determine whether perceived gains translate into measurable clinical improvements.
