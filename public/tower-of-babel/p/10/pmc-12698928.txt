CSF Mitochondrial DNA: Biomarker of Body Composition and Energy Metabolism in Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Yasuaki Mizutani, Tsuyoshi Nakai, Yasuhiro Maeda, Reiko Ohdake, Atsuhiro Higashi, Toshiki Maeda, Ryunosuke Nagao, Sayuri Shima, Kazuya Kawabata, Akihiro Ueda, Mizuki Ito, Hirohisa Watanabe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveCerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cell‐free mitochondrial DNA (cf‐mtDNA) is a potential biomarker for Parkinson's disease (PD), but its clinical relevance remains unclear. We investigated associations between CSF cf‐mtDNA levels, body composition, nutritional status, and metabolic biomarkers in PD.MethodsCSF cf‐mtDNA levels, defined as the copy numbers of two regions of the mtDNA circular molecule (mt64‐ND1 and mt96‐ND5), were quantified in 44 PD patients and 43 controls using multiplex digital PCR. The mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio was calculated to estimate mtDNA deletion burden. Associations with clinical features, body composition, serum nutritional markers, and plasma energy metabolism‐related organic acids were examined. Generalized linear models (GLMs) were performed to adjust for confounders.ResultsCSF mt64‐ND1 and mt96‐ND5 levels were lower in PD patients than controls (p = 0.002, p = 0.001), while the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio showed no group difference. GLM analysis identified body composition indices and serum albumin as key determinants of cf‐mtDNA levels. Subgroup analysis showed lower cf‐mtDNA levels in PD patients with preserved body composition and nutritional status. The mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio displayed a biphasic association with body composition and an inverse correlation with plasma 2‐ketoglutaric acid, suggesting a link to energy metabolism.InterpretationCSF cf‐mtDNA levels are reduced in PD and influenced by body composition and nutritional status, supporting their role as a metabolic biomarker. While the cf‐mtDNA deletion ratio remained unchanged, its association with body composition suggests a complex interplay between mitochondrial integrity and metabolism. These findings highlight the relevance of cf‐mtDNA in PD pathophysiology and the need for further study.Keywords: biomarker, movement disorder, Parkinson's disease

Introduction

Parkinson's disease (PD), characterized by progressive dopaminergic neuron loss in the substantia nigra pars compacta and alpha‐synuclein pathology, is strongly linked to mitochondrial dysfunction [1, 2]. This dysfunction involves impairment of the electron transport chain, elevated production of reactive oxygen species, dysregulated mitochondrial quality control, and cellular energy deficits. Both genetic predispositions and environmental exposures are recognized contributors to this mitochondrial dysfunction in PD [3]. Furthermore, primary abnormalities in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) itself, encompassing mutations, haplogroup‐specific polymorphic variants, and maintenance defects leading to multiple deletions and a decrease in copy number, have garnered increasing attention for their potential etiological significance [4].

Mitochondrial DNA encodes genes that are crucial for cellular energy production and for maintaining overall mitochondrial function [5]. mtDNA is released from cells as cell‐free mtDNA (cf‐mtDNA) in response to cell stress or injury, and extracellular release of depolarized mitochondria is also facilitated by compromised mitochondrial quality control [6, 7]. Consequently, cf‐mtDNA is increasingly recognized as a promising biomarker across a spectrum of diseases [8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Beyond its role as a disease biomarker, emerging evidence indicates that cf‐mtDNA may also reflect systemic metabolic states [16, 17, 18]. In the context of PD, studies have shown reduced cf‐mtDNA levels in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of patients with PD compared with healthy controls [19, 20]. To date, a limited number of studies have explored the association between CSF cf‐mtDNA levels and clinical manifestations in PD, with one study reporting a link to cognitive impairment and treatment status [20]. However, the clinical relevance of reduced CSF cf‐mtDNA levels in PD, particularly in relation to systemic metabolic states and various clinical phenotypes, remains largely unexplored. Moreover, the impacts of nutritional and metabolic interventions on cf‐mtDNA levels and their potential therapeutic implications are yet to be fully elucidated.

To address these critical knowledge gaps, this study aimed to comprehensively investigate the clinical significance of CSF cf‐mtDNA in PD, specifically focusing on its potential as a marker of systemic energy status. First, we quantified CSF cf‐mtDNA levels in patients with PD and compared them with those of controls. Next, within the PD group, we evaluated body composition, serum nutritional markers, and plasma organic acids relevant to energy metabolism. Finally, we examined the relationships between CSF cf‐mtDNA levels and anthropometric parameters, alongside the aforementioned biological fluid markers associated with energy status. This multifaceted approach sought to clarify the role of cf‐mtDNA as a clinically relevant biomarker of systemic energy metabolism in PD.

Methods

Subjects

This study included 44 consecutive patients with PD who were admitted to Fujita Health University Hospital between July 2021 and October 2023. All enrolled patients fulfilled the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) clinical diagnostic criteria for PD [21]. Additionally, 43 age‐ and sex‐matched individuals were recruited as controls. These controls were participants in an ongoing aging registry study at Fujita Health University that collects CSF samples from individuals without neurodegenerative diseases who undergo epidural anesthesia for surgical procedures related to urinary tract stones or benign prostatic hyperplasia in the urology department.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujita Health University Hospital (approval numbers: HM24‐221 and HG21‐015), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Participation was voluntary, with the option to withdraw from the study at any time.

Clinical Evaluation

We conducted clinical evaluations of the patients with PD during their “on” state. A comprehensive assessment of PD‐related symptoms was performed using the Japanese version of the Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS‐UPDRS). Cognitive functions were measured using the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), the Japanese versions of the Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination‐Revised (ACE‐R) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA‐J), as well as the Frontal Assessment Battery (FAB).

Additionally, quality of life and non‐motor symptoms in PD were evaluated using the Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire‐39 (PDQ‐39), Scales for Outcomes in Parkinson's Disease‐Autonomic (SCOPA‐AUT), Geriatric Depression Scale‐15 (GDS‐15), the Japanese version of the Questionnaire for Impulsive–Compulsive Disorders in Parkinson's Disease (J‐QUIP), the Japanese version of the REM Sleep Behavior Disorder Screening Questionnaire (RBDSQ‐J), the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), and Odor Stick Identification Test for Japanese (OSIT‐J) score. The levodopa equivalent daily dose was determined based on the latest conversion formula [22]. The time difference between clinical assessment and collection of CSF and plasma samples in the PD group was a mean of 8.05 ± 14.6 days [mean ± standard deviation (SD), days, range 0–96].

CSF Sample Preparation

CSF samples were collected via standard lumbar puncture after overnight fasting. Within 2 h of collection, the samples were centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min at 4°C. They were then divided into 500 μL aliquots, placed in polypropylene tubes, and stored at −80°C until subsequent analysis. The cf‐mtDNA and nuclear DNA in CSF were analyzed directly from the samples without prior nucleic acid purification, following a modified version of previously described methods using different reagents [5]. CSF samples were solubilized using Kaneka Easy DNA Extraction Kit Version 2 (Kaneka, Tokyo, Japan) [23]. Briefly, 20 μL of CSF was mixed with 17.5 μL of alkaline “solution A” and incubated at 98°C for 8 min. After incubation, the mixture was combined with 2.5 μL of “solution B”. The resulting mixture was used for absolute quantification of nucleic acids using droplet digital PCR (ddPCR).

Blood Sample Preparation

Blood samples were collected by venipuncture using EDTA blood collection tubes immediately after CSF sample collection. The samples were then centrifuged at 1500 × g for 10 min. After centrifugation, plasma was subdivided into 500 μL aliquots to prevent repeated freeze–thaw cycles and immediately stored at −80°C until analysis. Plasma samples were subsequently used for the analysis of organic acids related to energy metabolism.

Quantitative Assessment of Cf‐mtDNA, mtDNA Deletions, and Nuclear Genomes Using ddPCR

Cf‐mtDNA, mtDNA deletions, and nuclear genomes were quantified by ddPCR according to a previously established method [5, 24]. The cf‐mtDNA copy number was determined by absolute quantification of mt64‐ND1 (a region with low deletion frequency) to assess the total cf‐mtDNA molecules and of mt96‐ND5 (a common deletion site within the major arc) to evaluate cf‐mtDNA molecules without deletions. The results were expressed as copies per 1 μL of CSF. Additionally, the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio was used as an estimate of the mtDNA deletion rate. Alongside the above, a multiplex ddPCR assay for nuclear genomes in CSF samples was performed to control for potential cell contamination that could affect cf‐mtDNA measurements, as previously reported [5]. A previously reported freeze–thaw cycle assay confirmed that up to three successive freeze–thaw cycles did not affect CSF cf‐mtDNA levels [25]. Accordingly, all CSF samples, except for the four control samples, were either processed without any freeze–thaw cycle or subjected to a single freeze–thaw cycle, while the four control samples underwent no more than three cycles. Nuclear genomes were quantified by amplifying two single‐copy nuclear genes, human TATA‐box binding protein 1 (TBP1–73 amplicon) and mitochondrial transcription elongation factor (TEFM‐88 amplicon), simultaneously in a multiplex reaction to avoid sampling errors. CSF samples with two or more copies of nuclear genes were considered for exclusion; however, all samples passed this criterion, and therefore no exclusions were required.

Body Composition Analysis

Body composition in the PD group was measured using the InBody 770 multifrequency bioelectrical impedance analysis device (InBody Japan, Tokyo, Japan), as previously described [26]. The evaluated body composition parameters included muscle mass (kg), mineral content (kg), body fat mass (kg), and resting energy expenditure (kcal). Simultaneously, height and body weight were recorded, and body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight divided by height squared (kg/m2). The time difference between body composition analysis and collection of CSF and plasma samples in the PD group was a mean of 21.5 ± 27.5 days (mean ± SD, days, range 0–129). In the control group, anthropometric measures, including height, weight, and BMI, were retrospectively obtained from medical records, based on data collected the day before urological surgical procedures involving CSF sampling.

Measurement of Energy Metabolism‐Related Organic Acids in Plasma

Plasma concentrations of short‐chain fatty acids, medium‐chain fatty acids, and Krebs cycle‐related organic acids were quantified using ultra high performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry. The methods used for these measurements are described in the Appendix S1. In total, the following 17 organic acids were quantified: 12 Krebs cycle metabolites (lactic acid, pyruvic acid, acetoacetic acid, 3‐hydroxybutyric acid, 2‐hydroxybutyric acid, 2‐oxobutyric acid, citric acid, isocitric acid, 2‐ketoglutaric acid (2‐KG), succinic acid, fumaric acid, and malic acid), 2 short‐chain fatty acids (acetic acid and isobutyric acid), and 3 medium‐chain fatty acids (caprylic acid, capric acid, and lauric acid). Additionally, the lactate/pyruvate ratio was calculated.

Serum Nutritional Markers

The following serum nutritional marker data for the PD group were retrospectively collected from medical records: albumin (g/dL), cholinesterase (IU/L), transthyretin (mg/dL), retinol‐binding protein (mg/dL), and total cholesterol (mg/dL). These data were obtained from records within 2 weeks before or after CSF sample collection. Additionally, serum albumin data for the control group, measured on the day before urological surgical procedures involving CSF sampling, were retrospectively collected from medical records.

CSF Neurofilament Light Protein (NfL) Measurement

In this study, CSF NfL was measured as a marker of PD severity [27, 28], reflecting the extent of central neurodegeneration. The levels of CSF NfL were measured using a commercially available enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay kit (NF‐light ELISA RUO, UmanDiagnostics AB, Umeå, Sweden) following the manufacturer's protocol. All assays were conducted on the same day using the same reagents to maintain consistency. Out of the 43 control subjects, 20 were selected as a reference. These 20 control subjects were age‐ and sex‐matched with the PD group.

Statistical Analysis

JMP software, version 17 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) was used for statistical analyses. Significant differences were defined at p < 0.05. Fisher's exact test was used to compare the sex distribution among the two groups.

For the comparison of natural log (ln, base e)‐transformed mt64‐ND1 and mt96‐ND5 levels, as well as for the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio between groups, Student's t‐test was employed for two‐group comparisons, and for multiple group comparisons, a one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test was conducted. The Wilcoxon rank sum test was used to compare other continuous variables between the two groups.

Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation test. To adjust for multiple comparisons, we applied a false discovery rate (FDR) correction, using a significance threshold of 0.05. In addition, for the evaluation of cf‐mtDNA levels and cf‐mtDNA deletion ratios, we added a generalized linear model (GLM) to eliminate the influence of confounding factors. CSF mt64‐ND1 and mt96‐ND5 levels were ln‐transformed prior to GLM analysis. To address potential multicollinearity among variables related to body composition, we applied principal component analysis to generate a composite indicator (Figure S1), referred to as the comprehensive body composition index (CBCI).

The data for these variables were standardized (mean‐centered and scaled by their SD) prior to principal component analysis. The first principal component (PC1), which explained the majority of the variance across these variables, was extracted and used to define the CBCI. The CBCI was calculated as a weighted sum of the standardized variables, using the PC1 coefficients as weights:

This calculation included five variables associated with cf‐mtDNA in patients with PD: height, body weight, muscle mass, mineral content, and resting energy expenditure. The PC1 coefficients for each variable were as follows: height (0.84934), body weight (0.89285), muscle mass (0.99193), mineral content (0.97184), and resting energy expenditure (0.99256).

Finally, throughout the analysis, it became evident that CSF cf‐mtDNA levels were affected by albumin‐based nutritional status and body composition. Based on this observation, the PD cohort was further stratified into subgroups to examine these potential relationships in more detail, resulting in three groups for comparison: controls, and the two PD subgroups for each factor. For nutritional status, the PD cohort was divided into “Sufficient Nutrition PD” and “Suboptimal Nutrition PD” groups using the median serum albumin level (4.0 g/dL) as a threshold. Additionally, patients with a median albumin level were assigned to groups based on other serum nutritional markers (cholinesterase, transthyretin, retinol‐binding protein, and total cholesterol) to ensure balanced group sizes. For body composition, the cohort was divided into “Preserved Body Composition PD” and “Declined Body Composition PD” groups based on the median CBCI (0.10692). Continuous variables were expressed as the mean ± SD.

Comparison of CSF Cf‐mtDNA Levels and Deletion Ratio Between Groups

CSF mt64‐ND1 levels were significantly lower in the PD group compared with the control group (PD group: 4.11 ± 1.08 ln[copies/μL], corresponding to 117.1 ± 176.2 copies/μL; control group: 4.73 ± 0.667 ln[copies/μL], 140.7 ± 101.0 copies/μL; p = 0.002 for ln‐transformed values) (Figure 1A). Similarly, CSF mt96‐ND5 levels were also reduced in the PD group relative to controls (PD group: 3.64 ± 0.968 ln[copies/μL], corresponding to 67.1 ± 103.1 copies/μL; control group: 4.25 ± 0.668 ln[copies/μL], 87.2 ± 65.6 copies/μL; p = 0.001 for ln‐transformed values) (Figure 1B).

Given the significant differences in body weight and BMI between the PD and control groups (both p < 0.001) (Table 1), GLM analyses were employed to adjust for these potential confounding factors and other covariates (Table S1). After adjustment, PD remained a significant factor associated with the decrease in mt64‐ND1 levels (estimate = −0.544, p = 0.013) and mt96‐ND5 levels (estimate = −0.479, p = 0.022). Additionally, serum albumin level emerged as a significant covariate influencing mt96‐ND5 levels (estimate = −0.445, p = 0.039). In contrast, the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio did not differ significantly between the groups (PD group: 0.660 ± 0.208; control group: 0.634 ± 0.130; p = 0.485) (Figure 1C).

Relationships of CSF Cf‐mtDNA Levels and Deletion Ratio With Clinical and Anthropometric Parameters in the PD Group

Table S2 presents the correlations of CSF cf‐mtDNA levels and the mtDNA deletion ratio with clinical parameters in the PD group. After FDR correction, sex distribution was no longer significantly associated with the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio (q = 0.260), and no significant correlations were observed between mt64‐ND1 or mt96‐ND5 levels and other clinical parameters.

In contrast, several anthropometric parameters showed statistically significant associations with mt64‐ND1 levels and the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio, as shown in Table 2. Specifically, mt64‐ND1 levels were inversely correlated with multiple anthropometric parameters, including height, body weight, muscle mass, mineral content, and resting energy expenditure (all q < 0.05). Conversely, the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio showed positive correlations with these same variables, except for body weight, which did not remain significant after FDR correction (q = 0.051). These results indicate that CSF cf‐mtDNA levels and the mtDNA deletion ratio are more strongly associated with anthropometric than with clinical parameters in the PD group. To assess similar associations in the control group, we performed an exploratory analysis of height, weight, and BMI. A significant positive correlation was found between BMI and the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio (rs = 0.320, p = 0.036), without multiple comparison correction (Table S3). These results suggest that the associations observed in controls were more limited than those seen in the PD group.

Modeling Analysis for CSF Cf‐mtDNA Levels and the Deletion Ratio

GLMs for CSF cf‐mtDNA levels and the cf‐mtDNA deletion ratio were constructed using relevant clinical parameters, CBCI, and biological fluid markers, with age at examination included as a potential confounder (Table 4). Discussion

This study confirmed our primary hypothesis by demonstrating reduced levels of CSF cf‐mtDNA in patients with PD relative to controls, whereas the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio—an indicator of mtDNA deletion—remained unchanged when considering the overall PD group. Notably, these findings persisted after adjusting for body weight, BMI, and other potential confounders. Although FDR correction was applied to most exploratory analyses, the primary outcome of lower cf‐mtDNA in PD versus controls was treated as a main endpoint and remained significant in our model.

Although cf‐mtDNA levels showed limited associations with clinical parameters in the PD group, GLM analyses identified inverse correlations between mt64‐ND1 and mt96‐ND5 levels and both body composition indices and serum albumin. Additionally, the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio showed a positive association with body composition and a negative correlation with plasma 2‐KG. Subgroup analysis provided further insight, indicating that cf‐mtDNA reductions were most pronounced in PD patients with preserved nutritional status or body composition. Intriguingly, the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio displayed a biphasic pattern: it was elevated in the Preserved Body Composition PD subgroup relative to controls yet decreased in the Declined Body Composition PD subgroup. Taken together, these results underscore a nuanced picture of cf‐mtDNA dynamics in PD, where overall reductions in cf‐mtDNA levels are accompanied by relatively lower mtDNA deletions in patients who maintain better body composition. This complexity highlights the intricate interplay between cf‐mtDNA, metabolic state, and PD pathophysiology.

Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported reduced CSF cf‐mtDNA levels in neurodegenerative diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease [8, 9], and PD [19]. In Alzheimer's disease, decreased cf‐mtDNA has been associated with the preclinical stage or slowly progressive subtypes, while in PD, similar reductions have been observed early in the disease course. Conversely, elevated CSF cf‐mtDNA levels are documented in acute inflammatory conditions, such as relapsing–remitting multiple sclerosis [29] and anti‐N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate receptor (NMDAR) encephalitis [30], often correlating with symptoms or disease severity. Increased CSF cf‐mtDNA levels have also been reported in patients with mitochondrial deletion and depletion syndromes [31]. These observations indicate a biphasic model in which minimal neuronal damage in early neurodegeneration yields lower cf‐mtDNA release, potentially reflecting primary mtDNA alterations, whereas severe neuronal injury—typical of acute inflammation or advanced disease—prompts a marked increase in cf‐mtDNA levels [32]. In our study, this biphasic phenomenon may account for the apparent paradox seen in subgroup analyses, where cf‐mtDNA levels were generally depressed in PD but rose in individuals with suboptimal nutritional status or body composition. Such a pattern aligns with prior work showing overall reductions in cf‐mtDNA in PD, with later increases linked to disease progression, including cognitive decline and extended treatment duration [20].

While earlier research suggested a decreased mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio in PD, indicative of higher mtDNA deletion rates [5], we found no significant difference in this ratio in the overall PD group. Of note, our study did not include genetic testing to exclude familial PD cases, such as those with LRRK2 mutations. Prior research has shown that CSF mtDNA profiles differ between idiopathic and LRRK2‐related PD [5], raising the possibility that the inclusion of genetically heterogeneous PD cases may have contributed to the lack of replication of earlier findings. However, our subgroup analyses revealed a more complex picture. Among patients with preserved body composition, the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio was significantly higher than in controls, potentially implicating compensatory mechanisms, such as enhanced mitochondrial quality control [33]. By contrast, patients with declined body composition displayed a significantly lower ratio than patients with preserved body composition, highlighting a possible link between advanced mitochondrial dysfunction and loss of body composition. In this context, an elevated ratio in the preserved group might signify adaptive responses aimed at maintaining mitochondrial function despite underlying PD pathology, whereas the lower ratio in the declined group could reflect a breakdown of these compensatory mechanisms in the face of heightened metabolic stress or disease severity.

The observed inverse correlations between cf‐mtDNA levels and body composition or serum albumin in the PD group were initially noted, but these associations did not remain significant after FDR correction. A previous study has demonstrated the associations between mtDNA levels in peripheral leukocytes and body composition measures, such as BMI and visceral fat [34], indicating that mtDNA status and body composition are intricately linked. While our findings did not confirm these relationships at a statistically significant level post‐adjustment, the observed trends warrant further investigation in larger cohorts with greater statistical power. Similarly, although an inverse correlation between CSF mt64‐ND1 levels and serum albumin was initially observed (p = 0.022), this relationship was not statistically significant after FDR correction (q = 0.290). Serum albumin has been identified as a marker of systemic inflammation and metabolic status in PD, and previous studies have suggested its role in predicting cognitive decline and mortality in PD patients [35, 36]. While our results did not confirm a robust association between cf‐mtDNA and serum albumin, further research with larger sample sizes and additional metabolic markers is needed to clarify whether albumin might serve as an indirect marker of mitochondrial function and disease progression in PD.

Moreover, while the mt96‐ND5/mt64‐ND1 ratio initially showed a negative correlation with plasma 2‐KG (p = 0.006), this association also disappeared after FDR correction (q = 0.146). 2‐KG is a pivotal intermediate in both the tricarboxylic acid cycle and the glutaminolysis pathway, playing a crucial role in cellular metabolism [37]. It functions as an antioxidant and an energy substrate [38], and can enhance protein synthesis while inhibiting protein degradation in skeletal muscle [39]. Elevated plasma 2‐KG levels are also associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, particularly in metabolic disorders such as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [40]. Although our findings do not provide definitive evidence of a direct relationship between mtDNA deletions and 2‐KG levels, they highlight the need for further investigation into energy metabolism and mitochondrial function in PD. The loss of significance after multiple comparison correction underscores the exploratory nature of our results, emphasizing the need for validation in larger cohorts. Future studies should account for confounders, such as disease duration, medication effects, and genetic factors, to refine our understanding of cf‐mtDNA dynamics in PD.

This study has several limitations worth noting. Firstly, it was conducted at a single center with a relatively small number of participants. Secondly, this study did not assess CSF mtDNA levels in atypical parkinsonian syndromes, such as multiple system atrophy and progressive supranuclear palsy. Therefore, it remains unclear whether the findings in this study are specific to PD in comparison with these conditions. Thirdly, this study did not include genetic testing for PD, even though previous studies highlighted differences in CSF mtDNA dynamics between idiopathic PD and LRRK2‐related PD [5, 41]. This omission leaves room for further investigation to achieve a more comprehensive understanding of its role in the disease process. Finally, this study lacks longitudinal clinical data and predictive models for CSF mtDNA. Further studies addressing these limitations are crucial to validate the findings and analyses of this study.

In conclusion, this study indicated that CSF cf‐mtDNA levels are reduced in PD and strongly linked to body composition and nutritional status. Additionally, the cf‐mtDNA deletion ratio exhibits a complex pattern of relationships with body composition and is linked to 2‐KG, an essential intermediate in energy metabolism. These findings underscore the potential of cf‐mtDNA and its deletion ratio as a biomarker for systemic energy status and body composition in PD, reflecting a sophisticated interplay between mitochondrial function, metabolic health, and disease progression. Further research is warranted to translate these insights into practical clinical tools for PD diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic monitoring.
