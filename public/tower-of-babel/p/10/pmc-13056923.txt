HiCoBERT: a hierarchical transformer-based framework for Pakistan legal judgment section segmentation with multi-level XAI insights
Authors: Maryam Bibi, Zahoor-Ur Rehman, Khalid Mahmood Awan, Shahid Kamal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In many jurisdictions, legal judgments are written as long continuous narratives in which the functional progression of facts, issues, reasoning, and decision is expressed implicitly rather than through explicit section boundaries. Although recent advances in Transformer-based language models and large language models have significantly improved automated text analysis, these models still struggle with long judicial documents. Standard architectures encode documents as flat token sequences, do not explicitly model dependencies between discourse segments, and are constrained by input token limits, making it difficult to capture the ordered functional structure underlying judicial decisions. To address this limitation, we propose HiCoBERT, a hierarchically contextualized Transformer framework for segmenting functional sections in legal judgments. The model represents a judgment as an ordered sequence of contiguous text segments (fixed-length chunks). It first encodes intra-segment semantics and then models document-level dependencies across segments. This hierarchical design captures the logical flow of judicial decisions, enabling the efficient processing of lengthy judicial documents. Experiments on LeJA, a dataset of Supreme Court of Pakistan judgments curated for this study, show that the proposed framework achieves 0.80 accuracy, 0.70 macro-F1, and 0.79 span-F1, outperforming strong long-document baselines including Longformer, BigBird, and LongT5, as well as structured models such as LegalBERT + CRF. Additional comparisons with modern prompted LLMs (GPT-4o, Gemini Pro, DeepSeek-V3) further contextualize the performance. Cross-dataset evaluation on LegalSeg, a publicly available benchmark of annotated Indian legal judgments, demonstrates robustness under jurisdiction shift, while explainability analyses highlight the importance of hierarchical contextualization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45259-w.

Introduction

Legal judgments are crucial because they are written decisions of courts that set out the outcomes of cases, along with detailed records of the reasoning and facts. Recently, we have seen a surge in Legal NLP research across countries to address tasks such as document case classification, question answering, and document summarization1. However, legal judgments pose unique challenges for NLP systems due to their length, complex legal jargon, and narrative structure. Unlike generic texts, a court judgment is typically organized into distinct sections (e.g., background, issues, arguments, and Decision), often spanning dozens of pages. Effectively processing such documents requires models that can handle long texts and exploit their inherent hierarchical narrative.

One major challenge is that most off-the-shelf NLP models treat a document as a plain sequence of text, ignoring its logical segmentation into judgments2,3. Legal judgments tend to be hierarchical; for example, a judgment may begin with a summary of the case type and claims, proceed to a narration of the facts and evidence, present arguments from the plaintiffs and defendants, and finally state the court’s reasoning and final verdict. Failing to account for this narrative flow can lead to suboptimal performance in downstream tasks. Studies have shown that using the document’s functional elements can significantly improve outcomes. For instance, Gao et al.2 divide each judgment into four units, namely Case type, Claims, Facts, and Result, and showed that using this Type-Claim-Fact-Result segmentation boosts summarization quality. Similarly, in judicial outcome prediction, researchers note that the complex narrative flow of judgments requires identifying all relevant claim segments before making a prediction3. In short, understanding a judgment’s internal formation is key to unlocking its meaning.

Document segmentation can refer to two related but distinct objectives. Structural section segmentation divides a document using formatting and layout cues—such as headings, typography, indentation, page breaks, or templated markers—and answers: “Where does the document structure change?” In contrast, functional (rhetorical) section segmentation divides a document based on the role the text plays in the document’s meaning and progression, answering the question: “What is this part doing?” (e.g., stating facts, formulating issues, providing reasons, or delivering the Decision). In this work, we focus on functional (rhetorical) section segmentation of legal judgments under an ordered section schema. Our goal is to segment each judgment into a sequential set of four functional sections—Concise Statement of the Case → Points for Determination → Reasons → Decision—using the segment content and its document-level context, rather than relying on layout markers or explicit headings. While this schema reflects the typical progression of judicial writing, section labels are assigned based on what the text expresses; the model leverages the expected order as contextual guidance, but does not depend on fixed positions or formatting cues. For clarity, we use the term functional section segmentation throughout the remainder of the paper to refer to this task.

Figure 1 illustrates a typical legal judgment divided into four functional sections: (1) Concise Statement of the Case, (2) Points for Determination, (3) Reasons, and (4) Decision. Our research problem is to segment raw legal judgments into this ordered functional sequence automatically. By modeling a judgment as an ordered sequence of functional sections rather than a flat block of text, we enable more precise downstream processing. For example, we can summarize the facts separately from the court’s reasoning and extract the final Decision directly without having to scan the entire judgment as a flat text.

A common set of baselines for legal-text processing either (i) encodes the document as a single flat sequence for classification4–6, or (ii) assigns labels at the sentence level without an explicit document-level hierarchy7. Such approaches can be effective, but they do not explicitly introduce a segment-to-document hierarchy that is tailored to long judgments and their ordered functional progression. This motivates hierarchical designs that combine strong pretrained encoders with document-level context across segments. In this study, we introduce HiCoBERT, a hierarchically contextualized Transformer-based framework for functional section segmentation that models a legal judgment as an ordered sequence of contiguous segments, rather than a single flat token sequence. HiCoBERT first partitions each judgment into fixed-length segments, encodes each segment using a domain-specific encoder (LegalBERT), and then contextualizes segment representations across the entire document to capture document-level context and the judgment’s overall flow. This design is intended to better support section boundary decisions in long judgments than single-pass flat encoding. For evaluation, we curate LeJA (Legal Judgment Analysis) from publicly available Pakistan Supreme Court judgments and annotate each judgment with four non-overlapping functional sections as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Related work

Recent advances in legal NLP have led to the development of specialized models for tasks such as legal document classification, judgment summarization, case outcome prediction, and legal question answering. To place our work in this context, this section reviews major progress in four key areas: AI for legal text analysis, large pre-trained language models, and explainable AI for legal decision support.

Legal text analysis and AI applications

Over the past five years, AI applications in the legal domain have expanded significantly, driven by the increasing availability of digitized legal corpora and demand for automated decision-support tools8,9. Legal NLP encompasses a broad array of tasks, including legal document classification, court judgment summarization, question answering, case outcome prediction, and information retrieval8. These tasks use techniques from general NLP but face unique challenges. Legal texts are often long, written in specialized legal language, and carry high stakes for accuracy and fairness. Recent surveys also show the community’s focus on these challenges. They highlight specialized subtasks such as legal NER, judgment prediction, and case summarization across multiple jurisdictions8,9.

A basic characteristic of legal documents, particularly court judgments, is their structured, hierarchical form. A judicial opinion is organized into logical sections (e.g., facts, issues, arguments, holdings), unlike news articles or other general web text2. This built-in structure can be a benefit for AI models if properly utilized. Segmenting a judgment into its components can simplify targeted processing. For example, one might summarize the facts separately from the legal reasoning or focus a question-answering system on the decision section. Avoiding such a structure, on the other hand, can show suboptimal results. Existing NLP models, which typically process text as a flat sequence, struggle with very long judgments and miss context that spans multiple Sect10. As a result, growing research has looked at incorporating narrative flow awareness into legal NLP models. Gao et al. divide each judgment into four parts, namely Type of dispute, Claims, Facts, and Result, and feed these segments sequentially into a summarization pipeline. They achieved significantly higher ROUGE scores than narrative-agnostic baselines2. Similarly, Dan et al. (2025) segment judgments into three sections and apply tailored summarization models to each part. Then they integrated the partial summaries to form a complete summary10. Together, these studies show that narrative-aware techniques can produce more consistent and accurate results. They emphasize treating legal documents not as blobs of text but as segmented documents.

The rise of pre-trained (PLMs) and large language models (LLM)

The field of legal NLP has changed over the last five years. The coming of PLMs and LLMs drove this shift. Recent surveys further document this shift toward general-purpose large language models in legal applications, highlighting the growing reliance on prompting, instruction tuning, and foundation models across tasks such as legal reasoning, drafting, and case analysis11. The turning point came with models such as BERT (Devlin et al., 2019)4 and GPT. They proved that a Transformer trained on large general text can be fine-tuned to outperform task-specific models on a variety of NLP tasks. Legal AI researchers quickly recognized this potential. They began developing domain-specific variants of these models. In 2020, Chalkidis et al.12 introduced LegalBERT, a BERT model further pre-trained on legal texts, for example, court decisions and legislation. This decision has led to a paradigm shift in the field. Following its release, LegalBERT demonstrated improved performance over general-domain BERT on several legal NLP benchmarks8. These models provided a strong, out-of-the-box contextual understanding of legal language. They also reduced the need for extensive feature engineering and allowed transfer learning across legal NLP tasks.

A major strength of Transformer-based PLMs is their ability to capture contextual information. However, vanilla models like BERT still have a fixed input limit (512 tokens), which is often exceeded by legal documents13. This limitation has spurred research into architectures and training strategies suited to long texts. One notable development is Lawformer, a Transformer model designed for lengthy legal documents that uses sparse attention14. Models like Lawformer can capture long-range dependencies that are important in legal reasoning. Such models have been successfully used for complex tasks such as judgment summarization10.

Similarly, the rise of LLMs such as GPT-4, GPT-3.5, and GPT-3 has opened new possibilities for legal AI. These models with trillions of parameters exhibit impressive performance in zero-shot and few-shot settings. On one hand, tools like ChatGPT have been tested for tasks such as drafting legal documents, answering legal questions, and even taking law school exams. Savelka and Ashley (2023) assess GPT-4 and GPT-3.5 on semantic annotation tasks such as labeling parts of legal texts with zero-shot prompting. Their findings showed that GPT-4 significantly outperforms previous GPT versions and even approaches fine-tuned models on certain tasks15. These results suggest that LLMs with proper prompts can generalize to legal tasks without explicit training. This ability is especially valuable in low-resource settings where annotated data are short.

On the other hand, LLMs are generalists and may lack important legal knowledge or vocabulary. For example, GPT-3.5 often misunderstands legal terms or fails to apply jurisdiction-specific rules because it wasn’t explicitly trained on those differences16. To mitigate these limitations, researchers have begun adapting LLMs to the legal domain through fine-tuning and domain-specific instruction datasets. Beyond general evaluation, recent shared-task results further demonstrate the applicability of modern LLMs to legal reasoning. For example, Mistral-7B has been applied to argument reasoning in civil procedure (SemEval-2024 Task 5), showing competitive performance in structured legal reasoning tasks17. Such studies highlight the relevance of contemporary instruction-tuned LLMs for tasks related to identifying issues and reasoning components within judgments.

Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) in legal text analysis

As AI systems become increasingly involved in legal decision support, the demand for transparency and explainability has also increased critically. The goal of XAI in this context is to provide human-understandable justifications for a model’s predictions. So, in high-stakes domains such as law, explanations are not just a luxury but arguably a necessity for adoption.

One direction of XAI research in legal NLP is post-hoc explanation of model predictions. Lee (2025) shows that after training a classifier on Korean legal cases, they used XAI techniques to analyze misclassification errors. Lee’s study highlights that high performance alone is not enough, but interpretability is equally important in the legal domain. The use of XAI allowed them to propose refinements on the model’s decisions that could be better understood and trusted by legal practitioners16. The second approach is to build explainability directly into the model’s outputs. For instance, He et al. (2023) present a multitask framework for explaining legal judgment predictions by generating a textual rationale for each prediction. In their approach, they trained the model not only to predict the outcome of a case but also to produce a summary of the key facts and legal reasons that resulted in that outcome. Such approaches provide transparency into the model’s reasoning process18. Indeed, Benedetto et al. (2024) combined legal judgment prediction with an explanation module that highlights relevant legal entities and statutes, thereby boosting both accuracy and user confidence in the system19. Our work continues in this spirit by applying XAI tools like Integrated Gradients and attention visualization specifically to the task of judgment segmentation, aiming to make the section classification results interpretable to legal scholars.

Despite substantial progress in the above areas, we identify a clear gap in the literature at their intersection. Prior work has addressed legal judgment segmentation using either classical models (ignoring deep contextual cues) or modern Transformers applied in a flat manner. Still, none has fully leveraged a hierarchical modeling strategy tailored to legal judgments. Even though some summarization approaches segment documents before processing2,10, and rhetorical role labelers consider sentence sequences20, existing models do not explicitly encode the multi-level structure of judgments (segment-level and document-level) within a single learning framework. This is the methodological gap our work addresses by introducing HiCoBERT. This Hierarchical Contextual BERT-based framework operates in two stages (segment encoding and document encoding) to reflect the judgment’s inherent organization. While LegalBERT-style models have been fine-tuned for various legal tasks, they have not been studied for functional judgment segmentation with an accompanying multi-level analysis of model decisions. We address this gap by introducing HiCoBERT and reporting post-hoc XAI at both segment and document levels.

Proposed methodology

This section describes the design and implementation of HiCoBERT. We first explain the dataset collection, annotation, and the segmentation pipeline. We then present the model architecture, training objective, and implementation settings, which together form the basis for the experimental results and analyses reported in the next section.

Dataset construction

This section outlines the construction of our labeled dataset for legal judgment segmentation. We describe the procedures for data collection and sampling, section labeling, document segmentation, and the resulting data statistics and distributions.

Data collection and preprocessing

We obtained judgments from the Supreme Court of Pakistan’s publicly accessible judgment search portal21. To ensure broad coverage, we selected judgments across multiple case types, including civil appeals, civil petitions, criminal appeals, criminal petitions, and constitutional petitions. During preprocessing, header metadata (e.g., case number, date, party names) was removed using a simple keyword-based boundary detection procedure (e.g., “JUDGEMENT” and formatted variants). All text preceding this marker was discarded to retain only the substantive judgment body.

Data sampling and split integrity

To maintain representation across case types (civil petition/appeal, criminal petition/appeal, constitutional petition), we perform stratified sampling at the judgment (document) level. We select 26 judgments for training and 50 judgments for testing; the training set is further split into 80%/20% for training and validation. All splits are disjoint at the document level, i.e., no judgment appears in more than one split. Importantly, segmentation into fixed-length segments/windows is performed after the dataset split, ensuring that no segment derived from a judgment can occur across multiple splits. During dataset cleaning, we performed rigorous de-duplication to remove repeated judgments and ensure no duplicate documents are present across the corpus, thereby reducing the risk of accidental overlap between splits.

Section labeling scheme

To identify the optimal number of sections for segmenting legal judgments, we followed an empirical approach. We surveyed functional and thematic segmentation across international courts and reviewed local legal guidelines. Our analysis showed that each legal judgment contains four major components: Concise Statement of the Case, The Points for Determination, The Reasons, and The Decision (see Table 1). Guided by this survey, we designed our annotation framework around these four ordered sections—each section with a unique semantic and functional role. The annotation process followed detailed guidelines instructing that entire sections be labeled rather than individual sentences, that boundaries remain non-overlapping, and that the natural sequential order of sections within the judgment be preserved. In samples containing ambiguous or merged content, segments were labeled according to the most contextually appropriate section based on the surrounding legal discourse. To ensure consistency, the annotation process involved multiple iterative review rounds, during which previously annotated judgments were re-examined and the guidelines were refined where necessary to maintain coherent section boundaries and labeling decisions across the dataset. The resulting annotated corpus, therefore, serves as the human-validated benchmark against which model predictions are evaluated.

Document segmentation

Legal judgments are long and often exceed the maximum token limit of traditional Transformer architectures such as BERT or LegalBERT (512 tokens). Then come long-range models like Longformer (4096 tokens), which can process longer inputs, but they do so at the cost of higher memory usage and slower computation. To address these limitations, we divided legal judgments into segments (chunks) rather than tokens. This approach improves both the model’s efficiency and overall segmentation accuracy. It helps overcome the following max token limitations, reduces token-level noise, and lowers computational costs. We followed a dynamic segmentation approach. Instead of rigidly cutting sentences or paragraphs, we group sentences until the chunk reaches K = 128 tokens. This method protects sentence integrity by avoiding mid-sentence cuts. It also reduces padding that would waste token space. Prior work22 shows that 128 tokens is a good balance for computational cost and task performance. However, with a 128-token window, some judgments would generate an excessively large number of segments. To prevent runaway computation, we impose a cap on N = 118 segments per document. If a document exceeds N, we treat it as an outlier and exclude it (this occurs in only 2 cases in our dataset).

Data statistics & distributions

Document segmentation produced varying numbers of segments across classes. We therefore examine the distribution of segments across labels to understand class balance in the dataset. Table 2 presents the segment counts for each label in both the training and test splits after filtering and preprocessing. As shown in Table 2, the dataset exhibits a notable class imbalance. The Reasons section dominates the distribution in both splits, accounting for more than 60% of the segments in the training set and a similar proportion in the test set. The remaining labels—Concise Statement of the Case, Points for Determination, and The Decision—occur substantially less frequently. This imbalance reflects the inherent rhetorical structure of judicial decisions. In legal judgments, the reasoning section typically forms the most extensive part of the document, where judges elaborate on legal interpretations, analyze facts, evaluate evidence, and examine arguments, precedents, and counterarguments. In contrast, the Decision section is generally brief, as it contains only the final ruling or directive of the court. Although this distribution mirrors the natural organization of legal texts, it introduces challenges for machine learning models, which may otherwise bias predictions toward the dominant class. To mitigate this effect, our training procedure incorporates class-weighted loss, and we report macro-level evaluation metrics that treat all section types equally during performance assessment. Recognizing and accounting for this structural imbalance is therefore essential when designing models and evaluation strategies for legal judgment segmentation.

Overview of HiCoBERT

HiCoBERT is a hierarchically contextualized segmentation framework tailored for long legal judgments and designed to overcome the limitations of standard Transformer models when applied to lengthy, structurally complex legal texts. Standard Transformers such as BERT4 and even long-context variants like Longformer23 often face input length constraints, leading to truncation of critical content or disrupted discourse flow. HiCoBERT addresses this by dividing each legal judgment into a sequence of manageable segments and modeling both local semantics and global context in a principled two-stage pipeline consisting of (i) intra-segment understanding for local semantic encoding and (ii) inter-segment understanding for modeling document-level context across segments.

Figure 3 provides a high-level view of the proposed HiCoBERT pipeline. We process each judgment as a sequence of segments, allowing long documents to be handled without losing important content due to the length limits of standard Transformer encoders. The pipeline first learns a representation for each segment using LegalBERT for intra-segment understanding12. Then it refines these representations using information from other segments in the same judgment through inter-segment contextualization with a Transformer encoder24 followed by a BiGRU25. This second step helps the model make decisions that depend on a longer context, such as distinguishing between sections that use similar language but appear in different parts of the judgment. Overall, the framework is intended to preserve the narrative flow of judgments and improve section boundary detection compared to approaches that treat the document as a flat sequence.

Architecture details

HiCoBERT uses a two-stage hierarchical design. Each legal judgment is represented as a sequence of segments padded/truncated to a fixed maximum of 118 segments. Each segment is tokenized with a maximum length of 128 tokens. For intra-segment understanding, we use LegalBERT base uncased as the base encoder. Segment representations are obtained using [CLS] pooling, i.e., the final-layer hidden state of the [CLS] token. The resulting segment embeddings are combined at the document level by stacking them in their original order to form a segment sequence.

To preserve segment order, we add learned segment positional embeddings via an embedding layer of size 118 × 768, which is then concatenated with the segment embeddings. Head A is implemented as a linear classifier applied to these position-aware segment embeddings to generate pre-context logits.

For inter-segment understanding (cross-segment attention), we apply a Transformer encoder over the segment-embedding sequence, where cross-segment attention is implemented as multi-head self-attention (scaled dot-product attention) across segments. Concretely, the segment sequence is passed through 4 stacked Transformer encoder layers with 8 attention heads (d_model = 768). Padded segments are masked during cross-segment attention using a segment-level padding mask. The resulting contextual segment representations are then refined using a bidirectional GRU (hidden size 768), whose concatenated outputs (dimension 1536) are projected back to 768 using a linear projection. Head B is implemented as a linear classifier applied to context-aware segment embeddings to produce final segment-level logits used during inference. Training setup and reproducibility

We fine-tuned HiCoBERT using supervised training on the labeled judgments dataset. The batch size is equal to one judgment. Training was performed for seven epochs using the AdamW optimizer (lr = 2 × 10⁻⁵). We used LegalBERT as the intra-segment encoder. To address class imbalance (especially the overrepresented “Reasons” class), we employed class-weighted cross-entropy loss, with class weights computed from the training labels using a balanced weighting scheme. While class-weighted loss mitigates the impact of label imbalance during training, future work may explore additional strategies, such as data augmentation, to further improve robustness for minority sections. Padded segments were ignored during training using ignore_index = − 100. Deterministic training was enabled using a fixed random seed (SEED = 42). In the combined objective, we used α = 0.0. Frameworks comparative analysis with prior architectures

Rule-based and heuristic segmentation

Traditional long-document segmentation methods in industry and early research often rely on rule-based or heuristic strategies. Especially in the legal domain, practitioners use explicit sectional markers and expert knowledge to split documents – for example, using regular expression patterns to identify the adjudication or judge’s opinion sections in court rulings29. Such rule-based segmentation can indeed enhance task performance by isolating the most informative parts of a document. In a recent legal document classification study, for instance, extracting the judge’s “manifestation” (opinion) section and normalizing its vocabulary led to higher accuracy than using the entire document29. However, these approaches are brittle and labour-intensive. Rules must be handcrafted and finely tuned for each document type, and even within a single domain, they may fail when the format or writing style changes. As one study notes, a rule-based pipeline with regexes is typically specific to a particular collection of documents, and adapting it to other courts or contexts requires significant effort and expert knowledge29. HiCoBERT circumvents these limitations by learning how to segment text from data rather than predefined rules. It automatically infers segment boundaries based on the model’s understanding of language and context, enabling it to capture section breaks that align with semantic shifts even without explicit cues. This learned approach retains the benefit of domain insight while greatly improving flexibility.

Hierarchical attention networks (HAN)

Hierarchical Attention Networks (HAN) were an early attempt to exploit document structure by applying attention at multiple levels of granularity. A HAN encodes words into sentence representations and then builds a document representation from those sentences, using attention at each level to emphasize informative content. This two-tier RNN-based approach yielded significant gains over flat models in text classification30. However, HAN was trained from scratch on task data and did not leverage modern pre-trained language models31. HiCoBERT builds on the hierarchical insight of HAN but replaces RNN encoders with BERT-based segment encoders and Transformer-based aggregators. By doing so, HiCoBERT benefits from large-scale language pretraining, capturing long-range context and domain-specific nuances more effectively.

Longformer and long-context transformers

Longformer and similar long-context Transformers (e.g., BigBird) extend the standard Transformer with sparse attention patterns (local windows and selected global tokens) to handle longer sequences efficiently23. This allows single-pass encoding of documents beyond the 512-token limit by restricting attention to a neighbourhood of tokens and a few global points. However, such models treat the document as a single flat sequence and do not explicitly incorporate hierarchical organization, as windowed attention does not leverage higher-level functional units and can be considered a “greedy” approach to long text. HiCoBERT, by contrast, processes the input in segments and then uses an additional encoder to capture cross-segment context. This hierarchical segmentation strategy preserves section-level context and relationships that a purely sequential model might miss.

Experiments and results

This section presents a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed HiCoBERT framework for section segmentation in long legal judgments. It reports the overall segmentation performance of HiCoBERT, analyses the impact of contextualization and hierarchical components through ablation studies, examines the role of domain-specific versus general-purpose encoders, compares HiCoBERT against baseline models, and provides explainability insights to support and interpret model decisions.

Evaluation metrics

We use a two-level evaluation approach to measure performance at both the segment and document levels. For individual segments, we report standard classification metrics such as accuracy and F1 scores (macro and micro) to assess local prediction accuracy. For the entire document, we evaluate document-level context using sequence-level metrics such as Boundary F132, Span F133, and normalized Levenshtein distance34, which compare the predicted segmentation with the ground-truth structure.

Ablation study

This section provides targeted evidence for the two central design choices in HiCoBERT: (i) contextualization across segments and (ii) hierarchical modeling over segment representations. HiCoBERT first computes intra-segment representations using a segment-wise encoder, and then refines them through inter-segment contextualization (cross-segment encoder and document-level context modeling) before producing final predictions. The following ablations quantify (a) the importance of contextual supervision through the inter-segment pathway and (b) the contribution of each hierarchical component.

Contextualization analysis (Local vs. Inter-segment Supervision via α)

HiCoBERT outputs logits from two heads: Head A, an auxiliary intra-segment head operating on pre-context segment embeddings, and Head B, the principal head operating on context-aware embeddings after inter-segment modeling. Final predictions are produced using Head B, while Head A is included to provide an explicit local-only supervision reference, enabling a direct comparison between segment-only classification (pre-context) and context-aware segmentation (post-context). This design makes the role of contextualization explicit. When supervision relies only on Head A, performance reflects what can be learned without cross-segment dependencies, which is insufficient for the legal judgment section segmentation task.

As defined in Eq. 12, α controls the weighting of supervision between Head A (local) and Head B (contextual). Tables 5 and 6 summarize the results. The best performance occurs at α = 0.0, where training relies only on the inter-segment head (Head B). Under this setting, the model achieves the highest accuracy/micro F1 (0.8000), macro F1 (0.7017), and boundary F1 (0.6828). This indicates that incorporating cross-segment dependencies is important for handling long legal judgments. At the other end, α = 1.0 uses only the intra-segment head (Head A) and performs the worst across almost all metrics. This provides a useful reference point, as it reflects performance when decisions are made from a segment in isolation without a broader document context. Intermediate values such as α = 0.3 and α = 0.5 remain reasonably competitive, but they do not exceed the α = 0.0 setting.

Overall, these results support the motivation behind HiCoBERT: local segment semantics are necessary but not sufficient for section segmentation in long legal judgments, and performance improves when segment representations are contextualized with document-level information. Hierarchical modeling analysis (Component-level analysis)

To validate the hierarchical design of HiCoBERT, a series of component-level ablations were conducted by removing one component at a time—cross-segment Transformer layers, BiGRU refinement, segment positional embeddings, and LegalBERT fine-tuning—while keeping the rest of the pipeline unchanged. This isolates which parts of the hierarchy contribute most to boundary consistency and overall structural alignment.

As shown in Table 7, removing the cross-segment Transformer layers results in a clear drop in document-level performance (Boundary F1 = 0.6096). It worsens alignment (Norm. Lev. = 0.2533), confirming that cross-segment self-attention is important for capturing long-range dependencies across judgment sections. Removing the BiGRU produces a smaller but consistent decline, suggesting that sequential refinement helps smooth transitions across neighboring segments, although the attention-based inter-segment modeling remains the dominant factor. Removing positional embeddings also degrades boundary recognition (Boundary F1 = 0.6376) and increases the normalized Levenshtein distance, indicating that explicit segment-order encoding supports stable boundary prediction. Freezing the LegalBERT encoder results in the largest overall decline (Macro F1 = 0.5905, Boundary F1 = 0.5083), showing that domain-adaptive fine-tuning is critical for learning the rhetorical and argumentative patterns that characterize legal judgments. Overall, the full HiCoBERT configuration (α = 0.0) performs best across the metrics, supporting the contributions of both intra-segmental encoding and inter-segmental hierarchical contextualization.

Cross-study comparison with baselines

To evaluate HiCoBERT, we compare it against baselines spanning four model families: (i) flat Transformer encoders, (ii) hybrid structured sequence models, (iii) hierarchical sequence models, and (iv) prompted LLM baselines. We also considered layout-aware document models (e.g., LayoutLM variants). However, our labels represent rhetorical functions (facts, arguments, reasons, decisions), which are mainly driven by meaning rather than page layout. In addition, layout cues are often weak or inconsistent across real-world judgment PDFs. Therefore, we focus on text-based long-context baselines that are better aligned with the goal of content-driven segmentation. For flat Transformers, we include domain-specific and long-context encoders: LegalBERT12, LegalRoBERTa, Longformer-base23, Legal Longformer-base35, BigBird5, as well as LongT5 (encoder-only) long-document model evaluated in encoder-only form6. Additionally, we report results for LED Seq2Seq (encoder–decoder)23 to assess an encoder–decoder long-document formulation under the same labeling schema. For hybrid structured baselines, we evaluate LegalBERT+BiLSTM36 and LegalBERT + CRF37,38. For hierarchical sequence modeling, we include a Hierarchical BiLSTM baseline39. Finally, we evaluate prompted LLM baselines using consistent prompts and the same evaluation protocol, covering both open and closed models: Llama-3.140, Saul-7B-Instruct41, Mistral-7B-Instruct17, Gemini 2.5 Pro, GPT-4o42, and DeepSeek-V343.

Fine-tuned long-context transformer baselines

The results are summarized in Table 11. The proposed HiCoBERT model (α = 0.0) clearly outperforms all other architectures across both segment-level and sequence-level metrics. Flat Transformers like LegalBERT and RoBERTa perform well but fall short in terms of document-level soundness. For example, LegalBERT attains a Boundary F1 of 0.5929, showing that sequential token-level classification lacks sufficient global context.

We further expand our baseline analysis to Longformer, a Transformer architecture optimized for long-text processing with an attention window of 4,096 tokens. For legal section segmentation, we implemented a flat token-classification framework that combined full judgments into single sequences. Token-level predictions were aligned with ground-truth segment boundaries using global attention on the [CLS] token and local attention for context. This shows that increasing sequence length alone does not solve the hierarchical dependency problem natural in legal writings. Its domain-specific counterpart, Legal Longformer, performs slightly better (Macro F1 = 0.5930; Boundary F1 = 0.5998), but both models still fail to capture the rhetorical structure of judicial judgment reasoning effectively. This limitation is clearest in the Points for Determination class. Where Longformer achieved only 21.49% F1 compared to HiCoBERTs 44.19%, these findings show that while long-context Transformers address the technical constraint of input length, they still fall short in modeling inter-sectional relationships—a gap addressed by our hierarchical contextualized framework HiCoBERT.

Beyond Longformer-style encoders, the long-document Transformer baselines show mixed behavior. BigBird remains moderately competitive (Macro F1 = 0.5389; Boundary F1 = 0.5563), but still trails HiCoBERT on both segment quality and document-level consistency. LongT5 (encoder-only) performs worst overall (Macro F1 = 0.2465), with extremely low F1 for Points for Determination (0.0262) and Decision (0.0185), indicating difficulty learning rare, heading-like sections. To verify whether the observed performance differences are statistically meaningful, we further conduct a document-level paired bootstrap significance test comparing HiCoBERT with the strongest long-context Transformer baselines (BigBird and LongT5)(Table 12). For each judgment, Macro F1 scores were computed and resampled with 1,000 bootstrap iterations. The results show that the improvements achieved by HiCoBERT are statistically significant. Specifically, compared with BigBird, HiCoBERT achieves a mean Macro F1 improvement of + 0.1787, with a p-value ≈ 0.001 and a 95% confidence interval of [0.131, 0.228], indicating that the performance gain remains consistently positive across documents. When compared with LongT5, the improvement is substantially larger (+ 0.5600 Macro F1, p ≈ 0.001, 95% CI [0.493, 0.621]), confirming that the hierarchical architecture provides a clear advantage over encoder-only long-context models.

We also analyze performance variance across judgments to assess model stability. HiCoBERT shows a standard deviation of 0.187, compared with 0.229 for BigBird, indicating that the proposed model not only improves accuracy but also produces more stable predictions across documents. Interestingly, LongT5 exhibits lower variance (0.081), suggesting more consistent predictions; however, this stability reflects a systematic prediction bias rather than better modeling capacity. As shown in the per-class results in Table 12, LongT5 performs extremely poorly on the Points for Determination (F1 = 0.0262) and Decision (F1 = 0.0185) sections, indicating that it frequently fails to identify short or structurally distinctive segments. This behavior suggests that LongT5 tends to collapse predictions toward dominant classes such as Reasons, producing stable but inaccurate outputs.

The per-class results in Table 13 further illustrate typical failure patterns of flat long-context models. Sections such as Points for Determination are brief and often ambiguous, appearing between factual summaries and reasoning. Models without hierarchical document awareness frequently absorb these segments into adjacent sections, particularly Reasons. In contrast, HiCoBERT achieves the highest F1 score for this class (0.4419) and shows strong performance on semantically dense sections such as Reasons (0.9060) and Decision (0.8143). These results indicate that explicitly modeling inter-segment dependencies and hierarchical context is essential for capturing the rhetorical structure of legal judgments.

Together, the statistical testing, variance analysis, and per-class error examination demonstrate that the superiority of HiCoBERT is not merely descriptive but statistically validated and structurally grounded. The hierarchical architecture consistently improves both segmentation accuracy and document-level consistency across diverse judicial decisions.

Cross-dataset generalization

LegalSeg is a publicly available benchmark of Indian legal judgments whose annotation is closely related to our task7. It provides sentence-level labels with multiple rhetorical roles (e.g., Issue, Facts, Arguments of Petitioner, Arguments of Respondent, Reasoning, Decision, and None). To enable a fair comparison with our four-section schema, we harmonize LegalSeg into our labels as follows: Facts → Concise Statement of the Case, Issue → Points for Determination, and Reasoning/Arguments → Reasons. The None category largely corresponds to numbering, headings, or structural artifacts; therefore, it is merged into the surrounding section during document-level collapsing. After label harmonization, we apply the same dynamic segmentation strategy used in our pipeline to obtain section-consistent segments.

HiCoBERT trained on LeJA is directly evaluated on the LegalSeg test split (712 judgment documents). Due to the model’s 118-segment limit, we evaluate the 679 judgments that fit this constraint under dynamic segmentation. Table 16 summarizes the in-domain and cross-dataset performance. As expected under cross-jurisdiction shift, overall performance decreases (Macro-F1: 0.7017 → 0.5044; Boundary F1: 0.6828 → 0.5088; Span F1: 0.7875 → 0.5047). Importantly, HiCoBERT retains strong transfer for the most informative parts of judgments, Reasons (F1 = 0.8402), and Decision (F1 = 0.7967, indicating that it captures generalizable patterns of legal reasoning and dispositive language beyond the training jurisdiction.

The main weakness is the mapped Points for the Determination class (F1: 0.4419 → 0.0197). In LegalSeg, “Issue” content is often not a rich section: it frequently appears at the start of the judgment as short fragments dominated by numbers, punctuation, headings, or case/judge identifiers, and it is also relatively rare compared to other roles. Consequently, many “Issue/Points” segments are absorbed into nearby introductory facts or early reasoning. This behavior is visible in the confusion analysis: 78.0% of true Issue/Points segments are predicted as Concise/Facts, and 14.8% are predicted as Reasons (Fig. 4). Overall, the cross-dataset evaluation supports HiCoBERT generalizability for core sections while showing that issue/points detection is the most sensitive component under annotation and jurisdiction differences.

Statistical reliability and training stability

To assess the robustness of HiCoBERT under limited training data, we conducted additional experiments to evaluate its statistical reliability and training stability. The LeJA dataset contains a limited number of annotated judgments (76 documents, with 26 used for training). Specifically, the model was trained five times using different random seeds, resulting in different parameter initializations and train–validation splits while keeping the test set fixed. This procedure allows us to estimate performance variability under limited training data.

Across the five runs, HiCoBERT achieved an average Macro-F1 of 0.704 ± 0.038 and an accuracy of 0.777 ± 0.046, indicating that the model’s performance remains stable despite stochastic variations in training. Sequence-level metrics exhibited higher variability (Boundary-F1 0.593 ± 0.083, Span-F1 0.745 ± 0.051, Normalized Levenshtein 0.232 ± 0.061), which is expected because boundary-based evaluation is sensitive to small shifts in predicted section transitions, particularly for short or under-represented sections such as Points for Determination. Importantly, the configuration reported in Table 5 lies within the observed multi-run performance range and was retained as the reference configuration for direct comparison with baseline models.

Taken together, the qualitative and quantitative explainability results provide a coherent account of HiCoBERT behavior. At the token level, Integrated Gradients highlights semantically appropriate cues—fact-focused tokens in the Concise Statement, issue-framing markers in the Points for Determination, legal-analytic phrases in the Reasons, and decisive action verbs in the Decision—supporting the claim that the model relies on document-level context. At the context level, both the case study ablation matrix and test-set boundary analysis show a strong locality pattern: nearby context has the greatest influence, yet that influence can be supportive or harmful, particularly around the Reasons→Decision boundary. These observations are consistent with HiCoBERT two-stage design (segment encoding followed by cross-segment mixing) and motivate a concrete refinement: introduce boundary-aware context gating (or section-conditioned attenuation) to prevent short Decision segments from being unintentionally diluted by long, immediately preceding context.

Limitation and future work

The experimental findings presented in this research strongly support the success of HiCoBERT, a hierarchically contextualized Transformer-based framework designed for legal judgment section segmentation. Despite its strong performance and exploratory capabilities, HiCoBERT is not without limitations. First, the model handles a fixed max segment count (e.g., 118). This is practical for batch-level processing but may limit scalability for very short or unusually long judgments. Future versions will experiment with adaptive context windows or dynamic segmentation schemes to better handle documents of varying lengths.

Second, the dataset suffers from class imbalance. The Reasons section is much longer than other sections, such as Points for Determination or Decision. Although class-weighted loss helps reduce this issue, the model still shows a bias toward the majority classes. Future work could explore data augmentation, curriculum learning, or contrastive objectives to improve performance on underrepresented sections. Third, HiCoBERT shows reduced precision in ambiguous or boundary-blurred sections, especially in Points for Determination, where even human annotators often disagree. These sections often lack clear linguistic cues that indicate their boundaries. We could enhance the model with boundary-aware gating to better distinguish subtle stylistic or semantic shifts.

Finally, the dominance of long reasoning sections may occasionally overshadow shorter neighboring segments, causing minor misclassifications at functional boundaries. Incorporating context-gating mechanisms that account for section length may reduce such spillover effects, especially between the Reasons into Decision sections. Additionally, functional segmentation into downstream applications such as argumentation mining, judgment outcome prediction, and judgment summarization can be explored to assess HiCoBERT real-world utility in legal judgment analysis and legal NLP research.

Conclusion

In this study, we propose HiCoBERT, a hierarchically contextualized Transformer framework for segmenting legal judgments. The framework segments long judicial documents into four ordered functional sections: Concise Statement of the Case, Points for Determination, Reasons, and Decision. Given that judicial opinions frequently surpass standard Transformer input limits and present substantial document-level dependencies, we approached the problem as segment-level sequence labeling, integrating intra-segment encoding with inter-segment contextualization to more effectively model the rhetorical progression of judgments. Across a comprehensive experimental suite that included comparisons with flat Transformers, long-context encoders, structured models, and prompted LLM baselines, the proposed approach consistently achieved the strongest overall performance on the LeJA dataset. Ablation studies further demonstrate that modeling cross-segment dependencies is critical, emphasizing that the inter-segment contextualization component substantially improves performance, particularly for structurally subtle sections such as Points for Determination. Finally, post-hoc explainability analyses reveal token-level evidence through Integrated Gradients and context influence across segments through Δ-logit ablation, highlighting how boundary-adjacent context shapes model predictions.
