Distinct genetic variants from the whole-exome sequencing of syndromic anorectal malformations: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Vandana Suseelan, Anjali Krishna A, Akshaykumar Zawar, P Santosh Prabhu, Priyamvada Bhatta, Ashish Paliwal, Praveen Mathur, Krishna Mohan Medicherla, Sunil Kumar Polipalli, Prashanth Suravajhala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAnorectal malformations (ARMs) are congenital anomalies affecting the anus and rectum, with a global incidence of 1 in 5,000 live births. Despite clinical advancements, the genetic basis of ARMs remains largely unknown. Our study utilized whole-exome sequencing (WES) on ARM-diagnosed individuals to identify rare and potentially pathogenic variants. We prioritized 20 variants by using consensus variant pipeline for exome analyses (CONVEX) and the AION pipeline, followed by validation. Cross-referencing with existing genome-wide association study (GWAS) datasets identified two common variants, ISL1 and EFNA1, both of which are reported in European ancestry, suggesting a genetic architecture. Network analysis using Cytoscape and Cytohubba highlighted PKD1, PKD2, and PKHD1 as important hub genes and the pathways for ARM, including several developmental pathways. Furthermore, our structural variant analysis identified a prioritized variant within a copy number variant. Our findings provide novel insights into syndromic ARMs (SARMs), which may lead to improved genetic diagnosis and personalized interventions.Subject areas: health sciences, clinical genetics, disease

Introduction

Anorectal malformations (ARMs) are rare congenital defects that result from the malformation of the hindgut during embryogenesis, affecting the development of the anus and rectum.1,2 These defects vary from minor, easily treatable conditions, like a displaced anal canal, to complex cases, such as the complete joining of the anus, vagina, and urethra. The global incidence of ARMs is 1 in 5,000 live births, with most abnormalities identified immediately after birth and some unnoticed until adolescence, with no accrued statistics in India.3,4 A complete physical examination is conducted to detect any associated anomalies, with the most common accompanying malformations being vertebral, anorectal, cardiac, tracheo-esophageal fistula, renal, and limb (VACTERL).5 ARMs are primarily classified into two types: syndromic ARMs (SARMs), which occur with one or more associated anomalies, and non-syndromic ARMs, which are isolated.6 The most widely accepted international classification is the Krickenbeck classification7 which categorizes anomalies by the type and location of the fistula. It includes rare or regional variants and provides standardized outcome definitions to ensure comparable treatment and follow-up across different centers. Currently, the treatment for ARMs involves surgery, ranging from blind perineal exploration to image-guided repairs.8 For instance, posterior sagittal anorectoplasty (PSARP) is a standard method that provides optimal exposure and precise sphincter placement. Management strategies for these conditions range from conservative care for simple cases to individualized surgical repair for complex cases. A rare ARM is cloaca, resulting from the failure of separation between the genitourinary and anorectal tracts, which creates a single opening for urine, stool, and vaginal secretions.9 In female patients, early cloacal separation failure can lead to high colonic pouch connections with urinary or genital tracts, causing a double uterus or vagina and bladder neck stretching. In male patients, the bladder neck remains unaffected due to the absence of Mullerian duct involvement.10 Congenital pouch colon (CPC) is another rare, high ARM characterized by the partial or complete replacement of the colon by a dilated pouch that communicates with the genitourinary tract via a fistula.11

ARMs encompass a wide spectrum of anomalies with varying complexity and associated conditions. Successful management relies on accurate diagnosis, classification, and appropriate surgical approaches to achieve optimal outcomes. Despite the higher incidence of ARMs in socially and economically disadvantaged populations, whole-exome sequencing (WES) studies are scarce in India. This led us to initiate research pertaining to ARM manifestations in India, with an aim to discover genetic variations contributing to the phenotypic heterogeneity of ARMs and identify variants that play a role in this diversity. Earlier research from our lab used systems genomics framework to decode complex and rare genetic etiology of CPC, wherein we employed the WES and trio analysis across 18 affected individuals.12 After applying various filtrations, many coding mutations including frameshift variants in the genes EPB41L4A and CTC1 were identified, which link CPC to other disorders including colon, nervous system, and dyskeratosis congenita. Further discoveries included three rare variants—FRG1 and MUC58 (missense mutation) and TAF1B (stop-gain mutation)—which were seen in severe CPC.13 In total, 24 significant missense variants, nine stop-gain variants and three stop-loss mutants were identified. Many of these are active in colon development such as JAG1, PEX16, OAS2, GBA3, and PKD1L2. We also found variants of uncertain significance (VUSs), SQSTM1 and C10orf120, that could have regulatory roles. Moreover, our study discovered a long non-coding RNA (lnc-EPB41-1-1), which interacts with KIF13A (an important trafficking protein in CPC).14 This interaction was also confirmed experimentally using microscale thermophoresis (MST), which indicated a strong binding affinity. In this work, we sought to identify distinct variants associated with ARMs from the North Indian registry by utilizing WES. We analyzed eight ARM (n = 8) samples by using two independent pipelines: consensus variant pipeline for exome analyses (CONVEX) and GeneAssure, and further prioritized the results using downstream annotation tools before ascertaining the “pathogenic,” “likely pathogenic,” or “VUS” classification. We discuss the potential role of prioritized variants in the manifestation of ARM pathogenesis.

Results and discussion

We analyzed samples from eight individuals with varying severities of ARMs, using two independent pipelines, CONVEX and GeneAssure, and further prioritized the results using AION. For the initial screening, we focused on bona fide candidate variants identified as heterozygous calls (GT = 0/1), as we had only singletons (affected probands without parental sequencing information). We further prioritized variants based on clinical significance, specifically those classified as “pathogenic,” “likely pathogenic,” or “VUSs.” Additionally, we set a stringent filter for allele frequency, considering only the variants with a gnomAD frequency of less than 0.01, besides a host of attributes filtered by CONVEX and AION. Through this process, we shortlisted a total of 20 candidate variants across all eight samples for further analysis (Table 1). Of the 20 variants predicted by CONVEX, 9 were exclusively prioritized by the AION platform, while the remaining 11 were shared between both CONVEX and AION (Figure 1). Notably, several candidate variants were located in genes previously associated with developmental disorders and congenital anomalies, and the identified pathways were found to be relevant to ARMs.

Comparison of SARM with GWAS, yielding two common variants

Variants of NOTCH3 (Notch Receptor 3) were reported in SARM4 (NC_000019.10:g.15179137G>C) and SARM7 (NC_000019.10:g.15179052G>A and NC_000019.10:g.15185655T>C). NOTCH3 was found to be associated because of its role of signaling in cell fate of tissue patterning during embryogenesis and the gut development.15,16 MutationTaster predicted all three identified variants as missense mutations localized to extracellular epidermal growth factor (EGF)-like domains of NOTCH3 that are critical for ligand binding and receptor activation. Two of the variants, NC_000019.10:g.15179137G>C and NC_000019.10:g.15179052G>A, altered the conserved cysteine residues, which is a known mechanism for EGF-like domain structure disruption due to impaired disulfide bond formation. The other variant NC_000019.10:g.15185655T>C affected a conserved non-cysteine residue that may influence protein folding or receptor-ligand interactions. The three variants were rare in population databases, with probability of loss-of-function intolerance (pLI) of 0.4. The NOTCH pathway signaling plays an important role in embryonic gut development, epithelial differentiation, and mesenchymal-epithelial interactions. So, these findings suggest that the variants of NOTCH3 can be candidates for ARMs.

GLI2 is relevant to anorectal development in cloacal and hindgut formation, as it is a key mediator of the sonic hedgehog (SHH) signaling pathway. The mutation NC_000002.12:g.120797290G>A affects the splice site and is pathogenic. GLI2 plays an important role in mesodermal patterning through the fibroblast growth factor (FGF)-brachyury regulatory loop.17 It activates brachyury and Xhox3, linking the FGF and SHH pathways for mesodermal maintenance and posterior specification. This variant (NC_000002.12:g.120797290G>A) is located in the intron-exon boundary, which can potentially lead to altered gene function. The splice-acceptor variant in GLI2 is a haplo-insufficient gene with a high pLI (0.97), and, so, this variant may act as a causal or modifying allele within a polygenic or multifactorial framework.

ARHGAP29 affects the midline developmental defect—orofacial clefting—which can be an overlapping mechanism for craniofacial and anorectal anomalies wherein the variant (NM_004815.4:c.2224G>A) with missense mutation may cause the defect and lead to an ARM. We observed that an evolutionarily conserved residue in the Rho-GAP domain is affected by the ARHGAP29 variant. The gene shows intolerance to functional variation (pLI = 0.99), with potential sensitivity to missense changes. For tissue remodeling during craniofacial and embryonic development, ARHGAP29 regulates RhoA-mediated cytoskeletal dynamics and epithelial morphogenesis.18,19 ARHGAP29 dysfunction may contribute to ARM susceptibility as part of a shared midline developmental pathway. Similarly, variants of ZNF462, a zinc-finger transcription factor that plays a role in embryonic development and chromatin remodeling,20 were found to be associated with Weiss-Kruszka syndrome, which includes cardiac, skeletal, and urogenital abnormalities. Our study identified a missense variant of ZNF462, NC_000009.12:g.106972095C>G (p.Pro2173Arg), which can lead to developmental syndromes. This variant is seen as extremely rare in population databases, with pLI = 1, and is haplo-insufficient wherein the gene associated with the variant was predicted to be damaging, and its presence in a gene critical for embryonic patterning suggests that it can increase the risk of ARMs in combination with other genetic changes.

SCN1A is a sodium channel gene that is associated with epilepsy and neurodevelopment, having a syndromic context. In our study, NC_000002.12:g.166046808T>C with a missense variation was prioritized based on its functional relevance. While the variant was classified as deleterious, with a high confidence tree vote (83%–100%) and haplo-insufficiency (pLI = 1.0), it may be associated with the disruption of sodium channel function. Although the variant is currently classified as “VUS” in ClinVar, its extremely low population frequency, deleterious predictions, and gene-level intolerance point to a possible functional impact on ARMs.

PYCR1 and PDSS1 are metabolic genes wherein we observed the variants associated with ARM pathogenesis. The non-sense variant in PDSS1, NC_000010.11:g.26723857C>T, introduces a premature termination codon and is predicted to trigger non-sense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD), which results in loss of a functional protein. This variant is classified as “pathogenic” in ClinVar and is associated with a multisystem disorder linked to mitochondrial dysfunction (deafness-encephaloneuropathy-obesity-valvulopathy syndrome). PDSS1 encodes an essential enzyme in the coenzyme Q10 biosynthesis pathway, and loss-of-function variants affect mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, leading to developmental abnormalities.21 Mitochondrial dysfunction during early embryogenesis is important in complex syndromic cases, by which ARMs may arise as secondary features. PYCR1 causes cutis laxa syndrome, which includes multiple defects, from developmental disorders to connective tissue abnormalities.22 A missense variant, NC_000017.11:g.81935368G>A, was identified in the PYCR1 gene, with the variant predicted to be deleterious across all evaluated transcripts with high-confidence tree votes (95%–98%). The variant is rare in population databases, and the amino acid substitution supports a potential functional impact. PYCR1 plays an important role in proline biosynthesis, which is a pathway critical for collagen integrity, cellular redox balance, and normal embryonic development. Complex syndromic phenotypes may arise due to disruption of these processes, which can include ARMs as secondary manifestations.

PKHD1 and PKD1 are known for cilia-related signaling defects in congenital abnormalities associated with polycystic kidney disease, an ARM-associated anomaly.23,24 The identified missense variant in PKHD1 (NC_000006.12:g.51746863T>C) was predicted to be harmful by MutationTaster across multiple transcripts. This affects a conserved amino acid residue within the fibrocystin protein. PKHD1 encodes a large ciliary protein in epithelial morphogenesis and planar cell polarity signaling, which are essential for tissue remodeling during embryogenesis. Because the cilia-mediated signaling in gastrointestinal and cloacal development is important, this variant may be susceptible to ARMs. We identified two missense variants in PKD1 in SARM4. The variant NC_000016.10:g.2103386C>T was predicted to be deleterious across multiple transcripts, and it results in an amino acid substitution without affecting protein length or splice sites. The variant is present at low frequency in population databases, with a loss-of-function intolerance. Cilia-dependent pathway disruption can lead to abnormal hindgut and cloacal development.25,26 This suggests that the variant may act as a genetic modifier causing ARMs. Another PKD1 missense variant NC_000016.10:g.2103820C>T was also predicted to be deleterious, with a low population frequency and pLI = 1.

Mutations in the gene CCDC50 cause hearing loss. Because it is an associated anomaly of ARM, we considered prioritizing the variant NC_000003.12:g.191375440C>G, which causes protein termination (p.Ser276Ter). The stop-gain variant NC_000003.12:g.191375440C>G was prioritized based on this overlap. Although these genes have not been reported in ARMs earlier, GeneCards GTEx showed their expression in colon and gastrointestinal tissues, suggesting a potential influence of developmental pathways relevant to SARMs. At the variant level, NC_000003.12:g.191375440C>G change introduces a premature termination codon and is predicted to undergo NMD. The OSMR, an oncostatin M receptor that plays a role in cytokine signaling affecting the epithelial and mesenchymal development, harbors a stop-gain mutation NC_000005.10:g.38904479C>T, which results in loss of protein function with a termination (p.Gln421Ter). This non-sense variant is classified as harmful and predicted to undergo NMD and lead to truncation of the protein and loss of all extracellular fibronectin type III domains, the transmembrane region, and intracellular signaling motifs. OSMR is a key receptor in IL-6 family cytokine signaling that mediates JAK-STAT-dependent pathways, which are essential for epithelial-mesenchymal crosstalk and embryonic tissue patterning.27 Disruption of these signaling can affect the hindgut and cloacal development, plausibly contributing to loss of OSMR function in ARMs.

GUCY2C, a guanylate cyclase, regulates the intestinal epithelium and potentially causes gut malformations. NC_000012.12:g.14621060G>A, which causes a missense mutation p.Arg920Cys, was identified in this gene. While this variant is highly conserved across vertebrate species, it is located within the guanylate cyclase catalytic domain, suggesting that it may reduce enzyme activity. Because cGMP-mediated signaling plays an important role in intestinal epithelial maturation and gut morphogenesis,28 disruption of GUCY2C could contribute to ARM pathogenesis. TYR, primarily involved in melanin biosynthesis, is associated with pigmentation disorders, but it is expressed in neural crest derivatives, suggesting that the missense variant NC_000011.10:g.89284805C>T has a potential syndromic overlap. As a potentially damaging variant, TYR has not been reported to be associated with ARMs, but neural crest-derived cells contribute to melanocyte development and hindgut morphogenesis. So, the disruption of these developmental pathways likely contributes to SARMs because ARMs can arise from embryonic developmental disruptions.

PSAP (prosaposin) is important for lysosomal function and neural developments associated with SARMs. A rare missense variant was found in the PSAP gene (chr10:71819830T>G), which has been associated with disorders caused by defects in sphingolipid metabolism. The affected amino acid lies within the saposin B domain that plays an important role in proper protein function, suggesting that this change can disrupt the protein’s structure. Furthermore, PSAP is a highly conserved and functionally important gene, with pLI = 0.99. Lysosomal sphingolipid metabolism is crucial for normal embryonic development and tissue formation; so, this variant could increase the susceptibility to ARM.

ZEB1 is a transcriptional repressor that promotes the epithelial-mesenchymal transition, which has roles in developmental syndromes and could impact hindgut morphogenesis. In our sample, the ZEB1 variant NC_000010.11:g.31514613C>T caused a missense mutation, which can be causative for ARMs. While it is predicted to be deleterious across all affected transcripts and extremely rare in population databases, with pLI = 0.9, the gene is intolerant to functional disruption. MIEF1 is a mitochondrial dynamic regulator that modulates mitochondrial fission through interaction with DNM1L.29 A rare missense variant in MIEF1, NC_000022.11:g.39513632G>A, was identified in our study; it is present at a very low frequency in population databases and was predicted to be deleterious by MutationTaster across multiple transcripts. The altered residue lies in a conserved region involved in interaction with DNM1L, a key protein regulating mitochondrial fission. Tissue development and cellular differentiation could occur due to impaired mitochondrial dynamics during early embryogenesis.30

Our shortlisted variants (Tables 1 and S1), nonetheless, revealed an interesting observation regarding mutation sites at C and G bases. Previous studies have noted that germline variants in rare Mendelian diseases exhibit similar general biases to population variation, where transitions (purine ↔ purine or pyrimidine ↔ pyrimidine) significantly outnumber transversions—approximately 3–4 times more frequent in the germline.31,32 Specifically, C→T (and the complementary G→A) changes at methylated CpG sites predominantly contribute to the spectrum. For instance, large datasets like ExAC/gnomAD indicate that roughly 60%–80% of all possible CpG→TpG transitions occur in the population, compared with only a small percentage of possible transversions. Similarly, nearly half of missense mutations in specific recessive disease genes are found at CpGs, with about 74% of these being C→T/G→A transitions.33 These CpG→TpG transitions, resulting from spontaneous 5 mC deamination, correspond to the well-known germline signature SBS1. In contrast, other contexts, such as A/T sites or non-CpG changes, show much lower mutability. In summary, rare autosomal-recessive disease alleles are enriched for G/C→A/T transitions within CpG contexts. This enrichment reflects standard germline mutational biases.

Comparative analysis of SNVs across multiple datasets yielded unique variants

We attempted comparing CONVEX and AION classifications, particularly on variants not categorized by ClinVar. For example, AION, by integrating multiple annotation and population-based matrices, successfully identified pathogenic variants in GLI2 and OSMR, classifying them as “pathogenic.” Additionally, variants in ARHGAP29, PKHD1, NOTCH3, ZNF462, and MIEF1 were designated “likely pathogenic.” By classifying these variants, AION predicted the candidate genes that may play a crucial role in ARMs, extending beyond ClinVar’s current limitations. For instance, a NOTCH3 gene variant (NC_000019.10:g.15179052G>A) in SARM7, which ClinVar classified with conflicting interpretations of pathogenicity, was labeled as “pathogenic” by AION. Given NOTCH3’s role in developmental signaling, this suggests its potential involvement in SARMs, and AION could help resolve this interpretative uncertainty. Furthermore, several variants (NC_000002.12:g.166046808T>C, NC_000017.11:g.81935368G>A, NC_000003.12:g.191375440C>G, and NC_000012.12:g.14621060G>A) classified as “VUS” by ClinVar were flagged as “pathogenic” or “likely pathogenic” by AION. This highlights AION’s accuracy in reducing uncertainty and providing clinical relevance to these variants. Similarly, AION reclassified some ClinVar VUSs as “benign” or “likely benign,” demonstrating its ability to downgrade relevant variants and clarify uncertain classifications. However, there are some exceptions. For example, the variant NC_000007.14:g.6006003T>C was deemed “likely benign” by ClinVar but predicted as “pathogenic” by AION. Conversely, the NC_000011.10:g.89284805C>T variant, classified as “likely pathogenic” or “pathogenic” in ClinVar, was confirmed as “likely pathogenic” by AION, indicating good agreement. On the other hand, the variant NC_000010.11:g.71819830T>G was marked as “pathogenic” or “likely pathogenic” by ClinVar but as “VUS” by AION. indicating subtle variations in annotations. Furthermore, the PDK1 variant, initially categorized as “VUS” in ClinVar, was reclassified as “benign” by AION. Conversely, AION classified CCDC50 and TYR variants as “pathogenic.” Out of 9 variants, one (NC_000007.14:g.6006003T>C) was predicted as “likely benign” by ClinVar but “pathogenic” by AION, with an AION variant score of 0.94, possibly suggesting pathogenesis. We also compared our candidate variants with those previously reported in the Korean cohort study for age-related macular degeneration, but no common variants were identified. This disparity likely stems from differences in the design and methodology of assessing variants, besides significant variants that remain to be assessed for diagnostics.

We also discerned potential genetic factors contributing to ARMs and identified two genes, CABP4 and POLD1, which warrant further investigation. The CABP4 gene, which encodes a calcium-binding protein vital for renal synaptic signaling, is primarily associated with retinal dystrophies. However, its role in neural signaling during fetal development suggests that mutations could affect neural crest cells, which are crucial for anorectal development. While the exact mechanism is unclear, disruptions in neural differentiation processes due to CABP4 mutations could lead to ARMs. On the other hand, the POLD1 gene, encoding a subunit of DNA polymerase delta, is essential for DNA replication and repair. Mutations in POLD1 are linked to various cancers and conditions such as polymerase proofreading-associated polyposis syndrome, which increases colorectal cancer risk.34 POLD1 mutations could influence the development of the lower gastrointestinal tract, contributing to ARMs. Impairments in DNA repair mechanisms caused by POLD1 mutations might lead to abnormal tissue differentiation during fetal development, resulting in ARMs or related conditions.

Comparison of SARM with European GWAS yielded two common variants

On cross-referencing our SARM cohort with the genome-wide association study (GWAS) reported earlier, we identified two overlapping variants of interest (Table 2 and Figure 1). The first was a heterozygous missense variant in EFNA1 (NC_000001.11:g.155133751A>T), which was observed in seven of our samples. This variant was originally reported by Mingardo et al.35 in their GWAS, where it showed a strong statistical association with bladder exstrophy, with a p value of 4 × 1010. The second overlapping variant was located in ISL1 (NC_000005.10:g.51389671A>G), which was identified in five of our samples and previously reported by Draaken et al.36 in 2015. The variants appeared in both our data and the earlier European GWAS, showing possible shared genetic background for ARMs across populations. A genome-wide analysis of copy number variants (CNVs) in 450 individuals with ARMs identified four microscopic and nine submicroscopic CNVs (del2p13.2, del4p16.2, del7q31.33, del9p24.1, del16q12.1, del18q32, del22q11.21, dup2p13.2, and dup17q12). The researchers followed some filtrations and prioritized the chromosomal anomalies. Based on the embryonic expression and function, FOXK2, LPP, and SALL3 were suggested as the candidate genes for ARMs from the study.37

Common variants of SynARM with European GWAS

In our pipeline, these variants, however, did not meet the filtration criteria, which required a gnomAD allele frequency of less than 0.01 and inclusion of only “pathogenic,” “likely pathogenic,” and “VUS” variants. Moreover, we limited our analysis to SARMs, while the GWAS was focused on other types of ARMs as well. Nevertheless, we found some common variants while compromising the filters, which prompted us to further validate them through Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) (Figures S1–S18).

On a different note, what we observed was that the NC_000013.11:g.20189511C>T in GJB2 is associated with SARM1, which appeared due to AION’s Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) mapping. GJB2 is primarily known for causing hearing loss; however, the change NC_000013.11:g.20189511C>T introduces a stop-gain mutation (p.Trp24Ter). This is important because ARMs can occur in association with other anomalies like cat eye syndrome, which includes ocular coloboma, ear anomalies, and ARMs.38 A previous study reported that dysplastic ears, hearing loss, and ARMs co occur39 supporting the evidence that a pathogenic variant in the gene related to hearing is suggestive of a possible overlap genetic association with ARMs. While GJB2 is not associated with ARMs, we construe this as a limitation of AION inaccurately mapping the HPO terms. MutationTaster analysis supported the pathogenic nature of the GJB2 variant. A premature stop codon is introduced at the amino acid position 24 through this mutation, resulting in a truncated protein with loss of more than 90% of the coding sequence. The predicted truncation is consistent with a loss-of-function mechanism, potentially mediated by NMD, and removes all transmembrane helices, extracellular loops, and cytoplasmic domains essential for gap junction channel formation. In the context of our study, even though GJB2 was not recognized as a causal gene for ARMs, connexin-mediated gap junction signaling plays a critical role in epithelial cell-cell communication and embryonic tissue patterning.18,40 Disruption of these pathways may contribute congenital anomalies, making this variant as a secondary finding in the context of ARMs.

Identification of key hub genes

CytoHubba provided insights into the potential biological pathways associated with ARMs through the network-based prioritization of our variants. While PKD1, PKD2, and PKHD1 were identified as top hub genes, they encode proteins involved in abnormalities associated with polycystic kidney disease.41,42 They were seen to be largely expressed in colon regions and contribute to the defects in ciliary signaling affecting the organ patterning that is crucial during anorectal development. We also observed repeated occurrence of SMO and GLI2 in closeness, betweenness, and eccentricity analyses, which emphasized the importance of SHH signaling. SMO is smoothened, frizzled class receptor, and GLI2 is a glioma-associated oncogene family zinc finger protein. The SHH signaling is associated with VACTERL malformations and embryonic development, which is directly linked to ARM phenotypes.43 Furthermore, a study on miscarriages identified that GLI2 has associations with ARMs, particularly in patients with multiple congenital anomalies.44 We also observed that some hearing-related genes (CCDC50, LOXHD1, TMIE, and OTOA) formed distinct hubs in the eccentricity analysis. The direct role of these genes in ARMs is not yet identified completely, but GeneCards showed their expression in the colon and gastrointestinal tissues, suggesting that they can influence the developmental pathways associated with SARMs.

Our analysis revealed that POLD1 plays a significant role in several biological processes and is associated with colon and colorectal cancers. ARMs encompass a broad spectrum of anomalies that affect the anus and rectum, with varying severity. The close anatomical relationship and shared embryological origins of these structures indicate an overlap in the genetic and molecular pathways between ARMs and colon-rectum-associated diseases, which could go for functional validation. Furthermore, several genes including POLD1, POLD2, POLD3, RFC1, RFC4, PCNA, DNA2, and CDK2 are involved in replication, repair processes, and cell cycle progression. Disruptions in these processes can lead to developmental abnormalities, leading us to hypothesize a potential connection between POLD1 and ARM pathogenesis. CABP4 plays a major role in cellular signaling and developmental pathways, even as its interactions with other genes like PKD1 and PKD2 could indicate a potential link to congenital anomalies. KIAA0753, though not as well characterized, is known to encode a protein involved in ciliary function and centrosome organization, which are crucial for embryonic development and signaling pathways like SHH. Mutations in KIAA0753 are associated with ciliopathies, a group of disorders caused by defective cilia, including conditions like Joubert syndrome and other developmental anomalies affecting multiple organ systems. Its interaction with other genes like PKD1 and NPHP1 highlights its role in developmental processes. Disruptions in these pathways could lead to congenital anomalies, including ARMs.

Taken together, the integration of CytoHubba ranking algorithms revealed the overlapping hub genes within the SARM network (Figure 2). Venn diagram highlights PKD2 as the most consistently prioritized gene (maximal centrality measure [MCC], closeness, and betweenness), suggesting it as the common hub gene and a core regulator in the network, followed by PKD1 and PKHD1, common across the two measures. While PKD1 is haplo-insufficient, the analysis of gene-gene interaction network confirmed multisystem congenital anomalies observed in the cohort, including the ARM, cloaca and kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT). The defects could be the result of the disruption of highly connected genes and pathways. The clustering of NOTCH3, GLI2, PKD1, and PKHD1 genes showed a crosstalk between the Notch, SHH, and ciliopathy signaling pathways. These pathways are crucial for the development of caudal midline, kidneys, and genital tracts; thus, their defect could lead to urogenital defects.

Primary cilia help cells receive signals during early development. Many key developmental pathways, like the SHH pathway, depend on properly functioning cilia, with further defects leading to abnormal organ formation.26,45 The SHH pathway is important for hindgut development, and animal model studies have shown that the disruption of this signaling pathway causes anorectal and cloacal malformations.25 PKD1, PKD2, and PKHD1 encode proteins that are located in primary cilia and are important for maintaining ciliary function, regulating intracellular calcium signaling, and preserving epithelial organization during development; mutations in these genes disrupt cilia-related signaling and cell organization (Harris & Torres). The role of these genes in cilia function suggests that the changes may indirectly affect hindgut development by disrupting the developmental signaling pathways. PKHD1 has been shown to be expressed in epithelial derivatives during embryogenesis, including the gut, and localized to basal bodies and primary cilia.46 This highlights a broader developmental role beyond renal morphogenesis. We identified two missense variants in the PKD1 gene (NC_000016.10:g.2103386C>T and NC_000016.10:g.2103820C>T), which are currently classified as a “VUS” in ClinVar. Thus, the clinical impact of these variants on anorectal development remains unclear due to insufficient or conflicting evidence in current literature. Because PKD1 is essential for cilia-related signaling, these variants represent candidates for further investigation into the genetic architecture of ARMs.

Distinct CNVs are enriched associated with developmental anomalies

We analyzed structural variants across all eight samples and identified a total of 170 variants (Figure 3 and Table S2). Several of these variants were shared among multiple samples. Notably, a CNV, nsv429523, was observed in all eight samples (Figure 4). This CNV is located on chromosome 22 (23155487–50796027), a region that includes one of our prioritized genes, MIEF1. MIEF1 encodes a protein associated with mitochondrial dynamics, and alterations in mitochondrial function are known to contribute to developmental anomalies. The consistent presence of this CNV across all samples suggests a potential mitochondrial influence in the development of ARMs.

CNVs across different SARM samples

Chromosome-wise distribution of CNVs

We also sought to check the permutations and combinations of variants in the Genome India Project (GIP).47 Taken together, from the published list of alternative allele frequencies (AAFs) in GIP, we found variants that are present in ARMs but absent from large, healthy population databases like gnomAD and GIP, which indicates that they are indeed rare and, thus, strong candidates for pathogenicity. To signify, our candidate selection of ARMs is largely in lieu of VACTERL wherein the six variants absent from GIP may be prioritized as pathogenic variants as they may be having a high pLI, suggesting that functional variation in this region underlies the ARM manifestation.

Conclusion

We investigated the genetic basis of SARMs, a congenital anomaly with largely unknown genetic origin. We used WES to identify rare and potentially pathogenic variants discerned from our in-house developed CONVEX pipeline and further validated with GeneAssure in ARM-diagnosed individuals from a North Indian registry. Our findings include the prioritization of 20 variants and the identification of common variants (ISL1 and EFNA1) previously reported in European populations. Network analysis highlighted key genes like PKD1, PKD2, and PKHD1 and several developmental pathways associated with ARMs. Additionally, our structural variant analysis identified a prioritized variant within distinct CNVs. We believe these insights will contribute to improved genetic diagnoses and personalized interventions for SARMs.

Limitations of the study

Our work, however, has a few underlying observations and limitations.

1. CCDC50, OSMR, and NOTCH3 are known to be associated with developmental delays affecting the phenotypes in individuals with SARM5 and SARM7, while DCPS, a homozygous variant, was considered to be the other candidate associated with neuromuscular dysfunction apart from intellectual disabilities48 (Table S3). Nonetheless, among the genes harboring variants, ETV5 (ETS variant transcription factor 5) encodes a transcription factor for cell proliferation and differentiation during embryogenesis. The variant in the ETV5 gene (NC_000003.12:g.186079931T>G) was observed to be recurrently occurring across four independent SARM samples (SARM1, SARM2, SARM6, and SARM8). The T>G transversion at the position 186079931 of chr3 corresponds to the substitution in the coding sequence, resulting in a missense mutation (histidine to proline substitution). A study on early development in mice showed that ETV5 plays an important role in FGF signaling, which is crucial in embryonic body patterning and lineage specification.49 Thus, the amino acid shift from histidine to proline could disrupt the protein secondary structure, suggesting a plausible association with SARM pathogenicity. However, the gnomAD allele frequency in exomes is 0.0174734, which is quite high. Moreover, for the specific variant, even though the ClinVar annotation was “pathogenic” for myoepithelial tumor, ACMG classification was “VUS,” and AION was not able to predict the pathogenicity. Although numerous AI-driven variant prioritization approaches exist, we found that AION, aided by our CONVEX pipeline alone, was effective in prioritizing the variants.50

Participants were selected based on their phenotypes to ensure accurate categorization of ARM types with samples collected over two years, between 2022 and 2024. Method details

Library preparation

We extracted DNA from participant tissue samples using two methods: the Alexgen blood DNA kit for high-quality DNA and the CTAB protocol for preserving higher molecular weight DNA. Isolated DNA purity was confirmed with an A260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0, and samples were stored at -20°C until sequencing. We performed WES of protein-coding regions using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, generating 150bp paired-end reads with a 100X sequence depth encompassing a window for non-coding variant and 5’ UTR capture using Twist exome 2.0.

WES analyses

Exome analyses were conducted using our in-house, benchmarked Consensus Variant Pipeline for Exome Analyses (CONVEX).51 Briefly, raw FASTQ files underwent quality control with FASTQC which were aligned to the human reference genome GRCh38 using Bowtie2. Post-alignment quality assessments, including mapping quality and duplicate reads, were performed with Picard tools. Variants were identified via a consensus approach using Varscan, Bcftools, FreeBayes and Vt, followed by downstream prioritization. Genetic variants were identified by querying Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms in AION/Nostos Genomics (https://app.nostos-genomics.com/, last accessed September 30), an AI-driven platform for interpreting rare variants which utilizes multiple annotation sources for variant prediction and prioritization (Figure 5).

Workflow of identifying and validating the variants

We interpreted variants including single nucleotide variants (SNVs), copy number variations (CNV) and structural variants, from the annotated list, applying a genotype filter of 0/1 (heterozygous) and retaining variants with a gnomAD allele frequency below 0.01. Variants shortlisted from both the CONVEX pipeline and the AION platform were carefully assorted with ACMG classification and those that meet the criteria were validated using Integrated Genome Viewer (IGV). MutationTaster was used to predict the functional impact of the prioritized variants, for the disease causing potential of allele alterations based on evolutionary conservation, splice-site changes and protein features(https://www.genecascade.org/MutationTaster2025/ last accessed on February 1, 2026).

Meta-analyses

Additionally, we retrieved eight raw sequencing datasets of ARM cases from the NCBI SRA repository, originating from a Korean family with imperforate anus.52 This dataset was used to explore the genetic basis of the malformation in the family. Clinical and phenotypic information for each sample was recorded for comparison.

Cross-validation

To enhance the reliability of our findings, we cross-validated the results from our CONVEX pipeline using the GeneAssure Clinical suite (https://genespectrum.in/geneassure-clinical, last accessed on September 30, 2025) (Table S4). GeneAssure integrates GATK HaplotypeCaller53 and DeepVariant54 for the accurate identification of genomic variants. We correlated the identified variants with corresponding clinical phenotypes to assess potential genotype-phenotype associations. Variants shared among samples with similar phenotypes were prioritized to identify candidate genes and pathways that may contribute to Age-related Macular Degeneration. Additionally, we checked for previously reported ARM-associated variants from GWAS studies and confirmed their presence in our samples and the variants (Document S1). We also considered a WES employed study to identify the candidate gene for imperforate anus in a Korean family (six affected individuals in three generations). Many filtering steps were done and five candidate variants were identified in IL1RL2, GIGYF2, CCDC96, EBF2 and FADS6 genes.
