Efficacy of immunosuppressive therapy in low-risk and intermediate- risk idiopathic membranous nephropathy patients : a 2-year follow-up study
Authors: Yan Jin, Yan Zhang, Wei Chen, Lei Lan, Chaoyi Chen, Jun Jiang, Jielong Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe efficacy and safety of immunosuppressive therapy for low- and intermediate-risk idiopathic membranous nephropathy (IMN) remain unclear.MethodA total of 117 renal biopsy-confirmed idiopathic membranous nephropathy (IMN) patients were retrospectively enrolled from the Department of Nephrology, First Affiliated Hospital of USTC (2021–2023). Participants were stratified by baseline urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio (uPCR): low-risk group (≤ 3.0 g/gCr) vs. intermediate-risk group (> 3.0 g/gCr). The low-risk group included 57 patients, 26 of whom received immunosuppressive agents after a minimum of 3 months of supportive care due to persistent proteinuria. All remaining patients initiated immunosuppressive therapy at enrollment. Total remission rates (TRR, including complete remission rate [CRR] and partial remission rate [PRR]) and CRR were compared at 6, 12, and 24 months, with infectious events documented. Binary logistic regression identified risk factors for remission.ResultsA total of 117 IMN patients were stratified into low-risk (n = 57) and intermediate-risk (n = 60) groups. During the 2-year follow-up, the low-risk group exhibited significantly higher complete remission rates than the intermediate-risk group at 6 months (P < 0.01), 12 months (P < 0.01), and 24 months (P = 0.01). No significant between-group differences in total remission rates were observed at any time point. Binary logistic regression revealed intermediate-risk classification as an independent risk factor for non-remission across all follow-up points (6, 12, and 24 months; all P < 0.05). Male sex was positively associated with complete remission (CR) only at 12 months (OR = 3.24, P = 0.04), whereas no significant associations were detected at 6 and 24 months. Serum triglyceride levels, anti-PLA2R antibody status, and specific immunosuppressive regimens showed no significant correlations with CR at any follow-up time point (all P > 0.05). The overall incidence of infections was comparable between the two groups (P > 0.05), and no deaths occurred throughout follow-up.ConclusionAn observational association was identified between early immunosuppressive therapy and higher CRRs at all follow-up time points in patients with low-risk IMN, with no marked elevation in infectious risk observed. However, this correlation cannot be interpreted as definitive therapeutic benefit due to major confounding factors including unassessed spontaneous remission and absence of a supportive-care-only control group.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Idiopathic membranous nephropathy (IMN) is a leading cause of adult nephrotic syndrome and a significant contributor to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. Its pathogenesis primarily involves the deposition of immune complexes in the subepithelial space of the glomerular basement membrane [2]. The natural history of IMN is heterogeneous: while approximately 30% of patients achieve spontaneous remission, another 30% progress to ESRD within 10–15 years [3].

Given the risks associated with immunosuppressive therapy—including infections and metabolic disorders—current guidelines emphasize risk-stratified management. The 2021 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines recommend supportive care and close monitoring for 6–12 months for low- and intermediate-risk patients, reserving early immunosuppression for high-risk individuals with persistent nephrotic-range proteinuria or declining renal function [4]. The primary goals of IMN treatment are inducing proteinuria remission and preserving renal function while minimizing adverse events [5]. Available regimens include corticosteroids combined with alkylating agents, calcineurin inhibitors, and biologics like rituximab [6, 7]. Although immunosuppressive therapy has been shown to improve remission rates and slow ESRD progression compared to supportive care alone [8], the long-term efficacy and safety across different risk profiles—particularly in low-to-intermediate risk cohorts—require further investigation [9].

Comparative studies on long-term outcomes in large IMN cohorts remain limited. Therefore, this retrospective study evaluates the impact of initial immunosuppressive therapy on clinical outcomes and infection risks in 117 IMN patients stratified into low-risk and intermediate-risk groups according to baseline urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio (uPCR).

Methods

Data collection and patient enrollment

We retrospectively enrolled 117 patients with biopsy-confirmed IMN from the Nephrology Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China (Anhui Provincial Hospital) between 2021 and 2023. Patients were stratified into two groups according to their baseline uPCR: a low-risk group (uPCR ≤ 3.0 g/gCr, n = 57) and an intermediate-risk group (uPCR > 3.0 g/gCr, n = 60). Among the low-risk patients, 26 initiated immunosuppressive therapy due to persistent proteinuria after at least 3 months of supportive management. The remaining 31 received immunosuppressive therapy according to patient preference, progressive clinical changes during follow-up, and individualized clinical assessment.

This study followed the 2021 KDIGO guidelines. Nevertheless, simplified risk stratification criteria were adopted for this retrospective real-world cohort, resulting in minor discrepancies from the standardized guideline definitions. The inclusion criteria were defined as: (1) renal biopsy-confirmed IMN; (2) adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years); (3) no prior immunosuppressive therapy; (4) immunosuppressive therapy initiated upon strong patient willingness or clinical indications, including persistent proteinuria after minimum 3 months of supportive management, evidence of disease progression during follow-up, or individualized clinical assessment by attending physicians; (5) signed informed consent. Exclusion criteria included: (1) secondary MN (including but not limited to lupus-associated, hepatitis-related, drug/toxin-induced, or malignancy-associated forms); (2) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73m2; (3) patients meeting high-risk criteria. All patients routinely received Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia (PCP) prophylaxis with oral sulfamethoxazole (SMZ) throughout the treatment period. This study protocol adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and complies with relevant Chinese clinical trial regulations and laws. It has been approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China (Anhui Provincial Hospital) (Approval No.: 2025-RE-436). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Clinical data and treatment regimens

Using the hospital’s electronic medical record system, we retrospectively collected the demographic and clinical data, including: (1) Demographic characteristics: age, gender, and body mass index (BMI); (2) Laboratory parameters: baseline and follow-up measurements of urinary PCR, serum creatinine (sCr), eGFR, serum albumin (ALB), total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), hemoglobin, as well as binary variables indicating the presence or absence of dysglycemia and hypertension; (3) Anti-PLA2R antibody status: categorized as positive or negative. The evaluated therapeutic regimens included tacrolimus, which was administered either as monotherapy or in combined with glucocorticoids, at a fixed daily dose of 0.05–0.1 mg/kg, the total daily dose was split into two equal doses administered every 12 h. Cyclophosphamide (GC-CYC) was combined with glucocorticoids, given intravenously at a dose of 0.6 g once monthly during the initial 6-month induction phase, followed by a maintenance phase where the infusion schedule was adjusted according to clinical response, particularly urinary protein levels, with subsequent infusions administered every 2–3 months over a total treatment duration of 2 years. Rituximab (RTX) was administered using one of two evidence-based protocols: either as two intravenous infusions of 1000 mg each, separated by 14 days (on days 1 and 15), or as weekly intravenous infusions of 375 mg/m² for four consecutive weeks. Clinical outcomes and infection events were systematically documented throughout the follow-up period. Treatment efficacy and infection risk were assessed by comparing proteinuria remission rates and the incidence of infectious events between the two groups.

Definitions

eGFR was estimated via the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation. Treatment responses were classified into three categories according to proteinuria levels and renal function: (1) Partial remission (PR): Urinary protein excretion < 3.5 g/d (uPCR < 3500 mg/g or < 350 mg/mmol) with a ≥ 50% decline from peak proteinuria. Two consecutive tests separated by ≥ 1 week were required for confirmation, accompanied by normalized or elevated serum albumin and stable serum creatinine. (2) Complete remission (CR): Urinary protein excretion < 0.3 g/d (or uPCR < 30 mg/mmol, equivalent to 300 mg/g), confirmed by two measurements at least 1 week apart, with normal serum albumin and stable serum creatinine. (3) Treatment failure: Failure to meet the diagnostic criteria for either PR or CR [4]. Total remission(TR): included both PR and CR. Anti-PLA2R Autoantibodies Positivity: Defined as baseline serum levels > 20 RU/ml [10].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 26.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were tested for normality; normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared via independent samples t-tests, while non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed via the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, and between-group differences were assessed using Pearson’s χ² or Fisher’s exact test, depending on expected cell counts. The primary efficacy endpoints of this study were CRR and TRR. Binary logistic regression was applied to identify independent predictors of proteinuria remission and calculate odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI), adjusted for relevant clinical covariates. All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the 117 IMN patients

The study comprised 117 biopsy-confirmed IMN patients, including 69 males (59.0%) and 48 females (41.0%). Baseline characteristics were as follows: age 52.0 [41.0, 58.0] years, urinary PCR 2.9 [1.7, 4.7] g/gCr, eGFR 107.1 [98.2, 116.1] mL/min/1.73 m², and serum albumin 27.1 ± 5.9 g/L. Patients were stratified into a low-risk group (n = 57, 48.7%) and an intermediate-risk group (n = 60, 51.3%) according to their baseline uPCR. Comparative analysis of baseline characteristics revealed significant differences between the two groups. Specifically, the intermediate-risk group exhibited significantly higher TC and uPCR compared to the low-risk group (P < 0.01), whereas serum albumin and eGFR were significantly lower in the intermediate-risk group (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, sex, BMI, TG, Hb, dysglycemia, hypertension, anti-phospholipase A2 receptor antibody (anti-PLA2R AB) status, use of angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs) or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), or immunosuppressive regimens (cyclophosphamide, tacrolimus, rituximab) (all P > 0.05) (Table 1).

Outcomes of follow up

At the 6-month follow-up, the overall TRR was 79.5% (93/117), with no statistically significant difference between the low-risk (84.2%, 48/57) and intermediate-risk groups (75.0%, 45/60) (χ²=1.52, P = 0.22). However, a significant between-group difference in CRR was detected: the low-risk group had a markedly higher CRR (61.4%, 35/57) than the intermediate-risk group (26.7%, 16/60) (χ²=14.34, P < 0.01) (Table 2).

At the 12-month follow-up, a total of 12 patients (10.3%) were lost to follow-up, leaving 105 participants for final analysis (52 in the low-risk group and 53 in the intermediate-risk group). The overall TRR was 91.4% (96/105), with no significant difference between the low-risk group (92.5%, 49/53) and the intermediate-risk group (90.4%, 47/52) (χ2 = 0.14, P = 0.71). The CRR in the low-risk group (81.1%, 43/53) continued to be significantly higher than that in the intermediate-risk group (55.8%, 29/52) (χ² = 7.84, P<0.01) (Table 3).

Factors associated with 6-month, 12-month and 24-month complete remission

To explore potential influencing factors for CR, univariable logistic regression analyses were performed for CR at 6-month, 12-month, and 24-month follow-ups. Variables included risk stratification (intermediate-risk vs. low-risk), sex (male vs. female), anti-PLA2R AB (negative v.s positive), immunosuppressive regimens (CYC, Tac, RTX), and serum triglyceride levels.

Risk stratification was independently associated with CR across all follow-up time points (6, 12, and 24 months). Specifically, patients in the intermediate-risk group had lower odds of achieving CR compared with those in the low-risk group at 6 months (OR = 0.16, 95%CI : 0.06–0.38, P < 0.01), 12 months (OR = 0.23, 95%CI : 0.08–0.67, P<0.01), and 24 months (OR = 0.28, 95%CI : 0.09–0.85, P = 0.03). Male sex was positively correlated with CR only at 12-month follow-up (OR = 3.24, 95%CI : 1.09–9.67, P = 0.04), with no statistically significant association observed at 6 and 24 months. Furthermore, serum triglyceride levels, anti-PLA2R antibody status and different immunosuppressive regimens (CYC, Tac, RTX) showed no statistically significant association with CR at any follow-up time point (all P > 0.05) (Table 5).

Comparison of infection events and infection-related mortality

Among the 117 patients with IMN, a total of 10 (11.7%) infectious events were recorded. These included 1 herpes zoster, 1 urinary tract infection, 3 bacterial pneumonias, and 5 PCP cases. The distribution of infectious events was comparable between the low-risk and intermediate-risk groups (P > 0.05). The low-risk group experienced 6 infections (1 herpes zoster, 2 bacterial pneumonias, 3 PCP), while the intermediate-risk group had 4 infections (1 urinary tract infection, 1 bacterial pneumonia, 2 PCP). Importantly, no infection-related mortality events occurred during the entire follow-up period (Table 6).

Discussion

Idiopathic membranous nephropathy (IMN) continues to pose a significant challenge in nephrology, with proteinuria recognized as an independent prognostic risk factor [11]. Accumulating clinical evidence consistently demonstrates that early proteinuria remission attenuates eGFR decline and optimizes patient prognosis [12, 13]. Achieving early remission predicts better long-term renal survival [14], and early complete remission (CR) can reduce the 10-year risk of end-stage renal disease by 72% [15]. These findings collectively underscore the critical importance of timely and effective remission for optimal disease management.

The findings of our study are consistent with current evidence, showing that immunosuppressive therapy may induce remission in patients with IMN. At the 6-month follow-up, the overall TRR was 79.5%, in line with previous studies showing improved PR and slower ESRD progression with immunosuppressants relative to supportive care alone [8]. A key finding from our study was a consistent, significantly higher CRR in the low-risk group compared with the intermediate-risk group at all follow-up time points (6, 12, and 24 months). We observed an early gap in CRR: the low-risk group had a 6-month CRR of 61.4%, higher than the 32% reported by Fujimoto et al. [16]. Within our study population, this numerical difference may suggest that patients with milder baseline proteinuria tend to display more favorable remission responses during early treatment. The low-risk group maintained statistically higher CRR relative to the intermediate-risk group at all follow-up time points up to 24 months. Both groups achieved high TRR at 12 months, with values exceeding the 66.7%-80.9% TRR range reported by Sun et al. [17]. In addition, the 24-month CRR of 86.3% observed among low-risk patients exceeded the 60%–85.7% TRR range reported in previous research [18]. This finding generated a hypothesis that patients with milder baseline proteinuria may have a higher likelihood of achieving sustained complete proteinuria remission, consistent with prior IMN treatment studies [12] and further supported by recent work emphasizing the importance of early treatment initiation for improved outcomes [19]. Notably, our study supplements existing IMN literature by reporting detailed 12- and 24-month CRR data, which are frequently absent in prior IMN studies [20, 21]. Beyond baseline proteinuria, our analysis revealed additional independent predictors of CR. Male sex showed a positive correlation with CR exclusively at the 12-month follow-up, representing a transient association that differs from prior reports demonstrating an association between male sex and reduced odds of spontaneous remission in IMN [22]. This time-limited association may imply that the influence of sex on CR is modulated by other factors or treatment responses that are more prominent at specific time points. Alternatively, the well-balanced demographic profiles across our comparison cohorts may have diluted the consistent sex effect reported in other study populations [23, 24]. Further research with larger cohorts and stratified analyses is warranted to fully elucidate the role of sex in IMN remission.

Our univariate analysis revealed no significant association between baseline anti-PLA2R antibody status and CR across all follow-up time points. This finding, while seemingly unexpected given the well-established prognostic value of anti-PLA2R antibodies in IMN [2, 13], could be largely attributed to methodological limitations. Crucially, incomplete quantitative anti-PLA2R titer data forced us to adopt a simplified binary classification. This simplification likely obscured the intricate relationship between antibody levels and treatment response, as baseline quantitative titers and their dynamic changes over time are well-validated predictors of proteinuria remission and long-term renal outcomes [25, 26]. This combined with the univariate nature of the analysis, which does not adjust for confounding factors or interactions, limits definitive conclusions. While our results indicated no independent association within this study population, they do not negate the established clinical value of anti-PLA2R antibodies in the management of IMN. Large-scale, prospective studies with serial quantitative anti-PLA2R measurements and multivariable analyses are required to fully clarify its prognostic performance. Serum triglyceride levels exhibited no significant association with CR at any follow-up time point. This is consistent with the current understanding that while hyperlipidemia is a common comorbidity of nephrotic syndrome and elevates cardiovascular risk, its direct impact on the immunotherapy response or proteinuria remission is not observed [27, 28]. Accordingly, lipid-lowering interventions including statins are mainly administered to reduce cardiovascular complications, not to directly improve proteinuria remission. Furthermore, our analysis revealed no significant association between different immunosuppressive regimens (CYC, Tac, RTX) and CR achievement at any follow-up time point (all P > 0.05). While this result may seem counterintuitive given the diverse mechanisms and reported efficacies of these agents in IMN [6–8], it is important to note that this univariable analysis does not account for potential confounding factors or the specific clinical contexts guiding treatment selection. Treatment decisions are often individualized according to patient characteristics, disease severity, and physician judgment. It is also plausible that the study’s sample size and follow-up durations were insufficient to detect subtle differences between regimens. Multitarget therapy and specific regimens have demonstrated varying efficacy in other studies [29, 30], suggesting that a more detailed analysis, potentially involving multivariable models or subgroup analyses, may be necessary to further characterize the comparative effectiveness of these immunosuppressive agents.

Infection remains the most common complication associated with immunosuppressive therapy, with frequent infection sites including the respiratory tract, urinary tract, gastrointestinal tract, and skin [31]. In our study, infections were diagnosed according to clinical manifestations such as respiratory symptoms, urinary discomfort, with confirmation supported by imaging examinations as well as blood and urine laboratory tests. PCP was primarily identified through pathogen next-generation sequencing (NGS) from sputum or blood samples. Notably, the overall infection incidence observed in our cohort was numerically lower than the 42.1% reported by Eren Sadioglu R [32], the 27.6% reported by Yan P [33] and the 21.8% documented by Choi JY [19]. Meanwhile, our observed infection rate was comparable to the 6.3% reported by Artan et al. [34]. This relatively low infection rate and satisfactory safety profile in our study can likely be attributed to the standardized, universal administration of SMZ for PCP prophylaxis, which substantially reduced susceptibility to opportunistic infections.

While this single-center retrospective study offers preliminary observations regarding clinical outcomes of immunosuppressive therapy in low-risk IMN patients, these findings must be interpreted with substantial caution due to several inherent limitations. Firstly, a substantial selection bias was present within the low-risk group. In contrast to KDIGO guideline recommendations that low-risk IMN patients should avoid immunosuppressive therapy within the initial 6 months, all enrolled low-risk patients in our real-world cohort received immunosuppressive therapy. Among these participants, some had undergone a minimum 3-month observation period before treatment initiation, while others started immunosuppression owing to progressive clinical changes during follow-up and individualized clinical assessment. Importantly, our study did not include a parallel control cohort managed with supportive care alone, as recommended by the 2021 KDIGO clinical practice guideline. This non-randomized, uncontrolled design precludes definitive causal inference regarding the superior efficacy of immunosuppressive therapy in low-risk IMN patients. Furthermore, spontaneous remission—a well-documented phenomenon in IMN, particularly among low-risk patients—represents an important confounding factor. Without a sufficiently long initial observation period (e.g., 6 months or more) to identify and exclude patients who might achieve spontaneous remission, it is difficult to determine whether the observed remission in the treated low-risk group was attributable solely to immunosuppressive therapy or partly to the natural disease course. This confounding may overestimate the therapeutic benefits of immunosuppression in the low-risk subgroup. Notably, real-world treatment decisions are often influenced by factors beyond strict guideline compliance, such as patients’ preference for active intervention, dynamic worsening of clinical indicators (e.g., proteinuria or renal function), and individualized physician judgment. These practical factors frequently preclude a rigid 6-month observation period for all low-risk patients, further complicating efforts to distinguish the specific effects of immunosuppression from spontaneous remission. Anti-PLA2R antibody titer and dynamic changes, which carry important prognostic value in IMN, could not be fully evaluated. Our retrospective cohort only contained qualitative anti-PLA2R results for some patients, and complete quantitative titer data were inconsistent across all participants due to inconsistent testing methods during the study period. Accordingly, we classified all patients using a unified binary qualitative standard (positive vs. negative) to maintain cohort consistency. Additional limitations include the single-center design, which may limit the generalizability of our findings, and a relatively modest sample size, which could reduce the statistical power to detect subtle differences or associations. The inherent tendency toward active intervention in treatment-adherent patients within a real-world setting also introduces potential bias. In conclusion, this study only offers preliminary real-world observational evidence. Future large-scale, prospective, controlled, multicenter studies are required to validate these findings and further clarify the relative impact of early immunosuppression versus spontaneous remission in low-risk IMN patients.

Conclusion

This retrospective study preliminarily explored the potential correlation between early immunosuppressive therapy and proteinuria remission in low- and intermediate-risk IMN patients during a 2-year follow-up period, without excess safety concerns. Notably, a numerically higher CRR was observed in the low-risk group compared to the intermediate-risk group across all time points, which generates a hypothesis that patients with milder baseline proteinuria might experience more favorable remission outcomes. Nevertheless, several critical constraints restrict the generalizability of our data: the single-center retrospective nature of the analysis, the inability to separate treatment-specific effects from spontaneous disease remission, and risk stratification criteria not following KDIGO guideline. For these reasons, all findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered solely hypothesis-generating rather than definitive clinical evidence. Further large-scale, prospective, controlled multicenter studies are needed to verify these preliminary observations and clarify whether early immunosuppressive therapy delivers meaningful clinical benefits for the management of patients with low-risk IMN.
