Topological modeling of gene expression in the brain with Huntington’s disease reveals selective disruption of co-expression network
Authors: Yuko Okamura-Oho, Kazuro Shimokawa, Satoshi Oota, Atsushi Yoshiki, Masahiko Morita, Masaomi Nishimura, Sakiko Nakamura, Yuki Tsujimura, Shumpei Ishikawa, Hideo Yokota
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe applied transcriptome tomography to create a whole-brain model of early-stage Huntington’s disease (HD) in R6/2 mice, which ubiquitously express truncated human mutant HTT containing approximately 150 CAG repeats. Medium spiny neuron (MSN)-related genes showed abnormal expression in the HD brain, in terms of expression similarity to wild-type Htt. Bdnf was the most probable upstream regulator of these genes. Smarca4, the Bdnf-regulator, was similarly expressed to wild-type Htt in the control brain; however, this was not observed in HD, implying a possible involvement of Smarca4 in glutamate excitotoxicity in HD. Lhx6, a master gene for MSN-related pathway development ordinarily conserved postnatally and in adulthood, was lost in the HD co-expression network. And a network hub node, Fcho1, was lost in connection involving Lhx6 and mitochondrial gene clusters. Loss of Smarca4, Lhx6, and Fcho1 in co-expression may contribute to spatiotemporally specific neuronal loss in HD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56101-8.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Genetics, Molecular biology, Neurology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Htt is expressed in many cell types and is important for pattern formation in the early embryo, functioning as a transcriptional regulator1,2. An aberrant expansion of CAG trinucleotide repeats in Exon 1 of Htt causes a neurological disease called Huntington’s disease (HD), characterized by initial movement disorder in adults, and progressive motor, cognitive, and psychiatric abnormalities, ultimately culminating in lethal neurodegeneration after approximately 20 years of illness3. The initial histopathological change is the loss of medium-sized neurons (MSNs) in the striatum, a subcortical area of the cerebrum4. MSNs receive signals from neurons in the cerebral cortex using glutamate as a neurotransmitter and transmit signals to the ventral areas of the cerebrum through gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)5. Neuronal loss progresses from MSNs to the connected neurons, affecting cortical neurons first through retrograde degeneration along the cortico-striatal neural pathway6. This selective neuronal loss is a characteristic pathological feature of early-stage HD. In contrast, broadly expressed abnormal Htt products lead to various cellular dysfunctions in both patients and disease-model mice.

A part of the mutant gene of human Htt, including its 5′-URL and Exon 1 harboring abnormally expanded CAG repeats, is sufficient to cause clinical and histological phenotypes in transgenic mice R6/27. Several studies on disease model mice have clarified the molecular mechanisms of cell death and described the dominant effects of mutant genes. However, the mechanisms by which widely expressed mutant Htt leads to selective neuronal loss in a distinctive spatiotemporal manner are not fully understood8. In this study, to investigate the molecular basis of the characteristic neuron loss, we systemically analyzed whole-brain gene expression patterns of the HD model mice at the early stage of the disease.

For the systemic analysis of whole transcripts, we applied transcriptome tomography (TT), a framework that we invented for the integrated analysis of a 3-dimensional (3D) spatial overview of gene expression (considering anatomical structures) and the topological views of co-expression networks of transcripts9–11 (see Supplementary Video 1 and the video “Mapping genes onto brain structures” on RIKEN channel-YouTube: https://youtu.be/Td4rGRQIZuY). In a previous study, we applied this to the brains of normal mice and identified co-expression gene networks that followed the power law feature and were rich in genes of functional interaction or molecular association in discrete cell types or anatomical areas12. In the present study, we applied this system to the model mice with a subtle movement abnormality13, and using a bioinformatics approach, we aimed to identify key genes associated with spatiotemporally selective neuron loss in HD (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Results

Integrated analysis of anatomical expression maps and topological co-expression network graphs of transcripts with expression patterns similar to Htt (Htt-SETs)

Anatomical expression maps created using TT provided a 3D overview of the gene expression densities (Fig. 1a). Wild-type Htt was broadly expressed in the whole brain, with low expression in the anterior forebrain of the control (Cont) brain at 5 weeks of age. It was more broadly expressed in the HD brain than in the Cont brain. The expression of other genes was also altered. For example, the expression of Grin1, a type of glutamate receptor: N-methyl-D-aspartate subunit 1 (NMDA1) receptor14, was similar to that of wild-type Htt in the Cont brain. However, in the HD brain, the expression density was high in the striatum and low in the globus pallidus of the ventral area of the brain, resulting in loss of its expression similarity to wild-type Htt. Higher Grin1 expression in the striatum than in the globus pallidus in HD was consistent with previous histological results showing higher susceptibility to glutamate excitotoxicity in striatal neurons than in the neurons of the globus pallidus in HD14. The expression of Nrxn115 was low in the ventral forebrain of the Cont mouse, whereas it was broadly expressed in the HD brain, resulting in an increased expression similarity to wild-type Htt in HD.

Using the transcripts as nodes and linking two nodes with high expression similarities by edges, the loss or gain of expression similarity to wild-type Htt was depicted in a topological co-expression network graph (Fig. 1b). The graph comprised 210 nodes of similarly expressed transcripts to Htt (Htt-SETs; r > 0.85, with the threshold value adopted from our previous study12, at least in the HD or Cont brains; 56 nodes were unchanged in HD; 71, including Grin1, were lost; and 83, including Nrxn1, were gained in the co-expression with Htt in HD. A bioinformatics search revealed that Htt-SETs included 18 genes related to the molecular pathology of HD (Supplementary Reference numbers are displayed in Table 1). Moreover, we found that 21 genes, including Nlgn216 and Smarca4 (also known as Brg1)17, were co-expressed with Htt and Grin1 in the Cont brain; however, the co-expression with Htt was lost in the HD brain. Similarly, 23 genes, including Cdh2 and Pcdhac2 for synaptic formation, and Kcnma1 for postsynaptic potassium channel related to movement disorder, were co-expressed with Htt and Nrxn1 in the HD brain, but not with Htt in the Cont brain (Fig. 1b).

Nrxn1 and Nlgn2 proteins are known to localize to presynaptic and postsynaptic membranes, respectively, and form complexes at GABA-mediating synapses in the central nervous system15. In this study, we showed gain and loss of Nrxn1 and Nlgn2 expression similarity with wild-type Htt in the HD brain, respectively, together with additional genes functioning in synapse formation. Moreover, considering the HD-related molecular functions of Akt3, Atf2, Dnm1, Gls, Gnao1, Gsk3b, Mapt (Tau), Myo5a, Myt1l, Napb, Pde4dip, Pdpk1, Prkaa2, and Ssx2ip, we found that Htt-SETs harbored multiple genes functioning in the NMDA1-receptor positive and GABA-producing neurons and in their synaptic vicinities for neurite and synaptic formation and maintenance (Supplementary Reference numbers seen in Table 1). Hence, we hypothesized that the gain or loss of co-expression between these genes and wild-type Htt may be associated with alterations in their coordinated transcriptional relationships in HD (Fig. 2 Box a1–b2). Alterations in the Htt-linked co-expression network in HD

To assess the whole brain co-expression network, we constructed an Htt-linked network. Pairs of transcripts with consistently high expression similarities (r > 0.85) in normal mouse brains aged 3 days, 7 days, and 3 weeks were selected. Among them, 6,935 transcripts were in the brains of HD or Cont mice at 5 weeks of age (Supplementary Table 1a). Co-expression network graphs depicted conserved networks in the postnatal stages; large and multiple small networks were formed. The node for Htt was not included in the networks; however, 64 of 210 nodes of the Htt-SETs were members of the large, conserved network (Supplementary Table 1b–d). Subsequently, the co-expression network of Htt-SETs and the conserved network formed a single network comprising 4,617 nodes and 148,161 edges (Supplementary Table 1e). All nodes were linked to the node of the wild-type Htt, although not always directly; thus, we denoted this as an Htt-linked network (Fig. 3a). The gene annotation and network characteristics of the nodes and edges are presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3.

Mutant Htt expression was associated with alterations in the expression of other transcripts, accompanied by changes in transcript co-expression pairs and the overall Htt-linked network structure in the HD brain compared with the Cont brain. The adopted assay system in this study enabled the evaluation of wild-type Htt expression without cross-detection of mutant Htt, despite the mutant gene being expressed in the HD model mice7. Using this topological co-expression model, widespread transcriptional alterations associated with mutant Htt expression were observed in the HD brain (Fig. 3a).

In addition to the Htt-SET network or nearby areas, the alterations extended to the whole Htt-linked network: 82% (3,789 of 4,617 in Supplementary Table 1e) of nodes, and 75% (110,953 of 148,161) of edges were present in both Cont and HD networks (unchanged in HD), indicating that the whole network was robust to mutant Htt expression. However, 11% of nodes and 18% of edges were in Cont, but not in the HD network (lost in HD), indicating that normally maintained co-expression in the conserved network or the Htt-SETs of Cont were lost in HD. Moreover, 7% of nodes and 7% of edges were not present in Cont, but were present in HD, indicating that normally absent co-expression at 5 weeks of age was not deleted (retained in HD) in the conserved network or that abnormal co-expression was created in the Htt-SETs of HD (gained in HD, Fig. 3b–d).

These changes occurred unevenly in the network. A comparison of coding and non-coding genes and unannotated transcripts revealed that nodes lost in HD were rich in coding genes (Supplementary Table 4a), and nodes for coding genes were more changed (22% lost or retained/gained in total) than other types of transcripts (Supplementary Table 4b). Portions of the non-coding genes were similarly expressed and formed multiple node groups in the network components unchanged in HD (Fig. 3e–g).

Clustering analysis of the Htt-linked network

Overview and Cluster #1

To assess the topological effects of mutant Htt expression, we clustered the Htt-linked network, identified clusters severely changed in the HD brain, and characterized clusters by the functions of the genes included and by connections to other clusters (Fig. 4; the Cluster # for each node is presented in Supplementary Table 2). All nodes in the nine clusters were unchanged in HD, showing the robustness of these networks: Clusters #4, 7, 10, 12, 14, 18, 21, 23, and 24 for lncRNA, glial cell formation, opioid function, mitochondrial apoptosis and lipid metabolism, cannabinoid signaling, lipid metabolism, synaptic vesicle exocytosis, Notch signaling, and Gapdh pseudogene, respectively.

Cluster #1, the largest cluster, comprised 1,524 nodes and was in the center of the Htt-linked network (Fig. 4a). The genes are mostly significant in common cellular functions, such as mitochondrial oxidative metabolism, ribosomal function, transcription, translation, and ubiquitination. Almost all the nodes (93%) were unchanged. The distribution of nodes lost or retained in HD was non-uniform in the cluster. Sub-cluster #1, which included more lost edges than the other parts, is discussed later in mitochondrial dysfunction and propagation to Cluster #3. In the other part of Cluster #1, the retained nodes were rich in lncRNA genes (20 of 34 nodes). In the averaged network graph, Cluster #1 was connected to Cluster #6, which was rich in lncRNAs and was retained in HD (Fig. 4b). It was also connected to Clusters #11/12/16 rich in genes for mitochondrial function and was lost/unchanged/retained in HD. Cluster #1 was connected to Clusters #8 and #9 for estrogen function and RNA processing, respectively, but not to Cluster #2.

Cluster #2

The second-largest cluster contained 210 and 24 Htt-SET and neighboring nodes, respectively. Htt-SETs included nodes for genes functioning in NMDA1-receptor positive and GABA-producing neurons, as described above, and the neighboring nodes contained three genes related to HD pathology, namely Lmtk3, Ntrk2 (BDNF receptor, TrkB), and Tmeff1 (Supplementary Reference numbers are displayed in Table 1).

BDNF, which protects neurons from glutamate excitotoxicity18, was the most likely upstream regulator of Cluster #2 genes (p < 1.66 × 10− 5): 13 genes functioning downstream of BDNF were present (Table 1; Fig. 2). Among the genes, six are related to HD pathology thus: two, Grin119, as mentioned above, and Mapt (Tau)20 were lost in co-expression; Napb, engaged in the disassembly of the SNARE complex affected in HD21 and functioning in presynaptic release of glutamate modulated by BDNF22, was gained in co-expression; Gnao120, Myo5a20, and Ntrk223, were unchanged. A literature search revealed that the other seven genes could also be related to HD pathology regarding the neuroprotective function of BDNF as follows: Celf4 determines choline- or GABA-producing neuronal cell type20 and is lost in HD; Arih120, Nrxn120, Pafah1b120, and Slc16a7 gained; Mapk8ip320 and Slc12a5 were unchanged.

A literature search revealed two genes for upstream regulator proteins of Bdnf expression: Smarca4, the gene encoding a subunit of the chromatin remodeling regulator complex, BRG1/BRM associated factor (BAF)17, and Mef2d, the gene encoding a transcription factor working with BAF24. The BAF system controls the neural activity-regulated expression of genes, such as Bdnf, during neural development and plasticity25 (Fig. 2 Box c). As mentioned above, Smarca4 lost its co-expression with wild-type Htt in the HD brain. Consistent with this observation, co-expression patterns of BDNF-related genes were also altered in HD.

The Htt protein (huntingtin) is a transcriptional regulator and facilitates Bdnf transcription by inhibiting the neuron-restrictive silencer element (NRSE), and the abnormal Htt protein slightly inhibits NRSE, causing low BDNF expression and neuronal loss, as observed in patients with HD26,27. However, the mechanism by which Htt protein regulates Bdnf transcription in a neural activity-dependent manner and protects neurons from glutamate excitotoxicity is only partially understood. Here, we showed that Smarca4, the BAF component for the activity-regulated expression of Bdnf, was co-expressed with wild-type Htt and the glutamate receptor, Grin1, in the Cont brain, but not with Htt in HD. Together, these findings suggest altered co-expression relationships between Smarca4 and Htt-associated Bdnf-related pathways in HD (Fig. 2 Box c).

Clusters #15 and #19

The small Clusters #15 and #19, comprising 26 and 21 nodes, respectively, were connected to Cluster #2 (Fig. 4b). They included nodes for genes anatomically and functionally related to those in Cluster #2: Gabrb2, a type of GABA-receptor in Cluster #15 and Chrna4, a striatal interneuron marker gene for cholinergic receptor in Cluster #19 (Fig. 2). In addition, two genes, Adcy9 and Adcy7, adenylate cyclases that are related to movement and mental disorders, Rett syndrome, and autism spectrum disorder, were observed in Clusters 2 and 19, respectively (Fig. 2 Box d, Supplementary Reference numbers seen in Table 1). Although their co-expression with wild-type Htt was not changed in HD, the characteristic involuntary movements of HD might be related to the inappropriate activation of these brain-specific types of adenylate cyclase.

Cluster #3

The third largest cluster comprised 89 nodes and was isolated from the other clusters in the averaged network (Fig. 4b). Nodes for genes functioning in embryonic pattern formation and glutamate- or choline-mediated signaling were included as follows: transcriptional master genes for cerebral pattern formation, Lhx2 and Lhx628–30; transcription factors for cortical pattern formation, Emx1, Emx231, and Tbr132,33; AMPA-type glutamate receptors, Cnih2 and Cnih3; and a muscarinic cholinergic receptor, Chrm1. The network structure of Cluster #3 was robust in HD: 84 nodes were unchanged, only three, Ccdc106, Lhx6, and Smpd3, were lost, and two, Klf16 and Msantd1, were retained in HD (Fig. 5a, Supplementary References 25, 26, and 27 for Lhx2, Emx1, and Chrm1, respectively).

Lhx6 is a determinant of GABA-producing neurons in the striatum, such as MSNs, and parts of the cerebral cortex, and is a determinant of the GABA-mediated projection pathway in the subcortical regions of the embryo28–30. Moreover, several transcription factors combine with Lhx6 to form the pathway for glutamate-producing neurons that project their axons to MSNs. We constructed a network graph comprising nodes co-expressed with Lhx6 within the second degree in Cluster #3 and its neighbors (Fig. 5b). The graph contains nodes for genes of transcription factors functioning in the glutamate/GABA-mediated pathway formation in the embryo (Supplementary Table 5) as follows: Foxo6 functions in GABA-producing neuron specification during forebrain development34, and Tbr1 organizes the differentiation of cortical glutamate-producing projection neurons from striatal GABA-producing precursors and restrains areas of the projection neuron body to the lower-layer neocortex32,33. Nodes in the co-expression network graph that were normally conserved at 3 days, 7 days, and 3 weeks were maintained in the Cont brain at 5 weeks of age (Fig. 5c). Furthermore, the conserved co-expression of the genes, Lhx6/Tbr1/Foxo6/Foxg135/Nr2e1/Emx2, was observed in the co-expression network of Lhx6 in the 8-week-old adult normal mouse brain, as previously reported12. This conservation may be associated with shared molecular programs involved in embryonic development, neurogenesis, or postnatal neural plasticity.

Only Lhx6 was lost in the network graph of the HD brains at 5 weeks of age (Fig. 5d). Anatomical maps showed that Lhx6 was highly expressed in the ventral forebrain in Cont, but more diffusely in the forebrain in the HD brain, suggesting spatial dysregulation of Lhx6 expression in HD (Fig. 5e–h, Supplementary Fig. 2). Such dysregulated expression may be associated with abnormalities in glutamate/GABA signaling pathways.

Analysis of network hub genes

Network hub nodes with high betweenness centralities connected with clusters (Fig. 6a). Among them, a node for Fcho1, which fine-tunes synaptic vesicle endocytosis in an activity- dependent manner and may be associated with the molecular pathology of HD36, and a node for Fbxw537 connected parts of Clusters #1, #3, and #9 and their neighboring nodes, which we denoted as Sub-clusters #1, #3, and #9, respectively, as shown in the results of network propagation from the Fcho1 node (Fig. 6b). Nodes of genes related to the molecular pathology of HD (44 genes, p < 1.2 × 10− 6), synaptogenesis (18 genes, p < 4.0 × 10− 5), and mitochondrial dysfunction (14 genes, p < 6.8 × 10− 3) were abundant in this network (Fig. 6c; Table 2, Supplementary Reference numbers seen in Table 2: 11, 13, 28–38 for HD related, 39–56 for synaptogenesis, and 28, 57–67 for mitochondrial function). Most nodes (34 of 44) related to HD pathology, including Lhx6, were in Sub-cluster #3; 9 of 14 nodes related to mitochondrial dysfunction were in Sub-cluster #1, and among them, three were related to HD pathology. In the HD brain, two hub nodes, Fcho1 and Fbxw5, and 34% of the edges in the propagated network were lost, almost twice the average number of lost edges in the entire Htt-linked network. Correspondingly, the shortest path from Fbxw5 in Sub-cluster #1 to the nodes in Sub-cluster #3 via Fcho1 was no longer detected. This observation is consistent with the known molecular phenotype of HD, including cell death associated with mitochondrial dysfunction38. These findings may indicate a potential role of the two hub genes in linking genes in Sub-cluster #1, which were broadly associated with mitochondrial function, and those in Sub-cluster #3, which were selectively associated with neural formation. In HD, these network connections appeared to be altered, and the nodes of Lhx6, as described above, and Git1, an enhancer of Htt protein aggregation39, were no longer retained in the co-expression network with other functionally related genes.

The node for Smarca4 functioning in chromatin remodeling17 was a network hub connecting the nodes in Clusters #2/Htt-SETs and #9 (Fig. 6d). The propagation network of Smarca4 contained all nodes in Cluster #9. Smarca4 and 62 edges were lost in the HD network. The expression of mutant Htt appeared to be associated with altered co-expression between wild-type Htt and Smarca4, as described in Cluster #2/Htt-SET. Here, we further observed altered co-expression patterns between Smarca4 and genes associated with RNA quality control and cell fate determination.

Hub nodes in topological maps may play important roles in coordinating interactions among clusters. In this Htt-linked network, we identified at least two subnetworks; one involving Sub-clusters #1, #9, and #3 associated with Fcho1 and Fbxw5, and another involving Clusters #2/Htt-SETs and #9 associated with Smarca4. Although the two subnetworks intersected at Cluster #9, Fcho1 and Fbxw5 were not included in the propagation network of Smarca4 (Fig. 6d), and only 27 nodes (3%) were shared between the two propagation networks; these shared nodes were preserved in HD. These observations suggest that the alterations observed in the two subnetworks may represent relatively distinct topological and molecular features associated with mutant Htt expression.

Discussion

We found that the normally conserved co-expression network during postnatal stages was altered in association with mutant Htt expression. These alterations were observed in both the Htt-SET network and the whole-brain Htt-linked network (Fig. 3). In particular, genes associated with BDNF-related pathways in Htt-SETs/Cluster #2 (Fig. 2; Table 1), cerebral pattern formation in Cluster #3 (Fig. 5, Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 5), mitochondrial function in Sub-cluster #1 (Fig. 6b and c; Table 2), and cell fate decision in Cluster #9 (Fig. 6d and e) were exhibited alterations in co-expression patterns including loss or gain/retention of network connectivity. Among them, Smarca4, Lhx6, and Fcho1, emerged as candidate genes potentially associated with transcriptional alterations observed during the early stage of HD.

We identified Smarca4 as a candidate link of co-expression relationship between Htt and the activity-regulated Bdnf expression related to neuronal protection from glutamate excitotoxicity (Figs. 1b and 2). Smarca4 also emerged as a hub gene linking Clusters #2/Htt-SETs and #9 which included genes associated with cell fate regulation (Fig. 6d and e). In the HD brain, mutant Htt expression associated with altered co-expression patterns involving Smarca4 and wild-type Htt. These observations raise the possibility that Smarca4-related transcriptional networks, including those associated with chromatin remodeling and Bdnf regulation, may be altered in HD. However, the present findings do not directly explain the selective neuronal vulnerability observed in HD, because glutamate excitotoxicity may potentially affect multiple glutamate receptor-positive cell types beyond MSNs.

Furthermore, we identified Lhx6 as a candidate gene associated with neuronal loss in HD (Fig. 5). Lhx6 is a key developmental regulator implicated in the formation of glutamate-mediated cortico-striatal pathways to GABA-producing neurons, including MSNs, at the embryonic stage28. In the adult brain, Lhx6 is expressed and used as a marker gene for GABA-producing neurons5,40; however, its function after birth is partially understood. Consistent with a previous report12, we found that the co-expression network of Lhx6/Foxo6/Tbr1, which has been implicated in cortico-striatal pathway formation during embryonic development, remained conserved between postnatal day 3 and 8 weeks of age in the normal mouse brain. These findings suggest a potential association of this co-expression network with neural pathway formation or maintenance in the adult brain. We also observed that the co-expression of Lhx6 with other genes was diminished in the HD brain at 5 weeks of age.

Fcho1 connected Cluster #3, which included Lhx6, to Sub-cluster #1 associated with mitochondrial function, and this connection was less apparent in the HD brain at 5 weeks of age. Previous studies have reported that the mutant Htt protein affects mitochondrial function in multiple cell types38. Together, these observations suggest a potential association between mitochondrial dysfunction and alterations in the co-expression network involving Fcho1. In parallel, Lhx6 and other genes, including Git1, which has been implicated in Htt protein aggregation39, showed reduced co-expression with related genes at 5 weeks of age.

The neuroprotective effect of BDNF and the bioenergetic function of mitochondria are considered important targets of disease-modifying therapies for HD41. However, further studies are required to clarify how these broadly distributed pathways may address the spatiotemporally selective pathology of HD. Our findings raise the possibility that Smarca4, Lhx6, and Fcho1 may represent candidate molecules associated with neuroprotective and mitochondrial regulatory pathways in MSNs, warranting further investigation as potential therapeutic targets.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the anatomical resolution of the 3D expression maps was relatively low, limiting precise cell-type-specific interpretation of the observed spatial expression patterns and co-expression networks. In particular, the present approach does not directly resolve neuronal subtypes or pathway-specific cellular populations, and therefore inferences regarding MSNs and cortico-striatal/glutamatergic pathways should be interpreted cautiously. Second, although we provided 2-dimensional images of Lhx6 expression using in situ hybridization (ISH) in the Supplementary Fig. 2, systematic analysis of whole transcripts using whole-brain ISH or by independent approaches, such as RNA-seq and qPCR, remains limited. Third, the present study was primarily based on transcriptomic co-expression analysis, and functional validation by cell-based assays or mechanistic experiments was not performed. Therefore, the observed associations among Smarca4, Lhx6, Fcho1, and HD-related genes and their alterations should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistically causal. Future studies using cell-type-resolved transcriptomics and functional experimental approaches will be necessary to clarify the biological significance of these findings. Furthermore, limitation of this work is the use of single time point experimental data of HD mice. Further investigations using datasets from time points before and after 5 weeks could enhance our understanding of the progress of disease.

Recent HD transcriptomic resources, including HDinHD/HD Explorer42, provide large-scale RNA-seq and multi-omics datasets that may further support contextual interpretation of the present findings. Future integration with cell-type-resolved transcriptomic datasets may also help validate the observed co-expression relationships. Fine resolution datasets of ISH of whole brains at various ages are available in the Allen Brain Atlas43, which we used to validate our results9; however, comparable datasets from HD model animals are currently limited. Single cell resolution studies using deep RNA-seq reveal cell-based changes of transcripts in many types of neurons and glia, following dissection of areas of interest and annotation of cell types based on marker gene expression44. In contrast, TT methods provide a comprehensive overview of the whole brain through mechanical slicing, reducing potential regional sampling bias. In addition, stochastic co-expression analysis does not require prior assumptions regarding gene function or pathway relationships. Thus, despite the limitations of the present approach, our findings suggest that pathological changes in HD may be associated with alterations in some marker gene expression and co-expression patterns.

Because all datasets were generated using the same array platform, comparative co-expression analyses across developmental/adult stages and disease conditions may provide additional insights into HD-related transcriptional alterations. To facilitate future studies, all datasets containing more than 46,000 probe-based 3D expression maps and co-expression similarities from HD, Cont, and normal mouse brains across four developmental time points have been archived in the ViBrism DB and are publicly available for further analysis. Future expansion of TT-compatible datasets, including epigenetic and regulatory molecular profiles such as DNA methylation, chromatin modifications, and miRNAs, may further contribute to understanding molecular processes associated with disease progression. Furthermore, because TT can be applied to a wide range of biomolecules obtained from frozen tissues, archived datasets may also provide useful reference resources for comparative analyses of other neurological disease models.

Methods

R6/2 mice

HD model mice, R6/2 (males, body weight: 21.2–26.8 g, mean: 23.1 g), and the wild-type littermates as controls (males, body weight: 22.0–27.5 g, mean: 25.0 g), aged 5 weeks, were subjected to this study. R6/2 mice were obtained from The Jackson Laboratory, and R6/2 and wild-type littermates were generated by crossing ovarian-transplanted females and F1 wild-type males. Their genotypes were determined. CAG repeats were amplified through polymerase chain reaction (PCR) and sized via sequencing using standard methods7, and mice with 150–162 repeats (mean: 156) were selected as HD model mice to minimize molecular phenotype variations caused by repeat length differences45.

R6/2 mice show no HD symptoms for a few weeks after birth and then exhibit progressive symptoms. Using a neurorobotic approach, we observed a subtle, but notable coordination movement disorder in mice of the R6/2 colony at 5 weeks of age, particularly when the macroscopic anatomical abnormalities in the brain were not detected13. Thus, for molecular studies, we used mice aged 5 weeks as a model for the early stage of HD.

Brain samples were prepared following the protocol as described9. Briefly, R6/2 mice and wild-type littermates were first anesthetized with inhalational isoflurane (Viatris, Inc., Tokyo, Japan), and after reaching a deep anesthetic state, euthanasia was performed by continued inhalation of isoflurane. Immediately thereafter, the thoracic cavity was opened and transcardial perfusion with RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was performed. Brains were then dissected, embedded in Tissue-Tek OCT compound (Sakura, Japan) containing 0.005% toluidine blue, and rapidly frozen at − 80 °C.

TT methods

TT methods have been established9. The methodology schema can be found in the ViBrism DB < https://vibrism.neuroinf.jp/about.html%3E and seen in Supplementary Video 1 (and via the video “Mapping genes onto brain structures” on RIKEN channel-YouTube: https://youtu.be/Td4rGRQIZuY. Briefly, two data types of a whole mouse brain were obtained from a series of 1,000 micron-thick tissue fractions via sequential cross-sectioning (5 μm-thick cross-sectioning slices × 200 sections): (1) comprehensive expression density data of transcripts in the fractions and (2) each block-face image of the cross-sectioning planes. The experiments were performed using at least three brains, each processed in an orthogonal direction as a series. Expression density data were computationally assigned to the block-face image data. Image and expression density data from multiple series were mapped onto the coordinate space of the magnetic resonance (MR) images of the mouse brain (obtained from a different mouse than those specified above) using a volume rendering to create a dataset of 3D patterns of comprehensive expression measured in one virtual brain. For each transcript, expression density data of fractions were averaged in voxels of the virtual brain and a map with relatively high density was generated by applying an 80% cut-off density filter as described in our previous paper9.

In this study, 42 or 43 fractions produced using TT methods from five mice of each HD or Cont group, respectively, were used: three for orthogonal, and two for oblique sectioning directions. The expression density data in the fractions were measured using microarray probes for over 40,000 transcripts including coding, non-coding genes, and lncRNAs (SurePrint G3 Mouse GE 028005; Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Measured intensity values were per-chip normalized with GeneSpring GX software. Gene probes with at least one ‘present’ flag call were analyzed (40,635 in HD and 44,169 in Cont). Image and expression data of each HD or Cont group were reconstructed on 3D-MR images (Bruker Bio Spin AVANCE 400WBat 9.4 Tesla; Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) of an HD or Cont brain, respectively.

Datasets of gene expression

In the HD model mice both the wild-type mouse Htt and the mutant gene fragment of human Htt are expressed7; however, only the wild-type mouse gene could be quantified in this study owing to the design of the microarray probe that selectively hybridize with a 3′ portion of the wild-type mouse Htt but not with the human Htt.

New datasets (HD and Cont) were produced with the same experimental processes as previously described: 3D maps were validated by comparison with in situ hybridization image data of the adult mouse whole brain in Allen Brain Atlas42. Datasets of normal male mice (C57BL/6J) at three postnatal stages, 3 days, 7 days, and 3 weeks of age, were previously obtained with TT methods using the same microarray platform used in the present study10. Therefore, by adding the HD and Cont datasets, five datasets were used for subsequent probe-based network analyses. For TT methods for 8-week-old male C57BL/6L mice, a microarray platform from the previous version was used9; therefore, only gene-based network analyses were conducted.

Network graph formation and analysis

The Pearson correlation coefficient (r) of the normalized expression density data between two transcripts was calculated and used as a correlation measure of expression similarity. Stochastic co-expression network graphs, where nodes represent transcripts and two nodes with expression similarity over thresholds were linked with edges, were depicted with an edge-weighted spring-embedded layout or circular layout algorithms on the open-source software platform, Cytoscape ver. 3.9.146. The average r values in HD and Cont brains for each edge were used as the edge weights. Network propagation to detect 10% of all nodes with a shorter distance to a selected node was performed using the Core App: Diffusion in Cytoscape.

Using clusterMaker2 for Cytoscape 347, network clusters were extracted using the Markov-cluster algorithm, 25 clusters were selected based on the number of nodes > 12 in a cluster, cluster locations were denoted as circles on the Htt-linked network, and the averaged network of the clusters was depicted. Clusters were annotated with gene descriptive terms using AutoAnnotate ver1.3.548. The annotations were curated, and canonical pathways, molecular functions, and upstream regulators of the selected genes were analyzed using QIAGEN Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA)49.
