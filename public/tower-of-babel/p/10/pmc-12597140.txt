Evaluating the efficacy of Maternal Bovine Appeasing Substance (MBAS) administration on cortisol concentrations, animal behavior and welfare, and performance outcomes in beef-dairy calves during weaning
Authors: Rebecca A Bigelow, Eduarda M Bortoluzzi, Brad J White, Phillip A Lancaster, Alyssa Hobbs, Payton L Dahmer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWeaning is an unavoidable stressor for dairy and beef calves. Maternal bovine appeasing substance (MBAS) is a naturally secreted pheromone that induces an appeasing effect on nursing offspring. Our objective was to determine if a single topical dose of synthetic MBAS analog would lower cortisol concentrations, improve performance, and alter behavior outcomes of beef-dairy calves during weaning. Twenty-six individually housed male beef-dairy (Angus-Holstein cross) calves were blocked by weight (72 ± 6 kg) and randomized to either saline (CONT) or MBAS (MBAS; FerAppease, Fera D&B, College Station, TX). Calves received a single 10 mL dose of the designated treatment (5 mL on the poll, 5 mL on the muzzle) on day 0 prior to 3-d step-down weaning. Blood was collected on days 0, 1, 3, and 10. Calf starter intake and refusals were weighed and recorded daily. Body weights (BW) were collected every other day for 10 d following weaning. Average daily gain (ADG) and gain-to-feed (G:F) were calculated. Behavior was assessed using cameras, microphones, and accelerometers. Linear mixed models with effects of time, treatment and interaction were used to analyze cortisol, performance, and behavior variables with block and calf as random effects. Cortisol concentrations were significantly (P < 0.05) lower for CONT than MBAS. There were no treatment effects on BW or G:F between CONT and MBAS. Several treatment × time interactions (P < 0.05) were found for behaviors, with CONT having more lying bouts than MBAS on day 0. Additionally, CONT exhibited more nutritive oral behaviors (P = 0.01), defined as calf ingesting feed with their head over the feed bucket, post-treatment than MBAS. Vocalizations did not differ between treatments (P > 0.32) at any time during the trial. This research demonstrates that MBAS altered behavior during weaning, but it did not improve performance or blood cortisol compared to CONT.

INTRODUCTION

Calves in dairy and beef production systems go through many unavoidable stressors early in life including weaning (Hulbert and Moisá, 2016). Strategies for weaning beef calves have been well-documented in previous literature, but with increased use of beef semen to breed dairy cows, a greater number of beef-dairy calves are being produced and raised intensively (Boyles et al., 2007; Taylor et al., 2020; Weiker, 2024). These calves are often transported to a calf ranch where they are housed individually and introduced to calf starter very early in life prior to being weaned off milk, similarly to replacement heifer calves (Bigelow et al., 2025). The stress associated with these processes can negatively impact their affective states by activating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA) and the sympathetic nervous system, resulting in adrenocortical and acute-phase protein responses that can impact calf behavior, health, and performance (Carroll and Forsberg, 2007; Hulbert and Moisá, 2016).

Various weaning strategies, such as abrupt, fence line, nose-flap, etc. and their effects on health, performance, and stress response have been researched in beef and dairy calves (Boyles et al., 2007; Budzynska and Weary, 2008; Loberg et al., 2008; Taylor et al., 2020). An alternative to mitigating stress could include the use of appeasing pheromone analogs to elicit a calming effect on distressed animals (Liberles, 2014). The use of pheromones has been well-documented in swine, specifically during and following the weaning of piglets (McGlone and Anderson, 2002; Temple et al., 2016; Chasles et al., 2024). Additionally, previous trials have shown mixed effects of MBAS in different cattle production settings, such as in pre-weaned dairy heifers, weaning and pre-conditioning of beef calves, and prior to slaughter of finishing steers (Angeli et al., 2020; Schubach et al., 2020b; Bringhenti et al., 2023; Kvamme et al., 2024; Mackey et al., 2024). Several studies have evaluated calf behavior at time of weaning comparing MBAS to a placebo, typically saline; however, these studies were conducted using Bos indicus calves that were at least 6 mo of age that were weaned from a pasture setting (Schubach et al., 2020b; Vieira et al., 2023; Santos et al., 2025).

Maternal bovine appeasing substance (MBAS) is a pheromone naturally secreted by the sebaceous gland located on the skin of the mammary gland (Liberles, 2014). As the calf nurses, the pheromone stimulates the vomeronasal gland and desensitizes the amygdala and hypothalamus, preventing activation of the HPA axis, in order to decrease threat perception and elicit a calming effect on the offspring (Liberles, 2014). The synthetic analog of MBAS is based on a mixture of fatty acids to reproduce the composition of the naturally-secreted pheromone as described on the product label (FerAppease Bovine 300 mL, FERA Diagnostics and Biologicals, College Station, TX). This technology has gained interest from producers in the cattle industry, and to the authors’ knowledge minimal research has been conducted to investigate the effects of MBAS on reducing the stress associated with weaning of individually housed calves in a calf ranch setting.

It was hypothesized that calves dosed with MBAS would have decreased cortisol concentrations, spend more time lying down, vocalize less, present fewer non-nutritive oral behaviors (NNOB), and would have improved performance and health outcomes with greater body weight (BW), average daily gain (ADG), gain to feed (G:F) compared to control calves receiving saline. The research objective was to determine if a single topical dose of MBAS at the start of step-down weaning would decrease cortisol concentrations in blood, produce changes in behavior, and improve performance outcomes of beef-dairy male calves during and following the weaning process.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

All animal care and procedures were approved by the Kansas State University (KSU) Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee before study initiation (IACUC protocol: IACUC-4946). This trial was conducted at the KSU Sheep and Meat Goat Center (Manhattan, KS) from November to December 2023.

Experimental Design

Twenty-six pre-weaned castrated male beef-dairy calves (58 ± 4.8 d of age) from a single source in Kansas were used in this trial. Calves were individually weighed 4 d before the start of the trial. A complete randomized block design was used for this trial where calves were randomly allocated within weight block to treatment: similar color saline solution (Sterile Saline Solution—Saline Solution 0.9%, Vet One, MWI, Boise ID), and blue food coloring (Food Colors Assorted Neon, Kroger); CONT, n = 13) or maternal bovine appeasing substance (MBAS; FerAppease Bovine 300 mL, Fera Diagnostics and Biologicals, College Station, TX, n = 13). A study collaborator that did not participate in data collection or data analysis allocated calves to treatment to maintain blinding of calf caretakers and researchers collecting data. Randomization was accomplished using a random number generator (Microsoft Excel, Microsoft Corp.). Treatments were applied by an unblinded study collaborator, that did not participate in data collection or data analysis. A topical dose of the designated treatment was applied to the nuchal skin behind the poll (5 mL), and to the skin above the muzzle (5 mL) using a proprietary applicator as indicated per the manufacturer’s recommendation.

Animal Feeding Management

Calves were housed in elevated individual pens in a naturally ventilated barn with two open doors and continuous airflow through the building. Wooden partitions were placed along all four sides of each pen to prevent nose-to-nose contact between calves. Calves were weighed every other day starting on day 0. Prior to the 3-d step-down weaning process, calves received 1.85 liters of milk replacer (Land O Lakes® Herd Maker® Calf Milk Replacer, Arden Hills, MN) twice daily (6:30 a.m. and 5:00 p.m.), calf starter with 89% dry matter (Calf Grower B-68 16% Protein, Mid Kansas Coop, Manhattan, KS) at 1.02 kgs of feed per 45.36 kg of body weight (BW) once daily, and water ad libitum. Calves did not receive any type of roughage, such as hay, at any point in the study. On day 0, the calves only received the morning milk bottle and were not offered the evening milk bottle (removal of the second bottle). On this same day, CONT and MBAS treatment application took place at 0800 hours. Milk feeding remained the same on days 1 and 2. On day 3, calves were completely weaned off milk and were offered dry feed (calf starter) only. The amount of calf starter was adjusted based upon BW and fed once daily on days 3 through 9 at 1.02 kg/45.36 kg of BW. On day 9, calf starter was fed at 1.13 kg/45.36 kg of BW, and the total fed amount was split evenly between morning and evening offerings. On day 11, calf starter was increased to 1.25 kg/45.36 kg of BW. Calves were fed and weighed on the morning of day 13 prior to study conclusion. Interim ADG and interim G:F were calculated using BW and feed intake. Interim ADG was calculated to get ADG for every 2-d increment that BW was collected. Similarly, interim G:F was calculated for every 2-d increment between BW collections.

Blood Parameters Collection

Approximately 10 mL of blood was collected via jugular venipuncture from each calf at the following timepoints: 1, 8-, 12-, and 24-hours following treatment application, day 3 at 0800 hours, and 4-, 8-, 12-, and 24-hours following, and day 10 at 0800 hours. Blood samples were labeled with a unique identifier, mixed by inverting the tube (Greiner Bio-One VACUETTE™ Lithium Heparin Blood Collection Tube, Greiner Bio-One North America Inc., Monroe, NC, USA) immediately following collection, placed on ice and transported to the lab. Blood samples were centrifuged at 1000 g for 15 min, and serum was pipetted and stored in cryovials at −80 °C until cortisol enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays were performed as described by Mancke et al. (2025). Intra-assay variability was 9.78%, and inter-assay variability was 5.25%.

Behavior Data Collection

Video cameras (Camera Bullet 4 Megapixel Motorized 2.8 to 12 mm lens, Geovision, Taiwan) and microphones (Verifact® A Microphone, Louroe Electronics, Van Nuys, CA) were mounted on a wooden framework above the pens on a 45-degree angle to continuously record video and audio 24/7 for the duration of the study. A predetermined timeframe of 30 min after each intervention was used (milk bottle feeding, milk bottle removal/milk bottle not offered, and application of treatment) according to continuous recording approach described by Dawkins (2007) and similar to Vessier et al (2002). Therefore, subsets of 30-min videos were rendered for behavior scoring 30 min after morning (AM videos) and evening (PM videos) milk feeding on the day prior to weaning (day −1), day 0, and day 3. Behavior was evaluated within the same temporal window to avoid confounding effect of natural diurnal variation in calf activity. This approach was to ensure that any detected differences were attributed to the weaning event and treatments rather than inherent differences in behavior between morning and afternoon periods. Videos were also rendered to cover 30 min after time of treatment application (PT videos) on day 0. Calves were allowed 15 min of rest after human contact before videos were rendered to minimize behavior changes associated with human presence. Two trained observers that were blinded to treatment analyzed videos. To measure reliability between the two observers, the functions “icc” and “kappa2” from the “irr” package in RStudio were used to calculate the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and Kappa statistic, yielding an ICC of 1, indicating excellent reliability, and a Kappa of 0.66, indicating substantial agreement. An ethogram was created to describe behaviors evaluated (Table 1). Behaviors of interest included lying or standing, nutritive oral behavior (NOB), non-nutritive oral behavior (NNOB; Vessier et al., 2002, 2013), grooming, play behavior, vocalizing, non-vocalizing, and residing spatially close to the bottle holder or distanced from the bottle holder. Triaxial accelerometers (HOBO Pendant G Data Logger, Bourne, MA) were secured to the left lateral aspect of the right hind leg using a small amount of tag cement (Kamar Adhesive) and veterinary bandaging tape (AmerisourceBergen, Conshohocken, PA) prior to treatment. Accelerometers were removed and replaced on day 6 to ensure continuous data collection. At this time, the new accelerometers were secured to the opposite medial aspect of the rear leg. The axis position was converted into a binomial variable of standing and lying based on a published protocol (UBC AWP, 2013). Data were summarized as total time lying and standing per day, number of standing and lying bouts indicating change in position from standing to lying or vice versa, and duration of standing and lying per bout. A bout was defined as the act of lying or standing for longer than 60 s. Accelerometer variables were summarized in 24 hour increments for analysis.

STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Data Transformation

All data were analyzed using RStudio (Version 2022.02.3, Boston, MA). Histograms and Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to assess normality of all outcome variables using the “hist” function from “ggplot2” package and “shapiro.test” function of “stats” package. Due to lack of normality of cortisol and behavioral data, outcome variables were transformed by multiple methods. The final method of transformation was selected by using Shapiro-Wilk tests to determine which method was best-fitting. There were 3 models used to assess video behavior for different timepoints: 30 min post-treatment (PT), 30 min following AM milk feeding (AM), and 30 min following PM milk feeding (PM). Blood cortisol concentration, percentage of video time lying for AM and PM, percentage of NOB for AM and post-treatment (PT), percentage of NNOB for PT, percentage of time spent grooming for PT, percentage of time spent close to the bottle holder for PT, count of play behavior for AM and PM, and count of vocalizing for AM all required log-transformation (log10 + 1) for normalization prior to analysis. Count of vocalizations and grooming for the PM timepoint were square-root (square-root + 1) transformed to achieve normalization before analysis. Accelerometer data was filtered for removal of incomplete data collection due to accelerometer malfunction where increments with less than or equal to 1400 min and greater than 1441 min in total time recorded were removed for each calf prior to analysis. If data within a 24-hour increment was incomplete, that 24-hour increment was removed from the analysis to maintain data integrity and prevent any misrepresentation or skewing of the results. The total number of lying bouts required a transformation for severely skewed data which was accomplished with the following equation: ″Severe″lie bouts= 110+total # of lie bouts. Transformed data is reported as estimated marginal means and standard errors that were back-transformed, with p-values from model-associated transformed data. The remaining outcome variables did not require transformation prior to analysis.

Performance Models

For performance variables of body weight, interim ADG, and interim ADG, linear mixed effects models were used to analyze outcomes of interest using “lmer” function of ‘lme4’ package of R. Fixed effects of treatment, time, and treatment × time were included in each model. Weight block and calf ID were included as random effects to account for repeated measures over time. Statistical analysis of the blood cortisol concentrations was accomplished using a linear mixed effects model with the fixed effects of treatment, time, and treatment × time and random effect of calf ID and weight block.

Accelerometer Models

For analysis of accelerometer data, the total time lying in minutes per day, total time standing in minutes per day, total number of standing bouts per day, total number of lying bouts per day, average length of a standing bout, and average length of a lying bout were analyzed using linear mixed effects models with fixed effects of time, treatment, and time × treatment. Weight block and calf ID were included as random effects.

Camera Behavior Models

As mentioned previously, 3 models were used to analyze behavior: post-treatment (PT model) on day 0, behavior following morning milk feedings on days −1, 0, and 3 (AM model), and behavior following evening milk feedings on days −1, 0, and 3 (PM model). The variables assessed in each model included percentage of time with head still, lying, standing, performing NOB and NNOB, grooming, time spent distanced from the bottle holder, and time spent close to the bottle holder. Counts of play behavior, vocalization, and grooming were also analyzed. For the PT models, a linear mixed model was used and included the fixed effect of treatment and weight block was included as a random effect. For the AM and PM models, a linear mixed effects model was used with fixed effects of treatment, time, and treatment × time and calf ID and weight block were included as random effects. Estimated marginal means were adjusted using Tukey’s method. Significance was established at p ≤ 0.05.

RESULTS

Performance Results

When analyzing BW, there was no treatment difference (P = 0.45) between CONT and MBAS calves. There was a significant timepoint effect (P ≤ 0.01), with BW increasing over time from day 0 to 13. For interim ADG, a treatment effect (P = 0.02) and timepoint effect (P < 0.001), but no treatment × timepoint interaction (P = 0.47) were identified. The CONT calves had a greater interim ADG of 0.47 kgs/day compared to 0.40 of the MBAS calves. For the timepoint effect, interim ADG increased over time from day 0 to 13 (Figure 1). Interim G:F conversion was not different among treatments (P > 0.05), but there was a significant timepoint effect (P < 0.01) where G:F increased from day 0 to day 13. Blood Cortisol Results

A treatment effect (P < 0.05) was found for blood cortisol concentrations. There was also a significant timepoint effect (P < 0.05) on cortisol concentrations; however, there was no treatment × timepoint interaction detected (P > 0.05). Calves in the CONT treatment group had lower overall cortisol concentrations than MBAS calves (2,578 ± 207 pg/mL versus 3,064 ± 246 pg/mL, respectively). On day 3 at 1200 hours and 1600 hours, cortisol concentrations were highest, measuring 6,637 pg/mL and 5,938 pg/mL, respectively, and were significantly higher than cortisol measured at any time on day 0, day 1, at 2000 hours on day 3, and day 10.

Accelerometer Behavior Results

For automated stand/lie behavior collected via accelerometer, no treatment effect was found for total time spent lying or standing in minutes per day. There was a significant time effect on total time spent lying and standing in minutes for all calves (P < 0.001) where calves spent more time lying on days 1 and 2 than the start (day 0) and end (day 4) of the step-down weaning process. The total time standing during step-down weaning was the inverse of the lying time. There was a significant treatment × time interaction (P = 0.04) for the total number of lying bouts. The CONT calves tended to (P = 0.06) have a greater number of lie bouts on day 0, the first day of step-down weaning, and significantly (P = 0.02) had a greater number of lie bouts on day 2 (22.0 ± 0.2) compared to MBAS (15.7 ± 0.2) (Figure 2). For total number of standing bouts, there was no treatment effect or treatment × time interaction, but there was a significant timepoint effect (P < 0.001). The highest number of stand bouts were seen on day 0 and day 3 with an average of 20.2 ± 0.7 and 19 ± 0.7 bouts respectively, compared to the lowest number of stand bouts of 12.1 ± 0.7 on day 9. Similarly, there was a timepoint effect (P < 0.001) on the average length of each standing bout but no treatment or treatment × time effects (P ≥ 0.4). During the step-down weaning process on days 0 through 3, each stand bout had a decreased average length compared to the post-weaning period including days 4, 5, 8, 9, and 10. For the average length of each lying bout, there was a significant time effect (P < 0.001) and a treatment × time trend (P = 0.058). The average length of each lying bout was lower on day 0 compared to days 4 through 13. For the interaction trend, CONT calves had shorter lie bouts than MBAS calves on day 2 (50.3 min versus 68.8 min, respectively), then CONT had longer lie bouts than MBAS calves on day 12 (75.2 min versus 61.0 min, respectively). Estimated marginal means for each logger outcome variable can be seen in Table 3.

AM Camera Behavior Results

For AM and PM models, day −1 was the baseline from the day prior to treatment application, day 0 was the first day of step-down weaning as well as treatment application, and day 3 was the final day of step-down weaning i.e., the first day calves received no milk. In the AM models, no treatment, timepoint, or treatment × timepoint effects (P > 0.05) were found for the percentage of time residing close or distanced from the bottle holder, time spent standing or performing NNOB. A timepoint effect (P < 0.05) was found for percentage of time with head still, performing NOB, and count of vocalizations. The percentage of time with head still was greater on day 3 (68.1% ± 4.67%) than day −1 (45.0% ± 4.65%) or 0 (49.2% ± 4.65%). The percentage of time performing NOB was greater on day −1 (4.30% ± 1.27%) compared to day 3 (1.76% ± 0.66%). The count of vocalizations was at least 10 times higher on day 3 (45.21 ± 9.32) compared to day −1 (3.16 ± 0.83) and day 0 (4.52 ± 1.12). For count of grooming behaviors, there was a treatment, timepoint, and treatment × timepoint effect (P ≤ 0.04). The count of grooming for MBAS calves did not change over time, but the count for CONT calves increased from day 0 to day 3 (2.77 ± 0.96 versus 8.23 ± 0.96, respectively). Grooming behavior was greater for MBAS (7.69 ± 0.96) compared to CONT on day 0 (2.77 ± 0.96) (Figure 3).

PM Camera Behavior Results

For the PM models, no treatment, timepoint, or treatment × timepoint effects (P > 0.05) were found for the percentage of time spent grooming. There was a significant timepoint effect (P ≤ 0.02) for percentage of time lying, time spent close or distanced from the bottle holder, standing, performing NOB and NNOB, and count of vocalizations and grooming. The percentage of time spent lying increased from day −1 (11.7% ± 1.59%) to day 3 (20.9% ± 2.73%). The percentage of time spent distanced from the bottle holder decreased from day −1 (34.5% ± 3.36%) to day 3 (10.6% ± 3.34%). In contrast, time spent near the bottle holder increased from day −1 (65.5% ± 3.36%) to day 3 (89.4% ± 3.34%). The percentage of time spent standing decreased from day −1 to day 3 (96.3% compared to 79.1% respectively). The percentage of time performing NNOB decreased from day −1 (17.9% ± 1.52%) to day 0 (4.78% ± 1.51%) and day 3 (3.43% ± 1.51%). Conversely, the percentage of time performing NOB increased from day −1 (14.4% ± 3.97%) to day 0 (38.8% ± 3.94%) and day 3 (45.2% ± 3.94%). Counts of vocalizations decreased from day −1 to day 3 with 15.9 counts compared to 6.02, respectively. The counts of grooming decreased from day −1 with a count of 6.74 compared to day 3 with 3.34. There was a treatment effect (P < 0.001) and treatment × timepoint effect (P = 0.004) for count of play behavior. The CONT calves showed no play behavior compared to the MBAS calves who had increased counts of play behavior during the AM period on day −1 (0.53 ± 0.11) compared to day 0 (0.05 ± 0.08). DISCUSSION

The present findings demonstrated no treatment differences of MBAS vs CONT on BW and G:F conversion in beef-dairy calves for 10 d post-weaning. In contrast, previous studies demonstrated increased BW when comparing control treatment to MBAS. Angeli et al. (2020) compared performance measures between individually housed pre-weaned dairy heifers that received a placebo treatment (bovine appeasing substance (BAS) carrier, diethylene glycol monoethyl ether; n = 70) to calves that received bovine appeasing substance (BAS; IRSEA Group, Quartier Salignon, France; n = 70). In comparison to the present data, Angeli et al. (2020) had a longer period for step-down weaning, 3 d vs 31, respectively, MBAS was applied 5 times throughout the weaning process, and ADG was measured at 14-d intervals for 70 d vs 2-d intervals for 13 d in the present data. It was reported that calves receiving BAS had greater BW at weaning as well as increased ADG from days 42 to 56 and tended to be higher from day 56 to weaning on day 70 compared to the calves who received the placebo (Angeli et al., 2020).

Bringhenti et al. (2023) also conducted a study using individually-housed, pre-weaned dairy heifer calves that showed an increase in BW for MBAS-treated calves compared to a control group 7 d after calves were moved from the individual pen to group pens. Other studies have shown differences in BW between a control and MBAS treatment in group settings, such as application prior to weaning calves on pasture, prior to arrival at the feedlot, and prior to shipping for harvest (Cooke et al., 2020; Fonseca et al., 2021; Mackey et al., 2024). While there was no difference in final BW in the shipping trial, BW 45 d post-treatment was higher for calves who received MBAS at weaning or those who received MBAS prior to transport to the feedlot (Cooke et al., 2020; Fonseca et al., 2021; Mackey et al., 2024). Conversely, there have been studies that support the present findings in which no difference in BW was found between control and MBAS calves (Schubach et al., 2020a, 2020b; Kvamme et al., 2024). Similarly, for ADG there have been conflicting reports of significant treatment or treatment × timepoint effects on ADG, as well as, reports of no differences found in ADG between calves receiving MBAS and those who did not (Angeli et al., 2020; Cooke et al., 2020; Schubach et al., 2020a; Fonseca et al., 2021). The present findings showed a treatment effect on interim ADG with increased ADG for CONT calves at 0.47 kgs/day compared to 0.40 kgs/day for the MBAS group.

In previous literature, only one study demonstrated a difference in overall feed efficiency where Fonseca et al. reported that when applied prior to transport, calves that received MBAS compared to those that did not, had greater overall G:F (Fonseca et al., 2021). The remaining literature where G:F was reported showed no differences between control and MBAS groups and supports the present data; however, those studies calculated feed efficiency over a 40 to 42 d period (Schubach et al., 2020a, 2020b; Kvamme et al., 2024).

Previous literature evaluated cortisol concentration in the plasma of weaned calves in the feedlot and found no differences between CONT and MBAS treatment groups (Cooke et al., 2020; Schubach et al., 2020a, 2020b; Fonseca et al., 2021; Kvamme et al., 2024). This differs from the present data as overall cortisol concentrations were found to be lower for CONT calves compared to MBAS, measuring 2,578 ± 207 pg/mL versus 3,064 ± 246 pg/mL, respectively. Kvamme et al. reported cortisol levels ranging from 1,400 pg/mL to 3,100 pg/mL over a 42-d pre-conditioning period; however, these were reported values for post-weaned older calves than the population in the present study. It is important to recognize that the cited research differs from the present as it was performed in older animals housed in group settings prior to transportation to a feedlot or to pasture, and the present data evaluated individually housed calves experiencing the stress of weaning. It’s also important to note that cortisol is released during distress and eustress, and it is difficult to differentiate between the two. Thus, while researchers can measure cortisol levels in the plasma, there’s no way to tell whether it’s a response to distress, what some consider “bad stress,” and eustress, considered “good stress.”

Accelerometers have been used in previous literature to determine patterns and monitor changes in behavior in dairy and beef cattle (Robert et al., 2009; Bonk et al., 2013). To date, only one trial was found where physical activity was assessed between groups of calves who received MBAS or a placebo. However, Schubach et al. used pedometers to assess steps/day only whereas the present data used triaxial accelerometers to assess lying/standing time and bouts (Schubach et al., 2020b). In the present data, a treatment × timepoint interaction was identified for the total number of lying bouts. The CONT calves tended to be more active than MBAS calves on day 0, the first day of step-down weaning, and day 2, the middle of step-down weaning, as they were getting up and lying down more frequently compared to 5 d post-weaning and beyond. Overvest et al. (2018) examined how social housing influences the lying behavior of calves by comparing lying time and lying bouts between individually housed calves and calves housed in pairs throughout and following the weaning process (Overvest et al., 2018). Their results noted that lying bouts and lying time decreased by day during the weaning and postweaning period. This alteration in lying behavior was attributed to the calves adapting their feeding habits to cope with receiving less and then no milk (Overvest et al., 2018). The present data shows a similar trend over time with the total number of lying bouts decreasing for CONT and MBAS calves from the first day of the step-down weaning to post-weaning. Numerically, the CONT calves had a greater number of lie bouts until day 10 when lie bouts for the MBAS calves were greater, but the differences after the step-down weaning period were not statistically significant. Another weaning trial investigating two weaning methods, gradual weaning versus abrupt weaning, used automatic activity sensors that measured standing and lying bouts, time standing and lying, and duration of bouts (Scoley et al., 2019). That study found that abruptly weaned calves displayed a reduced number of lying bouts as well as decreased lying time in the post-weaning phase when compared to gradually weaned calves.

In the present data, there was a timepoint effect for total time lying, total time standing, total number of standing bouts, and the average length of each lying or standing bout. The calves had a greater number of standing bouts shorter in average length during step-down weaning compared to days post-weaning. This data demonstrates that the calves were standing up and lying down more frequently for shorter amounts of time throughout the weaning process than 5 to 7 d post-weaning. It is likely the more frequent bouts of shorter lengths could represent distress of some sort in the form of restlessness, and as the calves adapted to not receiving milk during the post-weaning phase, their behaviors were altered as well which has been shown in previous literature (Thomas et al., 2001; De Paula Vieira et al., 2008).

There have been many anecdotal testimonies about the use of MBAS to reduce stress in calves through changes in behavior such as showing signs of relaxation. In this trial, behaviors were evaluated after application of MBAS or saline to determine if there was a statistically significant behavioral change between the treatments. The CONT calves spent a greater percentage of their time performing NOB than MBAS calves. This means the CONT calves spent more time with their head over the bucket ingesting feed. Schubach et al. (2020b) reported the mean proportion of calves per pen feeding was greater for calves that received MBAS compared to a control group (Schubach et al., 2020b). This is a slightly different behavior sample method than the present data, where the proportion of calves ingesting feed was measured (scan sampling) rather than the percentage of time (duration over a set period of time). However, the two findings are conflicting where Schubach et al. (2020b) report increased feeding proportion in the MBAS group, and the results from the current trial report increased NOB in the CONT group. It is important to note the differences between these trials; the present data was collected on 42-to-56-d old beef-on-dairy calves raised in individual pens at and following weaning, while Schubach et al. (2020b) used 233-d old beef calves housed in group pens after weaning in a 42-d pre-conditioning period. The observations in Schubach’s trial also were not taken immediately following treatment application. To date, the authors have not found other literature evaluating the effects of MBAS on calf behavior.

There was a timepoint effect for the percentage of time with head still, performing NOB, and count of vocalizations for the period following AM feeding. The increase in vocalization over time was expected, however, there was no difference found between treatment groups. This data supports findings of previous literature where calves showed strong vocal responses to weaning with increased counts of vocalization following weaning (Budzynska and Weary, 2008; Jasper et al., 2008; Bittar et al., 2020). In the present data, percentage of time spent performing NOB decreased over time, meaning the calves spent less time with their head over the starter bucket ingesting feed after the step-down weaning process compared to the day before weaning. Bittar et. al. reported that calves that were gradually weaned compared to abrupt weaning spent less time eating starter during the week following weaning, then increased as they got older (Bittar et al., 2020). Grooming behavior did not change over time for MBAS calves, nevertheless grooming increased from day 0 to day 3 for CONT calves. The CONT group also groomed significantly less than the MBAS calves on day 0. This difference could be due to the oily consistency of the MBAS product versus the watery saline. Eckert et al. (2015) conducted a weaning trial with Holstein calves comparing weaning at 6 wk of age versus 8 wk, and it was reported that grooming tended to decrease during the step-down weaning period (Eckert et al., 2015). It is important to note the present research was conducted using beef-on-dairy calves, and the period where behaviors were evaluated was shorter than the period used by Eckert et al., (2015), 4 d compared to 4 wk respectively.

For behavior evaluations after PM feedings, the percentage of time spent close to the bottle holder increased from day −1 to day 3, which corresponds to the day prior to weaning and the first day calves did not receive any milk. The evaluations took place 30 min following when PM feeding normally took place, so residing close to the bottle holder is most likely attributed to the calves expecting and waiting for a milk bottle. The percentage of time lying increased from day −1 to day 3 while the percentage of time standing decreased. This shows a shift in behavior from standing on their feet to lying down more often throughout and following the weaning process. The percentage of time performing NNOB decreased over time from day −1 to day 3. This data is supported by reports from Veissier et al. (2013) who reported a decrease in NNOB after weaning compared to before weaning in both of their treatment groups, calves reared with their dam and calves artificially reared drinking milk from a bottle (Veissier et al., 2013). In contrast, the present data showed an increase in NOB from day −1 to 3. This could be explained as increased ingestion of starter to attain lack of calories previously provided by milk offered. Count of vocalizations decreased following PM feedings from day −1 to day 3. This may be due to the calves acclimating to not receiving milk in the evenings. As they adjusted to expecting but not receiving milk at night, their vocalizations decreased by the end of the step-down weaning process. Count of play behavior was the only behavior outcome in the PM evaluation that had a significant treatment × timepoint interaction. The CONT calves did not display any play behavior throughout the period evaluated, and the MBAS calves displayed more play behavior on day −1 prior to weaning than on day 0 which was the first day of the step-down weaning as well as day of treatment application, however, this could be due to variation between calves. Vázquez Diosdado et al. (2024) reported that compared to pre-weaned calves, total play time significantly increased during step-down weaning and when weaning was completed, which was not reflected in the present data. However, there were differences in breed and sample size as Vázquez Diosdado et al. followed 3 cohorts of 15 Holstein calves for up to 18 wk and the present data evaluated behavior of 26 beef-on-dairy calves over a 4-d period.

LIMITATIONS

A limitation of this research includes the short duration of the study as well as the small sample size used. Calf performance outcomes were measured and calculated over a total period of 14 d, and behavior was assessed over the 4-d period from pre-weaning, through the step-down process, to post-weaning. Another limitation includes the volatilization of the MBAS product, and although partitions were placed between pens, the product could have spread in the ambient air inside the barn. Further research is warranted to investigate how the application of MBAS would alter performance, blood cortisol concentration, and behavior in a large-scale setting more similar to calf-raising facilities found in the industry.

CONCLUSION

In summary, the administration of MBAS prior to step-down weaning did not lower blood cortisol concentration or improve performance outcomes of body weight or G:F in the present study. Interim ADG was increased for CONT compared to MBAS calves. Behavior as measured by accelerometers and video recordings was altered from pre-weaning through the step-down process and post-weaning. For behaviors evaluated post-treatment application, the CONT calves spent more time performing NOB compared to MBAS calves. Further research is needed to evaluate the effects of MBAS on a larger-scale operation that is more similar to the calf ranches where beef-dairy calves are typically raised. However, this research provides novel findings regarding the alteration of behaviors in beef-dairy calves during and following weaning when housed individually as there is little to no literature about this population.
