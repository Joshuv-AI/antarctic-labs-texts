Beyond Data: Artificial intelligence, knowledge graphs, and the next revolution in wheat breeding
Authors: Xiaoming Xie, Peng Zhao, Yuqi Zhang, Wenxi Wang, Zihao Wang, Zhaoxing Yu, Zhe Chen, Baoyue Zhang, Mingming Xin, Zhongfu Ni, Qixin Sun, Weilong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs a cornerstone of global food security, wheat (Triticum aestivum) faces unprecedented pressure from a growing population and a changing climate. However, traditional breeding approaches are increasingly insufficient to address the genetic complexity required to achieve substantial gains in yield and resilience. This review highlights key advances in the generation of large-scale, standardized datasets through the integration of high-throughput genotyping and multidimensional phenotyping. We explore how multi-omics integration and knowledge graph-based frameworks transform heterogeneous data into actionable breeding knowledge. In addition, we examine the pivotal role of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning in enhancing predictive modeling, refining genomic selection, and enabling intelligent decision-making. These advances underpin the emerging paradigm of Breeding 5.0, which leverages data-driven innovation and closed-loop iterative cycles. Looking ahead, multimodal AI and personalized breeding strategies will be critical for developing sustainable systems capable of ensuring global food security under climate change.Key words: wheat, data-driven agriculture, knowledge graphs, smart breeding, Breeding 5.0

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum), a hexaploid cereal crop with a complex genome, plays a central role in global food security, providing approximately 20% of the world’s dietary calories and protein (Shiferaw et al., 2013). Global wheat production has increased substantially, from approximately 220 million tons in the 1960s to 798 million tons in 2023. However, driven by rapid population growth and environmental change, demand for wheat continues to rise. To meet projected food demands by the 2050s, wheat production must increase by at least 60% (Ray et al., 2013). While the monumental achievements of the Green Revolution and the subsequent era of molecular breeding have laid the foundation for modern agriculture (Peng et al., 1999), traditional breeding approaches alone are insufficient to deliver such gains in yield potential. Progress is further constrained by a confluence of challenges, including slowing yield improvements, intensifying climate pressures, diminishing natural resources, and the inherent genetic complexity of key adaptive traits—particularly root system architecture, stomatal regulation, and reproductive viability—required to sustain yields under abiotic stress (Benitez-Alfonso et al., 2023). For a crop as vital and genetically complex as hexaploid wheat, overcoming these limitations requires a fundamental shift from incremental improvement toward a more holistic and predictive framework. This evolution is giving rise to smart breeding, a discipline emerging from the powerful convergence of high-throughput biotechnologies, computational science, and artificial intelligence (AI). This modern paradigm transforms crop improvement from an empirical art into a data-driven science. The central challenge has shifted from data generation to the ability to translate vast, heterogeneous datasets—including genomics, phenomics, and enviromics—into actionable knowledge. In this review, we address this central challenge by proposing a forward-looking strategy for genomic design breeding. We focus on how the deep integration of multi-omics data, AI, and precision technologies, such as gene editing, can bridge the gap between genomic discoveries and practical applications in breeding. We outline the foundational pillars of this approach, including scalable data acquisition and integration, the construction of sophisticated knowledge models, and the deployment of AI-powered predictive strategies. Ultimately, we propose a data-driven, intelligent breeding framework capable of designing resilient, high-yield, and high-quality wheat varieties to meet future global demands, while also addressing critical challenges in data standardization, model interpretability, and the seamless integration of breeding pipelines.

A New paradigm in breeding driven by genomics and AI

The advent of smart breeding marks a paradigm shift into the post-omics era, characterized by the deep integration of multi-omics, big data, and AI (Xu et al., 2022). At its core, this approach places data science and computational biology at the forefront, enabling a transition from observation-based to prediction-driven crop improvement through the construction of comprehensive genotype-to-phenotype models. This data-centric paradigm transforms plant breeding into a highly predictive discipline that requires close multidisciplinary collaboration (Lell et al., 2025). It not only addresses the complexities of polyploid genomes, such as that of wheat, but also enhances predictive accuracy by integrating AI-driven analyses with biological knowledge, thereby paving the way for a new era of efficient and precise crop breeding (Zhang et al., 2025a).

From traditional to genomics-driven breeding

To fully appreciate the significance of this paradigm shift, it is essential to trace the historical evolution of crop improvement. Over 10 000 years ago, humans began domesticating and selecting crop species based on visible traits. In the early 20th century, the discovery of Mendel’s laws of inheritance laid the foundation for scientific plant breeding, enabling systematic field experimental design, controlled hybridization, and statistical analyses for the selection of specific agronomic traits. Approximately 30 years ago, the advent of molecular marker technologies allowed breeders to begin dissecting the genetic basis of complex traits, marking the emergence of molecular breeding (Wallace et al., 2018). However, most traditional selection methods have relied heavily on breeders’ experience and phenotypic observations, often conducted without a clear understanding of the underlying genetic architecture (Gesteiro et al., 2023). Although these approaches have produced numerous elite cultivars, they are inherently time-consuming. Moreover, while concerns regarding genetic erosion persist, recent comprehensive analyses suggest that well-managed breeding programs can maintain long-term genetic diversity through dynamic germplasm exchange and selection (Voss-Fels et al., 2019). Breeders have traditionally addressed genotype × environment (G × E) interactions through extensive multi-environment trials, successfully developing elite cultivars with broad adaptability, such as the globally renowned spring barley ‘RGT Planet’. However, achieving such stability through phenotypic selection alone remains resource-intensive and time-consuming. To overcome these bottlenecks, a paradigm shift toward smart breeding is now underway, driven by transformative advances in genomics and data science.

The release of the high-quality reference genome of the bread wheat cultivar “Chinese Spring” in 2018 (IWGSC, 2018), together with subsequent improvements—including near-complete (Wang et al., 2025a) and telomere-to-telomere (Liu et al., 2025) assemblies—has provided comprehensive genomic maps for gene mining and quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping. With advances in sequencing technologies and declining costs, multiple additional high-quality reference genomes have been generated, including those of 15 globally diverse accessions (Walkowiak et al., 2020) and 17 representative Chinese cultivars (Jiao et al., 2025). As a result, wheat genomic research has entered the pangenome era, enabling a more comprehensive understanding of genetic diversity within and across wheat populations. Concurrently, the integration of large-scale genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic, and phenotypic datasets has driven a transition from traditional multi-omics studies to a more comprehensive pan-omics framework (Yao et al., 2025a). These resources facilitate the identification of favorable genes and haplotypes with potential breeding applications, thereby providing valuable genetic resources for wheat improvement. To fully leverage these extensive datasets, a new conceptual framework—smart breeding (or Breeding 5.0)—is required to translate raw data into actionable breeding decisions.

Powering predictive breeding through data integration and AI

With the rapid accumulation of genotypic and phenotypic data, together with advances in statistical modeling and algorithm development, breeders are increasingly able to link genotype to phenotype (Li et al., 2022b), identify genetic loci associated with target traits (Lell et al., 2024), and predict phenotypic performance based on genomic data (Ansari et al., 2025). Integrating these data-driven approaches with speed breeding technologies to accelerate generation turnover (Watson et al., 2018) enables breeders to rapidly design optimal parental combinations (Wang et al., 2023b) and precisely develop high-yielding, stress-tolerant varieties tailored to specific environments (Molero et al., 2023). Nevertheless, significant challenges remain. The acquisition and analysis of large-scale genotypic and phenotypic datasets require substantial labor and computational resources. Moreover, the accuracy of current predictive models is constrained by data quality, trait complexity, and environmental interactions. Although high-throughput sequencing has streamlined genotyping and high-throughput phenotyping (HTP) platforms are helping to alleviate data scarcity, the precise characterization of complex physiological traits remains challenging. Consequently, the bottleneck in modern breeding has shifted from raw data generation to the quality of data processing and the biological interpretation of these large-scale datasets.

To meet these emerging challenges, breeding is increasingly driven by an interdisciplinary fusion in which rigorous fieldwork is augmented by data science. Advanced machine learning (ML) algorithms are used to process vast multi-omics datasets (Zhang et al., 2025b), complementing rather than replacing traditional field trials. This technological convergence has been shown to significantly enhance selection outcomes. For instance, in maize drought tolerance breeding, genomic selection (GS) achieved a genetic gain of 0.124 t/ha/year, compared with 0.067 t/ha/year achieved through phenotypic selection (Vivek et al., 2017). Furthermore, prediction models for complex traits, including grain yield and maturity, have consistently achieved high accuracies ranging from 0.5 to 0.7 (Crossa et al., 2016), demonstrating the reliability of these data-driven approaches. By integrating extensive multi-omics data—including transcriptomic, proteomic, phosphoproteomic, and acetylproteomic layers—researchers can construct mechanistic systems biology models that elucidate complex agronomic traits. For instance, a recent multi-omics atlas of common wheat identified a critical post-translational modification module, revealing that the deacetylase TaHDA9 regulates TaP5CS1 to modulate proline content. This mechanistic insight provides a direct target for enhancing resistance to Fusarium crown rot, a trait previously difficult to dissect due to genome complexity (Zhang et al., 2025b). Such an interpretable framework offers the resolution required for breeders to transition from black-box phenotypic prediction to mechanism-driven breeding. By integrating comprehensive genetic profiles, breeders can make more informed decisions and identify specific functional modules necessary for developing climate-resilient crops (Wang et al., 2025b).

AI-assisted breeding decisions highlight the transformative value of computational biology by overcoming the limitations of linear statistical models. For instance, the newly developed MegaLMM framework enables the simultaneous integration of thousands of high-throughput phenotypes, significantly enhancing genetic value prediction beyond standard multivariate limits (Runcie et al., 2021). Empirical studies in wheat further demonstrate that such multi-trait modeling, when properly evaluated, improves the precision of prediction accuracy estimates by 5.8%–14% compared with uncorrected metrics (Montesinos-López et al., 2021). Moreover, applying ML algorithms with feature selection facilitates the capture of complex nonlinear G × E interactions, markedly reducing computational time while identifying superior allelic combinations (Gabur et al., 2022). These computational advances provide specific targets for precision gene-editing technologies, representing a direct and powerful outcome of this interdisciplinary synergy and enabling the validation of computational predictions through targeted genetic modifications. For example, Li et al. generated the Tamlo-R32 allele by introducing a targeted deletion of 304 kb in the TaMLO-B1 locus, which confers robust powdery mildew resistance without the growth penalties typically associated with mlo mutations (Li et al., 2022a). Beyond simple gene knockouts, the capacity for complex structural engineering has also advanced substantially. A newly developed dual-prime-editing system enables the precise inversion of large DNA fragments (up to 205.4 kb) in wheat, with efficiencies reaching 51.5% (Zhao et al., 2025). This ability to manipulate both specific loci and large chromosomal segments facilitates the development of designer wheat varieties while preserving the integrity of elite genetic backgrounds, marking a clear transition from random mutagenesis to a directed, high-precision breeding approach.

Embedding large-scale data into a predictive framework for wheat breeding

The transition from empirical to smart breeding relies on a predictive framework built upon three core capabilities. First, data acquisition has advanced from sparse molecular markers to multidimensional datasets, including whole-genome sequencing, transcriptomics, and drone-based HTP. This expansion necessitates the second component, data integration, which addresses heterogeneity by harmonizing genotypic data with complex environmental covariates (envirotyping) into standardized, machine-readable formats. These structured datasets provide the foundation for the final component, computational processing, in which statistical genetics and ML algorithms convert raw data into actionable outputs—such as genomic estimated breeding values and optimized mating designs—thereby enabling selection decisions that account for complex G × E interactions.

The era of high-throughput genotyping and phenomics

The era of data-driven breeding is characterized by the exponential growth of genotypic data, marking a shift from general diversity surveys to large-scale, high-resolution quantification (Figure 1A). For instance, the “Seeds of Discovery” initiative utilized DArTseq to characterize 79,191 wheat accessions, including 56,342 hexaploid and 18,946 tetraploid lines, generating a comprehensive diversity map based on more than 300,000 markers (Sansaloni et al., 2020). Deep resequencing has further enhanced this resolution; a recent analysis of 827 Watkins landraces and 208 modern cultivars revealed that modern wheat originates from only two of seven ancestral groups (Cheng et al., 2024). However, SNP-based approaches inherently miss a substantial proportion of structural variations. To address this limitation, pangenome frameworks have been developed to capture the full genomic landscape. The 10+ Wheat Genome Project released high-quality assemblies for 15 global varieties, enabling the characterization of complex introgressions and chromosomal rearrangements associated with stress-resistance loci such as Sm1 (Walkowiak et al., 2020). Similarly, high-quality assemblies of 17 representative Chinese cultivars identified 249,976 structural variations, nearly half (49%) of which exceed 5 kb in length. These large variants, often absent from the reference genome, drive key evolutionary adaptations, such as the VRN-A1 duplications responsible for the differentiation between spring and winter wheat types (Jiao et al., 2025).

Advanced models, such as deep learning networks, are then applied to predict phenotypes from genotypes, ultimately integrating both into a comprehensive, large-scale genotypic (G) and phenotypic (P) dataset that underpins modern breeding strategies.

(C) Key challenges hindering effective data integration. These include economic trade-offs between experimental scale and cost, statistical issues such as model overfitting in limited datasets, the exponential growth of data and its associated computational demands, and organizational barriers such as data silos that impede collaborative analysis.

Beyond cataloging individual variants, understanding their inheritance as functional units is the next logical step in deciphering the genetic architecture of complex traits (Figure 1A). Increasing evidence suggests that populations evolve through shifts in the frequencies of entire genomic blocks, or haplotypes, rather than at individual loci (Shipilina et al., 2023). The construction and application of haplotype maps have therefore emerged as powerful approaches that directly link genetic variation to functional outcomes. For instance, pangenome-scale assemblies provide a high-resolution framework for defining haplotype blocks and have been instrumental in characterizing key loci, such as the Sm1 insect resistance gene (Brinton et al., 2020; Walkowiak et al., 2020). Furthermore, these genomic resources serve as essential reference panels for high-density genotype imputation, enabling precise dissection of polygenic architecture and pleiotropic effects in complex multi-parent populations (Scott et al., 2021). By exploring haplotype diversity within historical collections, researchers can also uncover novel alleles for germplasm improvement (Cheng et al., 2024), reinforcing the central role of haplotype analysis in modern genetic dissection.

While genomic data provide the genetic blueprint, phenotyping remains the bottleneck in capturing realized agronomic performance (Zavafer et al., 2023). The field is currently undergoing a transition from static measurements to dynamic, multidimensional monitoring, characterized by distinct technical trade-offs. High-throughput automated platforms (e.g., gantry-based systems equipped with hyperspectral and 3D sensors) offer unmatched temporal resolution for tracking individual plant growth (Virlet et al., 2016; Sadeghi-Tehran et al., 2017) but are often constrained by high costs and the artificiality of controlled environments. In contrast, unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) have emerged as the preferred solution for field-scale breeding. While they may sacrifice the sub-millimeter resolution of ground-based systems, UAVs provide the throughput necessary to phenotype canopy traits across thousands of plots under realistic environmental conditions (Zarco-Tejada et al., 2013; Garzonio et al., 2017; Hassan et al., 2019). A similar trade-off is observed in root phenotyping, where the transition from labor-intensive excavation to non-destructive imaging techniques—such as computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging—has enabled the visualization of complex root architectures, albeit at lower throughput than canopy-based approaches (Yazdanbakhsh and Fisahn, 2009; Iyer-Pascuzzi et al., 2010; Garbout et al., 2012; Nagel et al., 2012; Mairhofer et al., 2015; Schmittgen et al., 2015). Finally, an important emerging trend is the democratization of phenotyping technologies; unlike expensive proprietary platforms, low-cost, mobile-based tools (Estrada et al., 2025) are helping bridge the gap between high-tech research and practical deployment in resource-limited breeding programs.

Laying the data foundation for computational breeding

The acquisition of massive, heterogeneous datasets from genomics and phenomics creates a new and formidable challenge: data integration (Figure 1B). Historically, these data streams have existed in isolated “silos,” making it nearly impossible to draw holistic conclusions about the complex interplay among genotype, environment, and phenotype (G × E). To be suitable for advanced analytics, this raw biological information must be transformed into a standardized, machine-readable format. On the genomic side, this requires the systematic digitalization and formal representation of all functional elements. This process involves more than simply cataloging sequences; it requires rich annotation to map gene functions and link variants to their potential biological impacts (Wang et al., 2022; Yang et al., 2022; Niu et al., 2024). It also extends to identifying complex regulatory networks and their component sequences (Chen et al., 2023; Xie et al., 2023) and must incorporate the roles of dynamic features such as transposable elements and epigenetic marks. This comprehensive digital mapping transforms static biological features into a dynamic, queryable architecture that serves as a critical substrate for AI.

In parallel, the torrent of high-dimensional phenotypic data demands a similar effort in standardization to ensure interoperability and reusability. Standardized vocabularies and ontologies, such as the Crop Ontology and environmental ontologies, are indispensable in this context. By providing a controlled, hierarchical language to describe traits, these frameworks ensure that data from different experiments can be meaningfully aligned and rendered machine-readable (Salim et al., 2022). The community has coalesced around the findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable principles, as well as the minimum information about a plant phenotyping experiment standard, which now serve as the backbone of modern repositories and prevent data from becoming “digital archaeology” (Röckel et al., 2022; Papoutsoglou et al., 2023). This structured framework forms the bedrock of multi-omics analysis, enabling researchers to connect specific gene variants to transcriptomic expression and metabolic outputs within unified platforms such as PLANTdataHUB (Weil et al., 2023). By developing common database frameworks and breeding application programming interfaces (APIs), the community is building the technical scaffolding for automated data sharing (Xu et al., 2022). Ultimately, creating this unified, computable language is a foundational step toward bridging raw biological information and intelligent prediction. It effectively creates a “biological digital twin,” facilitating powerful in silico modeling of genotype–environment–phenotype relationships (Karabulut et al., 2024).

Integration tools that leverage HTP data markedly enhance both genome-wide association study power and GS accuracy, often improving trait prediction by 30%–70% compared with genotype-only approaches (Persa et al., 2021; Kaushal et al., 2024). These tools, ranging from longitudinal mixed models to multi-dimensional multi-omics frameworks, excel at exploiting dense, dynamic HTP traits to explicitly model complex G × E interactions and enhance biomarker discovery (Moreira et al., 2020). However, the success of such integration is limited by the upstream phenotyping bottleneck, including high platform costs, stringent sensor calibration requirements, and logistical complexities in data standardization (Xiao et al., 2022). Furthermore, advanced statistical and ML models, although powerful, carry a substantial risk of overfitting, require considerable computational resources, and often suffer from limited interpretability, thereby complicating the mechanistic understanding of genetic relationships (Moreira et al., 2020; Pérez-Valencia et al., 2022). Critically, the benefits of dense genotyping are largely negated when the corresponding phenotypic data lack quality or standardization.

Strategic balancing of data depth and scale in modern breeding

As modern breeding moves beyond the foundational challenges of data acquisition and integration, a new strategic imperative emerges: balancing the trade-off between data depth and scale (Figure 1C). This marks a significant paradigm shift from traditional small-sample, high-dimensional studies toward a more optimized model that leverages large-scale, cost-efficient data acquisition.

Achieving the required data richness often involves confronting the economic and biological constraints that shape this trade-off. Pursuing extreme data depth—such as high sequencing depth in polyploids, the integration of multi-omics (e.g., transcriptomics and metabolomics), or high-resolution phenotyping—rapidly increases per-line costs and analytical complexity (Gemenet et al., 2020; Weckwerth et al., 2020). Consequently, although multi-omics approaches can capture biological plasticity and improve the prediction of complex traits, the high cost often restricts their application to smaller, intensively characterized training panels rather than entire breeding populations (Araus et al., 2018). Furthermore, in many GS applications, prediction accuracy reaches a plateau after a few thousand informative SNPs, indicating diminishing returns from maximizing genotyping depth for routine selection (Gemenet et al., 2020).

Conversely, expanding data scale also faces limitations. While larger training sets in genomic prediction initially increase predictive ability, gains often plateau once several thousand diverse genotypes or years are included (Fernández-González et al., 2024; Lell et al., 2025). A crucial challenge is that simply adding more data is insufficient; incorporating low-heritability or poorly curated data—a common risk in large, multi-program data-sharing efforts—can introduce substantial noise and significantly reduce accuracy, while also demanding extensive curation and imputation (Ballén-Taborda et al., 2022; Fernández-González et al., 2024). This recurring pattern highlights that a limited set of high-quality, information-rich samples often outperforms large volumes of redundant or low-quality data, confirming that a strategic focus on quality can substitute for brute quantity (Li and Chao, 2021).

Technological innovation lies at the core of this transition, providing the tools to rebalance the trade-off between depth and breadth. Advances in next-generation sequencing and bioinformatics are progressively lowering the cost of high-throughput genotyping, making large-scale germplasm analysis more accessible (Wang et al., 2024). To harness these vast and heterogeneous datasets, the paradigm is increasingly shifting toward data-driven strategies, where ML is no longer a theoretical possibility but a practical tool. ML models are now widely deployed to rapidly and precisely dissect complex phenotypic traits. For instance, Li et al. applied the Faster R-CNN algorithm to quantify spike numbers in wheat fields, achieving an average accuracy of 86.7% and identifying stable QTLs (e.g., QSnyz.caas-7DS) that were missed by manual counting (Li et al., 2022c). Similarly, Sun et al. developed “StomataTracker” to analyze video data, which not only accelerated trait extraction (from 207 to 1.47 s) but also revealed novel circadian rhythms in stomatal closure behavior (Sun et al., 2023). Ultimately, resolving the “depth versus scale” dilemma lies in this cost-effective synergy, where deep mechanistic insights and broad population-level discovery continuously reinforce one another.

From data to knowledge: Construction and application of wheat breeding knowledge graphs

The rapid accumulation of large-scale multi-omics datasets—including genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, and phenomics—has greatly enhanced our ability to investigate the genetic basis of complex traits in wheat (Yao et al., 2025a). However, data heterogeneity remains a major challenge for systematic interpretation (Subramanian et al., 2020). Effective integration and structured representation of multi-omics information—such as through the construction of knowledge graphs—are therefore essential to transform fragmented data into actionable insights for wheat breeding.

Unlocking valuable information via integrated omics analysis

Diverse datasets and multi-omics data are integrated through systematic preprocessing and standardization, coupled with automated data integration pipelines (Figure 2A). Initial data cleaning involves noise reduction, sequencing error correction, and imputation of missing values to improve data quality and reliability. To ensure multi-omics compatibility, gene identifiers are harmonized to unified reference IDs, variant coordinates are mapped to consistent genome versions, and phenotypic data are standardized using controlled frameworks. Following preprocessing and normalization, biologically meaningful features can be extracted. For instance, resequencing data enable the identification of SNPs, haplotypes, and high-confidence copy-number variations. Integrating transcriptomic profiles with genomic variants facilitates expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) analyses, linking sequence variation to transcriptional regulation, while co-expression network analysis (e.g., weighted gene co-expression network analysis) identifies regulatory modules associated with agronomic traits. Moreover, the integration of multi-environment phenotypic data with genomic markers enables the detection of environment-specific QTLs and the dissection of G × E interactions (Figure 2B). Through these coordinated preprocessing, standardization, and analytical workflows, diverse datasets—including omics data, literature-derived functional annotations, and breeder-recorded pedigree information—are transformed into interoperable, functionally annotated knowledge units. This structured integration not only supports the identification of favorable alleles and superior haplotypes but also provides a mechanistic, data-driven foundation for predictive wheat breeding.

Wheat breeding knowledge graphs: A framework for intelligent discovery

The knowledge graph represents this crucial evolution, transforming a static knowledge base into a dynamic reasoning engine poised to accelerate modern intelligent breeding. In this framework, agronomic traits and known genes that regulate these traits are represented as “nodes” (Figure 2C). It explicitly maps the complex, multi-layered relationships among them as “edges,” integrating multi-omics data, phenotypic information, and literature-derived knowledge into a vast, interconnected network (Figure 2C). By formalizing these relationships, knowledge graphs not only consolidate fragmented data but also create a system in which information can be inferred, rather than merely retrieved. This provides researchers with a comprehensive systems-level perspective, rendering complex biological data more intuitively accessible and substantially enhancing data utilization efficiency.

In terms of knowledge organization, wheat breeding knowledge graphs transcend the limitations of traditional single-source data storage by enabling hierarchical and networked structures across multiple dimensions, including genes and their regulatory interactions, functional elements, molecular mechanisms, and phenotypic traits. Users can explore specific genes to trace upstream and downstream regulatory pathways and associated traits or perform multi-level pathway analysis and functional mining based on regulatory networks. Such multidimensional structures support complex queries that cater to diverse applications, ranging from fundamental research to applied breeding decisions.

The wheat breeding field is undergoing a significant transition in knowledge paradigms, shifting from phase 3.0/4.0 to phase 5.0 (Zhu et al., 2025). Earlier stages relied primarily on large-scale data accumulation and conventional experimental validation, with knowledge existing as fragmented pieces or empirical summaries. In phase 5.0, breeding knowledge graphs standardize, digitize, and systematize historical data, genetic insights, and established gene–trait relationships to build machine-interpretable and inferable knowledge systems. Through the deep integration of AI and ML technologies, these knowledge graphs not only automate knowledge extraction and updating but also enable intelligent reasoning to generate breeding strategies.

Integrating multi-omics data and knowledge graphs for data-driven smart breeding in wheat

Traditional breeding (empirical selection) has played a pivotal role in crop improvement. Breeders develop new varieties by selecting materials based on phenotypic performance, often in the absence of detailed genetic background information. However, this strategy relies heavily on breeder experience and is time-consuming and generally inefficient (Alemu et al., 2024). The integration of multi-omics data and knowledge graphs provides a foundation for predictive breeding design and more efficient decision-making in genetic improvement (Xu et al., 2022).

Breeding design driven by predictive models

Predictive models form the foundation of modern breeding design. Statistical, ML, and deep learning (DL) methods are increasingly employed to uncover associations between genetic variants and phenotypic traits, enabling the development of predictive frameworks that support breeding decisions. Early approaches such as genomic linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP) (Sorensen and Kennedy, 1984) and ridge regression best linear unbiased prediction (rrBLUP) (Endelman, 2011) relied on linear models to associate genotypic variation with phenotypic traits and estimate breeding values for individual crop varieties or elite lines. Despite their widespread use, linear models are largely limited to capturing linear and additive genetic effects (Cuevas et al., 2017) and are therefore less effective for analyzing high-dimensional data and modeling complex, multivariate relationships between predictors and responses (Meuwissen et al., 2001). To address these limitations, ML methods (such as support vector machines, random forests, and LightGBM) have been introduced into genomic prediction. For example, CropGBM, which uses LightGBM as its core algorithm, improves prediction accuracy by approximately 15% compared with linear models while maintaining strong computational efficiency and model stability (Yan et al., 2021). However, many traditional ML approaches rely on careful feature engineering and representation design, and their performance may decline in the presence of noisy or redundant predictors (Zhu et al., 2025). In contrast, DL models can learn hierarchical feature representations directly from raw or minimally processed inputs and have demonstrated strong predictive performance in complex, high-dimensional settings. For instance, DNNGP, which employs a deep convolutional neural network to predict phenotypes from genotypes, achieves substantially higher prediction accuracy on large wheat datasets compared with GBLUP, LightGBM, and support vector regression (Wang et al., 2023a). In addition, Cropformer—an interpretable DL framework for crop genomic prediction—combines convolutional neural networks with multiple self-attention mechanisms to predict complex phenotypic traits and further improves prediction accuracy by up to 7.5% relative to other approaches (Wang et al., 2025b).

Although DL- and ML-based models can significantly improve prediction accuracy, they still face several challenges (Li et al., 2025). One major limitation is their strong dependence on large training datasets. For example, when the sample size increased from 100 to 2000 individuals, prediction accuracy for 1000-kernel weight in wheat improved by approximately 54.0%, highlighting the positive relationship between population size and predictive performance (Wang et al., 2023a). In addition, environmental factors have a significant influence on crop growth and development, and incorporating G × E interactions into predictive models can substantially enhance accuracy (Cuevas et al., 2017). Models that account for G × E interactions achieve approximately a 23% improvement in predicting environment-specific performance compared with GBLUP-based models (Gogna et al., 2026). The ongoing accumulation of large-scale, high-quality genotypic and multi-environment phenotypic datasets will substantially enhance the accuracy and robustness of predictive models in future breeding programs, particularly for complex traits and environment-sensitive phenotypes. Predictive models are therefore expected to transition from methodological exploration to routine decision-support tools in breeding programs.

Precision breeding design using knowledge graphs

High-quality breeding knowledge graphs serve as comprehensive repositories for crop improvement. Integrating these knowledge graphs with predictive models supports breeding design under complex, multi-objective demands (Figure 3). A critical first step in this process is the clear definition of breeding targets, such as developing new wheat varieties with high yield and superior quality. Based on these targets, previously reported favorable genes and QTLs can be retrieved and prioritized from breeding knowledge graphs to guide the selection of optimal parental combinations for breeding programs. By integrating large-scale genotypic data with multi-environment phenotypic data, novel genes and QTLs associated with target traits can be identified, and their performance can be evaluated under specific environmental conditions. Leveraging information extracted from knowledge graphs and biological big data, DL-based AI models that incorporate G × E interactions can integrate available genes, QTLs, and parental lines, enabling the prediction of progeny performance for target traits under defined environments and supporting the rational design of breeding programs. In addition to hybridization-based approaches, gene-editing strategies (Li et al., 2025) can also be developed for specific genes and traits. After program design, a comprehensive evaluation is conducted based on implementation feasibility, time requirements, and expected varietal performance, providing breeders with references for selecting appropriate breeding strategies. Moreover, breeders can iteratively refine these programs by contributing new insights, thereby further enhancing the performance of predictive breeding models.

This framework is based on defined breeding targets, involving the selection of favorable genes from the knowledge graph, the use of artificial intelligence for breeding design, the scoring of proposed breeding programs, and the selection of optimal strategies by breeders based on these scores.

Integration of breeding knowledge graphs, biological big data, and predictive models establishes a systematic framework for breeding design. This framework supports the rational design of hybridization and gene-editing strategies while enabling iterative optimization through breeder feedback and continuous data accumulation. It is expected to accelerate breeding efficiency, enhance prediction accuracy under complex multi-environment and multi-trait conditions, and ultimately facilitate the development of varieties with stable and superior performance.

Future prospects of the wheat intelligent breeding ecosystem

The convergence of AI with multi-modal data is setting the stage for a transformative shift in wheat breeding. The ultimate goal is to construct a sustainable, self-evolving intelligent ecosystem (Figure 4). This system can be conceptualized as a multi-layered architecture comprising a foundational predictive engine, a sophisticated knowledge-and-decision engine, and an overarching framework that ensures continuous and iterative improvement.

A framework for the AI-driven intelligent wheat breeding ecosystem.

The diagram outlines a hierarchical framework for AI-empowered wheat breeding. The process begins at the top data layer, where AI models integrate multi-source data, including high-throughput phenotyping (HTP), to perform predictive tasks such as genomic selection. This information then feeds into the central knowledge and tool layer, where a knowledge graph and multimodal large language models (MLLMs) synthesize data from databases and literature to enable autonomous tool orchestration. At the bottom decision layer, a synergistic loop is formed between the breeder and AI agents: the breeder sets targets and provides validation, while AI agents propose innovative breeding designs, facilitating a rapid design–iteration cycle. The entire framework functions as a self-improving system, transforming raw data into actionable knowledge and intelligent breeding strategies.

An AI-powered predictive framework for smart breeding

At the core of the smart breeding ecosystem lies a powerful predictive engine designed to decipher the complex interplay among genotype (G), phenotype (P), and environment (E). While early architectures such as DNNGP (Wang et al., 2023a) and Cropformer (Wang et al., 2025b) established the quantitative superiority of DL in raw predictive accuracy, the current focus has shifted toward systemic integration. This shift involves moving beyond single-modality genotypic data to incorporate high-dimensional soil, meteorological, and multi-omics datasets. Frameworks such as DeepG2P (Sharma et al., 2022) and GEFormer (Yao et al., 2025b) exemplify this trend by leveraging attention mechanisms to model environmental variables. However, the dual extraction of features from genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics remains a computational frontier, requiring approaches such as dual-extraction modeling to automate the integration of multi-source biological information for precise trait discovery (Ren et al., 2024).

The rapid integration of complex AI/ML models, particularly DL architectures, introduces several immediate and critical hurdles for their reliable deployment in breeding practice, including model interpretability, generalizability, data bias, and computational cost. Limited interpretability remains a major constraint, as the black-box nature of DL hinders the identification of causal mechanisms. Furthermore, poor generalizability restricts model applicability, as performance often declines sharply (domain shift) when models are applied to novel environments or different genetic backgrounds due to shifts in linkage disequilibrium structure or unobserved G × E interactions. Achieving robust cross-environment performance therefore requires specialized strategies such as domain adaptation, transfer learning, or more robust G × E modeling. Compounding these issues, data bias frequently arises from non-random sampling, where phenotyping efforts are skewed toward elite lines or limited environments, leading to overfitting and potentially biased genetic selection outcomes. Addressing this issue requires balanced sampling designs and bias-aware evaluation methodologies. Finally, for resource-constrained breeding programs, the high computational and data demands of DL models often necessitate a practical trade-off: simpler, cost-effective statistical approaches (e.g., MLM/GBLUP) or strategies such as selective phenotyping may provide comparable, operationally sufficient accuracy for additive traits (Akdemir and Isidro-Sánchez, 2019), while avoiding the substantial infrastructure requirements of DL (Fritsche-Neto et al., 2021). Consequently, future framework design must balance interpretability, generalizability, data fairness, and computational efficiency alongside predictive accuracy.

To address the limitations of single-modality analysis, emerging multimodal large language models (MLLMs) enable the unified processing of textual, visual, and environmental data (Huang et al., 2024). A representative example is the wheat breeding large model, which integrates UAV-based remote sensing and phenomics data to deliver accurate yield prediction and decision support (Yang et al., 2025a). Future advancements will depend on the continued accumulation of high-quality heterogeneous data to further refine these intelligent decision-making systems.

Knowledge and decision systems

Prediction alone is insufficient; data must be translated into actionable knowledge to guide breeding decisions. This is the role of the knowledge and decision engine, in which LLMs and MLLMs function as the central nervous system. These models serve as intelligent interfaces, bridging the gap between vast, siloed datasets and human experts. A representative example is SeedLLM, an LLM specifically developed for rice biology that integrates millions of publications with a comprehensive rice biological knowledge graph to provide precise decision support for trait analysis and cultivation strategies (Yang et al., 2025b). This vision culminates in the development of AI agents capable of autonomously orchestrating complex breeding workflows. Such an approach is exemplified by emerging frameworks such as SciToolAgent, which uses a knowledge-graph-driven agent to integrate and chain hundreds of scientific tools (Ding et al., 2025). Powered by a specialized knowledge graph that maps the functions and interdependencies of diverse analytical tools, these agents can interpret a breeder’s high-level strategic goals and automatically design and execute the required multi-step analyses, linking prediction models, bioinformatics software, and databases to generate novel and actionable breeding strategies.

However, the transition from research prototypes to reliable autonomous assistants faces significant practical challenges. First, the lack of standardized APIs across the fragmented landscape of breeding and bioinformatics software hinders seamless integration (Langer et al., 2025). Agents often struggle to reliably invoke tools that were not designed for automated interaction, highlighting the need for unified community standards for tool interoperability, similar to those in multi-agent systems. Second, robustness in error handling is critical; in complex, multi-step workflows, an agent must not only execute tasks but also identify, diagnose, and recover from intermediate failures without hallucinating results (Acharya et al., 2025). Consequently, human oversight remains indispensable. At the current stage, a “human-in-the-loop” approach is essential to verify the agent’s reasoning processes and validate the biological feasibility of its outputs, ensuring that AI autonomy complements—rather than replaces—expert judgment (Retzlaff et al., 2024).

In the future, such models will enable breeders to automatically access multi-source information and generate standardized visual reports in real time through natural language instructions or field video clips. Furthermore, using plant photographs, they could rapidly perform cultivar identification and trait prediction. Within the ecosystem (Figure 4), these MLLMs function as “AI agents” that receive high-level goals from breeders, autonomously orchestrate analytical tools, and generate novel breeding designs. This creates a powerful human–AI collaborative loop of design, verification, and iteration that substantially accelerates innovation.

From concept to reality: Building an intelligent breeding ecosystem

Ideally, an autonomous breeding ecosystem operates as a digital engine in which AI models continuously refine breeding decisions through an iterative cycle of genomic, phenomic, and environmental data (Zhang et al., 2025a; Sangjan et al., 2025). However, a significant gap remains between this vision and current practice. Most innovations remain in the prototype phase due to high costs and poor integration into routine workflows. The key challenge is to transition from fragmented, researcher-centric tools to a unified, breeder-friendly infrastructure capable of handling the complexity of hexaploid wheat without requiring breeders to have coding expertise (Reynolds et al., 2020; Morales et al., 2022).

To illustrate a practical application of this ecosystem, consider a “precision drought tolerance breeding” scenario. In a fully realized system, a breeder uploads UAV-captured multispectral imagery from a trial field. The platform, powered by MLLMs, automatically aligns visual stress indicators with soil moisture sensor data, cross-references these phenotypes with genomic data to identify QTLs associated with drought tolerance in the specific environment, and subsequently recommends optimal parental lines for the next crossing cycle. This automated workflow replaces weeks of manual field scoring and fragmented data analysis with a streamlined process completed in hours.

Implementing such a system, however, necessitates substantial investment beyond standard breeding budgets. It requires high-performance hardware, including computing clusters (GPUs) for model training and PB-scale cloud storage to integrate massive multi-omics datasets. On the software side, essential components include unified APIs (e.g., breeding APIs) to ensure interoperability among heterogeneous datasets (Selby et al., 2025), as well as low-code user interfaces that improve accessibility for non-technical users. Furthermore, success depends heavily on human capital, particularly the formation of interdisciplinary teams comprising breeders, data engineers, and AI specialists to develop and maintain this complex digital infrastructure.

Despite these high initial resource requirements, this ecosystem offers measurable value that justifies the investment. Automated, noninvasive phenotyping enables faster selection for complex traits across generations, thereby shortening the time required to advance germplasm through breeding programs and reducing labor-intensive field validation costs (Watt et al., 2020). Furthermore, by facilitating precise adaptation to local microclimates, it supports a more sustainable response to climate change, helping secure genetic gains where traditional empirical methods may fall short.
