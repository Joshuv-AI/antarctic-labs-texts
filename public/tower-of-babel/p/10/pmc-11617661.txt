Proteolysis Targeting Chimeras (PROTACs) in Breast Cancer Therapy
Authors: Yerim Jin, Yeongju Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBreast cancer (BC) accounts for 30 % of cancer cases among women cancer patients globally, indicating the urgent need for the development of selective therapies targeting BCs. Recently, proteolysis‐targeting chimera (PROTAC) has emerged as a promising strategy to target breast cancer. PROTAC is a chimeric molecule consisting of a target protein ligand, an E3 ligase ligand, and conjugating linkers, enabling it to facilitate the degradation of desired target proteins by recruiting E3 ligase in close proximity. Due to the catalytic behavior and direct degradation of BC‐causing proteins, PROTAC could achieve high drug efficacy with low doses, drawing great attention for its potential as therapeutics. This review provides cases of the currently developed PROTACs targeting BCs depending on the type of BCs, limitations, and future perspectives of PROTAC in targeting BCs.Keywords: PROTAC, Targeted protein degradation, Breast cancer, HER2, ERα

Introduction

Endogenous Protein Degradation with the Ubiquitin Proteasome System

The Ubiquitin Proteasome System (UPS) plays a crucial role in regulating protein degradation and controlling cellular processes. [1] The process of protein degradation is facilitated by the polyubiquitination of substrate proteins at lysine residues. This involves multiple steps of enzymatic reactions associated with E1, E2, and E3 enzymes, resulting in the tagging of substrate proteins with ubiquitin protein. This polyubiquitination allows substrate proteins to be destroyed by the protease complex (Figure 1).

Ubiquitin protease system and protein targeting chimeras (PROTACs).

The process begins with the activation of the ubiquitin protein by E1 ligases through ATP hydrolysis, generating a high‐energy thiol ester intermediate. The activated ubiquitin is then transferred to E2 ligase. The E2 ligase subsequently transfers the ubiquitin tag to the substrate protein, which has been recruited by an E3 ligase. The E3 ligase serves as a scaffold enzyme, facilitating the optimal position of E2 ligase and substrate protein. It also determines the specificity of the substrate protein through structural recognition. Finally, sequential ubiquitination of the substrate protein leads to its polyubiquitination, which is recognized by the 26S proteasome and degraded into short peptides.

PROTACs as Therapeutics to Target Disease

PROTACs (PROteolysis‐TArgeting Chimeras) take advantage of the UPS to degrade disease‐related proteins as a therapeutic approach. A PROTAC is a small molecule that can be used to target specific proteins for degradation by the UPS. PROTACs are typically composed of three domains: a ligand that binds to a specific protein of interest (referred to as the “target protein”), a ligand that binds to an E3 ubiquitin ligase, and a linker connecting the two ligands (Figure 1). By recruiting an E3 ubiquitin ligase to the target protein in close proximity, PROTACs can facilitate polyubiquitination of the target protein, marking it for degradation by the 26S proteasome.

The use of PROTACs (Proteolysis‐Targeting Chimera) in drug discovery has attracted significant interest due to their unique features compared to traditional small‐molecule inhibitors. Unlike traditional inhibitors, PROTACs are capable of inducing the direct degradation of specific target proteins rather than inhibition. This complete degradation of target protein enables the overcoming of drug resistance that traditional inhibitors often face. Traditional inhibitors encounter drug resistance due to mutations in the drug‐binding sites of target proteins, but PROTACs can overcome this resistance by directly degrading the target protein, regardless of such mutations. [2]

The catalytic nature of PROTACs enables multiple rounds of target protein degradation. This results in the desired activity at lower doses, providing high potency and efficacy with reduced toxicity. [3]

Additionally, PROTACs can target proteins that are considered undruggable with conventional inhibitors. [4] This is because PROTACs do not require a compound that inhibits the active site of the target protein, but rather, any ligand that can simply bind to the target protein can be used. Consequently, inactive ligands or inhibitors that did not show sufficient efficacy as drugs can be repurposed for PROTAC development. This facilitates the targeting of challenging proteins in drug development. [5]

The great interest in PROTAC as a novel therapeutic strategy has prompted the investigation into their utility as cancer therapeutics. Numerous PROTACs targeting cancer have been reported, [6] with breast cancer being a significant target in this strategy.

Breast Cancers (BCs)

Breast cancer (BC) is a devastating disease that affects millions of women around the world. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), breast cancer accounts for approximately 30 % of all cancers in women, making it the most common type of cancer among women. [7] In the United States, it is estimated that 1 in 8 women will develop invasive BC in their lifetime, [8] which highlights the importance of developing effective treatments for this disease.

Breast cancer is classified into four different types based on the expression of hormone receptors such as estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR), or human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) (Table 1). [9] These receptors play a critical role in determining the type of therapy and affecting the prognosis of the disease. The first type of BC is the luminal A type, which has hormone receptors (ER or PR) with low levels of HER2. This type of BC has a good prognosis with hormone therapy, also called endocrine therapy. This therapy targets hormone receptor‐positive breast cancers by inhibiting hormone production or interfering with hormone response, resulting in slowing and halting the growth of cancers.

The second type of breast cancer, luminal B, has hormone receptors and high HER2 expression. It is considered a more aggressive form of BC compared to luminal A. This type of BC is treated with a combination of hormone therapy and targeted therapy with HER2 receptor antibodies, such as trastuzumab. The prognosis of this type of BC is more critical compared to luminal A.

The third type, HER2+, has high HER2 expression but does not have hormone receptors. The overexpression of the HER2 receptor promotes the growth and division of cells, resulting in fast‐growing tumors. HER2+BC tends to be more invasive than luminal A/B types of BC and is typically treated with HER2‐targeting therapy, such as trastuzumab, pertuzumab, or trastuzumab‐emtansine (T‐DM1, antibody‐drug conjugate). [10]

The last type of breast cancer is known as triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC). TNBC does not express ER, PR, or HER2 receptors, making it difficult to utilize specific therapies to target BC‐related receptors. As a result, TNBC is typically treated with chemotherapy, surgery, or radiation therapy, which is less specific and less effective than targeted therapies. TNBC also has a more aggressive progression, a higher rate of recurrence, and a higher rate of invasion, resulting in a poor prognosis after therapy. [11]

PROTACs represent a promising new strategy for treating BC by targeting and destroying specific proteins implicated in the development of BC such as ER and HER2 (Figure 2). In this review, we will focus on the use of PROTACs in BC therapeutics.

Additionally, this review will provide an overview of the current trends, limitations, and future perspectives of PROTAC technology in BC.

PROTACs in BCs

ERα Targeting PROTACs

Estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) plays a significant role in the development and progression of breast cancer by activating signaling pathways that promote cell growth and division. In many cases of breast cancer, the cancer cells overexpress ERα and are dependent on estrogen signaling for their growth and survival. Therefore, it is referred to as one subtype of breast cancer cells, luminal A and B. As ERα overexpression has a positive correlation with luminal BC progression, several ERα‐targeting PROTACs were reported (Table 2). [12] The first PROTAC targeting ERα combines 17‐beta‐estradiol (E2), a type of hormone that binds to ERα, with a peptide‐based Von Hippel‐Lindau (VHL) E3 ligase ligand. [13] This early PROTAC model was developed before small‐molecule VHL ligands were discovered. The conjugate of E2 and the peptide‐based VHL ligand, known as Protac‐B, has been shown to inhibit the growth of breast cancer cells that express ERα and cause cells to be arrested in the G1 cell cycle.

To utilize another E3 ligase in PROTAC design, Ohoka et al. introduced SNIPERs (Specific and Nongenetic IAP‐dependent Protein Erasers), a new method for PROTAC design that recruits inhibitors of the apoptosis protein (IAP) E3 ubiquitin ligases. The authors conjugated tamoxifen, a selective estrogen receptor modulator (SERM) that blocks estrogen binding to the ERα, to a small‐molecule ligand of IAP E3 ligase using a pegylated linker, resulting in SNIPER(ER)‐87. [14] This compound was found to reduce ERα levels and suppress the growth of breast tumors in vivo. Further derivatization of the IAP ligand from SNIPER(ER)‐87 led to the development of SNIPER(ER)‐110, [15] which showed improved binding affinity to IAP E3 ligase and potent antitumor activity in vivo. It is worth mentioning that this PROTAC is unique as it utilizes IAP E3 ligase, while most PROTACs targeting breast cancer use either VHL or CRBN E3 ligase.

Raloxifene, another SERM with less risk of increasing endometrial cancer, was utilized in ERα‐targeting PROTAC design yielding in ERD‐308 and ERD‐148. [16] Both PROTACs are conjugates using raloxifene and the small‐molecule VHL E3 ligase ligand, which is the most commonly used E3 ligase ligand in a recent PROTAC design. As an effort to improve the pharmacokinetics of peptide‐based VHL E3 ligase ligand, a small‐molecule‐based VHL E3 ligase ligand was identified and used in most of the recent VHL‐based PROTACs. Although ERD‐308 and ERD‐148 differ in the existence of one oxygen atom in their linker, the length of the linker remains constant. It represents that the optimal length of the linker is important in PROTAC design. ERD‐308 and ERD‐148 both show antitumor activity in ERα+breast cancer cells, while no activity in ERα− breast cancer cells.

With the spotlight of PROTAC technology as a promising therapeutic strategy, Arvinas and Pfizer's collaboration led to the development of ARV‐471, an orally administered PROTAC drug, is currently in the phase 3 clinical trials to specifically target ERα+/HER2−breast cancer cells. The drug utilizes a piperidine linker that conjugates CRBN E3 ligase ligand and ERα ligand, resulting in improved pharmacokinetics. The phase 2 clinical trial of ARV‐471 was successfully completed in 2022, demonstrating continued efficacy and a favorable tolerability profile. The next key step in the development of PROTAC drugs for breast cancer treatment will be the results of the phase 3 clinical trial of ARV‐471. [17] Inspired by the ARV‐471, novel chemical structures of linkers aimed at improving pharmacokinetics are currently under development.

In addition to ARV‐471, AC0682, developed by Accutar Biotech, represents another promising candidate in the PROTAC landscape. AC0682 is an investigational orally bioavailable PROTAC targeting ERα, currently in phase 1 clinical trials for the treatment of ER‐positive breast cancers. AC0682 has demonstrated the ability to potently and selectively degrade both wild‐type and mutant forms of ERα. [18] Preclinical studies have indicated that AC0682 possesses favorable pharmacological properties, including blood‐brain barrier (BBB) penetration, and exhibits promising anti‐tumor activity in ER‐positive animal models. [19] The clinical advancement of these compounds would underscore the potential of PROTACs to revolutionize future cancer therapy.

Recently, selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD) has been developed as an endocrine therapy for breast cancer, and the first SERD, fulvestrant, was approved by the FDA for treating ER+BC patients. Inspired by the SERD, Xie et al have developed SERD‐utilizing PROTACs, ZD12, incorporating oxabicycloheptane sulfonamide (OBHSA), a novel selective estrogen receptor degrader (SERD), as the ERα ligand within a PROTAC framework. ZD12 exhibited great antitumor potency and ERα degradation activity across a broad spectrum of ER+breast cancer (BC) cells, including those resistant to tamoxifen and cell lines with mutated ERα. [20] Recognizing the outstanding oral bioavailability of the CRBN ligand and SERD molecules in clinical trials of PROTACs, [21] Chen et al. aimed to further enhance this characteristic by developing a novel CRBN ligand named TX‐16. TX‐16 demonstrates remarkable absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) properties. [22] Subsequently, the authors designed an ERα PROTAC called ERD‐3111 utilizing TX‐16 as a CRBN ligand and a modified version of AZD‐9833, an orally bioavailable SERD molecule, as the ER ligand core. It displays potent cellular degradation activity against ERα and shows high oral bioavailability in mice, rats, and dogs. Oral administration of ERD‐3111 effectively reduces the levels of both wild‐type and mutated ERα proteins in tumor tissues with 0.5 nM concentration efficacy.

In PROTAC design, not only the small‐molecule ligand of ERα was used, but also the binding DNA sequence of ERα. As a transcription factor, ERα plays a crucial role in gene regulation by binding to specific DNA sequences known as estrogen response elements (EREs). To investigate the potential of ERE‐targeted drug design, Zhang et al. designed PROTAC to degrade ERα using the nucleotide sequence of ERE and the VHL E3 ligase ligand. [23] ERE‐PROTAC induces cell death of breast cancer cells. This approach, which targets the ERα DNA binding site, holds promise for overcoming drug resistance in cases where mutations in the ligand‐binding domain of ERα occur. Despite a new approach utilizing nucleotide as a target protein binder, ERE‐PROTAC has shown degradation activity with moderate potency in 5 μM concentration.

Decoy oligonucleotides serve as ligands that interact with specific proteins, regulating their functions, particularly effective for proteins like transcription factors binding to DNA. To utilize decoy oligonucleotides in PROTAC development, Naganuma et al. designed LCL‐ER(dec) conjugating IAP ligand and ERα‐targeting decoy oligonucleotides. [24] However, LCL‐ER(dec) faced challenges with low intracellular stability resulting in 10 μM efficacy. To address this, researchers further introduced a T4 loop structure at the end of the decoy, creating LCL‐ER(dec)‐H46. [25] This modification led to prolonged ERα degradation activity in 1‐μM concentration and enhanced enzyme tolerance, demonstrating the advantageous use of hairpin structures in decoy oligonucleotide‐based PROTACs.

These authors also employed the use of nucleic acid aptamers for protein degradation. They utilized CRBN and VHL E3 ligase ligands to conjugate with the ERα‐binding aptamer sequence, which was identified using the SELEX method. [26] Among the designed ERα degrader, the pomalidomide (POM) conjugated degrader, POM‐ER(apt)D1, has been identified as the most potent ERα degrader with efficacy at 4 μM. [27]

As an alternative strategy to target ERα directly, the estrogen‐related receptor alpha (ERRα) has been targeted to influence the function of ERα in breast cancer cells. While ERRα functions independently of ERα, it plays a role in estrogen biosynthesis and metabolism, thus affecting ERα signaling indirectly. In a recent study, Shanmugasundaram et al. have demonstrated that ERRα can be degraded through the N‐end rule pathway by utilizing amino acids arginine (R) and histidine (H) as UBR E3 ligase ligands in PROTAC design. [28] The N‐end rule pathway is a mechanism that controls protein stability by recognizing specific destabilizing N‐terminal residues. The degradation of the target protein occurs at sub‐micromolar concentrations resulting in the inhibition of breast cancer cell proliferation. It is noteworthy that this example is the first study to use UBR E3 ligases in PROTAC development. Since a single amino acid was employed to recruit UBR E3 ligase, further optimization of the E3 ligase ligand is necessary. Moreover, utilizing UBR E3 ligases in PROTAC design has been conducted to target triple‐negative breast cancers.

Monitoring and visualizing the real‐time process of protein degradation has been a challenging task in the field of PROTAC.

Utilizing an ERα‐targeting fluorescent probe and a VHL ligand, compound A3 effectively degrades ERα proteins and exhibits impressive fluorescence imaging performance. [29] A3 combines the protein‐degrading capability of PROTAC with the visualization features of a fluorescent probe. This novel PROTAC not only allows real‐time observation of protein degradation processes but also possesses therapeutic characteristics. It represents a unique form that broadens the application spectrum of PROTAC technology.

HER2 Targeting PROTACs

Breast cancers of two types, luminal B and HER2+, have high expression levels of HER2. Although PROTACs directly targeting HER2 have not been explored yet, selective PROTACs targeting BC progression‐associated protein in HER2+ breast cancer cells are listed in Table 3. Trastuzumab (Herceptin) is a monoclonal antibody that targets HER2 and blocks its activity, slowing the growth and invasion of breast cancer cells. [30] Taking advantage of trastuzumab's specificity for HER2, Maneiro et al. reported a strategy for selective protein degradation in HER2+ breast cancer cells using a trastuzumab‐PROTAC conjugate (Ab‐PROTAC3). [31] When Ab‐PROTAC3 binds to HER2 and is internalized, the active PROTAC is released through the hydrolysis of the linker that connects the antibody to the PROTAC. The PROTAC selectively degrades bromodomain‐containing protein 4 (BRD4) in HER2+ breast cancer cells, where BRD4 activates genes that promote tumor growth. Ab‐PROTAC3 had no effect on HER2‐negative breast cancer cells.

Furthermore, Cimas et al. encapsulated the known BRD4 degrader, PROTAC3, within nanoparticles and conjugated these nanoparticles with trastuzumab to improve drug stability and therapeutic properties. [32] The developed compound, MZ1‐ACNP, showed increased toxicity against HER2+ and PROTAC‐resistant HER2+ breast cancer cells compared to Ab‐PROTAC3 and PROTAC3 alone.

RIPK2 protein has been known to have a high correlation between progression and invasion of BCs. Utilizing antibody‐PROTAC conjugates, Chan et al recently developed ADC‐2, conjugating RIPK2 protein PROTAC and trastuzumab with cleavage linkers, cathepsin. [33] ADC‐2 demonstrates potent degradation of RIPK2 in HER2+ cell lines, with no observed degradation in HER2 negative cell lines.

As an alternative therapeutic strategy to utilize HER2‐binding antibodies, targeting proteins associated with HER2 function and expression has been explored. One such protein, nucleolin (NCL), which shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm, has been found to interact with HER2 and drive the progression of HER2+ breast cancer. [34] As a result, targeting NCL is gaining interest as a potential treatment option for HER2+ and drug‐resistant HER2+ breast cancer patients. Zhang and colleagues have identified aptamer AS1411 as an NCL‐targeting ligand and converted it into the PROTAC, ZL216, by conjugating the VHL ligand. [35] ZL216 binds to NCL on the cell surface and leads to its internalization and degradation at a concentration of 10 nM. Additionally, ZL216 reduces NCL levels in vivo mice models.

A recent study has revealed that approximately 60 % of HER2+ breast cancer cells exhibit overexpression of the androgen receptor (AR). As a result, AR has been considered a viable alternative target for suppressing the development of HER2+ breast cancer. Zhao et al. demonstrated the efficacy of AR‐targeting PROTAC, ADR‐61, which utilizes both an AR antagonist (ARi‐16) and the VHL ligand. [36] ADR‐61 specifically reduces cell growth and induces G2/M cell cycle arrest in HER2+ breast cancer cells.

Triple‐Negative Breast Cancer (TNBC) Targeting PROTACs

Triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) shares similar properties with basal‐like breast cell lines but shows aggressive cancer cell growth and invasive features. As TNBC does not express hormone receptors and HER2, TNBC‐targeting PROTACs are designed to target proteins that are overexpressed in TNBC cell lines, such as bromodomain and extra‐terminal (BET) protein, steroid receptor coactivator‐1 (SRC‐1), poly [ADP‐ribose] polymerase 1 (PARP1), Fak, cMyc, and cyclin‐dependent kinases (CDKs) (Table 4). BET protein has been demonstrated to drive proliferation and reduce apoptosis in TNBC cell lines. [37] Bai et al. reported BETd‐246, a compound composed of a BET protein inhibitor, BETi‐211, and the CRBN E3 ligase binder, which effectively increases TNBC cell apoptosis and programmed cell death at a concentration of ~3 nM. [38] Other BET proteins targeting PROTACs with different BET protein ligands and E3 ligases have also been noted, and they hold potential as therapeutic options for TNBC.

SRC‐1, also known as steroid receptor coactivator‐1, is a protein that influences gene expression and cell growth regulation. In TNBC, high levels of SRC‐1 expression are linked to poor patient prognosis.[ 39 , 40 ] Lee et al. developed an SRC‐1 degrader, ND1‐YL2, that utilizes the UBR E3 ligase and the binding partner of SRC‐1, STAT6. The authors optimized the UBR E3 ligase binding peptide sequence from a single amino acid residue to four amino acid residues. [41] ND1‐YL2 was found to repress MDA‐MB‐231 TNBC invasion and migration in vitro and in vivo.

Furthermore, niraparib and defactinib, which are inhibitors of PARP1 and Fak respectively, have been converted into degraders.[ 42 , 43 ] PARP1 and Fak have been linked to the onset and aggressive progression of TNBC.[ 44 , 45 ] Consequently, it has been proposed that PARP1 and Fak inhibitors may serve as potential treatments for TNBC. The PARP1 inhibitor, niraparib, was conjugated with MDM2 E3 ligase binder and induces significant PARP1 cleavage and apoptosis in TNBC cells. [46] Additionally, defactinib, the Fak inhibitor, was conjugated to the VHL E3 ligase binder, leading to the degradation of the Fak protein and repression of TNBC migration and invasion. [47]

Cyclin‐dependent kinases (CDKs) have recently emerged as significant therapeutic targets in TNBC therapy. Aberrant CDK activation results in dysregulated cell proliferation, which is a critical pathological mechanism in cancer. [48] Previously, CDK4/6 inhibitors were approved by the FDA for the treatment of breast cancer. [49] As PROTAC technology gains attention as a promising therapeutic strategy, it has been integrated with these CDK4/6 inhibitors to develop potent compounds against TNBC. B. Zhao et al. designed pal‐pom using palbociclib, an FDA‐approved CDK 4/6 inhibitor, and CRBN E3 ligase ligand. [50] Pal‐pom has potent activity in initiating the degradation of CDK4 and CDK6 proteins. This degradation effectively suppresses the phosphorylation of retinoblastoma protein (Rb), leading to cell cycle arrest. This compound exhibits high potency, with activity observed at nanomolar concentrations, marking it as the first PROTAC targeting CDK4/6.

Moreover, PROTACs utilizing other E3 ligase ligands and palbociclib have been explored for the development of more potent CDK4/6 drugs. Steinebach et al. propose VHL‐based CDK4/6 targeting PROTACs. Identified CDK4/6 degrader, Compound 34, exhibited potent degradation capabilities for both CDK4 and CDK6. [51] Importantly, this VHL‐based CDK4/6 degrader did not alter the levels of VHL, IKZF1, or IKZF3, common unintended targets of pal‐pom.[ 52 , 53 ] Compound 34 also had significant inhibitory effects on breast cancer cell lines, including palbociclib‐resistant cells, indicating its potential applicability in TNBC therapy.

Similarly, Pu et al. developed compound A4 by combining the DCAF16 E3 ligase ligand, KB02, with palbociclib. [54] A4 effectively inhibits the transition from the G1 phase to the S phase by degrading CDK4/6 proteins, thereby preventing cell proliferation. Additionally, A4 exhibits seven times lower toxicity in normal cells compared to palbociclib, highlighting its potential as a targeted cancer therapy with minimized side effects.

Despite the selective degradation of CDK4/6 PROTAC, Kumarasamy et al. investigated that resistance of CDK4/6 PROTAC often arises due to the overexpression of Cyclin E, which can rescue the cells undergoing cell cycle arrest due to CDK4/6 degradation. [55] Therefore, the authors suggested a combination treatment with CDK4/6 inhibitor and Cyclin E degrader. Since Cyclin E forms a complex with CDK2, they utilized the CDK inhibitor FN‐1501, a multi‐kinase inhibitor to develop Cyclin E/CDK2 PROTACs. A pomalidomide moiety was conjugated to the CDK inhibitor FN‐1501, a multi‐kinase inhibitor, resulting in FN‐POM, which effectively degraded both Cyclin E and CDK2 in TNBC cell lines. Combination treatment of palbociclib and Cyclin E/CDK2 PROTAC effectively represses cell growth in TNBC cell lines and organoid models.

CDK12 and CDK13 proteins, members of the CDK family, regulate gene transcription through the phosphorylation of the C‐terminal domain of RNA polymerase II,[ 56 , 57 ] making them critical therapeutic targets in TNBC. However, previous small‐molecule inhibitors of CDK12/13 often led to mutation‐mediated resistance. [58] To overcome this and enhance the potency of the drug, selective degradation of CDK12/13 using PROTACs has been investigated. Niu et al. identified PP−C8, a PROTAC capable of selectively degrading CDK12 without affecting CDK13. [59] In TNBC models, PP−C8 showed rapid and highly selective degradation of CDK12‐cyclin K without detectable degradation of CDK13. PP−C8‐mediated CDK12‐cyclin K degradation suppresses DNA damage response (DDR) gene expression and exhibits substantial synergistic antiproliferative effects when combined with PARP inhibitors. These findings highlight the potential of PP−C8 as a targeted therapy for TNBC, minimizing off‐target effects while enhancing treatment efficacy.

For dual degradation of CDK12/13, Yang et al. developed dual CDK12/13 degrader compound 7 f by conjugating optimized CDK12/13 inhibitors with CRBN ligands. [60] Degradation of CDK12/13 by 7 f suppressed DDR gene expression and inhibited TNBC cell growth. Additionally, 7 f showed synergistic effects in inhibiting TNBC cell growth when combined with DDR inhibitors.

Additionally, dysregulation of CDK9‐related signaling is a hallmark in various cancers,[ 61 , 62 ] including TNBC, leading to upregulation of oncogenes such as c‐Myc and Mcl‐1.[ 63 , 64 , 65 , 66 ] The targeted degradation of CDK9 also has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach for the treatment of TNBC. Compound 45, a novel CDK9 degrader, has demonstrated potent inhibition of TNBC cell growth by inducing rapid and selective degradation of CDK9 both in vitro and in vivo. [67] This degradation results in the downregulation of downstream targets, such as c‐Myc, leading to decreasing tumor cell proliferation. Taken together, it suggests that targeting CDKs for protein degradation is an effective strategy for TNBC therapy.

As studies utilizing nucleotide‐based molecules in ERα and HER2 targeting PROTACs, transcription factors associated with TNBC progression were targeted with nucleotide‐based PROTACs. The Enhancer Box (E‐box) is a DNA sequence found in the regulatory regions of genes, essential for transcriptional regulation. [68] Transcription factors such as c‐Myc bind to the E‐box, either promoting or inhibiting the transcription of specific genes. Li et al. have recently developed a PROTAC based on Threose Nucleic Acid (TNA) and DNA to effectively degrade the c‐Myc protein, a therapeutic target in TNBC. [69] The authors conducted the in vitro selection of TNA aptamer to identify the specific binder to the c‐Myc/Max heterodimer. The identified TNA aptamer specifically binds to the c‐Myc/Max heterodimer and leads to form a high‐affinity interaction with the E‐box DNA sequence and c‐Myc/Max. The resulting TNA aptamer‐E box DNA‐pomalidomide (TEP) conjugate selectively degrades c‐Myc/Max, demonstrating inhibitory effects on TNBC cell proliferation. This study shows a novel approach utilizing TNA and DNA‐based PROTAC to address TNBC, emphasizing the efficient targeting and degradation of the c‐Myc protein.

Future Perspective

Recently, a number of new E3 ligases, including KEAP1, DCAF15, DCAF16, RNF4, RNF114, KLHDC2, SKP1‐CUL1‐F‐box, and FEM1B have been utilized in PROTAC design.[ 70 , 71 , 72 , 73 , 74 , 75 , 76 , 77 ] However, the majority of BC‐targeting PROTACs employ VHL and CRBN E3 ligases, and only a small number utilize IAP, MDM2, and UBR. Previous studies have shown that PROTAC activity can vary among different cell lines.[ 78 , 79 ] For example, Ottis et al. treated various cell lines with dBet6, which combines a BRD4 inhibitor with CRBN, and MZ1, which combines a BRD4 inhibitor with VHL, to study the cell‐specific effects. [80] Although both compounds degrade the same protein, dBet6 demonstrated superior degradation efficacy in breast cancer cells compared to MZ1. This demonstrates that choosing the appropriate E3 ligase in PROTAC design can result in cell or tissue specificity and significant degradation. For example, BC‐targeting PROTACs can utilize RNF5 E3 ligase, which is highly expressed in breast cancer but has not been used in BC‐targeting PROTAC design. [81] Therefore, the integration of newly discovered E3 ligases with existing ERα binders or ligands of BC‐related proteins could result in an enhancement of diversity, potency, and specificity compared to the current generation of BC‐targeting PROTACs.

As well as E3 ligase ligands, the linker property is crucial in PROTAC design. It connects the ligand for the target protein with the ligand for the E3 ligase, significantly impacting the efficacy and specificity of the PROTAC. The linker's length and flexibility determine the proximity of the target protein and E3 ligase, influencing ubiquitination efficiency. Stability affects the PROTAC's half‐life and bioavailability, while solubility influences cell permeability and distribution. Proper optimization of these linker properties is essential for the effective functioning of PROTACs in targeted protein degradation.

Most BC‐targeting PROTACs utilize pegylated or alkylated linkers due to their synthetic accessibility and ease of tuning linker length and chemical structure. Recently, researchers have explored alternative types of linkers that could enhance the pharmacokinetics of PROTACs, including heterocyclic scaffolds and alkene linkers for improved structural rigidity. [82] The use of heterocyclic scaffolds such as the pyridine/di‐piperidine motif in conjunction with the alkyne linker has been reported to significantly enhance aqueous solubility compared to pegylated or hydrocarbon linkers. However, only four over thirty‐reported BC‐targeting PROTACs use heterocyclic scaffolds as linkers. Therefore, incorporating linkers with improved pharmacokinetics could be a feasible option in the current design of BC‐targeting PROTACs.

As another alternative strategy to improve the pharmacokinetics of PROTACs, in‐cell self‐assembly of PROTACs, called CLIPTACs, has also been achieved through the use of tetrazine and alkyne precursors as linkers. [83] The target protein ligand and the E3 ligase ligand were conjugated with tetrazine and alkyne precursors, respectively, and then treated separately into cells, leading to the in‐cell self‐assembly of the PROTACs. This approach offers the advantage of treating the protein ligand and the E3 ligase ligand separately, thereby alleviating the cell penetration and solubility issues associated with high molecular weight and large polar surface area of current PROTACs. However, CLIPTACs still have limitations to overcome, such as the possibility of off‐target effects due to the reactivity of tetrazine and alkyne precursors, and the variable efficiency of the self‐assembly process within different cellular environments. By optimally designing PROTACs to eliminate potential limitations, this approach could be considered as a method to enhance the pharmacokinetics of BC‐targeting PROTACs. Taken together, linkers with self‐optimization or enhanced pharmacokinetics can be considered to develop future BC‐targeting PROTACs.

Additionally, designing PROTACs with high specificity for BC cells is crucial to eliminate off‐target effects and achieve selective degradation. One strategy to enhance specificity in BC‐targeting PROTACs is using antibody‐conjugated PROTACs, such as Ab‐PROTAC3 and MZ1‐ACNPs. As previously discussed, these compounds incorporate the Trastuzumab antibody, which targets HER2+ breast cells, thereby providing cell specificity. Pertuzumab, another FDA‐approved HER2 antibody, [84] has been used in a BC‐targeting PROTAC conjugate, ORM‐5029, which is currently undergoing a phase 1 trial. [85] These examples indicate that degrader‐antibody conjugates can be considered a strategy to enhance the specificity of existing BC‐targeting PROTACs.

Lastly, one considerable strategy for designing BC‐targeting PROTACs is expanding the scope of the target BC‐related proteins, such as Human Protein Tyrosine Phosphatase 1B (PTP1B). PTP1B, a key negative regulator of several signaling pathways related to metabolism and oncogenesis, [86] is overexpressed in BC, contributing to its aggressiveness.[ 87 , 88 , 89 , 90 ] While PROTACs targeting PTP1B have been reported in previous research,[ 91 , 92 ] PTP1B PROTAC have not been tested in breast cancer cells. Therefore, in cases where PROTACs have been developed for proteins overexpressed in BC but have not yet been used for BC treatment, repurposing these PROTACs for BC therapy should be considered. Additionally, the development of PROTACs targeting new proteins that are overexpressed in BC but have not yet been targeted by existing PROTACs is worth exploring.

Conclusions

In conclusion, PROTAC technology holds promise as a potential treatment option for breast cancer. By targeting and degrading specific proteins that play a role in the progression and invasion of specific breast cancer types, PROTACs have the potential to impede or halt the growth of breast cancer cells. Although further research and clinical trials are necessary to fully understand the efficacy of PROTACs in treating breast cancer, the initial results are encouraging.

To fully realize the potential of PROTACs in treating breast cancer, it is important to expand the scope of the E3 ligases and target proteins as well as improve pharmacokinetics. By utilizing a wider range of E3 ligases, PROTACs can be designed to degrade a broader spectrum of proteins involved in the progression of breast cancer. Moreover, by increasing the number of targetable proteins, PROTACs can be tailored to the specific needs of individual patients, enabling personalized and effective treatment. As this field continues to evolve, PROTACs may offer a new and innovative approach to the treatment of breast cancer, providing patients with improved outcomes.
