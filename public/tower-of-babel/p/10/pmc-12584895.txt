Challenges in evaluation and reimbursement of drugs for rare diseases in Saudi Arabia: a Delphi expert consensus
Authors: Laila Carolina Abu Esba, Consuela Cheriece Yousef, Mansoor Ahmed Khan, Sakra Balhareth, Hussain A Al-Omar, Amal Al-Najjar, Aljawharah Alkoraishi, Abdullah M Alhammad, Ziyad Saeed Almalki, Esraa S Altawil, Roaa Al Gain, Mohamed Ahmed, Hend Metwali, Layla Al Anizy, Fatma Maraiki, Abdulrazaq Al Jazairi, Abdulaziz Alhossan, Mohammed Al Harbi, Hind Almodaimegh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDrugs for rare diseases (DRDs) present unique challenges in evaluation and reimbursement due to high costs, limited clinical evidence, and complex healthcare decision-making. While international models exist for DRD reimbursement, Saudi Arabia faces distinct obstacles driven by high incidence of genetic diseases, healthcare system fragmentation, and evolving national policies. This study aims to identify key challenges in evaluating and reimbursing DRDs in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA modified Delphi method was conducted between January and February 2025, involving healthcare policymakers, clinicians, health economists, and formulary decision-makers across multiple institutions. The process included three rounds: (1) open-ended questionnaires to identify challenges, (2) voting on structured statements using a Likert scale, and (3) consensus refinement through a roundtable discussionResultsNineteen experts participated, with final consensus reached on 88 statements across eight themes: evaluation complexities, evidence limitations, economic and budgetary constraints, supply chain management, data generation and infrastructure, stakeholder concerns, patient and family barriers, and policy and collaboration gaps. The highest concern was economic constraints. Other challenges included inconsistencies in reimbursement decisions, and difficulties in managed entry agreements. Stakeholders emphasised the need for structured decision-making frameworks and national coordination to improve access and equity.ConclusionThis study provides the first consensus on challenges in DRD evaluation and reimbursement in Saudi Arabia. Addressing these issues through policy reforms, stakeholder collaboration, and data infrastructure enhancement is crucial to optimising resource allocation and patient access. Further research should focus on implementing value-based agreement to ensure sustainable reimbursement strategies for DRDs.KEYWORDS: Rare diseases, reimbursement, health technology assessment, formulary, orphan drugs, healthcare system fragmentation, managed entry agreement, value-based agreement

Background

The growing focus on drugs for rare diseases (DRDs) over recent years has been fuelled by breakthroughs in therapeutic discovery, regulatory shifts promoting drug development, and the complexities governments, policymakers, and payers face in balancing fair resource allocation for these high-cost therapies often with limited and uncertain clinical evidence. While the challenges are universal, and described by various healthcare systems, each country also struggles with unique hurdles that demand recognition in order to pave the way for tailored solutions (Felippini et al., 2024; Ng et al., 2024; Sirrs et al., 2023; Stafinski et al., 2022).

The prevalence and incidence of rare diseases, as well as the approaches to their management, vary significantly across regions and countries. Differences in healthcare system complexities, societal burden, availability of supportive care, diagnostic capabilities, regulatory frameworks, political and economic priorities, budgetary constraints, and mechanisms for funding and reimbursement of DRDs have led to variation in patient access to these therapies. Some countries, such as the United Kingdom (UK), have established specialised evaluation pathways and committee to assess DRDs, while others, like Australia have introduced alternative government funding mechanisms, and in Singapore a mixed government and public donation special funding path is in place. Nevertheless, many countries still apply evaluation and reimbursement processes similar to those used for non-rare disease drugs, which might not capture the full value of these therapies (Felippini et al., 2024; Goldblatt, 2013; Ng et al., 2024).

The burden of rare diseases in the Kingdome of Saudi Arabia (KSA) sets a special mixture of complexity, characterised by a higher incidence compared to many other parts of the world. This is due to the high prevalence of pathogenic alleles driven by the high rates of consanguineous marriages. This complexity is further compounded by a fragmented healthcare system with multiple governmental healthcare providers and private sectors operating independently, and the limited screening programmes. Regional variation in incidence, diagnostic capabilities and access to specialised care throughout the country also contributes to some of the inequity in care (Aleissa et al., 2022; Alqahtani et al., 2023; Khayat et al., 2024).

At the time of this study, KSA did not have a formal national definition of a DRDs, and reimbursement practices largely relied on international orphan criteria (≤5 in 10,000 in the EU or <200,000 individuals in the US). Rare disease drugs are primarily financed by public health institutions, with some private sector coverage. Recently, a national workshop involving multiple stakeholders proposed a formal definition of rare diseases for KSA, marking an important policy development (Abozaid et al., 2025).

With approximately 70% of rare diseases having a genetic basis, advanced genomic analysis techniques hold significant potential for identifying unique mutations. These advancements could enable more personalised treatment approaches for the rarest of the rare diseases, underscoring the need for robust, localised data to inform precision medicine efforts and reimbursement (Alqahtani et al., 2023; Khayat et al., 2024).

The drug reimbursement landscape in KSA is further shaped by the country’s evolving healthcare policies and transformation vision, and increasing demand for spending efficiency, where DRDs are primarily funded by public health institutions, with some coverage through private insurance. The healthcare system is undergoing major transformation under Vision 2030, with initiatives including the Health Sector Transformation Programme (2025), centralisation of procurement, establishment of a national HTA framework, and adoption of efficiency-driven policies that increasingly influence DRD evaluation and reimbursement (Abozaid et al., 2025; Balkhi et al., 2023; Vision, 2030, 2025).

A recent initiative by a multi stakeholder group of experts explored the feasibility of implementing a Multi-Criteria Decision Analysis (MCDA) framework for health technology assessment (HTA) of DRDs in KSA. The group set forth a list of criteria that would be relevant for the evaluation of orphan drugs, as an initial step in guiding and conducting an MCDA supported decision making process (Al-Jedai et al., 2024). By leveraging the collective expertise of stakeholders, this study aims to identify key challenges in the evaluation and reimbursement of DRDs in KSA to pave the way for recommendations to improve access and equity in treatment.

Methods

To achieve the aim of this study, we used a modified Delphi method, a widely recognised approach for achieving consensus among experts through multiple rounds of structured questionnaires and feedback. The method was tailored to focus on the unique challenges in evaluating and reimbursing DRDs in KSA (Murphy et al., 2006). The study took place between January 20, and February 14, 2025. Online questionnaires and voting links were provided using SurveyMonkey©.

Study participants

Purposive sampling was used to compile a diverse representation and expertise representing various healthcare sectors in KSA. We selected potential participants based on two inclusion criteria: either they had professional experience in evaluating DRDs for formulary addition, or they had direct professional knowledge about HTA, pharmaceutical funding, reimbursement mechanisms related to rare disease drugs (e.g. Healthcare policymakers, HTA representative, clinicians specialising in rare diseases, health economists, pharmacy and therapeutics committee members). The sampling ensured a comprehensive representation, together with the methodology of the multiple rounds; conceptual saturation was expected to be captured. All eligible experts were first invited via e-mail to participate in this study, and consented to participation.

Delphi process

Round 1: Identification of Challenges and Solutions

Round 1: Identification of Challenges and Solutions

Experts who consented to study participation received a link to an online open-ended questionnaire (see Supplemental Material). This asked them to provide minimal demographic data (i.e. years of experience, profession, affiliation) and, using free-text fields a total of 15 questions asked to list and describe challenges regarding the evaluation and reimbursement of drugs in rare diseases. To provide the necessary context for their responses, challenging areas identified via the document analysis were provided.

2. Round 2: Voting on Statement of Challenges

2. Round 2: Voting on Statement of Challenges

In round two, based on the results of the generated statements in round one, structured statements were developed, where participants were asked to vote in agreement or disagreement with each identified challenge on a 4-point Likert scale: strongly agree, agree, disagree, and strongly disagree. In addition, free-text fields under each statement were provided for participants to be able to comment on identified challenges, or propose an amendment to the statement, as well as specify any more challenges that might have been prompted during the second round. All answers were compiled, summarised and merged. The results were used to update the challenges statements for round 3.

3. Round 3: Consensus Establishing

3. Round 3: Consensus Establishing

In the third round, a roundtable discussion was held as a physical in-person meeting in Riyadh, allowing direct interaction and consensus building. During the roundtable discussion the statements with any level of disagreement were revised with the participants for a decision to retain, delete or modify the statement. To measure consensus, the updated list of statements was sent to all participants, after which they were asked to indicate their final agreement or disagreement with each identified challenge on a 4-point Likert scale: strongly agree, agree, disagree, and strongly disagree.

Data collection and analyses

Data was collected electronically to facilitate participation across regions. Thematic analysis was used for open-ended responses in Round 1. In discussion with two study team members, the first author compiled responses and analysed them using inductive thematic analysis outlined by Braun and Clarke to generate a comprehensive list of statements on challenges (Braun & Clarke, 2006). An open-coding strategy was employed, grouping similar concepts to develop key themes, and continued analysis until no new significant themes emerged. Responses were compiled verbatim with minimum changes for spelling and grammatical corrections only. Quantitative data from Rounds 2 and 3 was analysed using descriptive statistics (i.e. counts and percentages). Consensus was defined as ≥75% agreement (‘Strongly agree' and ‘Agree') among participants, based on the most acceptable threshold for a modified Delphi method (Diamond et al., 2014).

Ethical approval

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Research Ethics Board at King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC) (NRR25/081/1). Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Responses were anonymized to ensure confidentiality.

Results

Overview of expert panel & response rates

A total of 20 experts were initially invited to participate in the study, of whom 19 accepted. The expert panel were members of formulary decision committees, from various institutions within KSA. Participants were from the Ministry of Health, Ministry of National Guard Health Affairs, King Saud Medical City, King Faisal Specialist Hospital and Research Centre, and King Fahd Medical City. Experts included a physician, academic expert, clinical pharmacists, and policy and pharmacoeconomic experts.

The Delphi process was conducted over three rounds, with response rates as follows

Round 1: 9 out of 19 experts (47%) responded to the open-ended questionnaire, which generated the key themes and initial statements.

Round 1: 9 out of 19 experts (47%) responded to the open-ended questionnaire, which generated the key themes and initial statements.

Round 2: 15 out of 19 experts (79%) provided ratings on the generated statements, and suggested amendments.

Round 2: 15 out of 19 experts (79%) provided ratings on the generated statements, and suggested amendments.

Round 3: 17 out of 19 experts (90%) participated in the final roundtable discussion to finalise consensus statements.

Round 3: 17 out of 19 experts (90%) participated in the final roundtable discussion to finalise consensus statements.

Delphi process outcomes

Through thematic analysis of responses to the open-ended questionnaire in Round 1, eight overarching themes emerged, covering challenges related to drug evaluation, evidence limitations, economic and budgetary constraints, supply chain issues, stakeholder concerns, patient and family barriers, and policy gaps. These themes formed the basis for 90 consensus statements, which were refined and evaluated in subsequent rounds.

In Round 2, 44% (40/90) of statements achieved a 100% agreement, of the 90 statements, 5 (6%) did not reach the 75% consensus threshold, while 45 (50%) had at least one disagreement and were carried forward for further discussion. In Round 3, the final consensus was reached on all 90 statements, of which 88 were retained after amendment. The theme with the highest number of statements was (Economic and Budgetary Constraints), reflecting significant concerns about budgetary constraints, cost-effectiveness assessments, and financial sustainability in DRD reimbursement.

Finalised consensus statements

The Delphi process revealed a high degree of agreement among experts, with 99% of statements achieving consensus in the final round. Key insights from roundtable discussions

Operational and logistical issues

Participants shared day-to-day difficulties in managing small number of patients and the unpredictability of drug utilisation, coordinating access, and minimising waste when drugs are received after patient deterioration or death. The lack of a national coordination framework was highlighted as a major barrier.

In the context of outcome-based agreements (OBA), additional challenges arise when an OBA is established at one institution but the patient is followed up at a different institution. This creates issues for the patients and prescribers, such as difficulties in meeting follow-up requirements, inconsistencies in tracking patient outcomes, and challenges in ensuring compliance with the agreement’s terms. Furthermore, prescribers working outside the institution managing the OBA may face logistical and administrative obstacles in adhering to the agreement’s conditions.

Lack of dedicated evaluation infrastructure

None of the participants reported having a dedicated institutional committee for evaluating DRDs. While some national-level committees exist for highly specialised and high-cost drugs, like gene therapies; under the MOH and Saudi Health Council, there is no unified or specialised HTA process for DRDs in KSA.

Bypassing full evaluation

There was concern that some DRDs are occasionally approved via expedited, non-formulary pathways, bypassing comprehensive evaluation due to urgency or lack of alternative therapies.

The prioritisation of DRD evaluations remains unstandardised across institutions. While some institutions reported prioritising DRD evaluations when the drug had received a breakthrough designation, the panel noted that such designation often results in approval based on premature data, and lack the pharmacoeconomic studies that would typically necessitate delaying the evaluation process until further evidence becomes available.

Transparency concerns

The panel strongly emphasised the importance of transparency in decision-making for prescribers, patients and caregivers. It can help reduce appeals and build trust in the reimbursement process. A few experts also voiced concerns about peer pressure and conflicts that may arise, particularly with high-profile DRDs, when the reasons for reimbursement rejections are not clearly communicated.

Unregistered low-cost DRDs

Experts identified access issues with essential DRDs that remain unregistered by the Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA) due to a lack of commercial incentive for pharmaceutical companies to market the drug in the region, stressing the importance of regulatory action to close this gap.

Cost awareness debate

An interesting debate emerged around whether patients in a fully government-funded healthcare system with no co-payment should be informed of the cost of their DRD. Supporters of patient cost awareness argued that it could help patients better understand the value of their treatment and encourage them to communicate with their prescriber if they experience intolerance or wish to discontinue the drug. Additionally, awareness could promote greater care in handling their medications, reducing wastage, and aligns with transparency in reimbursement decision-making.

However, the opposing perspective highlighted that patient with rare diseases already face disparities and desperation to find effective treatments, making cost awareness of little practical value to them. Furthermore, given the current lack of transparency in how reimbursement decisions are reached and the lack of direct communication with patients regarding reimbursement rejections, simply disclosing drug costs without sufficient context may lead to frustration. Therefore, sharing cost information should be framed within the context of overall net value, rather than an isolated piece of information.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study conducted with multiple stakeholders to identify challenges in evaluating and reimbursing drugs for rare diseases in KSA. While multiple international studies have addressed issues related to access to drugs for rare diseases (Almalki et al., 2012; Cannizzo et al., 2018; Lopes-Júnior et al., 2022; Nestler-Parr et al., 2018), our study captured additional and unique aspects of these challenges that reflect the difficulties faced at a country level and within a fragmented reimbursement system. It provides an in-depth description of barriers that decision-makers face and institution-specific hurdles in handling such drugs. Although a recent study attempted to evaluate the challenges faced by rare disease patients and healthcare systems in the Middle East and North Africa, it was based on the perspective of pharmaceutical company representatives and presented an overall regional essence (Fasseeh et al., 2025).

The high level of agreement in our study underscored the shared concerns of stakeholders regarding inefficiencies and the need for structured evaluation frameworks and collaboration among institutions. In regard to the evaluation process, it is further complicated by the recent increase in the number of drugs approved for rare diseases, with a steady pipeline of drugs to come (Miller et al., 2021). In a report by the ISPOR Rare Disease Special Interest Group on the challenges in rare diseases, they grouped challenges into those related to research and those related to assessment, with lack of a specific method for DRD assessment identified as a major challenge (Nestler-Parr et al., 2021). There is lack of expertise in both the evaluation of DRDs and decision-making, in addition to a shortage of specialists in treating these conditions.

Additionally, in the absence of an independent national HTA, such as National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in UK, or a specialised committee for DRDs like the ultra-orphan medicines pathway in Scotland, efforts to establish a subspecialized HTA for DRDs in Saudi Arabia are in far sight (Grosios et al., 2010; Macaulay & Khatri, 2019). Ultimately, these complexities in the evaluation process are not supported by any collaboration at a national level.

The group emphasised the importance of adopting a holistic approach to managing patients with rare diseases. This includes evaluating non-health-related benefits, assessing the impact of the disease on caregivers, and implementing prevention strategies such as premarital genetic counselling and genetic counselling. These factors were identified as high priorities that ought to be considered and discussed in parallel to reimbursement drug decisions. Another key concern raised was the lack of comprehensive screening programmes, as current initiatives do not cover all rare diseases. Additionally, there is a scarcity of studies of the cost-effectiveness studies evaluating the benefits of screening programmes, particularly in the context of newly available high-cost DRDs (Dangouloff et al., 2021; FTI Consulting, 2025; Mohamed et al., 2020; Saudi Ministry of Health, 2025).

The persistent lack of representation in global, western-centered clinical trials remains a significant concern for the region. Nevertheless, several initiatives have recently been established to enhance collaboration and inclusion, such as the Clinical Trials Transformation Initiative (CTTI) (Rubin, 2021; Nature Publishing Group, 2024; King Faisal Specialist Hospital & Research Center, 2025). Notably, the Saudi Genome Programme aims to address knowledge gaps in genetic disease research. In addition, while the capabilities and infrastructure for research and evidence generation have grown tremendously in KSA, further efforts are needed especially in the tailored design of data collection platforms that would be required to support OBA and coverage with evidence generation agreements (CEA) (Valcárcel-Nazco et al., 2022; Vision, 2030, 2025).

Multiple ethical issues arise with DRDs, particularly regarding inequities in access within a fragmented healthcare system. A key concern raised in the discussion was the disparity in reimbursement, where a DRD may be reimbursed at one institution but not another, forcing patients to transition between institutions to gain access. However, these patients often struggle through the processes of eligibility and approvals. Even within a single institution, differences in patient eligibility criteria can create inconsistencies and inequities in access. Variability in valuation and reimbursement decisions for DRDs across countries has been well-documented. However, within a country with universal healthcare coverage, such disparities raise serious concerns (Szegedi et al., 2018; Vicente et al., 2021).

The high burden of chronic diseases, such as diabetes and cardiovascular diseases, further complicates the prioritisation of DRDs. Decision-makers are perplexed by the elements of limited time and resources for evaluating new treatments, overall budgetary constraints, coupled with evidence shortcomings, and marginal efficacy outcomes of some DRDs. As Sirrs et al relevantly stated, ‘Without high-quality evidence to assess value, we inadvertently prioritize patients with rare diseases over those with common diseases, creating conflict among ethical principles such as social utility, justice and the rule of rescue.' (Sirrs et al., 2023).

A key aspect of assessing a drug’s value is its ability to demonstrate clinically meaningful outcomes and patient-reported benefits, as well as the ability to interpret results and predict the reproducibility and durability of the benefits. Nevertheless, a Canadian report on DRDs highlighted that nearly half (45%) of DRDs recommended by the Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health (CADTH) offered slight to no improvement in clinical outcomes based on The Patented Medicine Prices Review Board (PMPRB)’s assessment (Government of Canada, 2025). However, in rare diseases the nature of the disease and the limited historical clinical data can make conventional studies with clinical endpoints difficult to impossible to conduct. Hence, the use of biomarkers and surrogate endpoints in clinical trials for rare diseases is becoming widely accepted and often necessary to expedite drug development and addressing unmet medical needs in rare diseases. Biomarkers and surrogate markers offer the advantages of detecting alterations before clinical symptoms appear which can accelerate the approval process for new therapies. However, relying on biomarkers is not without limitations as they may not fully capture the clinical benefits or reveal off-target adverse effects (Kakkis et al., 2015).

Regulatory authorities are putting in place programmes and guidance to support the development of biomarkers as tools for drug discovery such as the FDA Biomarker Qualification Programme. Through this programme, the FDA acknowledges the potential of biomarkers and surrogate endpoints in expediting drug development, particularly for rare diseases, by providing a structured pathway for their evaluation and qualification as reliable measures of drug efficacy and safety. Similarly, The European Medicine Agency (EMA) has issued the qualification of novel methodologies for drug development guidance to expedite the same efforts (EMA, 2025; FDA, 2025).

The economic impact of DRDs in Saudi Arabia presents distinct challenges due to the country’s economic dynamics and pharmaceutical landscape. Unlike regions with mature pharmaceutical industries, where investments in innovative therapies can generate economic returns, this concept is less applicable in countries where drug discovery and development are still in their early stages. In addition, the extended market exclusivity and life cycle of DRDs significantly prolong their financial burden compared to conventional drugs. This burden is further exacerbated by the higher incidence of certain rare diseases in Saudi Arabia, introducing uncertainty in cost projection and creates a high degree of unpredictability regarding its overall economic burden.

While the panel agreed on retaining the challenge related to the lack of an established national cost-effectiveness threshold for DRDs, which contributes to uncertainty in decision-making and difficulties in negotiations, further deliberation highlighted practical concerns regarding the feasibility of such a threshold. It was noted that establishing a fixed threshold for DRDs may be impractical, as cost-effectiveness studies are often not feasible due to limited data and methodological challenges. Additionally, economic modifiers, such as social value judgments, typically allow for exceptions to standard thresholds, emphasising the need for either an implicit or explicit framework to guide decision-making.4 For example in Japan, a 1.5 times higher cost-effectiveness threshold (¥7.5 million per quality-adjusted life year (QALY)) than the threshold for non-orphan drugs was adopted. However, this approach has been criticised for potentially deterring investment in research and development in the rare disease sector. That said, such concerns hold less relevance in the Saudi context at this time, given the current country’s limited role in drug development as previously described (FTI Consulting, 2025; Kamae et al., 2020).

In contrast, countries with complex, mixed-sources healthcare funding, such as the United States (U.S.), have adopted multi-modal solutions to overcome the difficulties in funding DRDs. In the U.S., government-funded health insurance programmes like Medicaid and Medicare, along with numerous non-profit organisations, play a dynamic role in facilitating access. Nevertheless, a proportion of patients remain unable to access DRDs due to high costs and coverage limitations. For example, the Institute for Clinical and Economic Review (ICER) has reported that high launch prices and payer restrictions continue to limit patient access, despite their availability through formal reimbursement channels (ICER, 2023). Similarly, Saudi Arabia must assess and establish a sustainable funding approach for DRDs while considering the country’s unique healthcare structure.

Meanwhile, single-payer healthcare systems like those in the UK and Australia have implemented specific funding mechanisms for DRDs. For instance, Australia’s Life Saving Drugs Programme (LSDP) provides funding for ultra-rare disease treatments, supported by risk-sharing agreements to manage uncertainties and cost. In the UK, the Innovative Medicines Fund (IMF) was introduced to expedite patient access to orphan drugs, like Zolgensma®, which was the drug that inspired the creation of the IMF (Department of Health and Aged Care, 2018; NICE, 2022; Ng et al., 2024).

A key strength of our study is the inclusion of diverse expert perspectives from across major healthcare institutions in KSA, which allowed us to describe highly detailed challenges, the structured modified Delphi with a roundtable discussion allowed conceptual insights not easily identified through surveys alone. However, the study also has limitations, although efforts were made to include a broad representation, the sample size was relatively small, and the participation rate in Round 1 was modest, potentially limiting the diversity of views during the initial theme generation. Furthermore, patient representatives were not included, as the study aimed to capture the institutional and policy-level challenges faced by decision-makers. While patient perspectives are valuable, they were outside the scope of this Delphi and are acknowledged as a limitation. Lastly, while the consensus statements reflect the current challenges, they may change as national polices are rapidly evolving.

Conclusion

Despite lack of standardised frameworks to evaluate DRDs and the multiple challenges described in this study, the healthcare system is facing a huge transformation project with one of its major goals being enhancing spending efficiency, with continued commitment to ensure patient access to life-saving and quality of life enhancing therapies. Therefore, research into aspects affecting decision making of high-cost drugs is vital to pave the way to logical and well-informed decision making. Thus far, a few initiatives and research have explored some of the issues related to rare disease drugs reimbursement in KSA. Therefore, this multi-expert perspective fills a portion of the knowledge gap and provides a critical overview on key challenges to be addressed in promoting equitable access to DRDs.
