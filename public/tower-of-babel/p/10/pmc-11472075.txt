Development of toxicity tests for Polyurethane foams
Authors: Julie Mallouhi, Enikő Hornyák-Mester, Miklós Varga, Béla Viskolcz, Béla Fiser, Emma Szőri-Dorogházi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolyurethanes (PUs) are a special class of polymeric materials that differ significantly from most other types of plastic in many aspects. They can be utilized in a wide range of products, including paints, coatings, elastomers, insulators, elastic fibers, and foams. PU foams are especially important as part of various convenience products. PU products often end up in landfills when they are no longer useful and can release toxic compounds when damaged by humans or microbes. Therefore, the ecotoxicological assessment of PU foams is essential. In this paper, five PU foam samples were prepared with different NCO indices (NCO-0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, and 1.2) and together with the Control sample (a previously tested non-toxic foam sample) were applied to develop toxicity tests procedure, while intentionally prepared Toxic foam has been used to verify the accuracy of the developed testing procedure. Two test organisms were successfully applied, Sinapis alba (white mustard) seeds and Escherichia coli (non-pathogenic) bacterial model organisms, and toxicity tests were adapted for the examination of PU-derived substances. Regarding Sinapis alba test, the highest NCO index (NCO-1.2) significantly reduced root length by 9.8 % compared to the Control sample. In the bacterial test, it was observed that the samples containing NCO-1.1 and NCO-1.2 had lower colony numbers (5.0 × 108 and 4.9 × 108 CFU/mL respectively) in comparison to the Control plate (9.6 × 108 CFU/mL). All in all, two toxicity tests were successfully adapted for PU foams, and both are applicable in their ecotoxicological assessment.Keywords: Polyurethane, Ecotoxicological assessment, Acute toxicity test, Sinapis alba, Escherichia coli

Introduction

Polyurethane (PU) is one of the most important polymers produced, within which the urethane bonds are formed by the chemical reaction between the OH (hydroxyl) groups of a polyol and NCO groups of an isocyanate and led to a chemically crosslinked structure [[1], [2], [3]]. The production of this polymer continues to grow at a rapid pace throughout the world, and because of its good mechanical, physical, biological, and chemical properties, lightweight, high insulating properties, and performance in absorbing energy, it is highly demanded in many applications [[4], [5], [6]]. PUs are ranked as the sixth most used plastic globally, and although millions of tons are produced worldwide but only 9 % of the material is recycled, 12 % is burned, and the rest ends up in landfills or the environment [7,8]. One of the most concerning issues is that this large amount of polyurethane waste accumulating in terrestrial and marine environments poses significant health and ecological risks for both humans and the environment [9,10]. The poor recycling, low repeated usage, long-term persistence in the environment, and potentially toxic chemical content of PUs can be harmful [[11], [12], [13]]. It is known that the raw materials used in PU production, such as isocyanates, polyols, and catalysts, can pose potential risks in terms of toxicity. For instance, isocyanates exhibit high toxicity levels until they react with other chemicals during the synthesis process. Organometallic compounds, alkali metal salts of carboxylic acids, phenols, and aliphatic and aromatic amines are some of the most common polyurethane catalysts [14]. Among these, short-chain aliphatic amines imply significant toxicity risk to humans, while exposure to aromatic amines can also result in a variety of illnesses such as acute skin sensitization, hepatoxicity, and carcinogenesis [15,16].

Major applications of PU include flexible and rigid foams, thermoplastics, elastomers, adhesives, and surface coatings [7,17,18]. To achieve special properties of PU, additives have to be used like flame retardants, surfactants, and fillers [3,19]. Flame retardants, including chlorinated and brominated, as well as organic phosphorus or nitrogen-based variants, can release toxic substances during the processing of PU waste [20,21]. Some of these chemicals are fat soluble, leading to their bioaccumulation in the fatty tissue and liver of aquatic organisms. Flame retardants in water also have adverse effects on aquatic organisms' ability to reproduce and survive including aquatic plants such as algae, worms, and daphnids. Furthermore, the presence of these toxic chemicals can disrupt hormonal balance in fish. If humans consume these contaminated organisms, it can lead to health issues [20].

To minimize the negative environmental effects of PU's, it is necessary to develop new polyurethane types that have lower carbon impact [22]. This can be achieved either by applying bio-based and nontoxic (i.e., made from renewable resources rather than fossil fuels) or biodegradable (i.e., their polymer structure which is willing to break down in the environment through physical, chemical, and biological processes) [23,24]. Before testing the biodegradability of PU, their toxicity needs to be assessed first. Because if a material is toxic, it will kill microorganisms before they can initiate the degradation process. Studying the ecotoxicity of a pollutant can provide information about its biological accessibility and help to understand its behavior in the environment [12,25]. The ecotoxicity of biodegradable PU foams (PUF) can be examined in leachate using alternative toxicity tests, such as test with Thamnocephalus platyurus model organism [1] or using other aquatic model organisms like Daphnia magna. The effects of exposing Daphnia magna to different PU products were previously studied, including foams containing cellulose as a filler additive and foams without cellulose. The results showed that leachate from non-cellulose-containing aliphatic PU materials exhibited higher toxicity to Daphnia magna compared to bio-based PU foams containing cellulose [16].

Ecotoxicological evaluation of plastic at various stages of abiotic and biotic degradation is a challenging issue that should be carried out not only on conventional aquatic test species in water leachates but also on organisms in soils [23]. In ecotoxicological assessments, the germination test is considered a short-term evaluation aimed at identifying acute toxicity by focusing on the germination rate [26]. Agricultural soils are prone to exposure to plastic which could affect the physiochemical properties and harm the organisms in the soil [27]. Plants are in direct contact with the soil therefore certain species of plants are often used in the evaluation of phytotoxicity [28], which refers to the adverse effects of chemical materials (fungicides, metals, or plastic waste) on plants. Phytotoxic symptoms can manifest in various parts of the plants, and assessment criteria commonly include measurements (e.g., height, root length) as well as visual estimations, such as deformations (e.g., curling, rolling, changes in size and shape of roots) and discoloration [29,30]. For instance, the residue of microplastic on the wheat affected negatively both above and below-ground parts [31]. The influence of biodegraded polyethylene (PE) on seed germination in several plant species (soya bean, safflower, groundnut, sesame, and sunflower) was investigated as well and it was observed that the pre-treated seeds exhibited a decline in the percentage of seed germination [32]. Some experiments have employed polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB), polylactic acid (PLA), and polypropylene (PP) plastics to examine their toxicity towards plants such as Sorghum saccharatum (a monocotyledonous plant) and Sinapsis alba (a dicotyledonous plant). It was found that Sinapis alba is more sensitive to plastic exposure compared to Sorghum saccharatum, indicating that these simple plants are excellent bioindicators for evaluating how plastics affect the early growth of plants [28]. The phytotoxic effects of Hydra-fill polyurethane foam in potting media were examined by pretreating the foam (washing with water) and observing its impact on radish (Raphanus sativus L.) and tomato (Lycopersicon esculentum Mill.) seed germination. The results indicated that using unwashed foam reduced root growth in plants, whereas washing the foam before use significantly enhanced plant growth [33]. There are several bioassays available for toxicity evaluation, utilizing organisms such as crustaceans, algae, fish, and rats, which are known for their sensitivity to various toxic substances. However, these methods have several limitations, including being expensive, requiring specialized equipment, and expertise in maintaining the model organisms, and consuming a significant amount of time to obtain results. In comparison, bacteria offer a relatively quick and cost-efficient alternative [34]. Therefore, bacterial tests belong to the simpler, faster, and less expensive detection methods, as bacteria can grow quickly in basic specified media [34]. Previous studies have successfully demonstrated that acute toxicity tests utilizing Vibrio fischeri provide sufficiently sensitive, rapid, and cost-effective detection compared to tests using higher organisms [34]. Recently, Gram-negative Escherichia coli has been effectively used in toxicological investigations on a variety of environmental contaminants. This model was used to test the toxicity of polystyrene particles of different sizes, and it was observed that the E. coli cell viability decreased as polystyrene concentrations increased, showing that plastic exposure inhibited cell growth [35].

Many studies have investigated the degradation of polymers (polyethylene, polystyrene, polyvinyl chloride, polypropylene, polyethylene terephthalate) [6,32,36,37] and toxicity tests were performed, as well as studies were carried out about how these materials affect different organisms, but limited results have been obtained regarding the impact of polyurethane foams on organisms. Therefore, in this study, we focus on investigating PUF, considered as the most widely consumed type of PU.

To examine the potentially toxic substances that can be formed during PUF degradation and aging of the material two different methods have been adopted and applied in this work. The toxicological assessment of different polyurethane foam samples was carried out using Sinapis alba (white mustard) seeds for liquid sample testing (e.g. dilution water used for soaking the PU foam) and bacteria-based toxicity test, where a piece of PU foam was soaked directly in the medium used for the growing of the bacteria.

Materials and methods

Preparation of flexible polyurethane foam

The precursors for the production of flexible polyurethane foam samples were Ongronat TR4040 (methylene-diphenyl-diisocyanate isomer mixture, Wanhua-BorsodChem, Kazincbarcika, Hungary) and Ongropur FFP-303 (polyether type polyol premix, Wanhua-BorsodChem, Kazincbarcika, Hungary). Catalysts and additional additives like blowing agents and surfactants were mixed into a base polyol system (Alcupol F2831, Repsol, Madrid, Spain) to create the applied polyol (Table 1).

Five polyurethane foam samples were prepared by mixing the precursors, and the raw material ratios were determined in a way to achieve 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, and 1.2 isocyanate indices (e.g. NCO-0.8). The isocyanate index is the ratio of the total number of NCO groups to the sum of the OH groups of the polyol premix which includes additives (e.g. water) within the reaction mixture. Previously, a Control sample (NCO-0.9) was also prepared, tested and it was found to be non-toxic. Thus, it was used as a reference to evaluate the toxicity of the newly prepared samples. The polyurethane samples were cut into small pieces 1 × 1 × 1 cm3, the weight range was 0.05 ± 0.01g and these PU foam cubes were used for the toxicity tests. Characterization of the structure of these polyurethane foams was carried out in previous studies [3].

Acute toxicity test using Sinapis alba (white mustard) seed

Sinapis alba was chosen as a test organism to evaluate the toxicity of PU foam samples due to its agricultural significance and its sensitivity to changes in the environment [25]. The toxicity tests were carried out by using a special water-based liquid. A solution of 1 L was prepared which contained 20 mM of CaCl2.2H2O, 5.0 mM of MgSO4, 7.5 mM of NaHCO3, and 0.75 mM of KCl [38]. To test the PU samples, the foam cubes were soaked in 15 mL of the prepared special water-based solution in plastic centrifuge tubes for 24 h, 48 h, or 72 h, respectively (Fig. S1). Thereafter, the cubes were removed, and the remaining soaking liquid was used for the tests (5 mL/plate).

In the meantime, for each sample, three glass Petri plates were prepared, in the following way: circular shape filter paper was placed into the plates and heat sterilized together with the Petri dish at 100 °C for 10 h. Then, 5 mL of the prepared water-based solution or 5 mL of the soaking liquid of the tested PU foams were put into Petri dishes using a 5 mL range automated pipette. After that, mustard seeds were distributed on the plates (30 seeds for each plate, (Fig. 1/a).The plates were covered with a glass cover and left on the desk for approximately 2 h for a bit to the rise of the seeds and thus, to avoid their shift from their arranged position in the Petri dish. Then, the Petri dishes were placed in the incubator for 72 h in the dark at a temperature of 20 °C. Thereafter the root length of the seeds was measured (Fig. 1/b).

Sinapis alba seed test: a) arrangement of the seeds in the plate at the start of the test, b) germinated seeds after incubation, and measurement of the length of the growing seed root (the numbers on the ruler represent cm).

The average root length of the longest 25 seeds for each sample and the length of the roots of the Control sample were compared. Reference plates were made as well, for which only the pure prepared water-based solution was used, and the seeds were treated in the aforementioned way (as described for the plates with the soaking liquid of the PU foams).

Reference plates served to verify that the test was performed methodologically in a correct way and that the seeds were appropriate for germination. If the root length is shorter compared to what has been measured for the Control seeds, the tested liquid (the soaking dilution in which the foam cube was held) is considered toxic. The greater the difference, the more toxic is the tested PU foam which means that potentially toxic chemicals are released during the soaking of the foam.

Bacteria-based toxicity test in the liquid phase using Escherichia coli model organism

To determine bacterial viability, conventional techniques such as plate counts and different dilutions of the initial bacterial suspension are utilized [39]. In this experiment, Escherichia coli DH5α was used as a model organism because its growth is fast (E. coli divides every 20 min) [40]. Only live cells of bacteria that have been plated on a growth-supporting media will grow, multiply, and form colonies. For this reason, colony-forming units (CFU) per mL of the sample are a basic unit for counting viable bacteria in the culture. The colonies will merge if there are too many viable cells on a plate, making them hard to count. When there is not enough space for all the cells to form colonies, this makes the count inaccurate, and that is why it is important to make dilution from the bacterial suspensions, but at the same time, one has to take care to avoid over-dilution and thus, formation of too few cells or colonies on the plate. So, only plates with colonies between 30 and 300 should be taken into account [41,42]. In the current study, a serial dilution was used to decrease the bacterial concentration to the level needed for the particular test which makes it easier to count the bacteria (Fig. S2).

Sterilization of PU foams for bacterial tests

LB (Lysogeny broth) medium was prepared for the bacterial tests (10 g tryptone, 5 g yeast extract, 10 g NaCl, 1000 mL distilled water). Then, 35 mL LB was dosed into Erlenmeyer flasks. One set of the PU foam cubes containing flasks together with the LB medium was autoclaved for sterilization of the foams (121 °C, 15 psi for 45 min) after the flasks were plugged with a cotton plug. The other half of the flasks (with the foam) remained unsterilized. This arrangement served not just for sterilization but to investigate whether high wet heat can promote the release of some chemical compounds from the foam (mimicking the aging process). The flasks were then left on the laboratory bench and cooled to room temperature.

Preparation of LB agar plates

LB agar was made by adding 15 g agar to 1000 mL of LB medium and applied for the colony counting method. Petri plates were prepared by pouring the LB agar into the plates in the biological cabinet and leaving them to solidify overnight.

Preparing the starter culture

Inoculation of the starter LB suspension was performed using a sterile inoculation loop and putting a single colony of E. coli into the LB medium. The starter was incubated in a shaker incubator overnight at 37 °C and 160 rpm.

Optical density measurement (OD600) and inoculation of the flasks

To measure the concentration of bacteria or other cells within a liquid sample, the OD600 measurement technique can be used [39,43]. The OD600 was measured by a BMG Labtech Clariostar Microplate reader (Fig. S3). According to the measured optical density of the starter (overnight) culture dilution was made with sterile LB medium to get bacterial suspension with OD600 = 0.15 optical density. The success of this dilution was remeasured and after that ten-fold dilution series (101-106) was made from the bacterial suspension with OD600 = 0.15 (Fig. S4). From the 104 dilutions (this dilution was chosen as a working bacterial solution) of the prepared dilution series, 30 μl was used to inoculate the flasks containing the PU foam to be tested. To determine the initial cell concentration, namely, to determine which CFU/mL corresponds to the working bacterial solution, 100 μl of the 106 dilutions was taken and spread on the top of five LB agar plates (Figure S2 and Figure S4). After overnight incubation, the formed colonies were counted.

Results and discussion

Acute toxicity test

The Sinapis alba test is valid if 90 % of the seeds grow on the reference plate, and the tested sample is not toxic if the roots are nearly reached the same length as on the Control [44]. Based on the seed test, the root lengths for all samples have been measured and compared (Table 2 and Table S1). The root length of Sinapis alba for the Control plate fell within a similar range as observed in the reference plates, where the seeds were germinated using the standard water-based solution. But in the case of the tested foams where the soaking solution of the foams was used for germination, as the soaking time of the foams increased the length of the root slightly decreased. This indicates that the toxicity is higher when the tested foams are soaked for a longer period (i.e. 72 h).

In this demonstration, it was also seen, that applying 24 h or 48 h for foam soaking was possibly too short for the release of toxic compounds from the foams, so the released amount was not enough to trigger a toxic effect. From all the data the highest sensitivity of Sinapis alba was observed for the sample with the highest isocyanate index (NCO-1.2), where the root growth was reduced in relation to the Control foam (4.1 cm for the Control, 3.7 cm for NCO-1.2, respectively, with reduction percentage 9.8 % (Table S1)) applying 72 h of soaking. It was also noticed that the largest difference between the 24-h and 72-h soaking time was found for the NCO-1.2 foam, with a difference of approximately 11.8 % (Table S1 and Fig. S7).

For the Acute Toxicity Test the germination index was calculated according to equation (1) (see Material and Method). The germination index (GI%) values differed based on soaking times and the composition of the samples. At 24 h, GI% ranged from 91.0 % to 93.3 % for all samples. After 48 h, the GI% ranged from 83.3 % to 94.3 %. While at 72 h, the GI% for all samples varied between 85.3 % and 92.0 % (Table S2). The lower germination rate was typically observed in tests conducted with PUF samples.

Therefore, it can be concluded that the tested foam compositions did not affect noticeably seed germination, but the root length slightly varied depending on the chemical composition and concentration of the substances present in the soaking solution of the polymers. Similar findings were presented formerly when polylactide (PLA), polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB), and petroleum-derived non-biodegradable polypropylene (PP) were used in germination tests. When the impact of different types of plastics on root growth was compared, it was observed that PHB and PLA had a more significant effect compared to PP, and all these polymers cause a reduction in the root length at higher concentrations of polymer particles used in soil [28].

The primary reason for the differences in Acute Toxicity Test results may be the different compositions of the PUFs tested. Of the two main components used in the formulation of polyurethanes (the polyol mixes and the component containing isocyanate), the isocyanate component may have a greater influence on the toxicity of the product. This is because the components of the polyol mix (catalysts, blowing agents, surfactants, and flame retardants) individually appear in smaller percentages, while the isocyanate component indeed constitutes 90 % isocyanate [45]. Therefore, the isocyanate index can significantly affect the toxicity of the final product, and the main toxic effect is likely induced by the presence of more isocyanate (e.g., NCO-1.2).

Bacteria-based toxicity test in the liquid phase

The bacterial toxicity tests were also performed to test the effect of the prepared flexible polyurethane foam samples on microorganisms (NCO-0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, and 1.2) (Fig. 3). The percentage of colony number of Control samples in comparison to the colony number of the studied foams with different isocyanate indices (NCO-0.8, NCO-0.9, NCO-1.0, NCO-1.1, and NCO-1.2) was calculated (Fig. 3). When the samples were autoclaved, no colonies were observed on the plates because by autoclaving the PUF becomes sterile (Fig. 3, Table S3). In case when the samples were autoclaved and inoculum of E. coli was added, the colony number of the Control plate and the Reference plate was comparable (Table S3, Reference1 (LB + E. coli) and the Control (Autoclaved foam + E. coli)) presuming that only non-toxic materials released from the foam into the bacterial growing media during heat treatment (autoclaving) of the foam.

Percentage of the average colony number for the tested samples (Control foam, NCO-0.8, NCO-0.9, NCO-1.0, NCO-1.1, and NCO-1.2). The colony number of the Control plate is considered 100 %. The lack of columns (the consequence of sterilization) is indicated by using red arrows.

Formation of the colonies can occur exclusively in the case when there is no toxic material released from the tested foam during this sterilization process. Similarly, comparable bacterial concentrations were observed for both NCO-0.8 PUF and Control when autoclaved foam was examined with E. coli inoculum. This suggests that this isocyanate index did not inhibit the tested bacterial strain (Fig. S8). For NCO-0.9 and NCO-1.0 foams, a slightly reduced cell concentration was determined, but samples with NCO-1.1 and NCO-1.2 had the lowest colony numbers compared to other samples with the same condition (Autoclaved foam + E. coli). The bacterial concentration for the NCO-1.1 and NCO-1.2 was 5.0 × 108 and 4.9 × 108 CFU/mL respectively, which means 48 % and 49 % lower colony numbers than that of the Control sample, respectively (Tables S3 and S4).

The Non-autoclaved PUFs were also tested. Since the PUF itself is not sterile and when it is not toxic, microorganisms from the surface of the foam cube can grow in the used medium, so the liquid will turn turbid (Fig. 4/a). However, primarily it is not known whether the tested foam is toxic or not, a flask with Non-autoclaved foam was also inoculated with E. coli. It turned out that Non-autoclaved Control and tested PUF samples (NCO-0.8 - 1.2) are non-toxic since bacteria could grow and numerous bacterial colonies with different morphology were counted on the plates belonging to these samples (as shown in a representative plate of NCO-0.8 sample, Fig. 4/b).

a) Difference in the look of inoculated LB medium before and after incubation of sample NCO-0.8 (Non-autoclaved NCO-0.8 PUF + E. coli), b) Colony-forming units on the plate with sample NCO-0.8 inoculated with E. coli (Non-autoclaved NCO-0.8 PUF + E. coli).

The bacterial concentration of the samples NCO-0.9, NCO-1.0, and NCO-1.2 was nearly the same as that of the Control sample ∼6.3 × 108 CFU/mL, but the reduction in the colony number was obvious in the case of NCO-1.1 which is 35 % lower than Control sample (Fig. 3, Tables S3 and S4).

These results are in good agreement with other findings, where E. coli bacterium was also used in toxicology studies. As it was observed exposure of E. coli to polystyrene could allow bacteria to grow but the viability of E. coli cells decreased with increasing concentrations of polymer [35]. In another study, the cytotoxic effect of open-cell polyurethane foams with different isocyanate indices was investigated on the keratinocyte cell line (HaCaTas). This study showed that the isocyanate index strongly influences the chemical structure of polyurethane foams, which is also reflected in cytotoxicity. These results provide important information for the design of polyurethane foams when used e.g. as composite matrices in biomedical applications [46]. All our results were verified using a Reference (reference plates spread from the flask containing only sterile LB medium and the appropriate amount of E. coli overnight starter suspension) to have evidence that the applied bacterial suspension includes viable cells and that the LB agar plates are appropriate for growing the bacteria.

Verifying the sensitivity of the solid PUF testing methods by using a toxic foam

-Seed test

Seed test

Moderate toxicity of the PUF samples was demonstrated with previous experiments, and the toxic effect depended on the time used for soaking the foams and on the isocyanate index as well. To verify that the applied method is adequate for the demonstration of the toxic effect of the solid foams, if any, a Toxic sample was used, and the previously presented seed test was carried out. In this case, a Control foam (the same as was used in previous tests), a newly prepared NCO-1.0 foam, and an intentionally toxic foam (named Toxic foam) were used. The Toxic foam was made by soaking the NCO-1.0 foam into a commonly used form separator (GORAPUR LK 8779-61 E, consisting of hydrocarbons, C9-C10, n-alkanes, isoalkanes, cyclics, <2 % aromatics) applied in flexible polyurethane production. This Toxic foam served to validate the sensitivity of the adapted tests for the polyurethane foam.

Based on the information sheet of the used foam separator it releases agents harmful to aquatic life (flammable liquid and vapor, may cause an allergic skin reaction, drowsiness, or dizziness) with long-lasting effects as well. After soaking the foam cubes in this toxic material, excess liquid was squeezed from the foam cubes, and for several minutes they were left on the laboratory desk and dried. The average root length of the seeds grown in the presence of the solution prepared by soaking Control foam, a new NCO-1.0 foam, and the Toxic foam in water-based liquid (with the same composition as used previously in this work) was calculated (Table S5).

The root length of the seeds in case of applying soaking dilution from the Control and new NCO-1.0 foams was in the same range when a 24 or 48 h soaking period was used. The root length was between ∼3.7 and 4.1 cm, while in the case of the Toxic sample, the length of the root decreased significantly. The reduction was the most obvious in the case of the 24 h soaking time, since in comparison to the Control the average root length was just around 60 % (Fig. 5, Table S6).

Percentage of the average root length distribution depends on the toxicity of the tested polyurethane foam (PUF). PUF samples are Control, a new NCO-1.0, and Toxic foam. Used soaking times were 24, 48, or 72 h respectively. The percentage of average root length exposed to the Control is considered 100 %.

It was also observed that the difference in the root length (measured for the roots from the Control and the Toxic foam's plate) decreased as the soaking time increased. The root length difference between the Control and the Toxic foam from 40 % (using 24 h soaking time) decreased to 22.5 % (after 72 h soaking period) (Fig. 5, Table S6). The reason behind this phenomenon is the presence of volatile materials in the used form separator liquid. Therefore, as the incubation time was longer, the quantity of the toxic compound in the foam (and consequently in the soaking liquid) decreased, presumably due to the evaporation of the volatile toxic compounds.

The germination index (GI%) was also determined for these tests for all samples and at different soaking times. The GI% ranged from 85.3 % to 92 % at 24 h, from 85.3 % to 93.3 % at 48 h, and from 87.6 % to 96.6 % at 72 h (Table S7), which means that the tests were evaluable, and the results obtained with the Toxic foam were relevant.

By performing experiments with the aforementioned setup, the sensitivity of the mustard seed model organism was shown. With this test, adapted to the toxicity evaluation of PUFs, it was not only possible to demonstrate the toxic behavior of a tested material but also its potential volatile properties (Fig. 5). It can be concluded that this test is sufficiently sensitive to detect the toxicity of solid PUFs, more specifically the toxicity of chemical compounds used for polyurethane preparation, or any chemicals released from the foams, if present.

-Bacteria-based test

Bacteria-based test

Similarly, to the mustard seed test, the sensitivity of the bacterial test was also verified. The changes in the LB medium are visible when there is bacterial growth (Fig. S9, Non-autoclaved Control PUF + E. coli before and after incubation) since due to bacterial division the used liquid medium becomes turbid. As explained earlier, the Non-autoclaved foams are not sterile, and microorganisms existing on the surface of the foam can easily divide, causing the initially transparent LB medium to become opaque when incubated at 37 °C in a basic bacterial medium. In the case of the Toxic Non-autoclaved foam, there was no change in the color and appearance of the used medium before and after incubation (Fig. S9, Non-autoclaved Toxic PUF + E. coli). Moreover, there was no turbidity observed even if a certain amount of E. coli was added to the Non-autoclaved Toxic foam. This means, that the chemical used for making the sample toxic releases toxic compounds into the liquid medium, which in turn kills all the microorganisms used for the test (including those naturally occurring on the surface of the foam, and the extra added E. coli bacterium as well).

In the case of using the autoclave sterilization process, after incubation of the flasks, no bacterial growth was observed, resulting in the LB medium remaining transparent regardless of whether Toxic or another foam was used. This was confirmed by spreading the required amount of these incubated liquids onto LB agar plates, where no colonies were formed (Fig. 6 and Fig. S10).

Thus, it can be concluded that the developed and applied E. coli-based test procedure is also sensitive enough to detect the toxicity of polyurethane foam samples. When evaluating the effectiveness of the E. coli inhibition test, it was found that this method is not only sufficiently sensitive but also rapid, providing results within 24 h, which is a significant advantage compared to other tests [39], which highlights the benefits of bacterial-based tests.

Conclusion

Plastic consumption is steadily growing, however, the accumulation of plastic waste in the environment poses significant dangers to all forms of life. Despite polyurethanes being highly demanded polymers, only a small percent of the produced polymers is recycled, and the rest are disposed of in landfills causing the release of potentially toxic chemicals, that can adversely affect the environment. To study the toxicity of PU foams caused by degradation and aging, two different methods have been successfully adopted and applied. Five PUF samples with different isocyanate indices (0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, and 1.2) were prepared and used for examination of the potential toxicity of the foams by applying Sinapis alba seed test and Escherichia coli-based test. In the case of the Sinapis alba-based test, all samples have been compared with a Control foam. The prepared samples (NCO-0.8 to NCO-1.2) did not express toxicity after 24 h soaking period used since there was no relevant decrease in the root length compared to the Control. However, by comparing the root length of the samples with different NCO indices to that of the Control foam, a slight decrease can be observed when foams were soaked for 72 h. The most obvious decrease was noticed in the case of NCO-1.2. In the case of using an intentionally made Toxic foam, the average root length was lower than the Control for all soaking times used which means that the test was sensitive enough to detect toxicity.

In the case of the bacterial tests, the results showed that when a sample is sterilized, no colonies are observed on the plates due to the heat and pressure which effectively eliminates all microorganisms present on the treated material. When Autoclaved Control PUF was applied alongside the E. coli inoculum, the bacterial concentration was similar to that, when only LB and E. coli were used, without any PUF. This indicated that the prepared Control PUF can be sterilized by autoclaving without releasing any toxic substances from the foams. Similarly, the Control PUF samples without sterilization (autoclaving) had no impact on the growth of E. coli. The sample NCO-0.8 in both cases (Autoclaved PUF + E. coli and Non-autoclaved PUF + E. coli), as well as the Non-autoclaved PUF (NCO-0.9, NCO-1.0 and NCO-1.2) + E. coli were non-toxic as bacteria could grow on the plates. In the case of the Autoclaved PUF (NCO-0.9, NCO-1.0, and NCO-1.2) + E. coli, the cell concentration was reduced to about half of the cell concentration of the Control sample. However, the sample NCO-1.1 had the lowest colony number in both conditions.

The opposite was observed for the Toxic sample since no bacteria could grow when the (Non-autoclaved Toxic PUF + E. coli) was tested. So, the bacterial test is also sensitive enough to detect toxic compounds and their volatile behavior, if any. It can be concluded that the methods adapted to solid PUF, using different model organisms, provide reliable and reproducible results for determining the toxicity of polyurethane foam samples.
