Ratiometric nitric oxide fluorescent probe in the second near infrared window for the visualization of inflammation
Authors: Ruixun Chen, Ranran Guo, Zhiyuan Wang, Weiping Yang, Jiachun Lin, Yutao Zeng, Ye Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nitric oxide (NO), a key inflammatory mediator, plays dual roles in both immune response and inflammatory progression. Real-time, dynamic monitoring of NO levels in vivo is therefore essential for elucidating related pathological pathways. To address this challenge, we developed a ratiometric fluorescent probe, CSQD@PIB, with emission in near-infrared-II window (NIR-II) for the visualization of NO in inflammation models. This probe integrates two luminescent components: lead sulfide core-shell quantum dots (CSQD) emitting in the 1500-1700 nm band as an invariant internal reference for spatial localization and a NO-responsive conjugated polymer (PIB) exhibiting specific fluorescence enhancement in the 1000-1300 nm band as a quantitative sensing unit. By correlating the intensity ratio of the two emission channels, the probe enables simultaneous, real-time visualization and quantification of NO in the inflammatory site. Experiments demonstrate that CSQD@PIB can monitor the dynamics of NO generation in various pathophysiological processes in vivo, providing a reliable, self-calibrating imaging tool with high signal-to-noise ratio for NO-related studies in living mammals.

Introduction

Nitric oxide (NO), a pivotal signaling molecule, regulates a wide spectrum of physiological processes, including the cardiovascular, immune, nervous, reproductive and endocrine systems [1,2]. In inflammation, NO as a key inflammatory mediator, exerts dual effects of promoting immune responses and exacerbating tissue damage [3]. NO derived from inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) in macrophages and other immune cells in the inflammatory microenvironment regulates vascular permeability, immune cell recruitment, and cytokine release, thereby profoundly influencing the initiation, progression, and resolution of inflammation [4]. Consequently, achieving real-time, precise and non-invasive visualization of endogenous NO dynamics in vivo holds great significance for the deep understanding of its complex pathophysiological mechanisms and developing diagnostic and therapeutic strategies for related diseases [5].

In recent years, optical imaging, particularly fluorescence imaging in the first near infrared window (NIR-I, 700-900 nm) [6], has provided powerful tools for the non-invasive observation of biological processes [7]. However, tissue autofluorescence and light scattering limit tissue penetration depth and compromise the signal-to-noise ratio and quantitative accuracy of imaging. Near-infrared-II (NIR-II, 1000-1700 nm) fluorescence imaging, utilizing longer emission wavelengths, significantly reduces tissue scattering and autofluorescence, thereby offering higher spatial resolution, deeper tissue penetration and superior signal-to-noise ratio, making it an ideal window for deep-tissue, high-fidelity imaging [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. Despite the significant advantages of NIR-II imaging, developing fluorescent probes emitting in this window while exhibiting sensitive and specific responses to specific biological molecules like NO remains challenging [13,14]. Most reported probes show fluorescence signals that are easily influenced by non-specific factors such as local probe concentration and instrument fluctuations, hampering accurate quantification [15,16]. Ratiometric fluorescent probes with duplex emissions can effectively correct for such interferences by calculating the intensity ratio of the two channels, providing a reliable strategy for the quantitative analysis of target molecules in vivo [17,18].

Motivated by the considerations above, we designed and constructed a novel NIR-II ratiometric fluorescent probe, CSQD@PIB, for sensitive, specific and quantitative visualization of NO in vivo. This probe employs lead sulfide core-shell quantum dots (CSQD) with an emission peak at 1615 nm as an internal reference. Simultaneously, a conjugated polymer PIB, produces enhanced fluorescence in the 1000-1300 nm channel upon specific reaction with NO. Superior spectral separation leads to improved accuracy in the quantification of NO in vivo. Imaging performance of this probe was validated in three typical inflammatory disease models (skin inflammation, inflammatory bowel disease, and acute hepatitis). The results demonstrate that CSQD@PIB not only enables non-invasive and dynamic monitoring of NO level changes in different organs, but also effectively distinguishes subtle differences in inflammation severity through ratiometric imaging, providing a powerful imaging tool for the investigations of NO-related pathophysiological processes.

Results and discussion

Characterization of CSQD@PIB

The synthetic route for the conjugated polymer PIB is shown in Fig. S1. Condensation polymerization of monomers M1 and M2 was achieved through Stille coupling catalyzed by Pd(PPh3)4 to yield polymer P1. Subsequently, the nitro groups in P1 were reduced to amino groups with iron powder to yield the target product, PIB. In this conjugated polymer, 4,4,9,9-tetrakis(4-hexylphenyl)-Indaceno[1,2-b:5,6-b’]dithiophene group works as an electron donor and 5,6-diaminoBenzo[c][1,2,5]thiadiazole works as recognition group for NO. Owing to the electron donating effect of amino groups on benzothiadiazole, fluorescence of the conjugated polymer in the near infrared spectral region is quenched. After the cyclization reaction with NO, [1,2,5]thiadiazolo[3,4-f]benzotriazole group with strong electron withdrawing effect is formed, which greatly red-shifts the fluorescence emission to the NIR-II region (Fig. 1a) [15,18]. Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectroscopy confirmed the successful synthesis of PIB (Fig. S2a). Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) determined its molecular weight to be 59.5 kDa with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 1.81 (Fig. S2b). Ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectroscopy showed that PIB has a maximum absorption at 367 nm, which exhibited a red shift upon reaction with NO under acidic conditions (Fig. S3). Its fluorescence emission peaked at 924 nm, with the maximum emission wavelength extending to 1200 nm, and displayed a significant enhancement after reaction with NO (Fig. S4). CSQDs were synthesized according to a previously reported method [19]. Subsequently, an amphiphilic polymer, oleylamine grafted polyacrylic acid (OPA) [20], was used to transfer the hydrophobic CSQD and PIB into aqueous phase, enabling their self-assembly in water to form the CSQD@PIB nanocomposite probe (Fig. 1a). Dynamic light scattering results showed that the hydrodynamic diameters of CSQD and CSQD@PIB were 12.78 nm (PDI 0.156) and 17.14 nm (PDI 0.096), respectively, indicating good dispersity (Fig. S5). Transmission electron microscopy images visually demonstrated that PIB coats the surface of CSQD (Fig. 1b). To verify the specific response to NO, CSQD@PIB was incubated with a NO donor (DEA NONOate) at concentrations of 0-70 μm. Spectral analysis revealed that as the NO concentration increased, the UV-Vis absorption spectrum of the probe exhibited a red-shift. Meanwhile, the fluorescence intensity in the 1000–1300 nm channel (Channel IIa, NO-responsive channel) showed a concentration-dependent enhancement, while the fluorescence intensity in the 1500–1700 nm channel (Channel IIb, reference channel) remained essentially unchanged (Fig. 1e and f). NIR-II imaging results were consistent with this trend (Fig. 1h). Quantitative calculation of the fluorescence intensity ratio (FIIa/FIIb) between channels IIa and IIb revealed a strong linear relationship between this ratio and NO concentration within the 0-70 μm range, with a R2 of 0.9854 (Fig. 1g), demonstrating that CSQD@PIB can be used for the quantitative detection of NO. Limit of detection (LOD) was calculated to be 0.87 μm. Although LOD of CSQD@PIB is not the lowest among NIR-II NO probes, outstanding spectral separation between NO-responsive unit and internal reference improves quantification accuracy in the in vivo NO imaging (Table S1).

Then response kinetics of CSQD@PIB toward NO was investigated. Time-dependent fluorescence intensity of NO-responsive channel was monitored at different NO concentrations (5 and 10 μm). As shown in Fig. S6, the curves fitted the kinetics characteristics of second-order reaction and time required for fluorescence intensity to reach half of its maximum value (t1/2) was inversely proportional to the initial NO concentration. It is also widely reported that the reaction between NO and 5,6-diaminobenzo[c] [1,2,5]thiadiazole demonstrates a pH-dependent kinetics [18]. Therefore, additional experiments were performed to evaluate the response of the probe to NO at different pH values. As the pH decreased, the response rate of the probe to NO gradually accelerated (Fig. S7). At pH 5, the probe signal reached its maximum within approximately 20 min, whereas at pH 6 or 7, it took about 40 or 60 min, respectively. At pH 8 or 9, even longer times were required. Importantly, the physiological pH environment in which the probe is used ranges from 5 to 7. Within this range, the probe exhibited a relatively fast response to NO, confirming its good applicability under physiological conditions. As an internal reference, the fluorescence stability of CSQDs in physiological environments is critical to the accuracy of NO quantification. Fluorescence of CSQDs was measured under different physiological environments. The results (Fig. S8) showed that NO concentration (0-70 μm), ROS (H2O2 concentration 0-0.2 mm), pH (5.5-7.4) and temperature (37-40 °C) had no significant effect on the fluorescence of CSQDs, demonstrating the fluorescence stability of the internal reference under physiological conditions. Next, the influence of tissue depth on NO quantitative analysis was further explored. Using Intralipid to simulate biological tissues, it was revealed that fluorescence intensity of CSQDs decreased with increasing thickness of Intralipid (Fig. S9), which was attributed to scattering. However, fluorescence intensity of PIB also decreased with increasing thickness of Intralipid and the intensity ratio of the two channels remained essentially unchanged. These results indicate that although increasing tissue depth leads to a decrease in fluorescence intensity, it does not affect the quantitative analysis of NO concentration, further demonstrating the advantage of ratiometric fluorescent probes for in vivo quantification of NO. Since tissue depth has no influence on fluorescence ratio, the standard curve established in vitro can be directly applied to in vivo measurements. Photostability of CSQD@PIB was investigated with continuous irradiation using a 660 nm laser at a power density of 0.2 W cm−2 for 1 h. As shown in Fig. S10, fluorescence intensities of PIB and CSQDs remained above 90% of their initial values throughout the irradiation period and the intensity ratio stayed unchanged. These results demonstrate that CSQD@PIB possesses excellent photostability.

To evaluate the selectivity of CSQD@PIB towards NO, responsiveness to some other biological species was characterized, including GSH, Cys, homocysteine (Hcy), NO2−, NO3−, ClO−, H2O2, ONOO−, Fe2+ and methylglyoxal (MGO). As shown in Fig. 1j, NO triggered a significant increase in IIa/IIb ratio, while all other species showed negligible responses. Notably, although the reaction between MGO and 5,6-diaminobenzo[c] [1,2,5]thiadiazole has been reported previously [21], the reaction kinetics of MGO is much slower than that of NO. Accordingly, no obvious increase in the fluorescence intensity of CSQD@PIB in NIR-IIa channel was observed during the test period (30 min). These results demonstrated the high selectivity of CSQD@PIB toward NO. Long-term stability of dispersion and fluorescence was studied. CSQD@PIB was dispersed in physiological media (PBS or DMEM with 10% FBS) and kept at 37 °C for a week. As shown in Fig. S11, hydrodynamic diameter remained stable at approximately 19 nm and IIa/IIb fluorescence ratio remained constant with no significant fluctuation. These results demonstrate that CSQD@PIB exhibits excellent colloidal and fluorescence stability under physiological conditions, ensuring its reliability for subsequent cellular and in vivo applications.

NIR-II imaging of NO levels in polarized macrophages

NO is a critical immune signaling molecule that is intimately involved in the immune response, playing a central role in regulating macrophage polarization. When macrophages are activated and polarize into a pro-inflammatory phenotype, NO is generated in substantial amounts [22,23]. To validate the performance of CSQD@PIB in quantifying intracellular NO levels, polarized Raw264.7 macrophages were used. Macrophages were cultured in complete medium containing 10 μg mL−1 lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 2 and 6 h, respectively. Immunofluorescence analysis showed that the expression of inducible NO synthase (iNOS) and NO levels (detected with a commercial fluorescent probe, DAF-FM DA) increased with prolonged polarization time (Fig. 2a and b), indicating a time-dependent upregulation of intracellular NO synthesis. Flow cytometry further confirmed that the mean fluorescence intensity of DAF-FM DA increased with polarization time (Fig. 2c), while the expression of CD86 (M1 polarization marker) showed a similar trend (Fig. S12). In this case, we subsequently evaluated the performance of CSQD@PIB to detect intracellular NO levels in polarized macrophages. First, Raw264.7 cells were incubated with different concentrations of CSQD@PIB for 24 h, and cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Results showed that cell viability remained above 90% at all concentrations (Fig. S13a) when the concentration of CSQD@PIB was less than 0.2 mg mL−1. When incubating macrophages with CSQD@PIB at 0.2 mg mL−1, NIR-IIb microscopy images under 660 nm excitation revealed that cellular fluorescence intensity increased gradually with prolonged incubation time and peaked at 30 min, followed by a stable state (Fig. S13b). This indicates that the cellular internalization reaches saturation at approximately 30 min. Subsequently, Raw264.7 cells incubated with CSQD@PIB for 30 min were subjected to dual-channel (IIa and IIb) NIR-II imaging (Fig. 2d). Imaging results revealed comparable fluorescence intensity in IIb channel (internal reference signal) between experimental and control groups. In contrast, in IIa channel (NO-responsive signal), fluorescence signals in cells treated with LPS for 2 and 6 h showed a gradient increase compared to the unstimulated control group (Fig. 2e). Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity ratio within regions of interest (ROI) revealed a significant increase in the ratio for LPS-treated groups when compared to the control group (Fig. 2f). c-PTIO, a NO scavenger, was further introduced to verify the probe's responsiveness toward NO. After LPS-polarized macrophages were incubated with c-PTIO (0.2 mm, 0.5 h), the fluorescence ratio decreased to a level close to that of unpolarized macrophages (Fig. 2e and f). These NIR-II imaging results indicate that the CSQD@PIB probe can effectively monitor the M1 polarization process of Raw264.7 macrophages through quantitative analysis of intracellular NO.

NIR-II imaging of skin inflammation

Before applying CSQD@PIB probe for in vivo imaging of NO, systemic toxicity and metabolic process were investigated. Healthy mice were intravenously administered with CSQD@PIB (10 mg kg−1) and body weight changes were recorded. As shown in Fig. S14a, no significant differences in body weight were observed in 11 days after administration, indicating no acute systemic toxicity. Also, blood samples were collected from mice on day 11 post administration for blood biochemical and hematological analyses. The results showed that, compared with the untreated control group, all measured parameters in blood samples exhibited no significant abnormalities and levels of serum inflammatory cytokines were also within the normal range (Fig. S14b). ICP-MS analysis of Pb content in blood at day 11 post-injection showed a concentration of 5.1 μg/L, which is below the permissible environmental exposure limit. Furthermore, mice were euthanized after blood collection and their major organs were harvested. H&E staining of organ sections (Fig. S14c) revealed no obvious pathological changes. These results demonstrated that our probe did not cause significant hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, hematotoxicity or systemic inflammatory responses after administration, indicating its favorable biosafety. We investigated the metabolic process of the probe via different administration routes to be used hereinafter respectively. As shown in Fig. S15, after subcutaneous administration, fluorescence intensity at the injection site gradually decreased and was completely cleared within 11 days. After rectal administration, CSQD@PIB was cleared from the intestine within 2 h. After intravenous injection, CSQD@PIB primarily accumulated in liver, spleen and lungs and was then gradually metabolized in 10 days. These results indicate that CSQD@PIB does not undergo long-term retention in the body after administration.

During the inflammatory process in the skin, NO modulates vasodilation, immune cell activity and release of cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β [24,25]. In this case, quantification of NO levels and visualization of its distribution provide critical insights for monitoring the progression of inflammation, understanding pathological mechanisms and guiding therapeutic strategies in the treatment of diseases including chronic wounds. To validate NO sensing capability of the CSQD@PIB probe in skin inflammation model, LPS was employed to induce skin inflammation in mice. Six hours after subcutaneous LPS injection on the dorsal side of mice, NIR-II fluorescent probe was injected subcutaneously at the same site (Fig. 3a). Following probe injection, in vivo NIR-II fluorescence imaging was performed at three time points (10, 30, and 60 min) using 660 nm laser excitation and clear fluorescent signals were observed at all time points (Fig. 3b). Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence intensity ratio within ROIs revealed a significant and time-dependent increase in the fluorescence ratio on the LPS-inflamed side compared to the contralateral control side (Fig. 3c). In vivo NIR-II imaging results revealed the promoted NO production in a skin inflammation model [26]. After in vivo imaging, mice were euthanized, and local skin tissue was collected for subsequent analysis. First, ex vivo NIR-II imaging was performed on skin sections, and fluorescence ratios were quantified at cellular level (Fig. 3d). The results showed that the trend of ratio changes was consistent with the in vivo imaging results (Fig. 3e), demonstrating that the CSQD@PIB probe can sensitively detect NO levels in vivo. It should be noted that the fluorescence ratio calculated from ex vivo imaging of tissue sections was significantly higher than that obtained from in vivo imaging, indicating that NO concentration was higher in the isolated tissue. We believe this is due to the use of saline perfusion during tissue harvesting, which essentially interrupts systemic blood supply. Subsequently, paraformaldehyde (PFA) perfusion was performed for fixation, but during systemic perfusion, the concentration of formaldehyde within the tissue increased gradually. Before the cells were fully fixed, both the interruption of blood supply and the gradual accumulation of formaldehyde served as strong stimulants, likely promoting substantial NO production within the cells. This explains why NO concentrations are elevated not only in inflamed tissues but also in normal tissues. To further validate the inflammation model and probe response, the harvested skin tissues were subjected to immunofluorescence and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining (Fig. 3d). Immunofluorescence analysis showed significant upregulation of CD86 (M1 macrophage marker) and iNOS expression in the LPS-treated skin, indicating increased M1 macrophage polarization and active NO synthesis at the inflammatory site. NIR-II imaging of inflammatory bowel disease

NO works as a homeostatic regulator in healthy gastrointestinal tract, but its expression in immune cells is significantly upregulated during the pathological process of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [27,28]. Therefore, real-time monitoring the spatiotemporal dynamics of this biomarker is essential for elucidating the pathogenic mechanism and developing targeted therapeutic strategies [29]. To evaluate the application potential of CSQD@PIB in the imaging of colitis, IBD model with various severity was induced by feeding mice with different concentrations of dextran sulfate sodium (DSS, Fig. 4a). During the induction phase, body weight and disease activity index scores were documented daily (Fig. 4b and c). Subsequently, on day 7, all mice were administered the CSQD@PIB probe (0.2 mL, 1 mg mL−1) via rectal injection (Fig. 4a) and underwent in vivo NIR-II fluorescence imaging at 10, 30, and 60 min post-administration. Imaging revealed obvious fluorescent signals in the abdominal region, with clear visualization of intestinal outlines, demonstrating the high signal-to-noise ratio of NIR-II imaging (Fig. 4d). Quantitative analysis of the fluorescence ratio indicated significant differences among the three groups (Fig. 4e), confirming that CSQD@PIB enables non-invasive detection and quantification of NO levels in the colon. Consistent with this, fecal samples from the three groups displayed the same trend in NIR-II fluorescence imaging (Figs. S16–17). Following in vivo imaging, mice were euthanized. Measurement of colon length revealed a strong dependence on DSS concentration, which correlated with the severity of colitis (Figs. S18–19). The colons were subjected to ex vivo imaging and histological analysis. NIR-II fluorescence imaging of both whole-colon (Figs. S20-21) and colon sections (Fig. 4f and g) showed the trend of increasing fluorescence ratio with higher DSS concentration, consistent with in vivo observations. H&E staining showed that compared to the control group with intact mucosal structure, the 2.5% DSS group exhibited mucosal damage, while the 5% DSS group displayed more severe damage, accompanied by extensive inflammatory cell infiltration, indicating aggravated inflammation with increasing DSS concentration (Fig. S22). Furthermore, we performed spatial heterogeneity analysis on the colons from 2.5% DSS group. Based on the results of ex vivo NIR-II imaging (Fig. 4h), intestinal segments with high (severe segment) and low (medium segment) fluorescence ratios were sectioned for study. NIR-II fluorescence imaging of the sections showed that the fluorescence ratio of the severe segment was significantly higher than that of the medium segment (Fig. S24), consistent with the whole-colon imaging results. H&E staining and immunofluorescence (CD86/iNOS) analysis of these segments also confirmed that the severe segment exhibited more severe tissue damage and stronger inflammatory signals, achieving correlation between macroscopic imaging and microscopic pathology. This spatial heterogeneity analysis indicates that the CSQD@PIB probe not only enables non-invasive detection of inflammation at the organ level but also possesses excellent spatial resolution to precisely reveal microscopic heterogeneity of inflammatory distribution within tissues. The fluorescence ratio shows a highly quantitative spatial co-localization with local pathological severity, demonstrating great potential of the probe for precise quantification and visualization of disease foci, surpassing traditional detection methods that only provide overall average information.

NIR-II imaging of acute hepatitis

During the progression of hepatitis, immune and hepatic cells express elevated levels of NO, which is associated with amplified inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress and stellate cell-mediated fibrosis [30,31]. Therefore, quantifying NO concentration provides crucial evidence for evaluating inflammation state, monitoring fibrotic progression and reflecting the effectiveness of clinical interventions. To further validate the responsiveness of CSQD@PIB in the biological internal environment, we applied it to the imaging of CCl4-induced acute hepatitis. Graded hepatitis models were established by intraperitoneal injection of CCl4 with different doses. Twenty-four hours after CCl4 injection, all mice received a tail vein injection of CSQD@PIB probe (0.2 mL, 1 mg mL−1) (Fig. 5a), followed by in vivo NIR-II fluorescence imaging within 8 h (Fig. 5b and Fig. S25). Quantitative analysis showed that compared to the control group, fluorescence ratios of all experimental groups were significantly increased within 6 h after administration and remained stable afterwards. Furthermore, at 6 h, ratios in the 0.75 mL kg−1 CCl4 group were significantly higher than those of the 0.5 mL kg−1 CCl4 group (Fig. 5c). These results confirm that the intravenously injected CSQD@PIB probe enables non-invasive detection and quantification of NO levels in the liver in vivo. Furthermore, c-PTIO was administered via intraperitoneal injection (10 mg kg−1), followed by tail vein injection of CSQD@PIB. No obvious increase in the fluorescence ratio was observed in 6 h (Fig. 5b and c). These results demonstrated that the elevation of the fluorescence ratio was solely induced by increased NO levels, rather than other factors in the inflammatory microenvironment. To compare the performance of CSQD@PIB and NO probe in the visible spectral region, a mouse with acute hepatitis was injected with DAF-FM DA, a commonly used NO probe, at the same dose via tail vein and fluorescence imaging was performed afterwards. As shown in Fig. S26, DAF-FM DA exhibited high background when imaging in the visible spectral region. In contrast, CSQD@PIB produced low background fluorescence in NIR-II imaging, achieving a much higher signal-to-background ratio (SBR). This result demonstrates that CSQD@PIB in NIR-II spectral region is more suitable for real-time monitoring of NO levels in vivo than visible probe. After in vivo imaging, mice were euthanized, and livers were collected for ex vivo imaging. NIR-II fluorescence imaging of both whole liver (Fig. 5d and e) and liver sections (Fig. 5f and g) indicated that the fluorescence signal intensity increased with higher CCl4 dose, consistent with the in vivo imaging results. H&E staining of liver tissues revealed that compared to control group with normal structure, the 0.5 mL kg−1 CCl4 group showed increased hepatocyte vacuolation, while 0.75 mL kg−1 CCl4 group exhibited more extensive vacuolar degeneration accompanied by more significant inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 5f). This result demonstrates that hepatic inflammatory damage escalates as CCl4 dosage increases. Immunofluorescence analysis showed a gradient increase in the expression of CD86 and iNOS in liver tissue with high CCl4 dosage (Fig. 5f). Conclusion

This study successfully developed a ratiometric fluorescent probe, CSQD@PIB, emitting in NIR-II spectral region for highly sensitive and specific visualization and quantitative analysis of NO in vivo. The probe was constructed via self-assembly, using CSQD as the core and a conjugated polymer, PIB, as NO-responsive unit, exhibiting good water dispersity, low cytotoxicity, and excellent biocompatibility. In vitro experiments confirmed high selectivity of CSQD@PIB toward NO, enabling quantitative detection. At cellular level, the probe effectively monitored the dynamics of NO levels during LPS-induced M1 polarization of Raw264.7 macrophages. More importantly, in three typical inflammatory disease models (LPS-induced skin inflammation, DSS-induced IBD, and CCl4-induced acute hepatitis), non-invasive, real-time NIR-II imaging of NO levels at lesion sites was achieved using the CSQD@PIB probe. The imaging results showed a positive correlation with the severity of inflammation and could precisely distinguish inflammatory gradients within tissues through spatial heterogeneity analysis. Ex vivo NIR-II imaging, H&E staining, and immunofluorescence (CD86/iNOS) analysis results for all models were highly consistent with the in vivo imaging conclusions, verifying the probe's reliability from macro to micro levels. In summary, the CSQD@PIB probe, with high signal-to-noise ratio, deep tissue penetration, and precise quantitative capability, provides a powerful imaging tool for in vivo, non-invasive monitoring of NO-related pathophysiological processes, holding significant application potential in inflammation-related research and drug evaluation.

Experimental section

Preparation of the ratiometric nanoprobes

CSQD and OPA were synthesized according to the methods reported by Ming-Xi Zhang et al. [19].

Synthesis of PIB: M1 (247 mg, 0.2 mmol), M2 (77 mg, 0.2 mmol), and Pd(PPh3)4 were dissolved in toluene (15 mL). The mixture was degassed via three freeze-pump-thaw cycles. It was then heated to 110 °C and stirred for 24 h. After cooling to room temperature, the polymer was precipitated by adding methanol (100 mL) and washed with hexane. After drying under vacuum, P1 was obtained as a dark solid (127 mg, 56% yield).

A solution of P1 (169 mg, 0.15 mmol repeat units) and iron powder (84 mg, 1.50 mmol) in acetic acid (6 mL) and CHCl3 (6 mL) was degassed via three freeze-pump-thaw cycles and then stirred at 80 °C for 4 h. After cooling to room temperature, the reaction mixture was poured into water and extracted with CH2Cl2. The organic layer was dried over MgSO4 and concentrated under vacuum. Methanol was added to precipitate the polymeric product, yielding PIB (125 mg, 78%) as a dark solid. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3, δ ppm):8.04 (br, 2H, Ar-H), 7.48-7.61 (m, 2H, Ar-H), 7.21-7.25 (m, 8H, Ph-H), 7.10-7.13 (m, 8H, Ph-H), 2.59 (t, 8H, J = 6.7 Hz, Ph-CH2-), 1.61 (br, 8H, -CH2-), 1.32 (br, 24H, -CH2-), 0.89 (t, 12H, J = 6.0 Hz, -CH3).

Preparation of CSQD@PIB: OPA (15 mg) was dissolved in CHCl3 (2 mL). Subsequently, hydrophobic CSQD (5 mg) and PIB (3 mg) were added to this solution and stirred continuously at room temperature for 30 min. The organic solvent was then removed via rotary evaporation to obtain a film. To this film, a Na2CO3 aqueous solution (0.5%, w/v, 2 mL) was added, and the mixture was sonicated until fully dissolved. The solution was then purified by ultrafiltration to remove Na2CO3. Finally, the purified product was redispersed in phosphate-buffered saline (5 mL) to obtain a stock solution with a CSQD concentration of 1 mg mL−1.

Other characterization

The hydrodynamic diameters of CSQD, PIB, and CSQD@PIB were measured by dynamic light scattering with Brookhaven 90Plus Zeta. The morphology of CSQD and CSQD@PIB was observed by TEM (JEM-1400, JEOL). UV-Vis absorption spectra of CSQD, PIB, and CSQD@PIB under different NO concentrations were recorded by NP80, Implen. The molecular weight of PIB was measured by Agilent GPC 50 & Wyatt dawn heleos II. The NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker AVANCE IIITM HD 600 MHz spectrophotometer with the use of TMS as the internal reference. The emission spectra covering 900 to 1700 nm were collected via a home-constructed optical setup that utilized the fiber spectrometer (NIR17S, Idea Optics; slit width: 25 μm). NIR-II fluorescence imaging was performed on a custom-configured wide-field NIR imaging setup equipped with an InGaAs camera (NIRvana HS, Princeton Instruments). The setup utilized a 660 nm laser (MDL-III-660D, Cnilaser) for excitation. Images in IIa channel (1000-1300 nm) were collected using a 1000 nm long-pass filter (FELH1000, Thorlabs) combined with a 1300 nm short-pass filter (89-676, Edmund Optics), and images in IIb channel (1500-1700 nm) were collected using a 1500 nm long-pass filter (FELH1500, Thorlabs). Fluorescence imaging of cells treated with CSQD@PIB and mouse tissue sections was performed on a custom-assembled NIR microscope. These imaging setups were reported previously [32]. For NO response and selectivity assay, the experimental parameters are as follows: the concentration of DEA NONOate ranged from 0 to 70 μm, other biological species 0.2 mm, the probe concentration was 1 mg mL−1, and the reaction was carried out in acetate buffer (pH 5.5) at 37 °C for 30 min.

Polarization experiment of Raw264.7 cells

Raw264.7 cells, categorized into three groups, were seeded and pre-cultured for 24 h. To induce polarization, experimental groups were treated with LPS (10 μg mL−1, ST1470, Beyotime) for 2 or 6 h, while controls received standard medium (C11995500BT, Gibco). All groups were then incubated for 0.5 h with CSQD@PIB (200 μg mL−1). After three PBS washes, cells were subjected to immunofluorescence and NIR-II fluorescence imaging. Cells were stained with FITC anti-CD86 (105005, Biolegend), PE anti-iNOS (CXNFT), DAF-FM DA (12-5920-80, S0019S, Beyotime), and analyzed by flow cytometry BD LSRFortessa X-20 and a digital microscope (Eclipse Ni-U, Nikon).

Experimental animal models

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Southern Medical University. All procedures involving animals were conducted in strict compliance with protocols approved by the Ethics Committee of Southern Medical University (SYXK(yue)2021-0167). Mice were anesthetized by induction in a chamber with 2.5% isoflurane delivered at an airflow of 10 L min−1, and anesthesia was sustained via a nose cone during all subsequent modeling and imaging operations.

Skin inflammation model

To establish the skin inflammation model, subcutaneous injections were administered on both sides of the back of 3 mice: the left side received LPS (100 μL, 50 μg mL−1) solution to induce inflammation, while the right side received an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline as a control. Six hours later, an equal amount of probe was injected subcutaneously at the same sites. After probe injection, NIR-II imaging was performed at three time points (10, 30, and 60 min). Mice were then euthanized by cervical dislocation, and skin samples were collected for sectioned NIR-II fluorescence imaging and H&E staining analysis. Then skin sections were stained with FITC anti-CD86 (105005, Biolegend), PE anti-iNOS (CXNFT) for immunofluorescence analysis.

IBD model

To establish the colitis model, 9 mice were randomly divided into three groups: control, 2.5% DSS group, and 5% DSS group. The control group drank deionized water freely, while the experimental groups drank deionized water containing 2.5% or 5% (w/v) DSS (D808272, Macklin), respectively, for 7 days. Mouse body weight and stool consistency were recorded daily during the experiment. On day 7, mice received a rectal administration of CSQD@PIB probe (0.2 mL, 1 mg mL−1), followed by in vivo NIR-II fluorescence imaging at 10, 30, and 60 min. Subsequently, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and their colons were collected for NIR fluorescence imaging, H&E staining analysis, then colon sections were stained with FITC anti-CD86 (105005, Biolegend), PE anti-iNOS (CXNFT) for immunofluorescence analysis. Spatial heterogeneity analysis was also performed on the colons from the 2.5% DSS group.

Acute hepatitis model

To establish the acute hepatitis model, 9 mice were randomly divided into three groups, and all received intraperitoneal injections with a unified volume of 3 mL kg−1. The control group received pure olive oil (CCl4 net dose: 0 mL kg−1); the moderate inflammation group received a mixture of CCl4 and olive oil (volume ratio 1:4, CCl4 net dose: 0.5 mL kg−1); the severe inflammation group received the mixture (volume ratio 1:3, CCl4 net dose: 0.75 mL kg−1). Twenty-four hours post-injection, all mice received a tail vein injection of CSQD@PIB probe (0.2 mL, 1 mg mL−1), followed by in vivo NIR-II fluorescence imaging at 1, 2, and 6 h. Subsequently, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation, and their livers were collected for NIR fluorescence imaging, sectioned NIR fluorescence imaging, H&E staining analysis, then liver sections were stained with FITC anti-CD86 (105005, Biolegend), PE anti-iNOS (CXNFT) for immunofluorescence analysis.

Statistical analysis

For each representative experimental result, the number of successful independent experiments performed was indicated in the corresponding figure caption. An unpaired two-sided Student's t-test was performed for comparison between the two groups in cell experiments. Paired t-test was applied to skin inflammation model, since LPS and PBS were injected into opposite sides of the same mouse. Two-way ANOVA was employed to assess the main effects of model group and imaging time, as well as their interaction for IBD and acute hepatitis model. For pairwise comparisons among groups at each time point, one-way ANOVA combined with Tukey's post-hoc test was applied. In this work, n represented biological replicates. For the analysis of NIR-II images, ROIs were defined based on fluorescence signal of NIR-IIb channel. Image acquisition and data analysis were performed blinded to treatment groups (animals randomly assigned, images coded with random numbers before analysis), and no outliers were excluded (all collected data points were presented).
