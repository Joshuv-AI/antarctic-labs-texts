Mapping structural variants in Populus tomentosa reveals adaptive signatures and improves prediction of wood properties
Authors: Leishi Zhong, Donghai Zhang, Rui Huang, Jiaxuan Zhou, Wenke Zhang, Lianzheng Li, Zhuoying Jin, Mengjiao Zhang, Chongde Tian, Mingjia Yuan, Yixuan Zhang, Peng Li, Liang Xiao, Mingyang Quan, Deqiang Zhang, Jinlong Li, Qingzhang Du
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStructural variants (SVs) are key drivers of genome plasticity and adaptation in plants, yet their roles in woody perennials remain understudied. Here, we build a comprehensive SV landscape based on a haplotype-resolved chromosomal genome assembly and population resequencing of 302 Populus tomentosa accessions. We reconstruct the evolutionary trajectories of SVs, revealing that they shape subpopulation differentiation through both independent and joint genomic variations. Integrating transcriptomics, we demonstrate that SVs exert broad dosage and cis-acting regulatory effects. Furthermore, segmental duplications drive SV hotspot formation, and these regions are significantly enriched for adaptive signals. SV-based GWAS reveals 61 candidate genes influencing wood-related traits under adaptive selection. Functionally, a causal 54-bp insertion in the PtoERD3 promoter balances lignin-mediated growth and drought adaptation. Additionally, we implement an optimized genomic prediction method with an accuracy of 0.94 for wood-related traits. Finally, we propose an SV-based breeding navigation map to improve the accuracy of multi-trait selection.Subject terms: Natural variation in plants, Agricultural genetics, Plant breeding

Introduction

The remarkable diversity of life on Earth is fundamentally underpinned by the vast array of genetic variation within and among species, serving as the raw material upon which evolutionary forces act1. Beyond the extensively studied single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and small insertions/deletions (InDels), structural variants (SVs), encompassing insertions, deletions, duplications, inversions, and translocations typically exceeding 50 bp, represent a critical yet often underappreciated class of genomic alterations2. While readily detectable SNPs have historically dominated population genetic and trait association studies, this prevailing focus has inadvertently overshadowed the profound impact of SVs. Emerging evidence strongly suggests that SVs possess an unparalleled capacity to reshape gene dosage, alter regulatory landscapes, and even create novel gene fusions, thereby orchestrating rapid and substantial phenotypic changes3,4. Compelling examples from agricultural species unequivocally demonstrate this power. For example, SVs have been directly linked to complex trait variations like tomato flavor5, driven cold tolerance in tea plants6, and remarkably, major phenotypic divergences such as pod color through large genomic deletions7. These cases illustrate how SVs, in contrast to subtle point mutations, can induce profound phenotypic effects and confer distinct adaptive advantages.

The evolutionary dynamics of long-lived woody plants, such as forest trees, are distinctly shaped by their extended lifespans, large effective population sizes, and complex interactions with heterogeneous environments8,9. Annual crops, shaped by intense artificial selection over short timescales, undergo rapid domestication bottlenecks that reduce genetic diversity and favor the fixation of beneficial alleles, often SNPs or small InDels10. In contrast, as keystone organisms in terrestrial ecosystems, forest trees maintain extraordinary genetic diversity through extensive gene flow, outcrossing mating systems, and prolonged generation times. This combination delays fixation of beneficial alleles while maintaining variants under balancing or purifying selection11. Unlike annual crops subjected to rapid domestication bottlenecks, forest trees experience natural selection over centuries, fostering the accumulation of variations that may confer adaptive advantages in response to fluctuating biotic and abiotic stressors. Despite the confirmed importance of SVs across biological systems, methodological constraints persist in woody plants genomics. The large, repetitive genomes characteristic of these species, combined with limitations of short-read sequencing for SV detection, have impeded systematic characterization of population-level SV landscapes. Consequently, the functional contributions of SVs to adaptation and phenotypic diversity in long-lived woody plants remain largely unexplored.

Populus, particularly Populus tomentosa, as a vital ecological and timber species in Northern China, serves as an exemplary model for dissecting the genetic basis of complex traits in woody plants due to its relatively compact genome, high genetic diversity, and strong environmental adaptability12,13. While previous genomic analyses in Populus have yielded valuable insights into population structure and adaptive SNP associations, their primary reliance on SNP-based approaches has inadvertently resulted in a limited understanding of SVs’ functional roles. The comprehensive detection and precise characterization of diverse SV types, especially complex ones, have historically been challenging with conventional short-read sequencing, often leading to their underestimation or complete omission in genomic studies. Consequently, the contributions of SVs to local adaptation, phenotypic diversity and breeding potential in undomesticated forest trees like P. tomentosa remain largely uncharted, representing a critical gap in advancing tree genetics and breeding.

To gain a more complete understanding of the genetic mechanisms driving environmental adaptation and phenotypic variance, we assembled a haplotype-resolved chromosome-level genome for P. tomentosa. By constructing a population-scale SV database, we reconstructed the evolutionary trajectories of SVs, focusing on their roles in historical selection, interspecific introgression, and the formation of adaptive hotspots. Through SVs based Genome-Wide Association Studies (SV-GWAS), we identified a key 54-bp insertion in PtoERD3 that underlies lignin-mediated growth and drought adaptation, highlighting the unique contribution of SVs to wood traits. Our study further shows that SVs capture heritability not accounted for by SNPs, which enhances the predictive power of genomic prediction strategies. We finally provide an SV-breeding navigation map for improving multiple-trait selection accuracy. Collectively, our work provides comprehensive insights into the evolutionary landscape of SV and establishes a blueprint for SV informed genomic breeding in long-lived woody plants.

Results

De novo assembly and annotation of the Populus tomentosa ‘1316’ genome

To generate a high-quality haplotype-resolved reference genome for P. tomentosa, we sequenced the elite clone ‘1316’ using PacBio HiFi reads. The assembly achieved high contig N50 sizes of 15.82 Mb and 15.08 Mb, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1). After anchoring, the scaffold N50 sizes of 19.48 Mb and 19.28 Mb. Benchmarking universal single-copy orthologs (BUSCOs) analysis of haplotype A and haplotype D assessed 99.4% and 99.1% genome completeness, indicating high continuity and quality. We annotated 58,919 protein-coding genes in the genome of ‘1316’, with average lengths of 4227 bp and 4881 bp, respectively. Phylogenetic analysis determined that HapA originated from the male parent (P. alba var. pyramidalis) and HapD from the female parent (P. adenopoda) (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Compared to the existing P. tomentosa genomes ‘Tianshui’ (not haplotype-resolved) and ‘GM15’, the ‘1316’ assembly demonstrates superior continuity (Table 1, Supplementary Data 1)13,14.

To investigate the interplay between the two subgenomes in P. tomentosa ‘1316’, we analyzed the allelic expression patterns of 16,260 expressed homologous gene pairs across root, stem, and leaf tissues (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Our analysis revealed extensive allele-specific expression (ASE). total of 7955 genes (48.93%) exhibited significant expression bias (|log2FC | > 1) in at least one tissue, which is higher than the ratio (34.7%) previously reported in Populus 84K15 Despite the high frequency of ASE, the two subgenomes maintained a strict global dosage balance. Among the biased genes, 4011 (24.67%) were preferentially expressed from the subgenome A, while 3944 (24.26%) were biased towards the subgenome D. The difference between the two directions was negligible (P > 0.05), indicating no subgenome-level dominance. Furthermore, this balanced pattern was consistent across all chromosomes. The proportion of ASE genes remained stable (ranging from 49.1% to 51.4%), with HapA biased and HapD biased genes equally distributed along the chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d).

Due to the better assembly quality of HapA, we used it as the representative assembly for subsequent structural and evolutionary analyses. We identified 438,289 transposable elements (TEs) in HapA, constituting 38.9% (151.8 Mb) of the whole genome. Long terminal repeat retrotransposons (LTRs; 59.6% of TEs) and DNA transposons (11.5%) predominated, with LTR/Gypsy elements comprising 15.1% of the genome (Fig. 1a). Among 9202 intact LTR-RTs, insertion time analysis indicated a proliferation peak ~0.33 million years ago (Supplementary Fig. 3). LTR-RTs preferentially insert into gene regulatory regions rather than coding sequences, likely due to open chromatin structures in promoter regions (Supplementary Fig. 3b, c, Supplementary Fig. 4). TE density in gene bodies is lower, consistent with selection pressures minimizing coding disruption.

‘1316’ and ‘GM15’ represent the northeast climate region, while ‘TianShui’ represents the northwest. Comparative genomic analysis of ‘1316’ and ‘TianShui’ revealed high collinearity but identified 1026 insertions/deletions, 33 inversions, and 628 translocations, with inversions frequent near telomeres (Fig. 1b, c). We found that the densities of both TEs and segmental duplications (SDs) were significantly enriched at inversion breakpoints compared to the internal regions of the inversions. This provides compelling evidence that the presence of these repetitive elements facilitates the formation of inversions. In particular, the enrichment of specific TE families such as LTR/Gypsy at these breakpoints strongly supports a mechanism involving non-allelic homologous recombination (NAHR) as a primary driver of these SVs16, a pattern we also confirmed in P. davidiana and P. trichocarpa, suggesting a conserved evolutionary mechanism across the genus (Supplementary Fig. 3d, and Supplementary Fig. 5). Duplications in ‘1316’ were nearly double those in ‘TianShui’ potentially linked to gene family and TE expansion. GO enrichment analysis of genes affected by SVs showed significant enrichment in salicylic acid synthesis (GO:0046244) and abscisic acid signaling (GO:0009738), likely lead to differences in disease resistance and stress resistance (Fig. 1d). Notably, population structure analysis revealed a distinct genetic background. This divergence in hybrid index suggests that the observed SV in ABA signaling pathways in ‘Tianshui’ are not random but are likely driven by the specific evolutionary history and local adaptation of the NW subpopulations lineage to arid environments. This distinct admixture components provide the genomic basis for the special ecotype accumulation of adaptive SVs.

The SVs landscape and signatures of selection across 302 P. tomentosa accessions

We identified 67,294 non-redundant SVs (>50 bp) across 302 P. tomentosa accessions to construct an SV map. These included 37,274 deletions (55.3%), 25,900 insertions (38.4%), 3858 duplications (5.7%), and 262 inversions (0.3%) (Fig. 2a). SV lengths ranged from 50 bp to 1 Mb. Deletions and insertions were predominantly between 50 and 300 bp in size, whereas duplications and inversions showed more uniform distributions across length intervals (Fig. 2b). Notably, chromosome 17 exhibited the highest SV density, while chromosome 14 had the lowest. Individual SV carrying rates showed significant heterogeneity, with peaks at 0–10%, 25–30%, and 90–100% (Supplementary Fig. 6a), indicating subpopulations with low, typical, and high genomic SV loads, respectively. The minor allele frequency (MAF) analysis confirmed a skew toward rare alleles, with 17,851 SVs having a MAF < 0.05, indicating non-uniform variation frequencies across the population (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Genetic structure analysis and phylogenetic studies consistently divided the 302 accessions into three major clusters, mirroring their geographic origins17 (Fig. 2c, and Supplementary Fig. 6c, Supplementary Data 2). Consistent with these findings, nucleotide diversity (π) was the highest in Pop_S, with significant genetic differentiation observed among the subpopulations (Supplementary Figs. 6d, e). Population genomic analyses, including PCA, phylogenetic reconstruction, and population structure profiling, further confirmed the concordance between SVs and SNPs. These analyses consistently recovered the same subpopulation differentiation patterns, demonstrating the utility of SVs for genetic studies.

All three subpopulations shared a core set of SVs. Pop_S harbored the largest number of private SVs (3,212), while Pop_NW contained the fewest (750). This suggests divergent selective pressures among P. tomentosa subpopulations despite their shared genetic background. (Supplementary Figs. 7a, b). We compared SVs frequencies between pairwise subpopulations to identify selective SVs (Fig. 2d, e, and Supplementary Fig. 7c, d, Supplementary Data 3). A total of 16,687 (24.8%) SVs were under intense selection pressure. Among these selected SVs, the highest number (9893, or 14.7% of total SVs) was associated with Pop_S, possibly reflecting its highest genetic diversity, which provides more raw material for selection. Conversely, Pop_NW was associated with only 951 (1.4%) selected SVs, indirectly suggesting its more recent differentiation time. Unlike domesticated crops showing directional selection trends, P. tomentosa exhibited complex allele frequency shifts, suggesting polygenic adaptation through divergent selection pressures18. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis in 50 kb windows indicated that most SVs (~80%) were in low LD with flanking SNPs (r2 < 0.5) (Supplementary Fig. 8). This indicates SVs are not fully captured by proximal SNPs in genomic analyses. Therefore, to obtain comprehensive selection signatures of P. tomentosa, we employed three complementary SNP-based methods: Fixation Index (FST), Nucleotide Diversity Ratio (π ratio), and Cross-Population Composite Likelihood Ratio (XP-CLR) to screen for genome-wide selection signatures associated with the adaptation of each subpopulation (Fig. 2f, and Supplementary Fig. 9). However, these SNP-based methods recalled only 19.3% of potentially selective SVs located within the SNP selective regions, including genes like heat-tolerant HSP60 and HSP101, salt-tolerant SOS1, and cold-tolerant CBF4 and LEA4 (Fig. 2g). This limited recovery highlights the necessity of an integrated approach. By combining both SNP and SV-based methods, we were able to identify a total of 7289 genes under selection, which is a far more comprehensive result than what either method could achieve independently (Fig. 2h, and Supplementary Fig. 10). This finding underscores SV frequency analysis as an essential complement to SNP methods for detecting selective regions19.

To investigate the contribution of interspecific gene flow to the genetic diversity of P. tomentosa, we performed a genome-wide introgression analysis (Supplementary Data 4). The ABBA-BABA test revealed robust evidence of gene flow from P. adenopoda into P. tomentosa, with a highly positive D-statistic (D = 0.83, Z = 125.9) and a substantial introgression proportion (f4-ratio = 0.48), consistent with a hybrid origin scenario. Genome-wide scanning using the fd statistic identified a mosaic pattern of introgression, with high introgression signals (Top 1% windows) distributed across multiple chromosomes (Supplementary Fig. 11a). To explore the evolutionary trajectories of these introgressed variants, we compared the allele frequencies of SVs in high introgression regions with the genomic background. Notably, insertions derived from P. adenopoda exhibited significantly elevated frequencies in the top 1% introgression regions (P < 0.01, Supplementary Fig. 11b, c), suggesting that these specific insertions have been preferentially retained under positive selection. Deletions also displayed a trend of increased frequency, although this difference was not statistically significant. Collectively, these results highlight the potential role of adaptive introgression in shaping the SV landscape of P. tomentosa.

SVs introduce expression variation in numerous genes

We observed that a significant proportion of SVs were located in gene regulatory regions, with 32.7% within 2 kb upstream or downstream, compared to 9.5% in gene exons and 15.3% in gene introns (Fig. 3a). A notable 35.3% of SVs were found in intergenic regions. Gene ontology analysis of SV-affected genes showed significant enrichment in functions critical for adaptation and development, such as “leaf senescence,” “response to salicylic acid,” and “cell wall organization” (Supplementary Fig. 12a). This indicates that SVs play important roles in growth and development, extending beyond their abiotic stress responses.

Our analysis revealed that SVs affecting regulatory sequences (upstream and downstream) exhibited higher polymorphism than those within coding sequences (exons) (Supplementary Fig. 12b). This strongly suggested weaker selective constraints on regulatory SVs compared to those directly disrupting protein-coding regions. To assess the genome-wide effects of SVs on gene expression, we performed RNA-seq using stem and leaf tissues from 120 P. tomentosa accessions and correlated the expression data with our SV catalog (Supplementary Data 5, 6). In stem tissues, 5533 (19%) of 29,631 retained SV-gene pairs showed significant changes in gene expression (Fig. 3b). Deletions showed the highest proportion (23.3%) of differentially expressed genes in stems, likely due to their higher frequency and potential to directly alter gene dosage. Interestingly, a distinct pattern emerged in leaf tissues. While deletions remained the dominant factor in altering gene expression in both tissues, we found that insertions exhibited a higher proportion of expression in leaf than stems (35% in leaf vs 20% in stem) (Supplementary Fig. 13). This contrast highlights a tissue-dependent specificity. Specifically, 2329 SV-gene pairs (7.9%) showed significant in both tissues. Furthermore, SVs in non-coding regions were also associated with a high proportion of differentially expressed genes, particularly intronic deletions (19.9%). To validate these findings, we confirmed the reliability of our results through two independent genetic experiments (Fig. 3c). Genes affected by coding SVs showed significantly altered expression in 23.5% of cases, compared to 17.7% for non-coding SVs. Notably, expression levels of genes with coding SVs were significantly lower, aligning with conclusions from nucleotide polymorphism analyses (Fig. 3d). Additionally, 54% of exon-derived SVs reduced gene expression (Supplementary Figs. 12c, d). These SV are likely constrained by purifying selection, potentially explaining their low accumulation and the persistently low or absent expression of affected genes (Supplementary Fig. 14). Overall, SVs exerted diverse effects on gene expression: 58% decreased expression, while 42% increased it, underscoring their substantial regulatory role in shaping the transcriptome landscape of P. tomentosa (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 13d).

Our analysis of SV breakpoint positions identified 1065 significant SV hotspots across the P. tomentosa genome. The total of 13,025 hotspot-associated intervals significantly exceeded expectations under a random distribution model (Fig. 3f, g, and Supplementary Data 7). These hotspots overlapped 3,373 genes, including key regulatory genes such as AGL19, NAC104, CESA1, ARF11 and UVR8 (Supplementary Data 8). The functional significance of SVs is evident in their non-random genomic distribution and enrichment within regulatory regions, suggesting a role in P. tomentosa’s adaptation to abiotic stresses. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we investigated the association between SV hotspots and three key genomic features: TEs, genes, and SDs. Our analysis revealed a significant enrichment of genes within SV hotspots when compared to non-hotspot regions. While SDs did not show a statistically significant enrichment in hotspots, they were nevertheless more prevalent in hotspot regions than in non-hotspot regions (Supplementary Figs. 15a, b). This finding, coupled with the observation that some hotspots exhibit exceptionally high π values and recombination rates (Supplementary Figs. 15c, d), suggests that these gene-dense areas are more prone to DNA damage and recombination, leading to the accumulation and conservation of complex mutations that ultimately manifest as rearrangement hotspots. In contrast, we found little evidence to support TE activity as a primary driver of SV formation in these regions. Our results indicate a low overlap between TEs and SVs, and TE coverage was significantly reduced in SV hotspots relative to non-hotspot regions (Supplementary Fig. 16). This suggests that TE activity is unlikely to be the main cause of SV formation in gene-rich areas. Instead, our findings point to the accumulation of SVs caused by NAHR events, which are mediated by SDs (Supplementary Data 9, 10). Furthermore, we calculate recombination rates and local principal component analysis (local PCA) in 10 kb windows across the genome to verify genomic regions with inversions. It was found that compared with normal regions, recombination in inverted regions was suppressed, exchange was reduced, there were more LD (high r²), linkage was stronger, and therefore decayed more slowly (Supplementary Fig. 17). These results are consistent with a growing body of literature that highlights the strong association between SV and NAHR16,20,21.

Genomic signatures of local environmental adaptation in P. tomentosa

To detect significant genomic regions associated with local environmental factors, we used a latent factor mixing model (LFMM) to identify candidate loci underlying 19 temperature and related variables. LFMM identified 3475 SVs strongly associated with environmental variables, with precipitation-associated variation significantly higher than temperature-associated variation (Fig. 4c). As expected, these significant SVs exhibited statistically higher effect sizes compared to the genomic background (Supplementary Fig. 18), confirming the robustness of the detected associations. The adaptive SVs were strongly skewed towards zero, with a median |Beta| of only 0.096. Many of these SVs associated with multiple environmental variables. In total, 1384 LFMM-associated SVs implicated 968 non-redundant genes, indicating a broad genomic response to climatic conditions (Supplementary Data 11). Considering multicollinearity, six climate variables with Spearman correlation coefficient (∣r∣) <0.7 and considering the rank of importance were selected for RDA analyses (Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Fig. 19). These six variables were associated with 1258 SVs harboring 1343 genes. Of the 1398 significant variants identified by RDA, 198 SVs displayed extreme loadings (standard deviation > 3) along one or multiple RDA axes, highlighting key genomic signals responding to climate factors (Fig. 4b). These core adaptive variants (CAVs), shared by both LFMM and RDA analysis, highlight climate adaptation mechanisms. Notably, only 7% of CAVs resided in coding sequences, while 93% were non-coding, suggesting that climate adaptation primarily occurs through selection on regulatory elements rather than protein-coding changes.

To assess recent selection in P. tomentosa populations, 844 (60.9%) shared SVs were identified based on frequency difference and genotype-environment association analysis (Fig. 4d). Of these, 716 SVs (51.7%) were strongly positively selected in Pop_NE. Our results indicate that climate-related variations exhibit stronger selection pressures, suggesting that local climate adaptation is achieved through polygenic selection acting on many variants of small effect (Fig. 4e). For example, a 64 bp insertion (Chr02:15108744) in the intron of Pto02G05141 (CIPK9), associated with bio3, is reported to be involved in plant cold stress and drought22. Additionally, SV Chr10:9157060, a 59 bp deletion upstream of Pto10G18742 (MYB88), is associated with bio4, bio6, bio7, and MYB88 is known for stomatal development and regulating salt/cold stress responses (Supplementary Fig. 20)23. These findings demonstrate that SVs likely drive evolutionary selection and climate adaptation in P. tomentosa.

SV-GWAS revealed causative SV loci underlying phenotypic variations of seven wood traits

Using a cohort of 302 P. tomentosa accessions, we performed GWAS between 49,380 SVs and seven key wood property traits, including arabinose, galactose, xylose, mannose, galacturonic acid, lignin, cellulose, and hemicellulose content. This analysis identified 334 significant SVs associated with 255 unique genes underlying phenotypic variation of these seven traits (Supplementary Fig. 21 and 22, Supplementary Data 12). These trait-associated SVs were distributed across intergenic regions, coding sequences (CDS), and non-coding regulatory elements. Notably, 11 SVs showed pleiotropic effects, appearing in association peaks for multiple traits. For example, SV Chr01:11436713 significantly associated with xylose and hemicellulose content, while SV Chr01:12409460 simultaneously associated with lignin and arabinose content.

To identify candidate genes potentially under adaptive selection and influencing wood traits, we intersected our SV-GWAS results with selection signature data. This analysis revealed 61 genes significantly associated with at least one wood trait and exhibiting evidence of differential allelic frequency. A compelling example is SV Chr04:1327804, located in the upstream regulatory region of Pto04G07723 (GPT2), a gene involved in glucose-6-phosphate transport (GO:0015760)24, was significantly associated with mannose content. Notably, GPT2 in Arabidopsis thaliana is regulated by MYB56 in a sucrose-dependent manner, influencing flavonoid and anthocyanin accumulation, thereby affecting growth and circadian rhythms. Our population genetic analyses further indicate that GPT2 has experienced divergent selection pressures in Pop_S compared to Pop_NE and Pop_NW. Indeed, accessions carrying different alleles of this leading SV (Chr04:1327804) showed significant differences in mannose content. Supporting this, the FST and π (nucleotide diversity) revealed more conserved genotype frequencies and a potential decline in genetic diversity near this locus in one or more subpopulations, consistent with selection (Fig. 5a, b). Given the strong correlation between mannose and xylose content with hemicellulose content, these findings suggest that selection on GPT2, potentially mediated by the upstream SV Chr04:1327804, may have contributed to the fast growth and high-quality wood characteristics observed in certain P. tomentosa accessions.

To dissect the genetic basis of heterosis, we found that phenotypic variation is driven by specific functional loci rather than global heterozygosity (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Data 13). Using a dominance-model GWAS, we identified 127 significant SVs, 82 of which exhibited distinct overdominance where heterozygous individuals significantly outperformed both homozygous groups (Supplementary Fig. 23a, and Supplementary Data 14). Analysis of the dominance degree (d/a) revealed a striking divergence: while the genomic background favored partial dominance (d/a ≈ 0.8), these adaptive SVs were significantly enriched in the overdominance range (|d/a | > 1.2; Supplementary Fig. 23b, c). This confirms that distinct from the background baseline, specific SVs with strong overdominance effects act as key drivers propelling the ‘breakthrough’ performance (hybrid vigor) in P. tomentosa (Supplementary Fig. 23d).

A 54-bp insertion in PtoERD3 promoter underlies lignin-mediated growth and drought adaptation

Combining GWAS, selective signature analysis, GEA, and tissue-specific expression screening, we detected a significant SV associated with lignin content (p = 1.02E-06), Chr02:13190129 (a 54-bp insertion), upstream of PtoERD3 which encodes an S-adenosyl-L-methionine-dependent methyltransferases superfamily protein (Fig. 5c–e, and Supplementary Fig. 24c, d). We found that the SV divided the natural population into two phenotypic groups. Accessions homozygous for the reference allele (0/0) exhibited significantly higher lignin content compared to those carrying the alternative allele (insertion) (Fig. 5f). Importantly, validation using natural population data confirmed that the 54-bp promoter insertion significantly represses PtoERD3 transcription and promoter activity, which subsequently leads to a decrease in lignin deposition at the cellular level (Fig. 5g, h, Supplementary Figs. 24b, 25). Further analysis of the geographical distribution of the three groups revealed that the 54-bp insertion had a lower frequency in Pop_NE and Pop_NW subpopulations, but was predominantly distributed in the Pop_S subpopulations, mirroring lignin content variation, suggesting it could be a feature of local adaptation (Fig. 5d–f, and Supplementary Fig. 24a). As this genetic variation correlated with changes in lignin content (a critical component that provides mechanical strength to xylem vessels to prevent collapse and maintain water transport under drought-induced tension), we hypothesized that the observed genotypic differences might be linked to varying long-term moisture conditions across these regions. To test this, we designed a drought experiment using transgenic Populus lines (Supplementary Fig. 26).

We generated PtoERD3 overexpression (PtoERD3-OE) and knockdown (PtoERD3-RNAi) lines and subjected them to drought treatment (Fig. 6a, b, and Supplementary Data 15). A central finding was that PtoERD3 directly controls lignin levels. Under normal conditions, PtoERD3-OE lines had an 18% increase in lignin content compared to the wild type (WT), while PtoERD3-RNAi lines showed a 14% decrease. After drought treatment, lignin content increased in all plants, but the increase was most pronounced in PtoERD3-OE (20%) and minimal in PtoERD3-RNAi (8%), strongly implicating the gene in this drought response (Fig. 6c). Given the critical role of lignin in the mechanical strength and hydraulic conductivity of the xylem, we next examined xylem morphology (Fig. 6f–i, and Supplementary Fig. 27c). As expected, PtoERD3-OE plants showed a significant increase in vessel density and a reduction in vessel lumen area. Crucially, scanning electron microscope (SEM) revealed that drought-induced xylem cell wall thickening was most conspicuous in PtoERD3-OE plants, but minimally observed in PtoERD3-RNAi lines. These lignin-mediated modifications in xylem structure translated directly into differences in drought tolerance (Fig. 6d, e, Supplementary Fig. 27). PtoERD3-OE lines maintained higher chlorophyll levels and significantly accumulated proline under drought, while knockdown lines showed the opposite trend. Ultimately, the drought-adaptive phenotypes were reflected in macroscopic growth (Supplementary Fig. 27): Under well-watered conditions, PtoERD3-OE lines displayed restricted growth, whereas PtoERD3-RNAi lines showed increased stem diameter and plant height. Under drought conditions, the enhanced lignin deposition in PtoERD3-OE lines was accompanied by the maintenance of higher chlorophyll levels and a significant accumulation of proline, while knockdown lines showed the opposite trend. We have supplemented the assessment using seedlings with highly consistent height and growth vigor (Supplementary Fig. 28, and Supplementary Fig. 29). These results collectively demonstrate that PtoERD3 enhances drought resistance by regulating lignin deposition, which in turn modifies xylem morphology to maintain efficient water transport under water-deficient conditions, ultimately shaping the plant’s overall growth and survival.

An SV molecular map provides comprehensive insights into wood properties improvement

SVs generally explained more heritability than SNPs and InDels. For gene expression, SVs accounted for the highest average heritability (h2 = 0.30) (Supplementary Fig. 30a). Heritability estimates by using joint SNPs and SVs across seven woody traits yield heritability estimates ranging from 0.046 to 0.385, consistently exceeding those from single-variant models (Supplementary Fig. 30b). For arabinose and lignin content, the joint analysis of SVs and SNPs produced higher heritability than the individual variant-type analyses, indicating additive or synergistic effects. In contrast, for galacturonic acid content, the joint heritability estimate (0.046) was notably lower. This suggests that for these wood traits, the contributions of SNPs and SVs may involve complex non-additive interactions, such as epistasis, or statistical redundancy in the combined model, rather than a simple additive relationship.

To leverage the minor-effect polygenic architecture of wood traits and accelerate breeding progress25,26, we implemented Genomic Prediction (GP) using a high-quality set of 3200 SV-GWAS markers. The GBLUP model, assessed via 5-fold cross-validation, consistently delivered high trait-specific prediction accuracies (0.54–0.94), with performance positively scaling with marker number (Supplementary Fig. 30c, and Supplementary Data 16). In arabinose, SNP-based models suffered from severe overfitting at high marker densities, whereas SV-based models maintained robust and high predictive power, highlighting the stability of SVs as genetic markers (Supplementary Fig. 30d, and Supplementary Data 17). Notably, even saturated SVs (mean = 883) maintained robust predictive performance (0.53–0.84), demonstrating the significant utility of these trait-associated SVs for marker-assisted selection (MAS) in wood quality improvement (Fig. 7a-c, Supplementary Fig. 30e, Supplementary Data 18). By aggregating saturated markers for each wood traits, we constructed a breeding navigation map comprising 3852 SVs, revealing the complex polygenic architecture of wood traits where most SVs exhibited small effect sizes (Fig. 7d, and Supplementary Fig. 30f). By utilizing the subset of significant SVs identified from GWAS, the GP model achieved a high average prediction accuracy of 0.65 for the seven wood traits. It is worth noting that this accuracy surpassed the estimated whole-genome heritability (mean h2 = 0.385). This is attributed to the exclusion of non-informative background markers, which enhanced the signal-to-noise ratio in the prediction model. Furthermore, using the Multi-Task Learning (MTL-GBLUP) framework with the union of these significant SVs, we demonstrated that a single consolidated marker set could robustly predict multiple wood composition traits simultaneously, maintaining high accuracy despite the complexity of the multi-trait architecture (Supplementary Data 19). Our precise calculation of phenotypic effects (beta values) for each SV revealed 2462 SVs with significant positive effects and 913 SVs with significant negative effects, with 1627 SVs exhibiting pleiotropic effects across multiple traits (Fig. 7d, and Supplementary Data 20).

Our framework identified two major genetic obstacles for simultaneous trait improvement. First, a subset of the pleiotropic SVs displayed antagonistic effects, simultaneously conferring both positive and negative impacts on different traits, rendering their improvement via conventional recombination-based breeding unfeasible. Secondly, we uncovered 70 high-confidence SV pairs with strong persistent LD (0.5 < R2 < 1 within 2 kb) (Fig. 7e). Critically, 52 pairs (75.6%) of SVs formed an advantage haplotype, which can be used as direct breeding targets. 17 of these pairs (24.4%) exhibited antagonistic pleiotropy, where the favorable allele of one trait was physically linked to the unfavorable allele of another. This “linkage drag” format was predominantly located in non-coding regions (89%) (Fig. 7e, and Supplementary Data 21). Despite these challenges, our map also highlights substantial untapped genetic potential (Fig. 7f). For negatively correlated traits like lignin and hemicellulose, we identified 447 SVs with opposing effects (+/-) that were highly enriched (99.7%) in the Pop_S subpopulation, reinforcing patterns of environmental selection. These SVs offer a genetic pathway for balancing wood components that is amenable to field screening. Conversely, for positively correlated traits like mannose and hemicellulose, nearly all pleiotropic SVs (99.49%) showed synergistic “win-win” (++) effects, indicating a high potential for simultaneous trait improvement. Such materials carrying these advantageous SVs will be highly valuable for future breeding programs (Fig. 7g, h, and Supplementary Data 22).

Discussion

Forest trees act as keystones in natural ecosystems and are pivotal for industrial resources and ecological protection. Populus, with its extensive geographical distribution, has accumulated abundant adaptive phenotypic variations over long periods of natural selection. Remarkably, different Populus species not only thrive in distinct regions but have also evolved specialized phenotypes to fully adapt to local environments. However, how SVs contribute to such adaptation under long-term selection remains poorly understood. In this study, we assembled a haplotype-resolved diploid reference genome for the elite P. tomentosa cultivar ‘1316’ (822.77 Mb). Unlike previous fragmented scaffold-level assemblies, this highly contiguous genome provides a robust foundation for identifying complex SVs with base pair precision13,14 (Table 1).

This resolution enables us to move beyond SNPs and explore the landscape of large-scale chromosomal rearrangements, which are increasingly recognized as critical drivers of phenotypic variation in forest trees5.

Leveraging this diploid assembly, we identified substantial structural divergence between the northeastern ecotype ‘1316’ and the northwestern ecotype ‘Tianshui’, particularly chromosomal inversions. We found that TEs and SDs were significantly enriched at inversion breakpoints, strongly suggesting that these rearrangements were generated via NAHR events21. This pattern appears conserved across distinct sections of the genus Populus (Supplementary Fig. 5). Evolutionarily, such TE-mediated inversions are significant and suppressing recombination in heterozygous individuals, they maintain co-adapted gene complexes (supergenes) that confer resilience to environmental stressors, effectively acting as “genomic islands” of divergence. Furthermore, our annotation revealed that TEs (mainly LTR retrotransposons) constitute 38.88% of the genome (Fig. 1a). Rather than mere genomic inflation, this TE proliferation likely serves as an “evolutionary capacitor.” For long-lived perennials like Populus that cannot rely on rapid generation turnover, TE drives structural plasticity including inversion formation, may be a crucial strategy for surviving fluctuating climates and expanding into diverse ecological niches27.

A defining contribution of this work is the establishment of the first high resolution SV map for P. tomentosa, creating an essential resource to dissect adaptation linked variation. By revealing pervasive SVs previously overlooked in SNP-based studies28, our catalog challenges traditional quantitative genetics. It demonstrates that comprehensive trait dissection requires integrating SVs due to their potential for altering gene dosage and regulatory architecture, thereby addressing the “missing heritability” problem. We validated our SV detection framework through rigorous population genomic analyses to confirm its high confidence13,29 (Fig. 2c–e). We further compared our catalog with a recent genome-wide analysis of P. trichocarpa30. Despite biological distinctions and divergent evolutionary histories, the overall SV landscape exhibited high concordance, particularly the prevalence of small deletions. This consistency points to a conserved evolutionary constraint across the genus, suggesting pervasive mutational pressure to remove non-essential DNA to counteract TE-driven genome expansion. Our evolutionary analysis reveals that SVs capture distinct genetic patterns compared to SNPs (Supplementary Fig. 2), identifying candidate selective genes (e.g., CBF4, HSP60, SOS1) often overlooked in SNP-based scans (Fig. 2f, g). While SNPs typically facilitate polygenic adaptation via fine tuning protein function, SVs often act as large effect mutations driving rapid phenotypic shifts in response to drastic environmental pressures. These results demonstrate that SVs must be considered to gain a full understanding of how selection shapes genomic variation31, emphasizing the necessity of integrating multiple variant types. Moreover, distinct nucleotide polymorphism patterns between regulatory and coding SVs suggest that cis-regulatory alterations may drive rapid adaptation. Integrating SVs with transcriptomic data demonstrates that deletions exert the strongest impact on gene expression, largely through dosage reduction or complete loss5 (Fig. 3c–f). Whereas insertions expand genomic complexity. Furthermore, SV hotspots overlapping key regulatory genes underscore the pleiotropic effects of structural rearrangements across biological processes. Collectively, SVs drive larger-scale genomic alterations and more pronounced expression shifts than SNPs, providing stronger selective advantages that accelerate adaptive evolution. While SV formation involves diverse molecular mechanisms, we observed a higher prevalence of SDs rather than TEs in SV regions, likely attributable to the limited domestication history of trees compared to crops.

To elucidate environmental drivers of adaptation, we applied GEA analysis, identifying key genes underlying local adaptation (Fig. 4a–c). In addition, Additionally, our SV-GWAS uncovered numerous SVs associated with key wood quality traits, confirming their contribution to these economically vital phenotypes. Our integration of GEA and SV-GWAS reveals that the adaptive landscape of P. tomentosa is overwhelmingly shaped by variation in non-coding regulatory regions. This resonates with the theory that regulatory divergence acts as the primary engine of adaptation in complex eukaryotes32,33. Unlike annual crops, long-lived sessile perennials must endure centuries of fluctuating climates. We propose that the abundance of cis-regulatory SVs provides necessary “genomic elasticity,” allowing trees to mount physiological responses without compromising essential protein functions. This regulatory architecture is particularly critical for managing the fundamental growth-defense trade-off. Furthermore, this landscape offers a nuanced perspective on heterosis: while the genomic background is largely governed by partial dominance (additive effects), we found that specific “breakthrough” performance is propelled by SVs with strong overdominance effects. This implies that maximizing genetic gain requires a two-tiered strategy that pyramiding additive alleles for stability while strategically exploiting heterozygosity at key regulatory loci for peak potential.

Finally, our SV-GWAS framework generates a “SV-breeding navigation map” for P. tomentosa, a compendium of markers with estimated effects on target traits. While our map identifies elite targets, it also uncovers a profound evolutionary hurdle: antagonistic pleiotropy reinforced by tight physical linkage. The nature of these 18 pairs, distinct from linkage drag in rice (~25% of the genome)34 or sunflower (~10% of the genome)35. Located predominantly in non-coding regions (~89%), these tight linkages likely reflect adaptive canalization selected to prevent maladaptive phenotypes in the wild. However, for modern breeding, these constraints function as an “unbreakable ceiling” that traditional breeding cannot resolve36,37. Crucially, future iterations of this map must expand beyond the current additive framework to incorporate epistatic interactions and epigenetic layers. Since most adaptive SVs are non-coding, resolving their regulatory logic requires chromatin-level data, while modeling gene networks will help pinpoint precise “hub nodes” for editing. By combining these multi-dimensional insights with targeted decoupling of linked antagonisms, we can pave the way for the de novo design of superior varieties beyond the limits of conventional breeding38.

Methods

Plant materials and phenotyping measurements

The study utilized a total of 302 P. tomentosa accessions were chosen because it maximizes the representation of the natural distribution of P. tomentosa in China (30-40°N, 105-124°E). Based on 16 environmental factors (including light, heat, and precipitation), these 302 genotypes are categorized into three distinct subpopulations based on geographic origins and genetic clustering: the South subpopulation (Pop_S), comprising 101 accessions from the southern region; the Northeast subpopulation (Pop_NE), comprising 108 accessions from the northeastern region; and the Northwest subpopulation (Pop_NW), comprising 93 accessions from the northwestern region17. These accessions were cultivated in Guan Xian County, Shandong Province, China (36°23′N, 115°47′ E) in 2009. The P. tomentosa genotype ‘1316’, an elite cultivar known for its rapid growth and high stress resistance in Northern China, was selected for genome assembly.

Wood composition analyses were conducted following a previously outlined method39. In summary, 0.3 g of oven-dried sample powder was placed in a pressure tube with 3 mL of 72% concentrated sulfuric acid and subjected to incubation in an oscillating water bath at 30 °C for 1 hour. The sulfuric acid was then diluted to 4% by adding 84 mL of deionized water before the tube was transferred to a sterilizing pot and incubated at 121 °C for 1 hour. The levels of glucose, xylose, arabinose, and other monosaccharides were determined by HPLC (HPLC, Waters, e2695, USA) paired with HPX-87H chromatographic column (Bio-Rad Laboratories, USA). Subsequently, the crucible and acid-insoluble residues were dried at 105 °C for 4 hours, then weighed, and transferred to a muffle furnace at 575 °C for 6 hours, and reweighed; the acid-insoluble lignin content was calculated by multiplying the weight difference by two. All measurements were performed with three biological replicates. Descriptive analysis and multiple comparisons were conducted using SPSS Statistics v19.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL).

Genome sequencing and assembly of Populus tomentosa ‘1316’

We sequenced the genome using Illumina NovaSeq 6000 and PacBio Sequel II platforms, complemented with Hi-C maps. We estimated the genome size of the ‘1316’ accession to be ~390 Mb based on the k-mer distribution of Illumina short reads, utilizing Jellyfish (v2.3.0)40 and GenomeScope (v2.0)41. This resulted in 68 Gb of Illumina data (174.36-fold coverage). For 10X Genomics sequencing, a total of 56.40 Gb of data (136-fold coverage) was acquired on the Illumina HiSeq X-Ten. For Hi-C, 61.6 Gb of data were sequenced on the same platform in 150-bp paired-end mode. To obtain a high-quality haplotype-resolved assembly, we utilized hifiasm (v0.16.1)42 to assemble the PacBio reads, integrating Hi-C data to generate phased contigs. Subsequently, we also performed de novo assembly using the CANU (v2.2)43 and HERA (v1.0)44 pipelines for comparison. Self-alignment of whole-genome contig sequences was performed using default parameters of BWA-MEM45, and heterozygous sequences were filtered with Redundans46 (parameters: -t 10, -identity 0.55, -overlap 0.80, --noscaffolding, and -nogapclosing) and Purge Haplotigs47 (default parameters). Overlaps between contig sequences were merged based on the BWA-MEM self-alignment results.

Transcriptome data were processed using both de novo and reference guided strategies

De novo assembly was performed using Trinity (v2.8.5)48 with default parameters to generate transcripts independent of the genomic sequence. Reference-based assembly was conducted by mapping RNA-seq reads to the ‘1316’ genome. The assembled transcripts from both methods were aligned to the genome using the PASA (Program to Assemble Spliced Alignments v2.5.3) pipeline49 to define gene structures. Candidate coding regions within the transcript sequences were identified using TransDecoder (v5.7.1) (Haas, BJ. https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder). Ab initio gene prediction was performed using Augustus (v3.3.3)50. To improve prediction accuracy, high-quality gene models generated by PASA were typically used to train the Augustus parameters specific to the species. Finally, EvidenceModeler (EVM v2.1.0)51 was used to compute weighted consensus gene structure annotations. EVM integrated the evidence from the transcriptome assemblies (via PASA), ab initio predictions (via Augustus), to generate the final non-redundant gene set.

Identification repetitive elements and segmental duplications

To structurally annotate transposable elements (TEs) in ‘1316’ genome, we used the Extensive De-Novo TE Annotator (EDTA) v.2.0.1 with parameter ‘--species others --step all --t 20 --u 2.5e-9’ to generate the non-redundant de novo TE libraries and annotated intact long terminal repeat retrotransposons (LTRs)52. The insertion time of each intact LTR was also provided by the software. The segmental duplications in ‘1316’ genome were identified using Brisk Inference of Segmental duplication Evolutionary stRucture (BISER) software53.

Genome resequencing and variant calling

Raw reads were trimmed through a series of quality control procedures. The clean reads obtained after sequencing were compared using BWA software45 against Haplotype A of the ‘1316’ genome, which was designated as the primary reference due to its superior assembly metrics, particularly in terms of contiguity and completeness, with the specific use of the parameter ‘bwa mem-M-R’. Subsequently, the alignment results underwent sorting and PCR duplicate marking using Genome Analysis Toolkit 4.0 (GATK 4.0)54. Reads with low mapping quality (MQ < 20) were filtered using the SAMtools program v1.19.255. Genetic variants, including SNPs and InDels, were then detected using the GATK 4.0 with conservative parameters, a pipeline selected for its distinct advantages in base quality score recalibration and highly accurate genotype likelihood calculations. SNPs were filtered using the following parameters: call quality divided by depth (QD) < 5.0; mapping quality (MQ) < 40.0; Fisher’s exact test (FS) > 60.0; SOR > 3.0; MQRankSum < −12.5; ReadPosRankSum < −8.0. InDels were filtered with the following parameters: QD < 5.0, RD > 5; FS > 200.0; SOR > 10.0; MQRankSum <−12.5; ReadPosRankSum <−8.0. Finally, we pruned SNPs by removing SNPs with more than two alleles, minor allele frequency (MAF) < 5% and a missing genotype (MG) > 20% using Vcftools v0.1.1756.

For SVs detection, BAM files from each sample were analyzed using Manta57. Furthermore, Manta was utilized for population-level SV discovery by directly importing BAM files from all individuals, a choice motivated by its high precision (low false discovery rate) and high recall performance benchmarked against other SV callers. Only SVs marked with “PASS” were retained for subsequent analysis. To consolidate the population’s SV calls, svimmer (https://github.com/DecodeGenetics/svimmer), a tool specifically designed for merging Manta-derived SVs was employed. Subsequently, Graphtyper was used for genotyping these merged population-level SVs58. Finally, all detected genetic variations were functionally annotated using ANNOVAR59.

Population structure and phylogenetic analysis

Population structure and phylogenetic analysis were performed using both SNP and SV datasets. To ensure data quality, variants were filtered based on a minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 0.05 and a missing rate of less than 20%. For the SNP dataset, we further minimized the influence of physical linkage by performing LD pruning with an r2 threshold of 0.7. Population structure was inferred by Admixture v1.3.060 with the cluster number K from 2 to 8. To avoid parameter standard errors, each K was run with ten replicates. K = 3 was chosen based on the minimum cross-validation (CV) error value. We calculated pairwise p-distances with VCF2Dis61 and FastME 2.062 to construct the Neighbor-joining tree (NJ tree). Visualization was conducted using MEGA763. Principal Component Analysis was performed using the GCTA parameters64, resulting in the visualization of the first three principal components (PC1, PC2, PC3). LD decay was estimated on the basis of the r2 value using PopLDdecay v3.34 with default parameters65. Detailed computational pipelines and custom scripts are available in the GitHub repository.

RNA isolation and sequencing

To investigate the expression patterns of genes in different tissues, a number of tissue types, including peak, leaves, bark, root, phloem, cambium, immature xylem, and mature xylem of 10-year-old P. tomentosa clones ‘LM50’ planted in Guan Xian County, were subjected to RNA-seq analysis. Total RNA was extracted using the Qiagen RNAeasy kit (Qiagen China, Shanghai, China) with three biological replicates. Paired-end sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2000 system (Illumina, San Diego, CA). The clean data obtained was aligned to the “1316” genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0)66. The BAM files for all the RNA-seq samples were assembled using Stringtie v.2.0.467. Total RNAs were extracted from the cambium of 120 unrelated P. tomentosa plants comprising 40 accessions from each of the Pop_S, Pop_NE and Pop_NW subpopulations (Supplementary Data 4). The clean data were mapped to the “1316” genome using HISAT2 with default parameters using the methods described above.

Selective sweep analysis and SV selection during Populus evolution

We evaluated the effect of selected SV on gene regions, including coding and non-coding regions, based on the location and length of SV (the 2 kb region before and after the gene was defined as the upstream and downstream). Our study detected outlier selection events between three geographical regions of P. tomentosa by population nucleotide diversity (π) and quantified population genetic differentiation (FST) and XP-CLR value68 calculated by SNP. The FST value was calculated by VCFtools, π value was using a window size of 10 kb and a step size of 5 kb. We selected windows with FST values (top 5%) as significant signals for selective sweep regions. According to the genome annotation, the genes annotated in the regions are defined as scanned genes. To evaluate the SV significance in Populus evolution, we calculated the SV allele frequencies of each of the three subpopulations. Depending on the direction of the evolutionary process, SVs with higher or lower frequency are identified as positive or negative selection. The significance of SV frequency difference between pairwise subpopulations was calculated, and the P-value was corrected by false discovery rate (FDR) test. SVs with significant frequency difference (P.adjust <0.01) were considered as candidate selected SVs.

Recombination rate and local PCA

We estimated recombination rates from intraspecific DNA polymorphism data using FastEPRR69, a machine learning-based approach, with a 10-kb sliding window. We then used the R package lostruct to perform local principal component analysis (PCA) on a set of SNPs, which enabled us to identify genomic outlier regions with anomalous population structure70. The dissimilarity between windows was quantified by calculating the distance between the first two principal components using the function and subsequently visualized in two dimensions via multidimensional scaling (MDS) with the function (pc_dist, cmdscale).

Identification of environment-associated genetic variants

Two distinct methods were employed to identify SVs associated with environmental adaptation. Firstly, the LFMM model from the R package LEA was utilized to investigate the relationship between genetic variation and environmental variables71. Ancestral clustering numbers, obtained from ADMIXTURE v.1.3.0, informed this analysis. For each environmental variable, five independent MCMC runs were performed, with 5,000 burn-in iterations followed by 10,000 sampling iterations. P-values for each variant across the five runs were subsequently averaged, and a 5% False Discovery Rate (FDR) correction was applied as a significance cutoff to adjust for multiple testing. Secondly, Redundancy Analysis (RDA)72,73 was employed to identify genomic variations strongly correlated with multivariate environmental axes, leveraging its ability to capture complex interplay among environmental variables. To select the most informative environmental predictors for RDA, 19 BIOCLIM variables74 were prioritized. Variables exhibiting a pairwise correlation coefficient of |r | <0.70 were retained, and their importance was further ranked using Random Forest analysis. These selected variables then served as environmental predictors in the RDA, implemented with the Vegan and RandomForest R packages. Environment-associated variants were identified as those with loadings in the extreme tails of the distribution, defined by a standard deviation cutoff of 3 along one or more RDA axes. Finally, ANNOVAR was used to functionally annotate the adaptive variants identified by both LFMM and RDA. These variants, termed “core adaptive variants,” were then subjected to detailed comparative analysis to elucidate their roles in mediating environmental influences on spatial genomic variation.

Structural variations based genome-wide association studies

In order to identify candidate genes for each trait, SV-GWAS was performed for seven wood traits (arabinose, galactose, xylose, mannose, galacturonic acid, lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose). To improve the accuracy of the results, we removed those with minor allele frequencies (MAF < 0.05) and missing rate (MR > 0.2) of the SVs. Similar to SNP-based GWAS, our SV-GWAS is based on the additive genetic model, where SVs are treated as bi-allelic markers. Specifically, SVs were genotyped based on their presence or absence and encoded numerically (e.g., 0, 1, 2) representing the dosage of the variant allele. SV-GWAS uses GEMMA software75, using the kinship matrix (K) and population structure (Q) as covariates. The significance level of the association results was defined as P < 2.03e-5 (1/n, n = 49380).

Dominance genome-wide association study based on genotypic model

To dissect the genetic architecture of wood traits and specifically capture non-additive effects (e.g., dominance and overdominance), we performed a GWAS using a general linear model (GLM). Unlike standard additive models that treat genotypes as continuous variables (0, 1, 2), we modeled genotypes as categorical factors. This approach avoids the assumption of linearity and allows for the independent estimation of phenotypic means for reference homozygous, heterozygous, and alternative homozygous genotypes. The model was implemented in R using the lm function, with the first two principal components (PCs) included as covariates to correct for population structure. For each significant locus (P < 0.001), the degree of dominance (d/a) was calculated using the genotypic means estimated from the GLM: d/a = (Mean_Het – Mid_Homo) / (0.5 * |Mean_Ref - Mean_Alt | ), where Mid_Homo = (Mean_Ref + Mean_Alt)/2. The loci were classified into additivity (|d/a | ≤ 0.2), partial dominance (0.2 <|d/a | ≤ 0.8), complete dominance (0.8 <|d/a | ≤ 1.2), and overdominance (|d/a | > 1.2). To ensure the statistical robustness of the ratio, traits exhibiting severe zero-inflation or vanishing additive effects (a ≈ 0) were excluded from this specific analysis to prevent mathematical artifacts caused by ratio inflation.

Heritability estimation and genomic prediction

Heritability of traits explained by genetic variations was estimated using LDAK (v5.1)76, which is available at https://www.ldak.org. Genomic prediction (GP) was performed utilizing the R packages BGLR77 and rrBLUP, while machine learning models were implemented with the Python package scikit-learn. Machine learning models include RandomForest, LightGBM, and XGBoost. A consistent 5-fold cross-validation strategy was employed across all analyses. SVs were encoded as 0, 1, and 2, representing the count of alternative alleles. This encoding follows the additive genetic model, assuming that the dosage of SVs contributes linearly to the phenotypic variance, thereby allowing the model to capture heritability derived from large-scale genomic rearrangements. Prediction accuracy was defined as the Pearson correlation coefficient between predicted and observed phenotypic values. To investigate the influence of marker density on prediction accuracy, we specifically used the GBLUP model incorporating trait-associated SVs as a priori information. Logistic regression was then applied to determine the “saturation number of markers,” defined as the number of markers required to achieve 95% of the maximum prediction accuracy.

Detection of genome-wide introgression

We performed D-statistic (ABBA-BABA test) to detect interspecific gene flow among the section Populus sect. Leuce using Dsuite v0.578 (Supplementary Data 4). Based on the phylogenetic relationships, we defined the four-taxon topology as ((P1, P2), P3, Outgroup), where the taxa were assigned as follows: P1: P. alba, P2: P. tomentosa, P3: P. adenoposa Outgroup: P. nigra. The Dtrios program within the Dsuite package was used to calculate the D-statistic and the f_4-ratio (admixture proportion) for this combination. To assess statistical significance, we performed a block jackknife procedure to estimate the standard error and calculate Z-scores. A D value was considered to indicate significant introgression if the absolute Z-score was greater than 3 ( | Z | > 3).
