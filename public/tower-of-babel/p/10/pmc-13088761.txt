An Integral Hybrid Polymeric Biostimulant to Cope With Drought Stress in Bread Wheat
Authors: Aldo Borjas, Tsiky Andriantelomanana, Céline Dupuits, Said Mouzeyar, Cédric Delattre, Jane Roche
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDrought‐induced water scarcity is a pressing challenge in agriculture, as it is exacerbated by climate change. Superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) have emerged as effective tools to mitigate drought stress by improving soil water retention and nutrient delivery. However, the environmental concerns surrounding synthetic SAPs necessitate the development of sustainable, biobased alternatives. This study evaluates the efficacy of two innovative biobased SAPs, alginate‐based (SAP) and alginate‐chitosan‐based (BioSAP), compared to a commercial polyacrylate (PAK) in enhancing drought resilience in bread wheat ( Triticum aestivum ). Physiological parameters, including Turgor Loss Point (TLP), leaf water potential, stomatal conductance, electrolyte leakage, and proline content, were monitored under well‐watered and drought‐stressed conditions. The results demonstrate that BioSAP significantly improved drought tolerance by enhancing osmotic adjustment, delaying TLP. BioSAP‐treated plants exhibited superior resilience, with reduced cell damage and moderated proline accumulation, indicating efficient stress tolerance. The dual functionality of BioSAP, combining water retention with biostimulant properties, promoted metabolic adaptations, including solute mobilization and cell wall rigidity. These findings highlight the potential of biobased SAPs, particularly BioSAP, as a sustainable alternative for drought stress mitigation, contributing to environmentally friendly and efficient agricultural practices.Keywords: biostimulants, bread wheat, drought stress, ecophysiology, superabsorbent

Introduction

Drought‐induced water scarcity is a critical issue in agriculture, requiring innovative strategies to improve crop resilience and productivity. Irrigated farming, despite being the largest consumer of water, plays a crucial role in supporting food production in arid regions (Molden 2007; Trail and Ward 2024). However, climate change exacerbates the frequency and severity of extreme weather events, including recurrent droughts, which lead to significant water shortages and reduced agricultural outputs (Rudraswamy and Umamahesh 2024).

Synthetic superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) have emerged as a promising solution to improve water management in soil and plants. They have been produced through the polymerization or crosslinking of monomers or polymers derived from acrylic acid or acrylamides with high water retention (Chen et al. 2022). SAPs are three‐dimensional polymeric networks composed of hydrophilic chains that can absorb, swell, and retain water solutions, often up to hundreds of times their dry weight. Their primary functionality lies in absorbing large quantities of water, which increases soil water reserves during periods of scarcity and minimizes surface runoff. Furthermore, SAPs are incorporated into double systems as slow‐release matrices for fertilizers, enhancing nutrient efficiency (Akbarzadeh et al. 2024; Cui et al. 2024; Dardeer et al. 2024; Hafezi Moghaddam et al. 2024; Song et al. 2024; Zhu and Yao 2013).

However, their poor biodegradability, estimated at only 10% per year (Rozman and Kalčíková 2021; Wilske et al. 2014) and their hydrophilic and reactive polar groups raise concern about environmental persistence and mobility (Nigro et al. 2024). These characteristics induce an alarming dispersion of synthetic SAPs into various environmental compartments, potentially leading to the accumulation of degradation by‐products (Groh et al. 2023; Schmidt 2019). Despite efforts to develop biosourced SAPs, the inclusion of acrylates in some formulations, even in reduced amounts, remains problematic, as these compounds continue to expose ecosystems to the same level of risks (Mondellini et al. 2022; Rozman and Kalčíková 2021; Weston et al. 2009).

These reports underscore the urgent need for truly sustainable alternatives to synthetic polymers, such as polysaccharides, to mitigate these environmental concerns. Polysaccharides, including starch, cellulose, chitosan, alginate, and glucomannans, have gained attention for their ability to create eco‐friendly SAPs (Qureshi et al. 2020). These natural polymers offer advantages such as biocompatibility, biodegradability, and renewability. Although they face challenges like limited reactivity, these can be addressed through chemical modifications such as crosslinking, blending, or functionalization, allowing them to achieve performance comparable to synthetic SAPs (Zhu et al. 2024). The abundance of functional groups in polysaccharides provides a basis for developing highly hydrophilic and effective superabsorbent materials, taking a significant step toward sustainable agricultural practices.

Chitosan and alginates are promising polysaccharides due to their valuable properties; their amines and carboxylic groups facilitate physical interactions as well as ionic and covalent crosslinking (Cheng et al. 2017). Moreover, they have been classified as biostimulants capable of mitigating environmental stresses (du Jardin 2015). Indeed, chitosan and alginates are known to induce drought tolerance by maintaining the osmolyte balance and reducing reactive oxygen species through an enhanced antioxidant system, but also by increasing the chlorophyll content and photosynthesis efficiency (Liu et al. 2013, 2020; Zhang et al. 2017). The novelty of combining water retention capacity with biostimulant properties in the natural SAPs offers a dual benefit, expanding the strategies for plant resilience. This dual functionality enables plants to increase survival rates during water scarcity or in reduced irrigation while the biostimulant effects also enhance plant health by detoxifying, enhancing photosynthesis, and promoting growth.

In this study, we evaluated the physiological effect of two formulations based on alginate and chitosanes, formulated as superabsorbents and with tuned biostimulant capacities (Borjas, Delattre, et al. 2025). The two formulations, SAP and BioSAP, used in this study constitute a source of polyelectrolyte with binding capacities, eco‐compatibility, biodegradability and as a source of biostimulation for plants and soil microbiota. One approach to evaluate the interaction between SAPs and plants is to analyze water dynamics within the plant–soil system under SAP treatment. Measuring parameters such as water potential, stomatal conductance, and relative water content can provide insights into the plant's overall response and its interaction with the surrounding matrix. Physiological markers such as the leaf water potential at zero turgor or the turgor loss point (Ψtlp; TLP) are particularly valuable. TLP is considered a reliable indicator of drought tolerance and water availability in plants (Bartlett, Scoffoni, Ardy, et al. 2012; Bartlett, Scoffoni, and Sack 2012; Farrell et al. 2017). Plants with lower Ψtlp values tend to perform better under low water potential, maintaining metabolic functions and growth even when soil water content is limited.

This study aims to evaluate and compare the efficacy of polyacrylate‐based and natural alginate‐chitosan SAPs in mitigating the effects of severe drought stress on bread wheat, focusing on both water retention and biostimulant properties. The performance of these new eco‐compatible SAPs was evaluated using pressure‐volume curves, water potential, relative water content, stomatal conductance, proline content, and electrolyte leakage to measure their impact on wheat drought resilience.

Materials and Methods

Plant Material and Treatment Application

Seeds of bread wheat ( Triticum aestivum , genotype Izalco) were sown in nursery pots (8 × 8 × 7 cm) with three seeds in each. The pots were placed in a growth chamber (21°C, 60% humidity, photoperiod of 16/8 h light/dark/400 μmol/m2/s). After 15 days of growth, the seedlings were subjected to vernalization at 4°C with the same photoperiod. After 2 months of vernalization, the plants were transferred into 5 L pots containing potting substrate supplemented with superabsorbent polymers or left untreated (control), as described below.

The potting substrate used (Humustar Terreaux) was composed of 15% brown peat, 20% blond peat, 20% fine composted pine bark and 40% disinfected topsoil (Table S1) and was supplemented with slow‐release fertilizer at 2 kg/m3 (Fertil S.A; Nutricot T140, NPK: 10/8/18+ Oligoelements).

Three superabsorbent polymers were evaluated: potassium polyacrylate (PAK; Alquera), a superabsorbent formulated with alginates (SAP), and one BioSuperabsorbent formulated with alginate and chitosan (BioSAP) (Borjas, Delattre, et al. 2025). These treatments were compared with a control without superabsorbent. See Table 1 for their characteristics.

For the superabsorbent treatments, the polymers (PAK, SAP and BioSAP) were homogeneously mixed into the topping soil at a dose of 3 g.L−1 of polymer, following the results of Abrisham et al. (2018). The control treatment received the same substrate without polymer addition.

Each treatment consisted of 16 replicate pots, each containing six plants. The soil weight was standardized to 1.4 kg of humid potting soil (53% humidity). The plants were grown for 1 month before measuring all parameters.

Physiological Kinetics Under Drought Stress

After 1 month of growth under the different treatments, the plants were watered to the maximum water holding capacity (WHC). Then 12 pots were subjected to progressive drought (WD) by withholding irrigation, while 4 were maintained well‐watered (WW) by maintaining 100% of the WHC (Figure 1).

Simplified representation of the experimental design over time. The experiment was conducted in three main steps: (1) plants were grown under controlled conditions and subjected to vernalization; (2) plants were separated into four treatment groups (Control, PAK, SAP, and BioSAP); (3) drought stress was imposed by withholding irrigation, followed by rewatering. Sampling was performed at different time points during the stress period, as indicated.

At 9 days of stress, 6 pots were rewatered (Recovery 1; R1). After 11 days of stress, another group of 3 pots was rewatered (Recovery 2; R2). The final group of 3 pots was left unwatered until the end of the experiment (12 days) to ensure samples that reach mortality. Relative water content, leaf water potential, stomatal conductance, electrolyte leakage, and proline content were followed till the plants showed a severe level of stress.

Relative Water Content (RWC)

Relative water content was evaluated every 3 days by selecting one leaf from the second node from the base. The leaf was then weighed to determine the fresh weight (FW) and then immersed in water for 4 h in darkness to determine the turgid weight (TW). Leaf Water Potential

Midday leaf water potential was measured between 12 a.m. and 2 p.m. at solar time using a Scholander‐type pressure chamber (PMS). Measurements were performed on the second leaf from the base of the plant. The measures were carried out every day until the pressure was too low to be measured.

Stomatal Conductance

Stomatal conductance was measured on the abaxial surface of the flag leaves using a porometer (SC‐1; Meter Group) between 12 a.m. and 2 p.m. Sampling was performed on the flag leaf of each individual and for at least three individuals per condition until no further measurements were possible (the stomatal conductance reached 10% because leaves dried out).

Electrolyte Leakage

Electrolyte leakage measurement was used to assess the cell damage induced by drought. Three leaves were collected every 3 days; the middle portion of the leaf was sampled (10 × 10 cm) and then cut into four pieces (2.5 × 2.5 cm). Samples were placed in tubes with 15 mL of ultrapure water and shaked 24 h at 5°C in darkness on an orbital shaker. After 24 h, the electrical conductivity of the solution (C1) was measured at room temperature using a conductivity meter (Cond 3310 m; WTW, Xylem Analytics Germany GmbH) and a sensor (Tetracon 325 sensor; WTW, Xylem Analytics Germany GmbH) with an accuracy of 1 μS/cm to 2 S/cm. Following C1 measurement, the tubes were autoclaved at 121°C for 40 min to disrupt the cells. Then, the samples were placed at room temperature to measure the last conductivity value (C2). Proline Content Determination

The proline content was evaluated at 0, 8, and 12 days after the beginning of the stress (DAS). At each point, two leaves per individual and at least three plants were collected from each treatment (Well Water, Water Deficient, and the rewatered pots, Recovery 1 and Recovery 2). Immediately, leaves were frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground, and stored at −80°C.

The free proline content was quantified using a colorimetric assay, following the protocol established by Abrahám et al. (2010). Approximately 50 mg of FW tissue was extracted with 500 μL of 3% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid and centrifuged at 9500 × g for 5 min at room temperature. Subsequently, 100 μL of supernatant was transferred to a clean 1.5 mL tube, and 500 μL of reaction mixture (composed of 100 μL of 3% (w/v) sulfosalicylic acid, 200 μL glacial acetic acid, and 200 μL acidic ninhydrin) was added, and the tubes were incubated at 96°C for 60 min. After incubation, the reaction was quenched on ice (5 min), and the chromophore was extracted by adding 1 mL of toluene, vortexing the samples for 20 s, leading to the separation of the organic phase from the aqueous phase. The extracted chromophore in the toluene phase was then measured at 520 nm, with toluene used as the blank reference. Proline concentration was determined using a standard curve (0–100 μg/mL) and expressed on a fresh weight basis.

Evaluation of Pressure–Volume Curves

Pressure–volume (PV) curves are used to evaluate the turgor pressure, the osmotic pressure, the elastic characteristics of the cells and the apoplastic fraction, along with tracking changes in these properties as cell water content varies under conditions of water/osmotic stress (Koide et al. 1989; Tyree and Hammel 1972) (Figure S1).

Following the application of treatments and prior to subjecting the plants to drought conditions, three pots per treatment were rehydrated in darkness for at least 24 h (Banks and Hirons 2019; Rodriguez‐Dominguez et al. 2022). Then, eight leaves per treatment were collected to generate the PV curves. First, the Ψ Leaf was measured using a Scholander‐type pressure chamber (PMS) and leaf weight evolution was monitored using an analytical balance (Sartorius CP224s; Sartorius AG). Successive measurements were conducted until the leaf mass was stabilized. The leaf dry mass was determined by drying the leaves at 70°C for 72 h.

A PV curve was also generated using the leaf water potential and RWC measured during stress.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis and graphs were performed using RStudio software (with R core v.4.4.1). For normalized data, an ANOVA test was performed followed by a post hoc Tukey HSD test when effects were significant. For data that were not found normal, Kruskal‐Wallis non‐parametric test was performed followed by a post hoc Dunn Test with BH correction when effects were significant.

During the drought stress kinetics, for each sampling day, the mean value was calculated for the physiological measurements and used as a single data point for sigmoidal curve fitting.

All the regression models were fitted with SIGMAPLOT. For the linear regression models and for sigmoid regression models (non‐linear regression (NLS), four parameters logistic curve) was fitted. All statistical analyses were based on a 0.05 significance level.

Results

Physiological Responses of Superabsorbent‐Treated Plants Under Well Water and Water Deficit Conditions

Visual phenotypic characterization (Figure 2) was followed throughout the experiment to evaluate the global and visual effects of the superabsorbent treatments tested. The effects of those superabsorbent treatments were monitored under two main conditions, well‐watered (WW) and water‐deficit (WD), as well as after two sets of recovery phases: Recovery (R1) and Recovery 2 (R2).

Phenotypic evaluation of control and treated plants. Plants grown without superabsorbent (Control), with polyacrylate of potassium (PAK), with alginate superabsorbent (SAP) or with alginate bioactive superabsorbent (BioSAP) under Well‐Watered (WW), Water deficit (WD), Recovery (R1; 9 days of stress and 2 days of recovery) and Recovery 2 (R2; 11 days of stress and 1 day of recovery).

Under well water conditions, the control‐ and PAK‐treated plants showed a higher number of leaves than SAP‐ and BioSAP‐treated plants. In fact, SAP and BioSAP decrease the number of stems. However, in the control‐ and PAK‐treated plants, the flowering stage was delayed. Globally, after 12 days of drought, plants exhibit a wilting phenotype regardless of the treatment. Additionally, the first leaves from the base were totally dried in control and PAK‐treated plants. Also, SAP‐ and BioSAP‐treated plants accelerated spike emergence by 1 week earlier than control‐ and PAK‐treated plants. Under drought stress, only SAP‐ and BioSAP‐ spikes were fully developed. Similarly, after the recovery periods (R1 and R2), only plants under SAP‐ and BioSAP treatments showed spike development. Generally, the bio‐superabsorbent treatments (SAP and BioSAP) alleviated the drought impact on vegetative development (less chlorosis) better.

Dynamics of Electrolyte Leakage (EL %) of Wheat Plants Under Drought Stress

Electrolyte leakage (EL) was used as an indicator of cell damage induced by the drought treatment (Figure 6). High levels of cell damage were observed in plants without the superabsorbent treatment, beginning at 6 days after water deprivation. By 8 days, a significant increase in EL was detected in control plants, whereas plants treated with PAK, SAP, and BioSAP exhibited considerably less cell damage. Beyond 8 days of water stress, differences among superabsorbents became evident, with BioSAP‐treated plants showing the lowest levels of cell damage, followed by the PAK‐treated plants.

Proline Content Evolution

At the onset of stress, proline content of the leaves remains relatively low, with no significant differences observed between the treatments (Control, PAK, SAP and BioSAP) or water conditions (WW vs. WD) (Figure 7). However, after 8 days of drought stress, a noticeable increase in proline content under WD compared to WW conditions was observed. Control and PAK‐treated plants exhibit significantly higher proline levels, reaching approximately 4 μg/g FW. In contrast, plants treated with SAP (p < 0.05) and BioSAP (p < 0.001) show a significantly lower proline content compared with the control, suggesting a differentiated response to water stress depending on the superabsorbent treatment. Finally, at 12 days of stress, proline levels in leaf tissues increased drastically, reaching more than 5 μg/g FW, indicating a significant physiological response to water deprivation, regardless of the treatment. But BioSAP‐treated plants (p < 0.001) displayed a notably lower proline production compared with the control plants, suggesting a mitigated stress response.

Osmotic Adjustment Before and During Stress

Three values of osmotic adjustment (OA) were obtained for each treatment (Table 3). The osmotic adjustment obtained before applying the stress is greater when the PAK and BioSAP treatments are present (0.29 and 0.25, respectively), while the SAP reveals a diminution of osmotic potential. When plants are submitted to water deprivation, an increase of osmotic potential was observed for all the treatments when compared with control treated plants. The greater impact is observed in BioSAP‐treated plants with 0.65 MPa of OA, followed by PAK and SAP treatments, respectively 0.46 and 0.31 MPa.

The total impact of the treatments was compared before stress and during stress. Plants without treatments show an increase of 0.17 MPa of OA; meanwhile, the PAK enhanced two times more than control (0.34 MPa), the BioSAP three times more than control (0.57 MPa), and the SAP six times more than control (1.02 MPa).

Discussion

Bioactive Superabsorbent Treatments Improve Physiological Efficiency Under Drought

The global phenotypes of the plants were observed throughout their development. In general, the plants under superabsorbent treatments maintain their turgidity under water stress, whereas the control plants exhibit a wilting phenotype. When the bioformulations were applied (SAP and BioSAP), an acceleration of the flowering stage leading to the emergence of the ears was observed when compared to Control and PAK‐treated plants, regardless of the water status (under water stress but also under well‐watered conditions).

In response to biotic stress, it has been demonstrated in the literature that plants accelerate their cycle. For example, in A. thaliana , the presence of Fusarium oxysporum accelerates flowering time and regulates the transcription of several floral integrated genes (Lyons et al. 2015). In addition, alginate is known to be an elicitor under biotic stress and might be recognized by the plant cell as a sugar signal produced by the degradation of the plant cell wall due to a fungal attack (Ochoa‐Meza et al. 2021). Therefore, alginate molecules could mimic a pathogen attack, as demonstrated by Bouissil et al. (2022) in date palm infected by Fusarium oxysporum.

Stomatal conductance measurements were performed to get insights into the effects of superabsorbent treatments at a cellular level. This parameter is known to reflect the movement of gases through plant stomata. Globally, the BioSAP and PAK treatments induced the stomatal closure more gradually compared to Control and SAP‐treated plants. Stomatal closure is a key adaptation to drought, as it regulates transpiration and CO2 uptake in leaves. Guard cells act as sensors, responding to signals from phytohormones such as abscisic acid and elicitors like chitosan (Agurla et al. 2018). In our experiment, stomatal closure occurs at around −2 MPa in BioSAP‐ and PAK‐treated plants, whereas untreated plants close their stomata at −1.2 MPa. This result suggests that BioSAP and PAK enhance water retention in plant tissues during drought stress. This effect would likely be due to one of the components of these polymers, chitosan (from the BioSAP) and potassium (from the PAK), which support the hypothesis of metabolic changes, leading plants to maintain gas exchanges before full stomatal closure. The TLP, derived from the pressure curves, has been shown to be correlated with stomatal closure, as shrinking guard cells lose turgor and close stomata (Bartlett, Scoffoni, and Sack 2012; MacRobbie 2006). In our experiment, the TLP values match with stomatal closure points, particularly for the BioSAP‐treated plants, suggesting that this treatment helps the plants to maintain a partial stomatal function, facilitating gas exchange while avoiding significant turgor loss, even under low water availability. As the only difference between SAP and BioSAP is the charge in chitosan particles, the partial closure observed in BioSAP‐treated plants could be explained by the elicitor capability of the chitosan in the formulation (Jahan et al. 2024).

WVC varied significantly among treatments. PAK‐ and SAP‐treated plants exhibited faster water consumption, whereas Control and BioSAP‐treated plants showed slower water reduction. It suggested that the superabsorbent behaves as a water stabilizer in soil but also in plants. In fact, in the range of water potential of −3 to −6 MPa (Figure 4), the superabsorbent‐treated plants show a stabilization of their leaf RWC around 40%–50% RWC, which is not the case for the control plants that harbor a constant decrease of RWC correlated to the soil water content diminution.

Under our experimental conditions, TLP of plants treated occurred later than control and may be linked to the maintenance of higher RWC and the reduction of stomatal conductance. Indeed, the impact of this modification may be explained by an improvement in the water management, as the TLP in control plants occurred at a soil of 14% VWC while superabsorbent‐treated plants delayed TLP to approximately 10% VWC. Two hypotheses explaining this modification of TLP could be proposed: an increased suction pressure due to the presence of superabsorbents or a direct increase in water uptake of the root system. Our previous studies have indicated that SAP and BioSAP absorbents based on alginate enhance root surface area and volume (Borjas, Delattre, et al. 2025; Borjas, Ahchouch, et al. 2025). These changes could be explained by a potential increase in root thickness, resulting in water extraction at lower soil water content (Segal et al. 2008).

BioSAP Treatment in Wheat Enhances Osmotic Adjustment and Cell Protection

The mobilization of osmolytes that decrease water potential at the point of stomatal closure can be responsible for maintaining turgor under superabsorbent treatments. Proline contents have been measured to evaluate the possible osmolyte contribution to the stress responses under treatments. Proline content in leaves is widely used as a marker of stress in plants: under drought conditions, proline levels increase, contributing to drought tolerance by acting as an osmolyte that protects against total dehydration and as a potential scavenger of free radicals (Farooq et al. 2009). In our study, the proline level in plants treated with the superabsorbent BioSAP did not increase as much as in control plants under drought stress (Figure 7). This finding aligns with a study conducted on four genotypes of durum wheat and two of bread wheat under drought stress showing that, although proline is known to be involved in the osmotic adjustment regulation, soluble sugars were the most prominent molecules under drought in wheat (Guizani et al. 2023). The proline content profile in leaves indicates that its production is closely linked to leaf water potential. At high leaf water potential, no significant proline production is observed. It is only when water reserves and leaf water potential begin to decline that proline levels increase. For instance, after 8 days of drought stress, proline content increases in Control and PAK‐treated plants, while SAP‐ and BioSAP‐treated plants maintain higher Ψ values around −3 MPa. These results suggest that proline production is not directly impacted by the treatments but is more correlated with the decrease in leaf water potential, serving as a stress‐triggered response and an alleviator during recovery phases. Indeed, the SAP and BioSAP treatments appear to delay proline biosynthesis, maintaining lower free proline levels compared to other treatments. This observation aligns with findings from Mulaudzi et al. (2022) and Liu et al. (2020), who reported that the application of chitosan reduces proline production compared to control plants under drought stress, while still providing strong antioxidant protection and an increase in soluble sugars.

During the recovery phase, the proline levels decrease rapidly, indicating its catabolism. It is well documented that proline biosynthesis is upregulated under abiotic stress, while its catabolism is repressed. Conversely, recovery triggers the opposite regulation (Szabados and Savouré 2010). After only 1 day of recovery (R2), proline levels remained high, but proline levels were reduced after 3 days of recovery (R1), regardless of the treatment, indicating that the recovery phase was well established. This modulation could be a process to adjust the osmotic pressure and enhance stress resilience, as demonstrated in barley after cold‐stress recovery (Peppino Margutti et al. 2024).

We found that the application of superabsorbent treatments, particularly BioSAP, increased total osmotic adjustment, enabling plants to recover more effectively and protecting them from oxidative imbalances and internal water deficits caused by stress conditions. Hence, BioSAP treatment not only improves physiological efficiency under drought stress but also confers structural and biochemical resilience and might be responsible for enhancing the overall plant survival. Indeed, our results showed that the BioSAP treatment reduces cell damage by 15% compared to the Control under severe drought stress. One key indicator of a potential protective effect is reduced cell damage, reflected in decreased ion leakage. In the SAP and BioSAP formulations, the major components, alginate and chitosan, are known to protect plant cells against oxidative damage and reduce electrolyte leakage, thereby mitigating cell membrane injuries (Liu et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2017).

TLP: A Relevant Marker to Evaluate Drought Tolerance Strategies Under the Combination of Superabsorbent and Biostimulant Properties

The turgor loss point (TLP) has been extensively used as a marker correlated with drought tolerance across various plant species. Conserved among species, it reflects the metabolic changes induced by stress that alter water dynamics, leading to a decrease in the osmotic pressure and water pressure under turgor loss. This reduction is associated with solute mobilization, which enhances tolerance to drought and osmotic stresses (Farrell et al. 2017). Although the pressure–volume curves focus on the TLP determination, osmotic adjustment, cell elasticity, and apoplastic water fraction can be used to give an approximation of the physiological behavior under biostimulant treatments.

The BioSAP treatment notably decreased TLP under drought conditions, suggesting a specific plant adaptation mechanism used to tolerate drought episodes. As reported by Bartlett, Scoffoni, and Sack (2012), plants with a lower TLP tend to maintain stomatal conductance, hydraulic conductance, photosynthetic gas exchange, and growth at lower soil water potentials. This observation aligns with our findings, indicating that BioSAP‐treated plants exhibit enhanced resilience under drought stress. In untreated wheat plants, the TLP varied only slightly under drought conditions, from −1.21 to −1.28 MPa, with an osmotic adjustment of 0.17 MPa (Table 3), which is lower than the ones observed under superabsorbent treatment. The osmotic adjustment increases significantly, twice with PAK and thrice with BioSAP. This finding suggests that wheat plants might mobilize more solutes under stress in response to superabsorbent treatments, indicating metabolic activation that aids the plant to adapt to low soil water content through total sugar mobilization (Guizani et al. 2023). In durum and bread wheat leaves (Guizani et al. 2023; Kikuta and Richter 1986), the turgor pressure responses in plants submitted to drought showed that they can modify their TLP and osmotic pressure to better tolerate drought. They described two main strategies: a rapid turgor loss to conserve internal water or a maintained cell turgor through osmolyte mobilization. In our study, plants that received no superabsorbent treatment primarily exhibited a water conservation strategy with reduced stomatal conductance, while BioSAP‐treated plants had enhanced ability to maintain turgor, supporting drought for a longer time. Superabsorbent treatments induced physiological responses that increased the osmotic potential at the turgor point, suggesting that, in those conditions, plants have a better ability to adjust their osmotic potential. Among the treatments, BioSAP resulted in the highest osmotic potential increase (φS100%), followed by PAK, SAP and the Control. Previous studies testing biostimulants with sugar elicitors have shown that chitosan and alginate oligosaccharides enhance the accumulation of soluble sugars (Wang et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2017), soluble proteins and proline (Liu et al. 2013; Wang et al. 2022) under osmotic, salt and drought stresses. Similar effects have been observed in barley (Hafez et al. 2020), sorghum (Mulaudzi et al. 2022), rice (Moolphuerk and Pattanagul 2020) and maize (Kakar et al. 2023). As BioSAP is formulated with chitosan nanoparticles loaded with oligosaccharides, it likely acts as a biostimulant for wheat plants.

Superabsorbent treatments also increased the symplastic bulk modulus (εsym), indicating a greater cell rigidity. Hypothetically, the rigidification of the cell wall could be explained by a higher production of callose and lignin, which is known to mechanically reinforce cell membranes. Callose deposition on cell surfaces and plasmodesmata, reported in plants under drought stress, plays a role in regulating cell rigidity and water transport (Apostolakos et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2024; Luna et al. 2011; Ton and Mauch‐Mani 2004). Sugar elicitors have been described to promote the production of secondary metabolites, such as callose and lignin (Nedukha 2015). For example, chitosan nanoparticles and oligochitosans are known to elicit the calcium flow into the cytosol, further promoting callose deposition (Conrath et al. 1989). Similarly, lignification, associated with the production of phenolic compounds, is enhanced under drought stress and differs in monomer composition at different developmental stages, reinforcing the cell wall (Gregorová et al. 2015; Stasińska‐Jakubas and Hawrylak‐Nowak 2022; Zhang et al. 2024).

Conclusion

By addressing both water retention and plant resilience, this study contributes to the development of environmentally sustainable and multifunctional SAPs for agriculture, particularly alginate‐chitosan‐based BioSAP.

The BioSAP treatment significantly enhanced physiological parameters such as stomatal conductance (gs), which remained functional under lower water potential, and relative water content (RWC), which was maintained at higher levels under drought stress compared to control plants. Moreover, BioSAP‐treated plants exhibited reduced electrolyte leakage, indicating lower cell damage and better cellular integrity under severe water deficit conditions.

In addition to enhancing water retention, BioSAP delayed turgor loss point (TLP), facilitated osmotic adjustment through solute mobilization, and moderated proline accumulation, suggesting an efficient stress response mechanism. These combined effects promoted drought resilience and faster recovery upon rehydration.

The dual functionality of BioSAP, integrating water retention with biostimulant properties, highlights its potential as a sustainable alternative to synthetic polyacrylate‐based SAPs. These findings underscore the relevance of incorporating eco‐friendly innovations into agricultural practices to mitigate drought stress and ensure crop productivity in water‐limited environments.
