Characteristics of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest due to cerebrovascular disorders: a nationwide, retrospective, observational study
Authors: Yoshiyuki Ueda, Tsuyoshi Nojima, Takafumi Obara, Takashi Hongo, Tetsuya Yumoto, Kohei Tsukahara, Takashi Yorifuji, Atsunori Nakao, Hiromichi Naito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundData on out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) due to cerebrovascular disorders is limited. This study aimed to describe the characteristics, outcomes, and annual trends of outcomes for OHCA originating from cerebrovascular disorders.MethodsThis study was a retrospective analysis using an Utstein-style Japanese National Registry. Adult patients with OHCA due to cerebrovascular disorders and transported to the hospital between 2005 and 2021 were included. The primary outcome was a favorable neurological outcome at 30-day. We analyzed factors associated with outcomes using a multivariable logistic regression model, then evaluated annual trends of outcomes for cerebrovascular-induced OHCA.ResultsAmong 2,081,023 OHCA patients, 52,969 had cerebrovascular-induced cardiac arrest. Of these, 1903 (3.5 %) achieved a favorable neurological outcome. In the multivariable logistic regression model, male sex (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.41, 95 % confidence interval [CI] 1.20–1.61), initial shockable rhythm (aOR 3.10, 95 % CI 2.18–4.40), witnessed cardiac arrest (aOR 1.92, 95 % CI: 1.57–2.34), and prehospital return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) (aOR 11.1, 95 % CI: 9.09–13.5) were associated with favorable neurological outcomes. Prehospital adrenaline administration was negatively associated with favorable neurological outcomes (aOR 0.22, 95 % CI: 0.16–0.30). The proportion of patients with favorable neurological outcomes increased over time, rising from 3.14 % in 2005 to 4.12 % in 2021.ConclusionsAlthough OHCA due to cerebrovascular disorders is generally associated with poor neurological outcomes, 3.5 % of the patients with cerebrovascular-induced OHCA in this study had favorable neurological outcomes, with a yearly trend improving over decades. Patient characteristics associated with a higher likelihood of a favorable neurological outcome included prehospital ROSC, initial shockable rhythm, and witnessed cardiac arrest.

Introduction

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), intracranial hemorrhage, or ischemic stroke are known to cause sudden cardiac arrest.1 Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) due to cerebrovascular origin, which has been associated with poor neurological outcomes, has led some studies to question the effectiveness of resuscitative for such patients.1, 2 As the optimal therapeutic strategy for OHCA often depends on its underlying cause, a comprehensive understanding of cerebrovascular-induced OHCA is essential. However, existing data on its incidence and outcomes are limited, often constrained by studies with small sample sizes.1

The largest past study of cerebrovascular-induced OHCA was conducted by Fukuda et al., and the same nationwide database as the one we used in this study.3 They reported that the proportion of patients with favorable neurological outcomes was 1.2 % among stroke-related OHCA cases from 2006 to 2009, which was lower than that of cardiogenic OHCA. However, their analysis included some patients who were not resuscitated by emergency medical service (EMS) personnel, and more than a decade has passed since then. Recent advances in neurosurgery and critical care management may have changed these outcomes, making it necessary to re-assess them in the current clinical context.4 Importantly, comparing cerebrovascular-induced OHCA with OHCA from other causes, and examining how prehospital factors influence outcomes in cerebrovascular cases, are important for better characterizing their prognosis and providing insights that may inform post-resuscitation management, such as early neurosurgical intervention or consideration of organ donation.5

The aim of our study was to conduct a large-scale, population-based, observational cohort study of OHCA patients to characterize the clinical profiles and annual trends of cerebrovascular-induced OHCA in Japan.

Methods

Study design

This retrospective, observational, population-based cohort study was conducted using data from the All Japan Utstein Registry for OHCA patients, which is maintained by the Fire and Disaster Management Agency. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Okayama University Ethics Committee (K2404-008). The requirement for informed consent was waived due to the anonymous nature of the registry data.

The emergency medical service system and in-hospital treatments

The EMS system in Japan is well-documented in previous reports.6 Public EMS services operate 24/7 from local fire stations to emergency scenes. Each EMS team, comprising at least three ambulance crew members, is dispatched from the nearest fire station to provide emergency care to OHCA patients. Each team includes at least one emergency life-saving technician who is authorized to establish supraglottic airways and intravenous access. Additionally, specially trained emergency life-saving technicians are permitted to perform endotracheal intubation and administer adrenaline. EMS activities are electronically recorded in the EMS database immediately after each dispatch.

In Japan, emergency medical services are structured as a three-tiered system consisting of primary, secondary, and tertiary emergency hospitals. OHCA patients are generally transported to the nearest secondary or tertiary emergency hospital, where advanced life support is provided in accordance with national guidelines, unless death is clearly confirmed at the scene.

In this database, the cause of OHCA is determined by the attending physician at the receiving hospital. Although the database does not include detailed information on the diagnostic process, whole-body computed tomography (CT) is generally performed after hospital arrival to evaluate the etiology of cardiac arrest,7 and postmortem CT is also widely used to identify the cause of death. Therefore, Cerebrovascular- induced OHCA are typically diagnosed based on CT findings combined with clinical assessments, information from EMS, and interviews with family members.8 Because postmortem CT is commonly used in Japan, autopsies are rarely performed except in some cases with suspected criminal involvement or accidents.

Data collection

The following OHCA patient data were prospectively collected by the local EMS based on the Utstein-style guidelines and were subsequently integrated into the national registry. Cardiac arrest was defined as cessation of mechanical cardiac activity substantiated by lack of circulation signs. Variables included sex, age, transported time, initial rhythm at scene, witness status, dispatcher instruction in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), bystander CPR, use of advanced airway, establishment of intravenous line, prehospital automated external defibrillator (AED), prehospital adrenaline administration, prehospital ROSC, neurological status at 30-day, and survival at 30-day. Prehospital AED use included both bystander and EMS defibrillation. Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) scores were used to evaluate neurological outcomes. Diagnoses of cardiac arrest etiology were clinically decided by in-charge physicians in collaboration with EMS providers.

Patient selection, endpoints

The inclusion criteria of this study were all OHCA patients registered in the database between 1 January 2005 and 31 December 2021. Patients for whom resuscitation was not attempted and those under 18 years of age were excluded. The characteristics of cerebrovascular-induced OHCA and non-cerebrovascular-induced OHCA patients were compared to provide background understanding. Then, among patients with cerebrovascular-induced OHCA, factors associated with favorable neurological outcomes and temporal trends in neurological outcomes were analyzed.

The primary outcome was the neurological outcome at 30-day, assessed using CPC. Favorable neurological outcomes were defined as CPC 1 or 2, while CPC 3, 4, and 5 were considered poor neurological outcomes.

Data analysis

Continuous variables were described using medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were described using numbers and percentages.

Univariable and multivariable logistic analysis was conducted to identify factors associated with favorable neurological outcomes. The factors adjusted for in the multivariable analysis were as follows: sex, age, transported time, initial rhythm at scene (shockable or non-shockable rhythm), witnessed cardiac arrest, dispatcher instruction in CPR, bystander CPR, prehospital AED, prehospital adrenaline administration, and prehospital ROSC and these variables were included in the same model (model: 1). A multiple logistic regression model was applied to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with their 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). Prehospital ROSC was defined as achievement of ROSC before hospital arrival. To ensure the robustness of the model, a sensitivity analysis was conducted for each independent variable individually using only age and sex for adjustment (model: 2). Interaction terms were not included, as the primary objective was to identify independent predictors of favorable neurological outcomes.

To investigate temporal trends in neurological outcomes among OHCA patients with cerebrovascular disorders, a binomial log-linear regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the association between calendar year and neurological outcome. Analyses were performed both with and without adjustment for potential confounding factors. The adjusted variables included: sex, age, transported time, initial rhythm at scene (shockable or non-shockable rhythm), witnessed cardiac arrest, dispatcher instruction in CPR, bystander CPR, prehospital AED, prehospital adrenaline administration, and prehospital ROSC. To illustrate this relationship, a quadratic fitted curve was applied to visualize the probability of achieving a favorable neurological outcome over time. Annual trends with 95 % CIs were plotted from 2005 to 2021 with or without adjustment. Stratified analyses were conducted for patients with shockable and non-shockable initial rhythms.

P-value below 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA/SE 18 (StataCorp, Lakeway, TX, USA).

Factors associated with favorable neurological outcomes at 30-day

Table 3 presents the univariable and multivariable analysis results for factors associated with favorable neurological outcomes. The following factors were significantly associated with favorable neurological outcomes (model: 1): male sex (aOR 1.41, 95 % CI: 1.20–1.67), shockable rhythm at scene (aOR 3.10, 95 % CI: 2.18–4.40), witnessed cardiac arrest (aOR 1.92, 95 % CI: 1.57–2.34), and prehospital ROSC (aOR 11.1, 95 % CI: 9.09–13.5). On the other hand, prehospital adrenaline administration was associated with worse neurological outcomes (aOR 0.22, 95 % CI: 0.16–0.30). Discussion

In this nationwide, population-based study of OHCA patients with cerebrovascular disorders, the proportion of patients achieving a favorable neurological outcome was 3.5 %. Factors significantly associated with favorable neurological outcomes included younger age, male sex, witnessed arrest, initial shockable rhythm, and prehospital ROSC, whereas prehospital adrenaline administration was associated with worse neurological outcomes. In addition, temporal trend analysis revealed gradual improvements in survival and neurological outcomes over time, suggesting incremental advances in resuscitation systems, post-arrest care, and neurosurgical management.

Previous studies have raised concerns about the futility of resuscitative efforts for patients with OHCA caused by neurological conditions, with some advocating for shifting the focus toward organ donation due to high mortality rates in intensive care units.1 However, our findings challenge this perspective by demonstrating that a subset of patients with cerebrovascular-induced OHCA can achieve favorable neurological outcomes, particularly those with initial shockable rhythms, witnessed arrests, and prehospital ROSC. This contrasts with previous studies suggesting that resuscitative efforts in such cases are largely futile. Given that the 30-day survival rate in our study was 8.0 % overall, including cases with unwitnessed arrests, absence of bystander CPR, and non-shockable rhythms, we argue that resuscitation efforts for stroke-related OHCA may not be futile. While overall outcomes remain significantly poorer compared to cardiac-origin OHCA, careful identification of patients with characteristics predictive of favorable neurological outcomes is crucial in clinical decision-making.

Patients with initial shockable rhythms, witnessed arrests, and prehospital ROSC had a markedly higher likelihood of survival with favorable neurological outcomes. It is known that stroke can induce fatal arrhythmias, such as ventricular fibrillation,9, 10, 11 suggesting that if ROSC is achieved through defibrillation, the outcomes of cerebrovascular-induced cardiac arrest may not necessarily be poor, depending on the underlying cause.3 These findings highlight the importance of early recognition, prompt resuscitative efforts, and aggressive post-arrest care in improving patient outcomes.

Adrenaline administration has been shown to improve survival in a randomized controlled trial.12 Further, in a study by Fukuda et al.,3 which used the same nationwide database as ours, adrenaline use was also associated with higher prehospital ROSC rates.3 However, in our study, adrenaline administration was negatively associated with neurological outcomes. This finding should be interpreted with caution, as it is most likely influenced by resuscitation time bias; patients with poor prognostic factors tend to receive prolonged resuscitation efforts, which may account for the observed negative association.13

The finding that dispatcher-instructed and bystander CPR were also associated with poorer neurological outcomes appears paradoxical. This likely reflects confounding by patient severity, as some patients with favorable outcomes may have achieved ROSC early before EMS arrival and were therefore classified as “no CPR”. This result should be interpreted cautiously, given possible residual confounding and classification bias.

Cardiac arrest can be a devastating complication of various cerebrovascular conditions. In our study, the proportion of patients achieving favorable neurological outcomes was lower in the cerebrovascular-induced OHCA group (3.5 %) compared with the overall OHCA population (4.7 %), indicating that cerebrovascular-induced cardiac arrest is associated with a poorer neurological outcome. While the specific cerebrovascular conditions underlying OHCA in our study were not fully identified, SAH can be one of the major contributors. Hubner et al. reported that SAH was the leading neurologic cause of cardiac arrest (48 %), followed by intracerebral hemorrhage (21 %) and epileptic seizures (15 %).14 Among the causes of SAH, vertebral artery dissection-induced SAH often presents with severe symptoms, which often results in OHCA with higher mortality compared to aneurysmal rupture-induced SAH.15

The brain–heart interaction in SAH-induced cardiac arrest involves complex mechanisms, including the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, catecholamine surge, and autonomic dysregulation, which can lead to arrhythmias, myocardial dysfunction, and ultimately cardiac arrest due to excessive sympathetic activation and impaired cardiovascular stability2, 16. Some patients who achieve ROSC following SAH-related cardiac arrest may undergo neurosurgical intervention to prevent rebleeding and subsequently experience neurological recovery. Our findings appear to be in line with those from previous studies that suggest the impact of these pathophysiological mechanisms. Research on SAH outcomes shows a general improvement over time. Case fatality rates have decreased, with an overall mortality reduction of up to 50 % over two decades.17, 18 Reports of successful resuscitations and neurological recoveries continue to emerge, with case studies documenting improved outcomes in select patients who receive timely neurosurgical intervention and post-arrest care.19, 20 The shift towards endovascular treatments has been correlated with better outcomes, highlighting the impact of evolving management strategies on SAH prognosis.

This study has several limitations. First, detailed classification of cerebrovascular disorders, such as subarachnoid hemorrhage, intracerebral hemorrhage, or cerebral infarction, was not available from the Utstein registry. In addition, information on whether and how many patients underwent CT imaging was not recorded, making it difficult to verify diagnostic accuracy. Previous studies have shown that subarachnoid hemorrhage accounts for a large proportion of cerebrovascular-induced cardiac arrest cases.14 Second, due to the observational nature of the study, causal relationships could not be established. In particular, data on the timing of prehospital ROSC and the timing of adrenaline administration were not available, and resuscitation time bias was not taken into account. Third, information on in-hospital treatments and imaging studies was not included in the registry, making it difficult to evaluate the quality of care provided. Fourth, neurological outcomes were assessed using CPC, and more detailed evaluations of neurological function were not available. Fifth, determination of the cause of cardiac arrest relied on the clinical judgment of the treating physicians, which may have introduced variability. Sixth, in Japan, the destination hospital is determined based on the judgment of EMS personnel; therefore, regional differences in prehospital triage, healthcare systems, and quality of care may have influenced patient outcomes. In addition, geographic and socioeconomic factors were not included as variables in this study and may have contributed to residual confounding. Lastly, as the study was conducted within the framework of the Japanese EMS and healthcare systems, the generalizability of the findings to other countries may be limited.

Conclusion

Although OHCA due to cerebrovascular disorders is generally associated with poor neurological outcomes, 3.5 % of the patients with cerebrovascular-induced OHCA in this study had favorable neurological outcomes, with a yearly trend improving over decades. Patient characteristics associated with a higher likelihood of a favorable neurological outcome included prehospital ROSC, initial shockable rhythm, and witnessed cardiac arrest.
