Antidiabetic and antioxidant effects of Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes extracts and their silver nanoparticles against streptozotocin induced diabetes in mice
Authors: Rabia Mushtaq, Shaukat Ali, Muhammad Hanif, Rimsha Abaidullah, Khushbukhat Khan, Tayyaba Afsar, Ali Almajwal, Houda Amor, Suhail Razak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The global burden of diabetes has increased substantially over recent decades, with the number of affected individuals rising from approximately 200 million in 1990 to 830 million in 2022. In ranking, Pakistan is among the top affected countries globally. Current study is intended to assess two Armillaria spp. mushrooms for their antioxidant and antidiabetic potential. Armillaria gallica (A.G) and Armillaria cepistipes (A.C) are the two medicinal mushrooms identified for their phenolic and polysaccharide profiles. Extracts and silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) of Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes were prepared and characterized using UV-visible spectrophotometry, X- ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), dynamic light scattering (DLS) and zeta potential analysis. Antioxidant activity was performed using DPPH (2, 2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) radical scavenging assay. The antidiabetic activity of A. gallica extract, A. Fenugreek cepistipes extract and their respective AgNPs was investigated in a streptozotocin-induced diabetic mice models. A total of seventy-two male albino mice were divided into twelve groups (n = 6 per group) comprising six non-diabetic groups and six diabetic groups. Control group was given normal saline, A. gallica extract (A.G; 200 mg/kg), A. cepistipes extract (A.C; 200 mg/kg), A. gallica nanoparticles (A.G AgNPs; 200 mg/kg), A. cepistipes nanoparticles (A.C AgNPs; 200 mg/kg), metformin (MET; 100 mg/kg) and streptozotocin (STZ; 150 mg/kg). Diabetes was induced using streptozotocin (150 mg/kg), and all treatments were administered orally for 28 consecutive days following diabetes induction. Organ’s histopathology (pancreas, liver and kidney) was performed both qualitative and quantitative using a semi quantitative scoring system 0-12. Biochemical parameters including glutathione (GSH), gamma glutamyl transferase (GGT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), high density lipoproteins (HDL), tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), low density lipoproteins (LDL) and sodium oxide dismutase (SOD) were analyzed. A. cepistipes silver nanoparticles (A.C AgNPs) exhibited 58.8% DPPH radical scavenging activity at 100 µg/mL and A. gallica silver nanoparticles (A.G AgNPs) showed 57%. Activity. These results indicate moderate antioxidant potential. In in-vivo study, Armillaria nanoparticles treated groups showed greater improvement in biochemical analysis and histopathology score.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-60223-4.

Introduction

The global burden of diabetes has increased substantially over recent decades, with the number of affected individuals rising from approximately 200 million in 1990 to 830 million in 2022. This increase has been particularly pronounced in low- and middle-income countries, where the prevalence of diabetes has grown at a faster rate than in high-income nations1. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), Pakistan is among the countries with the highest diabetes burden in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region. In 2024, the prevalence of diabetes among adults in Pakistan was estimated at 31.4%, corresponding to approximately 34.5 million adults living with diabetes out of a total adult population of 130.4 million. Furthermore, the IDF reported that the number of people with diabetes in the MENA region is projected to increase from 85 million to 163 million by 20502. The prevalence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is around 10% to 16.98%, and pre-diabetes is about 10.91% to 11%3,4. Type 1 diabetes can be caused by autoimmune responses, genetic environmental factors and age and family. Metformin (MET) remains the first-line synthetic drug for type 2 diabetes management due to its efficacy in reducing hepatic glucose production and improving insulin sensitivity5. Sulfonylureas, like gliclazide, stimulate insulin release from the pancreas. DPP-4 inhibitors, including sitagliptin, enhance insulin secretion and decrease glucagon levels. SGLT2 inhibitors, such as canagliflozin, reduce glucose reabsorption in the kidneys6. Additionally, alpha-glucosidase inhibitors delay carbohydrate absorption, while meglitinides, such as repaglinide, rapidly stimulate insulin release. Synthetic drugs for type 2 diabetes management exhibit a range of side effects. Gymnema sylvestre enhances insulin secretion and helps manage blood sugar, while bitter gourd (bitter melon) is effective in regulating glucose levels7. Mushrooms have shown significant potential in diabetes management due to their bioactive compounds, such as polysaccharides, proteins, dietary fibers, and antioxidants8. Species like Agaricus bisporus, Ganoderma lucidum, Cordyceps sinensis, and Pleurotus spp. exhibit hypoglycemic effects by inhibiting enzymes like α-glucosidase and pancreatic-amylase, which regulate glucose absorption9. The genus Armillaria includes fungi commonly known as honey mushrooms, which are primarily parasitic or saprotrophic. Two kinds of therapeutic mushrooms that belong to the genus Armillaria are Armillaria gallica, sometimes referred to as the bulbous honey fungus, and Armillaria cepistipes, also known as the cluster honey fungus Deveci et al.10–12. Armillaria gallica exhibits prominent reduction in oxidative stress, protection from cell damage and antioxidant properties13. Although the bioactivity of Armillaria cepistipes remains relatively underexplored, medicinal mushrooms are widely recognized as rich sources of antioxidant compounds, including polysaccharides and phenolic constituents Ahmad and Singh14,15. Both of these Armillaria spp. are evaluated for health applications. The novelty of this study is present in the examination of antidiabetic potential of Armillaria spp. utilizing streptozotocin (STZ) induced diabetic mouse model. Several mushroom species, including Ganoderma, Pleurotus, and Lentinula, have been extensively investigated for their antidiabetic properties Hussein et al.16. In contrast, the antidiabetic potential of Armillaria species remains comparatively underexplored. Although previous studies have reported various bioactive constituents and pharmacological activities of certain Armillaria species, limited information is available regarding the antidiabetic efficacy of Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes, particularly in in vivo diabetic models Masouri et al.17. Moreover, to the best of our knowledge, studies evaluating the antidiabetic and antioxidant effects of silver nanoparticles synthesized from these mushroom species are scarce. Therefore, the present study was designed to investigate the therapeutic potential of Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes extracts and their silver nanoparticle formulations in streptozotocin-induced diabetic mice. This research work is not only exploring the hypoglycemic effects of Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes but also inspects their role in checking the physiological and biochemical parameters. Additionally, this study shows the applications of mushroom mediated silver nanoparticles to increase the biological activity of crude extract. Edible and medicinal mushrooms are rich sources of biologically active compounds, including polysaccharides, phenolic compounds, flavonoids, terpenoids, and proteins, which have demonstrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antidiabetic activities. These bioactive constituents may contribute to the regulation of glucose metabolism and the reduction of oxidative stress associated with diabetes. Moreover, nanoparticle-based formulations have emerged as promising delivery systems because they can improve the stability, bioavailability, and therapeutic efficacy of natural compounds. Therefore, the synthesis of mushroom-mediated nanoparticles may enhance the biological effects of mushroom extracts and provide a novel approach for diabetes management.

The current study meant to explore the anti-diabetic potential of Armillaria cepistipes and Armillaria gallica extracts and their nanoparticles in STZ induced diabetes in mice. Most important target was the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles from the Armillaria spp. extracts and their characterization. It is familiar that nanoparticles-based formulations increase the bioavailability of natural materials, stability and its therapeutic efficiency. Moreover, the antioxidant, antibacterial activities are also increased by the formation of silver nanoparticles which increase the pharmacological benefits. Thus, the current study also sought to determine whether the creation of nanoparticles may improve the antidiabetic potential of extracts from Armillaria. Additionally, the antioxidant activity of the extracts and nanoparticles was assessed using the DPPH radical scavenging assay. To evaluate treatment impact at the tissue level, histopathological changes in the pancreas, liver, and kidneys were examined. Relevant biochemical markers, including oxidative stress parameters, lipid profile components, and inflammatory cytokines are analyzed across diabetic and treated experimental groups.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

All experimental procedures involving animals were carried out in accordance with the ethical standards of GC University, Lahore, and adhered to both institutional guidelines and international regulations. Ethical approval for the study was granted by the Faculty of Science Ethics Committee, GC University Lahore, Pakistan (Approval No. GCU-IIB-324 (A)) dated 29/08/2024).

Collection and identification of mushroom

Armilaria gallica was collected from various forested areas of Kumrat Valley, Upper Dir District Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan. Armillaria cepistipes was collected from Roshay Duty Forest, Kumrat Valley, Pakistan. The mushroom samples (Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes) were taxonomically identified by a qualified mycologist. The samples were deposited in the herbarium of Department of Botany, Government College University Lahore. A voucher number was assigned MH238822 for Armillaria gallica and MH276822 for Armillaria cepistipes for future reference.

Extract preparation

To prepare the mushroom extract, 3 g of dried and powdered fruiting bodies from each Armillaria species (A. gallica and A. cepistipes) were dissolved in 20 mL of ethanol. The mixture was placed in a shaking incubator at 37°C for 24 hours (Fig. 1). Following the incubation period, the solution was filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper. The filtrate was then placed in a water bath and heated for 48 hours, or until all of the ethanol had evaporated. A semi-solid extract remained, which was carefully collected, wrapped in aluminum foil, and stored at 4°C in a refrigerator for further experimental use18.Fig. 1The diagram illustrates the process for preparing an extract from Armillaria spp. mushroom. It includes the following steps: grinding the mushroom to powder, mixing it with ethanol, incubating the mixture, filtering the solution, and evaporating the solvent. The resulting dried extract was then characterized and tested for its antioxidant and antidiabetic activities.

Preparation of silver nitrate solution

A 1mM (17mg AgNO3 in 100ml distilled water) aqueous silver nitrate (AgNO₃) solution was freshly prepared by dissolving the required amount of AgNO₃ in deionized water to ensure accuracy and prevent photodegradation. The solution was protected from light during preparation and use to maintain the stability of silver ions19.

Synthesis of mushroom based silver nan oparticles

Using green synthesis method, the mushroom extract was used to create silver nanoparticles. A measured volume of the prepared Armillaria extract was combined with an aqueous solution of silver nitrate (AgNO₃). The mushroom extract (500 μl, pH 6.1) was mixed with 4.5 mL of 1mM aqueous AgNO3 solution (Cat. No. 209139, Sigma-Aldrich, MilliporeSigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) and kept at room temperature for 24–96 h. The color change from colorless to reddish brown was observed within 5 h of incubation with AgNO3, indicating the formation of silver nanoparticles. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature. The resultant suspension was centrifuged to collect the synthesized nanoparticles, which were subsequently washed several times with distilled water. Then the particles were dried and stored20,21.

Characterization of silver nanoparticles

The biosynthesized silver nanoparticles characterized using UV-Visible Spectrophotometry, X-ray Diffraction (XRD), Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) and Zeta potential analysis to confirm their formation, structure, and morphology.

UV-Visible spectrophotometry

The formation of silver nanoparticles was monitored using a UV-Visible spectrophotometer Inno TECH Spectrophotometer (Model: inno-DB6500; AC220V; 50Hz), manufactured by INNO Instruments, South Korea. The reaction mixture was scanned in the wavelength range of 300–700 nm, and the absorption spectra were recorded to monitor the formation of silver nanoparticles5,22.

X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)

The crystalline structure of the silver nanoparticles was analyzed using an X-ray diffractometer (Bruker D8 Advance, Germany) equipped with a Cu-Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å), operated at 40 kV and 30 mA. The diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 20°–80° at a scan rate of 2°/min with a step size of 0.02°23.

Fourier-Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR)

Functional groups involved in the reduction and stabilization of silver nanoparticles were identified using acculab USA Laboratory Spectrophotometer (Model: FTIR-3000; 50-60Hz frequency; 220V supply power). Dried nanoparticle samples were finely ground with spectroscopic grade potassium bromide (KBr) and pressed into translucent pellets using a hydraulic press. The spectra were recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm⁻1 with a resolution of 4 cm24,25.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

The surface morphology and particle size of the synthesized silver nanoparticles were examined using Scanning Electron Microscopy (JEOL JSM-6490LV, Japan). Samples were mounted on aluminum stubs with carbon adhesive tape and sputter-coated with gold (Quorum SC7620 sputter coater, UK) under vacuum to enhance conductivity. The images were captured at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV, with magnifications ranging from 10,000× to 50,000×26,27.

Nanoparticle size distribution and surface charge analysis

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) analysis was performed to determine the hydrodynamic particle size distribution of the synthesized nanoparticles. The measurements were carried out using a Zetasizer instrument (Anton Par, Austria) at room temperature. Nanoparticles were completely diluted in deionized prior to analysis and then sonicated for a few minutes. Intensity of scattered light was recorded at an angle of 90° and the data was investigated to get the average size of particles and polydispersity index28. Zeta potential was performed to indicate the surface charge and stability of nanoparticles through Nano Zeta Sizer (Malvern Zetasizer). The nanoparticle suspension was diluted appropriately with distilled deionized water to avoid particle aggregation and ensure accurate measurements. The diluted samples were transferred into a disposable zeta cell, and measurements were carried out at room temperature under standard operating conditions.

Radical scavenging assay

To evaluate the antioxidant potential of Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes extracts and their silver nanoparticles, a DPPH radical scavenging assay was performed29,30. A 1 mM DPPH solution was prepared in methanol and freshly used in dark conditions. Different concentrations (20, 40, 60, 80,100 µg/ mL) of Armillaria gallica extract, Armillaria cepistipes extract and their silver nanoparticles were prepared in methanol and mixed with an equal volume of DPPH solution. Ascorbic acid was used as the reference standard antioxidant and was tested under the same experimental conditions as the samples. The reaction mixtures were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 30 minutes to allow interaction between antioxidants and DPPH radicals. After incubation, absorbance was recorded at 517 nm using a UV–Visible spectrophotometer against a methanol blank. A control containing only DPPH solution and methanol was also prepared. The percentage of radical scavenging activity was calculated by comparing the absorbance of the sample with the control using the standard formula, and IC₅₀ values were determined from the dose-response curve. All experiments were performed in triplicate and results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Antidiabetic activity

Eight-week-old Swiss albino male mice weighing between 29.0 ± 0.5g were purchased for the experiment from the Animal House in the Department of Zoology at Government College University, Lahore, Pakistan. The animals were housed in standard polypropylene cages measuring 10 × 5 × 7 inches (length × width × height), with six mice per cage. Housing conditions included a controlled environment maintained at 19–21°C, relative humidity of 45–65%, and a 12-hour light/dark cycle. Prior to the commencement of the experimental procedures, the mice were acclimatized for seven days. Body weights were recorded weekly throughout the study31. A total of 72 male mice were randomly allocated into twelve experimental groups, with six animals in each group. G-I served as the normal control and received normal saline only. To evaluate the safety of the treatments in healthy animals, non-diabetic mice were assigned to G-II [A.G (ND)], G-III [A.G AgNPs (ND)], G-IV [A.C (ND)], G-V [A.C AgNPs (ND)], and G-VI [Met (ND)], which received Armillaria gallica extract (200 mg/kg), Armillaria gallica silver nanoparticles (AgNPs; 200 mg/kg), Armillaria cepistipes extract (200 mg/kg), Armillaria cepistipes AgNPs (200 mg/kg), and metformin (100 mg/kg), respectively. Diabetes was induced in G-VII - G-XII by a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ; 150 mg/kg body weight). Following confirmation of diabetes, G-VII served as the diabetic untreated group and received no further treatment (T). G-VIII [A.G (T)] received A. gallica extract (200 mg/kg), while G-IX [A.G AgNPs (T)] received A. gallica AgNPs (200 mg/kg). G-X [A.C (T)] was treated with A. cepistipes extract (200 mg/kg), whereas G-XI [A.C AgNPs (T)] received A. cepistipes AgNPs (200 mg/kg). G-XII [Met (T)] served as the standard treatment group and received metformin (100 mg/kg). This experimental design enabled the assessment of the safety of A. gallica and A. cepistipes extracts and their biosynthesized silver nanoparticles in non-diabetic mice, as well as the evaluation of their therapeutic efficacy against STZ-induced diabetes32.

Streptozotocin is the most commonly utilized diabetogenic agent. Diabetes was induced by a single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (150 mg/kg body weight). The dose was optimized through preliminary pilot experiments and further supported by literature reports demonstrating effective diabetes induction in mice at similar doses (Gavazava, I;33. Metformin was used as the standard reference drug in this study. A dose of 100 mg/kg was administered orally to two groups of mice. One group served as a non-diabetic control group receiving metformin alone, while the second group received metformin as a treatment following streptozotocin induced diabetes. Metformin was administered daily for 28 consecutive days to evaluate its anti-diabetic effects and to serve as a benchmark for comparison with the effects of Armillaria extracts and their corresponding silver nanoparticles. Blood glucose level measurement34. The dose was prepared in saline solution. After two days of administration the glucose level in blood was checked by the help of glucometer. Blood was taken from tail tips by pricking the tails. Excessive water and food were given to mice and extra care was taken so that the mice don’t get aggressive and show any negative behavior of hypoglycemia. The glucose level of each mouse was checked before starting the treatment with mushrooms.

Measurement of blood glucose level

During the treatment period, fasting blood glucose levels were measured every third day using a glucometer. Blood samples were collected from the tail vein, and glucose values were recorded consistently to monitor the progression and regression of hyperglycemia35.

Measurement of body weight

The body weight of each mouse was measured at regular intervals on days 1, 7, 14, 21 and 28 using a digital weighing scale. These measurements were taken to assess changes in metabolic status and the overall health condition of the mice during treatment36.

Mice dissection

After completing the treatment mice were dissected. First, mice were anesthetized with xylazine (10 mg/kg) and ketamine (100 mg/kg) in saline in the proportion of 1:4:15 (μl) intraperitoneally and the blood was taken from heart with the help of 1 ml syringe by cardiac puncture technique for the biochemical analysis. Then the mice were dissected and pancreas, kidney and liver were taken out and saved in working solution of formalin. Mice organ tissues were processed through dehydration and clearing steps, embedded it in paraffin, sectioned into thin slices (5-6 µm thick) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and finally examined them under a microscope to analyze the tissue morphology37.

Analysis of biochemical parameters

The blood samples were centrifuged for 10 minutes at 2000 RCF. Various biochemical tests were performed using the plasma that was produced from centrifuging blood samples. Different biomarkers in the serum such as glutathione (GSH), gamma glutamyl transferase (GGT), Alkaline phosphastase (ALP), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), Tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), Interleukin-6 (IL-6), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), Superoxide dismutase (SOD) were analyzed.

Histopathological analysis

Histopathological evaluation of pancreatic, hepatic, and renal tissues was performed using a standardized semi-quantitative composite scoring system to ensure objective and reproducible assessment of tissue injury. For pancreatic sections, three key parameters were evaluated, including islet architecture damage, β-cell degeneration/necrosis, and inflammatory cell infiltration38. Similarly, liver sections were assessed based on hepatocellular degeneration/necrosis, degree of steatosis (fatty change), and inflammatory infiltration39, while kidney sections were examined for tubular necrosis, glomerular damage, and interstitial inflammation40. Each parameter was graded on a scale of 0–4, where 0 indicated normal histology with no detectable lesions, 1 represented mild alterations, 2 moderate changes, 3 marked damage, and 4 severe pathological alterations. The scores of the three parameters were summed to obtain a cumulative histopathological score ranging from 0 to 12 for each organ. To minimize observer bias, scoring was conducted in a blinded manner without prior knowledge of group allocation. The composite scoring approach allowed quantitative comparison of histological alterations among control, diabetic (STZ-induced), and treatment groups. Statistical analysis of the scores was performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tuckey test, with particular emphasis on comparisons between the STZ group and treatment groups. This method enabled reliable interpretation of the extent of tissue damage and the protective effects of mushroom extracts and their nanoparticle formulations across the studied organs

Statistical analysis

For all in vivo studies, six animals were included in each experimental group (n = 6), and data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Biochemical, physiological, and cytokine analyses were performed using samples collected from all animals in each group. In vitro assays, including the DPPH radical scavenging assay, were conducted in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean ± SD of three independent measurements. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. For comparisons involving a single independent variable, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s or Bonferroni post hoc multiple comparison test was used, as appropriate. For experiments involving two independent variables, two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test was applied. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. All comparisons were predefined, including comparisons between the normal control and STZ groups, as well as between the STZ group and treatment groups. Results

Characterization of silver nanoparticles

Several techniques were used to confirm the formation and morphology of the silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) obtained from extracts of Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes.

UV–Visible spectrophotometry showed distinct peaks of surface plasmon resonance (SPR) of Armillaria gallica between 430-440 nm (Fig. 3 a), characteristic of spherical AgNPs whereas peak of Armillaria cepistipes at 420-435 nm indicating formation of silver nanoparticles. X- ray diffraction patterns of Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes shows the crystalline nature of their nanoparticles. Figure 4(a) shows X-ray diffraction (XRD) peaks at 2θ values of 27.2°, 31.2°, 38.1°, 44.5°, 64.5°, and 77.3°. The prominent diffraction peaks observed at 38.1°, 44.5°, 64.5°, and 77.3° correspond to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) crystallographic planes of face-centered cubic (FCC) silver, confirming the crystalline nature of the synthesized silver nanoparticles. The additional peaks at 27.2° and 31.2° do not correspond to the characteristic FCC silver reflections. Since detailed phase identification was not performed, these peaks were not assigned to a specific crystalline phase. Similarly, Fig. 4(b) exhibited diffraction peaks at 23.6°, 27.3°, 38.1°, 44.5°, 64.4°, and 77.4°. The reflections at 38.1°, 44.5°, 64.4°, and 77.4° correspond to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) planes of FCC silver, whereas the additional low-angle peaks were not assigned to a specific crystalline phase. Radical scavenging potential

The antioxidant potential of A. gallica and A. cepistipes extracts, as well as their biosynthesized silver nanoparticles, was evaluated using the DPPH radical scavenging assay. Ascorbic acid was used as a positive control. The results showed a concentration-dependent increase in radical scavenging activity across all test samples (20, 40, 60, 80, 100 µg/mL). At lower concentrations (20 µg/mL), both crude extracts exhibited significantly lower % inhibition (7.73% and 8.67 %) compared to ascorbic acid, indicating modest antioxidant capacity. However, the nanoparticle formulations (A.G AgNPs and A.C AgNPs) demonstrated substantially higher scavenging activity than their respective extracts at all concentrations, and showed improved performance particularly at 80 and 100 µg/mL. At 100 µg/mL, A.G AgNPs (57.07%) and A.C AgNPs (58.80%) showed comparable antioxidant activity to ascorbic acid (80.00%), with no statistically significant difference observed (p > 0.05). These results showed that the antioxidant efficiency of Armillaria spp. extracts is increased by the green synthesis of silver nanoparticles because of increased surface area and more interaction with the free radicals (Table 1). Biochemical markers

Biochemical analysis revealed significant alterations in oxidative stress, inflammatory, and lipid profile markers among the experimental groups. Glutathione (GSH) levels were markedly decreased in the STZ-induced diabetic group (4.4 ± 0.4 µmol/L), confirming diabetes-associated oxidative stress, while treatment with Armillaria extracts and especially their nanoparticles significantly restored GSH levels, with A.C AgNPs (T) and A.G AgNPs (T) showing the highest values (13.4 ± 0.9 and 11.2 ± 0.8 µmol/L, respectively) (Fig. 9). Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) was significantly elevated in the STZ group (91.6 ± 2.5 U/L) but was markedly reduced in treated groups, particularly in nanoparticle-treated mice, indicating protection from oxidative damage (Fig. 9). Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels, which were elevated in the STZ group (331.4 ± 8.0 U/L), were also significantly reduced by all treatments, with the strongest reduction observed in A.G AgNPs and A.C AgNPs-treated groups (Fig. 9). Regarding lipid markers, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels were severely lowered in diabetic mice (22.2 ± 3.1 mg/dL) and significantly increased upon treatment, especially in A.C AgNPs (T) (44.0 ± 5.1 mg/dL) and A.G AgNPs (T) (38.6 ± 4.8 mg/dL) groups (Fig. 10), whereas low-density lipoprotein (LDL) levels were markedly elevated in diabetic controls (148.8 ± 13.3 mg/dL) and were significantly reduced following treatment, most notably by A.C AgNP (T) and A.G AgNPs (T) (68.2 ± 3.1 and 77.8 ± 3.8 mg/dL, respectively as shown in Fig. 11. Inflammatory cytokines were also affected, with tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) showing sharp elevations in the diabetic group (160.9 ± 11.0 pg/mL and 274.2 ± 12.2 pg/mL, respectively) but pronounced reductions in the treated groups, especially in nanoparticle treatments (Fig. 10). Furthermore, superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, which dropped drastically in diabetic mice (61.4 ± 5.6 U/mL), was restored by all treatments, with A.G AgNPs (T) (131.4 ± 30.8 U/mL) and A.C AgNPs (T) (122.0 ± 13.8 U/mL) showing the highest recovery (Fig. 11). The outcomes showed that biochemical markers significantly improved after treatment. Elevated antioxidant markers, such as SOD and GSH, indicate improved oxidative defense systems. On the other hand, reduction of inflammatory responses was shown by decreased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such TNF-α and IL-6. Discussion

The UV–Visible spectra of Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes silver nanoparticles exhibited a characteristic absorption peak at approximately 420 nm, confirming the formation of silver nanoparticles. Similar SPR peaks have been reported in previous studies on biologically synthesized AgNPs. In addition, A. gallica and A. cepistipes are known to contain various bioactive compounds, including hispidin derivatives, which have been reported in earlier studies Al-Mosawi41,42. XRD analysis of both Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes mediated silver nanoparticles confirmed their crystalline nature. In A. gallica AgNPs, diffraction peaks were observed at 38.1°, 44.5°, 64.5°, and 77.3°, while A. cepistipes AgNPs exhibited corresponding peaks at 38.1°, 44.5°, 64.4°, and 77.4°. These reflections were indexed to the (111), (200), (220), and (311) crystallographic planes of face-centered cubic (FCC) silver, respectively, confirming the successful formation of crystalline AgNPs. Similar FCC reflections have been widely reported for green-synthesized silver nanoparticles, indicating successful reduction of silver ions and formation of crystalline metallic silver. FTIR showed the presence of many proteins, phenolic compounds and other polysaccharides as stabilizing agents and reducing agents already documented in many previous papers based on mushroom based nanoparticles McNeilly et al.43. Spherical morphology seen in SEM with the particle sizes 20 - 60 nm are comparable to previous reports Martinez and Patel44. 0.21 PDI value of A. gallica and 0.27 PDI value of A. cepistipes showed the stability of nanoparticles consistent with the previous studies45. Negative zeta potential values of A. gallica (-30.2 mV) and A. cepistipes (-27.3 mV) indicate good stability which suggests the decreased aggregation as shown in earlier reported studies Nowacka-Jechalke et al.46, Promyos et al.47,48. Similar results were shown in previous reports of green synthesized nanoparticles of many plants and fungal extracts Chen et al.49–51. Although the nanoparticle formulations demonstrated higher DPPH radical scavenging activity than the corresponding crude extracts, their antioxidant activity remained lower than that of ascorbic acid, indicating moderate but promising antioxidant potential. Furthermore, the production of nanoparticles increases the stability and bioavailability of bioactive substances, boosting their potential as antioxidants. DPPH assay provides a reliable indication of free radical scavenging potential, the use of additional antioxidant assays such as FRAP and ABTS would further strengthen the evaluation of total antioxidant capacity, as different assays are based on distinct reaction mechanisms.

Diabetes was successfully induced with the help of streptozotocin in mice. Mice were selected because they are highly sensitive to Streptozotocin-induced diabetes, economical to maintain, and easy to handle in experimental studies Liu et al.52,Masouri et al.17. Compared to rats, mice require less space, smaller doses of test compounds, and provide rapid and reliable diabetic responses. Mice are likewise well-established experimental models because of their genetic consistency, ease of handling, and suitability for controlled pharmacological investigations. The diabetes was checked in 7 days. A preliminary experiment was performed in which the low dose (100 mg/kg) moderate (150 mg/kg) and high dose (200 mg/kg) were compared. Low dose failed to induce diabetes and high dose caused the mortality in many mice. Only the moderate dose showed the balance reliability in diabetes induction. In previous studies ('Ahmad and Singh14 showed the diabetes induction at 150 mg/kg similar to our results.53,54 showed the diabetes induction at STZ at low doses but it was contrary to our results. (Wszola,55 results of disease induction through STZ were also similar to our results of preliminary experiment. Ahmad and Singh14 highlighted the similar results of low and moderate dose just like our preliminary experiment results. On the basis of preliminary studies, 200 mg/kg body weight was selected as the optimum dose for both Armillaria spp. extracts and its silver nanoparticles also consistent with the previous reports56.

Different biological markers were analyzed. Biomarker analysis in mouse models of diabetes reveals distinct molecular signatures for disease progression and recovery. Glutathione (GSH) and its associated enzymes exhibit complex roles in diabetes pathogenesis and complications. Diabetic STZ group showed the decreased level of glutathione as compared to the control groups. Mushroom treatments in diabetic mice consistently increase glutathione (GSH) levels compared to untreated STZ-induced diabetic group. Armillaria gallica nanoparticles (A.G AgNPs) and Armillaria cepistipes nanoparticles (A.C AgNPs) showed much more recovered increased level of glutathione (11.2 umol/L ± 0.8, 13.4 umol/L ± 0.9 respectively) as compared to the crude extracts of both mushrooms (8.4 umol/L ± 0.6, 7.3 umol/L ± 0.3) and the STZ group (4.4 umol/L ± 0.4). Our results were consistent with the previous studies. Widiastuti et al.57 also observed the restored GSH levels through the treatment of oyster mushrooms by 30-51% in oxidative stress models suggesting the similar protective effects against STZ induced oxidative damage. Gamma-glutamyltransferase (GGT) levels were significantly elevated in the STZ-induced diabetic group compared with the normal control groups, indicating hepatic dysfunction and oxidative stress associated with diabetes. Similar elevations in GGT have been reported in STZ-induced diabetic and diabetic liver injury models, where increased GGT activity reflects hepatocellular damage and impaired antioxidant defense mechanisms58. Treatment with mushroom-derived silver nanoparticles markedly reduced GGT levels (59.2 ± 1.8 and 50.0 ± 1.4 U/L) compared with the STZ group (91.6 ± 2.5 U/L), suggesting a hepatoprotective effect. Comparable reductions in GGT have been observed following treatment with natural antioxidant-rich interventions such as bay leaf extract, Satureja bachtiarica, quercetin, and other plant-derived therapies in diabetic models58. Metformin, used as the positive control, exhibited the greatest improvement in GGT levels, consistent with its established antidiabetic and hepatoprotective effects. ALP levels were markedly elevated in the STZ-induced diabetic group (331.4 ± 8.0 U/L) compared with the saline control group (113.6 ± 3.2 U/L), indicating diabetes-associated liver damage. Treatment with mushroom-derived silver nanoparticles significantly reduced ALP levels, with A.G AgNPs and A.C AgNPs lowering ALP to 194.8 ± 16.4 U/L and 158.4 ± 5.4 U/L, respectively. Similar findings were reported by Pirabbasi et al.59, who demonstrated that green-synthesized silver nanoparticles significantly improved ALP levels in STZ-induced diabetic rats, suggesting hepatoprotective and antioxidant effects.

High density lipoproteins (HDL) level is usually high in normal conditions as in all control groups (saline 68.4 mg/dl ± 3.6) but in diabetic STZ group it was extremely low reached up to 22.2 mg/dl ± 1.2. After treatment with mushroom nanoparticles its value recovered up to 38.6 mg/dl ± 1.8 in case of A.G AgNPs and 44.0 mg/dl ± 1.9 in case of A.C AgNPs. Our results were contradictory to previous study in which it was reported that mushroom extracts had no effect on HDL level Masouri et al.17. The possible reason for the differences might include variations in mushroom species, extraction methods, dosage, treatment duration, and the experimental diabetes model.

TNF-α levels are typically low under normal physiological conditions but become markedly elevated in diabetes. Upon treatment and improvement of the diabetic condition, TNF-α levels are reduced and approach normal levels. In control groups its value was low (saline 14.3 pg/ml ± 0.7) and in diabetic STZ group its value was extremely high 160.9 pg/ml ± 4.2. When its value was observed in treatment groups A.G AgNPs recovered up to 62.0 pg/ml ± 1.8 and in A.C AgNPs group 53.8 1.7. In s previous study neutralizing TNF-α in the mice worsened glucose tolerance, while over-expressing TNF-α in ob/ob mice enhanced insulin sensitivity, suggesting alternative receptors or pathways mediate its effects (Saeed Majeed,60. While mushrooms show glucose-lowering effects in other studies, the provided data does not directly address TNF-α modulation post-mushroom intervention.

Baseline IL-6 levels in non-diabetic mice are typically lower compared to diabetic models. STZ diabetic group showed a markedly elevated IL-6 level (274.2 ± 12.2 pg/mL) compared with the normal control group (28.4 ± 4.2 pg/mL), indicating a pronounced inflammatory response. Treatment with metformin, A. gallica (A.G), A. cepistipes (A.C), A.G AgNPs, and A.C AgNPs significantly reduced IL-6 levels to 103.4 ± 9.5, 133.4 ± 5.7, 122.8 ± 12.4, 109.6 ± 9.6, and 92.0 ± 9.1 pg/mL, respectively, compared with the STZ group. Among the treatment groups, A.C AgNPs exhibited the greatest reduction in IL-6 levels, suggesting a stronger anti-inflammatory effect than the other treatments. IL-6 levels are significantly increased in diabetic mice, both in serum and cardiac tissue, as observed in models of diabetic cardiomyopathy McNeilly et al.43. Interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels in mice undergo significant changes before and after diabetes development, reflecting both protective and pathogenic roles61. IL-6 levels of liver, serum and cardiac tissue significantly increase during diabetes induction, whether by streptozotocin or fat62.

Before the onset of diabetes in mice, LDL cholesterol levels are generally stable and within normal ranges, especially in standard laboratory conditions. However, after the induction of diabetes-such as with streptozotocin, in LDL receptor knockout (Ldlr−/−) mice-there is a marked increase in plasma cholesterol. Studies show that diabetic Ldlr−/− mice can have plasma cholesterol levels more than double those of non-diabetic controls, LDL becoming more cholesterol-enriched and exhibiting altered apolipoprotein content (Goldberg, Wang et al.63. This elevation is primarily due to decreased clearance of these lipoproteins from the plasma rather than increased production, a result of impaired receptor-mediated uptake in the liver. The increase in LDL is a key factor in the accelerated atherosclerosis observed in diabetic mice64, Zhou,65.

The current histopathology results explicitly show that streptozotocin caused serious damage in the tissues of the pancreas, liver, and kidneys, whereas Armillaria spp. nanoparticles treatment significantly reduced these adverse effects. In comparison to the control group (0.2 ± 0.32), the pancreatic tissue of STZ-induced diabetic mice with high score of 11.56 ± 0.22) showed β-cell destruction, vacuolar degeneration, islet atrophy as shown in previous studies also66. Armillaria spp. extracts decreased the pancreatic damage up to 7.58 ± 0.32 whereas the Armillaria nanoparticles of both mushrooms under this study showed the great recovery of islets and decreased inflammation up to 3.4 0.58 similar to the results of standard drug taken (metformin) 3.33 ± 0.38. Medicinal mushrooms have been shown to have similar protective properties against oxidative damage and β-cell degeneration67.

STZ administration caused pronounced hepatocellular swelling, cytoplasmic vacuolation, and necrosis in liver tissues, leading to a significantly elevated hepatic damage score (11.3 ± 0.12) relative to the control mice (0.21 ± 0.23). Such histopathological changes are commonly observed in diabetic liver injury and have been attributed to increased oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation resulting from chronic hyperglycemia68. A. gallica and A. cepistipes extract application resulted in moderate hepatoprotection, lowering the histopathology score to 6.2 ± 0.24, while mice treated with nanoparticles showed better recovery with significantly lower scores (3.23 ± 0.58), along with organized liver cells and very less necrosis. The presence of phenolic and flavonoid chemicals in mushroom extracts, which stabilize hepatic membranes and inhibit the inflammation may be connected to the reported hepatoprotective action Deveci et al.10,Yavuz et al.69.

Parallel to this, diabetic mice showed signs of significant kidney damage, including contraction in glomerulus, dilation of tubules—all of which are characteristics of diabetic nephropathy. Kidney morphology was improved by treatment with Armillaria extracts, however nanoparticles showed more distinct renoprotection and considerably lower renal damage ratings (4.2 ± 0.12) than crude extract (7.21 ± 0.23). Renal morphology in the group which was given metfomin was almost normal (3.13 ± 0.23). These findings are in line with earlier research showing that while natural antioxidant treatments successfully maintain tubular and glomerular structure, inflammation and oxidative stress play a major role in diabetic renal damage70. Altogether, the microscopic results and scoring of histology firmly imply that silver nanoparticles of Armillaria species have strong effects against renal, pancreatic and liver injuries caused by STZ. The inclusion of bioactive substances frequently found in medicinal mushrooms, such as phenolic compounds, polysaccharides and antioxidants, may be linked to the demonstrated antidiabetic effect of Armillaria spp. extracts. These substances have been shown to lower oxidative stress, increase sensitivity of insulin, and increase metabolism of glucose in diabetic patients. The results of the current study are corroborated by earlier research on medicinal mushrooms, which also showed their capacity to control glucose level of blood and enhance metabolic indices.

The findings demonstrated that the animals which were treated with the nanoparticles showed better biological activities as compared to those which were given crude extracts. This enhanced effectiveness may be caused by the particles’ nanoscale size, which increases surface area and enhances interaction and contact with biological systems. Furthermore, it is known that nanoparticles improve the stability of bioactive substances, leading to better therapeutic action. Additionally, the total response observed in treated groups is improved by the antioxidant properties and anti-inflammatory characteristics of silver nanoparticles.

All the experimental animals were carefully observed on daily basis during whole experiment to make sure the safe use of Armillaria spp. extract and as well as its nanoparticles. Mice were observed for their behavior, changes in body weight, and any toxic signs like avoiding food, water, any tremors or mortality. Throughout the period of experiment, no abnormal behaviors were observed not even any abnormality was seen physically. There was no death rate observed in the experimental groups as compared to control group showing that the suggested doses of Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes were well tolerated by mice. Further research protocols such as glucose uptake analysis, α amylase inhibition and α glycosidase inhibition are necessary to understand the mechanisms of Armillaria spp’ antidiabetic characteristics.

Conclusion

To conclude, our suggested products Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes exerted significant therapeutic effects on STZ induced diabetic mice showing notable antidiabetic and antioxidant activities. Improvements shown in histopathology after treatment with mice and recovery in biochemical markers prove that the suggested species have a considerable antioxidant capacity and glucose regulation ability. The findings of this study suggest that Armillaria gallica and Armillaria cepistipes extracts and their nanoparticles possess promising antidiabetic and antioxidant activities in STZ-induced diabetic mice. However, further investigations, including acute toxicity, pharmacokinetic, dose-optimization, and clinical studies, are necessary to confirm their safety and therapeutic efficacy.
