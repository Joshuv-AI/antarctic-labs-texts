Resveratrol mitigates aspirin-induced intestinal injury in rats in a high-altitude hypoxic environment associated with reducing the expression levels of angiotensin II
Authors: Paziliya Abulaiti, Wen Hui Shi, Huan Liu, Ailifeire Tuerxuntayi, Kailibinuer Nuermaimaiti, Zhuo Shuyi Liu, Yingying Xing, Najimangu Rehemutula, Kudelaiti Abdukelimu, Weidong Liu, Jiang Wei Liu, Feng Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate whether the polyphenolic compound resveratrol can protect against intestinal injury induced by aspirin in hypoxic rats associated with the inhibition of angiotensin II (Ang-II), as well as mitigating intestinal mucosal oxidative stress and inflammation.MethodThe plain control group rats received daily gavage in a conventional atmosphere, while the high-altitude blank control group rats underwent the same procedure but was housed in a chamber simulating the hypoxic environment in a high-altitude.while the remaining groups rats were also housed in this hypoxic chamber and received daily aspirin gavage to establish an Aspirin-induced intestinal injury model. The resveratrol intervention group was administered resveratrol at varying doses 2 h post-aspirin gavage. Small intestinal mucosal gross morphology and histopathological damage were observed and scored across all groups. ELISA quantified MPO and SOD activity and IL-1β, IL-10, TNF-α levels in intestinal tissues, while immunohistochemistry assessed Ang-II and Intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1) expression.ResultThe high-altitude blank control group rats not only showed substantial intestinal injuries but also exhibited elevated expression of Ang-II and ICAM-1 in the small intestinal tissues of rats, which positively correlated with the degree of histological injury to the mucosa. Furthermore, there was increased expression of pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β and TNF-α, decreased expression of the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10, and elevated levels of the oxidative stress indicator myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity, while the levels of superoxide dismutase (SOD) were decreased in the small intestinal tissues of rats. The administration of aspirin in the hypoxic environment exacerbated intestinal inflammation and damage to the intestinal mucosa. After treatment with Resveratrol intervention, the expression of Ang-II and ICAM-1 was reduced, while oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in the intestinal mucosa were also decreased. The results of HE staining directly demonstrated the damage to the intestines and the repair of the intestinal mucosa after Resveratrol intervention. The effect of medium-dose resveratrol appeared to be particularly pronounced.ConclusionResveratrol can effectively mitigate intestinal injury induced by aspirin at high altitude in hypoxic environments. The mechanism may involve downregulating the expression of Ang-II and ICAM-1, alleviating oxidative stress and the inflammatory response of the intestinal mucosa, and preserving intestinal barrier function.Keywords: Aspirin, Hypoxic environment, Resveratrol, Angiotensin II, Intercellular adhesion molecule-1

Introduction

The high-altitude environment is characterized by low atmospheric pressure, hypoxemia, cold temperatures, and intense ultraviolet radiation. These conditions collectively cause stress across multiple body systems [1] .With increasing elevation, the progressive decline in partial pressure of arterial oxygen induces tissue hypoxemia. This hypoxic state activates inflammatory pathways and oxidative stress cascades, leading to cellular damage and dysfunction [2, 3]. Consequently, These physiological changes make individuals more susceptible to gastrointestinal issues such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, peptic ulcers, and hemorrhage [4]. Wu et al. demonstrated that the incidence of gastrointestinal haemorrhage in workers who operated for an extended period at altitude was 0.49%, and that incidence increased with altitude [5]. These oxidative stress products increase the intestinal permeability, allowing harmful microorganisms and antigens from the luminal environment to enter the circulation and increasing the risk of systemic inflammatory response syndrome [6].

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are well-documented for their gastroduodenal adverse effects [7]. Recent attention has increasingly focused on NSAID-induced small bowel injuries, with current evidence suggesting that NSAIDs elevate the risk of bleeding and perforation in the lower gastrointestinal (GI) tract to a degree comparable to that in the upper GI tract [8]. Aspirin, a widely utilized NSAID in clinical practice, is renowned for its favorable anti-inflammatory, antipyretic, and analgesic properties [9]. Studies have revealed that the incidence of small intestinal mucosal erosions, ulcers, and mucosal bleeding reaches as high as 70% in patients undergoing video capsule endoscopy following long-term oral administration of NSAIDs, including aspirin [10, 11]. However, the characteristics of intestinal damage resulting from prolonged NSAID administration in high-altitude environments remain inadequately documented. This study aims to simulate and observe Aspirin-induced intestinal injury in a high-altitude hypoxic environment by administering aspirin via gavage to rats housed in a specialized hypoxic chamber, with the objective of investigating the potential exacerbating impact of high-altitude hypoxia on Aspirin-related intestinal injury.

Angiotensin II, a peptide hormone primarily synthesized by the lungs with additional contributions from the kidneys, plays a crucial role in regulating blood pressure, fluid-electrolyte balance, and kidney function [12]. Beyond these functions, angiotensin II modulates various physiological aspects of the gastrointestinal system, significantly influencing gastrointestinal blood flow, motility, and secretion [13]. Recent research has implicated angiotensin II in inflammatory responses, particularly in the context of intestinal inflammation [14]. It has been shown to regulate apoptosis in intestinal epithelial cells [15]. The angiotensin II receptor (AT2 receptor), widely distributed in the intestine, has been suggested to correlate with both the onset and progression of intestinal inflammation [16]. As a crucial component of the renin-angiotensin system (RAS), angiotensin II is known to be activated during hypoxic conditions, resulting in increased circulating renin activity, angiotensin II levels, and ACE activity, thereby exacerbating hypoxic injury to local tissues [17]. Nevertheless, the precise relationship between hypoxia, angiotensin II expression levels, and intestinal inflammation warrants further investigation. This study focuses on alterations in angiotensin II expression levels in intestinal tissues under high-altitude hypoxic conditions and the influence of aspirin, employing animal models to elucidate the mechanisms underlying intestinal injuries induced by high-altitude hypoxic environments and aspirin administration.

Resveratrol, a plant polyphenol extracted from grape seeds and peels [18]exhibits a spectrum of pharmacological properties including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects [19]. This natural compound shows promise for the prevention and treatment of intestinal diseases. Research has demonstrated that resveratrol protects against intestinal injury through multiple mechanisms, including mitigation of oxidative stress, inhibition of inflammatory responses, and modulation of apoptosis [20]. In this study, resveratrol was administered at various doses to evaluate its protective effects against intestinal injury and to determine whether it can alleviate aspirin-induced intestinal injury in rats by modulating angiotensin II expression levels under high-altitude hypoxic conditions. This research aims to provide valuable insights into the prevention and treatment of intestinal injury in patients undergoing long-term oral asprin therapy in clinical high-altitude settings.

Methods and materials

Chemicals

The aspirin was acquired from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co. Resveratrol was purchased from Sigma Company in the United States, with a purity of 98%, product number: V900386. Sodium pentobarbital was purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co., Ltd. Sodium carboxymethyl cellulose was purchased from Shanghai McLean Reagents Co., Ltd. The ELISA detection kit was purchased from Merck Company.

Reagent preparation

The dose of aspirin was determined based on the research method of Qiao et al. [21], with an administration dose of 200 mg/kg. To prepare the sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na) solution, 5 g of CMC-Na was dissolved in 1000 mL of deionized water to form a 0.5% CMC-Na solution. Aspirin was then dissolved in a 5% CMC-Na solution to formulate a solution with a concentration of 75 mg/mL. For resveratrol, . [], with different doses administered to rats via gavage: 25 mg/kg (low dose), 50 mg/kg (medium dose), and 100 mg/kg (high dose). When preparing the solution, resveratrol was also dissolved in a 5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose solution to obtain a solution with a concentration of 50 mg/mL.

Experimental animals and environment

This study was conducted in strict compliance with the ethical standards for animal research. All experimental protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Xinjiang Medical University (Approval number: KY20230209135) and performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the national standard “Guidelines for Ethical Review of Animal Welfare” (GB/T 35892 − 2018).Sixty male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats, aged 6 weeks with an initial body weight of 250 ± 50 g, were obtained from the Animal Experiment Center of Xinjiang Medical University. The animals were housed in the Northwest Key Laboratory of Special Environmental Medicine, located at the General Hospital of Xinjiang Military Region of the Armed Police in Xinjiang, China. The environmental conditions were maintained at a controlled temperature of 22 ± 2 °C, relative humidity of 40–60%, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The rats were housed in standard experimental cages (485 × 350 × 200 mm) with five animals per cage and provided with standard rodent diet and water ad libitum throughout the experimental period.To establish the hypoxic environment, the chamber parameters were precisely controlled to simulate an altitude of 5500 m, with atmospheric pressure maintained at 93.2 kPa (699.1 mmHg) and partial oxygen pressure at 19.54 kPa (146.6 mmHg). All procedures were designed to minimize animal suffering, and the experimental protocols were strictly followed in accordance with the ethical guidelines for animal research.

Experimental design

Following a one-week acclimatization period, all rats were randomly allocated into six experimental groups: plain blank control (PC group), high-altitude blank control (HC group), high-altitude NSAIDs injury (HN group), and resveratrol intervention groups with low dose (RES-L group, 25 mg/kg), medium dose (RES-M group, 50 mg/kg), and high dose (RES-H group, 100 mg/kg) administration. Considering the potential for increased mortality under simulated 5,500-meter hypoxic conditions, each group comprised 10 rats to ensure statistical reliability and adequate sample size.The daily oral dose of each drug was converted from the human oral dose by reference. The plain blank control group was exposed to the conventional atmospheric environment, while the other groups were placed in the hypoxia chamber in a special environment.The control group received daily gavage with saline (1 ml/100 g), while the remaining rats were administered aspirin at a dosage of 200 mg/kg daily. The resveratrol intervention group received resveratrol at doses of 25 mg/kg, 50 mg/kg, and 100 mg/kg in the low, medium, and high dose groups, respectively, for 2 h following the 200 mg/kg aspirin administration, continuously for 3 weeks.In order to minimize potential confounders, treatments, measurements, and animal/cage locations were randomized. During the experiment, all experimenters excepting the designer were blinded to group assignment.

Tissue sample collection

Following the final administration, the rats underwent a 24-hour fasting period before being anesthetized through an intraperitoneal injection of a 3% sodium pentobarbital solution at a dosage of 40 mg/kg. Under strict aseptic conditions, the abdominal cavity was immediately opened for macroscopic examination of its contents. Subsequently, intestinal segments were carefully dissected for damage assessment. Specifically, a 2 cm segment of proximal jejunum was excised from each rat and immersed in 10% formaldehyde solution for 24 h to ensure proper fixation. Following fixation, the tissue samples were embedded in paraffin wax for subsequent microscopic evaluation and immunohistochemical analysis. Furthermore, an additional 5 cm segment of intestinal tissue distal to the proximal jejunum was isolated and homogenized on ice using the homogenization medium from the test kit to prepare a 5% tissue homogenate. The activities of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-10, MPO, and SOD in the jejunal tissues were quantitatively determined using the Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) method, strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s protocol for each specific kit.

Intestinal injury score and HE staining

The severity of intestinal mucosal tissue injury was assessed, and the degree of intestinal damage was macroscopically scored according to the Reuten scoring system [23]. The excised intestinal tissue was fixed in 10% neutral formaldehyde solution and subsequently processed for routine paraffin embedding to prepare 5-µm-thick sections for morphological evaluation. The sections were then deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded series of alcohols, and stained sequentially with hematoxylin-eosin (HE). The degree of small intestinal injury was evaluated according to Chiu’s criteria (0–4 scale), where 0 points denotes normal intestinal mucosal villi; 1 point denotes the occurrence of cystic spaces under the epithelium at the top of the villi with capillary congestion; 2 points denotes an enlarged subepithelial space, a moderate lamina propria edema, and a dilated central chyle duct; 3 points denotes an obvious edema of the lamina propria, degeneration and necrosis of the epithelial layer of the intestinal mucosa, and a little villus tip shedding; and 4 points denotes degeneration and necrosis of the epithelial cell layer, some villi shedding, naked lamina propria, and telangiectasia [24].Histopathological changes in the small intestine were evaluated under a light microscope by three independent pathologists using a blinded method to ensure objective assessment.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Small intestinal tissues were fixed in 10% neutral formalin buffer, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 4 μm-thick slices. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed twice under high heat using EDTA antigen repair solution (50X). Following blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 35 min, the sections were thoroughly washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against angiotensinogen (1:200 dilution) and Icam-1, followed by incubation with goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody HRP Polymer Quanto at 37 °C for 35 min. Visualization was achieved using DAB horseradish peroxidase chromogen solution, resulting in brownish-yellow staining, and cell nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, mounted, scanned using a digital scanner, and analyzed using Visiopharm AI pathology analysis software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA). The results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA and the least significant difference (LSD-t) method were used to assess statistical significance between multiple groups. Result

Resveratrol inhibits hypoxia-induced macroscopic intestinal injury

No macroscopic damage, adhesion, or ulceration was observed in the rats of the PC group. In the HC group, mild intestinal mucosal adhesion and edema were evident, though no ulcers or bleeding points were detected; the tissue injury score was 1.15 ± 0.34, suggesting that the high-altitude hypoxic environment induces intestinal injury. In contrast, the HN group exhibited severe intestinal mucosal adhesion accompanied by varying degrees of congestion and edema, with some rats displaying necrosis of the intestinal wall, characterized by a purple-black discoloration. While no intestinal perforation or ulceration was observed, the tissue injury score in the HN group (2.00 ± 0.62) was significantly higher than that in the HC group (P < 0.001), demonstrating a correlation between aspirin administration and exacerbated intestinal injury in a high-altitude environment. In the resveratrol intervention groups, localized congestion, edema, and adhesion of the small intestine were observed; however, the severity of these lesions was notably reduced compared to the HN group, indicating that resveratrol mitigates intestinal injury. Among the intervention groups, the medium-dose resveratrol group exhibited the lowest tissue injury score (1.20 ± 0.42), which was significantly lower than that of the HN group (P < 0.01), show Fig. 1.

Resveratol inhibits hypoxia-induced histopathological intestinal injury

Histopathological analysis of the small intestine using HE staining revealed distinct morphological changes across the groups, as illustrated in Fig. 2. In the PC group, the small intestinal villi displayed intact architecture, with clearly defined layers, normal tissue morphology, and a pathological score of 0.20 ± 0.42. In contrast, the HC group exhibited villous edema accompanied by capillary congestion and lymphocyte infiltration, though the structural integrity of each layer remained evident; the pathological score of 1.20 ± 0.79 was significantly higher than that of the PC group (P < 0.05). The HN group demonstrated more severe pathological alterations, including pronounced edema in the lamina propria, degeneration and necrosis of epithelial cells, occasional villus tip detachment, and significant inflammatory cell infiltration, resulting in a pathological score of 2.9 ± 0.99, which was markedly higher than that of the HC group (P < 0.001). In the resveratrol intervention groups (low, medium, and high doses), the degree of pathological damage to the small intestinal mucosa was reduced compared to the HN group. However, some rats still exhibited villous edema, apical epithelial detachment, capillary congestion, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Resveratrol affects hypoxia-induced inflammatory factors

ELISA revealed significantly elevated IL-1β and TNF-α levels but reduced IL-10 levels in small intestinal tissues of high-altitude control (HC) rats compared to plain control (PC) animals (P < 0.01). In the high-altitude NSAID (HN) group, both IL-1β and TNF-α levels showed significant elevation concurrent with IL-10 reduction relative to PC controls (P < 0.01). These findings indicate that aspirin administration under hypoxic conditions potentiates pro-inflammatory cytokine production while suppressing anti-inflammatory responses in the intestinal mucosa, consequently aggravating tissue injury. Resveratrol intervention significantly attenuated IL-1β and TNF-α expression while elevating IL-10 levels in intestinal tissues (P < 0.05). Disscusion

The plateau environment, characterized by low barometric pressure, reduced oxygen partial pressure, and cold temperatures, induces oxidative stress and inflammatory responses through ischemia and hypoxia in critical organs, impairing normal physiological functions - particularly those of the gastrointestinal system [25]. This phenomenon may be attributed to the gastrointestinal tract’s heightened sensitivity to hypoxia. The small intestine exhibits particularly high oxygen demand owing to its intense metabolic and absorptive activities, complex vascularization, and rapid mucosal cell turnover [26]. Consequently, persistent hypoxia frequently induces pathological alterations in intestinal tissue structure and function. In this study, HC group rats exhibited mucosal adhesions, tissue edema, and pronounced villous edema in the small intestine, accompanied by capillary congestion, lymphocyte infiltration, and other histopathological alterations compared to PC group. ELISA analysis revealed elevated pro-inflammatory factors IL-1β, TNF-α and reduced anti-inflammatory IL-10 levels in intestinal tissues. Additionally, we observed increased myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity and decreased superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity. MPO is a specific marker of neutrophil infiltration, whereas SOD regulates intracellular superoxide radical homeostasis [27].These findings demonstrate that the plateau environment induces intestinal oxidative stress and inflammation, disrupting gastrointestinal structure and function,, which aligns with the results of previous studies.

Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), including aspirin, are widely used for their anti-inflammatory, antipyretic, and analgesic effects, as well as their ability to inhibit platelet aggregation. However, prolonged NSAID use compromises intestinal mucosal barrier integrity, leading to NSAID-associated enteropathies [28, 29]. While NSAIDs are known to cause upper gastrointestinal adverse effects, advancements in diagnostic techniques like capsule endoscopy and enteroscopy have highlighted their detrimental impact on the lower gastrointestinal tract, particularly the small bowel [30]. Chronic NSAID therapy induces small bowel inflammation in approximately 60% of patients [31] with severe small intestinal injury accounting for nearly one-third of NSAID-related adverse events [32]. Even short-term low-dose aspirin can cause small intestinal mucosal inflammation, while long-term use leads to lesions such as petechiae, villous atrophy, mucosal erosions, and severe complications like ulceration and perforation [33, 34]. This is particularly concerning given the necessity of lifelong aspirin regimens for managing cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, and neoplastic diseases, which may result in dose-dependent enteropathy [35]. In high-altitude populations, chronic aspirin use significantly increases the risk of gastrointestinal injury. To model aspirin-induced intestinal injury under hypoxia, we administered aspirin to rats exposed to simulated high altitude. Histopathological analysis revealed severe mucosal adhesions, multifocal congestion, edema, and localized intestinal wall necrosis in the HN group. Ultrastructural examination showed villous edema, apical epithelial necrosis, disorganized lamina propria glands, and extensive inflammatory infiltration. Inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers were significantly elevated compared to the HC group (P < 0.05). These findings demonstrate that NSAIDs induce small intestinal injury, and the combined effects of high-altitude hypoxia and aspirin administration exacerbate mucosal damage.

Angiotensin II (Ang II), a peptide hormone predominantly synthesized in the lungs with additional renal contribution, functions not only as a regulator of blood pressure, water-salt balance, and renal function but also as a proinflammatory mediator [36]. Ang II receptors are ubiquitously distributed in the kidneys, brain, heart, lungs, intestines, blood vessels, and immune cells [37] with the colon exhibiting the highest receptor density in the intestine, followed by the ileum, duodenum, and jejunum [38]. Elevated expression levels of Ang II are induced under hypoxic conditions through mechanisms involving inflammation, oxidative stress, and vascular dysfunction. Ang II mediates the effects of the renin-angiotensin system in the intestine primarily via angiotensin receptor type 1 (AT1) activation. This activation increases intestinal vascular permeability, disrupts tight junctions in vascular endothelial cells, promotes cellular exudation and edema formation [39] and modulates immune cell activation, thereby contributing to inflammatory cell infiltration, leukocyte rolling, and adhesion [40]. Moreover, binding to its receptor activates key signaling pathways; for instance, activation of NF-κB—a central hub in intestinal inflammation—leads to the production of adhesion molecules and chemokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, and ICAM-1, exacerbating the inflammatory response and causing tissue damage [41]. Ang-II also induces apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cells in an AT2 receptor-dependent manner through the GATA-6/Bax pathway, increasing mucosal barrier permeability and intestinal inflammatory injury [42]. Angiotensin II influences smooth muscle cell growth, proliferation, hypertrophy, and proinflammatory responses primarily via reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by NADPH oxidase [43]. Furthermore, Ang II plays a critical role in NSAID-associated small bowel injury in rats, with the RAS-p38MAPK signaling pathway implicated in its pathogenesis [44]. Concordant with previous studies reporting hypoxia-induced increases in Ang II expression via inflammation, oxidative stress, and vascular dysfunction [45] our study demonstrated significantly higher Ang II and ICAM-1 levels in intestinal mucosal tissues of rats in the HN group, which exhibited the most severe injury. Notably, Ang II and ICAM-1 expression levels were positively correlated with the severity of intestinal damage. These findings underscore a significant association between hypoxia, elevated Ang II expression, and intestinal inflammation, indicating that Ang II contributes to intestinal inflammatory injury through diverse signaling pathways.

ICAM-1 (Intercellular Adhesion Molecule-1) mediates leukocyte adhesion and migration to regulate inflammation [46]. Its expression is induced by cytokines and inflammatory mediators, promoting immune cell adhesion to endothelium. This subsequently triggers pro-inflammatory cytokine release, exacerbating tissue injury [47]. Pastore et al. confirmed Angiotensin II upregulates ICAM-1, amplifying inflammation [48]. Our data concur: Angiotensin II stimulates ICAM-1 via multiple pathways. ICAM-1 binding facilitates inflammatory cell adhesion and aggregation, releasing mediators that propagate inflammation and intestinal injury. In summary, the regulation of Angiotensin II expression may be implicated in the mechanism of aspirin-associated small bowel injury in rats exposed to a plateau hypoxic environment。.

Studies confirm no effective strategy exists for preventing NSAID-induced lower gastrointestinal injury [49]. Resveratrol, detected in plants like tiger nuts and grapes, regulates intestinal barrier dysfunction via anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic mechanisms [50]. Resveratrol emerges as a highly promising natural drug for preventing intestinal injury. Furthermore, it enhances the activity of pigment oxidase in intestinal mucosal epithelial cells and reduces the generation of reactive oxygen species, thereby demonstrating its antioxidant properties [51]. Research has shown that resveratrol inhibits apoptosis of intestinal epithelial cells to maintain the integrity of the intestinal barrier [52]. In this study, the resveratrol intervention group demonstrated a reduction in histopathological findings and macroscopic injury in the small intestinal mucosa compared with the HN group. These results indicate that resveratrol mitigates intestinal cell necrosis and apoptosis, consistent with previous research. Evidence indicates that the anti-inflammatory effect of resveratrol on the intestinal tract is primarily mediated by the down-regulation of pro-inflammatory factors and inflammatory mediators [53]. Compared to the HN group, the resveratrol intervention group showed significantly reduced expression of AngⅡ and ICAM-1 proteins in rat intestinal mucosal tissues. Additionally, pro-inflammatory factors including IL-1β, TNF-α, and MPO in rat intestinal tissues were significantly decreased, whereas the anti-inflammatory factor IL-10 and the antioxidant molecule SOD were increased. Notably, the difference was most pronounced in the middle-dose resveratrol intervention group, indicating that resveratrol alleviates intestinal injury by down-regulating the expression of AngⅡ and ICAM-1 in intestinal tissues, thereby reducing the production of further inflammatory factors and oxidative stress molecules. The efficacy of medium-dose resveratrol was particularly pronounced.

Limitations of this study include: (1) Modified intestinal injury resulting from prolonged oral NSAID administration under plateau conditions remains insufficiently characterized.(2) While resveratrol shows promise for preventing NSAID-induced intestinal injury, this investigation relied exclusively on animal models without in vitro validation, limiting direct human translatability and leaving clinical feasibility unverified.(3) The controlled laboratory setting may not fully replicate the multifactorial real-world conditions affecting plateau residents, potentially introducing deviations from actual pathophysiology. Thus, findings require validation through long-term replicated studies.

Conclusion

The dual stress conditions of a high-altitude hypoxic environment and aspirin can lead to the exacerbation of intestinal mucosal injury in rats. The expression level of Ang Ⅱ may be associated with the mechanism of NSAID-induced small intestinal injury in rats at high altitude. Resveratrol can effectively mitigate intestinal injury induced by aspirin in a high-altitude hypoxic environment. The mechanism may involve downregulating the expression of Ang Ⅱ and ICAM-1, alleviating oxidative stress and the inflammatory response of the intestinal mucosa, and preserving intestinal function.
