Multi-omics data integration using time-to event endpoint and supervised Cox penalized regression: a comprehensive review
Authors: Antoine Dubray-Vautrin, Christophe Le Tourneau, Jimmy Mullaert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe integration of multi-omics data, encompassing genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, and proteomics, has revolutionized medical research by enabling a more comprehensive understanding of complex diseases like cancer. Multi-omics prognostic models facilitate improved patient stratification through personalized prognostication. However, the high dimensionality, heterogeneity, and correlations between omics layers pose significant challenges for predictive modelling building, particularly in time-to-event analyses. This review synthesizes current methodologies for variable selection and regularization in high-dimensional settings, focusing on their application to survival outcomes. We explore global penalty approaches, such as LASSO, Ridge, and Elastic Net, which apply uniform penalties to control model complexity and improve generalizability. Parallel regression methods, which independently analyse different omics layers before integrating results, offering robustness but potentially missing critical correlation. Group regularization techniques, including Group LASSO and OSCAR regression, address multicollinearity by clustering correlated predictors, enhancing interpretability in high-dimensional datasets. Hierarchical regression models, such as Priority LASSO and IPF-LASSO, leverage prior knowledge of omics relationships to improve integration and interpretability but may overlook platform interactions. Kernel-based methods like KEN-COX are also examined for their ability to handle nonlinear relationships and reduce dimensionality. Each method presents unique trade-offs between interpretability, computational efficiency, and predictive performance. This review highlights the need for tailored approaches that balance these factors, emphasizing the importance of model transparency and clinical applicability. Future research should focus on refining these techniques to better capture the complex interplay of omics data in disease progression and survival outcomes.Keywords: Integrative analysis, Survival modeling, Omics: Regularization, High-dimensional statistics, Head and neck

Introduction

The multi-omics approach in medical sciences, which integrates genomic, transcriptomic, methylation, and radiomic data, is increasingly being used with the development of Next Generation Sequencing. Multi-omics prognostic models are particularly useful for better patient stratification and can enhance our understanding of the multiple, simultaneous disruptions in gene regulation whether through activation or inhibition associated with cancer and its progression [1].

Each omics layer (genomics, epigenomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics) provides complementary biological information about the studied system [2]. Uncovering relevant information and integrating multi-omics data into a coherent model is inherently challenging. In recent years, a variety of methods and strategies have been developed to address this complexity, enabling more comprehensive insights into biological systems. if the process of integration is not performed correctly, the inclusion of additional omics information may not enhance performance but can instead increase both the complexity of the problem and the computational burden. The complexity of the integration of multi-omics data is related to different problematics as: the high dimensionality [3], the correlation between the layers and the data heterogeneity with the difficulty of normalization [4]. Using multi-omics approach in predictive model with time to-event endpoint may predict disease activity, severity, and progression by leveraging integrated molecular data. Rather than relying on single molecule biomarkers, multi-omics studies use complex molecular signatures and patterns as predictors. While this approach promises more accurate predictions, it also introduces new challenges in incorporating these composite features into predictive models [1].

In high-dimensional settings, the performance of statistical models is significantly influenced by the processes of variable selection and model validation. As the complexity of data increases, particularly in contexts involving time-to-event outcomes, the choice of variable selection method becomes critical. Various approaches have been proposed in the literature, including supervised, semi-supervised, and unsupervised integrative analyses [5,6] using penalization and/or Bayesian framework [7]. These methods aim to effectively manage the challenges posed by high-dimensional data while ensuring robust model performance. Penalized regression techniques have emerged as a prominent solution for achieving sparsity and identifying non-zero coefficients in high-dimensional contexts. These approaches facilitate the selection of relevant variables while mitigating issues related to overfitting and multicollinearity and demonstrate their effectiveness for biological data modelling using linear and logistic penalized regression [8]. The literature describes different regularization pathways that enhance model selection and variable identification, allowing researchers to navigate the complexities inherent in high dimension. Regularization methods can be broadly classified into horizontal integration techniques, such as clustering methods, and vertical integration approaches, including parallel or hierarchical regression models. Each method presents unique advantages and limitations in terms of interpretability, computational efficiency, and adaptability to varying data structures. The performance of these methods is particularly sensitive when applied to time-to-event (TTE) outcomes, where the interplay between covariates can substantially influence survival estimates [5]. Unlike binary outcomes, TTE endpoints incorporate not only whether an event occurs but also when it occurs, introducing specific challenges such as right censoring, varying follow-up durations, and the need to model hazard functions over time. In this article, we focus on penalized Cox regression methods, aiming to preserve model interpretability while developing prognostic approaches with potential clinical relevance for survival outcomes. By prioritizing model transparency, we aim to enhance the understanding of key predictors and their relationships with outcomes, ultimately facilitating the application of these models in clinical decision-making.

This comprehensive review aims to synthesize the current understanding of variable selection methods and regularization strategies derived from cox penalized regression within high-dimensional frameworks, focusing on their implications for integrating time-to-event outcomes.

Methods

The main objective of this article was to update with a critical review the supervised and penalized Cox regression used in the field of multi-omics integration to synthetize their strengths, weaknesses and application to guide translational research and improve the robustness of statistical modeling using time-to event outcome. We conducted a comprehensive and critical review rather than a systematic review to underline methodologic aspect of the statistical models as an interface with the translational research.

Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive review using PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus at the date of 14th January, 2026. The key words were: “Cox penalization; regularization, high dimension, multi-omics, integration, prognosis” and their combination.

The criteria of inclusion were: “English language, available article, methodological article, Original research with methodological description of statistical process”. The exclusion criteria were: “Not English language, no methodological description of penalized regression”.

Objective

The principal objective was to synthesize the current understanding of variable selection methods and regularization strategies within high-dimensional frameworks, focusing on their implications for integrating time-to-event outcomes considering omics data integration.

Application

To illustrate the practical applications and predictive performance of the described models, we applied them to the publicly available TCGA Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma (HNSC) cohort (TCGA-HNSC). Clinical data (age, sex, TNM staging), somatic mutation profiles (binary 0/1 coding), and bulk RNA-seq transcriptomic data (normalized FPKM) were downloaded from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://tcga-data.nci.nih.gov/tcga/, accessed 12 october 2024). Complete-case analysis was performed, with transcriptomic variables filtered to exclude features with mean expression < 1 FPKM and log-transformed to log(TPM + 1) scale for normalization.

All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio version 4.4.2. For each tested statistical model LASSO-Cox, Elastic-Net, SCAD, MCP, Multiview Cox, Parallel three steps Lasso, Group-Lasso, IPF-LASSO, Priority-LASSO, XRNET and FusedTree regularization we evaluated the number, of variables with non-zero coefficients, alongside discriminative predictive performance measured by 3-year AUC using the TimeROC package.

To model and estimate the predictive performance we used a nested-cross validation approach with K1 inner loop (K1 = 10) for hyperparameters tuning of the model and a K2 outer loop (k2 = 5) to estimate the 3 years AUC with the best linear predictor obtained in the inner loops. We reported The estimated 3years AUC corresponded to an empirical mean-AUC throughout the nested-cross validation approach with a standard deviation calculated throughout the outer loop. The selected variable corresponded to the best model developed throughout the nested-cross-validation approach. We reported the best 3 years AUC corresponded to the best model.

This benchmarking framework enabled direct comparison of variable selection patterns and prognostic accuracy across global, group-based, and hierarchical regularization approaches in a clinically relevant multi-omics survival context.

Penalized regression methods such as ridge, lasso, and elastic net regression introduce a penalty term to the loss function of a regression model to prevent overfitting by shrinking the estimated coefficients towards zero. This approach applies a uniform penalty across all predictors, thereby controlling the complexity of the model and improving its generalizability. Global penalty regularization involves incorporating a penalty term into the regression model that applies uniformly to all coefficients of the matrix of predictors. This is particularly relevant in survival analysis, where the goal is to predict the time until an event occurs (e.g., failure, death) while accounting for censored data.

Non-convex penalization

Non-convex penalties often promote sparsity in model parameters, which is beneficial in high-dimensional settings where only a few predictors are expected to be significant by providing flexibility with the risk of non-convergence due to multiple local minima in the objective function and the risk of algorithmic instability.

Limits of global penalization

The complexity and dimensionality of these datasets often lead to models with suboptimal predictive performance. The application of uniform penalties in multi-omics approaches presents unique challenges, particularly in the context of multicollinearity among predictors. Multi-omics data, which integrates information from various biological layers such as genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics, often exhibits high correlations among features derived from different platforms. When uniform penalties are applied across all predictors, there is a risk of oversimplifying the complex relationships inherent in these data. This can lead to a loss of valuable additive information that each omics layer contributes to clinical outcomes. While uniform penalties effectively mitigate overfitting and enhance model stability, they may not adequately capture the nuanced interactions between different omics layers. As a result, important biological insights could be overlooked, potentially limiting the clinical utility of the model. Using complex matrices of predictors presents a significant challenge in identifying relevant biological factors, as the absence of pre-filtering complicates the selection process.

Parallel regression

To enhance the analysis of the association between multi-level omics measurements and cancer survival outcomes, a penalized Cox regression approach can be integrated into the existing framework. The regression is applied separately on the matrix of predictors with independence between the different resources of data [5]. As an example, we combined regularization on matrix of genomic data for hyper tuning and selection, parallelly for transcriptomics data and the predictors are select together in the final model [25], the selected predictors of each platform are concatenated in a unique matrix where the model is finally developed. This method allows for more robust modeling of complex relationships in high-dimensional data. In traditional single-level analyses, the association between individual molecular levels and a phenotype of interest is examined separately, the non-zero selected coefficients are integrated together in a third step. This method often overlooks the potential interactions among different omics layers. In contrast, parallel integration treats each type of omics measurement equally, potentially missing critical relationships.

Group regularization

Clustering and grouping Cox regularization methods enhance the analysis of survival data by addressing the complexities associated with high-dimensional predictors. These methods incorporate regularization techniques into Cox proportional hazards models to manage multicollinearity and improve predictive performance. Clustering and grouping Cox regularization methods are essential for multi-omics survival analysis where predictors naturally form biologically coherent units (pathways, omic layers, cell types). These approaches impose structured sparsity that respects prior biological knowledge, unlike standard Lasso which treats variables independently. Different grouping strategies can be applied as prior biological knowledge: Genes grouped by KEGG/Reactome pathways, or omic layers (mutations, RNA-seq, methylation) or data-driven latent grouping: Hierarchical clustering, k-means, or adaptive methods discovering structure from correlation patterns.

Octagonal shrinkage and clustering algorithm for regression

The OSCAR (Octagonal Shrinkage and Clustering Algorithm for Regression) is a regularization method that extends the LASSO and Elastic Net approaches by incorporating an additional penalty term designed to promote clustering of predictors [33,34]. When applied to Cox regression models, OSCAR-COX enables simultaneous feature selection and grouping of correlated covariates while accounting for the survival data’s censored nature using an added composite penalty term designed to induce sparsity and handle correlated features.

Kernel Elastic net regularized Cox regression

The Kernel Elastic Net Regularized Cox Regression (KEN-COX) method extends traditional Cox regression by introducing a novel kernel-based regularization framework [34,35]. It incorporates a kernel elastic net penalty to handle nonlinear relationships, sparsity, and grouped effects among covariates in high-dimensional survival data. The kernel framework enables the method to model complex dependencies while retaining the interpretability and regularization properties of elastic net regression. The kernel matrix is typically constructed using a symmetric positive-definite function, such as a linear, polynomial, or radial basis function (RBF) kernel, and optimized through a cyclic coordinate descent algorithm.

Different kernel choices allow the model to adapt to distinct data characteristics: the linear kernel reduces to the classical elastic net Cox model suitable for additive effects; the polynomial kernel captures higher-order interactions among features; the Gaussian radial basis function kernel flexibly models smooth nonlinear covariate effects on survival (e.g., gene expression interactions), outperforming linear kernels for non-additive omics patterns. The diffusion or graph-based kernels can incorporate network topology or pathway structures inherent to multi-omics data capturing multi-hop relationships (e.g., indirect pathway effects on survival), enhancing interpretability by prioritizing biologically connected features over isolated signals. Thus, kernel selection directly influences the model’s capacity to represent underlying biological relationships and should be matched to the expected structure and scale of the omic features. The KEN-Cox framework therefore combines the interpretability and regularization strength of elastic net with the representational power of kernel methods for robust, nonlinear survival prediction.

As an application, Rong et al. [35] applied regularized Kernel cox method to the GSE24080 multiple myeloma dataset (169 patients, 129 prognostic genes, and five clinical features). It jointly models linear clinical covariates and nonlinear gene effects through a garrotized Gaussian kernel, capturing complex dependencies among genes. Compared to LASSO-COX and COSSO-COX, RegGKM achieved higher predictive accuracy in CVPL, C-statistics, and AUC metrics. RegGKM consistently selected biologically relevant genes (FCGR2B, FGFR3, DUSP5), whereas LASSO-COX identified different markers (CD163, KIT, DSG2), reflecting its restriction to linear effects. These results highlight the ability of RegGKM to detect nonlinear gene–gene and gene–survival interactions overlooked by penalized linear models.

Hierarchical regression

A hierarchical regression have to optimize the integration of different platforms with hierarchical inclusion of different matrix of predictors or a hierarchical penalization according to the structure of predictors. Hierarchical integration represents a sophisticated approach that leverages prior knowledge of regulatory relationships among various omics data platforms during the integration process. By acknowledging the inherent biological connections and interactions between different types of omics data such as genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics this method enhances the relevance and accuracy of the integrated analysis. Hierarchical integration methods are designed to align more closely with the multidimensional nature of biological systems, allowing for a more nuanced understanding of how these diverse data types interact and contribute to complex phenotypes. This approach not only improves the interpretability of the results but also facilitates the identification of key regulatory pathways and mechanisms that drive biological processes, ultimately advancing our knowledge in fields such as cancer research and personalized medicine. Here we detailed thee methods of hierarchical regularization adapted to Cox regression.

The feature that prioritizes variables from higher-priority blocks over those from lower-priority blocks provides a significant practical advantage. This approach not only enhances the interpretability of the resulting prediction rules but also simplifies their application in real-world scenarios. Priority Lasso Cox offers strong clinical interpretability by forcing clinically important variables into the model first, followed by natural multi-omics hierarchy integration (clinical → genomic → transcriptomic layers), transportability through stable core predictors across populations, and flexibility via cross-validation testing of multiple block orders [Klau 2018]. IPF-Lasso enables modality-balanced multi-omics integration by assigning data type-specific penalties preventing large omics blocks from overwhelming sparse clinical signals. This approach offers superior statistical power, Type I error control, and flexibility without predefined ordering contrary to priority lasso modelling.

Boulesteix et al. [38] applied this method on breast cancer multi-omics study with Clinical modality (n = 6 variables: age, nodal status, tumor size, grade, ER/PR status) integrated with high-dimensional gene expression (n = 22,283 Affymetrix-U133A microarray features) achieving balanced variable selection. The penalty factors (1,32) penalized more the molecular modality than the clinical modality confirming literature consensus that microarray gene expression adds limited predictive value beyond clinical factors for breast cancer survival, thus validating IPF-Lasso’s ability to prioritize clinically relevant predictors in multi-omics settings.

The fusedTree method

The The fusedTree method [40] incorporates hierarchical or phylogenetic tree structures into high-dimensional Cox regression to enforce sparsity patterns that respect known biological relationships among predictors, such as gene ontologies or evolutionary trees in omics data.

This approach penalizes coefficient differences between related variables (e.g., parent–child nodes in a tree), promoting similar effects within clades while allowing divergence across branches, thus improving selection consistency and interpretability over unstructured regularization.

Goedhart et al. [41] described this method with colorectal cancer dataset (n = 914) with 4 clinical covariables and p = 21,292 gene expression covariables associated with relapse-free survival. Limits and perspectives

Despite their predictive power, high-dimensional models face significant limitations in translational research. The "curse of dimensionality" creates expansive blind spots in feature space, where small sample sizes fail to represent the combinatorial complexity of omics data (p > 10,000), leading to overfitting and poor generalizability across patient populations or centers [42]. Even with regularization (elastic-net, XRNET), model instability persists due to multiple local minima in high-dimensional loss landscapes, yielding variable feature selection across CV folds that undermines biomarker reproducibility. Contrary to the prevailing assumption that integrating multiple omics data types inherently enhances survival prediction performance, simpler combinations suffice for optimal prognostic accuracy in cancer. Specifically, Li et al. [43] supported that omics models achieved comparable or superior time-dependent AUCs versus more complex multi-omics integrations, consistent with recent pan-cancer analyses showing performance declines with redundant data types. These results challenge the "more omics = better prediction" paradigm, highlighting that cost-effective single-omics approaches often outperform comprehensive but suboptimal multi-omics strategies, with implications for clinical trial design and resource allocation in precision oncology.

Predictive modeling of therapeutic efficacy using high-dimensional data has become central to precision oncology. Cox proportional hazards models incorporating Lasso, Elastic Net, or network-based penalties (SCAD, MCP, Group Lasso) are well-established for genomic, transcriptomic, and multi-omics integration, as comprehensively reviewed by Ternès et al. [44]. These approaches enable robust prognostic modeling even when p ≫ n, with therapeutic interaction terms enhancing treatment effect estimation.

The R package ‘Biospear [45]‘ provides a unified implementation of these penalized methods, facilitating reproducible evaluation of treatment effects through nested cross-validation and bootstrap optimism correction. This framework paves the way for integrative prognostic models combining multi-platform omics data (DNA, RNA, methylation, proteomics) with clinical covariates, ultimately accelerating biomarker discovery and therapeutic stratification in oncology.

However, significant heterogeneity persists in translational research practices regarding model performance evaluation. While discriminative performance (C-index, time-dependent AUC) receives extensive attention, calibration assessment remains inconsistent, often limited to visual inspection of calibration plots without formal testing (e.g., calibration slope, Hosmer–Lemeshow adaptation for survival) [46]. Overfitting prevention through proper nested CV [47] and bootstrap validation is inconsistently applied, compromising model comparability and clinical translation. Standardized frameworks integrating optimism-corrected metrics, calibration testing, and reproducibility standards are urgently needed for clinically meaningful prognostic models in precision oncology.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the exploration of various Cox regularization methods in high-dimensional settings for multi-omics integration reveals distinct advantages and challenges associated with each approach. Global methods, while requiring pre-filtering or large cohorts to compute effectively, offer a more comprehensive integration of multi-omics data. This thorough integration allows for the identification of complex interactions among different molecular layers, enhancing the robustness of survival predictions. However, the necessity for extensive datasets can limit their applicability in studies with smaller sample sizes. Clustered and grouped approaches provide flexibility in handling diverse data types but come with the inherent risk of overfitting. This can compromise the interpretability of results, making it challenging to derive clear biological insights from the integrated models. The balance between model complexity and interpretability remains a critical consideration in these methodologies. Hierarchical regression models respect the additive nature of information from different omics platforms, promoting interpretability. However, they may overlook significant interactions between these platforms, potentially leading to a loss of valuable insights regarding the interplay of molecular mechanisms in cancer progression. Overall, the choice of method should be guided by the specific research questions and the nature of the available data. Future studies should aim to refine these approaches further, potentially integrating strengths from multiple methodologies to enhance predictive accuracy and biological relevance in cancer survival analysis.
