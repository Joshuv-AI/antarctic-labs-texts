Temporal associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms in the acute post-sexual assault period: An ecological momentary assessment study☆
Authors: Ashby Boland, Lauren D. Reyes, Jenny Black, Karen Serrano, Samuel A. McLean, Nicole A. Short
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nightmares are common after sexual assault and may play an important role in the development of posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD). However, little research has examined daily temporal associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms in the acute aftermath of sexual assault. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) was used to test within- and between-person associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms during the six weeks following assault. Participants were 54 women sexual assault survivors presenting for emergency care (Mage=24.64, 51.9% White, 29.6% Hispanic/Latina), followed with daily EMA surveys. Dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM) was used to estimate lagged bidirectional associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms. Most participants had clinically significant PTSD symptoms according to traditional self-report (97.9% at week one; 85.4% at week seven) and frequent nightmares measured by EMA (42.8% of days). At the within-person level, nightmares significantly predicted next-survey PTSD symptoms (the full model accounted for 18.4% of the within-level variance in PTSD), and PTSD symptoms significantly predicted subsequent nightmares (the full model accounted for 11.7% of the within-level variance in nightmares). However, these effects were no longer significant after adjusting for autoregressive effects. At the between-person level, individuals with frequent nightmares had higher PTSD symptoms and vice versa, with models accounting for 43.2–43.9% of the between-level variance. While nightmares are common in early recovery, short-term fluctuations in nightmare symptoms may not meaningfully drive PTSD symptoms changes once stability is considered. Nightmares may function as an indicator of overall PTSD severity, underscoring their importance in identifying survivors at risk for more severe PTSD symptoms.

Rates of sexual assault are high, with up to two-thirds of women and one-third of men in the United States reporting lifetime experiences of unwanted sexual contact [2]. In addition to being more likely to experience sexual violence, women are twice as likely as men to develop posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD; [15]). Sleep disturbances, such as nightmares, are especially common in the aftermath of trauma, with about 50% endorsing trauma-related nightmares and 12–30% reporting nontraumatic nightmares in the two years following trauma exposure [34]. Nightmares are particularly prevalent following interpersonal violence, with survivors of interpersonal violence being nearly five times more likely to report trauma-related nightmares compared to those who experienced other trauma types [45]. Furthermore, after trauma exposure, women are more likely than men to experience trauma-related nightmares, highlighting their heightened vulnerability to sleep disturbances [39], nightmares specifically [32], and PTSD more broadly [15].

In addition to their associated distress and impairment, sleep disturbances, including both insomnia and nightmares, may present as important comorbid diagnoses among those with PTSD. Specifically, although evidence-based PTSD treatments reduce overall symptom severity [14], [47], sleep disturbances frequently persist even after otherwise successful treatment [29], [3], [59]. Indeed, sleep disturbances often develop their own unique maintaining factors not typically addressed through PTSD treatment [22]. Nightmares, in particular, may be considered a hallmark symptom of PTSD, and are associated with more severe presentations of PTSD, including increased daytime distress, sleep disturbances, and suicidality among those with PTSD [31], [46], [50], [7]. Although it is clear that nightmares are a common and clinically relevant feature of PTSD, there is very little research focused on understanding how nightmares unfold during the early post-trauma period and potentially impact the development of PTSD symptoms.

Growing evidence suggests that sleep disturbances are not merely symptoms of PTSD but may actively contribute to its onset and maintenance [23], [42], [45]. Sleep, and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep in particular, plays a critical role in memory consolidation and emotional recovery following trauma, and disruptions to these processes may interfere with adaptation in the weeks that follow. Experimental and translational work shows that sleeping after exposure to trauma-related material reduces the frequency and distress of intrusive symptoms [19], [27], whereas disruptions to REM and non-REM sleep may heighten arousal and impair fear extinction [20], [25]. These findings align with the sleep abnormalities observed in PTSD [17], suggesting disruptions in sleep due to nightmares, may interrupt the emotional integration that typically occurs during sleep [18], though it is worth noting that posttraumatic nightmares, unlike idiopathic nightmares, have been shown to occur during both REM and non-REM sleep, with some suggesting equal frequency [43]. Despite these nuances in the underlying mechanisms of trauma-related nightmares, research has found that nightmares are associated with increased PTSD symptoms both cross-sectionally [54] and prospectively [44]. However, this research has often been conducted among those with chronic PTSD symptoms, or months to years after the trauma took place. Studying associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms in the early post-trauma period is critical in understanding whether nightmares play a role in the development (vs. maintenance) of PTSD symptoms.

Traditional retrospective assessments may not be well-suited to understanding temporal associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms. Ecological momentary assessment (EMA) may better address this research question by tracking nightmare and PTSD experiences in real time, allowing for examination of whether nightmares temporally precede increases in PTSD symptoms during early recovery. Although prior EMA studies have found that poor sleep quality is associated with increased next-day PTSD symptoms [51], [52], clarifying the temporality of this relationship is essential for identifying whether nightmares play a causal role in the development of PTSD symptoms, such as by disrupting the emotional processing and memory integration that occur during sleep, or simply reflect a correlate of ongoing PTSD.

Traditional analytic methods are often limited in their ability to account for both the nested structure of repeated assessments and the temporal dependencies between symptoms found in EMA data. Fortunately, relatively new analytic techniques have been developed to address these challenges, such as dynamic structural equation modeling (DSEM), an analytic framework that allows for modeling how variables influence each other over time while accounting for individual differences in baseline symptom levels and symptom stability across assessments [36]. This approach is particularly well-suited for testing associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms, as it can simultaneously estimate within-person lagged associations (e.g., whether nightmares predict subsequent changes in PTSD symptoms and vice versa) to address temporality of associations, as well as between-person associations (e.g., whether individuals with higher average PTSD symptoms tend to experience more frequent nightmares). Furthermore, DSEM can account for the fact that nightmares can only be measured once per day and that EMAs may be unequally spaced by using a Kalman filter [37]. Using DSEM therefore may provide a more precise test of the temporal and reciprocal relationships between nightmares and PTSD symptoms during the early post-trauma period.

Overall, substantial evidence indicates that sleep disturbances may contribute to the development and maintenance of PTSD [17], [44], [57], [6], but few studies have examined these processes during the acute post-sexual assault period among women sexual assault survivors. This period of time is important given nightmares and PTSD symptoms are common and recovering for some while becoming chronic for others. Furthermore, no studies to our knowledge have examined general associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms using EMA. Therefore, the current study tested associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms using EMA in the six weeks after sexual assault among women presenting for emergency care post-assault in the context of a randomized clinical trial testing a brief anxiety sensitivity (fear of anxious arousal; [56]) intervention. We refer to "PTSD symptoms" throughout for simplicity; however, symptoms assessed in weeks one through four post-assault are more accurately characterized as acute stress symptoms, while those assessed in weeks five and six fall within the window in which PTSD criteria may be met. Consistent with previous research, we hypothesized that: 1) PTSD symptoms and nightmares would be common but decrease over time, consistent with natural recovery patterns [49]; 2) at the within-person level, experiencing a nightmare will be associated with increased next-day PTSD symptoms relative to their typical levels of PTSD symptoms and vice versa [44], [54]. We also hypothesized these associations would persist when accounting for lagged effects of the outcome variable (e.g., prior PTSD ratings predicting subsequent PTSD symptoms). Finally, we hypothesized that 3) at the between-person level, participants with higher average PTSD symptom severity would experience more frequent nightmares across the study period [44], [54].

Method

Study design

Women over the age of 18 seeking emergency care following a sexual assault were recruited from two sites in the Better Tomorrow Network, a national network of emergency care centers for sexual assault survivors [53]. Data came from a pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) testing a smartphone-based program intended to reduce PTSD symptoms by addressing anxiety sensitivity (see Short et al., under review; NCT05305235). Participants in the intervention arm completed a smartphone-based, one-hour cognitive behavioral program targeting anxiety sensitivity over four weeks, supplemented with text message reminders. Participants in the control condition used a mobile application that provided instructions in deep breathing and relaxation techniques, completed over weeks 1–4 on their own time. Participants from both arms of the parent trial were included in the current analyses. The target sample size was selected to provide sufficient power for the parent pilot RCT. While the parent trial was pre-registered (NCT05305235), the analyses presented here were not.

Eligible participants were adults (18 years or older) who identified as female, presented to an emergency care site within 72 h of a sexual assault, and were able to speak, read, and write in English. Participants also needed to have a smartphone with at least one year of continuous service and report elevated anxiety sensitivity, indicated by a score of 17 or higher on the Anxiety Sensitivity Index−3 [56]. Participants were excluded if they were unable to provide informed consent, were incarcerated, pregnant, living with their assailant and intending to continue doing so, receiving inpatient care, lacked a mailing address, had previously enrolled in the study, or had not undergone a sexual assault nurse examiner (SANE) examination. Of 695 individuals screened for eligibility, 75 met inclusion criteria and enrolled. Ten were subsequently excluded from analyses for discontinuing EMAs, completing only one EMA after the first week, initiating EMAs more than two weeks late, or providing insufficient nightmare data (fewer than five EMAs), yielding a final analytic sample of 54 participants.

Procedure

Potential participants were referred by SANEs and connected with a research assistant via video call. Survivors were screened for eligibility and informed about study procedures. Those who agreed to participate provided consent and completed a brief baseline assessment. One week after enrollment, they received a follow-up survey. Participants who completed the one-week survey were randomized to either an experimental treatment condition targeting anxiety sensitivity or a relaxation-based control condition. Participants were asked to complete three EMAs per day from Day 1 through Day 42, though they received four daily prompts to account for any missed surveys, resulting in a target of 126 EMAs and a maximum of 168. A larger survey battery was administered at week 7, following the completion of the EMA period. EMAs were delivered via text message at random times within four daily time blocks (morning, early afternoon, late afternoon, and evening). All measures assessing sleep-related experiences (e.g., nightmares, sleep quality) were administered in the morning survey. Prompts were delivered regardless of prior survey completion. Compensation included $40 for the initial assessment, $1 per EMA, and $50 each for the one-week and seven-week follow-ups. All participants provided informed consent, and study procedures were approved by the relevant Institutional Review Board.

Measures

Traditional self-report

Anxiety Sensitivity Index−3 (ASI−3). The ASI−3 is an 18-item self-report assessment measuring anxiety sensitivity, defined as fear of arousal-related sensations [56]. Participants rated the extent to which they agree with each item (e.g., It scares me when my heart beats rapidly) and responded on a 4-point Likert-type scale, with higher scores indicating higher levels of anxiety sensitivity. Participants had to score > 17 on the ASI−3 to be considered to have moderate to high anxiety sensitivity and be eligible for the parent study. Internal consistency was excellent in the current study at week one (Cronbach α=.92) and week seven (Cronbach α=.92).

PTSD Checklist for DSM−5 (PCL−5). The PCL−5 is a 20-item self-report measure assessing the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorder 5 (DSM−5) symptom criteria for PTSD [11], [58]. This was administered at one and seven weeks to contextualize PTSD symptoms in the past week and past month, respectively, in the sample. Participants were instructed to indicate their level of distress in relation to their recent sexual assault for each item using a 5-point Likert-type scale ranging from 0 (Not at all) to 4 (Extremely). The PCL−5 has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including the ability to screen for probable PTSD with a cut-off score of 33 [58]. Internal consistency was good in the current sample at one week (α=.88) and excellent at week seven (α=.93).

Medical/forensic record data

Sexual Assault Characteristics. Information about assault characteristics was drawn from patients’ SANE medical/forensic records, which were obtained from sites with participant consent. Medical records were coded by trained undergraduate and post-baccalaureate research assistants, under supervision of the senior author. The training included a review of the protocol, practice coding three records, and then meeting with the group to review coding and resolve any disagreements. Each medical record was double-coded, and disagreements on coding were resolved through consensus discussion between coders and with input from the senior author, as necessary. Percent agreement between coders ranged from 62.8% to 96.8% across coded items, including perpetrator relationship to survivor (62.8%), loss of consciousness (82.5%), assault type (87.8%), and strangulation (96.8%) with a mean percent agreement of 82.4% (SD = 14.4%) across key variables.

EMA

EMA Version of the PCL−5. As used in [52], an abbreviated 10-item version of the PCL−5 using updated DSM−5 symptom verbiage was utilized in the current study (see Supplemental Materials). The scale consists of items that most strongly load onto the four DSM−5 symptom clusters, and also correspond to factors identified through factor analyses of the PCL−5, including anxious arousal and dysphoric arousal, but does not include items assessing externalizing behaviors [5]. Participants were instructed to indicate their level of distress related to their recent sexual assault for each item on the same 5-point Likert-type as the PCL−5 in the past two hours. Items were summed to create a total symptom severity score ranging from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater PTSD severity. The EMA version of the PCL−5 has shown good psychometric properties [9], and showed good internal consistency in the current sample at one week (α=.85) and week seven (α=.91). It is important to note that the nightmare item of the PCL−5 (item 2) is not included in the EMA version of the PCL−5.

Nightmares. Nightmares were assessed via self-report each morning upon waking. Participants responded to a single item indicating whether they had experienced a nightmare related to the assault the previous night (“Last night, did you have memories or nightmares that reminded you of your assault?), with responses coded as 0 (No) or 1 (Yes). This item was derived from the Pittsburg Sleep Quality Index and Addendum for PTSD (PSQI-A; Germain 2005), a measure that has demonstrated strong psychometric properties including good internal consistency (Cronbach's α =.85).

Data analytic plan

Two-level DSEM (assessments nested within participants) was used to account for the nested data structure of EMA data [1], [21]. Parameters were estimated with the Bayesian estimator with 40,000 iterations per chain, with 2 parallel chains. Per MPlus default settings, all models converged with Potential Scale Reduction (PSR)< 1.05 across all parameters. At Level 1, within-person variance is modeled to account for intraindividual or state associations, while at Level 2, the between-person variance is modeled to account for inter-individual or trait associations. Models were estimated in MPlus Version 8.8 [40] using the Bayesian estimator [35]. Variables (except for time) were latent mean-centered to decompose between- and within-person effects. The impact of time and condition on key variables were tested to determine if time and condition should be included in the models. In all models, a Kalman filter of 12 h was used to account for unequal time intervals between assessments, and for the fact that sleep was only assessed once per day [37]. A lag of −1 was selected for both theoretical and design-based reasons. Concurrent associations were not examined because morning assessments of PTSD symptoms may be biased by how participants slept the previous night, making it difficult to disentangle PTSD symptom levels from the effects of nightmare occurrence at the same time point. Given that an average of 7–8 h elapsed between successive completed EMAs, a lag of −1 captures associations within approximately the same day, consistent with our conceptual interest in same-day associations between nightmares and subsequent PTSD symptoms. Because nightmares were assessed as a binary variable (i.e., did or did not occur), nightmares were modeled as a categorical variable. Missing data were handled by the Bayesian estimator with Gibbs sampling in MPlus, with data imputation conducted as part of the Markov Chain Monte Carlo process [1].

In the first set of models, the within-level impact of time on the outcome (either nightmares or PTSD), was included, along with a within-level lagged effect of the predictor (e.g., nightmares) on the outcome (e.g., PTSD symptoms). The lagged effect was included to ensure a test of prospective associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms and vice versa. A between-level association between the predictor and outcome was also included. Finally, means and variances for all variables were included at the between-level. The second set of models was identical, but autoregressive effects were added. Specifically, the effect of the participant’s previous nightmare or PTSD rating on the subsequent nightmare or PTSD rating was added by inclusion of lagged effects (t–1).

Unstandardized regression coefficients are reported, and estimates were considered to be statistically significant if the 95% Bayesian Credible Interval (BCI) does not cross through zero [36]. Effect sizes were evaluated by assessing percent variance accounted for in the entire model at between- and within-levels [33], [8]. For Aim 2, heterogeneous variable (or pain variability) models were used by modeling residual variance of pain outcomes as a random effect at the within-persons level [37].

Results

The final analytic sample included 54 participants, yielding a total of 3664 EMA observations (range=12–161 observations per participant; M=67.85, SD=47.15, M dn =53.5). Across the 6-week EMA period, participants completed an average of 53.5% of surveys. On average, 8.0 h elapsed between successive EMAs (M=12.9, SD=22.5). Survey completion rates were comparable across time points, with highest adherence in the morning (28.9%), followed by the early afternoon (25.8%), evening (24.7%), and late afternoon (20.6%). Compliance rates were comparable across study conditions (p = .422) but declined over time. By the final two weeks, participants completed roughly one fewer EMA per week compared to the initial two weeks of data collection.

Participants were, on average, 25.7 years old (see Table 1). Just over half identified as White (51.9%), and the majority identified as non-Latina/Hispanic (57.4%). Most participants (72.2%) had completed at least some college, reported a family income below $60,000 per year (59.3%), and were employed outside the home (59.3%). Assault characteristics

Regarding sexual assault characteristics, 21/27 participants (77.8%) reported experiencing at least some loss of consciousness during the assault. Most participants (27/37, 73.0%) reported penile-vaginal penetration. When describing their assailant, participants identified them as a friend or acquaintance (11/35, 31.4%), former partner (2/35, 5.7%), stranger (9/35, 25.7%), or someone from a planned first encounter (e.g., a first date; 9/35, 25.7%). Approximately one quarter (12/49, 24.5%) reported experiencing strangulation. These data were extracted from medical records, and differences in sample sizes reflect variability in documentation and missing information.

Nightmares and PTSD over time

Trajectories of nightmares and PTSD symptoms over time and by participant were visually examined (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4) and statistically tested. There was not a significant effect of time on nightmares (B <.001, 95% BCI [-.001, <.001]). Nightmares were common over the six weeks of EMA, occurring on 42.8% of assessment days, with participants experiencing an average of 8.43 nights with nightmares (range=0–38, SD=8.23). On the other hand, PTSD symptoms significantly decreased over the six weeks of EMA (B = −.01, 95% BCI [-.02, −.01]). Discussion

The current EMA study examined daily experiences of nightmares and PTSD symptoms, and their associations, in 54 women presenting for emergency care in the six weeks following sexual assault. During these six weeks post-sexual assault, PTSD symptoms were elevated and nightmares occurred frequently in the EMA data, with participants reporting a nightmare on 42.8% percent of nights on average. These data underscore the substantial distress characterizing the early post-assault period [12], [26], [38]. In traditional self-report assessments, nearly all participants scored at or above the PCL−5 cutoff for probable PTSD (≥33; [58]) one week after the assault (97.9%), and a large majority remained above this threshold six weeks later (85.4%). Nightmares and PTSD symptoms showed bidirectional prospective associations at the within-level, but these effects were no longer significant once autoregressive effects were accounted for, meaning that nightmares did not predict next day PTSD symptoms beyond the level of PTSD severity from the previous assessment. Notably, PTSD symptoms demonstrated substantial day-to-day stability across the EMA period, suggesting that once elevated in the acute post-assault period, symptoms tend to persist from assessment to assessment rather than fluctuate meaningfully in response to specific triggers such as nightmares. In contrast, robust between-person effects between nightmares and PTSD symptoms were observed, indicating that individuals with a higher average propensity for nightmares consistently exhibited higher overall PTSD severity. These findings are consistent with traditional self-report studies, which have consistently shown that nightmares are linked to higher PTSD severity and poorer clinical outcomes [32], [39], [46], [50]. However, by capturing daily symptoms via EMA, results provide a potentially more accurate estimate of the frequency of nightmares in the aftermath of sexual assault, and suggest that nightmares appeared to temporally precede escalations in PTSD symptoms at the within-person level (and vice versa), but not after accounting for PTSD symptom carryover.

Initial unadjusted models suggested that experiencing a nightmare overnight was associated with higher PTSD symptoms at the next daytime assessment; however, these effects did not hold once autoregressive effects were accounted for. It should be noted this is a fairly rigorous analysis accounting for both temporality and carryover effects of PTSD symptoms, which has often not been conducted in other EMA research finding associations between other sleep disturbances and PTSD symptoms (e.g., [52]), but has been done in work regarding suicide and non-suicidal self-injury [28], [48]. Therefore, it is possible that associations between sleep disturbances and PTSD may not hold to this rigorous level of testing or that a larger sample size would be needed to detect these relatively smaller effects. On the other hand, it is also possible that nightmares do not necessarily predict increased PTSD symptoms, but instead reflect a stable, high level of PTSD symptom severity. Indeed, a similar pattern was observed by Slavish and colleagues, who found that nightmares were not associated with subsequent increases in PTSD symptoms across three annual assessment waves in World Trade Center responders (2023). Further, results align with work suggesting that early PTSD symptoms are driven more strongly by stable individual differences than by short-term fluctuations in specific symptoms [24]. Overall, pending replication, the daily impact of nightmares on PTSD symptoms may be less impactful than previously assumed once PTSD symptom stability is accounted for.

Initial unadjusted models shows that elevated PTSD symptoms were associated with increased likelihood of nightmare occurrence at the next assessment; however, this effect similarly did not hold once autoregressive effects were accounted for, indicating that short term fluctuations in PTSD symptoms may not meaningfully influence nightmare occurrences. As previously discussed, it is possible we did not have the power to detect these associations when conducting stringent analyses accounting for carryover of nightmares. It is also possible that spikes in PTSD symptoms may increase the distress associated with nightmares (which we did not assess) rather than the likelihood that a nightmare occurs. Additionally, symptoms in the acute period following assault may be universally elevated, leaving little day-to-day variability to detect. Therefore, future research may consider more comprehensive assessments of nightmares, including a nightmare distress rating, and replication of our rigorous analyses in clinical PTSD samples or later posttrauma timepoints to help clarify whether these null findings are specific to the acute period where symptoms may not be as universally elevated, and have less variability.

In terms of between-level findings, consistent with hypothesis, individuals with higher average PTSD symptoms reported more frequent nightmares across all analyses. This effect is consistent with traditional longitudinal studies demonstrating that nightmare frequency and PTSD severity covary at the between-person level across repeated assessments [55]. The strong between-person associations observed here support a chronicity model in which frequent nightmares serve as an indicator of heightened overall PTSD severity rather than a momentary trigger for next-day symptom escalation [13]. It should also be noted that nightmares are known to cause broader sleep disturbances [16], [7] and without a sleep quality covariate we cannot rule out that the between person associations observed partially reflects sleep disruption rather than nightmares specifically. Overall, between-level findings emphasize the importance of assessing nightmares early in the aftermath of trauma, not because they signal next-day symptom worsening, but because they may help identify survivors generally at elevated risk for more persistent or severe PTSD symptoms.

These findings also have clinical implications, and suggest it may be helpful to assess nightmares early after trauma exposure to help determine risk for PTSD. Identifying individuals who report nightmares in the acute post-assault period may help clinicians flag those who could benefit from earlier contact or prevention strategies aimed at reducing long-term symptom burden. For example, although there is no standard preventative intervention for PTSD after sexual assault, meta-analytic evidence does suggest cognitive behavioral strategies can reduce PTSD symptoms after sexual assault [53]. Further, several evidence-based treatments specifically target trauma-related nightmares, including Imagery Rehearsal Therapy and Exposure, Relaxation, and Rescripting Therapy [10], [30], yet none are designed for the acute post-trauma period. Overall, incorporating follow-up, monitoring or targeted early support for survivors with prominent nightmares may strengthen our ability to reduce chronic PTSD symptoms before they fully emerge.

Results must be considered in light of limitations. First, the current sample included women sexual assault survivors, which may limit generalizability to other genders and trauma types. Although previous work demonstrates women have higher risk of experiencing trauma-related nightmares [39], future work should examine trauma survivors of other genders and trauma types, to understand if results are generalizable. Second, nightmare occurrence was assessed using a single-item with binary response options, and it is possible the limited range may have affected results. Future research may employ self-report measures that assess additional facets of nightmares, such as distress and nightmare content, with Likert-type response options to better capture and their nuances, including measures like the Trauma-Related Nightmare Survey, or the Nightmare Distress Questionnaire. Third, the percent agreement for some medical record coding variables was lower than ideal. As these variables were used only to characterize the sample, this is unlikely to have affected primary findings; however, future studies should ensure more rigorous coding reliability. Fourth, the present study may not have had sufficient statistical power to detect smaller within-person associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms while accounting for autoregressive effects. This was partly due to sleep necessarily only being assessed once per day, so if morning assessments were missed, there was no alternative survey to draw from. This reduced the number of usable observations and limited our ability to model day-to-day fluctuations with greater precision. Future research should include larger samples to improve power for detecting subtle effects. Fifth, additional sleep-related factors, including insomnia symptoms, sleep quality, and sleep efficiency can affect the development and maintenance of both PTSD and nightmares [16], [41]. Sixth, pre-trauma nightmare frequency was not assessed, limiting our ability to determine whether post-trauma nightmares reflect a pre-existing vulnerability. Finally, because our study did not include any clinical interviews to determine diagnostic status, we relied on self-reported symptoms of PTSD which may be subject to response bias [4]. Our focus was specifically on nightmares because they have been largely overlooked in previous EMA research and are considered a hallmark symptom of PTSD closely tied to disturbances in REM sleep, which is necessary for emotional processing of emotional memories [18]. Future studies should incorporate broader sleep assessments to clarify how these related sleep processes interact with nightmares and contribute to PTSD symptom development. Finally, the current sample was restricted to individuals with elevated anxiety sensitivity (ASI−3 ≥ 17), which may skew findings toward greater overall pathology and limit generalizability to those with milder symptom severity. Future studies should examine these associations in samples with a broader range of anxiety sensitivity.

Our results build upon prior research demonstrating that nightmares may be a central feature of PTSD development and maintenance by testing associations between nightmares and PTSD symptoms using EMA in the immediate aftermath of sexual assault. The findings support a model of chronicity, suggesting that stable, trait-like PTSD severity explains the association between nightmares and PTSD more strongly than short-term day-to-day fluctuations. This underscores the importance of assessing nightmares early after trauma as indicators of heightened risk for more persistent or severe PTSD symptom courses. Future work is needed to test early interventions that target PTSD symptoms, sleep disturbance, or both to determine whether addressing these processes in the acute phase can disrupt maladaptive symptom trajectories.
