De novo biosynthesis of Astragalus bioactive isoflavonoid calycosin-7-glucoside in yeast
Authors: Cong Chen, Tengfei Liu, Ying Huang, Jiazhang Lian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIsoflavonoids, such as calycosin-7-glucoside, possess significant pharmaceutical value but are typically sourced from plants, where production is limited by slow growth and complex isolation process. Here, we engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae for heterologous production of calycosin-7-glucoside. First, via the introduction of isoflavone 4′-O-methyltransferase from Pueraria montana var. lobata (PlOMT9), isoflavone-3′-hydroxylase from Astragalus membranaceus (AmI3′H), and calycosin 7'-O-glucosyltransferase from A. membranaceus (AmUCGT) into our previously constructed daidzein-producing strain, we achieved de novo biosynthesis of calycosin-7-glucoside for the first time. Then, we disrupted the endogenous glucoside hydrolase (EXG1 knockout), enhanced UDP-glucose supply (UGP1 overexpression), and screened a glycosyltransferase (AmUGT88E29 from A. membranaceus) with higher catalytic activity to substantially improve the production of our target compound. Using LC-MS-based metabolomics, we analyzed pathway intermediates and metabolic flux distribution, revealing PlOMT9 as the major bottleneck. Subsequent optimization of PlOMT9 gene copy number further enhanced bioproduction of calycosin-7-glucoside, achieving a final titer of 0.22 ​mg/L. This study establishes a yeast-based platform for high-value isoflavonoid biosynthesis and provides a foundation for future pathway optimization.Keywords: Calycosin-7-glucoside, Isoflavonoids, Plant natural products, Yeast, Synthetic biology

Introduction

Plant-derived natural products are a great treasure and have been extensively investigated for their therapeutic potential. Many achieved Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for clinical applications, such as anticancer drug taxol (from Taxus species) and vinblastine (from Catharanthus roseus) [1,2], as well as anti-inflammatory drug colchicine (from Colchicum autumnale) [3]. Among medicinal plants, roots of Astragalus membranaceus, also called as Huangqi, serve as a well-known traditional medicine used globally in medicinal, dietary supplements, and agricultural sectors [4]. Huangqi extract contains three major bioactive compounds: triterpene saponins, polysaccharides, and isoflavonoids [5].

Isoflavonoids, particularly formononetin (2), calycosin (3), and calycosin-7-glucoside (4), have attracted significant attention for their cardioprotective effects, which are mediated by antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antiapoptotic mechanisms [5,6]. The growing demand for these compounds has driven efforts to enhance their production. While hairy root cultures of A. membranaceus yield 234.77 ​μg/g dry weight of key isoflavonoids (including formononetin (2), calycosin (3), and calycosin-7-glucoside (4) [5,7], UV irradiation can increase this titer to 533.54 ​μg/g dry weight [8]. However, the slow growth cycles of plant systems remain a major bottleneck for scalable production.

Microbial biosynthesis offers a promising alternative. Engineered Saccharomyces cerevisiae has achieved 867.67 ​mg/L liquiritigenin in a 5-L bioreactor and 85.4 ​mg/L daidzein (1) in shake flasks within one week [9,10]. Despite these advances, calycosin-7-glucoside (4) production in yeast has not been reported yet. Fortunately, its biosynthetic pathway has been fully elucidated (Fig. 1), which begins with daidzein (1) and is sequentially modified by isoflavone 4′-O-methyltransferase (OMT) to yield formononetin (2) [11], isoflavone-3′-hydroxylase (I3′H) to produce calycosin (3) [12], and calycosin 7'-O-glucosyltransferase (UGT) to generate calycosin-7-glucoside (4) [13,14].

Here, we report the complete de novo biosynthesis of calycosin-7-glucoside (4) in engineered yeast. Starting with a daidzein (1)-producing platform strain (GYN3) [15], we sequentially integrated pathway genes to reconstitute biosynthesis. A multi-strategy approach, including disruption of endogenous glucoside hydrolases, enhancement of UDP-glucose supply, and screening of high-activity glycosyltransferases, proved effective in increasing production yields. Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS)-based metabolomics enabled systematic identification of pathway intermediates and revealed additional metabolic bottlenecks. Subsequent gene copy number optimization of the rate-limiting enzyme further improved product titers. Our work promises a scalable microbial platform for isoflavonoid biomanufacturing.

Materials and methods

Strains, media, and reagents

Escherichia coli Trelief 5α chemically competent cells were used for the construction and amplification of recombinant plasmids. S. cerevisiae chassis strains used in this study are listed in Table 1. Luria–Bertani (LB) medium, containing of 10 ​g/L tryptone, 5 ​g/L yeast extract, and 10 ​g/L NaCl, supplemented with 100 ​mg/L ampicillin, was used for E. coli cultivation. LB solid medium supplemented with 20 ​g/L agar was used for selection of E. coli transformants. Yeast cultures were grown in YPD medium (20 ​g/L peptone A, 10 ​g/L yeast extract, and 20 ​g/L glucose) and shifted to YPG medium (with galactose replacing glucose) for inducing heterologous gene expression. Synthetic complete medium without histidine or uracil (SED-HIS or SED-URA), including 1.7 ​g/L yeast nitrogen base without amino acids and ammonium sulfate, 0.6 ​g/L complete supplement mixture without histidine or uracil, 1.11 ​g/L monosodium glutamate monohydrate, 20 ​g/L glucose, and 20 ​g/L agar, was used for selection of engineered yeast transformants. When necessary, 200 ​mg/L G418 sulfate was supplemented.

Plasmid construction

The calycosin-7-glucoside (4) biosynthetic pathway gene (AmUCGT) and UGP1 were amplified from A. membranaceus, and S. cerevisiae cDNA, respectively, followed by purification using the GeneJET Gel Extraction Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). These fragments were then cloned into linearized pESC-LEU or pESC-HIS vectors via Gibson assembly (TOROIVD One Step Fusion Cloning Mix). The sgRNA expression vectors used for genomic integration of heterologous gene expression cassettes were previously constructed in our laboratory [16]. For gene knockout experiments, sgRNA expression plasmids targeting glycoside hydrolases were constructed by first phosphorylating sgRNA primers with T4 Polynucleotide Kinase (New England Biolabs), followed by ligation into linearized sgRNA expression vectors using T4 DNA Ligase (New England Biolabs). All plasmids were purified using UE Plasmid Miniprep Kit (US Everbright, Shanghai, China) and verified by DNA sequencing (Tsingke Biotech). Codon-optimized genes were chemically synthesized by GenScript (Nanjing, China) (Supplementary Table S2). Complete gene, plasmid, and integration sites information is available in Supplementary Tables S3, S4, and S5.

Yeast strain construction and cultivation

The gene expression cassettes were PCR-amplified and purified. Approximately 400 ​ng of purified PCR product was co-transformed with 600 ​ng of sgRNA expression plasmid into Cas9-expressing yeast strains using the PEG/ssDNA/LiAc method [17]. Transformants were selected as single colonies and verified by diagnostic PCR. For shake-flask fermentation, verified strains were initially cultured in 1 ​mL of YPD medium in 24-well plates at 30 ​°C with 200 ​rpm shaking for 24 ​h. Cells were then harvested by centrifugation, washed with sterile water, and resuspended in 1 ​mL of YPG medium to induce heterologous gene expression. The cultures were incubated for an additional 24 ​h under the same conditions (30 ​°C, 200 ​rpm) to facilitate product biosynthesis.

Metabolite extraction and analysis

For metabolite extraction, 800 ​μL of the culture was homogenized with 0.1 ​mm glass beads in a 2 ​mL centrifuge tube using a high-throughput tissue grinder (SCIENTZ-48) at 60 ​Hz for 4 ​min, followed by metabolite extraction with 600 ​μL ethyl acetate at 40 ​Hz for 2 ​min. After phase separation at room temperature, the organic layer was collected by centrifugation (12,000 ​rpm, 5 ​min). A 300 ​μL aliquot of the supernatant was dried under a nitrogen stream, reconstituted in the same volume of methanol, and filtered through a 0.22 ​μm membrane for LC-MS analysis using a Shimadzu liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) 8045 system equipped with an Accucore™ C18 column (2.6 ​μm, 50 ​× ​2.1 ​mm). Chromatographic separation employed a gradient of 0.1 ​% formic acid in water (A) and methanol (B) at 0.3 ​mL/min (20–90 ​% B over 6 ​min, held for 2 ​min, then re-equilibrated), with the column maintained at 30 ​°C. Mass spectrometry was performed in positive-ion multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode with desolvation line and heating block temperatures of 250 ​°C and 400 ​°C, respectively, using 3 ​L/min nebulizing gas flow and 230 ​kPa collision-induced dissociation gas pressure, with specific MRM transitions and collision energies detailed in Supplementary Table S6. Data were acquired and processed using LabSolutions software (v5.91), with all experiments conducted in biological triplicates.

Identification of pathway intermediates

The potential pathway metabolites were first predicted based on enzymatic functions. Selected ion monitoring was used to look for the predicted metabolites based on their exact mass. Structural confirmation was achieved through product ion scanning at multiple collision energies (15, 25, and 35 ​V) to generate diagnostic fragment patterns. Finally, these fragmentation profiles were used to establish MRM transitions for sensitive and specific quantification of the identified metabolites.

Results

De novo biosynthesis of calycosin-7-glucoside in yeast

As daidzein (1) serves as a key precursor in the biosynthesis of calycosin-7-glucoside (4), we chose GYN3, a previously engineered daidzein-high-producing strain [15], as the chassis strain. To construct the biosynthetic pathway (Fig. 2A, Table 1), we first integrated the PlOMT9 (isoflavone 4′-O-methyltransferase from P. lobata) expression cassette into GYN3, yielding strain CAG1. Fermentation analysis revealed the production of formononetin (2) in CAG1. Next, we introduced a second expression cassette encoding A. membranaceus isoflavone-3′-hydroxylase (AmI3′H) into CAG1, generating CAG2, which synthesized calycosin (3) within 24 ​h of galactose induction. Finally, we integrated AmUCGT (calycosin 7'-O-glucosyltransferase from A. membranaceus) into CAG2 to construct CAG3. Although calycosin-7-glucoside (4) was detectable in CAG3 fermentation extracts via MRM analysis, its concentration remained notably low, suggesting glycosylation as the major rate-limiting step in the pathway.

We further analyzed the metabolic intermediates in strains GYN3, CAG1, CAG2, and CAG3 (Fig. 2B). Notably, formononetin (2) was nearly depleted in CAG2 and CAG3, while calycosin (3) accumulated substantially in CAG3 and daidzein (1) was not fully converted in all engineered strains. These findings indicate that AmI3′H exhibits high enzymatic activity, AmUCGT suffers from poor catalytic activity, whereas PlOMT9 may also serve as another pathway bottleneck.

Addressing the glycosylation bottleneck in calycosin-7-glucoside biosynthesis

To overcome the glycosylation bottleneck, we first investigated whether endogenous S. cerevisiae glucoside hydrolases could hydrolyze calycosin-7-glucoside (4). We individually knocked out glucoside hydrolase encoding genes (EGH1, EXG1, SCW2, SCW4, SIM1, or SPR1) in CAG3 [[18], [19], [20]], generating strains CAG4 to CAG9. While the disruption of glucoside hydrolase showed marginal effect on cellular fitness (Supplementary Fig. S1), CAG5 (ΔEXG1) exhibited the best improvement in the titer of calycosin-7-glucoside (4), with ΔEGH1 also showing a good effect (Fig. 3A). However, the titer of calycosin-7-glucoside (4) remained suboptimal, suggesting additional bottlenecks.

Addressing the glycosylation bottleneck with multi-strategies. (A) Knocking out glucoside hydrolase encoding genes and overexpressing UGP1 increase the titer of calycosin-7-glucoside (4). (B) AmUGT88E29 dramatically consumes 3 and increases the titer of 4 compared with AmUCGT. Data are presented as mean ​± ​s.d. (n ​= ​3 biological replicates).

We next evaluated whether UDP-glucose availability constrained glycosylation. Overexpression of UGP1 (UDP-glucose pyrophosphorylase) has been reported to enhance UDP-glucose supply [20]. While CAG10 (UGP1 overexpression in CAG3) showed only marginal improvement, the combination of UGP1 overexpression with EXG1 deletion in CAG11 increased the titer of calycosin-7-glucoside (4) by 10-fold when compared with CAG3 (Fig. 3A). Nevertheless, these modifications were still unsatisfactory to resolve the bottleneck, indicating that neither hydrolase activity nor UDP-glucose supply was the primary limitation.

We then hypothesized that the low catalytic efficiency of AmUCGT might restrict production. Previous studies demonstrated that AmUGT88E29 (a calycosin 7'-O-glucosyltransferase from A. membranaceus) significantly enhances the accumulation of calycosin-7-glucoside (4) in A. membranaceus hairy roots [14]. Upon integrating AmUGT88E29 into CAG2 to generate strain CAG12, we observed a clear decrease of calycosin (3) and a dramatic increase of calycosin-7-glucoside (4) after galactose induction (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. S2). The 4/3 ratio rose from 0.0026 (CAG3) to 2.1838 (CAG12), clearly implicating AmUCGT's low activity as the critical bottleneck.

Intermediates identification and pathway metabolic flux analysis

After resolving the glycosylation bottleneck, we proceeded to characterize the metabolic flux distribution by analyzing pathway metabolites. To achieve this, we engineered four distinct strains (CAG13 to CAG16) through systematic integration of enzyme combinations (AmI3′H, AmUGT88E29, and their pairings with PlOMT9) into the parental GYN3 strain (Fig. 4A). These genetic modifications were designed to produce possible metabolites: 3′,4′,7-trihydroxyisoflavone (5), daidzin (6), ononin (7), and glycosylated 3′,4′,7-trihydroxyisoflavone (8).

(B) LC-MS profiles of the metabolites 5 to 8 in yeast expressing the indicated enzymes and the parental GYN3 strain. (C) LC-MS peak area of the 5 to 8 in strains CAG3 and CAG12. Data are presented as mean ​± ​s.d. (n ​= ​3 biological replicates).

We performed metabolite identification using a comprehensive analytical approach, initial screening via selected ion monitoring, followed by structural confirmation through product ion fragmentation analysis, and final quantification achieved using optimized MRM methods. During this process, we observed an intriguing peak (m/z 271.05) exhibiting different retention times in both GYN3 and CAG13 strains (Fig. 4B). Further investigation, including comparison with authentic standards, identified this peak in the GYN3 strain as genistein, a byproduct (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S3), while the peak in the CAG13 strain may be 3′,4′,7-trihydroxyisoflavone (5) based on the characteristic Retro-Diels-Alder (RDA) fragments at m/z 137 (A-ring) and 133 (B-ring) (Supplementary Fig. S4). The glycosylated metabolites (6–8) were conclusively identified based on two key criteria: appropriate retention times relative to their precursors and characteristic fragmentation patterns featuring substrate-derived ions and glucose moiety loss (162 ​Da) (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. S4).

Subsequent comparative analysis of metabolite profiles between CAG3 and CAG12 strains revealed significant flux alterations following the replacement of AmUCGT with AmUGT88E29 (Fig. 4C). This modification resulted in a marked increase in the glycosylated products (6–8) and 3′,4′,7-trihydroxyisoflavone (5) decreased by half, demonstrating that the initial enzymatic steps in the pathway represent critical control points for further optimization efforts.

Optimization of pathway enzymes for enhanced calycosin-7-glucoside production

Building upon the CAG12 strain, we systematically optimized the initial enzymatic steps in the pathway to improve the calycosin-7-glucoside (4) production. First, we introduced an additional copy of PlOMT9 and AmI3′H individually, generating strains CAG17 and CAG18, respectively. As a control, we also doubled the copy number of AmUGT88E29 to create strain CAG19. As shown in Fig. 5, CAG17 (with an extra PlOMT9 copy) exhibited the highest production of calycosin-7-glucoside (4), consistent with our earlier findings that upstream enzymes critically influence pathway flux. Notably, formononetin (2) levels remained significantly lower than other intermediates, suggesting its synthesis remains a bottleneck. Interestingly, in all modified strains (CAG17 to CAG19), daidzein (1) was efficiently consumed without substantial accumulation of downstream intermediates (3, 5 to 8), reinforcing that PlOMT9 copy number optimization is the most beneficial for enhancing calycosin-7-glucoside (4) titer.

Analysis of pathway intermediates. Proposed scheme of calycosin-7-glucoside (4) biosynthesis network and LC-MS peak area of all pathway intermediates identified in strains CAG12, CAG17, CAG18, and CAG19. Dash arrows mean possible enzymatic reaction.

To further validate these observations, we introduced additional copies of PlOMT9, AmI3′H, and AmUGT88E29 expression cassettes into CAG17, yielding strains CAG20 to CAG22. However, fermentation analysis showed that strain CAG17 outperformed all other strains, achieving a final titer of calycosin-7-glucoside (4) at 0.22 ​mg/L (Fig. 6). These gene copy number optimization results (Fig. 5, Fig. 6) identified PlOMT9 as the pivotal target for further pathway engineering. However, simply adding extra copy of PlOMT9 failed to further improve the titer of calycosin-7-glucoside (4), indicating that PlOMT9 with a modest copy number was optimal and the necessity of other metabolic engineering strategies for further improve calycosin-7-glucoside (4) production.

Discussion

In this study, we engineered S. cerevisiae to produce calycosin-7-glucoside (4) and identified key rate-limiting steps in the pathway, facilitating future strain optimization for high-yield biosynthesis of formononetin (2) or calycosin (3). While previous studies have established plant (Nicotiana benthamiana) and microbial (such as E. coli and S. cerevisiae) systems for isoflavonoid production [10,21,22], there has been no report on heterologous biosynthesis of calycosin-7-glucoside (4) yet. Additionally, plant-based platforms face limitations, including complex endogenous metabolism, scarce regulatory elements, and slow growth [23,24]. In contrast, our engineered yeast strain produced calycosin-7-glucoside (4) in less than 48 ​h, offering a scalable, rapid, and cost-effective alternative for high-value isoflavonoid bioproduction.

Previous reports indicate that endogenous hydrolases in yeast, such as EXG1, degrade flavonoid glycosides (such as naringenin-7-O-glucoside and anthocyanins [19,25] (Fig. 3). However, hydrolase specificity varies; for steviol glycosides, SCW2 knockout enhanced glycosylation the most effectively among six tested genes (EGH1, EXG1, SCW2, SCW4, SIM1, and SPR1) [20]. Additionally, increasing UDP-glucose supply has been shown to improve flavonoid glycoside titers [[25], [26], [27]], suggesting precursor availability as a critical factor. AmUGT88E29 exhibited broad activity, synthesizing daidzin (6), ononin (7), and glycosylated 3′,4′,7-trihydroxyisoflavone (8) (Fig. 4). Similar promiscuity was observed in LjUGT72V3 (Lotus japonicus) and PlGT04F14 (Pueraria lobata) [28,29], highlighting the flexibility of legume-derived glucosyltransferases in flavonoid and isoflavonoid biosynthesis.

Despite reconstructing the calycosin-7-glucoside (4) pathway in yeast, metabolic flux remained suboptimal, a common challenge in heterologous biosynthesis [[30], [31], [32], [33]]. To address this, we employed LC-MS-based metabolomics to monitor pathway intermediates [[34], [35], [36]], revealing PlOMT9 as a key bottleneck. Compared with a titer of 24 ​mg/L daidzein (1) in the parental strain GYN3, the final titer of calycosin-7-glucoside (4) was only 0.22 ​mg/L, with a conversion yield around 0.9 ​%. Analysis of metabolic flux distribution by profiling intermediate metabolites revealed that PlOMT9, AmI3′H, and AmUGT88E29 exhibit obvious substrate promiscuity, with all three enzymes capable of catalyzing daidzein (1) (Fig. 4). Furthermore, the abundance of pathway by-products was comparable to that of calycosin-7-glucoside (4) (Fig. 4, Fig. 5). These observations suggested that a substantial portion of daidzein (1) and intermediate metabolites, e.g., formononetin (2) and calycosin (3), was diverted toward by-product formation, likely contributing to the low titer of calycosin-7-glucoside (4). In contrast, plants have evolved precise regulatory mechanisms to achieve rapid and reversible control of metabolic flux. One key strategy involves the formation of enzyme complexes through specific protein–protein interactions. For example, the interaction between chalcone isomerase-like protein and chalcone synthase (CHS) enhanced the catalytic specificity of CHS, promoting the production of chalcone instead of the byproduct p-coumaroyltriacetic acid lactone [37]. Similarly, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase associated with complexes containing downstream enzymes such as cinnamate 4-hydroxylase (C4H), facilitating channeling of carbon into the phenylpropanoid pathway [38]. Additional interactions, such as those between flavone synthase II or flavonoid 3ʹ-hydroxylase and flavonoid biosynthetic enzymes in snapdragon and torenia, were also reported [39]. Recently, interactions between GmIFS2 and GmIOMT8 or GmIOMT13 (G. max isoflavone O-methyltransferases) were identified: co-expression with GmIOMT8 redirected flux toward formononetin (2) rather than biochanin A, while co-expression with GmIOMT13 favored the production of methylated liquiritigenin over methylated naringenin [40]. Therefore, screening for more efficient interacting IOMTs or variants of GmIOMT8 and identifying potential partners of isoflavone-3ʹ-hydroxylase (I3ʹH) represent promising metabolic engineering strategies for enhancing the production of calycosin-7-glucoside (4).

In conclusion, we demonstrated the de novo biosynthesis of calycosin-7-glucoside (4) in engineered yeast, achieving a titer of 0.22 ​mg/L within 48 ​h. Through LC-MS metabolomics, we identified PlOMT9 as additional bottleneck and optimized its encoding gene copy number to enhance flux toward the target compound. Our work also revealed the substrate promiscuity of legume-derived AmUGT88E29 and hydrolase specificity (EXG1 for flavonoids). These findings provide a framework for engineering yeast to produce high-value isoflavonoids efficiently. Future efforts can expand this approach to other natural products, leveraging yeast's adaptability for industrial biomanufacturing.
