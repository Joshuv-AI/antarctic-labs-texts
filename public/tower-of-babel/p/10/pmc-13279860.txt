From Sparse Nanoparticles to Graded Interfaces: Beyond Classical Descriptions of LiF Growth on Organic Semiconductors
Authors: Pedro Oliviera, Felix Maye, Ayse Turak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSmall‐molecule π‐conjugated semiconductors provide an ordered platform for probing interfacial mechanisms in organic optoelectronics, where device performance and stability are dominated by interface effects. Lithium fluoride, despite its widespread use as an interlayer, remains paradoxical: essential for high device efficiencies yet governed by poorly understood growth processes that undermine reproducibility. Here, we use diindenoperylene (DIP) as an idealized small‐molecule substrate to examine LiF deposition with atomic force microscopy, grazing‐incidence x‐ray diffraction, and physically constrained stepwise x‐ray reflectivity (XRR) modeling. LiF nucleates as sparse nanoparticles distributed across the surface from the earliest measurable stages, roughening the interface and partially perturbing the organic top layer. Film formation proceeds through stochastic densification of nanoscale domains, rather than classical coalescence or wetting‐layer growth, ultimately giving rise to a laterally continuous but structurally graded interface. While the DIP lattice remains crystallographically intact, consistent with weak structural coupling, XRR reveals non‐monotonic interfacial electron density profiles that are inconsistent with conventional slab or island models. This coupled evolution of nanoparticle morphology and organic density profile provides a structural basis for how spatially inhomogeneous, partial coverage may exert disproportionate electronic effects. These results recast LiF as a sparse nanoscale interlayer whose spatial distribution and site selectivity govern interfacial behavior, establishing a framework for reconstructing graded nanoscale density profiles in organic semiconductor interfaces.

Introduction

Small‐molecule π‐conjugated semiconductors are central to organic optoelectronics, underpinning progress in OLEDs, OPVs, OFETs, and related devices. Their discrete molecular structures enable high purity and reproducibility, facilitating clear links between molecular packing, charge transport, and device function [1, 2, 3]. Unlike polymers, which are often polydisperse with batch‐to‐batch variability, small molecules can form highly ordered thin films, providing a precise platform for probing interfacial mechanisms [1, 2]. As device operation and stability in organic electronics are dominated by interface effects [4, 5, 6], small‐molecule systems are especially revealing: their vacuum‐deposited multilayers produce abrupt junctions where interfacial chemistry and structure exert a disproportionately strong influence on performance and stability [2, 7, 8, 9].

Among interlayers used for interfacial engineering in organic optoelectronics, lithium fluoride (LiF) has become nearly ubiquitous because of its well‐documented beneficial impacts [5]. When thermally evaporated, however, LiF develops a granular and porous morphology with large surface roughness [10, 11, 12]. As a wide band gap insulator, it is only effective when deposited as an ultrathin layer [5]; above a critical thickness its ability to enhance charge injection or transfer, as reported in device studies [5], diminishes, ultimately harming device performance. At such thicknesses, LiF typically fails to form a continuous, uniform layer on a variety of substrates [10, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18].

Paradoxically, despite this rough morphology and strict thickness limits, LiF remains indispensable as an interlayer. What is still missing is a clear understanding of how LiF actually grows on organic semiconductors, from the earliest stages of particle accumulation to the formation of a continuous film, and how this growth structurally interacts with the underlying organic layer. Without this process‐level picture, efforts to control LiF more reliably or to design alternatives remain. This gap has made reproducibility in organic devices a persistent challenge, often requiring large statistical datasets across many devices and deposition conditions to uncover genuine performance improvements [19, 20].

In particular, three aspects of LiF growth on organics remain unresolved. First, while progress has been made in following LiF film growth, it has not been possible to systematically track this process on device‐relevant substrates. Second, the role of structural defects in guiding LiF deposition has remained unclear, as most prior studies rely on rough or disordered films that obscure local accumulation and island size. Third, indirect spectroscopic probes dominate, leaving the pathway from initial island nucleation to complete film coverage unresolved [10, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18]. Such indirect measurements cannot distinguish whether growth proceeds by island enlargement or by progressive gap‐filling until continuous coverage, nor if these processes are governed by deterministic ripening or inherently stochastic deposition events. Unresolved, this remains a key obstacle to understanding interlayer formation and the limits of reproducibility in LiF‐based devices.

For directly tracking LiF overlayer accumulation, coverage evolution, and vertical distribution, we combine atomic force microscopy (multiple modes), grazing‐incidence x‐ray diffraction (GIXRD), and, most distinctively, a systematic x‐ray reflectivity (XRR) modeling framework. Unlike prior studies, our XRR strategy evolves from tightly constrained slab models to physically meaningful bilayer descriptions, benchmarked against AFM. This enables extraction of interfacial density profiles and coverage with high robustness, providing a framework underdeveloped for LiF/organic interfaces. To achieve this multi‐mode approach, we focus on diindenoperylene (DIP) as a structurally well‐defined small‐molecule substrate. With its large crystalline terraces and stable herringbone packing [7, 8, 9, 21, 22], DIP provides an ideal platform for resolving subtle interfacial density changes and particle behavior with high precision. Rather than serving as a device‐specific transport layer, DIP functions here as an archetypal, weakly coupled organic semiconductor that enables isolation of LiF overlayer growth physics from complications arising from structural disorder. By establishing the interfacial response on a crystalline model system, we aim to extract general principles governing LiF formation and coverage evolution at organic interfaces.

Our analysis quantifies LiF coverage and effective density across multiple stages of growth, revealing a two‐step sequence: sparsely distributed nanoscale domains at early deposition that steadily proliferate across the surface without growing in size until the film attains continuous coverage. Meanwhile, the DIP lattice remains largely intact, consistent with weak structural coupling, but requiring a perturbed interface structure to fully describe the heterojunction. This unified picture replaces fragmented descriptions of LiF growth on rough organic films and complements indirect spectroscopic interpretations. The resulting insights not only offer new insight into the longstanding role of LiF as a high‐performance interlayer, but also highlight a more general motif: sparse inorganic domains can influence interfacial behavior even without full coverage, providing a structural basis for the pronounced electronic effects widely reported for ultrathin LiF interlayers, and offering a template for designing next‐generation interlayers in small‐molecule devices.

Results and Discussion

Early‐Stage Accumulation: Surface Inhomogeneity Directed Overlayer Formation

Diiondenoperylene (C32H16, DIP) is a semiconducting small organic molecule with promising electron transport properties [23], favorable electronic structure [24], and long exciton diffusion lengths [25]. It has a well‐defined crystalline structure [26, 27] and grows in a layer‐by‐layer mode up to a vertical thickness of several monolayers on various substrates [21, 28]. The tendency to form large atomically flat islands makes them ideal model systems for studying the interfacial morphology by decoupling the active layer morphology from that of the inorganic overlayer.

At high substrate temperatures, DIP molecules form a continuous wetting layer on a silicon substrate, and then begins growth layer by layer with large islands having a step height of 15–20 Å [28] (see Figure S1). Figure 1a shows topography images of a LiF/DIP/SiO2 system with a nominal thickness of LiF deposition as 3 Å as measured by a calibrated in situ quartz crystal monitor (QCM). The DIP molecules maintain their island structure, while the LiF appears in this image as nanometer‐sized particles. LiF is observed at this deposition amount to form chain‐like structures that accumulate at island edges, or within plateau regions of DIP, as well as more disorganized clusters. High‐resolution non‐contact AFM (not shown) of a few individual LiF islands on DIP shows a tall round particle shape with heights at 50±10 Å with each island making contact with a small bump in the DIP lattice, which may be associated with a defect boundary. In contrast, on inorganic surfaces such as Si, the sticking coefficient of LiF is sufficiently high that a similar nominal 3 Å deposition already results in complete coverage [29]. This highlights why DIP provides an ideal platform to resolve early‐stage island formation.

Figure 1b shows a transverse shear microscopy image of the same LiF decorated DIP surface. Transverse shear microscopy (TSM) measures the torsional response of an AFM cantilever in contact mode when the sample is scanned parallel to its long axis, making it sensitive to variations in shear stiffness or crystallographic orientation rather than conventional sliding friction [30]. The contrast observed in Figure 1 is a result of varying crystallographic orientations of the DIP lattice on the substrate [30, 31]. However, it seems to offer limited contrast at island edges or at nanoparticle decorations, where shear properties may not change strongly relative to the surrounding material. Nonetheless, it provides strong evidence that the DIP islands consists of various crystallographic domains within a large DIP island plateau.

Figure 1c shows an electrostatic force image from the same system. In contrast to contact mode TSM, which probes short‐range mechanical shear response, electrostatic force microscopy (EFM) is a non‐contact AFM mode that senses long‐range Coulomb interactions between a biased conductive tip and the sample, enabling mapping of local surface potential, charge distribution, and electrical heterogeneity [32]. There is a large EFM contrast (∼200 mV) between the LiF nanoparticles and the regions of DIP, clearly highlighting the island‐like LiF features accumulating on the DIP surface visible in the topography map. This strong contrast is likely to arise from differences in net surface charge density and the associated band bending [33], influenced by each material's defect states and electron affinity (3.1eV [34, 35] for DIP and −0.035 [36, 37] eV for the LiF nanoparticles). Although the edges of the DIP islands can be seen in the topographic map, no difference between islands or the wetting layer underneath can be observed in the corresponding EFM map (see Figure S2 for a higher magnification region where the topography and electrostatic force can be more easily compared). However, within a DIP island, EFM shows different domains, with an ∼50 mV difference. The LiF chain particles appear to follow the domain boundary, suggesting that LiF preferentially accumulates at inhomogeneous sites on the DIP surface.

Friction force measurements in Figure 1d, performed under UHV conditions, show the structural composition of the atomically flat organic surface of a monolayer DIP surrounding of a LiF particle. Friction force microscopy (FFM) is a contact‐mode AFM technique that can achieve atomic‐scale resolution of the molecular packing in an organic van der Waals film by detecting periodic variations in lateral force as the tip slides across the surface lattice [38]. Enhanced friction (not shown) is observed at DIP step edges and defect boundaries, as well as at the LiF nanoparticle edges. An example of an inhomogeneous boundary in the DIP island is shown in the lateral force map of Figure 1d, as a disordered region, compared to the surrounding DIP film, where the lattice orientation of the DIP molecules does not change between the left and right sides of the boundary. A LiF particle is observed touching one of these structures.

Taken together, the topographic, TSM, EFM, and FFM measurements indicate that LiF nanoparticle distributions on the DIP surface are non‐random. Enhanced contrast and frictional differences along DIP island edges and inhomogeneous regions where LiF chains and particles are also observed (Figure 1) suggest that LiF accumulation is spatially correlated with structural and electrostatic inhomogeneities on the DIP surface. LiF particles are consistently observed at defect‐associated regions on organic molecules, which could include step edges, defect lines, and grain boundaries. Inhomogeneous sites such as these likely aid the formation of chain‐like LiF nanoparticles.

We note that this inhomogeneity‐directed accumulation is not unique to DIP. As shown in Figure S3, LiF deposited on evaporated mis‐cut single crystal Al preferentially decorates step edges before extending onto terrace plateaus. In that case, due to the single crystal nature of the Al surface, chain‐like features are only observed at step edges, with fewer disorganized islands occurring on the plateau. In DIP, terraces themselves are structurally inhomogeneous, as evidenced by domain contrast in TSM and EFM measurements. On inorganic amorphous substrates such as SiO2 (see Figure S1) and ITO [10], AFM likewise reveals roughness‐driven morphological variations in the LiF layer. Together, these comparative results support the general conclusion that LiF growth is governed by topography‐ and defect‐directed kinetics, consistent with our observations on DIP. While the supporting evidence is qualitative in nature, the convergence across both organic and inorganic systems strongly suggests that inhomogeneity‐driven accumulation is a general feature of LiF growth, with DIP providing a structurally well‐defined model to resolve this mechanism in detail.

To probe this mechanism further, we evaporated similar amounts of LiF onto DIP under elevated substrate temperature during deposition (see Figure S4), where we observe an increased prevalence of chain‐like LiF particle morphologies. The enhanced prominence of chain structures at elevated deposition temperatures is consistent with enhanced post‐deposition redistribution and selective accumulation of LiF at inhomogeneous regions. While this behavior suggests a greater degree of mobility following deposition, direct quantification of surface diffusion processes or alignment with specific defect structures is beyond the scope of the present measurements. Instead, these observations are most consistent with an inhomogeneity‐influenced accumulation process, in which elevated temperature enhances the spatial organization of LiF nanoparticles without fundamentally changing the underlying growth mode.

To move beyond qualitative observation and assess whether LiF distribution is systematically linked to measurable surface features, we quantify the spatial relationship between LiF nanoparticle coverage and DIP island geometry. Specifically, we examine the fraction of LiF associated with island edge regions as a function of island edge density (edge length per unit area), as shown in Figure S6a. Direct identification and quantification of grain boundaries or defect densities within DIP terraces is not possible from topographic AFM data alone. However, the combined TSM, EFM, and FFM analysis presented in Figure 1 demonstrates that the terraces themselves are structurally and electrostaticaly inhomogeneous. Accordingly, the edge‐based metric is used here as a conservative, experimentally accessible proxy for surface inhomogeneity. Within this framework, accumulation at island edges represents a lower bound for LiF accumulation across a broader class of inhomogeneous sites.

A generally positive correlation is observed between the edge‐associated LiF fraction and edge density, indicating that increased availability of edge‐like inhomogeneities is accompanied by a larger proportion of LiF localized in their vicinity. In parallel, we define an edge enrichment factor by comparing the local LiF coverage within the edge region to that expected from a random spatial distribution (see Figure S6b). This enrichment factor remains greater than unity for most measured images, while decreasing systematically with increasing edge density and approaching unity at higher values.

Taken together, the increase in edge‐associated LiF fraction with edge density, combined with enrichment factors exceeding unity across most measurements, supports a consistent picture of preferential accumulation at surface inhomogeneities within the limits of the edge‐based proxy. The reduction in enrichment factor toward unity at higher edge density reflects a geometric effect associated with decreasing island size, as edge regions occupy a larger fraction of the surface and the contrast between preferential and random occupation is reduced. In the same limit, the edge‐associated LiF fraction approaches a plateau, as the edge region itself constitutes an increasing fraction of the total available surface area. As discussed in the Supporting Information, this geometric dilution reduces the sensitivity of edge‐based metrics at high edge density, even if local preference persists. This interpretation is consistent with the elevated temperature results discussed above, where chain‐like LiF structures are observed throughout the terraces without a corresponding increase in edge‐associated accumulation, suggesting that terrace inhomogeneities can provide equally, or more, favorable accumulation sites under conditions of enhanced particle redistribution.

These results are consistent with a distributed, inhomogeneity‐influenced accumulation process. In this context, it is useful to examine how this behavior evolves with increasing LiF deposition, where the progression from sparse nanoparticle domains to more complete surface coverage can be directly observed.

LIF Morphological Evolution: Sparse Islands to Full Coverage Films

Given that the organic surfaces are not completely covered by LiF with measured deposition thickness typical of that used in organic devices [5], it is useful to understand how the LiF overalyer evolves with increasing LiF deposition. Figure 2 shows the evolution of LiF on DIP from discrete, sparse islands, and chain‐like structures at the DIP island edge into a low density wetting layer that slowly fills in the surface with self‐similar nanoparticles. This behavior is similar to that observed on Al surfaces in Figure S3, where the decoration of the step edges becomes less and less visible as LiF particles fill the surface. The substrate temperature for DIP growth was again set to create atomically flat surfaces on top of the organic film to be able to accurately visualize and quantify the nanoparticles. Representative AFM height profiles as shown above the corresponding AFM image for nominally 5 and 10 Å deposited LiF films, showing the height of the nanoparticle islands to be ∼50 Å.

Top panels show the height profile indicated by the blue line in the topographic images, where sharp peaks correspond to the LiF nanoparticles atop the flat DIP islands of the top layer.

Analysis of the LiF nanoparticle size and number was performed using Fourier subtraction [39] to remove the underlying topography of the DIP islands, as described in detail in Lee et al. [10] (see Figure S7). Height profiles from the flooded images show no significant difference in the particle size between both deposition amounts, but the number of visible LiF islands and the surface roughness increase as more material is deposited, as summarized in Table 1. As the QCM measured deposition thickness increases, the average island height stays the same (Δ=1Å) but the number of LiF islands increases (Δ=116%). We see complete coverage, with the formation of a closed LiF layer, at nominal thicknesses of around 20Å, where the DIP islands can no longer be resolved beneath the LiF. Therefore, for the thicknesses usually reported as effective for organic devices (5–15Å) [5], it is more appropriate to refer to the LiF thickness as a function of coverage. Unlike on organic substrates, LiF forms fully dense layers on inorganic surfaces at ∼3Å nominal deposition [10, 29], suggesting different sticking coefficients on the different surfaces. In both cases, the LiF forms crystallite islands, which gradually fill into a complete layer, after which the grainy topography from complete LiF coverage can be observed.

Based on the observed thin film formation, direct particle growth on the organic surface from dimers and trimers coalescing is unlikely as the particles would be expected to increase in size with greater deposition [40], which is not observed here. Rather what appears to occur is the deposition and accumulation on the organic surface of nanoparticles formed in the vapor phase during evaporation, initially localized at structural or electrostatic inhomogeneities, then slowly filling in the available terraces until a statistically full film is formed, via a densifying overlayer atop persistent roughness. In this context, we describe the process in terms of nanoparticle accumulation rather than nucleation, as the present measurements probe the spatial distribution of deposited particles and do not directly resolve nucleation kinetics. Formation of LiF clusters in the vapor phase with dimensions in the range of 5–8 nm have been previously reported by Wang et al. [41]. Desorption of larger clusters from the LiF source due to low temperature evaporation could originate from the strong cohesion in ionic clusters, due to potential interaction composed of an attractive part because of electrostatic monopole forces and a repulsive part from quantum mechanical overlap of the electronic clouds of ions with filled electron shells [42, 43]. Variations of growth parameters such as substrate temperature and growth rate do not show a change in the ultimate morphology of the LiF films. Neither the size, estimated from the island heights, nor the dispersion of the particles shows much variation.

X‐Ray Scattering Insight: Perturbed Interfaces Demonstrate the Failure of Abrupt Models

AFM was employed here not as a standalone metric, but in cross‐correlation with x‐ray reflectivity (XRR) measurements, ensuring that local morphological features are consistent with the globally averaged scattering response. Figure 3a shows XRR data for 2ML DIP with increasing amounts of LiF deposited, with reported thickness values from the QCM. For LiF deposition between 3 and 10Å, the number and spacing of interference fringes remain largely constant, suggesting minimal net thickness growth. This behavior is consistent with low‐density, discontinuous island formation.

(a) X‐ray reflectivity curves for samples of 2ML of DIP with increasing amounts of LiF deposition as nominally measured by QCM. (b) zoom into the first two Keissig fringes for each data set, inset shows simulated XRR curves with changing electron density x‐ray reflectivity and slab modeling of LiF growth on DIP. (c) Bilayer/effective coverage model fitting for XRR curves with increasing LiF deposition. (d) Corresponding scattering length density (SLD) profiles reveal a two‐stage bilayer structure: a low‐density interfacial region with perturbed electron density followed by a full‐density LiF layer.

A feature near qz≈0.7 Å is visible in some reflectivity profiles after LiF deposition. It coincides with the pseudo‐Bragg reflection expected for DIP, also visible in the thicker 8ML DIP film (black line), suggesting that it originates from variations in DIP ordering between samples rather than from a LiF‐induced effect. We therefore attribute this peak to variations in DIP ordering between samples, rather than to a structural change induced by LiF. From the pseudo Bragg peaks, the extracted DIP layer height was determined to be 17.52 Å. Assuming a nominal molecular length of 18.4 Å [26, 44], this corresponds to a molecular tilt of ∼17.8° relative to the surface normal.

For the nominal 20 Å LiF deposition, Kiessig fringe intensities are significantly damped, consistent with increased surface roughness and the formation of a laterally continuous film from LiF islands. Thermally evaporated LiF overlayers are generally known to exhibit a granular and porous morphology with large roughness on various substrates [10, 11, 12] and our observations are in agreement with this established behavior. In contrast to Si, where complete coverage is achieved already at sub‐monolayer thicknesses, the laterally continuous film on DIP only emerges after substantially higher nominal deposition, and still retains significant porosity and roughness.

Initial inspection of the XRR curves shows a systematic shift in the first Kiessig minimum toward lower q‐values with increasing LiF deposition, suggesting a net increase in surface electron density, as shown in Figure 3b. Simulated profiles in the inset, showing a shift to lower values with increasing electron density, confirm that this behavior is consistent with either densification of the overlayer or infiltration into the DIP surface region, providing motivation for exploration of graded interface models.

X‐ray reflectivity profiles were analyzed using a physically constrained slab modeling approach. The multilayer structure was represented as a sequence of thin slabs of defined thickness, density, and roughness, with reflectivity R(qz) calculated using dynamical recursion methods [45, 46]. Interfacial roughness was incorporated via a Debye–Waller factor exp(−qz2σj2/2). For DIP, we adopted an oscillatory electron‐density profile consistent with the known intramolecular packing, in which each molecule is divided into three sublayers (low‐high‐low density) [26]. Stacking two molecules vertically produces a five‐slab representation of a 2ML DIP film, as illustrated in Figure S8.

As specular XRR inversions are inherently non‐unique, our strategy was to begin with the most constrained physically meaningful model and relax parameters stepwise, assessing robustness at each stage. AFM‐derived island heights and coverage trends were used as independent constraints to discriminate between plausible models.

We first tested an abrupt‐interface model consisting of a uniform LiF slab deposited atop the intact DIP stack. While this model produced formally low figure‐of‐merit (FOM) values (∼10−4), the extracted scattering length densities (SLDs) were unrealistic and failed to capture the expected monotonic increase of LiF thickness with nominal deposition. Relaxing only the top DIP slab and the LiF layer improved the fits but did not yield systematic density trends. A more flexible bilayer/effective coverage model model was then constructed, in which all three sublayers of the top DIP lamella and one or two LiF layers were allowed to vary. This model accurately reproduced both the experimental reflectivity and the systematic evolution of thickness and SLD with increasing LiF coverage, while maintaining physical consistency with AFM observations.

For completeness, we also tested an unconstrained “free‐fit” model in which all DIP and LiF parameters were allowed to vary. While this approach produced the lowest FOM values, the resulting parameters were unphysical and lacked systematic trends with deposition, consistent with overfitting. As an alternative, we simulated a penetration model in which LiF infiltrates the top DIP lamella, represented by graded‐density sublayers or embedded nanoparticles. These fits again produced poorer FOM values and inconsistent LiF thickness/density evolution, confirming that such scenarios do not describe our system. Among all tested models, only the bilayer/effective coverage model description provided both quantitative accuracy and physical plausibility. Residuals from this model showed no systematic deviations beyond low‐q footprint effects and the DIP pseudo‐Bragg region (∼0.7 Å). Model comparisons and residual checks are presented in the Supporting Information (Figures S8 and S9; Tables S1 and S2).

The XRR‐derived densities reported in Table 2 reflect effective, laterally averaged SLDs of the LiF overlayer and the top DIP lamella, not the intrinsic densities of pure LiF or DIP. Their co‐evolution is consistent with a two‐stage effective coverage/graded‐interface picture: initially, compact LiF islands contribute a relatively high effective LiF SLD while deposition roughens and partially depletes the top DIP lamella, lowering its effective SLD. During partial coverage filling, a low‐SLD interfacial/mixed zone thickens, producing a transient dip in the LiF effective SLD and a depressed top‐DIP SLD. After percolation, a continuous LiF overlayer reduces roughness and voids, increasing both the effective LiF SLD and the apparent top‐DIP SLD.

To quantify LiF areal coverage, we used a spherical‐cap model anchored by the XRR‐derived equivalent solid thickness and AFM‐determined island height, adopting a shape factor κ=1.20±0.20 appropriate for the tall, weakly wetting islands observed by AFM (Equation S2). For comparison, a counts‐based estimator was also obtained directly from AFM island statistics; however, tip convolution biases the lateral dimensions and drives the implied κ toward the shallow‐cap limit (κ→0.5; Equation S5), so this method is used only as a consistency check. The XRR/AFM analysis yields a roughly linear increase in LiF coverage with deposition, reaching full coverage at ∼20 Å nominal thickness, which is consistent with our AFM observations. This behavior supports a two‐stage growth mode in which LiF nucleates as isolated islands that laterally space‐fill stochastically into a porous continuous film. The effective density evolution and coverage trends are in excellent agreement with AFM measurements and consistent with prior reports of granular LiF overlayers on organic semiconductors [13, 14, 15, 17, 18].

Preserved DIP Crystallinity With Perturbed Electron Density

To further probe the crystallographic interaction between the inorganic LiF and DIP, in situ in‐plane x‐ray diffraction experiments with increasing LiF amounts have been performed. The in‐plane GIXRD data presented in Figure 4a was acquired using a higher‐resolution detector on nominally similar DIP samples prepared under similar thermal and deposition conditions at those for the XRR study but with a slightly thicker DIP layer (∼ 5 monolayers), which offers clearer structural interpretation. While XRR modeling was not performed for this sample set due to differences in initial film structure, the crystallographic trends are consistent with the bilayer formation observed in the XRR‐modeled dataset discussed above (see Figure S10). Additionally, similar trends as discussed below were observed in GIXRD of the XRR samples (see Figure S11), though peak evolution was less well resolved due to detector limitations.

Inset above shows a schematic arrangement in the observable crystallographic planes in real space for DIP herring bone structure and LiF FCC structure. The lattice constant increases toward the bulk value (4.03 Å), consistent with structural relaxation during overlayer growth and nanoparticle film formation. (d) Cross sectional transmission electron microscopy images of LiF/DIP bilayer on a SiO2/Si(100) surface. The LiF overlayer forms discrete nanocrystalline islands, visible on top of the organic surface in the zoomed region (top right), with visible crystallographic planes. Grazing incidence data in Figure 4a shows peak signals from DIP for lower momentum transfers which are consistent with the reported herringbone structure of DIP with reflex positions assigned to (11), (20), (21), (31) [21, 47, 48, 49]. Upon LiF deposition, the in‐plane DIP diffraction peaks exhibit only minimal shifts to lower q values (δ≤0.005 Å); (see Figure 4b) with the calculated lattice parameter varing by up to ∼0.02 Å. Because DIP is monoclinic with oblique lattice angles, small changes in q can be geometrically amplified into larger apparent variations in the calculated lattice parameters. As illustrated in Figure S12, this projection effect means that changes as small as 0.005 Å in reciprocal space can translate into apparent lattice variations of up to ∼0.02 Å (see Table S4). Minor thermal drift during deposition may also contribute to this sensitivity. Importantly, the DIP diffraction peaks themselves remain essentially unchanged in their appearance and shape, indicating that the underlying organic film retains its crystalline order.

Within these limitations, we extract small systematic increases of ∼0.01‐0.02 Å in the DIP lattice parameters with LiF coverage, amounting to less than 0.2% relative to the bare film. These shifts lie within the uncertainty of the measurement, but their consistent trend suggests the possibility of minor relaxation or tilt adjustment in the uppermost DIP layers in response to LiF deposition. While not conclusive evidence of interfacial grading, this modest expansion is qualitatively consistent with the presence of a perturbed interfacial region. At the same time, the absence of any peak disappearance or structural reordering supports a crystallographically decoupled interface, in which the DIP lattice remains intact even as it interacts with the overgrowing LiF.

This interpretation is also supported by previous long‐duration annealing studies of DIP and LiF/DIP systems [50], which showed no systematic change in the in‐plane or out‐of‐plane Bragg peak positions with annealing at 90°C for up to 13.5 h. Particularly when LiF is present, both the morphology and crystallographic signatures of DIP remain unchanged under such annealing conditions, showing that DIP lattice parameters are already well relaxed after deposition. The subtle and localized shifts observed with increasing deposition of LiF are unlikely to reflect frozen‐in growth‐induced strain of the DIP or interfacial stress. Rather, they are consistent with an interaction‐driven interfacial modulation, in which LiF accumulation perturbs the uppermost DIP layers while leaving the bulk lattice intact.

The LiF signal is weak and broad for low deposition amounts and increases and sharpens with additional material deposition. At the same time the overall intensity of the organic response is decreased, which can be attributed to the decreased penetration depth of the x‐ray beam into the organic molecule film caused by the increased density of the LiF film. Only one reflex is observed for LiF, corresponding to the (200) peak for LiF rocksalt structure [51, 52].

The predominant (200) orientation of LiF observed on DIP, though unusual compared to standard powder patterns and previous thin film growth on non‐epitaxial surfaces [11, 52], is not unique to this system. We find similar (200) texturing on SiO2/Si(100) substrate, while LiF deposited on ITO and amorphous glass exhibits mixed or (111)‐preferred orientations (see Figure S13). These results suggest that LiF orientation is influenced by substrate structure, roughness, and energetics, rather than substrate temperature (as has been observed previously [52, 53]), as LiF deposition was performed at room temperature in all cases. As a result, the (200) texture arises more from kinetic or surface energy factors than from epitaxial templating. This further supports our interpretation of the DIP interface as structurally preserved, with LiF adopting a growth mode consistent with its kinetic preferences rather than strong epitaxial templating. Preliminary studies on other organic semiconductors suggest that LiF texture is similarly substrate‐dependent [29]; however, a comprehensive comparison across systems is beyond the scope of this work.

As deposition progresses, the LiF(200) peak sharpens and converges toward bulk q∥ = 3.121Å, as shown in Figure 4b. Due to the cubic structure, the LiF peak shifts by ∼0.02 Å which results in ∼0.025 Å increase in lattice spacing, as expected from its high symmetry and direct structure‐q relationship. This expansion of the cubic lattice is indicative of a gradual transition of sparse discrete islands into more continuous porous films, leading to changes in coherent scattering domain sizes and lattice relaxation. Lattice contraction is a well known behavior of nanoparticles due to induced strain within the crystallites [54]. Differences between surface and cohesive energies that originate in the uncoordinated bonds in the surface shell of nanoparticles result in smaller lattice constants as particle size decreases [55]. However, when a full film is established, the interparticle interactions can lead to changes in the lattice parameter [56]. As the LiF lattice constant does not completely reach the bulk value, even though complete surface coverage is observed above nominal 20 Å LiF deposition, it is assumed that full coverage is established by a porous arrangement of agglomerated particles. This lattice expansion is also not due to deposition‐related strain effects, as prolonged annealing for a LiF/DIP for did not result in increased particle size, agglomeration or any shifts in the crystallographic features [50].

As both the DIP and LiF structures are expanding, the change is probably not related to an interaction and energy transfer between the organic and inorganic material, rather it supports the non‐dissociative, intact nanoparticle model of LiF, aligning with the XRR behavior. If there were strong chemical interactions (e.g., LiF dissociation or chemical incorporation into DIP), one would expect peak suppression, broadening, or systematic shifts in the DIP peaks, none of which are observed, supporting the picture shown by the XRR modeling of a structurally decoupled but electronically perturbed interface.

The change in the peak breadth can be used to extract the nominal crystallite sizes for the LiF deposits, using the Scherrer equation [57] (see Figure S12). This shows crystallite sizes of ∼32 Å, which do not change substantially with LiF deposition, especially once a complete film is formed.

To further examine the interface formation, transmission microscopy for LiF/DIP bilayer grown on SiO2/Si(100) substrate was performed, as shown in Figure 4d, with nominal deposition of 5 Å LiF. The thickness of the amorphous SiO2 capping layer on top of the Si(100) surface has been determined as 12.5 Å. The thickness of the organic layer is around 230 Å as this was required by the TEM specimen preparation procedure [58]. Both the DIP film and the LiF nanoparticles are visible in the images in Figure 4. Length scales have been calibrated by the known plane distances of Si(111) that have been resolved in the images.

Cross‐sectional images from LiF in the incomplete coverage regime on organic DIP layers show the formation of spherical and crystalline LiF particles with sizes of around 50 Å in each dimension, supporting the extracted LiF nanoparticle heights observed in the AFM (see Figure 2). This is slightly larger than the crystallite size determined from the XRD breadth analysis, but is likely within the uncertainty in the estimated particle sizes. Observable lattice planes, measured with distances of the bulk fcc structure (see Figure S14 and Table S5), suggest an orientation of the crystalline particles with <100> ordering parallel to the DIP surface. The particles are disconnected from each other and no penetration inside the organic film is visible, consistent with our AFM and x‐ray measurements. Weak sticking on the organic surface is assumed as the particles are seen to diffuse into the glue used for TEM specimen preparation.

The vertical planar stacking of DIP molecules, represented by crystallographic planes between each organic monolayer, has been identified with a thickness of 14.6 Å using the modulation of the intensity profiles, as the features of the organic molecules vanish after several seconds in the beam. The weak contrast between organic monolayers in the image causes discrepancies to the monolayer distances of DIP measured in XRR and AFM measurements (∼17.5 Å). These value for the layer stacking nonetheless support the formation of DIP in the standing up phase (σ‐phase), suggested by XRD measurements. Friction force measurements of the DIP films in areas away from LiF nanoparticles confirm the herringbone packing from the σ phase.

The crystallographic (GIXRD) and real‐space (TEM) analysis of LiF deposited on DIP together, support the interpretation of a structurally decoupled but coherent LiF/DIP interface. In‐plane GIXRD shows the emergence of crystalline LiF(200) texture with increasing thickness, while DIP peaks remain unchanged. Cross‐sectional TEM confirms the presence of discrete, nanocrystalline LiF islands atop an ordered DIP base layer.

Cross‐Technique Insight: Resolving Nanoscale Perturbed Interfaces

While LiF has long been deployed as an interlayer in organic electronics, it has typically been treated as an idealized, abrupt film in both experimental analysis and device modeling. By combining complementary structural and surface‐sensitive techniques, we show that the early growth of LiF on DIP deviates strongly from this picture. Rather than forming a continuous overlayer, LiF deposits as nanoparticles that roughen the surface, partially perturb the organic top layer, and only at higher nominal thickness approach a laterally continuous film.

Previous studies of LiF island growth have relied primarily on spectroscopic signatures of interfacial charge transfer [13, 14, 15, 59] or on deposition on rough or amorphous substrates, where quantitative assessment of nanoscale morphology is limited [13, 16, 17, 18]. By contrast, our study resolves LiF nanoparticle formation on a well‐ordered small‐molecule surface, enabling direct comparison of particle size, density, spatial distribution, and the transition from sparsity to full coverage. A central result is the ability to distinguish between nanoparticle size evolution and densification as the dominant mode of film formation.

The observed invariance in particle size alongside increasing particle density indicates that LiF film formation proceeds through progressive filling of surface inhomogeneities, leading to densification of the overlayer rather than coalescence or classical wetting‐layer growth. This behavior is not consistent with classical nucleation‐diffusion growth models, where island size and density are governed by surface diffusion and critical nucleus formation; accordingly, extraction of parameters such as diffusion length or critical nucleus size from the present data is not physically meaningful.

Even for a fixed nominal LiF thickness, as measured by the QCM, the areal coverage of nanoparticles extracted from AFM images shows significant variability across samples. While this variation is not treated quantitatively here due to limitations in image‐based analysis, it is consistent with the stochastic nature of nanoparticle deposition and accumulation on structurally inhomogeneous surfaces. This reinforces that the interfacial state is not uniquely defined by nominal thickness alone, but also by the spatial distribution of nanoscale domains.

The fact that the regime in which LiF is most commonly used in devices corresponds to sparse nanoscale domains rather than a continuous film suggests that spatial distribution may be as significant as average thickness in determining interfacial function. Even partial coverage at electrostatically active sites could influence the interface beyond the local footprint of the nanoparticles, providing a structural basis for the pronounced dipole effects widely reported for ultrathin LiF interlayers. More broadly, this points to sparsity as a potentially generalizable design parameter for engineered interfaces, motivating further exploration of alternative interlayer and surface modification strategies.

The XRR analysis reveals modulated density profiles that cannot be reproduced by conventional slab or island models; instead, graded‐density descriptions, in which the effective DIP surface density evolves with LiF deposition, yield consistent fits across multiple thicknesses. The ability to separate effective SLD contributions from LiF and DIP, and to reproduce non‐monotonic density evolution across the deposition series demonstrates the power of a physically constrained yet flexible modeling approach. Beginning from fully constrained DIP slab models and incrementally relaxing parameters, guided by AFM‐derived coverage trends allows a systematic evaluation of model assumptions. This approach also demonstrates the systematic failure of abrupt junction models and establishes a bilayer/effective coverage model description that captures both the non‐monotonic density evolution and the coverage trends. Our complementary evidence suggests that morphological decoupling dominates the initial stages of growth.

While the present work focuses on structural and morphological evolution, the observed perturbation of the DIP electron density profile is consistent with concurrent electronic interactions, such as local polarization or weak charge transfer, reported in the literature. Indeed, the need to adjust the DIP electron density for XRR fitting suggests that subtle charge transfer or polarization effects would likely be present. In this context, LiF on DIP serves as a clean structural demonstration of the underlying growth pathway that may accompany such electronic effects.

Despite these interfacial perturbations, in‐plane GiXRD and TEM confirm that the underlying DIP lattice remains structurally intact: no reordering or peak broadening is observed, underscoring a decoupling between morphological disruption and crystallographic stability. This is possible only through the use of a small molecule system, as although DIP itself is not commonly coupled with LiF in practical devices, its structural regularity makes it an ideal model system to resolve these early‐stage growth processes, which would be difficult to track on technologically relevant but less ideal semiconductors.

Taken together, these results support an island accumulation –>increasing stochastic coverage –> statistically full film progression pathway for LiF growth on organic semiconductors, in which LiF deposits as discrete nanoparticles that exhibit enhanced spatial correlation with interfacial heterogeneities, and progressively densify the surface to form a composite bilayer. The central advance is not only the morphological insight on how LiF growth progresses, but the demonstration that XRR, when guided by AFM and grounded in physically constrained models, can reconstruct nanoscale interfacial density profiles with high fidelity.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrate that LiF deposition on highly ordered DIP deviates markedly from the conventional picture of an abrupt, continuous overlayer. Instead, LiF forms discrete nanoparticles that roughen the surface, partially perturb the organic top layer, and progressively extend coverage toward a laterally continuous film while simultaneously perturbing the underlying organic density profile. This coupled evolution of overlayer morphology and organic density profile is captured only by stepwise XRR modelling constrained by AFM and GiXRD, which reveals non‐monotonic electron density profiles inconsistent with simple slab or island descriptions.

Various AFM modes show that LiF nanoparticles are distributed across the DIP surface from the earliest measurable stages, while exhibiting enhanced spatial correlation with surface inhomogeneities, including island edges and internal domain structures. These observations underscore the role of local structural and electrostatic heterogeneity in influencing LiF growth. Film formation proceeds through progressive densification of nanoscale domains, rather than through continuous coalescence or classical wetting‐layer growth.

Despite these interfacial perturbations, the DIP lattice remains crystallographically intact, indicating a decoupling between nanoscale morphological disruption and long‐range structural order. These findings recast LiF not as a uniform spacer layer, but as a sparse nanoscale interlayer whose spatial distribution and site selectivity may influence interfacial properties as strongly as its average thickness. By resolving the graded structural evolution of sparse LiF domains, this work establishes a potential physical framework for understanding the pronounced electronic effects widely reported for ultrathin LiF interlayers. Even partial coverage at electrostatically active boundaries could strongly influence charge injection or dipole formation, amplifying interfacial effects well beyond the local nanoparticle environment, consistent with prior spectroscopic observations.

More broadly, the results suggest that variability in LiF‐based devices may arise not only from processing conditions, but also from the statistical distribution of interfacial inhomogeneities that guide nanoparticle accumulation. This framing helps explain why reproducibility remains difficult in LiF‐based devices. Inhomogeneity‐influenced accumulation followed by stochastic space‐filling growth suggests that reproducibility is limited not only by processing conditions but also by the statistical distribution of interfacial sites. By resolving LiF growth pathways directly on a model small‐molecule system, this work establishes a transferable methodology for reconstructing nanoscale interfacial density profiles by combining physically constrained XRR with complementary surface analysis. Extending this approach to other small‐molecule semiconductors and interlayer materials promises deeper mechanistic insight into inorganic/organic growth and may enable the deliberate use of sparsity and graded density as design parameters in organic optoelectronic interfaces.

Experimental

Diindenoperylene molecules were acquired from the Universität Stuttgart and were purified twice by gradient sublimation before use. Substrates of Si(100) covered with a 300 nm thick SiOx native oxide were used in all cases. Silicon substrates were cleaned in an ultrasonic bath for 15 min first with acetone and subsequently in ethanol, followed by a rinse with deionized water for 1 min and dried in a N2 stream before insertion into the UHV chamber. After inserting the substrate into the UHV chamber through the fast entry lock, the substrates were annealed under UHV conditions (pressure <1×10−9 mbar) at 500 for 30 min to remove adsorbed water and other contaminants. The deposition was performed by thermal evaporation from home built Knudsen cells with a deposition rate of approximately 0.2ML per minute for the organic and 1Å/min for LiF, determined from a calibrated quartz crystal monitor (QCM). The substrate temperature during growth was controlled by a resistive heater, with temperature oscillation less than 1.

Electrostatic Force Microscopy (EFM)

EFM measurements were conducted in lift mode using a Nanotec Electronica AFM with WSxM software. A 1 V DC bias combined with a 0.5 V AC modulation at 7 kHz was applied to the tip during the second pass at a lift height of ∼40 nm. Electrostatic contrast was extracted via lock‐in detection and reflects surface potential variations. A relative calibration based on a known 50 mV potential difference between reference regions was used to express the z‐scale in millivolts.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM)

AFM measurements were done in air using a Nanotec AFM system in tapping mode with a phase locked loop (PLL) dynamic measurement board. The non‐contact silicon tips were PPP‐ FMR‐50 (NanosensorsTM) with a resonance frequency of 45–115 kHz, a force constant of 2.8N/m and a tip radius of curvature <10 nm. The AFM images were processed with the free software WSxM (NanoTec) [60].

Contact mode imaging was performed under both UHV and ambient conditions. Under ambient conditions (relative humidity ∼40%), an Aglient 5500 AFM was used. In this case, contact mode cantilevers were used with an integrated tip (Nanosensors ‐ PPP‐CONT) having a force constant in normal bending between 0.05 and 0.09 N/m and lateral twisting between 30 and 60N/m. The stiffness of each cantilever in both normal bending and lateral twisting was determined using the Sadar method [61]. To achieve this calibration, a noise spectra of the normal and lateral signals were acquired and the thermal resonance of the normal bending and torsional twisting was fit to determine their respective frequencies and quality factors, as well as measuring the plan view dimensions (length and width) of the cantilevers. The deflection sensitivity of the photodetector was then determined by acquiring a normal deflection versus z‐sample position curve and subsequently fitting a line to the linear portion of the curve. Zero normal force is defined from the signal acquired from the cantilever when it is unbent and far from the surface. Negative normal forces refer to adhesive forces and positive normal forces refer to compressive forces. Lateral forces are the measured twisting signal of the cantilever while friction forces are determined as the half difference of the lateral forces over the forward and reverse directions. Transverse shear microscopy (TSM) forces are calculated in the same way as the friction forces, however during TSM imaging, the sample is scanned such that the fast scan direction is parallel to the long axis of the cantilever while for friction imaging, the fast scan direction is perpendicular to the long axis of the cantilever.

UHV‐AFM Experiments

All experiments under ultra‐high vacuum (UHV) were performed with a home‐built AFM, which employs the optical beam deflection method for force detection [62]. The AFM resides in an Omicron UHV chamber maintained at a pressure of <2×10−10 mbar. Silicon cantilevers with an integrated tip (Nano‐sensors) were used in all experiments. In contact mode experiments, PPP‐CONT cantilevers were used whose spring constant in normal bending was 0.05‐0.09 N/m and in torsion was 30–60 N/m. The stiffness of the cantilevers in both normal bending and lateral twisting was individually determined by the beam geometry method, where the thickness was determined from the resonance frequency of the first normal oscillation mode [63]. All other calibration procedures are the same as listed above. Images were analyzed using the WSxM software [60].

Xray Measurements

X‐ray measurements were performed at the MPI‐MF beamline at the third‐generation synchrotron ANKA (Angstromquelle Karlsruhe) at the Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT) [64]. A focused beam with an energy of 10keV (λ=1.24 Å), and a 2D CCD Pilatus detector were used to perform the out‐of‐plane and in‐plane scans in horizontal geometry. To limit beam damage, the devices were probed in a dedicated vacuum chamber, equipped with an x‐ray transparent beryllium dome, with base pressure <10−8 mbar during illumination. In‐situ experiments were also carried out in a vacuum chamber, consisting of three different slot positions for Knudsen cells, a quartz crystal monitor and a specially designed shutter system. The pressure condition in this particular chamber was less than 10−9 mbar. LiF has been deposited stepwise on top of the organic molecules with a cell temperature of 732 with x‐ray data collected after each growth step. Measurements are carried out on the same sample area during the experiment and sample was only moved for height alignment.

Reflectivity Modeling

An arbitrary electron‐density distribution ρe(z) is modeled by slicing the structure into thin slabs of varying density [45, 46]. The specular reflectivity R(qz) is then calculated recursively with the dynamical theory of reflection: the forward and backward wave fields are matched at every interface to obtain the Fresnel reflection and transmission coefficients. Interfacial roughness in real systems causes the smearing of the electron density profile and is accounted for by a Debye–Waller‐like factor exp(−qz2σj22), where σj is the Gaussian r.m.s. roughness of the jth interface [45, 46].

The electron‐density profile of the Si/SiO2/DIP stack was rebuilt from a small set of physically motivated slabs, informed by established knowledge of DIP's structure. To reproduce the oscillating electron‐density distribution for DIP reported by Dürr et al. [26], alternating slabs of high‐density and low‐density DIP layers were stacked together with each slab being characterized by a thickness, electron density, and roughness. Electron density reaches its maximum at the mid‐plane of every DIP layer, the position that defines each high‐density slab. It falls to a minimum in the head‐to‐tail transition zones between layers, which coincide with the centere of the low‐density slabs. GenX version 3.7.9. were used to analyze and fit the reflectivity profiles using differential evolution [65]. For fitting, the figure of merit used in GenX for all the fitting is given by the equation, (1) FOM=∑ilog10Yi−log10Si2log10Yi2 where Yi are the experimental data points and Si are the simulated data points. Log‐based merit functions are widely‐used in XRR analysis [46, 66, 67]. To obtain the best fit, the thickness, electron density, and roughness of each slab were initially constrained within narrow, physically reasonable limits and then relaxed individually until the simulated reflectivity reproduced the experimental curves while retaining meaningful SLD profiles.

The thickness of each internal slab was fixed at 8.3 Å giving a peak‐to‐peak distance of 16.6 Å in the electron‐density profile, matching the layer spacing reported for thermally evaporated DIP films [26]. At the film‐air interface, the slab thickness was set to 4.15 Å representing the low‐density region that is half the thickness of an internal low‐density zone formed by two adjacent layers. Figure S8 illustrates this model.

DIP‐LiF samples were modeled by adding an LiF layer atop the DIP structure. To address the inherent non‐uniqueness of XRR inversion, we deliberately implemented a stepwise fitting protocol: beginning with highly constrained single‐layer models, incrementally relaxing parameters, and cross‐validating against AFM‐derived coverage. ‐

Transmission Electron Microscopy

Cross‐sectional TEM data was collected in collaboration with the StEM (Stuttgarter Zentrum für Elektronenmikoskropie) of MPI‐MF. Preparation of TEM specimens was performed with the method, described in detail by Dürr et al. [58]. The sample is cut in two pieces and glued together in a sandwich to prevent damage during specimen preparation. The films were measured by a CM200 microscope (Philips) operated at 200 keV with a point resolution of 2.7 Å. The sample remained stable in the electron beam only for 1–2 min but the features of the organic molecules vanish already after several seconds in the beam.
