Iatrogenic atrial dysfunction after prophylactic left atrial appendage exclusion: a proposed post-procedural atrial phenotype
Authors: Jeffrey H Shuhaiber

Keywords: Left atrial appendage, Atrial fibrillation, Post-operative atrial fibrillation, Stroke prevention, Cardiac surgery, Structural heart disease

Prophylactic exclusion of the left atrial appendage (LAA) during cardiac surgery is increasingly applied to patients without preoperative atrial fibrillation (AF). While the practice carries theoretical stroke-prevention appeal, its functional consequences in the sinus-rhythm heart remain underappreciated. We propose that a clinically recognizable post-procedural state—iatrogenic atrial dysfunction syndrome (IADS)—may emerge in a subset of such patients, characterized by altered atrial mechanics, heightened susceptibility to post-operative AF, and downstream ambiguity in antithrombotic and rhythm management. Positioned within the established spectrum of atrial cardiomyopathy, IADS reflects the immediate, measurable costs of removing a functionally active appendage in an already-vulnerable perioperative atrium. The concept may extend to percutaneous LAA occlusion in patients with paroxysmal AF who spend prolonged periods in sinus rhythm. We outline a provisional five-domain clinical framework and propose IADS as a testable post-procedural phenotype warranting prospective study.

The left atrial appendage (LAA) is increasingly being excluded during cardiac surgery in patients who do not have preoperative atrial fibrillation (AF). This practice has understandable intuitive appeal. The appendage is accessible while the chest is open, the technical threshold for exclusion has fallen, and the success of surgical LAA occlusion in patients with established AF has encouraged broader enthusiasm for the manoeuvre. Yet in patients who begin in sinus rhythm, the field may be asking the wrong question. The issue may no longer be simply whether prophylactic exclusion prevents future stroke, but whether in some patients it creates a recognizable form of post-procedural atrial dysfunction.

We propose that prophylactic LAA exclusion in patients without preoperative AF may, in a subset of individuals, produce a clinically meaningful post-operative phenotype best understood as iatrogenic atrial dysfunction syndrome (IADS). We do not propose IADS as a formal diagnosis or guideline-endorsed entity. Rather, we propose it as a mechanistic and clinical phenotype within the broader spectrum of atrial cardiomyopathy: an iatrogenic, post-procedural state characterized by altered atrial mechanics, increased susceptibility to atrial arrhythmia, and downstream uncertainty in rhythm and antithrombotic management. That framing is important. Europace and allied consensus work have already established atrial cardiomyopathy as a useful umbrella for structural, architectural, contractile, and electrophysiological atrial abnormalities with clinically relevant manifestations.1,2 Positioning IADS within that spectrum makes the concept more biologically coherent and less vulnerable to the criticism that it is simply a new label in search of a disease.

The appendage as a functional structure

The conceptual foundation for IADS begins with a reassessment of the appendage itself. The LAA is too often treated as a vestigial or expendable structure whose main significance is thrombus formation in AF. That is an incomplete view. Experimental and imaging data suggest that the appendage contributes to left atrial compliance, reservoir function, and pressure buffering.3,4 In a normal sinus-rhythm heart, exclusion of the appendage is therefore not simply removal of a potential future embolic cul-de-sac; it is also alteration of a functioning component of atrial mechanics. In practical terms, a buffered atrium may become a less compliant and more mechanically stressed one.

Mechanism and the perioperative atrium

That transition matters most in the perioperative setting, when the atrium is already vulnerable. Cardiac surgery produces inflammation, autonomic fluctuation, fluid shifts, transient ischemia-reperfusion effects, and abrupt changes in loading conditions.5 In that environment, even modest additional perturbation of atrial compliance or wall stress may be enough to tip a susceptible substrate toward post-operative AF (POAF). This provides a plausible mechanistic bridge between prophylactic LAA exclusion and the recurring clinical signal that has emerged in recent studies.

That signal deserves attention. Observational analyses have reported higher rates of POAF after concomitant LAA closure in patients without preoperative AF, and more recent pooled analyses have kept that concern alive rather than resolving it.6–9 A reconstructed patient-level meta-analysis published in 2025 suggested lower long-term stroke risk with prophylactic LAA closure in non-AF cardiac surgery patients, but it also reinforced that the available data are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, not definitive.8 A 2025 systematic review similarly found an overall signal toward benefit in stroke prevention, while acknowledging the continuing uncertainty around perioperative trade-offs and the need for randomized confirmation.9 That is precisely the environment in which an atrial phenotype concept becomes useful: when the clinical community senses that something real may be happening, but the phenomenon is not yet fully named, organized, or prospectively validated.

Morphology, closure technique, and modifying factors

A critical and underappreciated distinction is that prophylactic LAA exclusion in sinus rhythm patients targets a fully functional structure. Unlike the remodelled, low-velocity appendage familiar from established-AF occlusion trials, the LAA in a sinus-rhythm patient is electrically coupled to the atrium, contracting synchronously, contributing to reservoir function, and actively secreting atrial natriuretic peptide. Epicardial clip application has been shown to produce acute complete electrical isolation of the LAA,10 and epicardial LAA exclusion acutely disrupts natriuretic peptide secretion and neurohormonal homeostasis in ways that differ substantially from endocardial occlusion.11 In the perioperative environment, these simultaneous perturbations—loss of a compliant reservoir, abrupt neurohormonal disturbance, and a new zone of periappendiceal inflammatory injury at the exclusion boundary—converge on an atrium already rendered vulnerable by surgery. LAA morpho-functional parameters, particularly volume, emptying velocity, and orifice area, likely modulate IADS susceptibility more meaningfully than categorical shape classification alone.

Importantly, the proposed benefit and the proposed harm do not live on the same timeline. The hoped-for benefit of prophylactic LAA exclusion is delayed and contingent. It depends on the future development of AF, on the appendage remaining the dominant source of embolic risk, and on exclusion being durable and meaningful enough to alter subsequent stroke biology. By contrast, the possible harms are immediate. They occur at or soon after the index operation and include atrial arrhythmia, altered hemodynamic, longer recovery, and new ambiguity regarding anticoagulation and rhythm follow-up. That asymmetry is central to the IADS concept. We may be accepting a real and early perturbation of atrial function in exchange for a long-term benefit that may never be realized in many patients.

IADS would be especially relevant if the post-operative phenotype extends beyond transient POAF alone. Incomplete closure, residual stumps, or persistent communication between the atrium and appendage remnant remain well-described after surgical exclusion.12,13 That residual anatomy has at least two implications. First, it weakens the assumption that appendage management automatically translates into durable embolic protection. Second, it increases the chance that clinicians will overestimate protection and underestimate ongoing risk. In real-world practice, the presence of a clip or documented ligation may subtly anchor subsequent management decisions even when follow-up imaging has not confirmed a functionally complete exclusion. This is where electrophysiology and general cardiology begin to inherit not just a rhythm problem, but a definitional one.

A governance problem, not only a mechanical one

For that reason, IADS should not be framed purely as an electrophysiological curiosity. It is also a governance problem. The decision to place a clip is often made intraoperatively, sometimes without a robust preoperative multidisciplinary discussion and almost always in a population for whom the evidence base is still evolving. Yet the downstream consequences are borne elsewhere. Cardiologists and electrophysiologists may first encounter the appendage clip on imaging, in an operative note, or during post-operative consultation after arrhythmia has already appeared. They are then asked to determine whether AF has been meaningfully “de-risked,” whether anticoagulation thresholds should change, whether imaging is needed to assess stump anatomy, and whether POAF in this setting should be treated as ordinary post-operative noise or as evidence of a newly altered atrial substrate. Seen in that light, IADS is not merely a mechanistic idea. It is a post-procedural phenotype with practical consequences for longitudinal cardiovascular care.

Extension to percutaneous left atrial appendage occlusion

In paroxysmal AF patients specifically, the LAA during sinus intervals retains full mechanical and electrical function—meaning percutaneous occlusion in this population imposes the same functional loss on an active structure as prophylactic surgical exclusion does in non-AF patients, and may introduce periocclusion scar as a new substrate in an atrium already predisposed to arrhythmia. A brief consideration is warranted for percutaneous LAA occlusion (LAAO). Current device-based LAAO is deployed primarily in patients with established AF, in whom appendage function may already be substantially impaired and the atrium has adapted accordingly. In that context, the mechanical premise of IADS is less compelling. A more conceptually relevant subgroup, however, is the patient with paroxysmal AF who spends prolonged intervals in normal sinus rhythm. In these individuals, the LAA may retain meaningful reservoir and compliance function during sinus intervals, and device-based occlusion could plausibly perturb atrial mechanics in a manner analogous to surgical exclusion in non-AF patients. An international consensus guidance on LAAO for non-implanting physicians emphasizes the importance of individualized rhythm and functional assessment in such patients.14 Whether IADS-related markers—atrial strain, mechanical function indices, arrhythmia burden—differ between paroxysmal and permanent AF patients undergoing percutaneous LAAO is an open question that prospective study should address. However, we have shown that excluding the left atrial appendage in patients with no history of atrial fibrillation and in sinus rhythm increases their clinical propensity to develop excess post operative atrial fibrillation.15

A concept paper like this must also acknowledge the counterargument. The most obvious objection is that POAF after cardiac surgery is common regardless of appendage management, and that inflammation, surgical trauma, and patient comorbidity are sufficient explanations. That objection is valid. We are not arguing that prophylactic LAA exclusion is the sole cause of post-operative arrhythmia, nor even that it is the dominant cause. The more measured claim is that in some patients it may be an additional and modifiable contributor—one that has not yet been adequately recognized because the procedure has been viewed primarily through the lens of stroke prevention rather than atrial function.

A provisional clinical framework

How, then, should IADS be proposed without overreaching? Not as a strict diagnostic entity, but as a provisional clinical framework. Five domains seem reasonable.

First, a procedural domain: surgical LAA exclusion in a patient without documented preoperative AF.

Second, a temporal domain: onset in the early post-operative period.

Third, an electrical domain: new atrial tachyarrhythmia, especially POAF.

Fourth, a mechanical domain: evidence, direct or inferred, of altered left atrial function, compliance, strain, or pressure handling.

Fifth, a management domain: new ambiguity in anticoagulation, rhythm surveillance, or interpretation of residual stroke risk.

This framework is intentionally permissive. Its purpose is to organize a pattern, not prematurely codify it. If the concept is valid, it becomes testable. Patients with prophylactic LAA exclusion in sinus rhythm should demonstrate, on average, higher rates of early atrial arrhythmia, measurable alterations in atrial mechanical function, and greater downstream variability in antithrombotic and rhythm-management decisions than matched controls without appendage exclusion. Those are tractable hypotheses.

Ultimately, the value of IADS is not in the label itself. Its value is in forcing the field to ask whether we have been too quick to regard the appendage as expendable in patients whose atria were functioning normally before surgery. If prophylactic exclusion in sinus rhythm creates a subset of patients with altered atrial mechanics, greater arrhythmic vulnerability, and more uncertain downstream management, then we are not merely preventing possible future embolism. We may also be inducing a new atrial phenotype.

That possibility deserves a name, a framework, and prospective study. For now, IADS should be viewed as a novel proposed post-procedural atrial phenotype—one that may help explain the growing gap between what is done in the operating room and what must later be understood in the clinic.
