From local production to global consumption: Assessing the carbon footprints of Chinese cities in global value chains
Authors: Shaojian Wang, Junyi Liang, Yuli Shan, Chuanglin Fang, Xuemei Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs cities take on increasingly specialized roles in global value chains (GVCs), the spatial disconnect between where emissions occur and where responsibility lies continues to widen. However, city-level carbon flows across GVCs remain largely underexamined, hindering the design of effective climate policies. Here, we develop a GVC-oriented carbon accounting framework by nesting a multiregional input–output (MRIO) model of 313 Chinese cities within a global MRIO system. We quantify the upstream and downstream carbon footprints of Chinese cities via GVCs and assess their economic gains from GVC integration. Our analysis reveals a strong concentration of foreign demand–driven emissions (FDEs) in major coastal hubs, with the top 10 cities accounting for 25% of national FDE. In these cities, up to 43% of FDE stems from intermediate goods trade. Carbon emissions per unit of gross domestic product from international trade were 1.5 times higher than those solely relying on domestic industrial chains. In 54 service- and high-tech-oriented cities, over 20% of emissions occurred outside their borders and were induced by international supply chains (foreign GVC segment), with the United States being the largest contributor. At the national level, the United States alone accounted for 43.7% of emissions embodied in Chinese 313 cities’ gross exports. Our framework may inform more equitable allocation of responsibilities to develop collaborative decarbonization strategies across cities.

As cities deepen their roles in global value chains (GVCs), their carbon footprints often extend far beyond local boundaries, complicating climate responsibility. Using a GVC-oriented carbon accounting framework for 313 Chinese cities, we trace upstream and downstream carbon linkages in international trade. A handful of coastal manufacturing hubs account for a disproportionate share of emissions driven by foreign demand, particularly through upstream emissions from intermediate goods production. Conversely, many service- and tech-oriented cities downstream in the value chain outsource substantial emissions abroad, with the United States emerging as a major driver of China's export-related emissions. These findings uncover the hidden carbon flows in global trade networks and stress the need for collaborative climate action aligned with cities’ interconnected positions in GVC.

Introduction

Urban integration in the global value chain (GVC) has become a defining feature of contemporary economic systems (1). As the world's largest emitter—accounting for 35% of global carbon emissions (12.6 billion metric tons in 2023) (2)—China has historically relied on carbon-intensive development pathways (3). This reliance is reinforced by structural inertia in energy transitions and the dominance of high-carbon manufacturing, which hinders the decarbonization of GVCs (4, 5). The transfer of trade-embodied emissions along GVCs has decoupled geographic emission boundaries from consumption-based responsibilities (6, 7), undermining incentives for emission mitigation and complicating responsibility allocation (8, 9). As key drivers of economic growth and carbon metabolism, cities are pivotal to achieving net-zero goals (10). With the rise of intercity cooperation, constructing collaborative networks and fostering systemic resilience have become essential to addressing carbon leakage and enhancing mitigation (11). This urgency is further amplified by emerging policy instruments—such as carbon border adjustments and supply chain disclosure mandates, which demand clearer attribution of emissions across jurisdictions.

In response, growing scholarship has questioned the adequacy of conventional carbon accounting frameworks within fragmented GVC architectures (12, 13). While production- and consumption-based approaches provide foundational principles for carbon responsibility allocation (14), their reliance on geographic boundaries obscures the spatial decoupling of carbon flows and value creation under GVC fragmentation (15). Despite some conceptual overlap between consumption-based and GVC-based methods (16, 17), efforts to systematically integrate them at the city scale—where cities operate as critical nodes in global economic networks—remain scarce. This has led to three key mismatches in Chinese cities: (i) spatial, where local boundaries fail to capture global responsibility chains; (ii) structural, where cities’ GVC roles (e.g. low-tier suppliers) are misaligned with their bargaining power; and (iii) institutional, where technological lock-ins and unequal access to value capture persist. Therefore, existing research on supply chain carbon transfer (18–20), equity in emissions distribution (21, 22), and policy reform (23) increasingly calls for reimagined accounting systems that link urban carbon responsibility with value appropriation.

Nevertheless, two knowledge gaps persist. First, while existing studies have examined intersectoral or intercountry GVC linkages (12, 24), city-level heterogeneity in GVC participation and functional roles remains poorly understood. Cities with different industrial bases, such as resource oriented, manufacturing, and service-based cities, may occupy distinct positions along global production. These structural distinctions indicate divergent carbon emission profiles, that is, some cities’ footprints are dominated by intermediate goods for other domestic cities or foreign countries, while others are shaped by final-demand-driven exports. Identifying these functional and emission roles is essential to understanding how urban economies participate in and are shaped by the GVC. Second, there is a lack of analytical frameworks that connect cities’ functional roles with their potential pathways for low-carbon upgrading within GVC. We move beyond the conventional geographic boundary-based framework for carbon emission responsibility allocation by quantifying the share of a city's emissions attributable to external demand for intermediate products (“upstream” spillover) and dependence on imported carbon-intensive inputs (“downstream” spillover). This supply chain–oriented approach reconceptualizes carbon responsibility beyond local emissions, offering a different angle for understanding how governments and social actors can jointly assume differentiated responsibilities in urban carbon mitigation.

To address these gaps, we develop a GVC-oriented city-level carbon accounting framework by systematically integrating upstream and downstream spillover effects into the allocation of emission responsibilities. We also identify the carbon costs embedded in gross domestic product (GDP) generation through international trade. We aim to investigate the differences in roles played by cities in GVC. Specifically, we focus on how different types of cities (e.g. resource based, manufacturing, and service based) are positioned in GVCs and their corresponding emission roles. This paper attempts to explore the relationship between the externalization of carbon emissions in cities and their position in GVC. Methodologically, a nested MRIO model refers to a working tool for hierarchically nesting local scale within subnational, regional scale within global country-scale MRIO tables. In this study, we construct an environmentally extended nested multiregional input−output (EE-NMRIO) model by embedding the 2017 China’s city-level MRIO table into the Asian Development Bank (ADB) global MRIO framework. This approach enables high-resolution mapping of city-level carbon flows across global and domestic value chains. Our framework traces emissions along three dimensions: (i) upstream industrial linkages capturing production-based emissions, (ii) downstream linkages quantifying consumption-based responsibilities, and (iii) value-added trade decomposition revealing spatial spillovers. By clarifying the GVC structure and spatial transfer mechanisms underlying city-level carbon footprints, this framework provides a systematic understanding of how emissions are generated, redistributed, and externalized across cities. It supports the design of differentiated urban decarbonization strategies aligned with cities’ positions in value-added hierarchies.

Results

Upstream linkages: tracing foreign demand contributions to production-based emissions

In 2017, 313 Chinese cities collectively emitted 9,615.3 Mt CO2 to support production activities for other domestic cities and international partners, of which 81.5% was driven by domestic demand (Fig. S1).

Figure 1 reveals significant spatial heterogeneity in foreign demand−driven emissions (FDEs) across cities. Specifically, the spatial mismatch is reflected in the uneven distribution of carbon responsibilities across inland and coastal cities. Elevated FDE levels were concentrated in major urban agglomerations—including the Yangtze River Delta, the Pearl River Delta, and the Beijing−Tianjin−Hebei region—while emissions in western regions were comparatively low. The top 10 FDE cities, largely located in these hubs, accounted for 25% of national FDE, with 43% linked to traditional international trade (final goods trade; Fig. S1). Both resource-intensive inland cities (e.g. Yulin) and export-oriented coastal cities (e.g. Guangzhou and Ningbo) exhibited high exposure to carbon lock-in under GVC integration, highlighting persistent emission concentration in specific functional city types.

Notably, in 285 cities, emissions embodied in complex GVC trade exceeded those in simple GVC trade (Fig. S2), indicating a widespread shift toward deeper participation in multistage global production networks. Consistent with prior studies (7, 25), developed economies were the main importers of China's embodied emissions, led by the United States (478.5 Mt CO2), the European Union (EU) and the United Kingdom (284.6 Mt CO2), and Japan and South Korea (139.0 Mt CO2; Fig. S1).

Figure 2A shows clear income-related differences in carbon outsourcing patterns between foreign economies and Chinese cities, reflecting differentiated city roles within GVCs. Lower-middle-income countries primarily outsourced more emissions directly through direct imports of intermediate goods from Chinese cities, whereas upper-middle- and high-income countries increasingly relied on complex, multistage supply chains. Specifically, lower-middle-income countries outsourced 0.51 and 0.13 Mt CO2 through intermediate goods imports from service-based and light manufacturing cities, respectively. In contrast, high-income countries outsourced 3.45 Mt CO2 (41.8%) from service-based cities via complex GVC trade. Similar patterns are observed for heavy manufacturing and energy cities, where lower-middle-income countries depend on direct intermediate imports, while higher-income groups externalize emissions mainly through third-party intermediates. Overall, less-developed economies remain directly linked to China's carbon-intensive production stages, whereas high-income countries increasingly externalize emissions through multilayered GVCs.

Figure 2B illustrates an intercity carbon emission network characterized by regional differentiation and functional specialization. Eastern metropolitan cities (e.g. Guangzhou and Shanghai) primarily absorb carbon outsourcing through intermediate goods trade. In central China, Wuhan and Changsha mainly undertake carbon outsourcing via final goods trade, whereas Zhengzhou and Taiyuan do so via intermediate goods trade, extending even to megacities such as Shanghai and Beijing. Western cities display more differentiated roles, with Nanning and Chengdu relying mainly on final goods trade, while Urumqi and Chongqing serve eastern cities through intermediate goods trade. Together, these patterns reveal a clear interregional division of labor in China's urban carbon transfer network.

Cities also diverge in light manufacturing GVC participation. In 2017, over 50% of emissions from Weifang's textile industry were associated with traditional trade, whereas >60% of emissions from Binzhou and Shijiazhuang were embodied in complex GVC trade (Fig. 3C), reflecting different stages of value chain upgrading. Agricultural cities exhibit a distinct rerouting pattern: over 60% of emissions from Shaoguan, Chaoyang, and Changji were embodied in complex GVC trade, as agricultural outputs are typically aggregated and reprocessed in domestic processing hubs—particularly in coastal regions—before entering global markets (Fig. 3D).

Service-based cities such as Suzhou, Nanjing, and Tianjin exhibit diversified “manufacturing-services” integration. Nearly 60% of emissions from Nanjing's service sector were embodied in complex GVC trade, whereas Tianjin relied more on direct exports of intermediate goods (Fig. 3E). Suzhou displays a more balanced distribution across traditional, simple, and complex GVC trade, particularly for the EU and the United Kingdom and ASEAN markets. Even among similar city types, substantial heterogeneity persists: in Xuzhou, over 50% of emissions from the machinery sector occurred through complex GVC trade, with nearly 80% linked to Japan and South Korea (Fig. 3F). These results underscore the role of industrial structure and functional position in shaping city-level GVC participation.

To assess the environmental costs of economic growth, we calculate the ratio of value-added trade to embodied emissions trade for each city. Our analysis is guided by three hypotheses: (H1) international trade promotes urban decoupling, while domestic trade may induce environmental overload; (H2) localized production (without trade) amplifies structural lock-in in carbon-intensive cities, while trade may provide mitigation pathways; and (H3) trade promotes the externalization of environmental costs, amplifying regional inequalities.

GVC pathways are categorized into three types: localized production without trade, domestic trade, and international trade. Cities are grouped into four zones based on their carbon intensity of GDP compared with the national average: burdened structural lock-in (zone 1), trade-induced environmental overload (zone 2), decoupling pioneer (zone 3), and trade-induced environmental mitigation (zone 4; Figs. 4A and S4). The Environmental Kuznets Curve hypothesis, which posits an inverted U-shaped relationship between economic growth and environmental degradation, informs our progression from structural lock-in (early-stage high-intensity growth, zone 1) to decoupling (advanced-stage mitigation, zone 3). The Pollution Haven Hypothesis, which explains trade-induced shifts in emissions, underpins trade-induced environmental overload (zone 2) and trade-induced environmental mitigation (zone 4) by highlighting how trade can either overload or alleviate environmental pressures through carbon leakage or technology spillovers.

In most cities, carbon emissions per unit of GDP from trade substantially exceeded that from localized production without trade (Fig. 4A), suggesting environmental cost externalization via cross-border specialization. A total of 111 cities (33.3%) qualified as decoupling pioneers, with carbon intensities below the national average across both traded (2.81 kt CO2/million USD) and nontraded (0.94 kt CO2/million USD) activities—likely due to clean technology adoption and strong environmental regulation.

Cities in the dual-burdened lock-in zone (27 cities) exhibited high-carbon intensities across trade and nontrade, implying structural inertia and limited mitigation capacity. However, cities in the trade-induced environmental overload zone (10 cities) had disproportionately high intensity from trade, revealing susceptibility to environmental cost transfers through global supply chains. Cities in the trade-induced mitigation zone (10 cities) reduced local environmental burdens by outsourcing pollution-intensive activities via international trade.

Interestingly, over half of all cities (153) exhibited higher carbon intensity from domestic trade than international trade (Fig. 4B). These carbon-intensive, trade-dependent cities highlight key differences in energy structure, regulatory stringency, and technological sophistication between domestic and foreign trade partners.

Downstream linkages: mapping transboundary consumption-based emissions

China's final goods production in cities generates significant upstream carbon emissions in other domestic regions via downstream industrial linkages (Figs. 5 and S4). This pattern is driven by China's extensive industrial system and high localization of intermediate inputs, which enable most components for final production to be sourced domestically. In 2017, Yulin (351 Mt), Tangshan (227 Mt), Ordos (169 Mt), and Yinchuan (136 Mt) recorded the highest emissions induced by demand from other domestic cities (Fig. 5A). China's energy system remains predominantly coal-based, with Yulin and Ordos ranking among the top coal-producing regions nationwide. These cities are key coal-producing hubs in China's fossil fuel–dominated energy system and serve as critical contributors to national energy supply and upstream emissions.

In 2017, foreign emissions induced by the production of final goods and services in China accounted for just 15.9% of national total carbon emissions, a relatively low proportion compared with some developed countries such as Germany (26). It reflects the high domestic self-sufficiency of supply chains and the concentration of high-emission manufacturing within China's borders, which limits the size of foreign segments of GVC (27). Eastern and coastal cities, such as Suzhou (69 Mt), Shanghai (40 Mt), and Ningbo (31 Mt), exhibited higher emissions triggered by foreign segments of GVC (Fig. 5B). Notably, these eastern cities were also emission producers for foreign exports (Fig. 1), showing a relatively high correlation (Pearson r = 0.54) with the outsourced proportions in Fig. 5. This indicates asymmetric carbon emission pattern within China: the developed eastern cities externalize a portion of their environmental costs through trade networks, while simultaneously enduring export-driven local pollution. This overlap amplifies regional inequality-underdeveloped western or central cities (such as Yuulin and Yinchuan) often serve as emission suppliers, yet derive fewer economic benefits. In total, 101 cities—around one-third of the national sample—recorded foreign GVC emissions exceeding the national average. These cities were primarily service-oriented or high-tech hubs located in the first quintile (quintile 1; Fig. 5C), where emissions from the heavy manufacturing industry, electricity, and services were significantly higher than those from other sectors.

Figure 6 illustrates the spatial distribution and destinations of foreign GVC-induced emissions. In 2017, the top 10 cities with the highest share of foreign segments of GVCs were mainly located in the Pearl River Delta and Yangtze River Delta. These cities also served as emission producers for foreign exports (Fig. 1). Among the 54 service- and high-tech-oriented cities—comprising 17% of all cities—over 20% of their downstream-induced emissions occurred in international GVC segments. Of these top 10 cities, 27% of emissions were attributed to production segments located in the United States, followed by 16% linked to the EU and the United Kingdom. These findings highlight the importance of foreign downstream linkages, particularly for innovation-driven coastal cities embedded in globally fragmented supply chains.

Bilateral linkages: tracing city-level emissions across GVC partners

To illustrate how our accounting framework proposed in Materials and Methods at the bilateral level, we selected six major export destinations: the United States; EU members and the United Kingdom; Japan and South Korea; the ASEAN members; and India, Bangladesh, and Pakistan. These regions represent China's key trading partners.

Among all trading partners, the United States accounted for the largest share of emissions embodied in most cities’ gross exports, with a median contribution of 43.7% relative to their total export-related emissions (Fig. 7A). The EU and the United Kingdom ranked the second, with a median share of 21.4%. These results underscore the prominent role of the United States as the principal recipient of carbon-intensive exports from Chinese cities. To examine regional differences, we aggregated cities into 12 urban agglomerations (Fig. 7B). In the Central Plains, Chengdu-Chongqing, and Pearl River Delta, the United States contributed the highest share of emissions embodied in gross exports, with a proportion of 52, 52, and 50% of the respective total. In contrast, cities in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region showed a higher share of embodied emissions linked to the EU and the United Kingdom (28%), while in Harbin–Changchun, Japan and South Korea were dominant (18%), likely due to geographic proximity.

In 2017, city-level emissions embodied in gross exports varied substantially, generally decreasing from east to west (Fig. 7C). This pattern reflects the spatial concentration of carbon-intensive manufacturing in the east, and the upstream role of western cities in supplying raw materials. Shenzhen (19.8%), Shanghai (13.4%), Dongguan (9.5%), and Suzhou (9.3%) recorded the highest shares of carbon emissions embodied in gross exports to the five major trading partners, relative to the national total (Fig. 7C). These cities serve as manufacturing and trade hubs embedded in national and GVCs. Their industrial clusters—dominated by electronics, machinery, and chemicals—are tightly linked to international supply chains. Their downstream role in processing and assembling imported inputs from inland regions also indirectly embeds upstream emissions from central and western China.

Figure 8 further disaggregates how local and foreign emissions were allocated across different GVC routes for each urban agglomeration. In coastal regions such as the Pearl River Delta and Yangtze River Delta, >40% of local emissions were embodied in route 1 exports (final goods) to major trading partners. These regions, as China's core export-oriented clusters, transfer a substantial portion of production-based emissions to international markets via final goods trade—driven by openness, favorable energy mix, and demand from carbon-regulated economies.

In inland urban agglomerations, such as Northern Slope of Tianshan Mountain and Hohhot−Baotou−Ordos−Yulin, 35 and 37% of local emissions, respectively, were embodied in route 3 exports destined for the five major trading partners. Intermediate products from these regions (e.g. ores, energy, and raw materials) primarily flow through domestic supply chains to coastal manufacturing hubs, where they are further processed and eventually exported as final goods to third countries, illustrating a structural mismatch between industrial specialization and carbon accountability. Consequently, the carbon emissions generated during the initial production stages in inland cities are effectively embodied in intermediate goods and ultimately transferred to overseas end consumers along the industrial chain.

Embodied emissions in routes 5 and 6 refer to the carbon emissions embodied in final products and intermediate products consumed by trading partners, thereby transferring the responsibility for these emissions to the importing countries. However, under production-based accounting, these emissions are still attributed to China. For most urban agglomerations, the share of emissions embodied in route 5 was substantially higher than that in route 6. For instance, in 2017, emissions embodied in route 5 accounted for 88, 87, and 84% of total foreign emissions in the Chengdu−Chongqing, Central Plains, and Pearl River Delta regions, respectively (Fig. 8). This pattern reflects the fact that Chinese urban agglomerations are located in the energy-intensive downstream stages of GVC, such as assembly, processing, and final goods production.

Discussion

This study maps the value-added and carbon flows of 313 Chinese cities within global production networks, revealing distinct emission roles across GVC segments. Eastern and coastal cities account for a large share of emissions through final goods exports, whereas inland and less industrialized regions contribute markedly less, highlighting a spatial mismatch between carbon responsibilities and economic returns. This spatial-functional differentiation extends the “pollution shelter” effect to the city level across multiple industries (e.g. information and communication technology, electrical and electronic equipment) (28, 29). By linking city-level industrial structure with GVC position, this study advances the carbon inequality literature by showing how asymmetric emission responsibilities emerge within national economies (30).

Cities located upstream of supply chains—particularly heavy manufacturing and resource-based cities—are constrained by structural and economic lock-in associated with long-lived capital investments and specialized infrastructure. In early stages of GVC participation, such cities are typically confined to resource extraction and assembly manufacturing, limiting short-term emission reductions through efficiency improvements alone. As a result, emission mitigation in these cities depends primarily on industrial diversification, value chain extension, and technological transformation, rather than incremental efficiency gains (31). While reshoring may alter the spatial distribution of emission-intensive activities, it does not fundamentally relax these structural constraints. For energy-intensive cities, sustained emission reductions therefore require long-term transitions toward cleaner production technologies and alternative energy inputs.

Although carbon leakage is typically conceptualized as a cross-border issue at the global and regional levels (32, 33), our findings reveal that substantial emission externalization also occurs within domestic trade networks—echoing insights from He et al. (34). This internal outsourcing reflects asymmetric carbon geographies, where economic development in one region imposes environmental costs on others (35). Decarbonization efforts by leading cities are critical, but broader participation requires interregional coordination and differentiated responsibility-sharing mechanisms. The result also reflects greater domestic demand for carbon-intensive goods relative to international trade, but this does not necessarily imply that domestic consumers have lower environmental standards, as consumption patterns are primarily driven by economic structures and policies rather than the level of scrutiny (36). We recommend future research integrate household consumption survey to investigate consumer standards to bridge the premise. More importantly, our city-level analysis reveals that cities within the same province, or even within the same urban agglomeration, can occupy fundamentally different positions in GVCs, with markedly distinct pathways of carbon outsourcing across simple and complex trade linkages. For instance, some cities, such as Chengdu, function primarily as processing and redistribution hubs, whereas others (e.g. Chongqing) serve as upstream suppliers of carbon-intensive intermediates, despite sharing similar administrative or geographic classifications. This divergence originates from economic specialization and sunk investments. By treating cities as functional nodes rather than administrative decision-makers, the city-level framework uncovers how urban specialization and production roles, rather than provincial boundaries or coastal location alone, shapes the structure of embodied carbon flows. This perspective provides insights for understanding the microgeographies of carbon leakage within domestic supply chains.

Conventional production-based carbon accounting obscures cities’ roles within fragmented globally supply chains (25, 26). Consumption-based approaches that trace emissions embodied in final demand provide a necessary complement. For most cities, these emissions primarily originated from domestic supply networks because of China's self-sufficient supply chains, leaving most responsibility domestically internalized but unequally distributed. These cross-border “shadow emissions” remain systematically hidden under conventional geographic accounting principle (37). While the concept of “global cities” emphasizes economic integration (38), it fails to capture transboundary transmission of environmental externalities (39). Our framework addresses this gap by mapping multiscale carbon flows and revealing the network of emission responsibilities (40).

Trade structures matter. The export structure, the coastal city clusters with emissions driven by final goods exports; the inland city clusters with emissions driven by the flow of intermediate goods to coastal cities before being reexported abroad, amplifies upstream emissions in regions with limited capacity to decarbonize and disperses environmental risks across fragmented governance regimes. These patterns challenge assumptions of automatic decoupling between economic upgrading and emissions reduction, highlighting the asymmetries in urban decarbonization trajectories. Although emissions embodied in routes 5 and 6—associated with goods consumed abroad—could theoretically be assigned to importing countries under consumption-based principles, current frameworks still attribute them to Chinese cities. This highlights the governance gap in attributing emissions within GVCs, particularly for cities operating as “global factories.”

While cities themselves do not directly determine industrial location or production decisions, they serve as key implementation and coordination units through which production, labor, and infrastructure systems are organized. In China, local governments possess strong implementation levers, including land-use regulation, industrial park development and oversight of state-owned firms, which shape how national strategies and market forces materialize locally. However, a city's fundamental role in the GVC remains largely constrained by historical development, resource endowments, and sunk investments. Resource-based or heavy manufacturing cities, for example, cannot readily transform into high-tech or service-oriented hubs without prolonged structural change. Thus, analyzing cities within GVCs captures how industrial structures and trade linkages manifest spatially in embodied emissions. This approach treats cities not as autonomous decision-makers but as functional intermediaries in global production networks—entities that reflect the spatial organization of industries and the geography of carbon responsibilities. This perspective aligns with recent scholarship that highlights cities as important governance scale for decarbonization and emission accountability (41).

These findings carry several policy implications. First, upstream resource and manufacturing cities that are constrained by structural lock-in should be prioritized for support via clean energy investments, fiscal transfers, and ecological compensation. Local governments can further establish partnerships with national ministries to access green financing and technology transfer mechanisms. Second, downstream consumer and export-oriented cities should assume greater responsibility for outsourced emissions by embedding carbon intensity metrics into procurement, industrial policy, and environmental regulation. The key challenges for these cities lie in governance and responsibility allocation rather than production constraints. Such measures can internalize externalized carbon costs and reduce the incentive to shift emission-intensive activities upstream. Third, addressing carbon leakage across cities requires overcoming interjurisdictional governance barriers. Isolated city-level actions are insufficient because urban carbon flows frequently cross administrative boundaries. Institutionalized intercity coordination mechanisms, such as cross-regional emission disclosure platforms, shared carbon accounting standards, and joint decarbonization initiatives, are essential to align incentives between upstream producers and downstream beneficiaries. Although national policies set the overall framework for emission reduction, cities play a decisive role in implementation through industrial planning, infrastructure investment, and land-use regulation. As such, city-level analysis provides actionable insights for local policymakers to target emission hotspots and coordinate cross-regional decarbonization.

This study provides a static snapshot of the carbon transfer network across Chinese cities based on the latest available city-level MRIO table, which is limited to 2017 due to data constraints. The year captures China's economy shortly before carbon market pilot policy and trade frictions. However, the use of a single-year dataset limits our ability to capture temporal dynamics, such as industrial upgrading, relocation, and decarbonization trends over time. Additionally, subsequent policy shifts, economic restructuring, and external shocks—such as the expansion of China's national carbon market and the COVID-19 pandemic—may have significantly reshaped production and emission patterns, which are not reflected in the 2017 data. Recent developments, such as the rise of renewable energy in inland China, may have altered emissions patterns and cities’ roles in GVCs. Future work with updated data is essential to capture these dynamics. Additionally, the nested MRIO framework illustrates intercity and international production linkages, but it does not fully capture firm-to-firm linkages, which may affect whether emissions are attributed to actual producers or to recorded exporters. Trade flows between cities and foreign markets are allocated based on proportionality assumptions derived from sectoral output structures. This approach may abstract from firm-specific routing strategies and contractual arrangements, particularly in complex GVC linkages. Future study may refine sectoral alignment and integrate more granular service trade and firm-level data to assess the sensitivity of the underlying modeling assumptions. Lastly, the sensitivity analysis applies a uniform ±10% adjustment to emission intensities across all cities and sectors, but it is unlikely to substantially alter spatial patterns, as proportional shocks preserve the underlying structure. In practice, emission intensities evolve heterogeneously across cities and sectors due to differences in technology, regulation, and industrial composition. Future research could incorporate city- or sector-specific emission trajectories to fully assess the robustness of spatial emission patterns under heterogeneous shocks.

Materials and methods

Building the environmentally extended nested China city-level-global MRIO table

EE-MRIO models are widely used to assess environmental footprints and emissions embodied in trade. To quantify the carbon footprint of Chinese cities within global supply chains, we construct a nested MRIO framework by integrating a city-level MRIO model of 313 Chinese cities (2017) into the ADB global MRIO table. Global emissions data are sourced from the Eora, while city-level emissions in China are derived from the inventory of Shan et al. (42).

As China is one of the 62 regions represented in the ADB MRIO framework, we first disaggregated the Chinese IOT from the ADB MRIO into a national MRIO with city-level resolution. According to Meng et al. (43) and Ni and Xia (44), we applied the fixed proportion coefficient allocation method to proportionally allocate Chinese city-level exports and imports to other economies. The nested China city-level-global MRIO table assumes that the intermediate input coefficients of foreign inputs for each Chinese city are equivalent to those of China in the ADB MRIO. Meanwhile, the allocation coefficients for exports from each Chinese city to foreign destinations are assumed to be the same as those of China in the ADB MRIO. Concordance had to be made to reduce the sector resolution to 16 sectors before the nesting process. Thus, the nested China city-level-global MRIO table consists of 375 regions and 16 socioeconomic sectors for the year 2017, which is illustrated in Table S1. The nested processing and concordance matrix details are given in Supporting Information (SI, Text S1). More importantly, city- and sector-specific emission intensities were incorporated into the nested MRIO framework to capture regional heterogeneity in energy structure and production efficiency.

Tracing city-level carbon emissions in GVCs under an integrated accounting framework

This paper draws on the conceptual framework proposed by Meng et al. (26), which integrates GVCs and emissions embodied in trade. We trace Chinese cities’ carbon emissions from the value-added trade perspective. The first accounting system traces emissions from upstream to downstream along GVCs as shown in Fig. S6A (for details, see Text S2). This is based on the logic that a city's production-based emissions are embodied in all downstream trading partners (including itself) via different GVCs routes, and finally absorbed by local, domestic, and foreign consumers. It aims to examine the consumption-driven carbon emissions of Chinese cities and identify the ultimate demand sources responsible for their CO2 footprint.

The second accounting system tracks city-level emissions from downstream to upstream along GVCs as depicted in Fig. S6B. It is because the production of a specific product may induce emissions in all upstream trading partners directly and indirectly based on backward industrial linkages. The carbon emissions generated during the production of final goods in cities can be attributed locally or to external regions. It aims to investigate the emission sources associated with cities’ integration into the GVC.

Due to vertical specialization, the segmentation of production processes has led to a rapid increase in cross-regional intermediate goods trade with repeated transactions, making GVC trade complex. The third accounting system disaggregates emissions generated from the production of a city's gross exports into different GVC routes, as illustrated in Fig. S6C. The first four routes on the left side of Fig. S6C represent emissions within the exporting cities, which are a by-product generating local economic benefits. The last two routes on the right side of Fig. S6C demonstrate emissions within foreign countries that provide intermediate goods for the exporting cities and create GDP for foreign countries. In this study, emissions of other domestic cities in supplying imported goods are not considered because we aim to discover how carbon emissions from a city's total exports are realized through different routes and whether they contribute to domestic or foreign GDP. Detailed mathematical proofs and explanations of various GVC routes are recorded in Text S2.

Data

We construct a large-scale EE-NMRIO nested by the ADB MRIO and China’s city-level MRIO to accurately identify the carbon emissions of Chinese cities based on forward industrial linkages, backward industrial linkages, and value-added perspective. ADB published MRIO tables with 35 industries and five final-demand categories for 62 economies and the rest of the world from 2007 to 2022. China’s city-level MRIO is sourced from Carbon Emission Account and Datasets (CEADs), consisting of 313 regions and 42 sectors for the years 2012 and 2017. The 313 regions covered include 309 cities and Yunnan, Tibet, Qinghai, and Hainan provinces, treated at the same level as cities owing to missing data. Some cities, such as Dazhou, Bazhong, Ngawa, and Guang’an in Sichuan, are missing data owing to the unavailability of emission data. The data of city-level and foreign carbon emissions is obtained from the CEADs (45, 46) and environmental accounts consistent with the Eora database. Considering the concordance of sectors in China’s city-level MRIO table and ADB MRIO, we aggregate to 16 sectors in the EE-NMRIO. Thus, the EE-NMRIO table contains 375 regions (including 313 Chinese cities and 62 regions) and 16 sectors for the year 2017 (Tables S2–S4).

Cities were classified into six distinct industry-dominated types: service-based cities, heavy manufacturing cities, light manufacturing cities, energy cities, agricultural cities, and high-tech cities (Fig. S3). This classification was based on the sectoral composition of city-level GDP (47). We first aggregated 42 distinct economic sectors into the five specified categories and calculated the value-added share of each sector. Next, we applied the K-means algorithm using the Euclidean distance metric, based on the GDP proportions of the five sectoral groups in 2017.

We also aggregated cities into 12 national urban agglomerations: Triangle of Central China, Harbin−Changchun, Chengdu−Chongqing, Yangtze River Delta, Central Plains, Beibu Gulf, Guanzhong Plains, Hohhot−Baotou−Ordos−Yulin, Lanzhou−Xining, Pearl River Delta, Beijing−Tianjin−Hebei, and Northern Slope of Tianshan Mountains (Table S3). Meanwhile, we selected five main trading partners from 62 countries based on their trade volume with China. The main trading partners are aggregated into the United States; the EU members and the United Kingdom; the ASEAN members; Japan and South Korea; and India, Bangladesh, and Pakistan (Table S2).

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by adjusting the emission intensities of two high-carbon sectors, Petroleum, Chemical, and Non-Metallic Mineral Products (S6) and Metal Products (S7), by ±10% for all cities. The results show heterogeneous impacts across Chinese cities. Heavy manufacturing cities exhibit the most increase of 4.3% in the emissions induced by demand from other domestic cities. Liaoyang, Xinyu, and Anyang are the top three affected heavy manufacturing cities. The results are summarized in Table S5.
