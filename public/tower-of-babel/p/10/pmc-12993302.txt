Analysis of clinical features of AECOPD at different altitudes and GOLD stages in high-altitude regions
Authors: Hongen Zhou, Macuo Bian, Xiaoxuan Huang, Yangzong Deji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo analyse the clinical features of AECOPD (acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) at different altitudes and GOLD (global initiative of chronic obstructive lung disease) stages in high-altitude regions.MethodThis was a retrospective cross-sectional study. Patients diagnosed with AECOPD through pulmonary function tests and hospitalised in People’s Hospital of the Tibet Autonomous Region from September 2018 to March 2024 were included. Patients were divided into three groups according to their native residential altitude prior to onset: low-altitude group (169 cases, 2700–3700 m), mid-altitude group (77 cases, 3700–4000 m) and high-altitude group (97 cases, 4000–4800 m). Patients were also classified into three groups according to their FEV1%pred in pulmonary function test on admission: mild (91 cases, FEV1%pred ≥80%), moderate (171 cases, 50%≤FEV1%pred <80%) and severe/very severe (81 cases, FEV1%pred <50%).Result(1) Altitude comparison: the mid-altitude group showed lower values for age (66.49±8.13 years), hospital length of stay (9.79±3.20 days) and time since diagnosis (1.89±1.73 years) compared with the low-altitude group. On the other hand, the mid-altitude group showed higher SPO2 (82.35±5.17%) and pulmonary fibrosis prevalence (40.26%) than the low-altitude group. The high-altitude group had higher rates of emphysema (56.70%) and pulmonary hypertension (51.55%) compared with the low-altitude group. The high-altitude group showed lower levels of B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP) (185.76±195.19 pg/mL), red blood cell count (RBC) (5.77±0.97×1012/L), D-dimer (D-D) (1.01±1.33 mg/L) and fibrinogen degradation product (FDP) (3.11±1.38 mg/L) than the low-altitude group. The high-altitude group had a longer time since diagnosis (2.63±3.39 years) than the mid-altitude group and lower SPO2 (80.01±7.60%), rate of pulmonary fibrosis (22.68%) and BNP level (185.76±195.19 pg/mL) compared with the mid-altitude group. These differences were statistically significant (p<0.05). No significant differences were found for other variables between altitude groups (p>0.05). (2) GOLD staging comparison: the moderate group had higher values for the time since diagnosis (2.61±2.95 years), prevalence of emphysema (50.29%), pulmonary fibrosis (28.07%), pulmonary hypertension (45.61%), cor pulmonale (40.94%), and levels of C-reactive protein (CRP) (23.89±34.01 mg/L) and procalcitonin (PCT) (0.32±0.71 ng/mL) than the mild group. The moderate and severe/very severe groups had lower SPO2 (80.77±6.71%, 78.38±8.31%) than the mild group. The severe/very severe group had higher rates of emphysema (54.31%), pulmonary bullae (28.40%), pulmonary hypertension (59.26%), cor pulmonale (56.79%), and levels of CRP (20.92±29.92 mg/L) and PCT (0.81±3.43 ng/mL) than the mild group. The severe/very severe group had a shorter time since diagnosis (2.27±1.97 years) than the moderate group. The severe/very severe group also had a higher percentage of males (72.84%), pulmonary hypertension (59.26%), cor pulmonale (56.79%), and levels of BNP (392.50±728.31 pg/mL) and PCT (0.81±3.43 ng/mL) compared with the moderate group. These differences were statistically significant (p<0.05). No significant differences were found for other variables between GOLD groups (p>0.05).ConclusionIn high-altitude regions, as the altitude increases, body mass index, the proportion of male patients, rate of emphysema and pulmonary hypertension become more prominent in AECOPD patients. Higher GOLD stages are associated with longer hospital stays, higher smoking indices and greater prevalence of emphysema, pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale. Lower SPO2 is associated with higher levels of RBC, haemoglobin, haematocrit (HCT), CRP, PCT, white blood cell count and neutrophil%. However, as native residential altitude increases, BNP, RBC, HCT, D-D and FDP decrease.Keywords: COPD Exacerbations; Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive; COPD

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

This study found that patients with AECOPD at higher altitudes had a higher body mass index and a higher proportion of males. With increasing altitude, emphysema and pulmonary hypertension are more pronounced. At the same time, it was found that with the increase of altitude, the related indicators of B-type natriuretic peptide and D-dimer decreased.

HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

In this study, the geographical factor of ‘altitude’ was incorporated into the clinical decision-making framework of AECOPD in plateau areas, prompting clinicians to shift from the thinking of ‘disease severity (global initiative of chronic obstructive lung disease grade)’ to the two-dimensional assessment model of ‘altitude×disease severity’. This shift is expected to improve the prognosis of patients with high-altitude AECOPD.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a respiratory disease primarily characterised by irreversible airflow restriction, with the number of patients reaching approximately 200 million globally. In the accelerating trend of population ageing, the prevalence of COPD remains high and continues to rise.1 As demonstrated by numerous studies, the progression of COPD can affect multiple systems including the respiratory, circulatory, digestive and endocrine systems and eventually lead to the failure of multiple organs and systems. Over the past 20–30 years, many researchers worldwide have focused on the prevention, control and treatment of COPD. The risk factors include smoking, biofuel combustion and air pollution, while main factors affecting prognosis include decreased pulmonary ventilation, hypoxaemia, hypercapnia and pulmonary hypertension.2 The unique geographic environment in high-altitude regions, characterised by low temperature, low pressure and low oxygen levels, may have a more pronounced impact on COPD patients who are exposed to these conditions for extended periods. This study analyses the clinical data of patients diagnosed with acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) and hospitalised in People’s Hospital of the Tibet Autonomous Region from September 2018 to March 2024. It aims to explore the clinical differences in AECOPD patients at different altitudes as well as of different severity in Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) staging.

Subjects and methods

Study subjects

Subjects: inpatients diagnosed with AECOPD through pulmonary function tests in People’s Hospital of the Tibet Autonomous Region from September 2018 to March 2024. No informed consent form was required for all enrolled cases. No patients or members of the public were involved in any stage of the design, implementation or dissemination of the results of this study. This research primarily involved a retrospective analysis of existing anonymised data; therefore, no patients or members of the public were invited to assist in defining the research focus, designing the study protocol, recruiting participants or interpreting/disseminating the findings.

Inclusion criteria: all COPD diagnoses met the diagnostic criteria outlined in the 2024 revision of the GOLD guidelines.3 The criteria for an acute exacerbation were an event occurring within 14 days, characterised by increased dyspnoea and/or cough and sputum production, possibly accompanied by tachypnoea and/or tachycardia, and typically associated with increased local and systemic inflammation caused by infection, pollution or other factors that damage the airways. All participants were indigenous Tibetan people who have lived in the Tibetan plateau region for generations.

Methods

Grouping: (1) A total of 343 patients were included in this study. Following the established grouping methods by altitude from a previous study,4 patients were divided into three groups based on their native residential altitude before the onset of the disease: low-altitude group (2700 m≤altitude ≤ 3700 m) with 169 cases including 107 males and 62 females, mid-altitude group (3700 m<altitude ≤ 4000 m) with 77 cases including 50 males and 27 females, and high-altitude group (4000 m<altitude ≤ 4800 m) with 97 cases including 64 males and 33 females. (2) The 343 patients were also grouped based on their FEV1%pred in pulmonary function test on admission, following the GOLD staging standard. The mild group included 91 cases (FEV1%pred ≥80%) with 57 males and 34 females. The moderate group included 171 cases (50%≤FEV1%pred <80%) with 102 males and 69 females. The severe/very severe group included 81 cases (FEV1%pred <50%) with 59 males and 22 females.

Pulmonary arterial pressure was measured by Philips EPIQCVx colour Doppler ultrasound diagnostic system (USA).

Diagnosis of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF): diagnosis was based on the 2022 clinical practice guidelines for adult IPF and progressive pulmonary fibrosis:7 (1) Patients whose chest high-resolution CT (HRCT) demonstrates a usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP) pattern would be diagnosed with IPF without requiring surgical lung biopsy. UIP was defined as subpleural, basal predominant fibrosis with honeycombing, in the absence of other diagnostic features.8 (2) Among patients whose chest HRCT demonstrates a possible UIP pattern, those with clinical risk factors (aged 60 or older, male, history of smoking, etc) would be diagnosed with IPF following multidisciplinary discussion without the need for lung biopsy confirmation. Possible UIP was defined as having subpleural, basal predominant fibrosis without definite honeycombing. (3) Patients whose imaging findings are not diagnostic underwent surgical lung biopsy for a definitive diagnosis of IPF.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS V.26.0 software. For measured data that followed a normal distribution and had equal variance, the LSD test was used for group comparisons, with results expressed as Mean±SD. For measured data that did not follow a normal distribution, the Kruskal-Wallis rank-sum test was used. Categorical data were analysed using the χ2 test, and proportions between groups were expressed as percentages (%). A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

AECOPD is a major factor for mortality among patients with COPD. COPD patients experience approximately 0.5 to 3.5 acute exacerbations per year, which significantly impacts their quality of life, disease progression and socio-economic burden. AECOPD is a critical determinant of the health status and prognosis of COPD patients.9

A nationwide survey conducted in China at the beginning of the 21st century, spanning 4 years, revealed that the prevalence of COPD was markedly higher in men than in women among those aged 40 and over, with a ratio of 12.4% in men versus 5.1% in women.10 Another Chinese study showed that female COPD patients generally exhibit milder clinical symptoms, better lung function indicators and milder complications compared with male patients, which is significantly related to changes in sex hormones after menopause.11 Female patients often present with airway lesions, while male patients typically show emphysema. Moreover, results in arterial blood gas analysis and the rate of decline in lung function in male patients are more severe compared with their female counterparts. In this study by altitude group, the proportion of men was 63.31% in the low-altitude group, 64.94% in the mid-altitude group and 65.98% in the high-altitude group. In different GOLD stages, the proportion of male patients was 64.64% in the mild group, 59.64% in the moderate group and 72.84% in the severe/very severe group, with men being significantly more prevalent than women in each group, consistent with previous findings.

Hypoxia is a risk factor for elevated vascular pressure. Prolonged exposure to a hypoxic environment results in a progressive reduction in the synthesis of nitric oxide by endothelial cells, which weakens the vasodilatory effects of nitric oxide and fails to counteract the vasoconstrictive effects of endothelin, leading to persistent pulmonary vascular constriction. This reduces the oxygen-carrying capacity of red blood cells and decreases blood oxygen saturation,12 severely impacting pulmonary function. This study demonstrated that SPO2% significantly decreases with increasing altitude and GOLD stage. At altitudes below 500 m, COPD patients are affected by prolonged hypoxaemia, leading to compensatory increases in erythropoietin and secondary erythrocytosis over time. In the Tibetan Plateau, COPD patients are not only affected by the disease-induced hypoxic state but also by the environmental factors of low pressure and low oxygen, resulting in more severe hypoxaemia. However, in this study, the change patterns of RBC, haemoglobin and HCT among different GOLD stages were consistent with those in plain regions. No significant differences in RBC, haemoglobin and HCT indicators across different altitude groups were found, which may be related to the adaptive responses of individuals living in high-altitude environments.

COPD prevalence is expected to rise progressively over the next 40 years, with the number of deaths due to COPD complications exceeding 5.4 million annually.13 The prevalence of pulmonary hypertension is significantly higher in patients with end-stage COPD.14 Reports indicate that the likelihood of developing chronic pulmonary hypertension in COPD patients is significantly higher in high-altitude regions compared with low-altitude areas, while pulmonary hypertension is positively correlated with the development of cor pulmonale and significantly related to prognosis and outcomes of COPD patients.15 16 This study shows that higher altitudes and more severe GOLD stages are associated with a greater prevalence of pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale. Pulmonary fibrosis is prevalent in older populations, while its causes and mechanisms as a comorbidity of COPD remain unclear. The incidence and mortality of COPD combined with pulmonary fibrosis are significantly higher in high-altitude and hypoxic conditions. An animal study indicated that high-altitude hypoxia can lead to various degrees of changes in bronchial mucosa, alveolar epithelium and interstitial lung tissue, eventually resulting in pulmonary interstitial fibrosis.17 This study found that the incidence of pulmonary fibrosis in COPD patients was significantly higher in mid and high-altitude groups compared with low-altitude groups. In recent years, researchers have increasingly recognised that the emphysema phenotype of COPD is usually accompanied by damage to the small alveoli at the distal ends of pulmonary airways, leading to lung tissue hyperinflation and reduced plasticity.18 Type II alveolar epithelial cells (AECII) are crucial to alveolar formation and maintenance of tissue structure, playing key roles not only in regulating surfactant metabolism but also in preserving alveolar homeostasis.19 Direct evidence from mouse models indicates that apoptosis of alveolar wall cells can drive emphysematous changes, and emphysema may be exacerbated through repeated induction of AECII apoptosis or via recruitment of inflammatory cells.20 Li et al observed that rat lung tissue showed interstitial proliferation and emphysema under simulated high altitude of 6000 m in a hypobaric oxygen chamber for 1 day.21 22 On the other hand, they were moved to the condition of 4500 m and stayed for 30 days. Pulmonary interstitial oedema was significantly reduced, with clear alveolar spaces and near-normal alveolar septal structures, although some areas still exhibited interstitial proliferation and mild emphysema. This indicates that chronic hypoxic environments can lead to increased lung tissue damage and tendency towards emphysema. This study found that the prevalence of emphysema in COPD patients was significantly higher in the high altitude group compared with low altitude group, which was consistent with these experimental results. COPD with pulmonary bullae is considered a diffuse lesion, which can worsen lung function impairment, reduce functional reserve and impair the body’s adaptive regulatory capacity, usually leading to disease progression with increased dyspnoea, reduced quality of life and increased mortality.23

BNP is a hormone synthesised and secreted by the ventricles with natriuretic, diuretic and vasodilatory effects. It can lower blood pressure, inhibit myocardial cell proliferation and compensatory hypertrophy, and counteract myocardial fibrosis.24 In high-altitude and hypoxic environments, human serum BNP levels increase with altitude. Hill et al25 found that BNP levels can rise by 70% during chronic hypoxia in both humans and rodents. Our data showed elevated BNP levels across different groups, but the degree of abnormality was more pronounced in the low-altitude group compared with the mid and high-altitude groups, which was inconsistent with previous studies. This discrepancy may be due to the adaptive tolerance developed in prolonged oxygen deprivation by individuals in mid to high-altitude environments, where BNP levels do not rise significantly in response to progressive hypoxia. Further research is needed to confirm this hypothesis. In addition, this study strictly excluded patients with left ventricular dysfunction, thereby directly eliminating a major factor contributing to elevated BNP levels. This may be a key reason for the lower BNP levels in this cohort. This study did not systematically evaluate the renal function of all patients, and therefore the potential influence of BNP accumulation due to renal insufficiency cannot be ruled out. Reasons underlying these findings require further investigation. C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT) and NE% are indicators of inflammatory levels in the body. CRP, which increases rapidly during acute inflammation, reflects infection progression and prognosis but is a non-specific marker.26 PCT, an inflammatory marker without hormonal activity, is closely linked to pulmonary function impairment in patients with COPD and reflects the severity of infection to some extent.27 Data analysis in this study showed that CRP, PCT and NE% levels increased with higher GOLD stages, consistent with previous findings. Although D-D and FDPs are indicators of coagulation status, studies have shown that long-term hypoxia and infection can cause the release of FDP by hepatocytes and macrophages, and elevated D-D levels are associated with intravascular and extravascular coagulation in acute and chronic lung injury and can further result from increased fibrinolysis during severe infections.28 While our data showed that D-D and FDP levels increased in COPD patients at high altitudes to different extents, these levels did not rise with altitude and even showed an inverse trend. This may be due to the less severe hypoxic state in the low-altitude group compared with the mid and high-altitude groups. In the low-altitude group, as hypoxia worsens progressively, the release of D-D and FDP increases. However, in the mid and high-altitude groups, where relatively severe hypoxia is a long-standing condition, this increase of release is less pronounced. This phenomenon requires further investigation.

This study is a single-centre, retrospective, observational study conducted in the Tibetan plateau region, with a relatively limited sample size. Owing to incomplete documentation of symptom scores and precise numbers of prior exacerbations in some patients, the GOLD symptom/exacerbation history classification could not be applied. Furthermore, certain indicators (eg, biomarkers) were measured at a single time point and therefore do not capture their dynamic changes over time. Although multivariable adjustments were performed, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (such as medication adherence and differential environmental exposures) may still have influenced the outcomes. Given the region-specific nature of this study, its findings are intended primarily for reference by researchers in relevant fields. Future research on AECOPD in high-altitude settings should adopt multicentre, large-scale and long-term prospective designs to provide more robust evidence to guide clinical management and related investigations.

Conclusion

In summary, AECOPD is a common chronic disease in high-altitude areas and has received attention from researchers. This study found significant differences in age, length of hospital stays, time since diagnosis, SPO2%, emphysema, pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension, BNP, RBC, D-D and FDP among different altitude groups. There were also notable differences in gender, SPO2%, emphysema, pulmonary fibrosis, pulmonary bullae, pulmonary hypertension, cor pulmonale, BNP, CRP and PCT across different GOLD stages.
