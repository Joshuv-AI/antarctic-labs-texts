The toxicity of psychedelic LSD derivatives: 1-acetyl-LSD (ALD-52), 1-propionyl-LSD (1P-LSD), 1-butyryl-LSD (1B-LSD), 1-valeryl-LSD (1V-LSD) and 1-cyclopropylmethanoyl-LSD (1cP-LSD)—prediction of toxicological parameters relevant to clinical and forensic toxicology using multi-in silico approach
Authors: Kamil Jurowski, Alicja Krośniak, Damian Kobylarz, Oktawia Fijałkowska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lysergamide analogs such as ALD-52, 1P-LSD, 1B-LSD, 1 V-LSD, and 1cP-LSD have emerged as new psychoactive substances (NPS) with hallucinogenic potential, yet their toxicological profiles remain largely unexplored. In this study, a comprehensive in silico assessment was conducted using multiple platforms (e.g., ADMETlab 3.0, Percepta, STopTox, ProTox 3.0, VEGA, TEST 5.1.2) to predict critical toxicological parameters relevant to clinical and forensic toxicology. Acute toxicity was predicted for all compounds, with LD50 values in rats ranging from 49 to 85 mg/kg (Percepta) and oral/inhalation exposure identified as the most concerning routes. Organ-specific toxicity highlighted elevated pulmonary risk for 1 V-LSD (92%) and 1cP-LSD (81%), and highest predicted hematotoxicity for 1P-LSD (76%) and 1B-LSD (75%). Genotoxicity alerts were consistently identified for ALD-52 and 1cP-LSD (up to 90% predicted probability, Percepta), while OCHEM classified all compounds as non-mutagenic. Cardiotoxicity assessment revealed the strongest hERG channel inhibition for 1 V-LSD (IC50 = 1.4 µM, Percepta), suggesting elevated proarrhythmic potential. Dermal and ocular irritation risks were uniformly low across platforms. The results support that structural modifications at the N-1-acyl position influence toxicity patterns, particularly affecting cardiopulmonary and genotoxic endpoints. These findings underscore the utility of in silico methods for early risk assessment and prioritization of lysergamide-based NPS for further toxicological evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00204-025-04255-1.

Despite their structural similarity to LSD, these compounds remain poorly characterized with respect to toxicological risk and long-term safety. Their ambiguous legal status and availability have contributed to their spread on online and gray markets, as N1-substitutions allow them to circumvent existing regulations (Halberstadt et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2023) (Brandt et al. 2022). Several studies have shown that many N1-acylated LSD derivatives undergo in vivo deacylation, releasing LSD into systemic circulation. Halberstadt et al. demonstrated rapid conversion of ALD-52, 1P-LSD, and 1B-LSD to LSD in animal models, explaining their behavioral potency despite reduced in vitro 5-HT2A affinity (Halberstadt et al. 2020). Likewise, 1 V-LSD and 1cP-LSD induce head-twitch responses in rodents, although at higher doses, consistent with lower intrinsic potency or slower conversion kinetics (Brandt et al. 2016, 2019, 2022).

From a forensic toxicology standpoint, the analytical detection of these compounds has advanced rapidly, with modern LC–MS/MS methods enabling sub-nanogram quantification (Wachełko et al. 2025; Adamowicz and Mulka 2022). Nevertheless, this analytical progress contrasts sharply with the scarcity of toxicological data. Case reports remain anecdotal and typically lack confirmed blood concentrations or toxicokinetic profiles. Structural variations, such as longer acyl chains or bulkier substituents—may also influence lipophilicity, metabolic stability and blood–brain barrier permeability in unpredictable ways, making risk assessment particularly challenging.

In this context, in silico approaches such as QSAR modeling, molecular docking, and ADMET prediction have become valuable tools for preliminary risk assessment. Modern computational toxicology is increasingly recognized as a key component of the “new vision” in chemical safety (Leist et al. 2008), offering a promising way to bridge the gap between analytical detection and mechanistic toxicological understanding for NPS, including N1-substituted lysergamides.

Without a toxicological dataset, clinical management of overdoses, forensic interpretation, and public health risk assessment remain largely speculative. Existing case reports are anecdotal and often lack confirmed blood concentrations or toxicokinetic profiles. Structural modifications introduced by different acyl side chains may unpredictably affect lipophilicity, blood–brain barrier permeability, metabolic stability, and receptor activity. For example, longer-chain derivatives such as 1 V-LSD may display prolonged half-lives due to slower deacylation, whereas bulkier groups like the cyclopropyl moiety in 1cP-LSD may introduce distinct metabolic or off-target effects. In this context, the present study systematically evaluates the predicted toxicological parameters of five N1-acylated LSD analogs (ALD-52, 1P-LSD, 1B-LSD, 1 V-LSD, 1cP-LSD) using a multi-platform in silico approach. Due to the scarcity of experimental toxicological data, we applied a multi-in silico approach integrating outputs from multiple prediction platforms, including SwissADME, pkCSM, ADMETlab 2.0, DruMAP, ProTox-II, and vNN-ADMET. This multi-tool approach allowed us to assess the consistency of predictions, identify shared and compound-specific toxicity alerts, and illustrate the practical value of in silico methods for early-phase risk profiling of lysergamide-based NPS. This integrative strategy reflects the broader shift toward computational toxicology as a rapid and ethical alternative when in vivo data are unavailable or impractical.

Methods

Qualitative in silico methods

STopTox

To evaluate acute systemic and topical toxicity of the investigated LSD derivatives, we employed the STopTox platform (https://stoptox.mml.unc.edu/), a publicly available computational tool developed as an alternative to animal-based “6-pack” tests. STopTox integrates rigorously validated QSAR models covering six standard toxicological endpoints: acute oral, dermal, and inhalation toxicity. The models were developed based on the largest curated public datasets available, strictly following OECD principles for QSAR model development and validation (Tropsha 2010; Gómez-Jiménez et al. 2018; Borba et al. 2022).

Each endpoint-specific model was constructed using curated experimental in vivo data and binary classification based on Globally Harmonized System (GHS) hazard categories. Molecular descriptors—including MACCS keys, Mordred descriptors, and Morgan fingerprints were calculated for all compounds and served as inputs for Random Forest classifiers implemented via the scikit-learn library. Models were trained and validated using fivefold external cross-validation, and performance was further confirmed by Y-randomization and applicability domain estimation using the z-cutoff method. Predictions are generated by consensus voting (majority rule) across models trained with different descriptor sets (Tropsha and Golbraikh 2007).

For each LSD derivative, chemical structures were submitted in SMILES format, and predictions were retrieved for all six endpoints. Each prediction included the toxicity classification (toxic/non-toxic), the model confidence (based on internal ensemble agreement), and the applicability domain status. Visualization of fragment-based contributions to toxicity was available via atom-level contribution maps, highlighting structural features most responsible for predicted outcomes (Riniker and Landrum 2013; Jurowski and Kobylarz 2025).

ADMETlab 3

As part of the multi-tool in silico strategy, we applied ADMETlab 3.0 (https://admetlab3.scbdd.com/) to predict selected toxicological endpoints relevant to human health risk assessment. ADMETlab 3.0 is a freely available, web-based platform designed for high-throughput prediction of absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) properties. For the purpose of this study, only endpoints related to toxicity were considered.

The platform integrates a multitask Directed Message Passing Neural Network (DMPNN) with classical molecular descriptors to achieve high prediction accuracy across diverse chemical structures. Toxicity-related endpoints covered by ADMETlab 3.0 include, among others: acute oral toxicity (LD₅₀, rat), eye and skin irritation, genotoxicity (Ames test), and cardiotoxicity (hERG inhibition) (Fu et al. 2024).

SMILES representations of all investigated LSD derivatives were submitted to the platform individually. All predictions were obtained using default model parameters and without manual intervention or retraining.

admetSAR 3.0: graph-based prediction of toxicological endpoints

The in silico assessment included the use of admetSAR 3.0 (https://lmmd.ecust.edu.cn/admetsar3/), a publicly available web platform designed to predict chemical toxicity and pharmacokinetic properties using graph-based deep learning algorithms. The system applies a multitask contrastive learning framework (CLMGraph) to simultaneously model dozens of endpoints from diverse experimental sources (Gu et al. 2024).

In this study, admetSAR 3.0 was used to predict toxicological outcomes for each compound, focusing on endpoints relevant to human health risk, including, hERG channel inhibition, acute oral toxicity (rat LD₅₀), eye and skin irritation. Input data were provided in SMILES format, and all predictions were obtained using the default settings of the platform.

Each result consisted a numerical probability score reflecting the model’s internal confidence. No mechanistic information or fragment-level attribution is included in the output. The platform operates as a purely data-driven classifier, trained on experimentally annotated chemical datasets.

Quantitative in silico methods

ACD/Labs percepta platform

As part of our comprehensive in silico assessment, we utilized the ACD/Labs Percepta Platform (version 2023.1.2) courtesy of a license obtained through the Institute of Medical Expertises in Łódź to predict various toxicological properties of the studied compounds. Percepta is a commercial software suite designed for the prediction of physicochemical, ADME, and toxicity properties based on molecular structure.

For toxicity prediction, we employed the Tox Suite module, which offers models for endpoints such as acute oral toxicity (LD₅₀), hERG channel inhibition, genotoxicity (Ames test), skin and eye irritation, and organ-specific toxicities. The software provides quantitative predictions along with reliability indices and visualizations of substructure contributions to the predicted properties.

Chemical structures were inputted in SMILES format, and predictions were generated using the default settings of the software. The results were used to complement findings from other in silico tools, contributing to a robust toxicological profile of the compounds under investigation.

ProTox-3.0

The toxicity of the investigated LSD derivatives was also assessed using the ProTox-3.0 webserver (https://tox.charite.de/protox3/), which provides predictive models for a broad range of toxicological endpoints. The platform integrates structural similarity, fragment-based features, pharmacophore modeling, and machine learning to derive toxicity predictions from molecular structure (Banerjee et al. 2024).

Compounds were submitted in SMILES format, and the platform returned LD₅₀ values (in mg/kg, oral rat) and cardiotoxicity.

ProTox-3.0 predicts toxicity by evaluating the two-dimensional (2D) structural similarity between the input compounds and those with established LD₅₀ values in a reference set of 38,515 substances. This process relies on identifying molecular fragments and fingerprints that are disproportionately present in toxic compounds. To validate the method’s robustness, a leave-one-out cross-validation strategy is applied. The prediction is based on determining the three most similar compounds from the training set using fingerprint similarity, which also serves as a basis for defining the model’s applicability domain, thereby supporting the relevance and reliability of the results.

Toxicity estimation software tool (TEST) 5.1.2.

Toxicity predictions were also obtained using the Toxicity Estimation Software Tool (TEST, version 5.1.2), developed by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. The software estimates key endpoints based on quantitative structure–activity relationships (QSAR), drawing on curated experimental data from public databases.

Each compound was processed as a SMILES string. Predictions were generated for acute oral toxicity (LD₅₀, rat) and genotoxicity (Ames test). Three independent QSAR methodologies were employed: the hierarchical method, the nearest-neighbour method, and the consensus method. The hierarchical approach uses Ward’s method to cluster chemicals based on structural similarity, generating predictions from cluster-specific models weighted by internal consistency (Martin et al. 2012). The nearest-neighbour method estimates toxicity by averaging LD₅₀ values from the three most structurally similar compounds in the training dataset (US EPA 2015). The consensus prediction represents the average of the available model outputs, taking into account each method’s applicability domain and reliability. TEST considers this consensus output the most robust and reliable estimate (US EPA 2015; Martin 2024).

VEGA QSAR: multi-model platform for predictive toxicology

Toxicological predictions were also conducted using the VEGA QSAR platform (version 1.2.3), developed by the Istituto di Ricerche Farmacologiche Mario Negri IRCCS. VEGA is an open-source application that provides over 100 QSAR models to predict toxicological, ecotoxicological, environmental, and physicochemical properties of chemical substances.

Chemical structures were inputted in SMILES format, and predictions were generated using the default settings of the software. For each compound, VEGA provided predictions for genotoxicity and acute toxicity. Each prediction was accompanied by an Applicability Domain Index (ADI), which assesses the reliability of the prediction based on the similarity of the compound to the training set and other factors.

OCHEM

To support genotoxicity assessment, predictions for the Ames test were obtained using the OCHEM platform (https://ochem.eu/), an open-access environment for applying validated QSAR models. The system integrates a curated chemical database with machine learning-based modeling tools (Sushko et al. 2011).

In this study, SMILES representations of the tested LSD derivatives were submitted to pre-built consensus models for bacterial genotoxicity.

Validation

Making predictions using in silico tools, carrying out a validation process of the methods is essential. It allows to check if the computer models and algorithms used by the respective software are reliable and accurate. The validation process is aimed at comparing the results for the tested compounds, obtained using in silico tools, with known experimental data of structural analogs of these compounds. In addition to structural similarity, the compounds selected for validation should also have a similar mechanism of action to the test compounds. Once the best candidates are selected, a predictive similarity coefficient (%) is calculated by comparing the LD50 values (reference values) that were determined experimentally on two animal models, i.e., the mouse and the rat, with the values that were estimated using in silico methods. Taking into account the fact that the tested compounds are analogs, validations were performed with respect to the results obtained for 1-acetyl-LSD (ALD-52). The following compounds were selected: Methylergonovine (CAS:113-42-8)—73.33% similarity, Lysergide (CAS:50-37-3)—88.89% similarity, Bromolysergide (CAS:478-84-2)—85.71% similarity, and Agroclavine (CAS:548-42-5)—60.71% similarity. These compounds were chosen because they exhibit characteristics that make them suitable for validation as structural analogs of ALD-52. ALD-52, despite its rather complex structure, shares several structural elements with the selected compounds. As for Methylergovine, both structures contain several aromatic and saturated rings, i.e. Benzene, cyclohexane, a ring containing nitrogen and a methyl group, and a pyrroline ring. In addition, the similarity between both compounds was distinguished taking into account the -C = O group with the -NH-CH2-CH3 group. Lysergide, on the other hand, is the most popular known analogue of ALD-52 therefore it was also selected, as well as Bromolysergide, which differs only in the presence of an attached bromine atom to the pyrroline ring.The last of the selected compounds i.e. Agroclavine similarly to Bromolysergide the common part with ALD-52 is shared at the level of aromatic and saturated rings. The similarity between the LD50 experimental results and those determined by in silico tools was calculated, taking into account different routes of administration, depending on the availability of data. Only the first compound mentioned allows checking the overlap of results after oral administration of the substance in the rat. The other results concern intraperitoneal, intravenous and oral administration in mice. The main in silico tool to obtain the results was the Percepta because it was the only one of those used to predict LD50 doses in both the rat and mouse by different routes of administration. The other programs used to perform the validation were TEST 5.1.2 with three models, i.e. Consensus, Hierarchical clustering and Nearest neighbor, VEGA QSAR 1.2.3 and ProTox 3.0.

In summary, the validation process is actually a key step in assessing the reliability of the predictive models used, and the overall process includes the fit of a given model to the training data and its ability/absence to predict unknown data. In the case of the LSD analogs under study, validation makes it possible to assess whether the model can be used to predict new properties of compounds, but structurally related to LSD. A summary of the validation process is described in Supplementary Material 1 (SM1).

According to ADMETlab 3.0, the majority of tested compounds (ALD-52, 1cP-LSD, and 1P-LSD) were classified as highly toxic, while 1B-LSD and 1 V-LSD demonstrated moderate to high acute toxicity. These distinctions may be partially influenced by the nature of the N-1-acyl substituents.

In STopTox, all five substances were predicted to be orally toxic, with confidence levels ranging from 74 to 76%. Notably, none of the compounds were flagged as dermally toxic, with higher confidence values between 74 and 83%, suggesting limited dermal hazard. Regarding inhalation exposure, toxicity predictions were positive for all analogues, though confidence levels were lower (58%-61%), indicating moderate model certainty. In addition to the generated results with their confidence based on how close the model is to a given estimate, StopTox also provides a color-coded visual interpretation of fragments of the molecule that may affect the behavior of the compound in the body. Structural fragments that could potentially increase toxicity are visualized in red, while those that are unlikely to contribute to toxic effects are highlighted in green (Borba et al. 2022). For acute oral toxicity, the entire molecular fragment of the compounds was visualized in red (except for the oxygen atoms, which are double bonded to carbon in the functional groups).

The admetSAR platform further supported the oral toxicity predictions by assigning > 99% probability of acute oral toxicity to all compounds. In contrast, while dermal toxicity was also predicted for each substance, the model produced lower confidence levels (81.0% to 84.8%), likely reflecting its limited applicability domain for lysergamide-like molecules.

Quantitative prediction of acute toxicity

Quantitative prediction of LD50 to ALD-52 and LSD analogues was performed using several in silico tools (Table 3). The first software used was Percepta, which is a licensed program predicts LD50 doses for both mouse and rat by different routes of administration. The results between the different compounds did not show much difference when comparing the same animal model and the same route of administration. The lowest results were obtained for the mouse with intravenous (53–66 mg/kg) and for the rat with the oral route of administration (49-85 mg/kg). Beyond acute toxicity, psychoactive substances may pose significant long-term health risks affecting multiple organ systems. This computational model enables the identification of molecular substructures associated with adverse effects in specific organs, thus serving as a valuable early-phase screening tool in toxicological profiling.

The present subsection focuses on three key systems frequently implicated in drug-related toxicities: blood, kidney, lungs and analyzes the predicted impact of the tested compounds on these biological targets. The findings provide important insight into potential health risks associated with lysergamide exposure and highlight structural alerts that may warrant further mechanistic investigation. Health effects related to blood

The predicted toxicological impact on the blood system varied among the tested lysergamides. The highest probability of blood-related toxicity was observed for 1P-LSD (76%) and 1B-LSD (75%), suggesting a moderate risk for hematotoxic effects. 1 V-LSD and 1cP-LSD demonstrated slightly lower probabilities (74% and 69%, respectively), while ALD-52 exhibited the lowest predicted risk at 68%.

Toxicophore visualizations showed recurring red-highlighted areas within the indole and amide substructures, indicating these moieties as likely contributors to potential hematotoxicity. Despite differences in overall probability, the similarity of structural alerts among all analogues implies a shared mechanistic basis for blood toxicity, possibly linked to oxidative or immunogenic effects commonly observed for psychoactive tryptamines.

However, the role of the N-1-acyl substituents in modulating hematotoxic potential should not be overlooked. While the observed differences in predicted toxicity are subtle, there appears to be a trend in which analogues bearing shorter or less sterically hindered side chains (e.g., acetyl in ALD-52) are associated with slightly lower toxicity probabilities. In contrast, compounds such as 1P-LSD and 1B-LSD, bearing longer or more lipophilic substituents, showed the highest predicted hematotoxicity. This may reflect differences in membrane interaction, plasma protein binding, or metabolic stability that influence systemic exposure and blood related effects.

Although Percepta’s program predicts blood toxicity at an average level (68%-76%) by the compounds tested, these numbers should already be the focus of those who study LSD and its analogs.

Health effects related to kidney

The predicted nephrotoxicity was consistently moderate across all substances, with probabilities ranging narrowly from 65 to 67%. 1cP-LSD had the highest probability (67%), while 1B-LSD and 1 V-LSD shared the lowest (65%). This tight clustering may indicate that renal toxicity is a shared concern across this lysergamide class, though less prominent than for other organ systems.

The toxicophore maps again highlighted structural features consistent with reactive oxygen potential and lipophilic moieties, which could contribute to renal accumulation or mitochondrial stress – a mechanism documented in structurally similar synthetic hallucinogens.

While the narrow range of nephrotoxicity probabilities suggests a common underlying risk profile, it is important to consider whether the N-1-acyl substituents contribute to subtle differences in renal burden. For instance, 1cP-LSD, which bears a cyclopropylcarbonyl group (a more rigid and sterically distinct moiety) showed the highest predicted renal toxicity. In contrast, 1B-LSD and 1 V-LSD, featuring more flexible linear substituents, showed the lowest values. These differences may reflect varying degrees of renal clearance, metabolic reactivity, or transporter affinity associated with acyl chain characteristics. Although speculative, such trends imply that N-1-acyl modifications may influence renal susceptibility through physicochemical modulation of distribution and excretion.

Health effects related to lungs

The predicted pulmonary toxicity showed the most divergence among tested compounds. The highest probability was observed for 1 V-LSD (92%), indicating a substantial in silico concern for lung toxicity, followed by 1cP-LSD (81%) and ALD-52 (74%). The lowest values were found for 1B-LSD (71%) and 1P-LSD (72%), though still above the mid-risk threshold.

Visual toxicophores revealed intense red zones on the fused aromatic ring systems, likely pointing to reactive intermediate formation or bioactivation via cytochrome P450 in pulmonary tissue, which aligns with known mechanisms for other volatile or psychoactive NPS.

Discussion

Acute toxicity

Qualitative prediction of acute toxicity

StopTox predicts that the entire molecule can cause AOT. This is substantiated in clinical studies, for example, a study by De Gregorio D and others (De Gregorio et al. 2016), where patients were orally administered 100 µg and 200 µg of LSD, found that systolic blood pressure, heart rate and body temperature were elevated in both groups after LSD administration. Also, close structural analogs can produce very similar effects. The situation is different with ADT, because StopTox, as already mentioned, predicts that the analyzed compounds will not cause this type of acute toxicity. Also, potential toxicophores have not been visualized, which means that there are probably no structural fragments in these molecules that could induce ADT. For predictions of acute toxicity by the inhalation route of administration, a fragment—the dimethylamine group—was visualized in red for all compounds. StopTox thus predicts that this fragment of the molecule, which from the center has a nitrogen linked to two methyl substituents (CH3), could be a potential toxicophore. It is also worth mentioning that all the results obtained with StopTox fall within the domain of applicability, which means that they are reliable because the predictions are for compounds that are similar to those on which the model was trained. This, in turn, indicates that they are very likely to have biological as well as chemical justification.

The presence of larger, more lipophilic moieties, such as the valeryl group in 1 V-LSD or the cyclopropylcarbonyl group in 1cP-LSD, may enhance membrane permeability or affect metabolic stability, thereby altering systemic exposure and acute toxicity profiles. These findings underscore how relatively minor modifications in the lysergamide scaffold may significantly influence predicted toxic potential, consistent with structure–activity relationship (SAR) principles.

Taken together, the acute toxicity profiles across all used tools suggest that variation in N-1-acyl substituent size and polarity may influence systemic toxicity, particularly in the oral and inhalation routes. Compounds bearing larger acyl groups (like 1 V-LSD and 1cP-LSD) tended to show higher predicted toxicity, potentially due to altered lipophilicity, passive diffusion, or metabolic susceptibility. However, these structure-based associations require empirical validation to confirm their mechanistic relevance.

Collectively, these predictions indicate a consistent profile of significant oral and inhalation toxicity among the studied analogues, whereas dermal exposure appears comparatively less hazardous, however with tool-dependent variability. While in silico methods provide valuable first-line screening, these results warrant verification through experimental approaches to refine risk assessments and elucidate possible mechanisms of action.

Quantitative prediction of acute toxicity

Predicted LD50 values generally fall within a narrow range despite structural differences. However, subtle variations, such as the presence of larger acyl groups (e.g., valeryl in 1 V-LSD or cyclopropylcarbonyl in 1cP-LSD), may modulate toxicity by influencing lipophilicity, steric hindrance, or metabolic activation. This potential structural toxicity relationship suggests that even minor substitutions at the N-1-acyl position may impact compound-specific toxicokinetic behaviour and should not be overlooked in interpretation. Further experimental studies of structure are needed to validate these hypotheses.

Health effects

Health effects related to blood

In experimental study (Schmid et al. 2015) showed that LSD significantly increased blood pressure, heart rate, body temperature, pupil size, plasma cortisol, prolactin, oxytocin and epinephrine levels. Therefore, these results should not be taken lightly.

Health effects related to kidney

As in the case of the effect of the tested compounds on the blood, the toxic effect on the kidneys as already mentioned is at an average level (65–67%). However, it should be noted that a huge number of substances have negative effects on the kidneys, e.g. Opioids, cocaine, MDMA and many others, so the results obtained should not be underestimated. One report (Berrens et al. 2010) showed negative effects of LSD on the kidneys. It was noted that LSD-induced hyperthermia leads to rhabdomyolysis, which can result in acute renal failure (Usui et al. 1973).

Health effects related to lungs

Interestingly, the distribution of predicted pulmonary toxicity may partially reflect the nature of the N-1-acyl substituents. Compounds such as 1 V-LSD and 1cP-LSD, bearing larger and more lipophilic acyl groups (valeryl and cyclopropylcarbonyl, respectively), showed notably higher probabilities for lung toxicity. This may be related to their greater affinity for lipid rich pulmonary tissue, altered metabolic activation, or differences in inhalation bioavailability. In contrast, analogues with shorter or less bulky side chains such as 1P-LSD and 1B-LSD demonstrated lower pulmonary toxicity scores. Although the correlation is not definitive, these trends suggest that N-1-acyl substitution may modulate the pulmonary burden of lysergamide analogues by influencing their partitioning behaviour or metabolic fate in lung tissues.

Eye and skin irritation

Given the overall low risk, the role of N-1-acyl substituents in modulating ocular irritation appears limited. However, it is noteworthy that analogues with shorter acyl chains like ALD-52, demonstrated slightly higher numerical predictions across all models, while more sterically hindered or lipophilic groups (e.g., in 1cP-LSD and 1 V-LSD) corresponded with the lowest predicted risks. Although these differences are minimal and not toxicologically significant, they may reflect minor changes in physicochemical properties, such as aqueous solubility or corneal permeability, potentially influenced by N-1-acetyl substitution.

Taken together, these findings suggest a low to moderate risk of skin irritation, with 1B-LSD and 1cP-LSD emerging as the most probable irritants, although still within a relatively safe range. These results support the view that dermal exposure to lysergamides may lead to transient irritation in sensitive individuals, warranting further investigation only under occupational exposure scenarios.

Importantly, the variation in predicted irritation potential may reflect the physicochemical influence of the N-1-acyl substituents. Specifically, compounds such as 1B-LSD and 1cP-LSD, which possess bulkier or more hydrophobic N-1-acyl groups (butyryl and cyclopropylcarbonyl, respectively), demonstrated the highest predicted irritation across models. These side chains may increase lipophilicity and membrane interaction potential, thereby enhancing local dermal reactivity. In contrast, derivatives with smaller or more hydrophilic N-1-acyl fragments like acetyl in ALD-52, showed lower or intermediate values. Although irritation levels remain generally low, these trends suggest that N-1-acyl group identity may subtly modulate cutaneous tolerability.

Genotoxicity

Genotoxicity is defined as the ability of chemical agents to cause damage to the genetic material within a cell, including DNA strand breaks, chromosomal aberrations, and gene mutations. Such alterations can disrupt cellular homeostasis, initiate carcinogenesis, or lead to heritable mutations, making genotoxicity a central endpoint in toxicological risk assessment (Brusick 1980). Regulatory agencies such as the OECD and ICH require genotoxicity testing as part of the standard safety evaluation of pharmaceuticals, industrial chemicals, and environmental contaminants (Kirkland et al. 2005).

One of the most commonly used and internationally validated assays for initial genotoxicity screening is the bacterial reverse mutation assay, commonly known as the Ames test. This method utilizes specific strains of Salmonella typhimurium or Escherichia coli that are sensitive to mutagenic agents, enabling rapid identification of substances capable of inducing point mutations (Mortelmans and Zeiger 2000). While relatively simple and cost-effective, the Ames test provides robust predictive value for mutagenic and carcinogenic potential.

In the case of novel psychoactive substances, including synthetic analogs of LSD, experimental data on genotoxicity are typically lacking due to their recent emergence and limited regulatory oversight. In this context, computational (in silico) toxicology has become an increasingly valuable tool, offering preliminary predictions based on molecular structure and known toxicophores (Tetko et al. 2001). These predictions support early risk stratification and guide the prioritization of compounds for further empirical testing.

Interestingly, some model results suggest a possible link between genotoxic potential and the identity of the N-1-acyl substituent. For example, compounds with smaller or more electrophilic groups (like the acetyl group in ALD-52 or the cyclopropylcarbonyl in 1cP-LSD) were consistently predicted to have the highest genotoxic probabilities across ADMETlab, Percepta, and Test 5.1.2. In contrast, analogues with longer or more sterically hindered side chains e.g., 1 V-LSD showed relatively lower genotoxicity scores. This pattern may suggest that smaller or strained N-1-acyl groups increase the likelihood of metabolic activation or covalent DNA interactions, a hypothesis that warrants mechanistic investigation. Although speculative, this observation supports the inclusion of N-1-acyl features in future genotoxicity relationship of structure modeling for lysergamides.

Overall, although individual models such as OCHEM and ISS suggest a lack of genotoxic potential, the broader panel, particularly ADMETlab, Percepta, Test 5.1.2, and multiple VegaQSAR algorithms, highlight a recurring presence of genotoxic alerts, especially for ALD-52, 1cP-LSD, and 1 V-LSD. These findings indicate that further experimental validation may be warranted to clarify the true genotoxic risk posed by these novel lysergamide analogues.

Cardiotoxicity

Cardiotoxicity refers to any structural or functional impairment of the heart caused by chemical substances, which may manifest as arrhythmias, myocardial injury, contractile dysfunction, or conduction disturbances. It is a major safety concern in pharmacology, toxicology, and clinical medicine, particularly because adverse cardiac effects are a leading cause of late-stage drug attrition and post-marketing drug withdrawal. One of the key molecular determinants of drug-induced cardiotoxicity is the human ether-à-go-go-related gene (hERG) potassium channel. This channel plays a key role in cardiac action potential repolarization, and its inhibition is strongly associated with QT interval prolongation and life-threatening arrhythmias such as torsades de pointes (Sanguinetti and Tristani-Firouzi 2006).

Beyond hERG inhibition, cardiotoxic effects may arise from a variety of other mechanisms. These include mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, disruption of calcium homeostasis, and direct cytotoxic effects on cardiomyocytes. Chronic exposure to toxic substances may also lead to cardiac remodeling, fibrosis, or hypertrophy, ultimately contributing to the development of heart failure (Bers 2002; Zhang et al. 2012). In certain cases, cardiotoxicity may be dose-dependent and reversible, while in others, even brief exposure can lead to irreversible myocardial injury. These risks underscore the need for comprehensive cardiotoxicity screening in drug development and chemical safety evaluation, even in compounds not intended for therapeutic use, such as novel psychoactive substances.

The observed cardiotoxicity patterns may be partially influenced by the nature of the N-1-acyl substituent present in each derivative. Specifically, 1 V-LSD, bearing a valeryl group, a longer and more lipophilic chain, demonstrated the strongest hERG inhibition in all models. This may suggest that increased lipophilicity and molecular flexibility contribute to greater affinity for the hERG channel binding pocket. In contrast, ALD-52, with a short acetyl group, displayed the weakest predicted hERG blockade, possibly reflecting reduced membrane permeability or interaction surface area. Intermediate effects observed for 1B-LSD and 1cP-LSD further support the notion that cardiotoxicity risk may be modulated by N-1-acyl chain length and steric features. These structure toxicity associations highlight the importance of incorporating acyl group variability into cardiotoxicity risk modeling for lysergamides.

Conclusions

In this study, we applied a multi-tool in silico approach to evaluate the toxicological profile of five LSD derivatives: ALD-52, 1P-LSD, 1B-LSD, 1 V-LSD, and 1cP-LSD. Using computational platforms including ADMETlab 3.0, STopTox, Percepta, admetSAR 3.0, TEST 5.1.2., OCHEM, VEGA Qsar 1.2.3 and ProTox 3.0, we predicted key toxicological endpoints relevant to clinical and forensic toxicology, such as acute toxicity, health effects, eye and skin irritation, genotoxicity, cardiotoxicity and overall systemic toxicity. These tools enabled the high-throughput screening of ADMET parameters and hazards depending on the organ, without the need for animal testing, in alignment with the principles of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement).

All tested compounds are predicted to pose a significant risk following oral and inhalation exposure, while dermal exposure appears to be less hazardous. These results are consistent across several models (ADMETlab, STopTox, admetSAR). Among the analogues, 1 V-LSD and 1cP-LSD were found to be the most toxic, potentially due to their larger and more lipophilic N-1-acyl substituents, which may increase cell membrane permeability and systemic exposure.

Genotoxicity predictions were dependent on a method, but several platforms (ADMETlab, Percepta, Test 5.1.2) identified ALD-52, 1cP-LSD, and 1 V-LSD as compounds with increased potential of damaging DNA. These trends appear to correlate with the presence of smaller or more strained N-1-acyl groups (e.g., acetyl, cyclopropylcarbonyl), which may promote metabolic activation pathways, supporting the hypothesis that the identity of N-1-acyl substituents may influence mutagenicity.

Regarding target organ toxicity, moderate risks to the circulatory system and kidneys were predicted, particularly for 1B-LSD, 1P-LSD, and 1cP-LSD. These analogues exhibited structural signatures in regions common to hallucinogenic tryptamines and were characterized by toxicophoric patterns, suggesting the possibility of oxidative stress or mitochondrial stress. Pulmonary toxicity, on the other hand, varied considerably, with the highest scores recorded for 1 V-LSD (92%) and 1cP-LSD (81%), likely reflecting enhanced pulmonary oxygen release due to their hydrophobic N-1-acyl chains.

Eye and skin irritation potential was generally low, but 1cP-LSD and 1B-LSD showed slightly elevated skin irritation scores in various models, likely due to their more hydrophobic or sterically complex amide modifications. Although unlikely to pose a significant health risk under typical conditions of use, these effects may become significant under occupational or repeated exposure conditions.

The most concerning endpoint was cardiotoxicity, specifically inhibition of hERG, a predictor of arrhythmogenic potential. 1 V-LSD consistently demonstrated the strongest hERG inhibition, indicating a potentially increased risk of QT prolongation and cardiac dysfunction. In contrast, ALD-52 exhibited the lowest predicted cardiotoxicity, which may be related to its smaller acetyl substituent and reduced lipophilicity. The indirect effects observed for 1B-LSD and 1cP-LSD further support the hypothesis that cardiotoxicity risk may be modulated by N-1-acyl chain length and steric characteristics. These structure dependent toxicity relationships highlight the importance of considering acyl group variability in modeling the cardiotoxicity risk of LSD derivates.

Overall, the in silico toxicological assessment revealed that all five lysergamide analogues exhibit moderate systemic toxicity, with compound-specific concerns. 1 V-LSD showed the highest predicted cardiopulmonary risk, including the lowest hERG IC50 and highest pulmonary toxicity (92%). 1cP-LSD also demonstrated elevated respiratory and renal toxicity, along with moderate genotoxic alerts. 1B-LSD was associated with the highest hematotoxicity and hERG inhibition probability, while 1P-LSD displayed notable hematotoxic potential but a generally balanced profile. ALD-52 showed the lowest hematotoxic and dermal risk but still raised genotoxicity and cardiotoxicity concerns.

Across all compounds, oral and inhalation toxicity were consistently predicted in the 50–85 mg/kg LD50 range, while ocular and dermal hazards were minimal. Genotoxicity predictions varied across tools, emphasizing the need for further empirical validation. These findings suggest that while overall risk is moderate, specific liabilities (particularly cardiopulmonary and genotoxic) may be relevant for certain analogues and warrant further investigation.

The main advantage of the employed methodology lies in its efficiency, reproducibility, and ethical neutrality. In silico methods enable rapid hazard assessment of compounds that are otherwise poorly characterized in the literature or inaccessible for in vivo studies. This is particularly important in the case of emerging lysergamides, where experimental data remain scarce and analytical detection remains challenging (Brandt et al. 2017). Nevertheless, certain limitations persist. Predictive performance is constrained by the scope of the training data used to develop these models. Furthermore, the metabolic behavior of these compounds (some of which may act as prodrugs for LSD) cannot be fully captured in static models.

Future work should aim to validate these predictions through experimental studies, particularly those assessing organ-specific toxicodynamics such as hepatocyte viability, mitochondrial respiration, and cardiomyocyte calcium handling. Expanding current models through machine learning integration and curated lysergamide-specific datasets could increase predictive precision. Additionally, targeted toxicogenomic and metabolomic profiling may help uncover compound-specific liabilities, informing both risk assessment and potential therapeutic repositioning.
