IL-3/STAT5/miR-155-5p axis supports stem-related pathway reprogramming in TNBC
Authors: Alessandro Sarcinella, Sharad Kholia, Saveria Femminò, Massimo Cedrino, Marta Tapparo, Xiaofen Wen, Maria Rosaria De Miglio, Vincenzo Salemme, Andrea Scavuzzo, Matteo Poncina, Giulia Orlando, Rebecca Senetta, Isabella Castellano, Maria Felice Brizzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTriple negative breast cancer (TNBC) remains one of the most aggressive subtypes of cancer with a poor prognosis and limited treatment options. Building on our previous findings of elevated Interleukin-3-Receptor-α (IL-3Rα) expression in TNBC, this study investigates the mechanisms underpinning IL-3-mediated actions in TNBC.MethodsGEO database (GSE25066) was interrogated to evaluate the expression of IL-3. RNAseq data were acquired from the TCGA-BRCA (Breast Carcinoma) project. Seven TNBC cell lines were used to validate the expression of IL-3 by ELISA assay. Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay was performed to evaluate the binding of STAT5A to the miR-155-5p promoter in TNBC cells. FACS analysis and ALDH activity were performed to evaluate the expansion of ALDH-1A1 + and CD44high/CD24low subpopulations. Mammosphere formation efficiency (MFE) was evaluated using the standard assay, while chemoresistance by applying the incucyte cell viability assay. miR155-5p silencing served to validate the expression of all target proteins both in vitro and in vivo.ResultsBioinformatic analysis of breast cancer patient gene datasets revealed significant upregulation of the IL-3 gene in TNBC patient samples compared to the non-TNBC group (GEO: p = 0.004: TCGA p = 2.7e−30 respectively). We also found that TNBC cells secrete IL-3, which activates STAT5A promoting miR-155-5p expression by binding to its promoter in TNBC cells. Correlation analysis based on TCGA-BRCA confirmed elevated miR-155-5p levels in TNBC compared to non-tumoral tissues (p = 2.1e−33) and non-TNBC (p = 6.5e−30), with positive correlations between the IL-3 and miR-155-5p (r = 0.157, p < 0.001), as well as between miR-155-5p and miR-155-3p and STAT5A (r = 0.250, p = 0.002; r = 0.245, p < 0.005 respectively). Functional studies demonstrated that miR-155-5p downregulates programmed cell death 4, APC, and GSK-3β, enhancing β-catenin nuclear translocation and c-myc expression. Silencing miR-155-5p reversed all these effects. IL-3, via miR-155-5p, also drives ALDH-1A1 + and CD44high/CD24low subpopulation expansion and ALDH activity, enhances MFE and chemoresistance. Notably, blocking IL-3 impaired MFE, suggesting an autocrine loop sustaining IL-3 action in TNBC. In vivo, IL-3 promoted tumour growth, β-catenin activity, and metastasis, while miR-155-5p silencing mitigated these effects.ConclusionsOverall, our results underscore the crucial role of IL-3 in tumour progression, thereby advocating IL-3/IL-3Rα axis targeting as a promising therapeutic approach for TNBC.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-025-02143-1.Keywords: IL-3, miR-155-5p, Triple negative breast cancer, Pdcd4, β-catenin, STAT5

Introduction

Triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) represents a heterogeneous group of breast neoplasms characterised by diverse histological, genomic, and immunological profiles. These tumours share a common absence of oestrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) expression as well as effective therapeutic approaches [1]. The increased Cancer Stem Cell (CSC) sub-population in TNBC significantly contributes to treatment resistance and prevents tumour eradication [2]. CSCs, characterized by the CD44high/CD24low/aldehyde dehydrogenase 1A1 (ALDH1A1) + phenotype, exhibit self-renewing capabilities and persistence to following therapy. TNBC is enriched in several stemness-related pathways [2] with aberrant activation of Wnt/β-catenin axis serving as a hallmark of poor outcome [3]. As a matter of fact, TNBC patients, displaying dysregulated Wnt/β-catenin signalling, are more prone to develop lung and brain metastases [4]. Moreover, nuclear accumulation of β-catenin has been shown to promote stem-like features and chemoresistance in TNBC, establishing Wnt signalling as a crucial driving force of TNBC aggressiveness [5].

Dysfunctional microRNAs (miRs) play a pivotal role in cancer progression including miR-155-5p [6] which is generated from the B-cell integration cluster (BIC) in two different forms (miR-155-3p and miR-155-5p) [7]. The BIC promoter is regulated by different transcription factors including activation protein 1 (AP-1) [8, 9], nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NFkB) and Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription (STAT)5 in hemopoietic cells [10]. miR-155-5p, the predominant active form, has been implicated in lymphoma [11], acute myeloid leukaemia (AML) [10, 12] and recently breast cancer (BC) [13, 14]. In addition, elevated miR-155-5p expression has also been linked to epithelial to mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, invasion, metastasis, clinicopathologic markers, poor survival rate [13, 14], tumour initiation, stemness maintenance [15], and early disease relapse in TNBC [16, 17]. miR-155-5p has also been detected in both circulation and tissue samples of patients with BC, including TNBC [6, 18]. While its role as an oncogene or tumour suppressor remains debated across many cancer subtypes [19], recent studies have shown that knockdown of miR-155-5p inhibits breast tumour growth, highlighting its crucial role in regulating tumour suppressor genes in BC [6, 13–16]. Programmed cell death 4 (Pdcd4) is a key tumour suppressor gene that is frequently downregulated in many cancers [20] and more recently, it has been identified as a miR-155-5p target in oral and lung cancers [8, 21]. In addition, Pdcd4 knockdown has been shown to activate β-catenin/T-cell specific transcription factor (Tcf)-dependent transcription and promote the expression of Myc [22].

IL-3, originally designated as an hemopoietic-related cytokine, has been extensively studied in acute myeloid leukaemia (AML), and its receptor ligand subunit, IL-3Rα, investigated as a therapeutic target [23, 24]. Cytokine-induced miRNA network analysis in primary AML cells linked IL-3 signalling to Janus Kinase (JAK)/STAT5-mediated miR-155-5p expression [12] and β-catenin nuclear accumulation [25]. Of note, the increased expression of IL-3 [26] and its ligand subunit IL-3Rα [27] was recently found to be associated with disease progression, and poor outcome in BC particularly in TNBC. Additionally, in mice bearing TNBC tumours, IL-3 was able to reshape the tumour immunological features and reprogram tumour cells to form large dysfunctional vessels [27]. Molecular analyses have shown that the JAK/STAT family members, mainly linked to IL-3 action, are overexpressed in almost 10% of TNBC patients, and that their activation is associated with tumour cell proliferation and vascularization [28].

In the present study we aimed to explore the IL-3 mechanism of action in TNBC and particular attention has been dedicated to investigating the impact of miR-155-5p in IL-3-mediated TNBC aggressiveness.

Experimental section

Bioinformatic analysis: data collection and processing

RNAseq data were acquired from the TCGA-BRCA (Breast Carcinoma) project https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/projects/TCGA-BRCA. The RNAseq data underwent further processing through the STAR workflow and was extracted in TPM format. Samples lacking duplicates were excluded. The average signal intensity value for each gene was logarithmically converted to base 2 to simplify data handling. Subsequently, a total of 1180 samples from breast cancer patients were included, encompassing both clinical information and available sequencing data. Among these, 160 cases were pathologically identified as TNBC, and 111 adjacent tissue samples were utilized as control tissue. The mRNA expression data were formatted in level 3 HTSeq-FPKM, normalized as transcripts per million reads (TPM) for analysis. Additionally, 508 samples have been collected from the GEO database (GSE25066) (GEO Accession viewer (nih.gov)) through expression profiling by array to validate the expression of IL-3. miRNA expression profile of BC patients has been collected from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) database (n = 1097), including 160 TNBC samples. Data obtained from TCGA were processed through the BCGSC pipeline and extracted in RPM format for further analysis. Statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.2.1). The significance of expression differences was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and correlations were examined using Spearman’s correlation test.

Cell cultures

The following cell lines were used: MCF7 (luminal A/B), MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-453 (HER2 +), MDA-MB-436, MDA-MB-157, BT-549, HCC-1395, Hs-578 T, HMEC and GDM-1 (AML-M4). All cell lines were purchased from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The M07 leukemic cells were established in the laboratory [29]. Details are reported in Supplementary Materials.

Western blot analysis

Cells were extracted at 4 °C in RIPA lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific #89,900) in the presence of 1X diluted Protease/Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Cell Signaling Technology #5872) for 1 h. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min, and the supernatants were collected and analysed for protein concentration using a PierceBCA protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific #23225). Details are reported in Supplementary Materials.

RNA extraction, retro transcription and quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

For the measurement of human APC and GSK-3β transcript levels, total RNA was extracted using RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen #74104) and reverse-transcribed using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific #4368814). Details are reported in Supplementary Materials.

ELISA assay

Supernatants from cell cultures were harvested after 16 h of starvation followed by 5 min centrifugation at 1200 rpm. Relative quantification of human IL-3 secretion was performed using DuoSet ELISA Development Systems (R&D Systems #DY007B) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The final IL-3 concentration was normalized to the number of cells and expressed as ng/ml/106 cells.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

Protein-DNA interactions were analysed using the CUT&RUN (Cleavage Under Targets & Release Using Nuclease) Assay Kit (Cell Signaling Technology #86652). For each reaction, 3 × 105 cells treated with or without IL-3 were fixed with formaldehyde and processed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Phospho-STAT5 antibody (abcam #ab277762) was used at a 1:25 dilution, while the rabbit mAb IgG antibody supplied in the kit served as a negative control. Both antibodies were incubated with the cells overnight at 4 °C. DNA purification was performed using DNA Purification Buffers and Spin Columns (Cell Signalling Technology #14209). Binding of phospho-STAT5 to the BIC promoter was quantified by qRT-PCR using the SimpleChIP Universal qPCR Master mix (Cell Signalling Technology #88989) following the manufacturer’s protocol. PCR primers for the BIC (MIR155HG) promoter upstream regions flanking STAT5 binding sites were: 5′-AAAGGGAAAGGGGAAAACACC-3′ (Fw), and 5′-CTGTTCTTGGAACCTACAGCTTGAA-3′ (Rv). The spike-in primer (Cell Signalling Technology #85756) for normalization was included in the assay kit.

FACS analysis

For FACS analysis, cells were harvested using non-enzymatic dissociation buffer (Sigma-Aldrich #C5914) and washed in PBS. For the detection of surface markers (IL-3Rα/CD123, CD24, CD44), cells were directly stained with the respective fluorophore-conjugated antibody for 30 min at 4 °C, while for the intracellular marker (ALDH1A1) cells were treated with the Intracellular Fixation & Permeabilization Buffer kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific #88-8824-00) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the Doxorubicin-PE (Sigma-Aldrich #D1515) analysis, cells were washed twice in PBS and immediately analysed with the cytometer. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using the Cytoflex flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, United States) equipped with the CytExpert software version 2.3.0.84. During acquisition, the median fluorescence intensity (MFI) was corrected for medium background and gated based on their respective fluorescence intensity as per the manufacturer’s instructions. The following anti-human antibodies were used: IL-3Rα-APC, (Milteny Biotec #130-113-322), CD44-FITC (Miteny Biotec #130-113-341), CD24-PE (Milteny Biotec #130-112-656), ALDH1A1-PE (abcam #ab209537). Isotype-FITC (REA Control Antibody, human IgG1, FITC, REAfinity™) (Milteny Biotec #130-113-449) and Isotype-PE (REA Control Antibody, human IgG1, PE, REAfinity™) (Milteny Biotec # 130-113-450) were used as negative control for the staining.

Cell viability assays

MDA-MB-436 and Hs-578 T cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in 48-well plates containing 500 μl of medium without antibiotics. The following day, cells were treated with 5 ng/ml of interleukin-3 (IL-3) and transfected with 100 pmol of antagomiR-155 or negative control (NC) oligonucleotides. All treatment conditions were also exposed to 0.5 μM doxorubicin for up to 96 h. Real-time cell imaging was performed using the Incucyte® SX5 Live-Cell Analysis System (Sartorius). Four fields per well were imaged every 24 h at 10 × magnification for a total duration of 96 h. Cell viability was quantified as percent confluence using the Incucyte Basic Analyzer software (version 1.5). All Incucyte experiments were performed in triplicate. In addition, cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay (3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide; Sigma-Aldrich, #CT02), as follow: cells were incubated with 10 μl/well of MTT reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, #CT02) for 3 h at 37 °C, followed by addition of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 5 min to dissolve the formazan crystals. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Each experiment was independently repeated three times.

Poly-HEMA coating

A 12% solution of polyhydroxyethylmethacrylate polymer (poly-HEMA) in 95% ethanol was mixed overnight at 60 °C with a stirrer in agitation, centrifuged at 2500 rpm to remove undissolved particles, and diluted 1:10 with 95% ethanol. The solution was filtered with 0.1 µm filter before using it. 6 well plates (Corning) were coated with 4 ml of poly-HEMA solution and left to dry at room temperature inside the biosafety cabinet under sterile conditions under UV light with no lid for at least 24 h or until the ethanol is evaporated. Dishes were washed twice with PBS before use.

Mammosphere formation assay

Adherent cells were detached at 80–90% confluency with non-enzymatic dissociation buffer (Sigma-Aldrich #C5914) for 5 min at 37 °C and 5% CO2. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 1200 rpm for 5 min and the supernatant discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in Tumorsphere Media XF (PromoCell #C-28070) and counted with Muse Count & Viability kit (Luminex Corporation #MCH600103) on the Muse Cell Analyzer (Merck Millipore). Cells were then plated in non-adsorbent poly-HEMA 6 well plates (Corning) at a concentration of 104 cells in 4 ml of tumorsphere medium and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Non-adherent spherical clusters of cells were cultured for 5 days. After the culture period, spheres were observed and counted with a Nikon inverted microscope (10X) affixed with a Leica-digital camera. Five different fields/well were counted by two blinded independent operators (each experiment was performed in triplicate). Only spheres greater than 50 µm were considered. The mammosphere formation efficiency (MFE%) was calculated using the following equation: MFE (%) = (number of mammospheres per well) / (number of cells seeded per well) × 100 [30].

ALDH activity assay

ALDH activity was measured using the ALDH Activity Assay Kit (Abcam, #ab155893), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, NADH standards and cell culture supernatant samples were added in triplicate to a 96-well plate. The reaction mix, containing acetaldehyde and the ALDH substrate, was then added to each well to a final volume of 50 µl. The plate was incubated for 10 min at room temperature, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

In vivo experiments

Six/eight-week-old female NSG mice were purchased from Envigo (Bresso, Italy) and treated in accordance with the European Community guidelines. The study received ethical approval from the Ministry of Health, protocol number 518/2022-PR (Project code CC652.180). Tumours were obtained by resuspending 1 × 107 MDA-MB-436 or Hs-578 T cells in 100 μl of growth factor reduced Matrigel (Corning® #354230) and then injected into the left mammary fat pad. Mice were randomized in four different group (4 mice/group) and 10 days after the injection were subjected to intratumorally treatment of saline, IL-3 (20 ng/ml), antagomiR-155 (10 nmol) + IL-3 (20 ng/ml) and NC (10 nmol) + IL-3 (20 ng/ml) every 3 days for 3 weeks. The size of the tumours was evaluated every three days using a digital calliper in blinded experiments: maximum and minimum diameter were measured, and the volume was calculated using the following ellipsoid formula: (4/3π(d/2)^2*D/2). At the end of the treatment, mice were euthanized using a CO2 chamber. Tumour and the lungs were collected and fixed for immunohistochemical analysis.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Three-µm-thick serial paraffin sections were processed by immunohistochemistry using an automated platform (Ventana Bench Mark Auto Stainer; Ventana Medical Systems, Tucson, AZ) with human antibody against non-phospho (Active) β-catenin (Ser33/37/Thr41) (Cell Signalling Technology #8814), IL-3 (abcam #ab190941) and Pdcd4 (Cell Signalling Technology #9535). Appropriate positive controls were included for each slide. Ten field/sections were analysed using Fiji software, and the results expressed as the percentage of positive area ± SEM. Lung section were stained with Haematoxylin & Eosin (H&E) staining for the metastasis evaluation and counting.

Reagents and treatments

Adherent cells confluent at 70–80% and starved for 16 h, were treated in 6 well plates with 5 ng/ml of human recombinant IL-3 (abcam #ab83685), or with 100 pmol of antisense oligonucleotide miR-155-5p (antagomiR-155), or with 100 pmol of non-coding scramble (NC), both from Eurofins Genomics, and resuspended in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Details are reported in Supplementary Materials.

Statistical analysis

All data are reported as mean ± SEM. Comparison between two groups was analysed out by Student’s t-test while comparisons amid 3 or more groups have been performed using one-way ANOVA test. p-value < 0.05 was considered as significant. All in vitro and in vivo data are representative of at least three independent experiments. Graph Pad Prism (Graph Pad Software, 8.0.1 version) was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

IL-3 expression in human TNBC positively correlates with STAT5 and miR-155-5p

Although the expression of IL-3 has been recently associated with poor outcomes in BC patients [26], its biological impact, particularly in TNBC, has been poorly investigated. Based on our previous results demonstrating that all TNBC cell lines included in this study express high levels of IL-3Rα [27], we first analysed the GEO and TCGA databases. In line with the results by Thompson et al. [26], we found that IL-3 was significantly upregulated in TNBC samples compared to non-TNBC group (p = 0.004 and p = 2.7e−30 respectively) (Fig. 1A and B) and between normal tissues and TNBC (p = 0.005) (TCGA dataset) (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, the release of IL-3 was also observed in seven different TNBC cell lines displaying heterogenous phenotypes (Fig. 1D), suggesting that IL-3 can potentially act as an autocrine factor. Based on previous results [27] and the above observation, we sought to investigate the role of IL-3 in TNBC. To better understand the mechanism of action of IL-3 in TNBC, we utilized data from AML-M4 cells showing that miR-155-5p is a downstream mediator of IL-3 action [12]. First, a correlation between the expression of IL-3 and miR-155-5p was evaluated in patients with TNBC by analysing the TCGA dataset. Of note, we found a significantly increased expression of miR-155-5p in TNBC compared to non-tumoral tissues (p = 2.1e−33) and non-TNBC breast cancers (p = 6.5e−30) (Fig. 1E-F) and a positive correlation between the expressions of IL-3 and miR-155-5p, (r = 0.157, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1G). Based on this information we evaluated the expression of miR-155-5p in different TNBC cell lines upon IL-3 administration. Out of the various diverse cell lines tested, we found that IL-3 further increases miR-155-5p expression in MDA-MB-436 and Hs-578 T cells (Fig. 1H and Supplementary Fig. S1). Since IL-3 -mediated miR-155-5p expression in AML-M4 activated STAT5 [11], we performed a similar correlation analysis in TNBC patients. Spearman analysis confirmed the positive correlation between both miR-155-5p and miR-155-3p and STAT5A in TNBC (miR-155-5p: r = 0.250, p = 0.002; miR-155-3p: r = 0.245, p = 0.005) (Fig. 1I-J), while no correlation was observed with STAT5B (Supplementary Fig. S2). STAT5A phosphorylation was therefore assessed in IL-3 responsive and unresponsive cells. As shown in Fig. 1K, IL-3 triggers STAT5A phosphorylation only in MDA-MB-436 and Hs-578 T cells, while a constitutive STAT5 activation was noticed in TNBC cell lines in which IL-3 did not further increase miR-155-5p expression (Supplementary Fig. S3). By contrast, no changes in STAT5 expression were detected in any of the cell lines evaluated (Fig. 1K and Supplementary Fig. S3). Furthermore, ChIP analysis demonstrated the interaction of STAT5A with the BIC promoter in unstimulated cells which was boosted significantly in the presence of IL-3 (Fig. 1L and Supplementary Fig. S4). Overall, these data provide new insight on the mechanisms regulating miR-155-5p expression in TNBC and demonstrated that IL-3 by enhancing STAT5 activation further induces its expression. Based on these results both MDA-MB-436 and Hs-578 T cells were used to investigate the molecular and biological impact of IL-3 in TNBC.

miR-155-5p post-transcriptionally controls the expression of APC and GSK-3β and triggers the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway and c-Myc expression

miR-155-5p controls the transcription of different genes involved in both physiological and pathological processes [19, 31]. In order, to identify potential miR-155-5p targets, the TargetScan software (v7.0; targetscan.org) was implemented (Fig. 2A). The analyses identified two components of Wnt/β-catenin destruction complex, known as Adenomatous Polyposis Coli (APC) and Glycogen Synthase Kinase-3β (GSK-3β) (Fig. 2A). We therefore evaluated the role of miR-155-5p in the regulation of APC and GSK-3β mRNA levels in MDA-MB-436 and Hs-578 T cells upon IL-3 treatment. Data reported in Fig. 2B-C demonstrate that, indeed, IL-3 stimulation significantly reduces the expression of both transcripts either at 12 and 24 h, while the antagomiR-155 prevented IL-3-driven APC and GSK-3β down-regulation.

The targeting of APC and GSK-3β mediated by IL-3-miR-155-5p was also analysed at the protein level, demonstrating that IL-3 lowers APC and GSK-3β expression in both MDA-MB-436 (Fig. 2D-E) and Hs-578 T cells (Fig. 2H-I), and is reversed upon blocking miR-155-5p. As APC and GSK-3β form part of the β-catenin destruction complex, we evaluated the levels of β-catenin downstream. As shown in Fig. 2F and J, we observed an increased expression of both the active β-catenin (Ser33/37/Thr41) and its transcriptional target c-Myc upon IL-3 treatment (Fig. 2G and K). Noteworthy, the inhibition of β-catenin degradation was prevented in cells transfected with the antagomir-155 (Fig. 2F and J). These results provide the first evidence that IL-3 drives miR-155-5p expression which in turn is instrumental for the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway and the expression of c-Myc in TNBCs.

miR-155-5p is instrumental for IL-3-induced stem-like phenotype in TNBC cells

In TNBC a constitutive activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway is associated with a poor outcome [32] as it plays a pivotal role in mammary stem cell identity, and tumour outgrowth [33]. In addition, abnormalities in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway have been linked to the expansion of BCSCs [3]. The enrichment of CD44high/CD24low cells and the expression of ALDH1A1 are considered as hallmarks of BCSCs [34], while c-Myc expression has been reported to enhance TNBC stemness properties [35]. Our observation that IL-3 prevented β-catenin degradation, leading to c-Myc expression, led us to investigate its impact on the expansion of BCSC markers. As shown in Fig. 3, treating both MD-MBA-436 and Hs-578 T cells with IL-3 significantly increased the population of cells expressing ALDH1A1 (Fig. 3A and C) as well as CD44high/CD24low (Fig. 3B and D). To confirm the presence of a CSC phenotype, we evaluated ALDH enzymatic activity following IL-3 stimulation and miR-155-5p inhibition. Both cell lines exhibited increased ALDH activity in response to IL-3 treatment (Fig. 3E, F), consistent with an enrichment of CSC-like properties. Notably, silencing of miR-155-5p attenuated the IL-3-induced increase in ALDH activity, suggesting that miR-155-5p plays a role in mediating IL-3-driven CSC features. Tumour spheres formation recapitulates the mammary tumour microenvironment (TME), preserving and maintaining the CSC population [36]. Therefore, we tested the impact of both IL-3 and miR-155-5p on reshaping the stemness compartment. IL-3 administration significantly increased MFE, while inhibiting miR-155-5p with the antagomiR-155 prevented this effect in both MDA-MB-436 (Fig. 3G) and Hs-578 T (Fig. 3H) cells. Notably, both IL-3 and IL-3R blockade impaired MFE even in unstimulated cells (Fig. 3G–H).

Overall, these findings highlight the role of the IL-3/miR-155-5p axis in driving TNBC cells towards a CSC phenotype. Numerous studies have demonstrated that nuclear accumulation of β-catenin promotes stem-like features and chemoresistance [37, 38]. Consistent with these observations, we found that IL-3, via miR-155-5p, enhanced doxorubicin efflux (Fig. 4A–B) and attenuated its anti-proliferative effects in TNBC cells (Fig. 5A–D). In the Incucyte cell viability experiments, we did not observe a significant difference between the Saline and NC + IL-3 conditions in either cell line. This outcome may be attributed to the limited sensitivity of the instrument in detecting subtle changes in cell morphology and/or potential off-target effects of the scrambled control, which could contribute to increased variability and higher standard deviation in the statistical analysis.

The impact of IL-3/miR-155-5p signalling axis was also examined in vivo by injecting MDA-MB-436 and Hs-578 T in the mammary fat pad of immunocompromised mice as illustrated in Fig. 6A. The results demonstrated that IL-3 treatment increased tumour volume in both MDA-MB-436 (Fig. 6B) and Hs-578 T (Fig. 6H) derived xenografts. Interestingly, the effects of IL-3 were prevented when mice were co-injected with IL-3 and the antagomiR-155 (Fig. 6B, G, H, M). As reported in Fig. 6D-F and J-L, IL-3 administration enhanced the expression of active β-catenin in both tumour models, an effect that was mitigated by miR-155-5p silencing.

miR-155-5p contributes to IL-3 autocrine release in TNBC cells

To gain an insight on IL-3 release, we first evaluated whether it depends on the functional state of the cells by comparing IL-3 secretion in 2D and 3D cultures. As shown in Fig. 7A and E, 3D cultures significantly increased the release of IL-3 without affecting the expression of its receptor IL-3Rα (Supplementary Fig. S5). This observation, along with the finding that TNBC cells express miR-155-5p and release IL-3, led us to investigate whether miR155-5p can contribute to IL-3 release in basal conditions. As shown in Fig. 7B and F, both IL-3 inhibition and miR-155-5p silencing led to a marked reduction in IL-3 secretion, supporting the existence of an autocrine IL-3 signalling loop. Furthermore, analysis of IL-3 expression in tumours confirmed that miR-155-5p plays a role in sustaining IL-3 autocrine production in TNBC cells (Fig. 7D, H). However, since IL-3 release was not completely abrogated by miR-155-5p inhibition both in vitro and in vivo, these results indicate that additional regulatory mechanisms contribute to IL-3 secretion by TNBC cells. Furthermore, the observation that miR-155-5p inhibition prevents any additional effect of IL-3 administration further supports the role of miR-155-5p as a key regulator in this pathway.

Pdcd4 expression is regulated by IL-3 and post-transcriptionally controlled by miR-155-5p

Pdcd4 is included among the genes post-transcriptionally regulated by miR-155-5p [8]. Since Pdcd4 functions as a tumour suppressor involved in controlling the progression of various cancers, we sought to determine whether it may also act with β-catenin to enhance IL-3-mediated tumour progression. As shown in Fig. 8A, IL-3 stimulation down regulates the expression of Pdcd4 in TNBC, an effect that is prevented by the silencing of miR-155-5p (Fig. 8B). However, likely due to the complexity of the tumour microenvironment and its dynamic, temporally restricted regulation in vivo [9], we were unable to detect significant changes in Pdcd4 expression within the tumour tissues (Supplementary Fig. S6).

Discussion

In this study, we first demonstrated that in TNBC, IL-3 induces STAT5 activation and enhances miR-155-5p expression by binding to its promoter. This results in the downregulation of Pdcd4, APC and GSK-3β, leading to β-catenin activation. Consequently, these changes are associated with the expansion of cells expressing stemness markers and the acquisition of drug resistance. Additionally, we found that miR-155-5p regulates IL-3 autocrine production and is involved in IL-3-mediated TNBC tumour growth and metastasis formation in vivo. Overall, these data support the notion that miR-155-5p is a target of the IL-3 signalling, contributes to IL-3 release, and reprograms stem-related pathways in TNBC.

IL-3, traditionally considered a hemopoietic cytokine that acts on progenitors and committed cells, exerts its functional effects via a heterodimeric receptor composed of a binding α-chain (IL-3Rα) and a β-common transducing subunit [39]. While IL-3Rα is mainly expressed on hematopoietic cells, it is also found in vascular and epithelial cells [27, 39, 40], emphasizing its role as a potent inflammatory cytokine involved in several diseases [41]. It is well-established that inflammatory cell infiltrates and the cytokines they release contribute to tumour development and progression [26, 42]. Additionally, given the overlap in signalling pathways between inflammation and cancer, inflammation has long been considered as a hallmark of cancer progression [43].

We have previously shown that IL-3 is secreted autocrinally by tumour endothelial cells, playing a role in recruiting endothelial progenitors and inflammatory cells into tumours by promoting the expression of adhesion molecules [44]. In this study we demonstrate that IL-3, in addition to being released by a vast array of inflammatory cells and tumour endothelial cells within the TME of TNBC [40], is also autocrinally secreted by TNBC cells. Our analysis based on GEO database, further supports this finding, demonstrating that IL-3 is significantly upregulated in TNBC samples compared to the non-TNBC group.

As observed in hemopoietic neoplasia, we and others have shown that TNBC express IL-3R, and that the co-expression of IL-3R and IL-3 is associated with poor overall survival in TNBC patients [26, 27]. IL-3 has a pivotal role in several hematologic neoplasms due to the overexpression of its receptor [23]. Moreover, in hematopoietic disorders IL-3R has long been considered as an aberrantly expressed marker exploitable as a therapeutic target, particularly in acute leukaemia [23, 24]. In blasts or leukemic stem cells, IL-3R expression drives increased cell proliferation, resistance to apoptosis, and poor patient survival [29, 45]. Additionally, in the M4 subtype of acute leukaemia, IL-3-mediated action has been linked to the activation of STAT5A, miR155-5p expression and β-catenin nuclear translocation [12, 25]. Our analysis of TCGA-BRCA data demonstrated a positive correlation of miR-155-5p in TNBC compared to non-tumoral tissues and non-TNBC, as well as a significant association between IL-3, miR-155-5p and STAT5A expression. In vitro studies show that miR-155-5p is highly expressed in TNBC cell lines, and that IL-3 stimulation increases its expression by activating STAT5A which in turn, binds to the BIC sequence of the miR-155-5p promoter, enhancing its expression. Interestingly, the IL-3-induced upregulation of miR-155-5p was less pronounced in other tested cell lines. Based on these observations, we hypothesize that miR-155-5p expression may be regulated by a threshold-dependent mechanism, whereby IL-3 enhances expression only when basal miR-155-5p levels are sufficiently elevated, as is often the case in tumorigenic or highly activated cells. This hypothesis is further supported by the observation that STAT5A binds to the BIC promoter even in unstimulated cells. Together, these findings suggest that the IL-3/miR-155-5p axis may function in a tumour-specific manner and contribute to the heterogeneity of miR-155-5p responses across different cellular contexts. Further studies are needed to define the molecular determinants underlying this threshold effect and to investigate whether other cytokine signalling pathways similarly modulate miR-155-5p expression in a context-dependent manner. Finally, since STAT5A is constitutively activated in several TNBC cell lines and binds to the BIC promoter even in the absence of IL-3 stimulation, these findings provide novel evidence supporting its role in the transcriptional regulation of miR-155-5p in TNBC cells.

Consistent with IL-3 action in hemopoietic cells [12, 25], we demonstrated that IL-3-mediated miR-155-5p expression is instrumental for β-catenin activation and c-Myc expression through the downregulation of two key members of the β-catenin disruption complex. β-catenin activation has been linked to epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), metastasis formation, and chemoresistance in TNBC [3]. The latter has been specifically associated with the synergistic action of two well-known Wnt/β-catenin target genes, c-MYC, and HIF-1α [46]. In addition, in basal like TNBC subtype, the activation of β-catenin together with the presence of a specific YAP signature, has been shown to induce the expansion of CSCs and predict patient overall survival [47]. EMT and CSC formation in TNBC have been widely associated with dysregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signalling pathway [46]. We and others have previously shown that IL-3 can induce EMT and endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition in TNBC, leading to the formation of a tumour derived vascular network that further supports tumour growth [26, 27]. Here, we show that IL-3 can drive stem cell reprogramming signals, including the expansion of ALDH1A1 + and CD44high/CD24low cell populations, β-catenin activation, c-Myc expression, increased MFE efficiency, as well as chemoresistance. These data strongly suggest that nuclear translocation of β-catenin and the expression of c-Myc are key hallmarks of IL-3-mediated tumour cell reprogramming towards a stem-like phenotype, regulated by miR-155-5p expression both in vitro and in vivo.

Several mechanisms influence miR expression during TNBC progression [48]. Based on data provided by Grimaldi et al. [48], multiple miRs have been identified as potential therapeutic targets in BC, among which miR-155-5p has been specifically linked to loss of functional BRCA1 [49]. BRCA1 has been shown to directly target miR-155-5p promoter, inhibiting its expression and thereby suppressing the JAK/STAT3 signalling pathway [50]. In this study, we observed that IL-3 stimulation induced STAT5A phosphorylation and upregulated miR-155-5p not only in the metastatic-derived BRCA1 mutation positive MDA-MD-436 cell line, but also in Hs-578 T cells, which originate from a primary tumour and lack the BRCA1 mutation. This suggests that mechanisms beyond BRCA1 mutation can contribute to STAT5A/miR-155-5p activation in TNBC. Bioinformatic analyses further demonstrated a correlation between miR-155-5p and IL-3 expression, as well as between miR-155-3p or miR-155-5p and STAT5A. These findings support the idea that, independent of BRCA1 mutations, IL-3 can drive specific cues that promote CSC expansion and TNBC progression.

Within the TME, activated inflammatory cells, particularly T cells, are the primary source of IL-3 production [51, 52]. IL-3 expression in T cells is tightly regulated by both transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms [39, 44], with AP-1 identified as a key transcriptional regulator [53]. Notably, AP-1 family members are also downstream targets of IL-3 in haematopoietic cells suggesting a potential feedback loop [54]. AP-1-mediated miR-155-5p expression has been implicated in the regulation of Pdcd4 in several cancers, including lung and tongue tumours [8, 21]. In tongue carcinoma, a miR-155-5p/Pdcd4/AP-1 positive feedback loop was identified as a key driver of miR-155-5p-mediated effects [8]. Although we did not observe significant changes in AP-1 expression in our TNBC model (data not shown), our data suggest that STAT5A may serve a similar role to AP-1 in promoting miR-155-5p expression even under unstimulated conditions. Therefore, since miR-155-5p suppresses Pdcd4, a known inhibitor of AP-1 [9], we propose the existence of a regulatory feedback loop in which Pdcd4 may act as a molecular brake on IL-3 secretion. This model is supported by our findings that miR-155-5p inhibition restores Pdcd4 expression and reduces IL-3 release. In addition, previous studies have demonstrated that Pdcd4 suppresses β-catenin/TCF-dependent transcription and c-Myc expression [22], pathways critical for stemness and tumour proliferation. Therefore, IL-3-mediated STAT5A/miR-155-5p signalling, through Pdcd4 repression, may further promote β-catenin activation and CSC reprogramming, ultimately driving the development of a chemoresistant phenotype. Consistent with this, we observed increased ALDH activity, following IL-3 treatment, which was reversed upon miR-155-5p inhibition.

Overall, this study provides new insights into the mechanisms driving TNBC outgrowth, dissemination, and chemoresistance. We show that IL-3 promotes the expansion of cells with CSC-like properties via miR-155-5p. Notably, we identify STAT5A as a novel upstream regulator of miR-155-5p in TNBC, acting through direct binding to the BIC promoter sequence, a process enhanced by IL-3 stimulation. Finally, given that TNBC cells both secrete IL-3 and express functional IL-3Rα, an emerging therapeutic target [26, 27], our findings suggest that targeting the IL-3/IL-3Rα axis could represent a promising strategy to overcome resistance and improve outcomes in this aggressive breast cancer subtype, which currently lacks approved targeted therapies.
