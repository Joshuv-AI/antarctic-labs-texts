MRI‐Based DeltaHabitat Radiomic Model Predicts Pathological Complete Response in Oral Cavity Cancer Treated With Neoadjuvant Chemoimmunotherapy
Authors: Lin Ding, Jialing Wu, Yangxin Liang, Jiaxuan Ding, Shu Zhou, Liting Li, Lehang Lin, Jiale Zeng, Changlong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to develop and validate an MRI‐based DeltaHabitat radiomics model to predict pathological complete response (pCR) in oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (OCSCC) patients treated with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy.MethodsPatients treated with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy and surgery were retrospectively included from one institution and randomly divided into training and test cohorts using a 7:3 ratio. The region of interest (ROI) for the primary tumor was manually delineated on contrast‐enhanced T1‐weighted MRI, and radiomic features were extracted. The volume of interest was segmented into three subregions using the K‐means clustering algorithm. Following feature selection, five models were constructed to predict pCR in both the training and test cohorts. The efficacy of the models was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsOne hundred and ninety‐five patients were enrolled. The median age was 57 years, and 127 (65.1%) patients were male. Features were extracted from three separate regions, and a total of 5502 features were yielded. After the feature selection process, 12 features were retained. Among radiomic models, the DeltaHabitat model demonstrated a satisfactory area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) in both the training and test cohorts (0.923, 95% CI: 0.880–0.967; 0.878, 95% CI: 0.791–0.964, respectively).ConclusionMRI‐based DeltaHabitat radiomics model demonstrated good performance in predicting pCR in OCSCC patients treated with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. This non‐invasive approach may facilitate early identification of responders and support individualized treatment decision‐making.Keywords: magnetic resonance imaging, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma, pathological complete response, radiomics

Introduction

Locally advanced oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (OCSCC) patients are at significant risk for both distant metastases and local recurrence, even with multidisciplinary treatments that combine surgery, radiation, and chemotherapy [1]. Surgery, including en bloc resection of the primary tumor with or without neck dissection, remains the primary treatment modality for OCSCC patients; however, it can result in disfigurement and organ dysfunction, which underscores the need for less invasive and more effective therapeutic strategies. An innovative therapeutic approach is needed to improve the quality of life and prolong survival for patients. The combination of Programmed cell death 1 (PD‐1) blockade and chemotherapy has received approval for managing recurrent or metastatic OCSCC [2]. Administering PD‐1 inhibitors at earlier disease stages could yield greater efficacy due to the less advanced tumor microenvironment and a less compromised immune system [3]. Moreover, integrating chemotherapy with immunotherapy in a neoadjuvant regimen has shown promising clinical results across different types of cancers [4]. Recent evidence, including the KEYNOTE‐689 study, further supports the expanding role of neoadjuvant immunotherapy in head and neck cancers [5].

Neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy has also emerged as a promising treatment modality, demonstrating improved treatment responses with a pathological complete response (pCR) rate ranging from 16.7% to 55.6% and a major pathological response (MPR) rate ranging from 27.8% to 74.1% in OCSCC patients [4, 6, 7, 8]. Researches indicated that patients who achieved pCR exhibited better survival outcomes [9, 10], and investigations are currently being carried out to explore the efficacy of de‐intensified adjuvant therapy [11, 12]. However, patients who respond poorly to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy may postpone surgery and subsequent treatments. Therefore, it is vital to screen for pCR in oral cavity cancer treated with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. The PD‐L1 combined positive score (CPS) is a commonly utilized predictor for the efficacy of immunotherapy in OCSCC; but it is fraught with the issue of inconsistent PD‐L1 expression across different tumor lesions in a single patient, and even within the same tumor [13]. Other appropriate biomarkers should be investigated to select patients who can benefit from neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, minimizing costs and avoiding unnecessary toxicity.

Radiomic analysis, the technique of obtaining and evaluating a vast number of quantitative features from medical images, has rapidly gained momentum in recent years. By capturing the spatial distribution and texture characteristics of tumors, radiomics holds tremendous potential for predicting treatment response and survival outcomes in various cancers [14, 15]. However, traditional radiomics approaches primarily focus on static analysis of pre‐treatment images, which may overlook the dynamic changes within tumors during treatment. Delta radiomics, by contrast, leverages the temporal variations in radiomic features between pre‐ and post‐treatment images to capture the dynamic evolution of tumor heterogeneity [16, 17, 18]. In addition, habitat analysis further refines the radiomic analysis by identifying distinct subregions within the tumor, providing a more comprehensive understanding of intratumor heterogeneity [19, 20, 21].

In this study, we proposed a novel MRI‐based radiomics signature of the primary tumor, which united tumor temporal and spatial heterogeneity, to predict pCR in OCSCC patients treated with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. By integrating delta radiomics and habitat insights, we aim to develop a robust prediction model for pCR, facilitating personalized treatment decisions and improving patient outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Participants

From November 2020 to April 2023, 540 consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed OCSCC who participated in clinical trial of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy in our institution (NCT05941338, NCT05980702, NCT06130332, NCT04718415) were identified. The exclusion criteria were: (a) patients with recurrent or metastatic malignancy (n = 32), (b) patients who did not receive either neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy or surgery (n = 208), (c) patients without baseline MRI data or MRI data within 2 weeks prior to surgery (n = 81), and (d) patients whose MRI quality was compromised by artifacts (n = 22). The patient enrollment process is shown in Figure 1.

Flow Chart of the patients enrollment process. NCI, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy; OCSCC, oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma; pCR, pathological complete response.

This study ultimately enrolled 195 eligible patients. Patients included in the study were randomly assigned to training and test cohorts in a 7:3 ratio. To ensure the splits were unbiased, the randomization process was repeated multiple times. This was done until the p‐values for nearly all features were greater than 0.05. Tumor‐node‐metastasis (TNM) staging for OCSCC was determined through a comprehensive analysis of pretreatment assessments and postoperative pathological results, adhering to the seventh edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer manual. Patients' clinical characteristics had been obtained from the medical records of our institution. Peripheral blood samples were drawn and subjected to laboratory testing within a week before neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy.

Treatment and Definition of Pathological Complete Response

Of all patients, 95.6% received two to four cycles of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy before surgery. Neoadjuvant immunotherapy included Tirelizumab, Pembrolizumab, and Sintilimab. The neoadjuvant chemotherapy regimen primarily consisted of a combination of nab‐paclitaxel with either cisplatin or carboplatin. All patients underwent cure‐intent surgery, including the resection of the primary tumor and neck dissection. Histopathological assessment was performed on surgical specimens. The definition of pCR was no residual viable tumor cell in both primary tumor and lymph nodes of surgical specimens.

MRI Protocol

Contrast‐enhanced T1‐weighted imaging (CE‐T1WI) with fat suppression was used. All MR imaging scans were conducted on a Philips Ingenia 3.0 T scanner, employing the Turbo Spin Echo (TSE) sequence. Water‐fat separation was achieved using the modified Dixon technique. The contrast agent Gd‐DTPA (gadopentetate dimeglumine, from Germany) was administered at a dose of 0.2 mL/kg and injected manually at a rate of 1 mL per second. Scanning was performed 3 min after the injection of the contrast agent. For detailed MRI acquisition parameters, please refer to Table S1.

Image Segmentation and Data Preprocessing

The primary tumor region of interest (ROI) were manually delineated on each consecutive transverse slice of the CE‐T1WI by two experienced radiation oncologists (D.L. and C.L.C.) who were blinded to the histopathological results. One of the radiation oncologists (D.L.) then delineated the ROI of 30 randomly selected patients 1 month later to assess the consistency and reliability of radiomic features using intraobserver intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) analysis. The delineation was carried out using the ITK‐SNAP software (version 4.0.2; www.itk‐snap.org). This study enhanced medical image analysis by employing standardization and normalization techniques. The voxel spacing was standardized to 1 mm × 1 mm × 1 mm, ensuring uniformity across images from various sources. Figure 2 outlines the workflow of the present study.

Overall workflow of this study.

Habitat Generation

Following data preprocessing, this study conducted a detailed analysis of local features within the volume of interest (VOI) using MR images. For each voxel, habitat regions were constructed based on pre‐ and post‐neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy MR images. A 3 × 3 × 3 moving window technique was employed to compute local features, resulting in the construction of a comprehensive feature vector for each voxel. These vectors facilitated the application of the K‐means clustering algorithm for segmenting the VOI, with the number of clusters ranging from three to ten. The optimal number of clusters was identified using the Calinski‐Harabasz (CH) score. More detailed information regarding the habitat generation process can be found in the Supporting Information (Part I). Figure 3 presents the habitat regions generated.

The habitat regions and five characteristics pre‐ and post‐neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy.

Feature Extraction

Radiomic features were classified into geometry, intensity, and texture, with each category serving to analyze different aspects of tumor characteristics. Geometry features define the tumor's shape, intensity features evaluate voxel brightness levels, and texture features capture spatial patterns. Radiomic features were extracted from both the entire VOI and discrete subregions within the tumor. Due to the unsupervised clustering algorithm's nature, we employed the K‐Nearest Neighbors method to label uncluttered areas. Feature extraction was conducted with the Pyradiomics tool (version 3.0.1).

Feature Selection

Radiomic features with ICCs lower than 0.80 were discarded from subsequent analyses. Feature selection began with z‐score normalization for consistency across scales. Statistically significant features were identified using t‐tests at p < 0.05. Redundancy was minimized by eliminating feature pairs with Pearson correlation coefficients ≥ 0.9 and applying the minimum Redundancy Maximum Relevance (mRMR) algorithm to narrow down to the top 32 relevant features. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression with 10‐fold cross‐validation to determine the optimal λ in the training cohort was essential in final feature selection, further increasing the robustness of our radiomic analysis.

Models Building

Five models were constructed to predict pCR in both the training and test cohorts: (1) delta radiomics model, (2) pre‐habitat model, (3) DeltaHabitat model, (4) clinical models, and (5) combined model. All clinical features were used for the construction of clinical model. To construct a combined model, we integrated clinical characteristics that showed p‐value < 0.05 in both univariate and multivariate analyses, along with the DeltaHabitat features. Combined model was constructed using logistic regression (LR), and visually represented in the form of a nomogram. Machine learning algorithms, including LR, random forest (RF), ExtraTrees, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) were performed using Scikit‐learn version 1.0.2.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were presented as means with standard deviations and counts with percentages, respectively. The normality of the distribution of clinical features was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed continuous variables, we used independent t‐tests to evaluate significance, whereas the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non‐normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were analyzed using Chi‐square (χ 2) tests. Association between different clinical features was calculated by spearman's rank correlation coefficient. An extensive univariate analysis of all clinical features was conducted. Features exhibiting p‐values below the 0.05 threshold in the univariate analysis were subjected to the multivariate analysis.

The efficacy of the models for predicting pCR to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy was meticulously evaluated. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were employed to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy. DeLong test was used to compare differences in area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values between models. Calibration curves and the Hosmer‐Lemeshow (HL) test were employed to validate the models' precision and predictive reliability. Finally, decision curve analysis (DCA) was utilized to assess the clinical utility. All data analyses were conducted using Python 3.7.12. For statistical analysis, Statsmodels version 0.13.2 was utilized. A two‐sided p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Results

Patients Characteristics

Detailed demographics and clinical characteristics of 195 patients, including 136 patients in the training cohort and 59 patients in the test cohort, are presented in Table 1. Baseline characteristics were balanced between the two datasets (all p‐values > 0.05 except for cT). The mean age was 56 years (range, 26–86 years; interquartile range, 48–65 years), and 127 (65.1%) patients were male. 54.9% of the patients were T3‐4, and 108 (55.4%) patients had lymph node metastasis. 59 (30.3%) patients achieved pCR (ypT0N0). Univariate analysis showed that cT stage, cN stage, central memory T cells, neoadjuvant chemotherapy, treatment course, drinking history, age, LDH, ALP, and ALB, among others, were associated with pCR. Subsequently, multivariate analysis indicated that central memory T cells were an independent predictor of pCR. The results of univariate and multivariate analyses are presented in Table S2. To further evaluate the potential impact of tumor stage on treatment response, a subgroup analysis was performed comparing early‐stage (stage I–II) and locally advanced (stage III–IV) patients. The pCR rate was 39.1% in early‐stage patients and 27.5% in locally advanced patients. No statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups (χ 2 = 1.73, p = 0.188) (Table S3). These findings suggest that tumor stage did not significantly influence treatment response in this cohort. The distribution of pCR and non‐pCR rates among different immunotherapy agents is summarized in Table S4. Although numerical differences were observed across groups, no statistically significant difference was detected (χ 2 = 6.49, p = 0.166).

Number of Clusters

The impact of various clustering centers from three to ten was assessed. Both pre‐ and post‐neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy MR imaging data were analyzed, and three was identified as the optimal number of clustering centers for delineating subregions in our habitat analysis. This number provided the best balance between capturing intratumor heterogeneity and preventing over‐segmentation. Radiomic Features

A total of 1834 habitat radiomic features were extracted and classified into shape, first‐order, and texture. The delta radiomics approach employed the same set of 1834 features. Additionally, features were extracted from three separate regions, which collectively yielded a total of 5502 features. After the feature selection process, 12 features with non‐zero coefficients were retained for the construction of the DeltaHabitat model (Figure 5).

Predictive Performance of DeltaHabitat Model Constructed by Different ML Algorithms

The RandomForest model exhibited the highest AUC values in both the training (0.923, 95% CI: 0.880–0.967) and test (0.878, 95% CI: 0.791–0.964) cohorts. The ExtraTrees (0.880, 95% CI: 0.822–0.939; 0.826, 95% CI: 0.702–0.951) and XGBoost (0.887, 95% CI: 0.826–0.949; 0.816, 95% CI: 0.679–0.954) models also showed high AUC values. The results are presented in Figure S1. Other diagnostic metrics of the models derived from different machine learning algorithms on the training and test cohorts are shown in Table S5.

Models Performance for Prediction of pCR and Clinical Utility

The DeltaHabitat model demonstrated satisfactory AUC in both the training (0.923, 95% CI: 0.880–0.967) and test (0.878, 95% CI: 0.791–0.964) cohorts. The combined model, which integrates DeltaHabitat features with clinical data, showed further improvement in AUC, achieving values of 0.964 (95% CI: 0.936–0.991) on the training cohort and 0.915 (95% CI: 0.841–0.988) on the test cohort (Table 2 and Figure 6a,d). Additional details regarding delta radiomics, pre‐habitat radiomics, and clinical model can be found in Table S6–S8 and Figure S2–S4. DeLong test revealed a statistically significant improvement in the AUC for the combined model compared to the other models, except for the DeltaHabitat model in the test cohort (Figure S5). A nomogram was employed to visualize the results of the combined model, as shown in Figure S6. The combined model exhibited excellent calibration, with HL values of 0.383 in the training cohort (Figure 6b) and 0.124 in the test cohort (Figure 6e). The DCA curve analysis revealed that the combined model provided a significant net benefit based on its predicted probabilities. The results can be found in Figure 6c,f.

Discussion

Immunotherapy in the neoadjuvant setting for treatment of oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma (OCSCC) is becoming more prevalent in clinical practice [22]. However, identifying patients who can benefit from immunotherapy continues to be a significant clinical dilemma [13]. In recent years, advancements in machine learning and deep learning technologies have led to an increase in studies emphasizing the potential of radiomics in diagnosing, predicting survival outcomes, and estimating treatment responses across various types of malignancies [14, 15, 18, 23]. By capturing the spatial distribution and texture characteristics of tumors, radiomics can provide valuable information regarding the treatment response mechanisms. Studies have shown that radiomics demonstrated excellent predictive performance in predicting treatment outcomes, including those for induction chemotherapy [18, 24], immunotherapy [15], and chemoradiotherapy [25]. Our study demonstrated the feasibility and effectiveness of using radiomics analysis to predict pCR in OCSCC patients treated with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. The DeltaHabitat model achieved high performance metrics (AUC, training cohort: 0.923; test cohort: 0.878), indicating the critical role of capturing temporal changes in tumor microenvironment during treatment.

In the context of nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC), studies have employed radiomics as a non‐invasive tool to forecast therapeutic response. For instance, Wang et al. examined the potential of pretreatment MRI‐derived radiomic features in predicting the initial response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in patients with NPC. They found that the multiple image‐based radiomics model performed better than single, with 15 features selected from morphological MR imaging exhibiting the highest AUC value of 0.822 [26]. Similarly, another study aimed to distinguish response from non‐response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy in patients with NPC. Radiomic features derived from CE‐T1WI and T2WI were selected and combined with clinical characteristics to construct radiomic models, which exhibited AUCs of 0.940 and 0.952 in the primary and validation cohorts, respectively [27]. These studies suggested that radiomics can be used to identify patients who are sensitive to specific treatment modalities, thus guiding clinicians to make personalized treatment decisions.

However, in other head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, including OCSCC, the application of radiomics predominantly focused on two main areas: first, in the diagnostic domain, predicting lymph node metastasis [28, 29, 30], tumor staging, and tumor cell differentiation [31]; second, in the prognostic prediction of survival outcomes, including the prediction of recurrence [32, 33], overall survival [34, 35, 36], and the incidence of treatment‐related adverse effects [14, 37]. Therefore, our study is the first to explore the utility of radiomics in predicting treatment response in OCSCC patients who have undergone neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. Our study may further serve as a reference for the application of radiomics to predict treatment response in other head and neck squamous cell carcinomas, such as hypopharyngeal, laryngeal, and sinonasal cancers.

Moreover, studies have shown that, compared to traditional radiomics, habitat‐based radiomics can better reflect tumor heterogeneity [18, 38]. Habitats, or tumor subregions, are characterized by cellular homogeneity in genotype and phenotype. Meanwhile, intratumor heterogeneity is a known predictor of therapeutic resistance and unfavorable clinical outcomes [21]. Previous studies have demonstrated that radiomic features generated by the habitat method can be leveraged to predict lymphovascular space invasion [39], facilitate diagnostic determinations [20, 40], predict progression‐free survival [38, 41], and evaluate treatment response [42, 43, 44], among other applications. In our study, segmenting the tumor into different subregions using the K‐means clustering algorithm [20] allowed us to identify specific intra‐tumor habitats and gain deeper insights into intratumor heterogeneity.

Additionally, in our study, compared to the pre‐habitat model, the DeltaHabitat model showed stronger discriminative ability to differentiate between pCR and non‐pCR. Delta radiomics captures feature changes from various acquisition times, including before and after specific treatments. The true potential of delta radiomics lies in its strengths in predicting treatment efficacy and guiding adaptive treatment approaches [16, 17, 37, 45]. Our study revealed that changes in MRI‐based radiomic features before and after treatment were more predictive than pre‐treatment features alone, highlighting the importance of radiomic feature changes for personalized cancer treatments. Lastly, the combined model, which integrates DeltaHabitat features with clinical data, further improved prediction accuracy, highlighting the importance of integrating imaging and clinical information for comprehensive patient assessment, and our findings were consistent with previous studies [18, 27, 43].

From a clinical perspective, the proposed model may provide additional value in treatment decision‐making. Specifically, early identification of non‐responders to neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy could help avoid prolonged ineffective treatment and facilitate timely transition to definitive surgery, thereby potentially reducing unnecessary delays in surgical management. It is important to recognize a few of our study's shortcomings. First, the accuracy of the DeltaHabitat model depends on reliable tumor subvolume registration across longitudinal MRI scans. Potential misregistration may introduce bias in the extraction of subregional features. Second, the relatively limited sample size may reduce the statistical power of subgroup analyses, and future studies with larger cohorts and prospective designs are warranted. For example, although the combined model showed a high AUC in the training cohort, its performance remained stable in the test cohort, suggesting acceptable generalizability. However, potential overfitting cannot be completely excluded and requires further validation in larger cohorts. Second, this study lacks external validation. All data were derived from a single institution, which may limit generalizability. External validation in multi‐center cohorts is warranted. Third, our study focused on OCSCC, and further investigation is necessary to determine the applicability of our radiomic strategy in other types of head and neck cancers. Fourth, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy has not been incorporated into clinical practice guidelines and does not currently qualify as a standard treatment regimen in OCSCC; it remains an experimental strategy under clinical research. Nonetheless, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy is gaining traction as a promising therapeutic approach, with preliminary data demonstrating its potential to yield positive clinical responses. Another limitation of this study is the lack of standardized data on response‐adapted surgical strategies. Although surgical extent may be influenced by treatment response and could affect pCR outcomes, surgical decision‐making in current clinical practice is largely based on pre‐treatment evaluation, as no established consensus on response‐adapted strategies has been reached. We also noticed a slight imbalance in cT stage that remained due to sample size constraints. However, both multivariate analysis and sensitivity analysis indicated that this imbalance had minimal impact on the study conclusions. It is also crucial to acknowledge that the area of radiomics is still evolving, and further investigation is required to refine and validate radiomic models for predicting treatment response in OCSCC.

In conclusion, our study presented a novel and promising model for predicting pCR in OCSCC patients treated with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy. The integration of delta radiomics and habitat analysis offered a more comprehensive understanding of tumor heterogeneity and treatment response dynamics, potentially leading to more personalized management of OCSCC.
