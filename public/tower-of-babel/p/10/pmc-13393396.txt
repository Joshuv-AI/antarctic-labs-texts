Surfactant-Induced Interfacial Anisotropy Governs Polarization-Selective Whispering Gallery Mode Excitation in Dye-Doped Trapped Microdroplets
Authors: Sadok Kouz, Qisheng Xu, Naheed Hossain, Aatir Khan, Igor Chaussavoine, Satoko Yoshizawa, Dominique Fourmy, Abdel I. El Abed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Surfactant-stabilized liquid microdroplets are soft photonic cavities whose optical response is strongly influenced by the molecular organization of their liquid–liquid interface. In this work, we investigate polarization-dependent whispering-gallery-mode (WGM) excitation in Rhodamine 640-doped benzyl-alcohol microdroplets stabilized with a custom fluorocarbon surfactant. The surfactant layer promotes interfacial ordering of Rhodamine 640 transition dipoles, creating an anisotropic boundary environment that preferentially enhances TE-like WGM emission under vertically polarized excitation. Compared with horizontally polarized excitation at matched pump energy, vertically polarized excitation produces WGM peaks up to approximately 1 order of magnitude stronger. Fourier-transform analysis confirms that the dominant resonances circulate along the droplet equator, with extracted path lengths consistent with the optical perimeter for both polarization states. Representative analytical labeling further supports the assignment of interleaved TE-like and TM-like resonance families within the dominant first-radial-order branch. Control measurements on surfactant-depleted droplets and nematic liquid-crystal droplets support an interfacial origin of the observed polarization selectivity. These results show that surfactant-mediated interfacial ordering provides a practical route for tuning polarization-dependent light–matter coupling in soft WGM microcavities, with potential relevance for droplet-based microlasers and polarization-sensitive sensing.

Introduction

Whispering-gallery modes (WGMs) in spherical dielectric microcavitiesincluding solid microspheres and liquid microdropletsoffer exceptional optical confinement and high quality factors, making them powerful platforms for label-free sensing, tunable microlasers, and fundamental studies of light–matter interactions. − Liquid microdroplets, in particular, provide unique advantages due to their inherent softness and reconfigurability, enabling dynamic tuning of optical resonances through changes in size, shape, or composition. − However, these benefits come with challenges related to stability, reproducibility, and precise control over mode excitationlimitations that have hindered the full exploitation of droplets as tunable photonic cavities.

Beyond static microdroplet cavities, recent work has pushed WGMs toward more application-oriented sensing modalities, including active WGM biosensors, hybrid optoplasmonic architectures, and microfluidically designed monodisperse WGM/LSPR composite microspheres. − These developments motivate renewed interest in soft WGM platforms, where the cavity boundary is not a rigid solid interface but a chemically programmable liquid interface that can be tailored for functionality rather than treated as a limitation.

The role of surfactants in governing the interfacial properties of emulsions is well-established in colloid and soft-matter science. Beyond their classical role as emulsion stabilizers, surfactants induce molecular ordering at liquid–liquid interfaces that can be probed by optical methods such as second-harmonic generation, ellipsometry, or polarized fluorescence. Here, whispering-gallery modes serve as an especially sensitive optical probe: the polarization-dependent coupling efficiency of WGMs directly encodes the degree and orientation of interfacial molecular anisotropy, enabling a cavity-enhanced readout of interfacial order.

A critical yet often overlooked degree of freedom in soft microcavities is the molecular organization at the droplet interface. Surfactants, essential for stabilizing emulsions, can induce interfacial anisotropy that governs the orientation of embedded fluorescent dyes. − This anisotropy can selectively couple to specific optical polarizations, offering a novel route to control whispering-gallery modes without altering the droplet’s size or refractive index.

From an interfacial-physics viewpoint, the droplet boundary can concentrate, orient, and dynamically reorganize fluorescent molecules, so that emission becomes a sensitive reporter of interfacial composition and electrostatic/chemical conditions. Such interface-localized fluorescence effects in droplet microfluidics provide a concrete precedent for expecting polarization-dependent emission once molecular ordering is imposed at the oil–water boundary.

While polarization-dependent coupling and selection rules in solid WGM resonators are well documented , its manifestation in surfactant-stabilized dye-doped dropletswhere interfacial order mediates the optical responseremains far less explored. This gap is significant, as interfacial control could enable new functionalities in soft photonics, such as polarization-selective lasing, sensing, and dynamic mode tuning. , Unlike solid microcavities where polarization selectivity arises from birefringence or coupling geometry, here, we demonstrate that surfactant-induced interfacial anisotropy enables polarization-selective excitation of whispering-gallery modes in rhodamine-doped microdroplets. Using a custom fluorocarbon surfactant (Krytox–BTA), we engineer tangential alignment of Rhodamine 640 molecules at the oil–droplet interface. This molecular alignment creates an anisotropic optical environment that strongly favors excitation of transverse-electric (TE) modes under vertically polarized light, enhancing their intensity by up to an order of magnitude compared to horizontally polarized excitation. Through Fourier-transform analysis of emission spectra, we confirm equatorial WGM circulation and resolve polarization-dependent TE-like and TM-like resonance families. , While the intrinsic polarization selectivity of spherical WGM cavities arises from Fresnel reflectivity differences between TE and TM modes, our results demonstrate that surfactant-stabilized interfaces can further enhance this inherent asymmetry through interfacial molecular alignment. These results connect soft-matter interfacial science and cavity photonics, establishing surfactant design as a practical strategy for tailoring optical confinement in reconfigurable microcavities. This approach opens new pathways for tunable droplet-based microlasers and polarization-sensitive biosensors, where molecular-level order at the interface becomes a functional handle for photonic control.

Experimental Section

Design and Fabrication of the Microfluidic Platform

To investigate the role of surfactant-induced interfacial anisotropy on WGM polarization, we developed an integrated microfluidic platform for precise droplet generation, trapping, and polarization-resolved spectroscopy. The microfluidic devices used in our experiments were fabricated by standard soft lithography techniques in poly­(dimethylsiloxane) (PDMS). The system consisted of a dropmaker with a flow-focusing geometry, followed by a reservoir and a set of microtraps designed to capture individual droplets, as illustrated in Figure (for further details on different geometries and observation techniques, see refs − ).

The entrance of the dropmaker, shown in Figure a, incorporates a specially designed filter to prevent dirt particles larger than 20 μm from entering the channel. This ensures a clean microfluidic environment and maintains the quality of droplet formation. The dropmaker device has two outputs: one to remove excess oil and another to transfer droplets, through tubing (indicated by a red dashed line in Figure ), to a secondary microfluidic chamber equipped with microfluidic traps (microwells).

The microfluidic trapping area, depicted in Figure b, consists of a 2D reservoir populated with regularly spaced microwells. These wells are formed from cylindrical pillars in the mold, which transform into trapping holes in the final PDMS device. This design enables efficient immobilization of individual droplets, allowing for stable optical measurements under static conditions.

Furthermore, the microfluidic chamber integrates SU-8-based optical waveguides, represented in yellow in Figure c. These waveguides are fabricated within the device plane to efficiently guide the light emitted from the droplets out of the PDMS chip. This configuration reduces scattering losses and significantly improves the quality and sensitivity of the spectroscopic measurements, as shown in the present study.

To ensure that droplets keep their spherical geometry when trapped in the microwellessential for supporting whispering gallery modes (WGMs)the SU-8 mold used to fabricate the microfluidic chip was spin-coated to a thickness of approximately 120 μm. This thickness was chosen to match the average droplet diameter (ranging between 60 and 120 μm) and was achieved using SU-8 2075 at a spin speed of around 2000 rpm. A shallower trap would lead to vertical compression of droplets, deforming them into oblate shapes unsuitable for sustaining high-Q optical modes. By matching the trap height to the droplet size, we ensured full spatial confinement without distortion, which is critical for the formation of angularly symmetric WGMs.

Finally, Figure d illustrates the various phases of the droplet filling and trapping process: droplets are guided into the reservoir, trapped into wells, and excess droplets are flushed out by increasing the oil flow rate. After complete trapping, the channel can be disconnected and transferred to the optical setup for spectral analysis.

To enhance the detection of WGM optical signals, we incorporated optical waveguides made of SU-8 photoresist on the PDMS chip, as illustrated in Figure b–c. The waveguides were fabricated in-plane within close proximity to the droplet trap wells, extending outward to a collection port. The high refractive index (n ≈ 1.60) guarantees that the emitted light is effectively coupled from the droplet interface into the waveguide through the PDMS slab which separates the microfluidic trapping chamber and forward to the detection optics. This setup minimizes bulk PDMS scattering and increases the spectral signal-to-noise ratio. The refractive-index gradient creates optimal conditions for preferential coupling of WGMs while effectively suppressing background fluorescence. The waveguide configuration was particularly useful for resolving subtle spectral features that were essential for the subsequent Fourier analysis of mode structure presented in Section .

This design improved the signal quality required for reliable spectral analysis in droplet-based spectroscopy. Previous studies involving SU-8 optofluidic waveguides , have demonstrated notably enhanced coupling efficiency under optimal alignment conditions, representing a substantial improvement over conventional free-space collection methods, which typically recover less than 10% of the emitted signal. , This enhanced collection efficiency, combined with reduced scattering losses (α < 0.5 dB/cm), enabled clear resolution of adjacent modes and the free spectral range (FSR). Furthermore, directional propagation through the waveguide preserved polarization information that proved essential for accurate mode identification.

Droplet Generation, Trapping, and Surfactant Engineering

Highly monodisperse microdroplets were produced using a flow-focusing geometry with Nemesis syringe pumps (Cetoni GmbH). The dispersed phase consisted of Rhodamine 640 dissolved in benzyl alcohol (0.2 mM), chosen for its high refractive index (n = 1.54 at 25 °C) and strong absorption at 532 nm. The continuous phase was fluorocarbon oil HFE-7500 (n = 1.29), which does not swell PDMS and minimizes dye partitioning. The large refractive-index contrast (Δn ≈ 0.25) between droplet and oil ensures efficient total internal reflection, a prerequisite for whispering-gallery modes (WGMs). The effective refractive index of the dye-doped solution, measured using a Fabry–Perot cavity, was 1.60 ± 0.02, corresponding to a ∼5% uncertainty that does not hinder mode identification.

As illustrated in Figure , droplets were transferred via PTFE tubing to the trapping chamber. Once all wells were occupied, the flow was stopped and excess droplets were flushed out by increasing the oil flow rate. The trapping mechanism is depicted in Figure : droplets entering the well are initially deformed by the channel walls but relax to a spherical shape once trapped, owing to the dominant role of surface tension and the existence of trapping minima associated with surface-energy landscapes. The system operates in the Stokes regime (Re = ρvD/μ ≪ 1), where viscous forces dominate and flow is laminar. The corresponding capillary number Ca = νρv/γ remains ≪1, indicating that surface-tension forces maintain droplet sphericity against deformation. , This hydrodynamic stability is essential for preserving high-quality optical resonances during spectral acquisition.

(a, b) Mechanism of droplet trapping and release in a microwell: (a.i–a.iii) a droplet enters the well, deforms temporarily, and relaxes to a sphere upon trapping; (b.i–b.iv) under increased flow, drag overcomes surface tension and the droplet is released. The surfactant reduces interfacial tension, enhances droplet stability, and promotes tangential molecular alignment at the oil–droplet interface.

To prevent coalescence and control interfacial properties, we added 2 wt % of a custom fluorocarbon surfactant to the oil phase, following standard considerations on surfactant adsorption and kinetic emulsion stabilization in microfluidic conditions.

The surfactant (Krytox-BTA) was synthesized by reacting a commercial carboxy-terminated fluorinated polymer (Krytox 157-FSH, DuPont) with benzyl-trimethylammonium hydroxide (BTA, Sigma-Aldrich). This amphiphile strongly adsorbs at the oil–droplet interface, leading to the formation of a dense interfacial layer, as evidenced by the significant reduction in interfacial tension from 8.0 mN/m (without surfactant) to 4.5 mN/m (with surfactant), as measured by the pendant-drop method (Figure ). While a systematic concentration-dependent study was not performed, the chosen concentration of 2 wt % Krytox–BTA is expected to lie in a regime where interfacial coverage approaches saturation under typical microfluidic conditions. Fluorinated surfactants such as Krytox-based derivatives are known for their high interfacial activity and rapid adsorption kinetics, leading to efficient interface occupation even at moderate concentrations. The observed reduction in interfacial tension therefore provides indirect but strong evidence of substantial surfactant coverage at the interface. This interfacial layer is central to the molecular alignment effects discussed in the following sections. Beyond stabilization, this surfactant layer induces tangential alignment of Rhodamine 640 molecules at the interface , a key factor for the polarization-dependent optical response reported in Section .

Lowering the interfacial tension has two main benefits: (i) it enhances droplet stability against coalescence during trapping and handling, preserving spherical geometry and optical quality; and (ii) it allows higher flow rates during droplet generation without breakup, improving throughput and monodispersity. Prior to experiments, channel walls were treated with a commercial fluorosilane coating to promote wetting by the oil phase and ensure reliable droplet transport.

Absorption Spectrum of Rhodamine 640

To ensure efficient optical pumping of whispering-gallery modes (WGMs), we characterized the absorption spectrum of Rhodamine 640 in two high-refractive-index solvents: benzyl alcohol (n = 1.54) and ethylene glycol (n = 1.43). Normalized absorption spectra (Figure ) show a broad peak centered near 530 nm for both solvents, overlapping strongly with the 532 nm Nd:YAG pump laser used in our experiments. Although the absorbance in benzyl alcohol is slightly higher, the primary reason for selecting this solvent was its higher refractive index, which enhances the contrast with the surrounding oil (n oil = 1.29) and thus improves total-internal-reflection confinement.

Optical Setup and Polarization Control

The whispering-gallery mode (WGM) spectra were recorded using a custom free-space optical setup optimized for polarization-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy (Figure ). A frequency-doubled pulsed Nd:YAG laser (λ = 532 nm, pulse duration = 500 ps, repetition rate = 10 Hz) served as the excitation source. The laser output was confirmed to be highly linearly polarized, with a modulation depth of M = (I max – I min)/(I max + I min) ≈ 0.94 measured using a polarizer and power meter. The beam was expanded to a diameter of ∼100 μm and focused onto individual trapped droplets through a 10× microscope objective, ensuring uniform illumination of the droplet cross-section.

Schematic of the polarization-resolved optical setup. A pulsed 532 nm Nd:YAG laser passes through a linear polarizer for intensity control, followed by another λ/2 plate to set the excitation polarization (vertical or horizontal). The beam is focused onto a trapped microdroplet; emitted light is collected in the equatorial plane and coupled into a spectrometer via a multimode fiber. A secondary imaging path enables real-time droplet monitoring.

To probe the polarization sensitivity of whispering-gallery modes, the incident polarization was precisely controlled using a combination of a half-wave (λ/2) plate and a linear polarizer, enabling sequential excitation with two orthogonal linear polarization states. In our experimental geometry, the pump laser propagates vertically downward (along the ẑ-direction, normal to the microfluidic plane). The trapped droplet rests in the horizontal xy-plane. We define our polarization states as follows:

Vertical (TE-like) polarization: The incident electric field oscillates horizontally and is tangent to the droplet equator (ŷ-direction in our optical frame), maximizing the coupling to equatorial TE modes. These modes exhibit dominant electric field components parallel to the interface. ,

Horizontal (TM-like) polarization: The incident electric field oscillates perpendicular to the tangential direction (x-direction), introducing a larger effective radial component with respect to the droplet surface; this configuration favors coupling to TM-like WGMs, which typically exhibit higher radiation leakage in curved dielectric cavities. ,

These TE/TM-like labels follow the standard classification of polarization families in whispering-gallery resonators, where boundary conditions at the dielectric interface govern the field orientation and coupling behavior. ,,

A linear polarizer together with a λ/2 plate allowed continuous adjustment of the pump intensity without altering the polarization state. These controls were essential for distinguishing between transverse-electric (TE) and transverse-magnetic (TM) mode families, which exhibit different coupling efficiencies depending on the incident polarization and the spatial orientation of the electric field relative to the droplet sphere. This coordinate system convention (x̂, ŷ, ẑ with the droplet in the xy-plane and the pump propagating along −ẑ) is maintained throughout all subsequent polarization-resolved measurements and analysis.

Emission was collected in the equatorial plane via a far-field lens (18 cm from the sample) and coupled into a multimode optical fiber connected to a grating spectrometer (Acton SpectraPro 2500i, Teledyne) equipped with a thermoelectrically cooled CCD camera (PIXIS 110B, Princeton Instruments). The spectrometer was configured with a 1200 grooves/mm grating and a 50 μm entrance slit, providing a resolution of ∼0.1 nmsufficient to resolve individual WGM peaks. Signals were averaged over 10 consecutive laser pulses to improve the signal-to-noise ratio. A secondary imaging path with a CMOS camera (UI324xCP-C, IDS Imaging) and a Navitar 6000 zoom lens enabled real-time monitoring of droplet position and emission patterns, ensuring accurate alignment throughout the measurements.

The pulse energy was adjusted to ∼4–6 μJ, corresponding to the operating range in which WGM peaks are most clearly resolved above the broadband Rh640 fluorescence background. At lower pulse energies, resonances are detectable but exhibit reduced signal-to-noise ratio and less reliable linewidth (Q-factor) extraction, whereas higher pump energies can increase the broadband background and spectral complexity. We therefore use the 4–6 μJ range as an optimized excitation window for polarization-resolved WGM analysis rather than as a universal stimulated-emission threshold. Representative power-dependent spectra illustrating the progressive emergence and sharpening of WGM resonances with increasing pump energy are provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S1). This “enhanced-emission” regime amplifies the resonant peaks relative to the broadband fluorescence background, facilitating subsequent Fourier-transform analysis.

Uncertainty Quantification

The extraction of the geometric path length l geo from whispering-gallery mode (WGM) spectra is subject to measurement uncertainties originating from four principal sources: the spectrometer resolution δλ, the fitting error on the free spectral range δ­(Δλ), the variation in the group refractive index δN, and the uncertainty in droplet radius δR.

The geometrical optical path length l geo can be estimated from the free spectral range of whispering-gallery modes using the standard relation , 1 lgeo=λ2ngΔλ where λ is the central wavelength of the analyzed spectral window, Δλ is the measured free spectral range, and n g is the group refractive index of the droplet medium. This expression is used here to extract the effective round-trip optical path length associated with the dominant resonance family, rather than to assign the radial mode order. For higher radial-order modes, the detailed resonance wavelengths are shifted by radial-order-dependent terms in the full WGM characteristic equation. However, eq remains useful for identifying the dominant spectral periodicity and testing whether the corresponding optical path length is consistent with equatorial circulation around the droplet perimeter. In the present work, radial-order assignment is therefore not based on eq alone, but on its consistency with the expected perimeter 2πR, the polarization dependence, and the analytical WGM considerations discussed below.

The wavelength uncertainty δλ = 0.1 nm is set by the spectrometer resolution, while the FSR fitting error δ­(Δλ) = 0.08 nm reflects the finite width of the dominant peak in the Fourier amplitude spectrum. The group-index uncertainty δn g/n g = 7% accounts for batch-to-batch differences in dye concentration, solvent composition, and dispersion over the analyzed spectral window. For typical values λ ≈ 600 nm and Δλ ≈ 1.2 nm, eq gives δl geo/l geo ≈ 9.6%.

This value is used as a conservative bound when assessing whether the dominant FT peak is consistent with equatorial WGM circulation.

Results and Discussion

Whispering Gallery Modes and Polarization Control in Trapped Microdroplets

Fluorescence Imaging: Onset of WGM Emission

The optical characterization of whispering-gallery modes (WGMs) in trapped dye-doped droplets begins with direct fluorescence imaging. Under low pump energy (<1 μJ), droplets emit homogeneous, isotropic fluorescence (Figure b), consistent with spontaneous emission from Rhodamine 640 distributed throughout the droplet volume. When the pump energy is increased into the optimized WGM-emission regime, a bright ring forms at the droplet periphery (Figure c), which is the spatial hallmark of light confined by total internal reflection along the droplet boundary and recirculating as WGMs.

(b) Single droplet emission under low-intensity excitation (<1 μJ), displaying homogeneous fluorescence. (c) Same droplet under high-intensity excitation (∼6 μJ), exhibiting ring-like emission from whispering gallery modes confined at the droplet interface.

Interface-Weighted WGM Emission

In whispering-gallery-mode (WGM) resonators, the optical field is strongly confined near the droplet boundary, with maximum intensity localized within a thin region close to the interface. As a result, emission from molecules located at or near the interface is preferentially coupled into WGM resonances, whereas emission from molecules distributed in the bulk contributes primarily to a broadband fluorescence background. Therefore, even if only a fraction of Rhodamine 640 molecules resides at the interface, their contribution can dominate the observed resonant peaks due to this strong spatial overlap with the WGM field. In the presence of the Krytox–BTA surfactant, this effect is further enhanced by the tangential alignment of transition dipoles at the interface, which promotes efficient coupling to TE-like modes and reinforces the polarization-selective emission mechanism discussed below.

Surfactant-Depleted Droplets

To establish a baseline with reduced surfactant-induced interfacial ordering, we performed a control measurement on a surfactant-depleted (washed) trapped droplet. In practice, droplets initially produced and stabilized with the surfactant were held in the traps while pure HFE-7500 oil was flowed for an extended period, progressively stripping surfactant from the oil-droplet interface (schematic protocol; see caption for details). The resulting spectrum (Figure a) exhibits sharp, quasi-periodic resonances superimposed on the broadband Rh640 background, confirming WGM feedback under these baseline conditions. In this case, individual whispering-gallery modes split into multiple subpeaks (doublets, triplets, and higher-order multiplets) with typical nearest-neighbor spacings of ∼0.2–0.4 nm (Figure a, inset). Notably, these subpeaks exhibit comparable intensities, indicating no strong polarization selectivity in the absence of surfactant ordering. This contrasts sharply with the surfactant-stabilized case (Figure ), where vertically polarized excitation produces WGM peaks ∼10× stronger than those produced by horizontally polarized excitation at the same pump energy.

To distinguish between the possible origins of these splittings, we first estimate the expected intrinsic TE-TM splitting using the asymptotic expansion for spherical resonators. , The resonance condition for a WGM of polarization p (TE or TM) and angular momentum l is given by 4 ndropletkR=l+12+αq(l2)1/3−χpn2−1+O(l−1/3) where k = 2π/λ, n = n droplet/n oil, α q is the q-th root of the Airy function (q being the radial order), and χp is a polarization-dependent correction: χTE = 1 and χTM = n 2. The difference in the size parameter x = n droplet kR between TE and TM modes of the same l and q is then: 5 Δx≡xTE−xTM≈n2−1n2−1=n2−1 Converting to wavelength, the TE–TM splitting is approximately: 6 ΔλTE−TM≈λ22πndropletR·Δxx≈FSR·n2−1n where we used FSR ≈ λ2/(2πn droplet R) for the free spectral range. With our experimental parameters (n droplet = 1.54, n oil = 1.29, hence n ≈ 1.194, and FSR ≈ 0.82 nm from Figure ), we obtain ΔλTE–TM = 0.45 nm.

Two key observations distinguish the surfactant-depleted spectrum from this prediction: (i) the observed fine-structure spacings (∼0.2 nm) are smaller than the estimated TE–TM splitting, and (ii) the subpeaks have comparable intensities rather than showing the strong polarization dependence that would be expected if they corresponded to TE and TM modes. Together, these indicate that the multiplet structure arises from backscattering-induced clockwise/counterclockwise mode coupling and/or local refractive-index variations, rather than from intrinsic polarization splitting. This complex, depolarized spectrum serves as a critical reference against which the surfactant-stabilized polarization-selective regime can be compared.

To confirm that these resonances correspond to equatorial WGMs despite their complex fine structure, we applied Fourier-transform (FT) analysis to the control spectrum. The dominant FT peak yields a geometric path length l geo consistent with the droplet perimeter 2πR within uncertainty (Figure b–c), validating that the observed resonances circulate near the equator. This control therefore provides a reference equatorial WGM spectrum under reduced interfacial ordering, characterized by unresolved mode splittings with comparable intensities, against which the surfactant-stabilized polarization-selective regime can be compared.

Surfactant-Stabilized Droplets: Strong Polarization-Selective Excitation

Building on the ring-like emission signature, we systematically investigated how excitation polarization influences mode coupling and intensity. We recorded emission spectra under controlled vertical and horizontal polarization states at a fixed pump energy of 4.75 μJ. Representative spectra are shown in Figure .

A clear intensity difference is observed: vertically polarized excitation produces whispering gallery mode (WGM) peaks approximately 10 times stronger than those observed under horizontal polarization at the same pump energy (Figure a). The latter polarization yields comparably low intensities, indicating that the strong enhancement is specific to the vertical (TE-like) orientation. This pronounced contrast persists across multiple droplets and cannot be attributed to variations in droplet size or pump power, which were carefully matched. Applying the same FT-based procedure as in Figure yields identical l geo for vertical and horizontal excitation (Figure b,c), consistent with equatorial WGMs and confirming that the polarization contrast does not arise from a change in droplet geometry. For both polarization states, the extracted l geo ≈ 411.6 μm matches the theoretical droplet perimeter 2πR ≈ 408.4 μm (for R = 65 μm) within experimental uncertainty, confirming that the observed resonances are equatorial WGMs circulating along the droplet circumference.

The observed contrast originates from the distinct boundary conditions of transverse-electric (TE) and transverse-magnetic (TM) whispering-gallery modes at the droplet–oil interface. In spherical resonators, TE modes have a predominantly tangential electric field at the interface, whereas TM modes possess a significant radial component; this leads to different polarization-dependent phase shifts and curvature-induced radiation leakage close to total internal reflection (TIR), typically yielding Q TE > Q TM. ,,,

Fresnel-Limit Estimate

To bound the polarization selectivity expected from planar-interface Fresnel reflection alone, we consider light incident internally from the droplet (n 1) onto the oil phase (n 2), with n 1 ≃ 1.54 (benzyl alcohol) and n 2 ≃ 1.29 (HFE-7500), i.e., Δn ≃ 0.25. The critical angle is 7 θc=arcsin⁡(n2n1)≈arcsin⁡(1.291.54)≈56.9° For θ i > θ c (TIR), the transmitted angle becomes complex and the Fresnel intensity reflectivities at a planar interface satisfy 8 RTE(θi)=|rs|2≃1,RTM(θi)=|rp|2≃1 i.e. both polarizations undergo (nearly) perfect reflection in the TIR regime. A direct numerical evaluation for incidence angles slightly above θ c confirms that R TE and R TM are indistinguishable from unity (planar-interface Fresnel model) for our refractive-index contrast. Therefore, the order-of-magnitude (∼10×) intensity contrast observed experimentally cannot be explained by Fresnel reflectivity differences alone; in this regime, TE/TM differences enter primarily through polarization-dependent reflection phase shifts and evanescent-field structure, which can translate into different curvature-induced radiative losses in a spherical cavity.

From Fresnel Phase to Q (Curvature Leakage)

Although the Fresnel intensity reflectivity is essentially unity under TIR (R ≃ 1), the interface still imposes a polarization-dependent phase on the reflected field, rs=eiϕs(θi),rp=eiϕp(θi) with ϕ s ≠ ϕ p in general. Equivalently, the evanescent wave in the low-index medium is polarization dependent: TE and TM have different ratios of tangential/normal field components at the boundary and different effective decay constants outside the droplet. In a strictly planar geometry this does not produce radiation loss, because the wave remains perfectly guided.

A whispering-gallery mode, however, propagates along a curved interface of finite radius. Physically, WGMs leak not because the planar interface reflects poorly, but because curvature enables a small tunneling-like radiation loss through the finite evanescent barrier outside the droplet (set by index contrast and curvature). The polarization-dependent phase shift (and associated evanescent-field structure) changes the effective radial boundary condition of the surface-confined wave, thereby modifying the external evanescent tail. A slightly larger tail increases the tunneling probability and thus the radiative loss per round trip, making the intrinsic radiative quality factor polarization dependent. This provides the physical basis for the standard trend that, for otherwise comparable mode numbers, TE WGMs typically exhibit lower radiative leakage (and thus higher Q) than TM WGMs in spherical resonators. ,

This intrinsic TE/TM difference is generally a moderate effect: it changes line widths and thresholds, but by itself, it rarely produces an order-of-magnitude change in measured peak intensities at fixed pump conditions. Therefore, while curvature-induced leakage provides the correct direction of the asymmetry (Q TE > Q TM), the observed ∼10× contrast in our experiment requires an additional mechanism that strongly modifies excitation/emission coupling; here, surfactant-driven interfacial alignment of Rh640 transition dipoles. Polarization- and angular-momentum-dependent selection rules for free-space excitation of WGMs have been discussed in detail in the context of microsphere coupling. ,

Interfacial Alignment as the Dominant Polarization-Selective Mechanism

The dominant contribution to the observed enhancement is surfactant-mediated molecular alignment of Rhodamine 640 at the droplet interface. Krytox–BTA forms a dense interfacial layer that promotes the tangential alignment of Rh640 transition dipoles at the oil–droplet boundary. , These oriented dipoles act as an interfacial “polarization filter” , strongly favoring absorption and emission for the vertical (TE-like) polarization tangent to the equator.

In our geometry, vertically polarized pump light (ŷ, tangent to the equator) aligns with the dominant electric-field component of equatorial TE modes, maximizing coupling to these higher-Q resonances. Conversely, horizontal polarization (x̂, with a larger effective radial component) couples more efficiently to TM-like modes, which exhibit higher radiative leakage and broader resonances. , The ∼10× intensity hierarchy thus reflects the combination of (i) a modest intrinsic TE/TM difference in curvature-induced losses and (ii) the much stronger polarization-dependent coupling imposed by surfactant-driven interfacial dipole orientation. While the present study focuses on Krytox–BTA as a model surfactant system, the observed polarization-selective behavior arises from interfacial molecular alignment rather than from a specific chemical identity. Such interfacial ordering is commonly reported for amphiphilic and charged surfactants at liquid–liquid interfaces, where molecular organization induces anisotropic environments for embedded fluorophores. In this context, the underlying mechanism is expected to be general to surfactants capable of promoting interfacial anisotropy, although a systematic comparison between different surfactants remains an interesting direction for future work.

Despite the large intensity variations, the Fourier-transform analysis yields essentially identical geometric path lengths under both excitation polarizations. For a representative droplet of radius R = 65 μm (theoretical perimeter 2πR ≈ 408.4 μm), the dominant FT peak gives l geo = 411.6 ± 41 μm for both vertical and horizontal excitation (uncertainty ∼10%, see Section ). The extracted free spectral range is likewise unchanged (FSR ≈ 0.678 nm), confirming that the same equatorial WGM family is probed irrespective of polarization.

Assignment of TE and TM Mode Families

In spherical whispering-gallery-mode (WGM) resonators, the supported modes are characterized by azimuthal and radial quantum numbers, as well as by their polarization (transverse electric, TE, and transverse magnetic, TM), which arise from distinct boundary conditions at the interface. , Similar notation has also been used recently for microfluidically designed WGM/LSPR composite microspheres. Analytical expressions for the resonance wavelengths depend on both polarization and radial order. In the present work, we focus on the dominant resonance family and assign TE- and TM-like modes based on their polarization-dependent excitation behavior. Vertically polarized excitation leads to enhanced coupling to modes associated with tangential dipole alignment at the interface, consistent with TE-like WGMs, whereas horizontally polarized excitation results in weaker coupling, consistent with TM-like modes. Following common practice, we restrict our analysis to the dominant first radial order modes, which provide the strongest contribution to the observed spectra, while higher-order radial modes are not explicitly considered.

In contrast, the coupling efficiency is strongly polarization dependent: vertically polarized (TE-like) pumping produces WGM peaks of approximately 10× higher intensity than horizontally polarized excitation at matched pump energy, while the quality factors reported here correspond to average values extracted from multiple resonances within each spectral window for a given polarization state. Specifically, we obtain Q ≈ 5000 ± 500 for TE-like modes and Q ≈ 4000 ± 400 for TM-like modes, where the uncertainty reflects the standard deviation across the ensemble of measured resonances.

Analytical TE/TM Mode Labeling

To further support the assignment of the observed resonances, we performed an analytical mode labeling directly on the polarization-resolved spectra shown in Figure . Following standard approaches for spherical WGM resonators , the angular mode number l is estimated from the equatorial resonance condition 9 l≈2πnRλ which yields l ∼ 103 for the present droplet (R = 65 μm, n ≈ 1.54, λ ≈ 600–650 nm). In the zoomed spectral region shown in Figure a, the representative labeled peaks correspond to azimuthal orders l ≈ 976–982. The expected free spectral range (FSR) is given by 10 Δλ≈λ22πnR leading to Δλ ≈ 0.6–0.7 nm, in excellent agreement with the experimentally observed spacing between successive resonances in Figure . A closer inspection of the spectra in Figure a reveals that the resonances form interleaved peak pairs. Each pair exhibits a small sub-FSR splitting on the order of 0.11–0.14 nm, while the spacing between successive peaks of the same family is approximately 0.65–0.66 nm, consistent with the calculated FSR. This structure is characteristic of two polarization-dependent WGM families (TE-like and TM-like) associated with the same angular mode number l, but exhibiting slightly different resonance conditions due to their distinct boundary conditions at the interface. Based on this analysis, each pair of closely spaced peaks in Figure is assigned to a common angular order l, with the stronger component (enhanced under vertical excitation) attributed to the TE-like family and the weaker component (observed under horizontal excitation) to the TM-like family. Successive peak pairs correspond to adjacent angular mode numbers (l, l – 1, l – 2, ...), consistent with the measured FSR and the Fourier-transform analysis. This analytical labeling provides a self-consistent interpretation of both the spectral structure and the polarization dependence, while remaining fully consistent with the FT-based determination of the geometrical path length. A full modal indexing including higher radial orders is beyond the scope of this work; following common practice, we restrict the analysis to the dominant first radial-order family. This assignment is further confirmed by the polarization-resolved measurements in Figure , where the TE-like family is selectively enhanced under vertical excitation, while the TM-like family remains weaker under horizontal excitation.

Clarification of Higher-Order FT Peaks and Exclusion of Higher-Order WGMs

The Fourier-transform spectra in Figure exhibit additional weaker peaks at larger apparent geometrical lengths (e.g., ∼1238.5 μm and ∼1273 μm). These peaks are close to integer multiples of the fundamental optical path length l geo ≈ 411.6 μm. Specifically, they correspond to approximately 3.01 l geo and 3.09 l geo, respectively. Such near-integer scaling is a well-known consequence of the Fourier transform of a finite quasi-periodic signal: truncation of a periodic spectrum produces harmonic components at integer multiples of the fundamental frequency (or equivalently, optical path length). These higher-order FT peaks are therefore mathematical artifacts and do not correspond to additional physical resonances. Importantly, higher radial-order whispering gallery modes would manifest as shifts in resonance wavelengths and slight modifications of the free spectral range, but they would not generate apparent optical path lengths equal to integer multiples of the equatorial circumference. Therefore, the observed peaks at ∼3 l geo cannot be attributed to higher-order WGM families and are instead attributed to Fourier-transform harmonics.

Occasional Multifamily Spectra: Evidence for Internal Inhomogeneity

In some droplets, the emission spectrum under polarized excitation clearly reveals two distinct groups of resonant peaks, visibly separated in wavelength (Figure , top row). For example, under vertical polarization, one family appears in the 620–630 nm range and another near 645–665 nm. Fourier-transform analysis of each group separately yields slightly different geometric path lengths, confirming that they correspond to separate whispering-gallery-mode families with distinct optical confinement. This type of Fourier-domain separation of overlapping WGM periodicities is consistent with recent FT-WGM metrology approaches developed for soft photonic microcavities, where distinct spectral periodicities can be assigned to different effective optical or geometrical path lengths.

In approximately 20–30% of measured droplets, the emission spectrum revealed two spectrally distinct WGM families with slightly different effective path lengths. Both families satisfy the equatorial condition (l geo ≈ 2πR) and appear across all polarization states, indicating a refractive-index variation rather than polarization-dependent coupling. We interpret this splitting as arising from transient dye concentration gradients within the droplet that persist after trapping.

During microfluidic generation, rapid mixing of the dye-doped phase (0.2 mM Rh640 in benzyl alcohol) with the oil continuous phase typically produces well-mixed droplets; however, minor variations in local mixing efficiency can result in residual concentration inhomogeneity in a subset of droplets. Two regions with distinct [Rh640] and thus different refractive indices (Δn ≈ 10–3, consistent with the solvent-dependent absorption spectra in Figure ) support WGMs with slightly different effective indices, producing the observed ∼0.3% splitting in geometric path length. The ∼20–30% incidence frequency aligns with the stochastic nature of microfluidic droplet generation, where mixing efficiency varies across the population.

In a perfect sphere, all WGMs with the same radial order n and angular momentum l are degenerate. The two families observed here likely arise from spatial refractive-index inhomogeneity rather than geometric distortion, as evidenced by (i) the appearance of both families across all polarization states (ruling out purely polarization-dependent mechanisms); (ii) the consistent splitting magnitude across multiple measurements of the same droplet (indicating a stable, persistent inhomogeneity); and (iii) the magnitude of the effective-index difference (Δn eff ≈ 10–3), which is physically reasonable for dye concentration variations in this system.

The fact that two visibly distinct spectral groups appear across both polarizationseven when their intensities differ by an order of magnitudeindicates that the splitting is not an artifact of polarization selectivity, but a genuine property of the droplet cavity reflecting refractive-index inhomogeneity. Importantly, the vast majority of droplets (∼70–80%) exhibit well-resolved single-family peaks, which provide the basis for our quantitative analysis of polarization-dependent coupling and surfactant-mediated anisotropy presented in the following sections.

The clear spectral separation of two mode families in a subset of droplets demonstrates that the optical properties of soft microcavities depend sensitively on the homogeneity of the dye distribution. As shown in Figure , the relative intensity of the two families varies with polarization: under vertical polarization both families appear as sharp resonances at moderate pump energy, whereas under horizontal polarization the effective excitation threshold is significantly higher. In the measurement shown, whispering-gallery modes only emerge at increased pump energy, and even then a single family dominates the spectrum, indicating that each family exhibits a distinct polarization-dependent coupling efficiency. This behavior highlights the complex interplay between cavity geometry, dye concentration, interfacial organization, and optical polarization in determining the mode structure of soft photonic resonators. The majority of our subsequent analysis focuses on the well-mixed droplets (∼70–80% of the population) exhibiting single, well-resolved peaks, enabling precise characterization of polarization-mediated mode control without the complicating effects of concentration gradients.

Validation of Surfactant-Mediated Interfacial Anisotropy Using Nematic Liquid-Crystal Droplets

The pronounced polarization contrast observed in benzyl-alcohol droplets suggests that the surfactant layer imposes anisotropic interfacial organization of Rh640. To test whether this mechanism is genuinely interfacial (rather than a peculiarity of the isotropic host), we performed the same polarization-resolved measurements on MBBA nematic liquid-crystal (NLC) droplets prepared under identical conditions: same oil phase (HFE-7500), same surfactant concentration (Krytox–BTA, 2 wt %), same Rh640 concentration (0.2 mM), and the same trapping and optical protocols. This comparison is stringent because MBBA is intrinsically anisotropic; observing the same qualitative polarization selectivity would therefore support an interfacial origin that is robust against bulk molecular order.

Three experimental observations support surfactant-mediated interfacial alignment as the dominant origin of polarization selectivity. First, the emission is systematically stronger for the excitation polarization that preferentially couples to TE-like (tangential-field) WGMs in both isotropic benzyl-alcohol droplets (Figure ) and NLC droplets (Figure ), indicating that the selectivity does not rely on the specific bulk phase. Second, the analyzer-resolved polarization contrast is reproducible and of the same order in both systems, consistent with an interface-imposed preferential orientation of Rh640 transition dipoles (rather than a geometry change of the cavity). Third, Fourier-transform analysis preserves the same equatorial optical path length (l geo ≈ 2πR) across polarization states and across both material systems, which rules out droplet deformation as the source of the polarization-dependent response.

Taken together, these results are consistent with Krytox–BTA forming a dense interfacial layer that promotes tangential alignment of Rh640 at the oil–droplet boundary. , This interfacial dipole ordering acts as a polarization-selective coupling mechanism that strongly favors TE-like excitation and amplifies the intrinsic (moderate) TE/TM asymmetry of spherical WGMs.

To visually encapsulate this universal mechanism, Figure presents a schematic model of surfactant-imposed alignment at the droplet boundary. The custom fluorocarbon surfactant (Krytox-BTA) layer at the oil–droplet interface orchestrates tangential alignment of Rhodamine 640 transition dipoles in both benzyl-alcohol and nematic-liquid-crystal environments. This molecular-level interfacial order preferentially enhances transverse-electric (TE-like, vertically polarized) whispering-gallery modes over transverse-magnetic (TM-like, horizontally polarized) modes, directly accounting for the observed 10-fold intensity enhancement under vertical excitation. Importantly, the observation of a similar qualitative polarization trend in both benzyl-alcohol and nematic liquid-crystal droplets supports the role of surfactant-mediated interfacial ordering as a robust mechanism for controlling polarization-dependent light–matter coupling in soft photonic systems. Overall, the comparative measurements on isotropic (benzyl-alcohol) and nematic (MBBA) droplets support an interfacial origin of the polarization selectivity, consistent with surfactant-induced alignment of Rh640 transition dipoles at the oil–droplet boundary. This interfacial ordering provides a practical handle to tune the relative coupling to TE-like and TM-like WGM families without changing droplet size or composition. Such polarization-selective excitation may be exploited in droplet-based microlasers and polarization-sensitive sensing schemes.

Conclusion

We developed an integrated optofluidic platform that combines monodisperse droplet generation, stable single-droplet trapping, polarization-resolved excitation/detection, and Fourier-transform (FT) analysis to study whispering-gallery modes (WGMs) in dye-doped liquid microcavities. The FT approach provides a robust identification of the dominant resonance periodicity and yields a geometric path length consistent with equatorial circulation (l geo ≈ 2πR) across droplets, enabling reliable mode-family assignment under well-controlled conditions.

Using this platform, we observed a strong polarization dependence of WGM excitation in Rh640-doped benzyl-alcohol droplets stabilized by Krytox–BTA, with vertically polarized (TE-like) pumping producing WGM peaks up to ∼10× stronger than horizontally polarized excitation at matched pump energy. Because l geo remains unchanged when the excitation polarization is rotated, the polarization contrast cannot be attributed to droplet deformation. A Fresnel-limit analysis further indicates that planar-interface reflectivity under total internal reflection is essentially unity for both polarizations, implying that the observed contrast is not explained by Fresnel intensity reflectivity differences alone; instead, it is consistent with a combination of (i) moderate intrinsic TE/TM asymmetry from curvature-induced leakage and (ii) surfactant-mediated interfacial alignment of Rh640 transition dipoles that acts as a polarization-selective coupling mechanism.

Comparative measurements on MBBA nematic liquid-crystal droplets prepared under the same surfactant and dye conditions support an interfacial origin of the polarization selectivity that is robust against bulk anisotropy, while the analyzer-resolved polarization contrast in these spectra is expected to be reduced by partial depolarization and polarization mixing in collection. More broadly, these results establish interfacial engineering via tailored surfactants as a practical route to control polarization-selective coupling to WGMs in soft, reconfigurable microdroplet resonators without changing droplet size or composition.

The strong polarization selectivity reported here originates from interfacial molecular ordering, which suggests a general strategy for engineering the readout channel of soft microcavities without changing droplet geometry. A natural extension is to integrate WGM-based fluorescence readout with an orthogonal spectroscopic modality on the same platform; for example, plasmonic substrates can provide SERS specificity while the droplet cavity provides narrowband, polarization-selective WGM signatures for cross-validation in complex samples. In parallel, future work will focus on quantitative modeling of polarization-dependent coupling efficiencies, systematic variation of surfactant chemistry and concentration, and active tuning of droplet resonances via controlled deformation, external fields, or controlled refractive-index perturbations. − Such stable trapped-droplet platforms could also provide an experimental route to probe mode-family switching and sensitivity-collapse effects predicted in spherical WGM refractometry, where refractive-index perturbations can change the dominant resonant branch and reduce the apparent sensing response. Together, trapped-droplet stability, polarization control, and FT-based mode identification provide a foundation for droplet-based microlasers and polarization-sensitive sensing schemes.
