Circulating mitochondrial cell-free DNA levels are associated with the recurrence of atrial fibrillation after catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation
Authors: Tomoko Takahashi, Takeshi Soeki, Kazuki Tezuka, Tomomi Matsuura, Etsuko Uematsu, Hiroyuki Aibara, Ryou Bando, Robert Zheng, Tomonori Takahashi, Yoshihito Saijo, Yutaka Kawabata, Muneyuki Kadota, Tomoya Hara, Rie Ueno, Takayuki Ise, Koji Yamaguchi, Shusuke Yagi, Hirotsugu Yamada, Daiju Fukuda, Masataka Sata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough catheter ablation, including pulmonary vein isolation, is the primary nonpharmacological therapy for atrial fibrillation (AF), atrial arrhythmia often recurs. Recent studies have linked circulating mitochondrial cell-free DNA (mt-cfDNA), a potential contributor to inflammatory disorders, with AF. In this study, we evaluated the relationship between plasma mt-cfDNA levels and AF recurrence after catheter ablation in patients with AF.MethodsPeripheral blood was obtained from 58 patients with AF (34 paroxysmal and 24 persistent AF) who underwent catheter ablation and from the control group (10 patients without AF). We extracted total cfDNA and mt-cfDNA from the patients.ResultsThe total cfDNA concentration and copy number of mt-cfDNA were significantly higher in patients with AF than in controls. During the 16-month follow-up after catheter ablation, AF recurred in 11 patients (19.0 %). In patients with AF, the copy number of mt-cfDNA before catheter ablation was higher in the recurrent group than in the non-recurrent group. However, total cfDNA concentrations did not differ significantly between patients with and without AF recurrence. The left atrial volume index (LAVI) was higher in patients with recurrent AF than in those without. Cox proportional hazards regression analysis showed that the mt-cfDNA copy number was significantly associated with AF recurrence after catheter ablation, independent of age, sex, body mass index, LAVI, and AF type.ConclusionElevated mt-cfDNA levels may be a sensitive predictor of AF recurrence after catheter ablation in patients with AF.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Catheter ablation, Cell-free DNA, Mitochondria, Recurrence

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common arrhythmia observed in clinical practice. It is associated with increased mortality and morbidity, including cardiac dysfunction and thromboembolic events [1]. Although many previous studies have suggested that inflammation contributes to the onset and progression of AF [2,3], the association between chronic inflammation and AF is not yet fully comprehended. Recent studies have demonstrated that cell-free DNA is implicated in damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) across various inflammatory diseases, including end-stage renal dysfunction [4] and myocardial infarction [5]. Toll-like receptor (TLR) 9 recognizes bacterial DNA [6] and activates innate immunity, whereas it provokes inflammation in response to DAMPs including cell-free DNA released from mammalian cells [7]. Recently, we reported that TLR9 plays a pivotal role in the development of vascular inflammation and atherogenesis by promoting proinflammatory activation of macrophages [8]. Given the pivotal function of mitochondria in cardiomyocyte stress and apoptosis [9], the pathology associated with AF may result in a preferential release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). In fact, circulating mitochondrial cell-free DNA (mt-cfDNA) has been linked to the presence of AF [[10], [11], [12], [13]].

Catheter ablation, including pulmonary vein isolation (PVI), has become the mainstream nonpharmacologic therapy for AF because of its greater effectiveness in sustaining sinus rhythm compared to anti-arrhythmic drugs [14]. Additionally, catheter ablation improves quality of life and reduces heart failure hospitalizations, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality [15,16]. However, long-term freedom from atrial arrhythmias following a catheter ablation procedure is accomplished in only half of the patients undergoing the procedure [17]. Therefore, identifying patients at risk of developing recurrent AF after catheter ablation remains a key clinical need.

Although cfDNA is associated with the pathophysiology of AF through inflammation, few studies have investigated its relationship with recurrence after AF ablation. Therefore, we evaluated the relationship between circulating mt-cfDNA levels and AF recurrence after catheter ablation.

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively studied 58 patients with AF who underwent radiofrequency catheter ablation, including PVI, between May 2022 and February 2023 and were followed up at Tokushima University Hospital. The control group consisted of 10 patients with paroxysmal supraventricular tachycardia (PSVT) but without AF who underwent catheter ablation during the same period. Patients were excluded from the study if they had active cancer, connective tissue disease, severe chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, end-stage renal disease requiring hemodialysis, moderate or severe valvular insufficiency or stenosis, decompensated congestive heart failure, cerebral or myocardial infarction within 6 months of onset, or had undergone implantation of pacemakers or implantable cardioverter defibrillators. Patients with longstanding persistent AF (duration >1 year) were excluded. All participants provided written informed consent before the study procedures. The Ethics Committee at Tokushima University Hospital approved all the procedures used in this research.

Protocol for AF ablation

A standard radiofrequency-based PVI was performed in all patients who underwent ablation. Any additional ablation (cavotricuspid isthmus ablation and/or linear left atrial ablation and/or non-PV trigger ablation including superior vena cava isolation and/or complex fractionated atrial electrogram ablation, etc.) were performed at the physician's discretion.

Measurement of cfDNA

Peripheral blood was obtained immediately before the catheter ablation procedure from 58 patients with AF (34 with paroxysmal and 24 with persistent AF). The control group consisted of 10 patients with PSVT. Whole blood was drawn into EDTA-2Na tubes, and the plasma was stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Total cell-free cfDNA was extracted from plasma using the ISOSPIN Blood and Plasma DNA kit (NIPPON GENE). The concentration of total cfDNA was measured using QuantiFluor® dsDNA System (Promega) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The level of separately extracted mt-cfDNA was assessed using the Human mtDNA Monitoring Primer Set (Takara), which was designed to quantify the relative number of copies of human mtDNA by real-time PCR using nuclear DNA (nDNA) content as a standard. The kit includes primer pairs designed for the amplification of two mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) genes, ND1 and ND5, as well as two nDNA genes, SLCO2B1 and SERPINA. The relative copy number of mtDNA was assessed by calculating the difference in Ct values between mtDNA and nDNA.

Follow-up

AF recurrence was monitored during the follow-up period. Episodes during the blanking period of the first 90 days after the ablation procedure were not considered as recurrences. Data on AF recurrence during the follow-up period were retrieved from hospital medical records and discharge summaries, as well as from other institutions. Specifically, the routine ECG recordings were performed every 2–3 months and 24-h Holter recordings were obtained 3–6 months and 12–15 months after ablation. In the case of symptoms suggesting an arrhythmia episode during follow-up, the patients were asked to undergo a 24-h Holter monitoring or ECG immediately.

Statistical analysis

Results are expressed as mean ± SD. Means or proportions of clinical characteristics and measured total cfDNA or mt-cfDNA were compared between the two groups using the Student's t-test or chi-squared test. This analysis identified potential predictors of AF recurrence, and the variables were entered into Cox proportional hazards models to estimate the contribution of possible risk factors to the prediction of recurrent AF during the follow-up period. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

This study found that the copy number of mt-cfDNA before catheter ablation was higher in the recurrent group of patients with AF than in the non-recurrent group, while the total cfDNA concentration did not differ significantly between the groups. In Cox proportional hazards regression analysis, the copy number of mt-cfDNA was significantly associated with AF recurrence after catheter ablation, independent of age, sex, BMI, and LAVI.

Although the sample size was limited, the concentration of total cfDNA and copy number of mt-cfDNA were significantly higher in patients with AF than in controls. These results are partially consistent with those of previous studies. Wiersma et al. [10] evaluated two mtDNA genes (ND1 and COX3) in the peripheral blood of control patients, patients undergoing cardiac surgery without a history of AF, and patients with AF at different stages who underwent either AF treatment or cardiac surgery. They found that mt-cfDNA levels increased significantly in the early stages of AF (paroxysmal AF) compared with controls but decreased in end-stage AF (longstanding persistent AF). Yamazoe et al. [11] demonstrated that the AF group showed a higher total cfDNA concentration than the non-AF group and that patients with persistent AF exhibited higher mt-cfDNA levels than those with paroxysmal AF. Conversely, Myamoto et al. [12] found no significant difference in mt-cfDNA levels between paroxysmal and persistent AF and reported significantly lower mt-cfDNA levels in patients with tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy compared to those without. Recently, Pool et al. [13] reported a significant, gradual increase in the number of DNA lesions in atrial tissue samples from patients with paroxysmal and persistent AF and those with AF duration <3 years, compared to patients with sinus rhythm. Additionally, they showed that mt-cfDNA levels in atrial tissue samples decreased in patients with >3 years of AF compared to those with <3 years of AF. They found that serum mitochondrial DNA levels were lower in patients with long-term (>3 years) AF than in those with short-term (<3 years) AF. Overall, studies have shown that circulating cfDNA, especially mt-cfDNA, is higher in patients with AF than in those without, which is consistent with our findings. Whether circulating cfDNA levels decrease or increase after AF progression remains debated. Some studies suggest that the lower mt-cfDNA levels observed in patients with persistent AF may reflect disease severity, including mitochondrial functional exhaustion, rather than inflammation. As our study excluded patients with longstanding persistent AF, we could not confirm any changes in cfDNA levels associated with long-term persistent AF.

Catheter ablation for AF, mainly via PVI, is an effective and safe intervention for restoring and maintaining sinus rhythm. However, the recurrence rate of AF after catheter ablation is estimated to be approximately 50 % over the long term [17,18]. Therefore, identifying patients at risk of recurrence following the procedure requires better pre-ablation screening tools, including biomarkers. However, the risk factors associated with recurrence are less well established, although LA size or volume and persistent or longstanding persistent AF, as counterparts to paroxysmal AF, are widely recognized as markers for predicting AF recurrence after ablation [19,20]. Therefore, this study investigated whether cfDNA, a novel biomarker, could predict recurrence after catheter ablation for AF. We found that circulating mt-cfDNA levels were higher in the recurrent group than in the nonrecurrent group after catheter ablation for AF, whereas total cfDNA levels did not differ significantly between patients with and without recurrence. Additionally, mt-cfDNA levels were significantly associated with AF recurrence after catheter ablation, independent of age, sex, BMI, LAVI, and AF type.

To the best of our knowledge, only two studies have investigated the predictive value of circulating cfDNA levels for AF recurrence after catheter ablation. One study showed that in patients with paroxysmal AF but not persistent AF, increased mt-cfDNA levels for COX3 and ND1 were associated with AF recurrence after treatment of catheter ablation or electrical cardioversion [10]. Another study found that serum cfDNA levels for β-globin were higher in the recurrence group than in the non-recurrence group after catheter ablation or electrical cardioversion in patients with persistent AF but not paroxysmal AF [13]. Our findings are partially consistent with those of these two studies. However, these previous studies evaluated the recurrence after catheter ablation and electrical cardioversion. Due to the inclusion of both catheter ablation and electrical cardioversion cases in these two studies, the specific recurrence rates associated with each procedure remain unknown. Recurrence after catheter ablation is often because of incomplete ablation or abnormal atrial remodeling related to inflammation, oxidative stress injury, and atrial fibrosis [21], while recurrence after electrical cardioversion is more likely related to the underlying cause of arrhythmia [22]. Therefore, recurrence after catheter ablation and recurrence after electrical cardioversion must be considered separately. Moreover, the two previous studies did not evaluate any association between cfDNA and other predictors of recurrence. Conversely, our study demonstrated that mt-cfDNA was a predictor of AF recurrence, independent of left atrial size and AF type.

This study demonstrated a significant association between AF recurrence after catheter ablation and circulating mt-cfDNA levels but not total cfDNA levels. Since AF is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and damage [23], mitochondrial stress may contribute to the release of mitochondrial DNA into the circulation. Circulating mitochondrial DNA has been shown to act as a DAMP [24] and may cause an inflammatory response, probably via TLR9 [25]. We previously found that cfDNA released from degenerated adipocytes promotes the activation of macrophages, at least partially, via the TLR9 pathway, leading to the development of adipose tissue inflammation and insulin resistance [26]. Additionally, we found that TLR9 plays a pivotal role in the development of atherogenesis and that circulating cfDNA in the coronary artery positively correlates with inflammatory features of coronary plaques in patients with acute myocardial infarction [8]. Another study showed that administering total cfDNA and mt-cfDNA to macrophages significantly promoted IL-1β and IL-6 expression through TLR9 [11]. From these studies, we speculate that mt-cfDNA may be recognized as DAMPs partially through TLR-9 in patients with AF and that this plausible mechanism may predict the recurrence of AF after catheter ablation. Future studies are needed to directly investigate the role of the mt-cfDNA-TLR9 axis in promoting atrial remodeling and AF recurrence. However, since several studies have shown that mt-cfDNA levels decrease in patients with advanced AF [10,12,13], further studies are also needed to verify the ability of mt-cfDNA to predict recurrence after catheter ablation in patients with long-standing persistent AF.

The findings of this study indicate that mt-cfDNA may have several potential clinical implications. Elevated levels of mt-cfDNA may indicate more advanced myocardial remodeling and inflammation. In this patient cohort, such conditions may necessitate more extensive atrial substrate modification in addition to pulmonary vein isolation. For patients exhibiting elevated levels of mt-cfDNA, the implementation of intensive postoperative monitoring strategies including increased frequency of electrocardiogram assessments may facilitate the early detection of recurrence and prompt intervention. Thus, our findings suggest that mt-cfDNA possesses potential as a biomarker to improve patient stratification, optimize ablation strategies, and facilitate personalized postoperative management following AF ablation. However, further prospective studies examining mt-cfDNA levels in AF patients are required to confirm its clinical utility.

Our study had a few limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. Notably, the AF and control groups (patients with PSVT but without AF) had significant differences in age and sex as background, which may have introduced significant confounding bias and potentially affected the comparison of cfDNA levels between AF and non-AF patients. Second, excluding patients with long-persistent AF prevented the evaluation of changes in circulating mt-cfDNA levels and their predictive value in more advanced cases. Third, we did not evaluate the serial changes in mt-cfDNA levels after catheter ablation; thus, we were unable to assess whether mt-cfDNA may vary according to AF status. Forth, although no statistical differences were observed in procedural characteristics of AF ablation between patients with and without AF recurrence, it is plausible that variations in ablation techniques in individual cases may have influenced the outcomes.

Conclusion

Elevated cf-mtDNA levels may be a sensitive predictor of AF recurrence after catheter ablation in patients with AF. Incorporating mt-cfDNA measurement into clinical practice could improve patient selection for catheter ablation and inform post-procedural monitoring, ultimately enhancing treatment outcomes in AF management.
