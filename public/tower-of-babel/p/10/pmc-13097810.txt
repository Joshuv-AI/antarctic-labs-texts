Therapeutic modulation of the gut-liver axis with faecalibacterium prausnitzii in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis
Authors: Han Chen, Yan Wang, Wei Su, Yicheng Liu, Shuo Li, Yun Liu, Xiaoying Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFaecalibacterium prausnitzii (F. prausnitzii) has attracted interest in recent research regarding its possible involvement in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). However, their causal relationship is still unclear.MethodsMicrobial biomarkers of gut microbiota associated with obesity-related phenotypes were identified from the GMrepo database. Information was retrieved on specific metabolites and host genes related to F. prausnitzii from the GutMGene database. Subsequently, a high-fat diet (HFD) was administered to C57BL/6 J mice for 16 weeks. The intragastric injection of F. prausnitzii ATCC 27766 strain was administered and changes in serum and liver metabolic indicators were then assessed. Fecal short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) were measured using Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS). RNA-sequence (RNA-seq) was performed to identify differentially expressed genes related to F. prausnitzii treatment.ResultsIn individuals with obesity, MASLD, and Type 2 diabetes, the abundance of F. prausnitzii was significantly reduced by 22.4%, 16.2%, and 34%, respectively, compared to healthy controls (all p-values < 0.05). In comparison to HFD-fed control mice, those administered F. prausnitzii exhibited a substantial reduction in liver index (17.5%, p < 0.05), along with a 17.6% decrease in serum total cholesterol (TC) and a 12.7% decrease in triglycerides (TG) (all p-values < 0.05). Additionally, there was a substantial increase in the serum interleukin-10 (IL-10, 20.6%) levels and reduction in expressions of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1α (21.1%), IL-2 (32.2%), IL-5 (18.4%), and IL-6 (29.6%). GC–MS analysis confirmed a 91.4% increase in total fecal SCFAs concentrations, including acetic (91.9%), propionic (93.8%), and butyric acids (93.0%), with all p-values < 0.01. Both RNA-seq and polymerase chain reaction analyses consistently identified substantial changes in genes associated with de novo lipogenesis (Srebp-1c, Fas, Acc), fatty acid oxidation (PPARα, Acadvl, Acadm and Cpt2), and oxidative phosphorylation (Cox5b, Sdhb), with all p-values < 0.01.ConclusionThis study highlights the promise of F. prausnitzii ATCC 27766 strain, especially regarding its anti-inflammatory and metabolic regulatory functions, as a next-generation probiotic agent in prevention and treatment of MASLD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04892-1.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has emerged as a predominant chronic liver condition globally, affecting an estimated one-third of the adult population worldwide [1]. Its clinical burden is substantial, as MASLD can progress to steatohepatitis (MASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma, posing a significant challenge to healthcare systems. Despite its high prevalence and serious complications, effective pharmacological therapies remain limited, with lifestyle modification being the cornerstone of management and only a few drugs approved in specific regions [2]. This pressing unmet medical need underscores the critical importance of elucidating its pathogenic mechanisms. One of the main pathological mechanisms of MASLD is the accumulation of fat within the liver, arising from an imbalance between lipid uptake and export, alongside impaired fatty acid oxidation. This accumulation results in the formation of lipid droplets (LDs) within hepatocytes, which can initiate inflammatory responses and hepatocyte injury. The inflammatory process is mediated by various factors, including oxidative stress, cytokine release, and immune cell activation [3].

The gut-liver axis represents a crucial physiological link between the intestine and the liver, playing an essential role in the onset and progression of MASLD [4]. Recent research has highlighted the intricate relationship between gut microbiota and the development of MASLD [5]. The intestinal microecology represents a complex ecosystem comprising a diverse array of microorganisms residing in the intestine, their metabolic byproducts, and the host organism [6]. An imbalance in intestinal flora can exacerbate liver inflammation and metabolic disorders through various mechanisms, including disruption of intestinal barrier function, facilitation of the transport of intestinal toxins such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS), and alteration of short-chain fatty acid and bile acid metabolism [4].

Microbiome-based therapeutics aim to ameliorate metabolic disorders, chronic inflammation, and dysfunctions in intestinal barrier integrity resulting from microbiota imbalances by restoring equilibrium within the intestinal microecological system [7]. Several studies have explored interventions such as probiotics, synbiotics, fecal microbiota transplantation, among other methods in patients with MASLD or animal models [8, 9]. Notably, certain genus or species, such as Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron [10], Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus [11], Akkermansia muciniphila [12] and Clostridium butyricum [13], have been reported to enhance hepatic steatosis resolution and improve liver enzyme levels.

Faecalibacterium prausnitzii (F. prausnitzii) is regarded as a next-generation probiotic with significant potential in microbiota-targeted therapies. F. prausnitzii is a Gram-positive, strictly anaerobic member of the Firmicutes phylum and one of the most abundant commensal bacteria found in the intestines of healthy individuals, accounting for approximately 5–15% of the total intestinal microbiota [14, 15]. It plays important roles in butyrate production, anti-inflammatory properties, and maintenance of intestinal barrier integrity [16, 17]. Numerous studies utilizing 16S rRNA sequencing have demonstrated that the abundance of F. prausnitzii is significantly reduced in patients with MASLD [18–20]. Its deficiency may be closely linked to states of intestinal inflammation, disruption of barrier function, and increased permeability to lipopolysaccharides [21]. Supplementation with F. prausnitzii in MASLD animal models has been shown to improve liver tissue fat deposition, reduce levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and increase concentrations of butyric acid. These effects are associated with reduced levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines [18, 22]. However, despite these promising observations, the mechanistic basis underlying the protective effects of F. prausnitzii remains poorly defined. In particular, there is a critical knowledge gap regarding how F. prausnitzii influences the host transcriptional landscape in the context of MASLD. A comprehensive understanding of the transcriptomic changes and gene regulatory networks modulated by F. prausnitzii intervention is urgently needed and represents a pivotal, yet largely unexplored, area of research that could unlock novel therapeutic strategies.

Therefore, we hypothesized that supplementation with F. prausnitzii can ameliorate MASLD by modulating gut microbiota-derived metabolites, which in turn suppresses hepatic inflammation and dysregulated lipid metabolism. Distinct from prior research that has primarily documented phenotypic improvements, this study is designed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms, with a particular focus on the previously unexplored transcriptional regulatory networks in the liver following F. prausnitzii intervention. To address this hypothesis, we investigated the effects of F. prausnitzii intervention on disease phenotypes in a MASLD mouse model and employed high-throughput transcriptomic sequencing to systematically characterize its impact on hepatic gene expression profiles related to metabolism and inflammation. This integrated approach aims to bridge the gap between microbial modulation and host transcriptional responses, providing a novel and comprehensive theoretical foundation for developing targeted microecological therapies against MASLD.

Methods

GMrepo database

The online resource GMrepo (https://gmrepo.humangut.info) was employed to identify gut microbial markers in individuals diagnosed with obesity, type 2 diabetes (T2D), and MASLD, along with healthy control groups. The findings were derived from analyses conducted through metagenomic sequencing. Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) was employed to identify significant bacterial biomarkers that differentiate between obesity-related phenotypes and control samples. A threshold for significance was established with a Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) score exceeding 2.0 and a p-value less than 0.05.

GutMGene database

The GutMGene database version 2.0 (https://bio-computing.hrbmu.edu.cn/gutmgene) was utilized to systematically navigate, search for, and retrieve associations pertaining to the species F. prausnitzii, its related metabolites, and host genes. Specifically, the 'Search' function was employed to query the database using the keyword 'Faecalibacterium prausnitzii' under the 'Microbe' category. Results were filtered to retain entries linked to microbial metabolites and host genes. Metabolite outputs included names, types (e.g., short-chain fatty acids) based on curated evidence. Selection prioritized entries with experimental or high-confidence predictive associations to ensure relevance. The GMrepo and GutMGene database analyses are exploratory and that the associations identified, while statistically significant, cannot account for all potential human confounders and thus do not imply causality.

In vitro culture of F. prausnitzii

The strain F. prausnitzii ATCC 27766 was purchased from BioVector NTCC Inc. The culture medium comprised LYHBHI, incorporating 37 g/L of brain heart extract, 5 g/L of yeast extract, and 5 mg/L of blood crystal. Additionally, 1 g/L of maltose, 1 g/L of cellobiose, and 0.5 g/L of cysteine were supplemented. The solution was completed with the addition of double-distilled water [23]. Subsequently, the bacteria were transferred into an anaerobic glove box (Coy Scientific Products), where the gaseous environment was controlled to consist of 90% nitrogen, 5% hydrogen, and 5% carbon dioxide, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The bacterial suspension was diluted with PBS in the anaerobic glove box following centrifugation, achieving a concentration of 2 × 109 CFU/100 μL, before being transferred into an anaerobic test tube for immediate application.

Animal experiment procedure

The study on laboratory animals was conducted in strict compliance with national regulations and was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the Experimental Animal Center of Nanjing Medical University (IACUC-2207010).

The animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the National Standards for Laboratory Animal Welfare and relevant ethical guidelines, ensuring compliance with principles such as avoidance of unnecessary harm and provision of appropriate care. The research involved four-week-old male C57BL/6 J wild-type mice, which were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., located in Beijing, China. After a 2-week acclimation period under standard housing conditions (12-h light/dark cycle, 22 ± 1 °C, 50–60% humidity, food and water ad libitum), all mice were fed a high-fat diet (HFD) for 16 weeks to induce metabolic dysfunction and MASLD-like phenotypes. Subsequently, the mice were then randomly divided into two groups: the HFD control group (n = 5) and the F. prausnitzii-treatment group (HFD_f, n = 5). The HFD_f group received the bacterial intervention while continuing on the HFD, whereas the HFD group received a placebo. The sample size was selected based on previous studies in comparable MASLD intervention models [22–24], aiming to provide initial mechanistic insights while acknowledging the constraint on statistical power for detecting smaller effect sizes. Randomization was performed using a computer-generated sequence (https://www.randomizer.org/) with allocation concealed via sealed envelopes. Researchers responsible for histological analyses and data quantification were blinded to group allocation.

During the experiment, a high-fat diet was present (HFD: 60% kcal from fat; D12492, Research Diets) was fed continuously for 16 weeks to establish the dietary-induced obesity (DIO) mice. The control group received intragastric gavages of 200 μm of PBS, and the HFD_f group received 200 μm of F. prausnitzii solution (2 × 109 CFU/100 μL) diluted with centrifugal PBS 5 times a week for 8 weeks. The 16-week HFD duration and dosing regimen were chosen based on previously validated MASLD models [19, 22, 23]. The control group received a vehicle of PBS supplemented with sterile-filtered Liver Yeast Hemoglobin Infusion (LYHBHI) medium supernatant. The LYHBHI medium was prepared with brain–heart infusion (37 g/L), yeast extract (5 g/L), hemin (5 mg/L), cellobiose (1 g/L), maltose (1 g/L), and cysteine (0.5 g/L). The supernatant was processed in parallel and identically to the F. prausnitzii preparation but omitting the bacteria. All mice were anesthetized with 1.5% isoflurane and euthanized by cervical dislocation after tissue collection.

MASLD model validation

The successful establishment of the MASLD model was confirmed at the end of the 16-week HFD feeding period prior to the commencement of F. prausnitzii intervention. Validation was based on the following criteria: 1) Metabolic Phenotype: A significant increase in body weight and liver-to-body weight ratio compared to age-matched mice fed a standard chow diet; 2) Hepatic Steatosis: Histological assessment (Oil Red O and H&E staining) revealing pronounced macrovesicular steatosis affecting > 5% of hepatocytes; 3) Liver Injury: Significantly elevated serum levels of ALT and AST, measured using commercial assay kits.

Analysis of serum biochemical indicators

At the end of the experimental period, mice were anesthetized, and blood samples were collected via retro-orbital bleeding. This method routinely yielded 500–800 µL of whole blood per mouse, which was sufficient for the comprehensive analysis of all serum parameters listed below. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Serum triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), interleukin-1 alpha (IL-1α), IL-2, IL-5, IL-6, and IL-10 were analyzed using commercial testing kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Biotechnology Company, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT)

At week 26, following a 12-h fasting period, all mice underwent an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). This test was conducted on the mice after an overnight fast that lasted from 7:00 p.m. to 7:00 a.m. Blood samples from the tail vein were analyzed for glucose levels utilizing a glucose analyzer (OneTouch Ultra, Lifescan, Johnson & Johnson, Milpitas, CA). A fasting blood sample was collected, followed by glucose administration, and then blood sampling at the specified time points.For the serial glucose measurements mentioned in the manuscript, tail vein sampling was employed. A minimal volume of 1–2 µL was drawn per measurement, which is adequate and standard for accurate assessment using specific glucose test strips and a glucometer. This method is well-established for longitudinal glucose monitoring in mice as it minimizes stress and allows for repeated sampling.

Histological examination

Tissues obtained from the gastric and hepatic regions were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, then embedded in paraffin and cut into 3 μm sections for hematoxylin–eosin staining or for immunohistochemical analysis. The use of 4% PFA was optimized to best preserve tissue architecture and antigenicity for the subsequent immunohistochemical analyses. The immunohistochemistry procedures included the use of primary antibodies against CD3 (Servicebio, GB11014, dilution 1:300) and F4/80 (Servicebio, GB113373, dilution 1:200). For the purpose of quantitative analysis, the stained sections were evaluated as follows to ensure objectivity and reproducibility. For each tissue section, five non-overlapping fields of view were randomly selected at 400 × magnification, avoiding areas of tissue folding, tears, or edge artifacts. The selection process was performed in a blinded manner with respect to the experimental groups using a systematic random sampling approach, starting from a random initial field and subsequently moving the microscope stage at fixed intervals. Positively stained cells were defined as those displaying a distinct brown (DAB) signal above the surrounding background, and the area of positive staining was quantified using a predefined color threshold in Image J software (version 1.52). The levels of expression for each marker were then calculated as (1) the average number of positive cells per field and (2) the percentage of positively stained area relative to the total tissue area analyzed, across the five fields per section.

Targeted metabolomics (GC–MS for SCFAs)

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) were measured according to a modified protocol [25]. SCFAs were measured in fecal samples collected at the end of the 8-week treatment period, prior to tissue collection. In brief, fecal samples (weighing between 80–100 mg) were treated with 600 µL of 0.5% ortho-phosphoric acid, followed by vortexing. Afterward, 600 µL of methyl tert-butyl ether was used for extraction of the samples. The resulting mixture was stirred for 20 min and then centrifuged for 10 min at room temperature at 18,000 × g. The organic layer was then separated, and 5 µL of 1 mM ethyl butyrate was added as an internal standard. The sample was examined utilizing a gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) system, particularly an Agilent 7890 A gas chromatograph linked with an Agilent 5975 C mass selective detector. The separation process occurred on a DB-WAXetr fused silica capillary column (30 m in length, 0.25 mm internal diameter, 0.25 µm film thickness; Agilent), with helium serving as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The temperature program for the column began at 90 °C, increased to 150 °C at a rate of 15 °C/min, subsequently rose to 170 °C at 5 °C/min, and ultimately reached 250 °C at 20 °C/min, maintaining this final temperature for 2 min. In electron impact ionization mode, the mass spectrometer functioned with an electron energy of 70 eV, scanning within a mass-to-charge (m/z) range of 30–250. The temperatures for the quadrupole, interface, and ion source were kept at 150 °C, 289 °C, and 230 °C, respectively. For the identification of SCFAs, standard compounds were employed, including acetic, propionic, butyric, isobutyric, isovaleric, valeric acids, and hexanoic acid. The absolute quantification of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) was conducted using external calibration curves (Table S1). The limits of detection (LOD) and limits of quantification (LOQ) for each SCFA are presented in the Table S2. Raw data from the fecal GC–MS for SCFAs was processed by Personalbio Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Bulk RNA-seq instrumentation

RNA was extracted from mouse liver tissues using Trizol (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA) to prepare for RNA sequencing. After isolation, the RNA samples underwent treatment with TURBO DNase (Thermo Fisher, AM2238) and were then purified using the Zymo RNA Clean and Concentrator kit (R1014). The RNA concentration was evaluated with the Ribogreen reagent (Thermo Fisher, R11490) and validated by the Agilent TapeStation system (5067–5579), confirming all samples had RNA Integrity Numbers (RIN) > 8.0. For sample preparation, RNA amounts between 2.5 and 10 ng were used. Libraries of complementary DNA (cDNA) designed for sequencing were generated with the SMARTer Pico V2 kit (Takara, 634,412). To enable paired-end sequencing (101 bp × 101 bp) on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, these libraries were quantified using the Kapa Library Quantification Kits (Roche, KK4824) and their quality was verified via Agilent Bioanalyzer. Raw RNA-seq data quality control was performed using FastQC (v0.11.9), with all samples showing Q30 scores > 90% and no adapter contamination. Adapters and low-quality reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) with parameters: LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:36. The cleaned reads were then aligned to the GRCm38 mouse reference genome using the STAR aligner (version 2.7.2) with default parameters, achieving an average alignment rate of 89.5–92.3% across samples. Gene-level counts were quantified using featureCounts from the Subread package (version 2.0.3), and count matrices were calibrated and normalized for downstream analysis using DESeq2's median-of-ratios method. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with an absolute log2 fold change |> 1.5 and an adjusted p-value < 0.05 using the Benjamini–Hochberg method for multiple hypothesis testing correction. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were conducted on the DEGs using the clusterProfiler R package (version 4.3.1), with terms considered significantly enriched at an adjusted p-value < 0.05.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues of mice separately using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). The concentration and purity of the RNA were assessed using a Nano-Drop ND-1000 spectrophotometer (Nano-Drop Technologies, Wilmington, DE, USA). To produce cDNA, the purified total RNA underwent reverse transcription with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara). Quantitative PCR was carried out on the ROCHE LightCycler® 96 System employing SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Takara). The amplification efficiency for each primer pair was validated using a standard curve from serial cDNA dilutions, with all efficiencies ranging between 90 and 110% (R2 > 0.99). Amplification specificity was confirmed by melt curve analysis, showing a single peak for each product. The conditions for amplification were specified as follows: 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 60 s, followed by melt curve analysis. Gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin expression, whose stability across experimental groups was confirmed by one-way ANOVA (p > 0.05), and analyzed using the comparative 2-ΔΔCq method. Primers used in this research were summarized in Table S3.

Measurement of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

Intracellular ROS levels in liver tissues were measured using the fluorescent probe 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA; Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Briefly, fresh liver tissues were rapidly homogenized in ice-cold PBS to prepare single-cell suspensions. The suspensions were then incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove excess probe. Fluorescence intensity, corresponding to intracellular ROS levels, was detected using a BD FACSCanto II flow cytometer. A minimum of 10,000 events was acquired per sample. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1), and the geometric mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of the DCF-positive cell population was used for quantification. Results are expressed as fold change relative to the control group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 25.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was evaluated via the Shapiro–Wilk tests. Data that demonstrated a normal distribution had a p-value above 0.05 and are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while data not adhering to a normal distribution are reported as median with interquartile range (Q). For unpaired data with abnormal distribution, Mann–Whitney U tests were utilized. Spearman's rank correlation test was conducted, and an adjusted p-value of less than 0.05 was obtained using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure for correcting multiple hypothesis testing.

Metabolites and genetic modifications related to F. prausnitzii

Using the GutMGene database, we examined 20 earlier published studies (PMID in Supplemental materials) that showed the potential modulation of gene expression by F. prausnitzii. The results highlighted that the key metabolites produced by F. prausnitzii are SCFAs, including acetate and butyrate. Furthermore, this bacterium plays a role in the metabolic processes of glycogenolysis/glycolysis (D-glucuronic acid and D-lactic acid), colorectal cancer (2-amino-1-methyl-6-phenylimidazo[4,5-b] pyridine), tryptophan metabolism (3-indolepropionic acid and tryptamine), as well as antioxidative functions (dihydrocaffeic acid), amino acid processing (histidine), purine metabolism (hypoxanthine), and palmitoylation (palmitic acid). F. prausnitzii attenuates hepatic inflammation and lipid steatosis in DIO mice

Based on the information presented in Tables 1 and 2, we designed animal experiments to investigate the potential effects of F. prausnitzii on MASLD. Our focus was directed towards pathways and molecules most pertinent to MASLD pathology, including SCFAs and genes associated with lipid metabolism and inflammation cytokines. F. prausnitzii was orally administered to HFD-fed mice for 8 weeks (Fig. 2A). The body weights and liver index of mice treated with F. prausnitzii exhibited a significant reduction compared to those in HFD controls, as illustrated in Fig. 2B-D. Additionally, we observed a reduction in fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels and an increase in serum insulin concentrations (Fig. 2E-F). Serum concentrations of TC, TG, LDL, and AST were significantly lower in the F. prausnitzii-treated mice relative to the HFD controls (Fig. 2G-K). To further elucidate the effects of F. prausnitzii on the liver, we performed histological analyzes of liver tissue using H&E staining, Oil Red O staining, and F4/80 immunohistochemistry. The treatment with F. prausnitzii resulted in an attenuation of microvesicular steatosis severity, accompanied by a significant reduction in the number of infiltrating macrophages, as indicated by F4/80-positive cells, in the F. prausnitzii-treated mice (Fig. 2L). Considering that oxidative stress is a significant contributor to hepatic steatosis, we utilized Dihydroethidium staining to evaluate the levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the livers of mice. F. prausnitzii enhances intestinal SCFA levels, which were negatively associated with serum inflammatory cytokines levels and liver steatosis

In alignment with results from the GutMGene database, we sought to further elucidate the influence of F. prausnitzii on intestinal metabolites through fecal GC–MS (Fig. 3A). Consistent with prior research, we identified a significant elevation in total SCFA levels in the intestines of mice administered with F. prausnitzii. Specifically, there was a marked increase in the concentrations of acetic, propionic, butyric, isobutyric, and valeric acids (Fig. 3B). To corroborate the gene interactions associated with F. prausnitzii as reported in the GutMGene database, we also evaluated serum inflammatory factor levels in the two groups. Our findings demonstrated a significant reduction in serum levels of IL-1α, IL-2, IL-5, and IL-6 in mice treated with F. prausnitzii, alongside a significant increase in IL−10 levels (Fig. 3C). Moreover, these significantly altered SCFAs exhibited a notable positive correlation with serum IL-10 levels and negative correlations with serum IL-2 and IL-6 levels (Fig. 3D, Table S7). Glucose intolerance and insulin resistance were markedly improved in mice treated with F. prausnitzii (Fig. 3E). F. prausnitzii alters genes expression related to de novo lipogenesis, FAO, and steatohepatitis

To further elucidate the impact of F. prausnitzii on gene expression, we conducted RNA-seq on two groups of mice. The transcriptomics analysis identified a total of 298 genes exhibiting significant differential expression (|FoldChange|> 1.5, P < 0.01), comprising 86 genes that were significantly upregulated and 212 genes that were significantly downregulated (Fig. 4A, Table S9). Subsequent KEGG analysis revealed the top 25 differential pathways between the F. prausnitzii-treated groups and controls, with the five most prominent pathways including the PI3K − Akt signaling pathway, MAPK signaling pathway, Endocytosis, Rap1 signaling pathway, and Proteoglycans in cancer (Fig. 4B). Notably, the hepatic genome of mice treated with F. prausnitzii demonstrated substantial changes in genes associated with fatty acid oxidation (FAO) (PPARα and Cpt1a), oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) (Aco2, Atp5a1, Cox5b, Sdhb, Uqcrc2, and Ndufaf8), and steatohepatitis (Il5, Il8, C3, C4b, TNF, TLR2, TLR4, TGF-β1, Ccl2, and CD36) (Figs. 4C-E). We also observed that several genes related to glycogenolysis/glycolysis exhibited significant changes in F. prausnitzii-treated mice, including HK2, GK, and FBP1. Discussion

This study analyzed F. prausnitzii abundance in individuals with obesity-related phenotypes and healthy controls using the human GMrepo database, revealing its significant depletion in T2D, obesity, and MASLD. Thus, we subsequently examined the attenuating effect of the F. prausnitzii ATCC 27766 strain on hepatic steatosis in DIO mice. The strain notably increased intestinal SCFAs levels and improved serum and hepatic lipid metabolism and inflammation. It also appeared to modulate key genes related to hepatic de novo lipogenesis and the PI3K-Akt and AMPK signaling pathways.

First, our study observed that supplementation with F. prausnitzii ATCC 27766 was associated with attenuated body weight gain, ameliorated dysregulation of blood glucose, and reduced liver steatosis and inflammation in DIO mice. These findings align with several previous studies reporting similar improvements with specific strains of F. prausnitzii in MASLD models [18–20]. However, one study using fecal-derived strains A2-165 and KLE1255 did not observe significant metabolic benefits [26], suggesting that functional heterogeneity may exist among strains. Indeed, emerging evidence supports the notion that different strains of F. prausnitzii exhibit varying effects on host metabolism. For example, Hu et al. reported that, among 12 distinct strains tested, only five (including A2-165, LB8, and LC49) significantly alleviated MASLD [19], whereas Shin et al. demonstrated that five other strains (including A2-165 and several newly isolated ones) ameliorated liver steatosis and inflammation [20]. These functional differences may be attributable to gene diversity and variations in metabolic output among strains—such as in butyrate production, utilization of dietary fibers, or expression of immunomodulatory molecules—though comparative -omics studies are still needed to definitively identify strain-specific determinants of efficacy. The strain used in our work, ATCC 27766, has been consistently associated with improved metabolic parameters in studies of atherosclerosis [27], type 2 diabetes [28], and anxiety [29], supporting its potential therapeutic relevance. Nonetheless, the generalizability of these effects to other strains remains unclear. Future studies incorporating comparative transcriptomic, or metabolomic profiling across multiple strains will be essential to pinpoint genetic or metabolic features underlying functional differences in MASLD mitigation.

Furthermore, our study reveals a multi-faceted association between F. prausnitzii supplementation and the attenuation of MASLD progression, alongside changes in intestinal SCFAs and inflammatory pathways. Supplementation was followed by a significant increase in total intestinal SCFA levels, with a marked elevation in propionate alongside butyrate and acetate. Propionate levels, in particular, were inversely correlated with hepatic expression of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic genes (C3, TGFβ1, CD36) and positively correlated with IRS-3, aligning with reports that propionic acid is associated with improved insulin resistance [30, 31]. These metabolic changes occurred concurrently with the significant downregulation of key immune-related pathways. Integrated analysis revealed an inverse relationship between supplementation and the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF, IL8) and toll-like receptor signaling (TLR2, TLR4), which are pivotal drivers of hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis. The administration of F. prausnitzii was associated with the attenuation of these pathways, which in turn correlated with improved histology and reduced inflammatory markers. Collectively, these data support an integrated model wherein F. prausnitzii-mediated increases in SCFAs, including propionate and butyrate, are correlated with a favorable modulation of both systemic metabolism and hepatic immune gene networks. The known immunomodulatory functions of SCFAs—such as inhibiting NF-κB signaling and promoting regulatory T-cell differentiation—offer a parallel and hypothetically relevant explanation that is consistent with the observed correlations, positioning the gut-liver axis as a potential target for intervention. Direct studies are warranted to test whether these relationships are causal and to validate probiotic strategies targeting these pathways.

To further investigate the impact of F. prausnitzii treatment on the gene expression modulation in the liver, we conducted RNA-seq analysis. We noticed that the expression of the gene CYP4A14 was markedly downregulated in the mouse liver after F. prausnitzii treatment. CYP4A14 is known to catalyze the ω-hydroxylation of medium-chain fatty acids and arachidonic acid in mice and serves as an activator of oxidative stress in nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, which are involved in the PPAR signaling pathway [32, 33]. The lower expression of this gene after F. prausnitzii treatment is associated with attenuated hepatic fat accumulation and a deceleration in the progression of MASLD. Furthermore, based on insights from the GutMGene database, which suggests that F. prausnitzii may induce alterations in metabolites such as lactic acid, we concentrated on genes involved in the hepatic glycolysis pathway.

Moreover, our analysis indicated significant down-regulation in the expression of glycolysis-related genes, particularly HK2, GK, and FBP1, following F. prausnitzii treatment. This suggests a potential decrease in anaerobic glycolysis levels in the liver, which may be advantageous for enhancing hepatic aerobic metabolism. The observed suppression of glycolytic flux, particularly through key enzymes such as GK (governing glycerol entry into glycolysis) and FBP1 (a rate-limiting enzyme in gluconeogenesis), suggests a shift in hepatic substrate utilization away from glucose-centric metabolism. This is further supported by the concurrent upregulation of FAO pathways, indicating enhanced lipid catabolism. We propose that the increased production of SCFAs (especially propionate and butyrate) induced by F. prausnitzii serves as the primary signal mediating this transcriptional reprogramming. SCFAs, particularly butyrate, can function as histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, leading to increased histone acetylation and altered expression of metabolic genes [34, 35]. Furthermore, SCFAs can activate specific G-protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs, e.g., GPR41, GPR43) or modulate AMPK signaling, which is a master regulator of cellular energy homeostasis and can inhibit glycolysis while promoting FAO [35]. The downregulation of these metabolic genes collectively contributes to reduced de novo lipogenesis and improved hepatic lipid clearance, thereby attenuating steatosis and insulin resistance. While this study establishes a strong correlation between SCFAs and metabolic gene expression, future investigations using specific receptor antagonists or AMPK pathway modulators are warranted to precisely delineate the causal mechanistic links.

Our investigation is constrained by specific limitations. First, the GMrepo database currently employs the “NAFLD” phenotype label, which has not yet been updated to reflect the newer “MASLD” terminology. Consequently, analyses of F. prausnitzii abundance variations within these cohorts may be subject to bias due to this definitional inconsistency. Second, although we achieved encouraging outcomes concerning the treatment with F. prausnitzii, our study focused exclusively on a single F. prausnitzii ATCC 27766 strain. Additional studies are essential to ascertain if other strains of F. prausnitzii can similarly ameliorate MASLD. Furthermore, the generalizability of our findings is constrained by the relatively small sample size employed in this animal study. While the large effect sizes observed across multiple endpoints provide compelling preliminary evidence, future studies utilizing larger cohorts are necessary to confirm and extend these findings. Furthermore, we did not assess parameters related to intestinal permeability or barrier integrity (e.g., tight junction proteins, serum LPS). Therefore, although F. prausnitzii is known to support gut barrier function, we cannot provide direct experimental evidence for this mechanism in our model. This aspect should be explored in future research. Another limitation of this study is the absence of direct functional validation experiments to establish causality. While our multi-omics analysis reveals a strong associative link between F. prausnitzii supplementation, increased SCFA levels, and the beneficial reprogramming of hepatic metabolic pathways (including the downregulation of glycolysis and lipogenesis genes), the precise cause-and-effect relationships remain to be definitively proven. Our findings are currently limited to the mRNA level. Specifically, we did not employ more stringent interventional models, such as using germ-free mice, performing SCFA receptor blockade (e.g., with GPR41/43 antagonists), or applying pathway-specific inhibitors (e.g., targeting AMPK) in conjunction with our probiotic treatment. These approaches would be necessary to confirm whether the observed metabolic improvements are necessary and sufficiently mediated by F. prausnitzii-derived SCFAs acting through the proposed signaling axes. Consequently, the mechanistic pathways discussed, although plausible and supported by prior literature, should be considered as a testable hypothesis generated by our data. Future studies incorporating these functional assays will be crucial to translate these correlative findings into a definitive mechanistic understanding and to strengthen the rationale for F. prausnitzii as a therapeutic agent.

In conclusion, supplementation with F. prausnitzii ATCC 27766 correlated with marked amelioration of HFD-induced MASLD in mice, including improved hepatic steatosis, reduced serum and hepatic inflammatory markers, and enhanced systemic glucose metabolism. These effects coincided with elevated intestinal SCFA levels, alongside upregulated FAO pathways and suppressed de novo lipogenesis and glycolysis pathways. Collectively, these preclinical findings provide evidence supporting further investigation of F. prausnitzii ATCC 27766 as a potential probiotic candidate. Future studies are necessary to establish its efficacy, optimal dosage, and safety profile in humans prior to clinical translation.
