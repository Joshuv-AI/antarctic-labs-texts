ST3GAL1 drives colorectal cancer metastasis by mediating NRF2-induced activation of the integrin-α6β4 signaling pathway via sialylation modification
Authors: Yang Yang, Simeng Bao, Qingyang Xing, Yupeng Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundColorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most lethal malignancies. ST3 beta-galactoside alpha-2,3-sialyltransferase 1 (ST3GAL1) is a glycosyltransferase that catalyzes the transfer of sialic acid to Galβ1-3-GalNAc-Ser/Thr in an α2,3-linked manner. This study aimed to investigate the function of ST3GAL1 in CRC and reveal its potential mechanism.MethodsThe transcriptional regulation of ST3GAL1 by Nrf2 was validated using dual luciferase mutation experiments and ChIP-qPCR. The expression of ST3GAL1 in CRC tissues was assessed using western blot and immunohistochemistry. Functional experiments including CCK-8, Transwell and flow cytometry were conducted to evaluate the malignant behavior of tumor cells. In vivo tumor growth, metastasis, and chemotherapy response were assessed using subcutaneous, splenic injection, and intrarectal xenograft models. Lectin affinity immunoprecipitation and site-directed mutagenesis were used to identify sialylation sites on integrin-α6β4.ResultsWe first identified that NRF2 directly binds to two key regions on the promoter of ST3GAL1 (-1107~-771 and − 437 ~ + 195) to enhance the transcriptional activity of ST3GAL1. Subsequently, ST3GAL1 was proved to be abnormally highly expressed in CRC clinical samples and serves as a prognostic marker for poor prognosis of patients. Next, we confirmed that down-regulation of ST3GAL1 suppressed the proliferation and metastasis of CRC cells in vitro and in vivo, and also enhanced the sensitivity to 5-fluorouracil chemotherapy. Moreover, overexpression of the wild-type ST3GAL1 (ST3GAL1-WT) promotes the malignant phenotype of CRC, whereas the catalytic domain-mutated vector (ST3GAL1-H299A) has no effects, indicating that the tumor-promoting function of ST3GAL1 is dependent on its sialyltransferase activity. Mechanistically, the lectin affinity immunoprecipitation and site-directed mutagenesis revealed that ST3GAL1 mediates sialylation of integrin-α6β4 at specific O-glycosylation sites (ITGA6: S934, S937, T944; ITGB4: S1515, S1517, T1524), thereby activating the downstream FAK/SRC signaling pathway.ConclusionThis study uncovers a novel Nrf2-ST3GAL1-integrin-α6β4/FAK/SRC regulatory axis driven by sialylation modification, which promotes CRC progression. ST3GAL1 represents a potential therapeutic target for CRC, providing a new strategy for treating metastatic and chemotherapy-resistant CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08025-0.Keywords: Colorectal cancer, ST3GAL1, Nrf2, Integrin-α6β4 signaling pathway, Sialylation

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common cause of cancer deaths worldwide, leading to nearly 850,000 deaths each year [1]. Similar to many other solid tumors, the deaths caused by CRC are not due to the primary tumor itself, but to tumor metastasis. Approximately 70–75% of patients diagnosed with metastatic CRC survive more than 1 year, and less than 20% survive 5 years after diagnosis [1]. Cancer cell metastasis is a very complex process that requires changes in the extracellular matrix to support invasion, increased cell motility, cellular extravasation, and the ability of the cells to initiate and sustain growth at distant sites [2]. Studies have shown that during metastasis, cancer cells will experience high levels of oxidative stress, and only cells that can cope with this challenge can form distant metastases [3, 4].

The Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1/NF-E2-related factor 2 (KEAP1/Nrf2) signaling pathway is a key regulator of redox homeostasis that protects cells from oxidative stress in physiological settings, and aberrant activation of this pathway is frequently found during cancer initiation and progression [5, 6]. This pathway is regulated with Nrf2 as the core. High levels of Nrf2 have been found in several types of human cancer cells [7, 8]. The most common effect of Nrf2 in tumors is to inhibit peroxide production, in addition, Nrf2 can lead to increased expression of cell protective proteins and transporters, which permits cancer cells to gain an advantage by enhancing cell proliferation and can lead to chemotherapy resistance [9–11]. Recent studies have indicated that NRF2 also plays an important role in CRC metastasis [12, 13]. However, the exact mechanism is unclear.

Glycosylation is the most common post-translational modification of membrane-bound and secreted proteins that leads to the expression of tumor-associated carbohydrate antigens [14]. Most tumor-associated carbohydrate antigens contain sialylation structures, and increased sialylation of N-linked or O-linked oligosaccharides frequently occurs on the surface of tumor cells [15, 16]. It is well known that abnormal sialylation is closely related to tumor cell proliferation, metastasis formation, and resistance to treatment [17]. ST3 beta-galactoside alpha-2,3-sialyltransferase 1 (ST3GAL1) is a glycosyltransferase that catalyzes the transfer of sialic acid to Galβ1-3-GalNAc-Ser/Thr in an α2,3-linked manner. Changes in the expression level or activity of this enzyme may result in changes in the composition and length of O-glycans attached to mucin-type proteins [14, 18]. In addition, abnormally high expression of ST3GAL1 has been associated with proliferation, metastasis, and immune escape in a variety of solid tumors [14, 18, 19]. However, its role in CRC has not been reported. In addition, we found that ST3GAL1 may be a downstream target gene of Nrf2 through database analysis. Therefore, this study aims to investigate whether ST3GAL1 can mediate Nrf2 signaling to participate in CRC metastasis.

In this study, we first verified the transcriptional regulation of ST3GAL1 by Nrf2. Next, we evaluated the expression level of ST3GAL1 in CRC and its impact on the malignant behavior of CRC cells. Finally, we explored the potential molecular mechanisms of ST3GAL1 in CRC progression. This study reveals for the first time that ST3GAL1 may be a therapeutic target for CRC.

Materials and methods

Data collection and bioinformatics analysis

The raw data for factors regulated by Nrf2 were obtained from the GEO dataset (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and three datasets (GSE13601, GSE141740, and GSE189804) were selected. DEGs were selected using Log2FC < 0.5, P < 0.05 as the screening condition. The prognostic value of GCNT3 and ST3GAL1 in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients was analyzed by the Gene Set Cancer Analysis (https://guolab.wchscu.cn/GSCA/). The correlation between Nrf2 and ST3GAL1 was analyzed by the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis database (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/). The correlation of ST3GAL1 expression with macrophage infiltration and M2-type markers in CRC was analyzed using the TIMER 2.0 database (https://compbio.cn/timer2/).

Clinical specimens

Thirty-eight paired CRC tissues and adjacent tissues, and one hundred and fifty-nine cases of CRC tissues were provided by Liaoning Cancer Hospital & Institute. All procedures involving human subjects were approved by the Ethics Committee of Liaoning Cancer Hospital & Institute (KY20230715).

Cell culture and treatment

The human CRC cell lines (HT29 and HCT116) were purchased from the Saibaikang company (China). HT29 cells were cultured with the McCOY’s 5 A (Servicebio, China) containing 10% FBS (Tianhangbio, China) in a 34 °C, 5% CO2 incubator. HCT116 cells were cultured with the McCOY’s 5 A containing 10% FBS in a 37 °C, 5% CO2 incubator.

Lipofection transfection experiments were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions for Lipofectamine™ 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). In brief, cells were inoculated in a 6-well plate in the indicated growth medium and transfected after the cells had adhered to the wall. After transfection was completed, the cells were further cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C.

Viral production and transduction using lentiviral vectors

Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfecting HEK293T cells (Saibaikang company, China) with three plasmids: the shuttle plasmid pLVX-shRNA2-Luc-Puro (14 µg, Fenghui Bio, China), the packaging plasmid pSPAX2 (10.5 µg, Fenghui Bio), and the envelope plasmid pMD2.G (3.5 µg, Fenghui Bio). The shRNA sequence targeting ST3GAL1 (NM_003033) was cloned into the BamHI/EcoRI sites of the shuttle vector with the insert: 5’-ggttcgatgagaggttcaacc-3’ or 5’-ggttgaacctctcatcgaacc-3’. Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) when cells reached approximately 60% confluency. At 48 h post-transfection, the viral supernatants were harvested, filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, and concentrated by ultracentrifugation at 72,000 × g for 120 min. The viral pellets were resuspended and titrated, yielding final titers of 1.7 × 10⁸ TU/mL for the control virus (LV-shNC) and 1.4 × 10⁸ TU/mL for the knockdown virus (LV-shST3GAL1). The CRC cells were then washed with PBS, infected with lentivirus at a Multiplicity of Infection (MOI) of 20, and further screened with puromycin to obtain the stable infected cells.

Model 1: The 6 weeks male BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Huachuang Sino Technology Co., LTD (China) and fed ad libitum. The mice were randomly divided into two groups, shNC and shST3GAL1 groups. HT29 and HCT116 cells (1 × 106) in the logarithmic growth phase were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of mice, and the tumor volume was measured every 4 days after growing to a tumor visible to the naked eye, then the tumor was taken after 28 days. Tumor tissue was collected to be weighed, photographed, and used for subsequent testing.

Dual-luciferase assay

To detect the transcriptional regulation of ST3GAL1 by Nrf2, the ST3GAL1 promoter region containing different binding sites was linked to pGL3 vectors, and 293T cells were transfected with vectors and treated with 100 nM KI696. Cells were further cultured at a 37 °C and 5% CO2 incubator. After 48 h, luciferase assays were performed using the luciferase assay kit (KeyGen Biotech, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity values.

The dual luciferase mutation experiment is based on the previous publication by Hiroyuki et al. [20]. The domain mutants were obtained by General Biosynthesis Company (China) from the pGL3-Basic−-1107/+195 or pGL3-Basic−-437/+195 plasmids.Mutants-1 and − 2 (ST3GAL1 promotor (-1107/+195) Mut and ST3GAL1 promotor (-437/+195) Mut) were respectively changed from 5′-GGCTGAATAAC-3′ (-873 to -862) to 5′-ATAACAGGCTG-3′, and from 5′-GTGGCTTGGCA-3′ (-335 to -324) to 5′-TGGCATGTGGC-3′. The transfection study was performed as described earlier.

ChIP-qPCR assay

The ChIP assay was performed according to the ChIP Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Briefly, cells were fixed with 1% formaldehyde for 10 min and then quenched with glycine solution for 5 min at room temperature. Cells were then lysed on ice for 15 min and sonicated until the desired lengths were achieved. After centrifugation of the sample at 12,000×g-14,000×g for 5 min, 20 µL of the samples were removed as Input. The remaining samples were immunoprecipitated with target antibodies and control IgG. Finally, immunoprecipitated chromatin DNA was purified and analyzed by PCR according to the manufacturer’s procedures. The primers were shown below: ST3GAL1 F, 5′-TGACCCCAAAGGCAAACT-3′, ST3GAL1 R, 5′-CTTCCAGATGCGGAGGCT-3′.

Lectin affinity immunoprecipitation

The extraction and quantification of protein samples were performed as described in the western blot section. For the lectin affinity immunoprecipitation, the target IP antibody Maackia Amurensis Lectin II (MAL-II; Vectorlabs, USA) were immobilized onto AminolLink Plus Coupling Resin (Thermo Fisher, USA) using the crosslinker sodium cyanoborohydride. The immobilized antibody resins were then incubated with the protein lysates at room temperature for 2 h with gentle agitation. Following incubation, the resin-antibody-protein complexes were extensively washed with IP wash buffer. Bound proteins were eluted using the elution buffer. The input, unbound flow-through, and co-immunoprecipitated eluates were then separated by SDS-PAGE gels and subsequently transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with blocking buffer and probed overnight at 4 °C with the respective primary antibodies. After washing, the membranes were then incubated with secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 40 min. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL substrate. The primary antibodies including ST3GAL1 antibody (1:1000, Affinity, China), Integrin α6 antibody (1:1000, ABclonal, China), Integrin β4 antibody (1:1000, ABclonal, China), EGFR antibody (1:3000, ABclonal, China). The secondary antibodies including goat anti-rabbit (1:10000, Proteintech, China) or goat anti-mouse (1:10000, Proteintech, China).

Flow cytometry

The THP1-M0 macrophages were induced using 100 ng/mL PMA. These M0 macrophages (2 × 105) were placed in the lower chamber, while CRC cells (5 × 105) were cultured in the upper chamber. The cells were collected after 48 h and centrifuged at 150 g for 5 min to remove the supernatant. The sample was resuspended with 100 µL of buffer, 5 µL of CD206 antibody (Cat# E-AB-F1161C, Elabscience, China) was added, and the cells were incubated for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Finally, the expression of M2-type marker CD206 in the cells was detected by flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

All results are presented as the mean ± SD and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 9.5 software. The comparison between the two groups was performed by the unpaired Student’s t-test, whereas the comparison among multiple groups was performed by one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons tests. The criterion for significance was p < 0.05 for all comparisons.

Results

Identification of ST3GAL1 as a novel Nrf2-regulated gene with prognostic value in CRC

NF-E2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) has been found to promote the malignant progression of colorectal cancer (CRC), but the specific mechanism of action is unclear. We first analyzed three independent GEO datasets (GSE136011, GSE141740, and GSE189804) and identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) positively regulated by Nrf2 using a threshold of log₂FC < 0.5 and p < 0.05. This analysis revealed 15 overlapping genes that were continuously downregulated when Nrf2 was knockdown (Fig. 1A). To gain functional insights into these 15 candidate genes, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. The GO and KEGG analysis highlighted significant involvement in pathways related to glycosylation and cancer metastasis (Fig. 1B-C). Since glycosylation-associated proteins play an important role in tumor metastasis, this study focused on glycosylation-associated factors, among which glucosamine (N-acetyl) transferase 3 (GCNT3) and ST3 beta-galactoside alpha-2,3-sialyltransferase 1 (ST3GAL1) caught our attention. We further analyzed the expression of GCNT3 and ST3GAL1 in CRC and their correlation with CRC metastasis, as shown in Fig. 1D-E. Unlike GCNT3, ST3GAL1 is highly expressed in CRC and may promote CRC metastasis. To assess the clinical relevance of these findings, we evaluated the prognostic value of GCNT3 and ST3GAL1 using the GSCA database, and the results revealed that high ST3GAL1 expression was significantly correlated with worse disease-specific survival (DSS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in CRC patients (Fig. 1G). In contrast, GCNT3 expression showed no significant association with patient survival outcomes (Fig. 1F). In summary, based on its upregulation in CRC, association with metastatic status, and significant prognostic value, we selected ST3GAL1 as a promising downstream target of Nrf2 for further functional investigation in CRC.

Nrf2 promotes the expression of ST3GAL1 in CRC cells

Next, we assess whether the expression of ST3GAL1 in CRC cells is regulated by Nrf2. We first treated CRC cells (HT29 and HCT116 cells) with 100 nM KI696, a small molecule inhibitor of the Keap1-Nrf2 interaction, which can antagonize the effect of KEAP1 on Nrf2 and protect the Nrf2 protein from degradation. As expected, KI696 treatment resulted in rapid accumulation of Nrf2 as well as ST3GAL1 protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 2A-B). In the presence of KI696, we further transfected cells with siNrf2 or siNC and showed that the knockdown of Nrf2 partially reversed the increase in Nrf2 and ST3GAL1 expression caused by KI696 (Fig. 2C-D). The above findings provided more powerful evidence that Nrf2 positively regulates the expression of ST3GAL1. To elucidate the transcriptional mechanism, we first identified potential Nrf2 binding sites on the ST3GAL1 promoter. Analysis of the ST3GAL1 promoter sequence from the UCSC database using the JASPAR database revealed several putative Nrf2 binding sites (Fig. 2E). We further transfected cells with dual-luciferase vectors containing different ST3GAL1 promoter sequences and treated them with KI696, and a dual-luciferase assay was performed 48 h later. As shown in Fig. 2F, KI696 treatment significantly enhanced the luciferase activity, and Nrf2 may have binding sites on the regions “-1107 to -771” (containing site3) and “-437 to + 195” (containing site1) on the ST3GAL1 promoter. Consequently, we focused on these two key regions for site-directed mutagenesis of the predicted core binding sequences within them (Fig. 2E). We generated the luciferase reporter vectors containing either the wild-type (WT) or mutant (Mut) versions of these two promoter regions and transfected them into HT29 cells, followed by treatment with the Nrf2 agonist KI696 (100 nM). As shown in Fig. 2G-H, KI696 treatment markedly increased the activity of the ST3GAL1WT promoter, whereas this enhancement was inhibited when the putative binding site was mutated. We further examined the binding status of Nrf2 to the ST3GAL1 promoter under KI696 treatment conditions, and the ChIP-qPCR results showed that the association between Nrf2 and ST3GAL1 promoter was significantly increased in CRC cells treated with KI696 (Fig. 2I). In addition, we further found that Nrf2 and ST3GAL1 were highly correlated in CRC through the GEPIA database (Fig. 2J). Taken together, our findings suggested that ST3GAL1 was positively regulated by Nrf2 in CRC cells. To explore the transcriptional regulation landscape of ST3GAL1, we also evaluated the effects of other common transcription factors. Successful overexpression of AP-1 and SP1 in CRC cells was confirmed by qPCR (Fig. S1A-B). Interestingly, overexpression of either AP-1 or SP1 also led to a significant upregulation of ST3GAL1 mRNA levels (Fig. S1C-D), indicating that ST3GAL1 expression can be modulated by multiple transcription factors.

ST3GAL1 is highly expressed in CRC and associated with advanced TNM stage

We collected thirty-eight pairs of fresh CRC and adjacent tissues to detect the expression of ST3GAL1 and found that the expression of ST3GAL1 in CRC tissues was significantly higher than that in adjacent tissues (Fig. 3A-B). To further investigate the clinical significance of ST3GAL1 in CRC, we collected data from hundred and fifty-nine clinical samples of CRC patients and analyzed the relationship between its expression level and clinicopathological parameters. The data showed that the expression level of ST3GAL1 was closely related to the TNM stage (Table 1). Figure 3C showed immunohistochemical staining maps of low and high expression of ST3GAL1. Taken together, we found that ST3GAL1 was highly expressed in CRC and may be associated with poor tumor prognosis.

ST3GAL1 knockdown inhibits the proliferation, invasion, and migration of CRC cells

Next, we assessed whether ST3GAL1 affects the malignant behavior of CRC cells. We first transfected the cells with siST3GAL1 and its control and verified the transfection efficiency (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, we found that ST3GAL1 knockdown significantly inhibited CRC cell viability compared to negative control cells (Fig. 4B). The metastasis and invasion of cancer cells are important factors leading to the malignant development of CRC, so we examined the effects of ST3GAL1 on tumor cell migration and invasion. As shown in Fig. 4C-D, compared with negative control cells, the migration and invasion rate of cells in the ST3GAL1 knockdown group was slower. We further examined the expression of two epithelial-mesenchymal transition markers, E-cadherin and Vimentin. As expected, E-cadherin expression was significantly up-regulated in cells with ST3GAL1 knockdown, while Vimentin expression was decreased (Fig. 4E). Additionally, we observed decreased expression of key EMT-transcription factors, including ZEB1 and Snail, in ST3GAL1-deficient cells (Fig. 4F). Collectively, these findings demonstrated that ST3GAL1 knockdown effectively suppresses the malignant phenotypes of CRC cells, including proliferation, migration, and invasion, potentially through the reversal of EMT processes.

ST3GAL1 knockdown suppresses tumor growth, metastasis, and chemoresistance in vivo

To validate the tumor-promoting function of ST3GAL1 under physiological conditions, we established multiple xenograft models in immunodeficient mice. The CRC cells with ST3GAL1 knockdown were injected subcutaneously into nude mice, and tumor growth was observed and photographed 28 days later. The results showed that cells in the shST3GAL1 group caused significantly less tumor volume and weight than cells in the shNC group (Fig. 5A-C). The expression of ST3GAL1 protein in tumor tissues was further detected, and the results showed that the expression of ST3GAL1 protein in tumor tissues of the shST3GAL1 group was significantly lower than that of the shNC group (Fig. 5D). In addition, we demonstrated that the expression levels of Ki67, a marker for tumor growth, were downregulated after ST3GAL1 knockdown (Fig. 5E). To specifically assess the role of ST3GAL1 in hepatic metastasis, we employed a splenic injection model in NOD-SCID mice, and the luciferase-labeled CRC cells (5 × 10⁵) with shST3GAL1 or shNC were injected under the spleen capsule (Fig. 5F). The bioluminescence imaging demonstrated a remarkable reduction in liver metastatic burden in the shST3GAL1 group compared to the shNC control (Fig. 5H). This finding was corroborated by histopathological examination, where H&E staining of liver tissues showed significantly fewer metastatic foci in mice receiving shST3GAL1 cells (Fig. 5I). We further investigated spontaneous metastasis using an intrarectal implantation model with suspension of CRC cells was injected into the submucosal layer of the rectum in BALB/c nude mice (Fig. 5G). As shown in Fig. 5J, ST3GAL1 knockdown markedly suppressed metastatic spread to distant organs, subsequent H&E staining confirmed a substantial decrease in metastatic nodules in both lung (Fig. 5K) and abdominal lymph node (Fig. 5L) tissues from the shST3GAL1 group. Additionally, we examined the potential of ST3GAL1 as a therapeutic target by evaluating its influence on chemotherapy response. First, in vitro sensitivity assays were performed by treating control and ST3GAL1-knockdown CRC cells with increasing concentrations of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU; 0–10 µM). As shown in Fig. S2A-B, ST3GAL1 knockdown significantly increased the sensitivity of CRC cells to 5-FU, with a notable reduction in IC50 values (Fig. S2A-B). In the subcutaneous model, ST3GAL1 knockdown enhanced the sensitivity of CRC tumors to 5-FU, with the combination of shST3GAL1 and 5-FU treatment resulting in the most pronounced tumor growth inhibition (Fig. S2C-D). In addition, immunohistochemical analysis further confirmed the lowest Ki67 proliferation effect in this combination group (Fig. S2E-F). Taken together, these data provided compelling evidence that ST3GAL1 is crucial for CRC tumor growth, metastatic progression, and chemotherapy resistance, highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for CRC.

ST3GAL1 mediates Nrf2-induced malignant behavior of CRC cells

Next, we evaluated whether ST3GAL1 serves as a functional mediator of Nrf2 in promoting CRC progression and performed a series of rescue experiments. We first transfected cells with siNC or siST3GAL1 and added KI696 treatment at the same time, and then detected cell viability by CCK-8. As shown in Fig. 6A, the viability of cells receiving KI696 treatment was significantly enhanced, but were inhibited after transfection with siST3GAL1. Similarly, the migration and invasion ability of CRC cells treated only with KI696 was enhanced but significantly inhibited after transfection with siST3GAL1 (Fig. 6B-C). Overall, these findings suggested that ST3GAL1 functions as a crucial downstream effector of Nrf2 signaling in CRC cells. ST3GAL1 promotes the malignant progression of CRC through its sialyltransferase activity

It is well known that ST3GAL1 is a key sialyltransferase, and the maintenance of its sialyltransferase activity has a non-negligible role in tumor development [14, 21]. Jeanneau et al. pointed out that compared with the wild-type ST3GAL1 (ST3GAL1-WT), the H299A mutant significantly inhibited the enzyme activity but did not affect the expression of ST3GAL1 22. We first transfected CRC cells with ST3GAL1-WT or the catalytic domain mutated vector (ST3GAL1-H299A) and detected ST3GAL1 expression. As shown in Fig. 7A, the results confirmed comparable overexpression of both ST3GAL1-WT and ST3GAL1-H299A proteins, indicating that the H299A mutation does not affect protein expression or stability. Next, we examined whether the H299A mutant vector affected the proliferation, migration, and invasion of CRC cells, respectively. The results showed that the proliferation, migration, and invasion ability of cells in the ST3GAL1-WT group were significantly enhanced compared with the vector group. In contrast, cells expressing the catalytically inactive ST3GAL1-H299A mutant showed proliferation and migration rate comparable to control cells, demonstrating that the sialyltransferase activity is indispensable for ST3GAL1-mediated malignant behavior of CRC cells (Fig. 7B-D). We further explored whether ST3GAL1-mediated sialylation influences the tumor microenvironment. We first comprehensively evaluated the correlation between ST3GAL1 expression and multiple immune cell types using the TIMER 3.0 database. As shown in Fig. S3A, the ST3GAL1 expression showed no significant correlation with the infiltration levels of CD8⁺ T cells or Natural Killer (NK) cells. However, it remained positively correlated with macrophage infiltration abundance and the expression of key pro‑tumor markers. This initial bioinformatic evidence strongly suggested a potential link between ST3GAL1 and the recruitment and/or polarization of pro-tumorigenic macrophages. To experimentally validate this finding, we established a co-culture system where M0 macrophages derived from THP-1 cells by PMA treatment. Compared to the control cells, M0 macrophages exhibited enhanced migration toward ST3GAL1-WT-overexpressing CRC cells (Fig. S3B). Furthermore, co-culture with ST3GAL1-WT-overexpressing CRC cells significantly promoted M2 macrophage polarization, as demonstrated by increased expression of the characteristic surface marker CD206 (Fig. S3C) and elevated levels of the canonical M2 markers CD163 and MRC1 (Fig. S3D-E). Notably, this immunomodulatory effect was abrogated by the catalytically inactive ST3GAL1-H299A mutant, indicating a dependence on functional sialyltransferase activity. In summary, our findings suggested that ST3GAL1 promoted the malignant progression of CRC by maintaining its sialyltransferase activity.

ST3GAL1 activates the Integrin-α6β4 signaling pathway through its sialyltransferase activity

Having identified the tumor-promoting role of ST3GAL1 in CRC, we further explored its possible regulatory mechanisms. Pietrobono et al. analyzed the sialylation of proteins affected by ST3GAL1 through proteomics [19]. Based on this study, we conducted GO and KEGG enrichment analysis of sialylation factors downstream of ST3GAL1. As shown in Fig. 8A-B, we mainly present terms related to cell invasion and migration and demonstrated the factors enriched in the above terms through the chordal diagram, among which EGFR, ITGA6, and ITGB4 caught our attention. The regulatory effect of ST3GAL1 on EGFR has been demonstrated and both of them are involved in the malignant lesions of CRC [23, 24]. Unlike other integrins, the dimer formed by ITGA6 and ITGB4 has a large β subunit, which is mainly expressed in intestinal epithelial cells, and its activity is inhibited by desialylation [25–27]. Therefore, this study mainly focused on whether ST3GAL1 affects the activation of the Integrin-α6β4 signaling pathway. For this purpose, we first employed lectin affinity immunoprecipitation using MAL-II, which specifically recognizes α-2,3-linked sialic acids, to assess the sialylation status of potential targets. As shown in Fig. 8C, ST3GAL1 knockdown in the Input group was accompanied by downregulation of ST3GAL1 expression, but the expressions of EGFR, ITGA6, and ITGB4 were not affected, but analysis of MAL-II immunoprecipitate revealed that ST3GAL1 knockdown reduced the sialylation of both EGFR, ITGA6, and ITGB4. To further characterize the sialylation modification on Integrin-α6β4, we performed bioinformatic prediction using the online site YinOYang, which identified multiple potential O-glycosylation sites on ITGA6 and ITGB4 (Fig. 8D). We then experimentally validated these predictions by treating cell lysates with Neuraminidase and O-Glycosidase. As shown in Fig. 8E-F, Neuraminidase treatment alone reduced the molecular weight of both ITGA6 and ITGB4, indicating desialylation. In addition, a more pronounced molecular weight shift was observed upon combined Neuraminidase and O-Glycosidase treatment, confirming the presence of sialylated O-glycans on these integrins. To pinpoint the exact residues sialylated by ST3GAL1, we selected three predicted O-glycosylation sites with the highest score, and generated FLAG-tagged constructs of ITGA6 and ITGB4, each of which contained a point mutation at one of the sites (ITGA6: S934A, S937A, T944A; ITGB4: S1515A, S1517A, T1524A). We then co-transfected these mutants, along with the wild-type (WT) constructs, with an ST3GAL1-HA plasmid into CRC cells. The co-immunoprecipitation using MAL-II lectin demonstrated that wild-type ITGA6 and ITGB4 showed enhanced sialylation upon ST3GAL1 overexpression. In contrast, the glycosylation-deficient mutants exhibited a substantial loss of sialic acid modification (Fig. 8G-H). These results pinpointed S934, S937, T944 on ITGA6 and S1515, S1517, T1524 on ITGB4 may as major sites for ST3GAL1-mediated sialylation. Taken together, these data suggested that ST3GAL1 activated the integrin-α6β4 signaling pathway through its sialyltransferase activity.

ST3GAL1 promotes the malignant behavior of CRC cells by regulating the integrin-α6β4 signaling pathway

Finally, we investigated the functional consequence of this specific sialylation on Integrin-α6β4 signaling. As shown in Fig. 9A-B, ST3GAL1 knockdown did not alter total protein levels of ITGA6, ITGB4, FAK, or SRC. However, it significantly attenuated EGF-induced phosphorylation of both FAK at Y397 and SRC at Y419. Furthermore, overexpression of ST3GAL1 promoted the phosphorylation of FAK/SRC, but cells expressing the catalytically inactive ST3GAL1-H299A mutant were significantly inhibited in terms of FAK/SRC phosphorylation. CRC cells were co-transfected with ST3GAL1-WT and siITGA6, siITGB4, or treated with FAK inhibitor PF-573288, and SRC inhibitor PP2 in advance. In migration and invasion assays, the migration and invasion ability of tumor cells transfected with siITGA6 or siITGB4 was significantly inhibited compared with the ST3GAL1-WT group. Similarly, when FAK and SRC signaling was suppressed, the malignant behavior of CRC cells was also suppressed (Fig. 9C-D). In addition, to examine the functional hierarchy within this pathway under NRF2-activated conditions, we treated cells with KI696 (100 nM) to activate NRF2 signaling while perturbing different pathway components. NRF2 activation significantly enhanced cell migration and invasion, but these effects were substantially attenuated by either ITGA6/ITGB4 knockdown or FAK/SRC pharmacological inhibition (Fig. 9E-F). Overall, these results indicated that ST3GAL1-mediated sialylation enables activation of the Integrin-α6β4 signaling complex, which in turn facilitates FAK and SRC phosphorylation, ultimately driving the migration and invasion capacities of CRC cells.

Discussion

Nrf2 is a key transcription factor for cell adaptation and defense against oxidative stress, giving tumor cells enhanced resistance to oxidative damage [28]. Growing evidence has established the Nrf2 pathway as a driver of cancer progression and metastasis [5, 6, 29]. Wolowczyk et al. noted that inhibition of Nrf2 can increase the level of intracellular reactive oxygen species and significantly reduce the ability to form primary tumors and lung metastases [4]. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that Nrf2 has a non-negligible role in cancer metastasis. Given that metastasis contributes to the high mortality rate of CRC, we explored possible mechanisms by which Nrf2 promotes tumor metastasis.

ST3GAL1 is a member of the sialyltransferase family of enzymes that partially transfer sialic acid to the hydroxyl groups at the ends of glycoproteins or glycolipid oligosaccharide chains on cell membranes, causing them to undergo sialylation [30]. The high sialylation of tumor cells will make the polysaccharide on its surface become the initial ligand of sialic-binding immunoglobulin-like lectins on the surface of immune cells. Once combined with the sialylated glycans, it will promote the immunosuppressive signal, thereby protecting tumor cells [31]. In addition, cancer-associated excess sialylation affects tumor cell interactions and is associated with metastatic cell behavior [32, 33]. Recent studies have shown that ST3GAL1 promotes tumor angiogenesis and epithelial-mesenchymal transition via TGF-β, thereby exacerbating breast and ovarian cancer metastasis [34]. Therefore, it is reasonable to assume that inhibition of sialic acid transferase is a potential strategy to prevent cancer metastasis. Our research has revealed that inhibiting the expression of ST3GAL1 not only can suppress the proliferation, migration, and invasion of colorectal cancer cells, but also enhances their sensitivity to chemotherapy. This indicated that ST3GAL1 is a target with great potential for treating metastatic and chemotherapy-resistant CRC.

Abnormal sialylation has long been associated with various tumor diseases [35, 36]. ST3GAL1 was found to promote immune escape in cancer by regulating salivary acidification of CD55 14. ST3GAL1 can also form a closed-loop pathway with TGF-β, and ST3GAL1 promotes TGF-β pathway by sialylation of VASN [21, 37]. Therefore, the maintenance of ST3GAL1 sialyltransferase may promote the malignant behavior of tumor cells. In this study, we found that the promotion of CRC cell proliferation and metastatic ability was reduced by H299A mutant vector ST3GAL1-H299A, which was also consistent with the previously reported inhibition of ST3GAL1 enzyme activity by the H299A mutant [22]. Notably, ST3GAL1’s impact extends beyond direct regulation of CRC malignant phenotypes to modulation of the tumor microenvironment. M2-type macrophages are well-documented to secrete pro-tumor cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases, which remodel the extracellular matrix and suppress anti-tumor immunity, thereby facilitating tumor invasion and metastasis [38]. We found that ST3GAL1 overexpressing CRC cells robustly promoted M2 polarization of macrophages, while this effect was abolished by the catalytically inactive mutant ST3GAL1-H299A, underscoring the dependence on functional sialylation.

We found that integrin-α6 and integrin-β4 may be downstream targets of ST3GAL1 through bioinformatics analysis. Integrin α6β4 is a laminin receptor, and increased expression levels are associated with malignant progression and poor survival in various cancers, and its cytoplasmic domain phosphorylation is a well-documented trigger for metastatic cascades [39, 40]. Our findings extend this understanding by revealing that ST3GAL1-mediated sialylation acts as a “molecular switch” for integrin-α6β4 activation, independent of changes in its total protein levels. This regulatory mode adds a new layer of complexity to how integrin signaling is fine-tuned in CRC cells. Prior study has established that integrin-α6β4 activation typically relies on ligand binding or cross-talk with growth factor receptors, which induce conformational changes and cytoplasmic domain phosphorylation [41]. Our work challenges this paradigm by showing that sialylation of specific O-linked glycans (ITGA6: S934/S937/T944; ITGB4: S1515/S1517/T1524) can also activate integrin-α6β4 signaling. By demonstrating that ST3GAL1 targets these specific O-glycosites, we align with emerging evidence that post-translational modification via sialylation can serve as an alternative activation pathway for integrin-α6β4, expanding our knowledge of how integrin signaling is modulated in cancer cells. For instance, Li et al. showed ST3GAL1-mediated O-linked sialylation of CD55 enables immune evasion by altering protein conformation and ligand binding [14]. For integrin α6β4, we hypothesize that sialylation at these sites may stabilize its active conformation to enhance its ability to recruit and activate FAK/SRC, which will be further demonstrated in our subsequent studies. The co-expression of FAK and SRC in CRC is associated with tumor recurrence and metastasis formation [42, 43]. We further tested whether ST3GAL1 could regulate the FAK/SRC pathway, and the results showed that ST3GAL1 affected the phosphorylation of FAK/SRC only through salivary acidification. These data suggested that ST3GAL1 regulated the malignant progression of CRC by exerting its sialyltransferase activity to regulate integrin α6β4 and downstream signal FAK/SRC.

While our study delineated a coherent signaling axis from NRF2 to ST3GAL1-mediated sialylation of integrin α6β4 and subsequent FAK/SRC activation, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although bioinformatic analysis using public databases strongly supports the prognostic value of ST3GAL1, the majority of patients in our own clinical cohort still survived, and some patients had lost contact for follow-up to perform robust multivariate survival analysis. Future prospective studies with extended follow-up will be essential to validate ST3GAL1 as an independent prognostic marker in our patient population. Second, we noted that in the in vivo combination treatment group (shST3GAL1 + 5-FU), the degree of tumor growth inhibition did not appear to be substantially greater than that in the 5-FU alone group. We speculate that this may be due to the relatively high dose of 5-FU used in our current experiment, thereby limiting the observable additive benefit of ST3GAL1 knockdown. However, due to current animal ethics constraints, which restrict further dose reduction studies, we were unable to optimize the chemotherapy dosage within this experiment. In future studies, we plan to systematically titrate the dose of 5-FU and explore combination strategies with other chemotherapeutic agents to better elucidate the synergistic potential of targeting ST3GAL1 in sensitizing CRC to chemotherapy. Third, in our study, ST3GAL1‑mediated sialylation of integrin‑α6β4 leads to the activation of the FAK/SRC kinase axis, which in turn promotes cell migration and invasion. We speculate that FAK/SRC may phosphorylate multiple downstream targets to further enhance the metastasis-promoting signals. This concept is strongly supported by the recent studies, which demonstrate that the FAK/SRC axis directly phosphorylates some proteins involved in metastasis of other cancers [44–48]. This work provides a clear precedent for FAK/SRC-dependent phosphorylation of diverse effector molecules in cancer progression. Importantly, this regulatory pattern is highly relevant to CRC [49, 50]. Therefore, conducting a systematic proteomic analysis of protein phosphorylation under the regulation of ST3GAL1 will undoubtedly provide a more comprehensive picture of the metastasis signaling network that this glycosyltransferase is involved in. This offers a valuable direction for future research. In summary, ST3GAL1 plays a catalytic role in the progression of CRC and aggravates the malignant phenotype of tumor cells. Our results not only demonstrate the role of ST3GAL1 in promoting the proliferation and migration of CRC cells but also reveal the upstream and downstream regulatory mechanisms of ST3GAL1 (Fig. 10). Our findings suggest that ST3GAL1 may be a promising biomarker for CRC diagnosis and treatment.
