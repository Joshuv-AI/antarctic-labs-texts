Comparison of the ED90 of Esketamine for Suppressing Laryngeal Mask Airway Insertion Responses During Propofol Induction with or without Intravenous Lidocaine Pretreatment: A Randomized Double-Blind Dose-Finding Trial
Authors: Liangguang Zhang, Long Zhang, Jianlin Wang, Shunli Diao, Yingying Liu, Jun Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEsketamine has been increasingly used as an adjunct to propofol induction because of its analgesic and sympathomimetic properties. However, the ED90 of esketamine for suppressing laryngeal mask airway (LMA) insertion responses under a fixed propofol background, with or without intravenous lidocaine pretreatment, remains unclear. This study aimed to determine and compare this ED90 in patients receiving intravenous lidocaine or normal saline.MethodsIn this randomized, double-blind, dose-finding trial, adult patients undergoing elective arthroscopic shoulder surgery received intravenous lidocaine 1 mg·kg−1 (Group L) or an equal volume of normal saline (Group N) before anesthesia induction. Thirty seconds later, esketamine was administered according to a biased coin design, followed by propofol 2 mg·kg−1. LMA insertion responses were assessed using a predefined four-item behavioral scoring system, and successful suppression was defined as a total score of 4. Enrollment continued until at least 45 effective responses were obtained in each group. The primary outcome was the ED90 of esketamine, estimated by centered isotonic regression with bootstrap confidence intervals.ResultsNinety-nine patients completed the study. The estimated ED90 of esketamine was 0.173 mg·kg−1 (95% CI, 0.138–0.209 mg·kg−1) in Group L and 0.229 mg·kg−1 (95% CI, 0.190–0.263 mg·kg−1) in Group N, with an absolute between-group difference of −0.056 mg·kg−1 (95% CI, −0.102 to −0.002 mg·kg−1). Compared with Group N, Group L also showed lower heart rate immediately before and 3 min after LMA insertion, as well as a lower incidence of tachycardia. Other peri-induction adverse events were similar between groups.ConclusionIntravenous lidocaine pretreatment was associated with a lower ED90 of esketamine for suppressing LMA insertion responses during propofol induction, suggesting an esketamine-sparing effect within this specific induction framework.

Introduction

Shoulder arthroscopy is commonly performed as a minimally invasive procedure associated with limited surgical trauma and rapid postoperative recovery. In this setting, the laryngeal mask airway (LMA) is frequently used for airway management, particularly when neuromuscular blocking agents are avoided and early recovery is desired. Compared with endotracheal intubation, LMA placement generally causes less airway stimulation and fewer hemodynamic disturbances; however, successful insertion still requires adequate suppression of upper airway reflexes during anesthesia induction.1–5 Propofol remains one of the most widely used agents for facilitating LMA insertion because of its rapid onset and favorable hypnotic profile. However, because it lacks intrinsic analgesic activity, increasing the dose to improve insertion conditions may increase the risks of hypotension, respiratory depression, and apnea.3–5 Therefore, adjunctive strategies that improve insertion conditions while limiting propofol exposure remain clinically relevant.

Esketamine and intravenous lidocaine are two commonly used peri-induction adjuncts with potential value in this context. As the S-enantiomer of ketamine, esketamine provides potent analgesic and antinociceptive effects and may reduce propofol requirements while preserving hemodynamic stability.6–15 However, higher or unnecessary doses of esketamine may be associated with delayed recovery or emergence-related psychomimetic effects in some settings, highlighting the importance of identifying an appropriate dose within a specific induction regimen.14,15

Intravenous lidocaine has also been reported to attenuate airway reflexes and improve LMA insertion conditions during propofol induction, and may reduce peri-induction anesthetic requirements in selected settings.5,8 These findings suggest that both agents may facilitate LMA placement and optimize propofol-based induction. However, previous studies have focused mainly on propofol requirements or clinical insertion conditions, rather than on the dose requirement of adjunctive esketamine itself under a standardized propofol background.5–8

We hypothesized that intravenous lidocaine would reduce the esketamine requirement for successful suppression of LMA insertion responses and improve peri-induction hemodynamic responses. Therefore, the present prospective randomized dose-finding study was designed to determine and compare the 90% effective dose (ED90) of esketamine required to suppress LMA insertion responses in patients undergoing shoulder arthroscopy, with or without intravenous lidocaine pretreatment, using a biased coin design sequential method.

Methods

Study Design, Ethics, and Registration

This prospective, randomized, dose-finding study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of Ningbo No. 6 Hospital (Approval No. 2025–54L). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The study was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2500108472) on September 1, 2025, prior to patient recruitment. Patient enrollment was conducted from September 2025 to January 2026.

Participants

Adult patients aged 18–65 years who were scheduled for elective shoulder arthroscopy under general anesthesia with a laryngeal mask airway (LMA) were screened for eligibility. Eligible patients had American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status I–II and a body mass index (BMI) of 18–30 kg·m−2. Exclusion criteria included anticipated difficult airway, as determined by routine preoperative airway assessment, significant cardiopulmonary, hepatic, renal, psychiatric, or neurological disease, airway hyperreactivity, contraindications to N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor antagonists, known allergy to study drugs, and use of sedative or analgesic medications before surgery.

Randomization and Blinding

This study was a prospective, randomized, double-blind, controlled dose-finding trial. Patients were assigned in a 1:1 ratio to the lidocaine group (Group L) or the control group (Group N) according to a computer-generated randomization sequence created using SPSS version 26.0. Allocation codes were sealed in sequentially numbered, opaque envelopes and opened only by an independent anesthesia nurse responsible for study drug preparation immediately before anesthesia induction. Group allocation was concealed from all investigators involved in patient management, LMA insertion, response assessment, and data analysis.

Patients, the anesthesiologist performing induction and LMA insertion, and the investigator assessing LMA insertion responses were all blinded to group assignment. The independent anesthesia nurse, who was not involved in intraoperative management, outcome assessment, or statistical analysis, prepared all study medications. Lidocaine in Group L was diluted with normal saline to a total volume of 10 mL, and an identical volume of normal saline was prepared for Group N, ensuring that study solutions were indistinguishable in appearance. Esketamine doses were prepared by the same nurse according to the predefined biased coin design algorithm based on the response of the preceding patient. The adaptive dose-allocation process was therefore implemented without revealing group assignment or dose information to the blinded investigators.

Anesthesia, LMA Insertion, and Outcome Assessment

All patients fasted for at least 8 h before surgery and received standard monitoring on arrival in the operating room. Baseline hemodynamic variables were defined as the mean of three consecutive measurements obtained within 10 min before induction. Before induction of general anesthesia, ultrasound-guided interscalene brachial plexus block was performed using 10 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine, combined with a superficial cervical plexus block using 5 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine.

After preoxygenation, patients received intravenous lidocaine 1 mg·kg−1 in Group L or an equal volume of normal saline in Group N, followed by intravenous esketamine. Thirty seconds later, propofol 2 mg·kg−1 was administered. The Modified Observer’s Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (MOAA/S) score was assessed every 10s by a blinded investigator, and LMA insertion was attempted by the same experienced anesthesiologist once the MOAA/S score reached 0. LMA insertion was performed using a disposable LMA (Zhejiang Sungood Technology Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China), and the size was selected according to body weight. Thereafter, anesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane, and subsequent airway management followed standardized institutional protocols.

LMA insertion responses were evaluated by a blinded investigator using a predefined four-item behavioral scoring system, including swallowing, coughing/gagging, head or body movement, and laryngospasm (Table 1).16 The total score ranged from 4 to 12, with lower scores indicating more favorable insertion conditions and better suppression of airway reflexes. Mouth opening and ease of insertion were not included because they are more susceptible to upper airway anatomy and operator-dependent factors. Dose-Finding Design, Outcomes, and Safety Monitoring

The dose–response relationship of esketamine was independently determined in each group using a biased coin design sequential method targeting a 90% probability of effective response (ED90). The initial esketamine dose was 0.20 mg·kg−1, with a fixed step size of 0.05 mg·kg−1. This initial dose was selected because 0.20 mg·kg−1 has been evaluated as a low-dose esketamine level in previous esketamine–propofol studies involving LMA insertion or procedural anesthesia, and was considered an appropriate starting dose to balance expected efficacy and safety based on our preliminary clinical experience.6,7 If the previous patient exhibited an effective response, the same dose was administered to the subsequent patient with a probability of 0.89, or the dose was decreased by one step with a probability of 0.11. If the response was ineffective, the dose was increased by one step. Enrollment continued until at least 45 effective responses were obtained in each group, based on a stopping criterion used in a previous biased coin design dose-finding study.17

The primary outcome was the effectiveness of suppression of LMA insertion responses. Secondary outcomes included LMA insertion time, hemodynamic changes at predefined time points (T0: upon arrival in the operating room; T1: immediately before LMA insertion; T2: 3 minutes after LMA insertion), additional propofol requirements, and the incidence of peri-induction adverse events.

During anesthesia induction and airway management, patients were continuously monitored for adverse events. Hypotension was defined as a decrease in mean arterial pressure greater than 30% from baseline or an absolute blood pressure <85/50 mmHg lasting more than 2 minutes. Hypertension was defined as an increase in systolic or diastolic blood pressure exceeding 30% of baseline. Bradycardia was defined as a heart rate <60 beats·min−1, tachycardia as a heart rate >100 beats·min−1, and hypoxemia as peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) <90%. All adverse events were recorded from anesthesia induction until 3 minutes after successful airway establishment and were managed according to standardized institutional protocols. Patients were also monitored for clinically apparent signs suggestive of local anesthetic systemic toxicity, such as seizure activity, arrhythmia, or unexplained hemodynamic instability.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for non-normally distributed data. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the independent-samples t test or the Mann–Whitney-U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Hemodynamic variables measured at different time points were analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (repeated-measures ANOVA), with group as the between-subject factor and time as the within-subject factor. When significant main effects or interaction effects were identified, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed for within-group comparisons across time points and between-group comparisons at each time point, as appropriate. In addition, within-subject contrasts were used to evaluate linear and quadratic trends over time and their interactions with group.

For the dose-finding analysis, the ED90 of esketamine, defined as the dose associated with a 90% probability of successful suppression of LMA insertion responses, was estimated separately in each group on the basis of binary response outcomes (effective vs ineffective). Analyses were conducted in R using the cir package for centered isotonic regression. Observed response probabilities across dose levels were first constrained to a monotonic dose–response relationship using isotonic regression with the pooled-adjacent-violators algorithm (PAVA). The dose corresponding to a predicted response probability of 0.90 was then estimated using centered isotonic regression. Confidence intervals for ED90 were calculated using the bias-corrected Morris method with 2000 bootstrap replications. Between-group differences in ED90 were assessed by estimating the ED90 difference (Group L minus Group N) together with its bootstrap-based 95% confidence interval.

Dose–Response Relationship and ED90 Estimation

Using centered isotonic regression analysis, the ED90 of esketamine required to suppress responses to LMA insertion was estimated to be 0.173 mg·kg−1 (95% CI 0.138–0.209) in the lidocaine group and 0.229 mg·kg−1 (95% CI 0.190–0.263) in the control group. The between-group difference in ED90 (Group L − Group N) was −0.056 mg·kg−1 (95% CI −0.102 to −0.002), corresponding to an approximately 24.5% relative reduction in the esketamine ED90 with lidocaine pretreatment. Hemodynamic variables are shown in Figure 3. Repeated-measures ANOVA showed significant time effects for systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and heart rate. In both groups, systolic and diastolic blood pressures decreased significantly at T1 and T2 compared with baseline (T0) (all P < 0.05), with no significant difference between T1 and T2, whereas heart rate increased significantly at T1 and T2 compared with T0 (all P < 0.001), also without a significant difference between T1 and T2. Between-group comparisons showed no significant differences in systolic blood pressure at T0 or T1, although systolic blood pressure at T2 was slightly lower in Group L than in Group N (P = 0.041). No significant between-group differences in diastolic blood pressure were observed at any time point. For heart rate, Group N had a significantly higher value than Group L at T1 (P = 0.043), whereas no significant difference was found at T0 or T2. Trend analysis further showed a significant group-by-time interaction for heart rate (linear trend: P < 0.001; quadratic trend: P = 0.004), but not for systolic or diastolic blood pressure. Discussion

In this prospective randomized double-blind dose-finding study, we determined and compared the ED90 of esketamine required to suppress LMA insertion responses during propofol induction with or without intravenous lidocaine pretreatment. The primary finding was that the estimated ED90 of esketamine was lower in patients receiving lidocaine pretreatment than in those receiving normal saline. Specifically, lidocaine pretreatment reduced the esketamine dose required to achieve a 90% probability of successful suppression of LMA insertion responses. The absolute reduction in ED90 was 0.056 mg·kg−1, corresponding to an approximately 24.5% relative decrease. As secondary findings, lidocaine pretreatment was associated with a lower heart rate immediately before LMA insertion and a lower incidence of tachycardia. Taken together, these findings suggest that intravenous lidocaine exerted an esketamine-sparing effect under a fixed propofol background and may have attenuated the sympathetic response associated with airway manipulation.

This result is pharmacologically plausible. Successful LMA insertion without neuromuscular blockade depends on a balanced interaction among hypnosis, antinociception, and suppression of upper airway reflexes.1–5 Propofol provides the main hypnotic component, but when used alone it may require dose escalation to achieve adequate tolerance of airway instrumentation, with a corresponding increase in cardiorespiratory depression.3–5 Esketamine may partly offset this limitation by providing analgesic and antinociceptive support while better preserving circulatory stability than opioid-heavy or propofol-only strategies.6,7,9–15 Intravenous lidocaine, in turn, has been shown to improve LMA insertion conditions during propofol induction and to reduce peri-procedural hypnotic requirements in selected settings.5,8,18,19 The lower esketamine ED90 observed in the lidocaine group is therefore consistent with an adjunctive interaction in which lidocaine reduces the afferent airway stimulation and reflex reactivity that must otherwise be overcome by deeper hypnosis or higher adjunct dosing.

Our findings are also concordant with the direction of prior literature, while addressing a different pharmacodynamic question. Baik et al demonstrated that intravenously administered lidocaine improved LMA insertion conditions during propofol target-controlled infusion, supporting the concept that lidocaine can favorably modify airway tolerance during supraglottic airway placement5 Recent studies in hysteroscopy and procedural anesthesia have similarly shown that esketamine-containing regimens can reduce propofol requirements, improve insertion or procedural conditions, and in some settings mitigate hypotension or desaturation.6,7,9–13 However, most of these studies were designed to estimate the propofol dose requirement under adjunct supplementation or to compare regimen performance, rather than to determine how much esketamine itself is needed once propofol dosing is fixed. In that respect, the present study extends the current literature by shifting the dose-finding target from the hypnotic component to the adjunctive component of induction. This distinction is not merely methodological. In clinical practice, once a propofol regimen is standardized for workflow or institutional preference, the more relevant question may become how to optimize the accompanying adjunct to achieve reliable airway conditions with minimal hemodynamic disturbance.

The hemodynamic findings merit careful interpretation. We observed a lower heart rate at T1 and a lower incidence of tachycardia in the lidocaine group, whereas between-group differences in blood pressure were limited. This pattern is physiologically reasonable. The transition from deepening sedation to airway insertion is characterized by competing influences: propofol tends to reduce blood pressure, while airway stimulation tends to increase sympathetic outflow and heart rate. Under such conditions, heart rate may be a more sensitive marker of residual procedural stimulation than blood pressure alone. The finding that lidocaine was associated predominantly with attenuation of the heart rate response, rather than broad improvement across all hemodynamic variables, suggests that its contribution may have been more closely related to blunting reflex stimulation than to fundamentally altering the circulatory depressant profile of the induction regimen. This interpretation is also consistent with pharmacodynamic modeling work showing that hemodynamic responses during anesthesia induction reflect the net interaction between drug effect and noxious stimulation, rather than the action of any single agent in isolation.20

The safety findings should also be interpreted conservatively. Peri-induction adverse events were infrequent in both groups, and lidocaine pretreatment was not associated with higher rates of hypotension, bradycardia, hypoxemia, coughing/gagging, rescue propofol requirement, or clinically apparent signs suggestive of local anesthetic systemic toxicity. These findings suggest that, within the tested dosing framework, reducing the esketamine requirement with lidocaine was not accompanied by an obvious increase in common short-term induction-related adverse events. However, the study was not powered primarily for safety comparisons, and absence of statistically significant differences should not be interpreted as proof of equivalent safety across all clinically relevant outcomes. This is especially important for intravenous lidocaine, whose perioperative use should always remain dose-conscious and context-specific.18

Within the limitations of this single-center dose-finding study, the findings may have practical clinical implications. First, the observed reduction in esketamine ED90 suggests that lidocaine pretreatment may permit the use of lower esketamine doses while preserving a high probability of successful suppression of LMA insertion responses. This is potentially relevant because higher esketamine doses, although often hemodynamically favorable, may be associated with delayed recovery or increased psychomimetic burden in some settings.14,15 Second, the reduction in tachycardia may be valuable in patients in whom excessive peri-induction sympathetic activation is undesirable, even if the broader adverse-event profile remains similar. Third, the concept of regimen-specific adjunct optimization may be particularly applicable to short ambulatory or minimally invasive procedures, where efficient induction, rapid airway control, and avoidance of exaggerated cardiovascular fluctuations are all important.

A methodological strength of this study lies in the use of a biased coin design sequential approach targeting ED90 rather than ED50. High-quantile dose estimates are often more relevant for airway management, where clinicians generally seek a high probability of success rather than the median effective dose. Recent anesthesia dose-finding studies have increasingly used biased coin sequential designs to estimate ED90 or EC90 in settings where failure carries meaningful procedural consequences.17,21–27 In our study, this design concentrated observations around the clinically relevant high-response region, while centered isotonic regression preserved monotonicity and improved estimation stability. The resulting framework was therefore well suited to quantifying the esketamine requirement under a standardized induction regimen.

At the same time, the reported ED90 values should be interpreted as regimen-specific rather than universally applicable. All patients underwent shoulder arthroscopy and received regional nerve block before induction. Although these blocks do not abolish the airway stimulation associated with LMA placement, they likely reduced overall nociceptive input and may have contributed to the generally stable insertion conditions observed in both groups. In addition, the propofol dose was fixed at 2 mg·kg−1, and the observed esketamine ED90 values are therefore conditional on that hypnotic background. Different results might reasonably be expected in the absence of regional analgesia or when using target-controlled infusion, ciprofol-based induction, remimazolam-based co-induction, opioid-based strategies, or different propofol dosing schemes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study in relatively healthy adults undergoing elective shoulder arthroscopy, which limits generalizability to elderly, obese, higher-risk, or more heterogeneous surgical populations. Second, because the sequential design was optimized for ED90 estimation, the study was not intended to characterize the full dose-response relationship across a broader range of probabilities. Third, our primary endpoint intentionally emphasized complete suppression of observable insertion responses rather than ease of LMA placement. This stringent endpoint strengthens internal consistency for dose-finding purposes but may not be identical to the minimum conditions that some clinicians would consider clinically acceptable Fourth, although adverse events were recorded prospectively, the sample size was not designed to detect modest differences in uncommon safety outcomes. Finally, recovery-related outcomes, such as emergence time, PACU length of stay, emergence quality, and patient-centered recovery measures, were not systematically evaluated in this dose-finding study. Thus, whether the esketamine-sparing effect of lidocaine pretreatment may translate into improved recovery profiles warrants further investigation. In addition, the present findings do not establish a mechanistic synergy between lidocaine and esketamine, but rather demonstrate a clinically observed dose-sparing association within a specific induction framework.

Conclusion

In summary, this randomized dose-finding study determined and compared the ED90 of esketamine required to suppress LMA insertion responses during propofol induction with or without intravenous lidocaine pretreatment. Intravenous lidocaine pretreatment was associated with a lower estimated ED90 of esketamine and a lower incidence of peri-induction tachycardia, while common short-term induction-related adverse events were infrequent. These findings suggest an esketamine-sparing effect of lidocaine within this specific induction framework; however, their generalizability and potential recovery-related implications require further evaluation in broader surgical populations.
