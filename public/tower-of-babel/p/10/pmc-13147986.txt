Topological hydrogels for 3D organic electrochemical transistors
Authors: Bochao Xie, Qin Xue, Wenjin Zhou, Yigang Yu, Guohua Xie, Bin Yang
Published: 2026

Main text

Hydrogels are promising candidates for soft electronics because they could replicate the ionic and mechanical characteristics of living tissues. However, their potential as volumetric electronic media has remained limited by the absence of materials capable of supporting thick three-dimensional (3D) modulation. Organic electrochemical transistors (OECTs) provide mixed ionic and electronic transport but are currently constrained to two-dimensional (2D) thin films due to incomplete ion penetration in thicker channels. A recent study introduced a hydrogel semiconductor that enables millimeter-scale volumetric modulation, offering a pathway toward fully 3D OECT architectures and biointegrated electronic systems.

Hydrogels as 3D ionic matrices

Hydrogels have become key materials for soft and biointegrated electronics because they combine high water content, mechanical softness, and continuous ionic transport.1 These characteristics allow hydrogels to match the physical properties of biological tissues and support hydrated ion conduction across 3D volumes. As hydrogel chemistry matures, these materials now function in soft sensors, ionic circuits, and tissue-interfacing platforms. Nevertheless, their integration into high-performance electronic systems has remained limited to micrometer-scale active regions. The lack of hydrogel materials capable of sustaining electronic modulation across macroscopic thicknesses has deferred the development of real 3D electronic structures entirely based on soft matter.

Dimensions matters in OECTs

OECTs provide a mature platform for achieving mixed ionic and electronic conduction in aqueous environments.2 By allowing ions to penetrate a redox-active polymer channel and modulate its conductivity, OECTs offer high transconductance and low-voltage operation suitable for sensing and neuromorphic computing. However, the same bulk modulation mechanism also imposes a fundamental constraint. Once the channel thickness exceeds several micrometers, ion penetration becomes incomplete, and volumetric capacitance ceases to scale proportionally with thickness. This leads to non-uniform doping and diminished electronic response. Consequently, OECT implementation remains limited to thin films, preventing their integration into the millimeter-scale deformable environments common in biological and soft robotic systems. Even efforts to improve mechanical compliance or introduce porosity in the polymer layer do not fundamentally resolve this limit, since ion transport through thick poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) (PEDOT) based networks in hydrogels remains inefficient and depth dependent.3 In addition, repeated electrochemical cycling often alters the electrolyte/semiconductor interface, producing drift and hysteresis that further restrict stable operation under biologically relevant conditions.4 At the core of this limitation is the mismatch between the characteristic diffusion length of hydrated ions and the length scale over which electronic percolation must be preserved. Conventional PEDOT-based architectures cannot synchronously support these two transport processes across macroscopic distances. Looking ahead, millimeter-scale channels shift the performance bottleneck from peak transconductance to operational robustness. Key figures of merit should include bias stability and drift in physiological electrolytes, hysteresis under repeated gating, and thickness-dependent switching kinetics, together with clear reporting protocols for long-term cycling and accelerated aging.

Porous hydrogels that achieve full volumetric modulation

A recent study resolves this constraint by designing a hydrogel semiconductor capable of uniform volumetric modulation across nearly 1 mm of thickness.5 The authors identified that achieving 3D modulation requires simultaneous continuity in both electron-conducting and ion-permeable pathways (Figure 1). To accomplish this, they employed a double-network hydrogel architecture in which a secondary supporting network guided the assembly of the primary conducting network based on poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene) polystyrene sulfonate (PEDOT:PSS). Phase engineering generates continuous PEDOT-rich domains that sustain long-range electronic transport, while structural engineering introduced controlled microporosity that enabled efficient ion penetration. Through coordinated tuning of polymer concentration, cross-linking density, and pore structure, the hydrogel maintained linear thickness capacitance scaling across several orders of magnitude. This ability restores the fundamental requirement for bulk modulation that conventional OECT films lose above approximately 10 μm. As a result, the hydrogel semiconductor displays strong on and off contrast, high volumetric capacitance, and mobility capacitance products that exceed those of 2D thin-film-based OECTs. Its mechanical resilience also allows fabrication into free-standing fibers and interconnected networks that mimic aspects of neural topology. A key next step is to quantify how fast such thick channels can be gated while maintaining uniform volumetric modulation. Toward 3D OECTs and biosystems

These results provide a practical pathway toward the realization of fully 3D OECTs. Future hydrogel semiconductors may incorporate anisotropic porosity, spatial gradients in redox activity, or localized conductive domains to create directional transport and programmable electronic responses within bulk materials. When coupled with fiber spinning, volumetric printing, or scaffold-guided assembly, such materials could enable circuits that process signals through their entire physical volume rather than across a planar interface. This capability aligns with the requirements of organoid interfaces, volumetric neural models, and soft robotic systems where electrical, mechanical, and biochemical functions coexist in a 3D form. The demonstration of millimeter-scale modulation therefore establishes a materials foundation for biointegrated electronic systems that operate within the 3D architecture of living tissues rather than on their surface. For integration, the challenge is no longer only material design but also system architecture. Scalable 3D addressing, low resistance interconnects, and multiplexed electrolyte delivery must be engineered to preserve volumetric gating while suppressing parasitic leakage. Packaging strategies that stabilize hydration but retain ionic access, together with manufacturable routes such as printing or scaffold-guided assembly, could enable high-density 3D arrays for organoids and soft robotic control.

Conclusion

The development of a hydrogel semiconductor capable of millimeter-scale volumetric modulation represents a decisive advance for soft electronics and human-machine interfaces. By enabling simultaneous long-range electronic transport and unrestricted ion penetration, the material overcomes the intrinsic thickness constraint that has limited OECTs to 2D films. This achievement opens huge opportunities for creating 3D circuits, neuromorphic systems, and biohybrid interfaces entirely within soft and hydrated matter. As fabrication strategies and material designs continue to evolve, hydrogel-based OECTs may become foundational components in future volumetric electronic and bioelectronic technologies toward living systems and novel medical applications. A decisive next milestone will be the demonstration of addressable 3D device arrays with reproducible fabrication, month-scale operational stability, and bandwidth benchmarks that explicitly account for volumetric ion transport, moving 3D OECTs from a material advance to an integrated bioelectronic platform.
