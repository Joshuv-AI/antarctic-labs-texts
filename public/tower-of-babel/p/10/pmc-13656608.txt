Spinal muscular atrophy type 2 with severe kyphoscoliosis: a case report
Authors: Weifan Ren, Bo Jin, Yungen Hu, Lei Han, Renfu Quan, Zhenfei Xiong
Published: 2026

Background

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is an autosomal recessive neurodegenerative disorder caused by homozygous deletion or mutation of the survival motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene on chromosome 5q13 [1]. The incidence is approximately 1 in 6,000–10,000 live births. A highly homologous gene, SMN2, produces only 10–15% of functional SMN protein due to alternative splicing that excludes exon 7. The number of SMN2 copies is inversely correlated with disease severity [2].

SMA is classified into five types (0–4) based on age of onset and maximal motor milestone [2]. Type 2 SMA typically presents between 6 and 18 months of age; patients can sit independently but never walk. Progressive scoliosis occurs in up to 90–100% of non-ambulant SMA patients [3]. Spinal deformity impairs sitting balance and exacerbates restrictive lung disease, leading to reduced quality of life [4].

We report a late-diagnosed SMA type 2 teenager from rural China. He underwent successful scoliosis surgery through a charity programme. This case shows that posterior spinal fusion can still greatly improve quality of life when disease‑modifying therapies are not available.

Case presentation

Patient information

The patient is a 16-year-old Chinese boy from rural Sichuan. He was admitted to our department in July 2022 with a 14-year history of progressive lower limb weakness and a 7-year history of progressive spinal deformity. His parents reported that he had been unable to walk since the age of 2 years and had used a wheelchair for mobility. Over time, his spinal curvature worsened, and he could no longer maintain an upright sitting posture even in his wheelchair, often leaning to one side with his neck tilted (Fig. 1). His quality of life was severely impaired. He had no history of respiratory symptoms, feeding difficulties, or cognitive impairment. There was no family history of neuromuscular disorders.

Clinical and radiographic findings of the patient. Preoperative appearance showing severe kyphoscoliosis with the patient unable to sit upright

The patient lives with his parents. His mother is his primary caregiver and is not employed; his father works as a labourer, and the family has limited financial resources. He had been unable to attend school regularly because of his physical limitations and often felt isolated and depressed. His parents noted that he rarely socialised with peers and frequently expressed frustration about his appearance and inability to sit properly. Before this admission, he had never received any disease‑modifying therapy (nusinersen, onasemnogene abeparvovec, or risdiplam) nor had he undergone any surgical or orthotic treatment for his spine.

Physical examination

On admission, the patient was alert and cooperative. Muscle strength was graded as 3/5 in the proximal upper limbs, 4/5 distally; lower limb proximal strength was 2/5 and distal 3/5. Generalised hypotonia and areflexia were present. Tongue fasciculations were absent. A severe thoracolumbar kyphoscoliosis was evident, with a positive Adam forward bend test.

Imaging

Preoperative supine whole-spine radiographs showed a thoracolumbar kyphoscoliosis with a Cobb angle of 83° (Fig. 2). Supine left and right lateral bending radiographs were performed to assess spinal flexibility, which was calculated as 42% (Cobb angle improved to 48° under maximum passive bending). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed spinal curvature, rotation, and kyphosis, but the spinal cord was of normal caliber without compression or signal alteration. Pulmonary function testing demonstrated restrictive ventilatory dysfunction with small airway impairment.

Genetic testing

Peripheral blood was analysed using multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) and ARMS‑PCR. The results showed homozygous deletion of SMN1 exon 7, three copies of SMN2, and one copy of the NAIP gene. No point mutations or large fragment duplications were detected (Fig. 3). These findings confirmed the diagnosis of SMA type 2.

Diagnosis and differential diagnosis

Despite symptom onset at age 2 years, the patient was not diagnosed with SMA until age 16. Several factors contributed to this delay: limited access to genetic testing in rural China, initial misdiagnosis as cerebral palsy or unspecified myopathy at local hospitals, absence of newborn screening for SMA in the region, and lack of prior evaluation at a specialised neuromuscular centre. Other conditions considered in the differential diagnosis included cerebral palsy (ruled out by normal cognitive development and absence of upper motor neuron signs), congenital myopathy (ruled out by genetic testing and lack of specific histological features), and Duchenne muscular dystrophy (ruled out by normal creatine kinase and X‑linked inheritance pattern). The final diagnosis of SMA type 2 was established based on the combination of clinical phenotype and genetic findings.

Treatment

After multidisciplinary discussion, the patient underwent posterior spinal fusion on July 30, 2022. Under general anaesthesia in the prone position, a posterior midline incision from T1 to S2 was made. Pedicle screws were inserted from T1 to S1 under navigation guidance, and S2 alar–iliac screws (S2AIS) were used for distal fixation at the S2 segment. No spinal osteotomy was performed. Correction was achieved by concave distraction and convex compression. Intraoperative fluoroscopy confirmed good screw position and satisfactory correction. Somatosensory evoked potentials remained normal throughout. Estimated blood loss was 650 mL, and 500 mL of autologous blood was reinfused.

For bone graft fusion, thorough decortication of the lamina and facet joints was performed first, followed by interlaminar and interfacet bone grafting with artificial bone materials. Bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) was applied simultaneously to enhance bone fusion and reduce the risk of pseudarthrosis.

The patient recovered well-postoperatively.

Outcome and follow-up

One week postoperatively, supine radiography showed that the Cobb angle had decreased from 83° to 42° (Fig. 4). At 1 month, the patient was able to sit upright independently in his wheelchair (Fig. 5). By 1 year, radiography confirmed that the correction was maintained, with no implant failure or surgical site infection. Pulmonary function tests were not repeated after surgery, but the patient reported no worsening of shortness of breath. From the patient’s perspective, he noted that he could now sit upright for several hours, attend school, and study without discomfort; he felt less tired and more confident, and his family observed that he was happier and more willing to interact with others. The patient adhered well to postoperative rehabilitation, including respiratory physiotherapy and gradual mobilisation, and tolerated the surgery without any major complications. No adverse or unanticipated events occurred during the follow-up period.

Discussion

This case illustrates the typical clinical and genetic features of SMA type 2 and the successful surgical management of severe kyphoscoliosis.

Genetic basis and genotype‑phenotype correlation

Approximately 95% of SMA patients have homozygous deletion of SMN1 exon 7 [1]. Disease severity is primarily determined by SMN2 copy number: patients with 2 copies usually have type 1, 3 copies type 2, and 3–4 copies type 3 [2]. Our patient had three SMN2 copies, consistent with type 2 phenotype. The NAIP gene is located in the same 5q13 region; its deletion is more frequent in severe type 1 (50%) than in type 2 (6.5%) [1]. Our patient had one NAIP copy, which may have contributed to severity but did not alter the overall classification.

Natural history of scoliosis in SMA

Scoliosis in SMA type 2 is almost universal. Wijngaarde et al. reported a progression rate of 7.2° per year, accelerating to 10.2° per year before surgery [3]. The main curve is typically a long C-shaped thoracolumbar curve. Respiratory function is compromised by both intercostal muscle weakness and the restrictive effect of spinal deformity [4]. In our patient, the Cobb angle reached 83° by age 16, highlighting the progressive nature if left untreated.

Surgical management

For skeletally mature patients with curves exceeding 50°, posterior spinal fusion (PSF) is the standard treatment. Holt et al. reported correction rates of 50–60% and improved sitting balance [5]. Our patient achieved a correction of 49.4%, comparable to published results [5, 6]. For younger patients, growth-friendly techniques (growing rods, magnetically controlled growing rods, or VEPTR) are preferred to preserve spinal growth [6]. Our patient was 16 years with near-skeletal maturity, so definitive fusion was appropriate.

In this case, S2 alar–iliac screws (S2AIS) were used for distal fixation, which provides superior biomechanical stability compared to standard S2 pedicle screws, especially in patients with poor bone quality [7]. No spinal osteotomy was required, as satisfactory correction was achieved through standard distraction and compression techniques, consistent with the preoperative flexibility assessment of 42%.

For those receiving intrathecal nusinersen, surgical planning should consider preserving access for lumbar puncture, e.g., using “skip” constructs [8]. Our patient did not receive nusinersen, so this was not a concern.

Impact of disease-modifying therapies

Three approved drugs (nusinersen, onasemnogene abeparvovec, risdiplam) have revolutionised SMA care [9–11]. Pre-symptomatic treatment can prevent scoliosis entirely, while post-symptomatic treatment slows progression. Our patient was diagnosed late, after irreversible deformity had developed, emphasising the urgent need for newborn screening programmes.

Multidisciplinary care

Optimal management of SMA requires a team, including neurologists, orthopaedic surgeons, pulmonologists, nutritionists, and anaesthesiologists. Preoperative pulmonary function testing and nutritional assessment are essential. Our patient had restrictive ventilatory dysfunction, and careful perioperative respiratory management contributed to an uneventful recovery.

Strengths and limitations

This report has a few strengths. We provide a detailed description of the clinical presentation, genetic findings, imaging, and surgical technique in a late-diagnosed SMA type 2 patient from rural China. Most published SMA surgical series come from well-resourced centres; our case adds experience from a region, where newborn screening and disease-modifying therapies are not yet standard. Comprehensive coronal and sagittal radiographic parameters were measured to objectively evaluate the surgical outcomes.

There are several important limitations to this study. First, this is a single case report from a single centre, so the findings may not be generalizable to all SMA type 2 patients or patients with different disease severities. Second, the follow-up period is only 1 year, which is insufficient to evaluate the long-term outcomes, including the risk of late implant failure, pseudarthrosis, proximal junctional kyphosis, or progressive decline in respiratory function. Third, the patient did not receive any disease-modifying therapies before or after surgery, so we cannot assess how these therapies might affect the natural history of scoliosis or the surgical outcomes in SMA patients. Fourth, we did not use standardised, validated quality-of-life questionnaires to objectively measure the improvement in the patient's quality of life; the reported outcomes are based on subjective patient and caregiver reports. Fifth, postoperative pulmonary function tests were not repeated, so we cannot quantitatively evaluate the impact of spinal fusion on respiratory function. Sixth, no long-term bone fusion rate data are available, as we did not perform CT scans to confirm solid fusion at the final follow-up. Seventh, this study lacks a control group, so we cannot compare the outcomes of posterior spinal fusion with other treatment modalities or natural history. Still, we believe the case offers a practical lesson for clinicians treating similar patients in settings with limited access to advanced drug therapies.

Conclusions

In this single case of a skeletally mature SMA type 2 patient with severe kyphoscoliosis, posterior spinal fusion with S2AIS fixation achieved satisfactory deformity correction and significantly improved the patient's sitting balance and quality of life. Early genetic diagnosis through newborn screening and prompt multidisciplinary management are essential to prevent irreversible spinal deformities in SMA patients. Further large-scale, multicentre studies are needed to confirm the long-term efficacy and safety of this surgical approach in SMA patients.
