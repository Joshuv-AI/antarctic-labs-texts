Impact of patient suicide on psychiatry residents: protocol of a qualitative study
Authors: Peri Fenwick, Emmanuel Tse, Gina Nicoll, Deanna Chaukos, Zainab Furqan, Andrew Lustig, Mark Sinyor, Sophie Soklaridis, Vicky Stergiopoulos, Juveria Zaheer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPsychiatrists’ first exposure to patient suicide often occurs during residency training. Previous research shows that experiencing a patient’s death by suicide during residency can have significant impacts on trainees’ well-being, self-esteem and approach to practice. However, existing research on this topic is mostly limited to survey-based data, which does not facilitate nuanced exploration. This study will use a qualitative approach to gain an in-depth understanding of Canadian psychiatry residents’ experiences of a patient’s death by suicide and the types of supports that may help trainees to process this loss and integrate this experience into their professional identity formation.Methods and analysisThis study will conduct 15–25 semistructured qualitative interviews with psychiatry resident physicians across Canada to explore their experiences of patient loss by suicide during training. Interview data will be transcribed verbatim and analysed using the principles of Constructivist Grounded Theory.Ethics and disseminationThe study findings will be reported and accessible to residency training programmes, the academic community, the media and the public.This study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health (Protocol Identifying Number 2024/125).

Introduction

It is estimated that up to 69% of psychiatry residents experience the death of a patient by suicide during residency training.1 This adverse event has been associated with emotional responses including feelings of shame, guilt and diminished self-efficacy.1 2 The levels of distress after experiencing a patient’s death by suicide during training can resemble symptoms of post-traumatic stress disorder.3 There can also be long-term impacts and modifications to clinical practice, including anxiety about discharging patients and increased concerns about medicolegal issues.4 Psychiatry trainees may experience particularly high levels of work-related stress compared with other healthcare providers due to the nature and intensity of training, lack of experience and variable availability of supports.1 5 Coupled with the impacts of losing a patient to suicide, there is an elevated risk of burnout and mental health difficulties among trainees.6 These vulnerabilities highlight the importance of supporting residents who have experienced a patient’s death by suicide to help them process this loss and build resilience.

Prior research by our team has examined psychiatrists’ experiences of patient suicide, including during residency training.7 8 Psychiatrists described receiving practical supports from supervisors after the event, such as offers of a modified workload or time off to process the experience. However, psychiatrists identified a need for additional support to emotionally process a patient’s death and to integrate the experience into their developing professional identity.7

Research examining current psychiatry residents’ experiences of patient suicide is limited. A systematic review from 2019 on encountering patient suicide during psychiatric residency found that existing qualitative studies are primarily case reports of heterogenous quality.1 Findings highlighted the need for further research to better understand the impact of patient suicide on psychiatry trainees and how to best support them. This qualitative study aims to fill this gap by exploring Canadian psychiatry residents’ experiences of a patient’s death by suicide, including lessons learnt and impacts on practice. In addition, this study aims to identify the types of supports that help psychiatry trainees in coping with a patient’s death by suicide as well as potential areas for improvement at the supervisor, program and institutional level.

Methods and analysis

Study design

We describe a qualitative research study consisting of semistructured interviews of psychiatry residents who have a history of providing direct clinical care to a patient who died by suicide. Qualitative interviewing allows for an in-depth exploration of an aspect of life in which the interviewee has substantial experience, eliciting their subjective perspective.8 Participant recruitment began in October 2025, and the study is currently ongoing. We anticipate that participant recruitment and qualitative analysis will conclude by October 2026.

Qualitative methodology

This study is informed by the principles of constructivist grounded theory (CGT), a qualitative research methodology that emphasises the generation of theory grounded in empirical data.9 CGT is a contemporary revision of grounded theory, which acknowledges multiple subjective realities, including those of both the researcher and the participants. It encourages reflexivity, allowing researchers to engage with existing literature and pose guiding research questions while remaining open to emergent insights.9 CGT supports the exploration of social and psychological processes through iterative data collection and analysis, culminating in an interpretive theoretical framework.9 In this context, qualitative interviews serve to elicit rich, experiential accounts of participants’ lives and perspectives.9 CGT was chosen as the qualitative methodology for this study because it moves past description to generate an explanatory model of how psychiatry residents experience and adapt to patient suicide loss. By emphasising participants’ meaning-making processes, this approach yields conceptual insights that will guide the design of educational modules and targeted supports for trainees who may encounter a patient’s death by suicide.

Identifying potential participants

The sample will be composed of psychiatry trainees enrolled in a Canadian residency programme or within 6 months of graduating. We plan to recruit 15–25 resident physicians across different ages, gender identities, years of training and training programmes.

Sample sizes of 10–40 participants are the established standard for health services research as they provide sufficient information to develop reliable qualitative models.1015 A literature review of qualitative studies involving physicians examining service delivery, professional identity or practice experiences uses sample sizes between 10 and 30 participants.16 Sample size is not prescriptive according to the principles of CGT.17 Rather, recruitment will continue until theoretical saturation is reached, with data collection and analysis occurring in an iterative manner to promote high quality analysis.17

Participant recruitment

Recruitment for the study will take place through distribution of emails and posts using the authors’ existing networks including listservs and social media platforms. We will contact resident organisations such as Resident Doctors of Canada and each of the provincial residential bodies. In addition, we will reach out to programme directors, chief residents of residency programmes and postgraduate medical education offices in Canada, with an invitation to forward the recruitment email to their resident body. We will also advertise on Facebook groups specifically catered to psychiatry residents. The post will stay active for 3 months, with re-posting every thirty days to ensure visibility. We plan to have diverse participants from various programmes with respect to demographic characteristics such as age, gender and location of medical school training.

After the recruitment of the first five participants, we will also use a snowball sampling strategy for this study alongside the general recruitment strategy as described above. Snowball sampling is used commonly in qualitative research in healthcare and refers to primary data sources nominating other potential primary data sources to be interviewed (chain referral process).18 Snowball sampling allows the investigators to take advantage of their professional networks while also recruiting participants with whom they may not have regular contact and can accelerate the recruitment process.19 Exponential discriminative snowball sampling refers to recruiting a limited number of new subjects from multiple referrals based on the study’s aim.20 Because this study focuses on psychiatry residents across Canada, exponential discriminative snowball sampling allows us to move beyond the University of Toronto to include residents training in diverse programmes.

The end of the recruitment process will be triggered by saturation (ie, when no new data appear after iterative sampling and analysis).

Data collection

A study team member will schedule a convenient time for the participant to complete a short demographic survey (online supplemental appendix 1) and a semistructured qualitative interview. Interviewers and participants will require a private space for remote interviews via WebEx videoconferencing system which uses strongly encrypted channels to protect privacy and confidentiality. Only audio files will be recorded and shared with a contracted professional transcription service.

The survey will capture demographic information including age, gender, ethnicity, previous occupation/educational training, medical school location, location of training programme, year of residency, areas of interest within psychiatry (eg, education, research, clinical, child psychiatry, geriatric psychiatry), and clinical setting they plan to practise in (eg, hospital-based, private practice).

Qualitative interviews will take place over 45–60 min, either in person or virtually. A trained qualitative interviewer will use a semistructured interview guide (online supplemental appendix 2), which will ask open-ended questions about the resident’s experiences of patient suicide, emotional/behavioural reactions to the event, types of interventions provided by peers, supervisors and training programmes, as well as impacts on their practice.

Qualitative interview guides

The interviewer will use a semistructured qualitative interview guide, composed of topics and open-ended questions to be covered during the one-on-one conversation. The initial guide closely follows the research questions; however, the guides may be revised throughout the data collection process in light of emergent themes. This process of adding questions as the study progresses is consistent with the qualitative research tradition.17 Interviewing is flexible and allows for narrative space, and interviewers can pursue important ideas and emotions during the interview, prompting the participants to provide specific examples of the types of interactions and emotions they have experienced, specifically by asking ‘what’ and ‘how’ questions.17

Semistructured interviewing has several benefits.21 It provides a clear set of instructions for interviewers and can provide reliable, comparative qualitative data, but is flexible enough to allow the researchers to develop an understanding of the topics of interest in order to continually revise the semistructured interview.21 The inclusion of open-ended questions and the ability of interviewers to follow relevant topics that may stray from the interview guide provide the opportunity for understanding the topic and allow the respondents the freedom to introduce their own meanings, views and ideas to the interview.21

The interview will begin with several open-ended questions, which will be asked slowly and clearly, to foster reflection but not to interrogate. The interviewer will take a ‘life history’ approach, allowing the respondent the opportunity and space to discuss their lives, experiences, training and work to build rapport, obtain rich narrative data and to situate the experience of losing a patient to suicide in context. The questions will be broad enough to cover a wide range of experiences related to the circumstances that led up to the participant’s experience of losing a patient(s) to suicide, their understanding and recollections of the patient and aftermath, the supervisory experience/supports received, and their views on its impact on their training and professional development. The interview is designed to place an emphasis on learning the participant’s meanings and stresses the participant’s actions and individual experiences. The interview is meant to be informal and conversational, and the interviewer will conclude with positive and supportive questions in order to transition the participant out of the interview.

As the participant answers the initial framing questions, the interviewer will be cognisant of several ‘sensitising concepts’ related to resident physicians’ experiences of losing a patient to suicide. The interviewer will be careful not to introduce these concepts in order to ensure that the participants themselves are describing the phenomena and to strengthen the data for analysis. Sensitising concepts may not apply to each interviewee—these concepts are derived from the existing literature in this field and will be revised iteratively during the study process.

Data analysis

The interviewer will record the interviews and an experienced and a confidential transcriptionist will transcribe the recordings into a secure Word document. The research team will verify the accuracy of the transcripts and enter them into NVivo-14, a qualitative data management software. They will input participant demographic information into an MS Excel spreadsheet.

Data collection and analysis will follow the principles of CGT, as outlined by Charmaz.17 Key strategies include simultaneous data collection and analysis, theoretical sampling, constant comparative methods and inductive development of conceptual categories. The coders, who have experience in qualitative analysis, will meet every 2 weeks to work on data analysis, ensure consistency in coding, compare analytic memos and collaboratively build the emerging theoretical framework.

Several strategies will also be employed to ensure the trustworthiness of data collection and analysis, including triangulation, member checking, continuous checking, reflexivity, negative case analysis and random auditing.16

Patient and public involvement

There are no patients directly involved in this research; however, the participants interviewed in this qualitative study will describe their experiences of providing clinical care to patients who died by suicide. The study design will emphasise the importance of patient-provider confidentiality when describing sensitive clinical experiences, especially as they pertain to the topic of suicide. The research questions have been reviewed by a team of physicians and researchers who have been involved since the conception of the study and have a wealth of experience in qualitative analysis, suicidology and medical education. The design and protocol of this study include collaboration with suicidology researchers with lived experience of mental illness and suicidality to ensure that the research methods and sensitising topics are patient-centred. Participants will be informed during the informed consent process about the study methodology and plans for knowledge translation and dissemination.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Research Ethics Board of the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health, Protocol Identifying Number 2024/125.

We recognise that qualitative interviews may be distressing and anxiety-provoking to participants considering the personal nature of these questions, specifically as the subject matter is suicide. Several steps will attempt to minimise this risk. The qualitative interview guide is semi-structured; the questions that will be asked will stem from the respondents’ answers. Respondents will be reminded that they do not have to answer every question asked and if they are experiencing distress, they are able to check in with the interviewer.

Since qualitative research aims to achieve an in-depth understanding of an issue, including an exploration of the reasons and context for participants’ beliefs and actions, participants have experienced anxiety and distress during the interview process.22 Furthermore, the questions that lead to anxiety and distress cannot always be predicted, as they depend on the personal experiences of the respondents. However, these risks can be managed by ensuring the researcher has adequate levels of expertise and supervision, as well as debrief sessions offered. In our study, we will ensure that the researchers carrying out the interviewing are trained in qualitative interviewing, have worked with resident physicians in the past and have the expertise to identify levels of distress.

Resources will be available for participants including information on crisis services offered by the Canadian Medical Association and university-specific resident wellness offices.

Dissemination

Findings from this study will be shared through both oral and written formats, including presentations at conferences focused on psychiatry, suicide and medical education. A comprehensive report and academic publications will be prepared to disseminate results to the scientific community, residency training programmes and the broader public.

The results of this study will inform the development and piloting of educational training modules for psychiatry residents, focused on navigating the experience of losing a patient to suicide during residency. These modules will include evidence-informed content on common emotional and behavioural responses to patient suicide, as well as guidance on accessing appropriate supports. In parallel, we will explore the development of a structured postvention framework to further support residents affected by such losses. Ultimately, we will disseminate this teaching nationwide and contribute to developing a standardised curriculum for psychiatry residents on the topic of patient suicide loss during training.

Impact

Psychiatry residents play a vital role in the provision of mental healthcare. Given the demanding nature of their training, including high work demands (eg, overnight call shifts) and the emotional toll of repeated exposure to patient distress and suicidality—it is essential that they are adequately supported to foster resilience and prevent burnout. This project addresses a critical gap in understanding psychiatry residents’ experiences following a patient’s death by suicide and aims to clarify the types of supports they find most helpful in coping with such a loss. This is an important area of study, as such events can have profound and lasting effects on residents’ emotional well-being, professional identity and career trajectories.2 3 They may also influence clinical decision-making, potentially contributing to avoidance of high-acuity patients, defensive practice or diminished confidence in managing risk.4 Despite these impacts, limited guidance exists within postgraduate training on how to prepare for and process a patient suicide.7

The findings from this study will inform the development and implementation of an educational curriculum aimed at enhancing support for psychiatry residents, training programmes and clinical supervisors. By providing evidence-based teaching on managing the impact of patient suicide, this curriculum will equip trainees with the knowledge and skills needed to navigate these complex experiences. Ultimately, this initiative seeks to promote psychological safety and strengthen residents’ capacity to deliver compassionate, nuanced care—particularly to patients experiencing suicidal ideation and behaviours.
