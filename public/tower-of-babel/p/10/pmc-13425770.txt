Evaluation of intensive care nurses’ attitudes toward the importance of care-focused nurse–patient interaction and associated factors
Authors: Hatice Dilek Doğan, Seda Tuğba Baykara Mat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIntensive care units, where critically ill patients are treated, are dynamic units equipped with high technology. The quality of intensive care is greatly dependent on the effectiveness and quality of nursing care. The level of ongoing verbal and behavioral interaction between the nurse and the patient affects the quality of care of an intensive care unit.AimThis study aimed to evaluate intensive care nurses’ self-reported perceptions regarding the importance of care-focused nurse–patient interaction during the pandemic period, along with associated factors.Research design and methodsThe research group included nurses working in the intensive care units of a university hospital on the European side of Istanbul and the intensive care units of a state hospital located in the same region, under ov-19 pandemic conditions. The study data were collected using the “Nurse Information Form” and the importance dimension and 10 subscales of the “Caring Nurse-Patient Interaction” (CNPI) Scale.Ethical considerationsResearch ethics approval was obtained, and the participants provided informed consent.ResultsIt was found that the nurses’ average age was 31.25 ± 6.97 and that their mean length of service in the intensive care unit was 4.87 ± 3.58 years. It was determined that 80.4% of the participants chose to work in intensive care of their own accord, and that 44.1% were able to establish team collaboration in intensive care. During COVID-19 pandemic 69.6% nurses worked 40 h or more per week. In the study, the total score for the importance dimension of the Caring Nurse-Patient Interaction Scale was found to be 274.87 ± 44.98. It was determined that not choosing to work in intensive care decreased the total importance score 29.8-point and that failure to establish team collaboration decreased the same total score 31-point.ConclusionsIn this study conducted during the pandemic, intensive care nurses reported positive perceptions regarding the importance of care-focused nurse–patient interaction and evaluated nurse–patient interaction as an important factor in intensive care. It was concluded that, in order to strengthen care-focused nurse–patient interaction, the choice to work in intensive care should be left to nurses, duties, working hours, and authority should be clarified in accordance with ethical principles, and team collaboration should be supported under all circumstances.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Intensive care units (ICUs) are multidisciplinary critical care units in which modern technological devices are available and serious medical interventions are carried out on individuals whose vital functions are threatened. In these units, where numerous nursing practices are performed, verbal and behavioural interaction with the patient occurs continuously. In addition to physiological care, holistic interaction occurs in intensive care units by addressing patients’ psychological, social, cultural, and spiritual needs. In fact, nursing interventions are the sum of all care practices carried out in continuous interaction with patients and occurring along the science-art axis. The nurse is the person who initiates, manages and is responsible for interaction with the patient [1, 2]. Through their caring practices, nurses both affect the patient and are affected by the patient. For this reason, the concept of care is defined by many theorists as the process of interpersonal interaction, communication between nurse and patient, and therapeutic intervention [2, 3].

Watson defined nursing care as a scientific, ethical, aesthetic and professionally individualised interpersonal process that occurs through the mutual interaction of two individuals by physical, mental, spiritual and sociocultural means [4]. In fact, in the intensive care process, the patient’s relatives are as much a part of this interaction as the patient is. The focus of interaction between the nurse and the patient/patient’s relatives is to determine and meet the needs of the healthy/sick individual. The interaction with the patient/patient’s relatives during the COVID-19 period is the best example of this situation. Therefore, in addition to a sound knowledge of nursing science, intensive care nursing requires positive interaction skills, positive human relations, and high motivation. These qualities increase the power of nurse-patient interaction and also affect the speed of recovery and patient satisfaction [5]. Positive nurse-patient interaction significantly impacts the quality of the ICU [6, 7]. In fact, in studies conducted have revealed the positive relationship between nurse-patient interaction and the disease process, patient satisfaction, clinical experience and quality of care [8, 9].

Nurses play a key role in monitoring various situations in ICUs, such as early detection of complications, appropriate management, and continuing care in line with ethical principles. The success of care-focused interaction depends on the extent to which nurses can demonstrate positive attitudes and behaviours. Achieving the desired level of care-focused nurse-patient interaction is possible by recognising the barriers to interaction and removing these barriers under all circumstances. In order to explain the factors that play a role in care-focused nurse-patient interaction, it is important to identify the problem, manage the process, and generate solutions [5–9]. To increase the effectiveness of care, it is necessary to examine the effects of professional background, education level, and economic and physical conditions on the care process. In a previous study, it was found that nurses’ professional experiences impact their caring work and their communication with patients. Moreover, there is a great need in ICUs intensive care units to recognise a number of other factors that are thought to play a role in nurse-patient interaction [10].

This study evaluated intensive care nurses’ attitudes and perceptions regarding the importance of care-focused nurse–patient interaction during the pandemic, using the importance dimension and its subscales of the Care-Focused Nurse–Patient Interaction Scale. Additionally, it was examined the factors associated with care-focused nurse–patient interaction in this context.Perhaps the need for a care-focused approach has never been important and greater during the pandemic, as no one knew exactly what the individual response to COVID-19 would be. The primary contribution of this study is to determine how nurses sustain their perceptions regarding the importance of care-focused nurse–patient interaction during periods characterized by uncertainty and crisis and to identify the conditions associated with these perceptions. The findings of this study provide guidance for the development of new strategies aimed at identifying and overcoming barriers to care-centered nurse–patient interaction.

Background

Studies have been conducted on care-focused nurse-patient interaction among both student nurses and working nurses In the study by Tan and Polat, it was found that nursing students’ attitudes and behaviours towards nurse-patient interactions were positive, and that students who were female, had no communication problems with patients, and had sufficient social activity status possessed more positive attitudes and behaviours [11]. In a similar study conducted with nursing students, it was found that those who were in their final year, chose the nursing profession because they loved it, thought that care was the primary duty of the nurse, and did not have difficulty in patient communication had positive attitudes and behaviours towards care-focused nurse-patient interaction [12]. In their study, Ailen et al. reported that good nurse-patient interaction reduced the length of hospital stay, improved recovery outcomes, and increased patient satisfaction [13].

It has been determined that, studies in the literature have mostly been conducted among student nurses to determine negative or positive attitudes in nurse-patient interaction, and that the factors affecting attitude are limited to the student period. However, different studies have not been found in the literature on the status of nurse-patient interaction in ICUs and under pandemic conditions and the factors affecting this. In a similar study conducted during the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic to describe the characteristics and quality of care interactions between nurses and patients in acute and home care settings, nurses’ perceptions of supporting individuality and their scores for providing individualized care were found to be high [14]. It is thought that in nurse-patient interaction, the nurse’s attitude will be affected by a wide variety of factors such as interpersonal relationship skills, ethical approach, holistic perspective, physical conditions, time, working hours, team collaboration and professional perception [15]. In this study aimed to examine the extent to which care-focused nurse-patient interaction occurred in ICUs during the pandemic, whether nurses regarded this interaction as important under difficult conditions, and what factors affected nurse-patient interaction during the pandemic.

Aim

This study aimed to evaluate intensive care nurses’ attitudes and perceptions regarding the importance of care-focused nurse–patient interaction during the pandemic and to determine the factors associated with these perceptions. The study focuses on identifying the structural and individual determinants that shape this interaction under conditions of clinical uncertainty.

Methodological aspects of the study

This study, aiming to evaluate intensive care nurses’ attitudes and behaviors regarding care-oriented nurse-patient interaction in relation to associated factors, was conducted as a cross-sectional research focusing on relational aspects.

Sample and recruitment of participants

The study population consisted of nurses employed during the pandemic in various intensive care units (emergency, adult, and neonatal ICUs) at Beykent University Hospital and Beylikdüzü State Hospital between December 15, 2021, and April 30, 2022. A total of 125 nurses were invited to participate through voluntary online recruitment, and 102 intensive care nurses who met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate were included in the study, resulting in a participation rate of 81.6%. Because participation was based on voluntary response to online forms, the study employed a convenience (voluntary) sampling method. The sample may not fully represent all ICU nurses due to workload and pandemic-related constraints. Based on Patricia Benner’s “Novice to Expert” model (1984; 2019), nurses who had worked in intensive care units for at least three months during the pandemic and who were caring for COVID-19 patients in the ICU were included. This model suggests that approximately three months of continuous practice allows nurses to progress beyond the novice stage and gain a proficient understanding of ICU routines and patient interactions [16, 17]. Furthermore, nurses who use social media and completed online forms completely and accurately were included in the study. Nurses working outside of the ICU, newly appointed nurses, nurses who had worked in the ICU for less than three months, and nurses who had not cared for COVID-19 patients were excluded from the study.

Data collection

All data were collected electronically. Data confidentiality was shared only with the nurses participating in the study via a separate link on the online forms, and the consent form was also collected online confidentially. Since COVID-19 patient hospitalisations were still ongoing, intensive care nurses were informed about the study and asked to fill in the forms online via Google Forms when convenient. The consent form was added to the online participant form and approved by the nurses. As nurses were informed prior to the study, none of them were found to have completed the online forms incorrectly or incompletely.

The study data were collected using the “Nurse Information Form” and the importance dimension and 10 subscales of the “Caring Nurse-Patient Interaction” (CNPI) Scale.

Nurse Information Form

This is a form structured in light of the literature. The form included a total of 15 questions asking about nurses’ socio-demographic characteristics (age, gender, marital status, education level), professional experience, length of service in intensive care, type of intensive care unit employed in, weekly working hours, number of monthly night shifts, intensive care certification, and work preference.

Caring Nurse-Patient Interaction Scale (CNPI-70)

The scale was developed by Cossette et al. as a scale covering all of Watson’s 10 carative factors in order to evaluate nurses’ attitudes and behaviours [18]. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the Nurse–Patient Interaction Scale (NPIS), adapted from English, was conducted by Atar and Aştı. The scale demonstrated high levels of validity reliability, and its use was recommended for both nurses and nursing students [4]. The scale consists of a total of 70 items, 3 dimensions and 10 subscales. The subscales are: 1. Humanism, 2. Hope, 3. Sensitivity, 4. Helping relationship, 5. Expression of emotions, 6. Problem solving, 7. Teaching, 8. Environment, 9. Needs, and 10. Spirituality. The dimensions are expressed as “importance,” “competence” and “feasibility”,” and each included the same questions. The lowest obtainable score for the three dimensions of the scale is 70, while the highest obtainable score is 350. An increase in participants’ scores on the scale indicate a positive increase in their attitudes and behaviours towards care-focused nurse-patient interaction. Cronbach’s alpha values for the importance, competence, and feasibility sub-dimensions of the scale were calculated as 0.99, 0.98, and 0.99, respectively [4]. In this study, research was conducted on the ‘importance’ sub-dimension an Intensive care unit nurses and the importance sub-dimension’s Cronbach’s alpha found to be 0.98. In this study, Cronbach’s alpha, respectively Humanism 0.86; Hope 0.94; Sensitivity 0.92; Helping relationship 0.88; Expression of emotions 0.88; Problem solving 0.96; Teaching 0.93; Environment 0.88; Needs 0.91; Spirituality 0.93 were found. It was anticipated that nurses working under prolonged and challenging conditions might provide unclear or inaccurate data regarding the “competence” and “feasibility” dimensions during such a difficult period. Therefore, the “importance” dimension was analyzed, as it best reflects the value nurses attribute to care. This study does not focus on competence and feasibility; rather, it focuses on the perceived importance of nurse–patient interaction. The use of the importance dimension of the scale does not affect the reliability or validity of the scale.

Data analysis

The statistical analysis for this study was done with the aid of the SPSS 20 computer software (SPSS, Chicago, United States). Since the skewness and kurtosis values of the data remained within the + 2.0/-2.0 range, it was observed that the data showed a normal distribution [19]. The computer-aided data analysis program was used for the basic analyses (correlation, frequency) of the research data. In the study, regarding the CNPI Scale, the independent samples t-test was used between two variable groups, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for three and more variables. Post hoc evaluations in ANOVA were examined using Tukey’s test. In addition to statistically examining the relationships between variables in the study, regression analyses were also used to identify factors that were statistically significantly related to the dependent variable. In this respect, the explanatory power of the independent variables was evaluated and potential related factors were identified. The factors affecting the total score for the importance dimension of the CNPI Scale were explained through linear regression. The study analyses were examined with a 95% confidence interval and a p < 0.05 significance level. Since the nurses were informed before the study, all forms were completed correctly, and no missing data were found. All sample number were analyzed without missing forms.

Results

In the study, the nurses’ average age was determined as 31.25 ± 6.97 years, their mean length of service was 7.07 ± 4.79 years, and their mean length of service in the ICU was 4.87 ± 3.58 years. It was observed that the nurses’ mean monthly number of night shifts before the pandemic was 10.95 ± 5.49 days, while their mean monthly number of night shifts during the pandemic was 12.20 ± 4.97 days. It was determined that 39.2% of the nurses participating in the study were male, 58.8% had a bachelor’s degree, 84.3% worked in the adult ICU, and 80.4% stated that they chose to work in ICU of their own accord (Table 1). It was identified that 52.0% of nurses worked more than 40 h per week before the pandemic, and this rate increased to 69.6% during the pandemic (Table 1; Fig. 1). During the pandemic, the ability of nurses to establish teamwork in the ICUs was examined, and it was found that 44.1% of participants were able to collaborate and work compatibly within a team. However, only 3.9% of the nurses stated that they could maintain this collaboration under all conditions and at all times (Table 1).

When the relationship between the participating nurses’ length of service and number of night shifts and their scores for the importance dimension and subscales of the CNPI Scale was examined, a weak positive significant relationship was found only between their length of service as a nurse and the environment subscale (p < 0.05; r = 0.215) (Table 2).

The relationship between the nurses’ weekly working hours and their scores for the importance dimension and subscales of the CNPI Scale is examined in Table 2. As a result of the analysis, a significant relationship was observed between weekly working hours during the pandemic and the hope and spirituality subscales. It was determined that those who worked less than 40 h per week during the pandemic had lower levels of hope than those who worked 40 h or more (F = 3.46; P = 0.03). Within the spirituality subscale, a significant difference emerged between nurses working more than 40 h per week and those working 40 h or fewer. Nurses who exceeded 40 working hours per week demonstrated higher spirituality scores than their counterparts working 40 h or less. (F = 3.21; p = 0.04) (Table 2).

No significant relationship was found between the importance subdimension of the CNPI Scale and its subscales, and whether the nurses had received certification or intensive care training (Table 3).

A significant relationship was found between nurses’ ability to establish team collaboration in ICU and the total score for importance, hope, sensitivity, and problem-solving subscales (Table 3). The significant relationship was found to arise from nurses who answered “yes” and “no” for team collaboration on the total score for importance of CNPI with those who answered “yes” having a higher total score (F = 4.29; p = 0.01) (Table 3). Those who answered “always” to the total score for importance were found to have the highest total score for importance (Table 3).

A significant relationship was found between participants’ hope subscale scores and team collaboration. This relationship was observed to stem from the responses of participants who answered “yes”, “no” and “partly”, with those who answered “yes” having higher total scores (F = 6.24; p = 0.00) (Table 3). The highest score on the hope subscale was observed among those who reported “always” being able to establish team collaboration (Table 3).

A significant relationship was found between participants’ sensitivity subscale scores and team collaboration. This relationship was attributed to nurses who answered “yes” and “no”, with those who responded “yes” having higher total scores (F = 3.56; p = 0.02) (Table 3). The highest score on the sensitivity subscale was found among those who answered “always” regarding their ability to establish team collaboration (Table 3).

A significant relationship was identified between participants’ problem-solving subscale scores and team collaboration. This relationship was attributed to nurses who responded “yes” and “no”, with those answering “yes” showing higher total scores (F = 4.53; p = 0.00) (Table 3). The factors affecting the importance sub-dimension of the CNPI Scale were examined using multiple linear regression analysis, and the established regression model was found to be statistically significant (F = 3.58; p = 0.00). The R² value, which indicates the explanatory power of the model, was 19%, showing that the variables included in the model explained 19% of the variance in the importance sub-dimension of the CNPI Scale. According to the results of the analysis, age, preference for working in the ICU, and the ability to establish teamwork in the ICU were identified as significant factors affecting the importance sub-dimension.

According to the model, each one-unit increase in age leads to a 1.91- point decrease in the importance sub-dimension score of the CNPI Scale (B₁= -0.30; B₀ = -1.91; p = 0.02). This result indicates that as age increases, nurses’ perception of the importance of care-oriented nurse-patient interaction decreases (Table 4).

The preference for working in the ICU was analyzed with “yes” as the reference category. Nurses who responded “no” had significantly lower scores in the importance sub-dimension of the CNPI Scale (B₁= -0.26; B₀ = -29.8; p = 0.01). Accordingly, nurses who did not prefer to work in the ICU, ıt was determined that they scored 29.8 points lower on the importance subscale (Table 4).

For the variable “being able to establish teamwork in the ICU “the reference category was set as “yes” Nurses who stated that they were unable to establish teamwork in the ICU had, according to the model on average, 31- points lower scores in the importance sub-dimension of the CNPI Scale (B₁ = -0.24; B₀ = -31; p = 0.03) (Table 4).

In this study, a multiple linear regression model was developed to identify the factors influencing the importance nurses assign to care interactions. Age, as an individual/experiential factor; willingness to work in the intensive care unit as an indicator of professional motivation; and the ability to establish teamwork as a proxy for institutional support mechanisms were included in the model as independent variables, and the model was found to be statistically significant (F = 3.58; p = 0.00). The coefficient of determination (R²), indicating the explanatory power of the model, was calculated as 19%. In the health sciences, where human behaviors and perception are influenced by numerous uncontrollable external variables (e.g., personality traits, organizational culture, and workload); an explanatory power of 19%, is considered meaningful and acceptable in the literature. Although limited, this statistically significant proportion highlights the multidimensional nature of nurse–patient interactions and demonstrates that the three key variables examined (age, willingness, and teamwork) independently explain a substantial portion of the variance. This finding underscores the complex and multifaceted character of nurse–patient interactions. While the variables included in the model provide critical insights, the remaining 81% of the variance suggests the influence of other complex individual and systemic dynamics not captured in the present study.

In the following Discussion section, these results will be discussed in detail in the light of the theoretical framework and previous research.

Discussion

Our analysis highlights that intensive care nurses maintain a profoundly positive orientation toward the significance of nurse–patient interaction, identifying it as a pivotal determinant of care quality even amidst the unprecedented constraints of the COVID-19 pandemic. The elevated scores observed underscore that such interactions are not merely supplementary, but fundamental clinical engagements that enhance the visibility and efficacy of nursing practice. These findings demonstrate that, despite pandemic-induced systemic pressures, nurses prioritized relational care as a central component of high-quality treatment. This attitudinal commitment aligns with international literature, which identifies effective nurse–patient engagement in ICUs as a primary driver for optimizing recovery, satisfaction, and clinical outcomes [20–22]. Furthermore, our results confirm that nurses continued to navigate these challenging conditions with professional dedication, upholding ethical standards and care responsibilities without compromise [23, 24]. Consistent with the present findings, previous research indicates that nurses perceived the maintenance of an “interactive space” as a professional obligation, even under pandemic constraints. Within this context, relational engagement was not viewed as optional but as an essential component of sustaining patient care. This perception reflects a broader professional commitment to preserving the relational dimension of care despite environmental and systemic challenges [25].

It is known that professional experience improves the ability to better determine and manage environmental factors. In the study, identified a weak but significant positive correlation between length of service and the environment subdimension of the importance scale in the CNPI, independent of age. In a study conducted during the pandemic, it was found that nurses with 6 to 11 years of professional experience considered care-focused nurse–patient interaction to be more important [26]. Persson et al. emphasized that verified clinical experience constitutes a form of evidence in the clinical decision-making process [27]. International evidence shows that experienced nurses are more successful in providing patient-centered care, and that their professional experience and emotional intelligence facilitate clinical decision-making and strengthen communication. It was found that nurses with more than 11 years of professional experience reported significantly higher perceptions of caring and more positive attitudes toward care compared to those with fewer years of experience [28, 29]. Over time, nurses appear to develop a more reflective and proactive professional orientation, enhancing intuitive decision-making capacities [30, 31]. Although some studies indicate that experienced nurses may adopt a more task-oriented focus [32], the present findings support the view that professional experience remains a key determinant in sustaining high-quality, interaction-centered care [33, 34].

The analysis demonstrated that, a negative association between age and the CNPI perceived importance dimension, indicating that each incremental increase in age was linked to a 1.91-point reduction in scores. This result may suggest that, during the pandemic, younger nurses who had experienced fewer cumulative years of ICU-related fatigue—maintained higher perceived engagement, whereas increasing age was associated with lower motivation and greater emotional exhaustion. However, it is known that during the pandemic, nurses of all ages, regardless of age, worked in intensive care units without a choice of workplace. On the other hand, it is stated in the literature that when nurses have high levels of self-efficacy, an increase in workload can increase their time management skills and improve their interaction with patients [35]. Furthermore, some studies have shown that in ICUs, nurses can establish meaningful and effective patient interaction even under high workloads by developing new time management strategies [36]. Findings show that the effects of workload and time pressure on patient interaction may vary depending on nurses’ individual abilities, age, support systems, and the dynamics of the work environment.

In this study, it was observed that not choosing to work in intensive care resulted in a 29.8- point decrease in nurses’ total score for the importance dimension of the CNPI. Data shows that a negative result of not choosing to work in ICUs, where care-focused interaction is needed the most, is a difficult of care and the speed of patient recovery. In the study conducted during the pandemic 61% of the nurses indicated that they were willing to care for patients with COVID-19 [37]. Whereas, in a pandemic-era study entitled “If Not Me, Then Who? ”, nurses assigned to ICUs described the experience as both challenging and demanding. Findings suggest that the decision to work in intensive care may vary depending on factors such as nurse-to-patient ratios, exposure to risks, team motivation, professional competency, and the available institutional resources. Moreover, this choice appears to be a distinguishing factor in nurse–patient interactions [38]. This situation highlights the importance of voluntarily choosing to work in the ICU, ensuring adequate team support, and implementing effective leadership strategies to sustain nurses’ motivation and maintain nurse–patient interaction.

The study further revealed that receiving intensive care certification did not have a significant effect on the importance dimension and subscales of the CNPI Scale. In this context, it is considered that obtaining certification does not have a direct effect on nurses’ perceptions of the importance of nurse–patient interaction; that reviewing the content and methods of certification programs may be beneficial; and that preference in continuing to work in intensive care units may be a stronger determinant of nurse–patient interaction.

The study revealed that nurses working a standard 40-hour week exhibited higher levels of dispositional hope, underscoring its critical role in facilitating effective nurse-patient interactions. This finding is particularly significant for intensive care management, as it suggests that balanced working hours are foundational to both professional efficacy and spiritual well-being. Furthermore, the data suggests that enduring extended shifts is only sustainable through the establishment of robust interprofessional teamwork [39]. In this study, 44.1% of the nursing staff successfully established team collaboration, identifying it as a pivotal factor in the quality of nurse-patient engagement. Notably, nurses who engaged in collaborative teamwork demonstrated significantly higher scores in hope, moral sensitivity, and problem-solving competencies. Conversely, a substantial decline was observed among those unable to establish collaboration; specifically, their total scores in the “importance” dimension of the Caring Nurse-Patient Interaction (CNPI) scale decreased by 31 points. This result aligns with existing literature emphasizing the necessity of regulating workloads and providing systemic support for ICU nursing staff [40, 41]. Similarly, evidence suggests that in ICUs characterized by strong teamwork and support structures, the quality of nurse-patient interactions remains resilient despite high workloads, effectively mitigating the stress induced by temporal pressures [42]. These findings are further corroborated by Eckerblad et al. (2025), who highlighted that strong collaboration and structural adaptations enabled ICU nurses in Sweden to maintain high standards of care amidst escalating demands and evolving clinical challenges [43].

The findings from the research indicate that intensive care nursing management should prioritize voluntary placement, ensure the retention of experienced nurses, strengthen interdisciplinary collaboration, and implement staffing strategies that support work-life balance. These practical measures in intensive care nursing management are supported by recent international qualitative studies that emphasize professional leadership, team collaboration, continuous education, supportive and motivating work environments [44, 45].

The utilization of the CNPI Scale in this study facilitated a comprehensive, multidimensional evaluation of nurse-patient interactions. The findings provide critical insights into the perceived significance of these interactions within the complex crisis dynamics precipitated by the COVID-19 pandemic. Furthermore, these results establish a robust framework for assessing how healthcare systems can be strategically restructured to bolster nursing resilience and interprofessional collaboration, thereby optimizing service delivery and standardizing the quality of care during analogous public health crises. These findings suggest that the institutionalization of collaborative teamwork may be pivotal for the consistent delivery of ethically grounded nursing care.

Limitations

The study has certain inherent limitations due to the conditions imposed by the COVID-19 pandemic. Owing to difficulties in obtaining research permissions, the study was conducted in a limited number of hospitals. Because participation was based on voluntary response to online forms, the study employed a convenience (voluntary) sampling method. So that The sample may not fully represent all ICU nurses. Conducting the study in a limited number of ICUs with volunteer nurses may have limit its representativeness and introduce selection bias. Due to the prolonged, exhausting, and demanding working conditions experienced by intensive care nurses during the pandemic, the “competence” and “feasibility " subscales —which include the same questions as the “importance” scale were not used, as they could lead to unrealistic, inaccurate, or ambiguous results. This study does not focus on “competence” and “feasibility; rather, it presents findings on the perceived importance of nurse–patient interaction. Due to pandemic-related constraints, conducting the analysis using the ‘importance’ dimension may limit comparability with studies that examine the scale’s ‘competence” and “feasibility’ dimensions; however, enables comparison with similar research and preserves construct validity. Furthermore, due to significant difficulties in data collection, a voluntary-based data collection approach was deemed inevitable, practical, and necessary in implementation. Accordingly, G*Power was not used for sample size determination. This may have limited the statistical power to detect small to medium effect sizes. These issues are acknowledged as methodological limitations of the study. Second, the study was designed cross-sectionally, it allows the determination of relationships rather than causal inferencesso it may not fully account for changes over time or long-term effects. Finally, the sample included nurses working in different types of ICUs, including adult ICUs. This may have led to variability in experiences and perceptions due to uncontrollable factors such as differences in unit dynamics, patient populations, infection risks, working hours, rest opportunities, emotional burden, intra-team stressors, psychologimacal resilience, and care workload. Thus, we suggest that future research involve comparisons among diverse ICU settings to minimize confounding variables.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study contributes to the literature by demonstrating that intensive care nurses maintained a strong perception of the importance of nurse–patient interaction even under pandemic conditions. The findings indicate that nurses continued to regard nurse–patient interaction as a fundamental component of care. Age, professional experience, effective team collaboration, and voluntary choice to work in the ICU were identified as significant factors associated with the perceived of care-oriented interaction. Regulating weekly working hours to 40 h or fewer and improving the structure and frequency of night shifts may strengthen nurses’ psychological and physical resilience, thereby supporting the sustained perception of interaction as essential to quality care. Establishing structured support systems in intensive care units may help retain experienced nurses, while strategies that strengthen team collaboration and motivation can enhance both collaborative practice and the sustained perception of the importance of care interaction. Also educational and certification programs should also be revised to strengthen nurses’ awareness of the importance of nurse–patient interaction in clinical practice.

Ultimately, longitudinal research is warranted to evaluate the long-term impact of the pandemic on nursing professional identity and to refine strategies that sustain interactional quality during protracted public health crises. Also should be explored the impact of nurses’ age on care interaction in greater depth, as well as to investigate the long-term effects of the pandemic on nurses.Synthesizing these empirical insights will facilitate the translation of past experiences into institutional learning, ultimately cultivating a resilient and highly prepared intensive care nursing workforce capable of navigating the complexities of future.
