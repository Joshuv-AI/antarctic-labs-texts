The role of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) in major depressive disorder: an update meta-analysis
Authors: Xun Song, Sa Xiao, Ze Wang, Wenwen Miao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) is a prevalent mental health condition characterized by persistent feelings of profound sadness, emptiness, or hopelessness, as well as a marked loss of interest or pleasure in almost all activities. Evidence indicates that vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is essential for neuronal survival, angiogenesis, and synaptic connectivity, and is also involved in stress resilience and antidepressant mechanisms, offering new perspectives on the pathophysiology of MDD. Although several studies have examined the relationship between peripheral VEGF levels and MDD, their findings remain inconsistent or inconclusive. Therefore, this updated meta-analysis was conducted to clarify the role of VEGF in the etiology of MDD.ObjectiveThis study aimed to conduct an updated meta-analysis to investigate the relationship between plasma VEGF levels in patients with MDD compared to healthy controls (HCs).MethodsFour databases, PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, and Web of Science, were systematically searched for the meta-analysis from their inception until December 2024. A total of 14 case-control studies involving 2,919 participants (1,256 patients with MDD and 1,663 HCs) were included. The meta-analysis utilized both the ratio of means (RoM) and the standardized mean difference (SMD) methods to enhance interpretability and clinical relevance.ResultsThe meta-analysis confirmed that patients with MDD had significantly higher VEGF levels compared to HCs (RoM = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.06–1.38, p < 0.001; SMD = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.13–0.77, p < 0.001).ConclusionThe findings suggest that elevated plasma VEGF levels are associated with MDD, as indicated by the updated meta-analysis. A potential biological link between VEGF and MDD exists, offering important insights into the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08387-1.Keywords: Vascular endothelial growth factor, Major depressive disorder, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Major depressive disorder (MDD) affects over 380 million individuals globally and significantly increases the risk of suicide and chronic medical conditions such as cardiovascular disease (CVD) and diabetes mellitus (DM) [1, 2]. Despite the availability of various pharmacological interventions, including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), and noradrenergic and specific serotonergic antidepressants (NaSSAs) drugs which are considered first-line treatments for MDD [3]. However, a significant proportion of patients exhibit treatment resistance, underscoring the need for a deeper understanding of the biological mechanisms underlying MDD. In response to this phenomenon, research has shifted from monoaminergic dysfunction to neurotrophic factors, particularly vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which regulates neuronal survival, angiogenesis, and synaptic connectivity. VEGF is also implicated in stress resilience and antidepressant mechanisms, offering novel insights into the pathophysiology of MDD [4–6].

VEGF is a glycoprotein that promotes endothelial cell proliferation, enhances vascular permeability, and contributes to neurogenesis, particularly within the hippocampus, a brain region significantly associated with mood regulation and depression [7, 8]. Dysregulation of VEGF signaling has been observed in individuals with MDD, and variations in VEGF levels have been correlated with both the onset and progression of depressive symptoms [9–11]. Preclinical and clinical studies suggest that VEGF modulates neuronal plasticity, enhances synaptic connectivity, and exerts anti-inflammatory effects, all of which are essential for maintaining optimal brain function [12–15]. Furthermore, antidepressant treatments, particularly SSRIs and ketamine, have been shown to increase VEGF expression, indicating that VEGF might play a vital role in mediating their therapeutic effects [16–20]. Given its multifaceted role, VEGF has been proposed as a biomarker for MDD severity and a potential therapeutic target for novel antidepressant strategies [21, 22].

Initially recognized for its role in vascular growth, VEGF is essential for hippocampal neurogenesis and for maintaining the integrity of the blood-brain barrier (BBB). It is also implicated in mood regulation and depression, thereby contributing to the etiology of MDD [17, 23]. Preclinical studies using rodent models have indicated that chronic stress elevates hippocampal VEGF expression, potentially reflecting a compensatory neurotrophic response to stress-induced neuronal damage [17]. Clinically, Lee et al. reported that plasma VEGF levels were significantly elevated in patients with acute MDD compared to healthy controls (HCs) [24]. However, contradictory findings exist; certain clinical studies indicate reduced plasma VEGF levels in patients with MDD, which tend to normalize following successful treatment [25]. The dual role of VEGF in angiogenesis and neurogenesis establishes it as a key mediator of the structural and functional brain deficits observed in MDD [26, 27].

Previous studies have suggested a potential relationship between the peripheral VEGF levels and MDD. However, the findings of these studies have been inconsistent or even contradictory, likely due to variations in psychiatric diagnosis criteria of the patients, age of participants, gender distribution, whether the episode is a first or recurrent one, drug-naïve status or ongoing treatment, severity of depression, sample sizes, and sample sources (plasma, serum, or whole blood). This meta-analysis aimed to synthesize data from existing studies and address sources of heterogeneity to clarify the relationship between VEGF levels and MDD.

Previous meta-analysis examining VEGF alterations in MDD have been limited by methodological variability and a lack of focus on this specific subgroup. To address these limitations, an updated meta-analysis was conducted to provide a comprehensive synthesis of the evidence regarding VEGF levels in MDD patients and offer insights into the potential role of VEGF as a biomarker for early-stage depression.

Methods

Search strategy

Following the PRISMA [28] reporting guidelines, the search strategy incorporated both subject terms and keywords. The study protocol was pre-registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420261377021). Two investigators (X.S. and S.X.) independently searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases from inception to December 31, 2024, utilizing the following search terms: “Depression,” “Depressive Symptoms,” “Depressive Symptom,” “Symptom, Depressive,” “Emotional Depression,” “Depression, Emotional,” as well as “Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor A,” “Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor,” “Vascular Endothelial Growth Factor-A,” “Vascular Permeability Factor,” “Permeability Factor, Vascular,” “Vasculotropin,” “VEGF,”“VEGF-A,” “Glioma-Derived Vascular Endothelial Cell Growth Factor,” “Glioma Derived Vascular Endothelial Cell Growth Factor,” and “GD-VEGF.” The detailed search strings utilized in this review are available in the supplementary materials accompanying the online version of this article.

Eligibility criteria for study selection

Inclusion criteria for meta-analysis are as follows: (1) Study participants: The study participants were patients with a definitive diagnosis of unipolar depression (excluding those with other mental disorders such as bipolar disorder or anxiety disorders). These patients should meet the diagnostic criteria for depressive disorder or MDD as outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), Fourth Edition (DSM-IV), or Third Edition (DSM-III), or the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) [29]. The study must report or provide clear scoring information from depression assessment tools (such as Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D), Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS), Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), etc.); (2) Control group: The study included an HC group, with participants having no history of depression or other mental illnesses. The control group should be matched to the case group in terms of age and gender ratio and should not have major health issues, such as neurological, endocrine, or cardiovascular diseases, that could influence neurotrophic factors; (3) Study Design: The study should utilize a case-control design, comparing VEGF levels between patients with MDD and HCs. Included studies must provide at least the sample size, mean (M), and standard deviation (SD) for both the case and control groups, or data that allows for the calculation of these values; (4) Language and Publication: Only published studies (including journal articles, conference papers, etc.) in English, with complete research reports, are to be included. For multiple publications originating from the same study, only the version with the most comprehensive information, the largest sample size, or the most relevant data to the research objectives was included to avoid duplication.

Exclusion criteria for meta-analysis are as follows: (1) Study participants: Patients diagnosed with non-unipolar depression or those presenting with comorbid mental disorders and significant physical illnesses are excluded; (2) Insufficient data availability: Studies with incomplete data, such as those reporting data solely for certain groups or where the necessary statistical data cannot be derived from the available information; (3) Inadequate research design: Reviews, abstracts, case reports, animal experiments, and similar study designs are excluded. Studies lacking a control group (such as those including only patients with depression) or with an insufficient sample size that precludes the determination of a meaningful effect size are also excluded; (4) Data duplication: Studies with duplicate publications or overlapping data are excluded. In the case of duplicate studies, only the most comprehensive and current version is included to ensure each research finding is counted only once.

Data extraction and processing

Data extraction for this analysis involved collecting pertinent information from the included studies, such as the first author’s name, publication year, country, sample sizes of the case and control groups, treatment status, sample source, gender ratio, age, diagnostic criteria, and the M ± SD of VEGF, etc. Two researchers (X. S. and S.X.) independently extracted the relevant information and data from each eligible study. In the case of discrepancies during the extraction process, consensus was reached through discussion and consultation with a third researcher (Z.W.). The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [28], which provides quality scores ranging from 0 to 9. Studies with scores of 6 or more were considered high quality.

Statistical methods for meta-analysis

This study integrated continuous outcome data using the standardized mean difference (SMD) and the ratio of means (RoM). RoM quantifies the relative change between groups while preserving the original measurement scale, making the interpretation of results more intuitive and clinically meaningful. Compared to the SMD, RoM reduces heterogeneity across studies, particularly when measurement units are consistent or comparable. By employing a single, consistent method, the analysis minimizes potential discrepancies arising from the use of mixed effect size metrics and enhances the clarity and interpretability of the pooled results [30].

Data processing and statistical analysis were conducted using Stata 17.0 software. All statistical tests were two-sided, with a significant level set at p < 0.05. Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated using the I² statistic, along with the Q-test for significance analysis. An I² value of less than 50% indicated low heterogeneity, justifying the use of a fixed-effects model for data pooling. Conversely, an I² value of 50% or greater suggested moderate to high heterogeneity, necessitating the use of a random-effects model for analysis. An I² value exceeding 75% indicated substantial heterogeneity, requiring further investigation into its sources. Given the high heterogeneity observed in this study, a random-effects model was employed for data pooling, supplemented by subgroup analysis and meta-regression to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Egger’s funnel plot was utilized to visually assess publication bias, and Egger’s test was applied for quantitative analysis. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using a leave-one-out approach to assess the robustness of the pooled results. In this method, each study was sequentially excluded, and the pooled effect size recalculated.

Results

Search results

A total of 2,384 relevant articles were identified across various databases, including PubMed (n = 725), The Cochrane Library (n = 640), Web of Science (n = 961), and Embase (n = 58). After removing duplicates, 1,091 articles were retained for initial screening. During this preliminary screening phase, 1,043 articles were excluded based on their titles and abstracts. Subsequent full-text reviews led to the exclusion of 34 additional articles due to issues related to study design and data availability: absence of a control group (n = 17), lack of usable data (n = 7), presence of co-morbid bipolar disorder (n = 2), presence of co-morbid somatic disorder (n = 2), sample size less than 10 (n = 1), cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) levels of VEGF (n = 1), and mRNA levels of VEGF (n = 4). Ultimately, 14 studies [11, 31–42] were included in the meta-analysis. The literature screening process is shown in Fig. 1. Included research characteristics

Fourteen studies were identified, encompassing a total of 2,919 participants, including 1,256 patients and 1,663 HCs. These studies were conducted across eleven countries: China (n = 2); Japan (n = 2); Germany (n = 2); Hungary (n = 1); the United States (n = 1); Denmark (n = 1); Italy (n = 1); Poland (n = 1); Turkey (n = 1); Korea (n = 1); and France (n = 1). Eleven studies employed the DSM as the diagnostic criteria for depression, while three studies utilized the ICD-10. Twelve studies had a higher number of female participants compared to male participants, with one study involving only female participants. According to the NOS, all fourteen studies were rated as high [11, 31–42].

The meta-analysis of the primary outcome

A total of 14 case–control studies (n = 2,919; MDD = 1,256, HC = 1,663) were included in the meta-analysis. Utilizing the RoM as the effect size, peripheral VEGF levels in patients with MDD were significantly elevated in comparison to HCs (RoM = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.06–1.38, p < 0.001, Fig. 2). Similarly, when the SMD was used as the effect size, patients with MDD also exhibited significantly elevated peripheral VEGF levels compared with HCs (SMD = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.13–0.77, p < 0.001, Fig. 3). These findings suggest that, on average, VEGF levels in MDD patients were 21% higher than those in healthy individuals. Sensitivity analysis showed that this significant difference in VEGF levels was not influenced by any single study (Fig. 4). In addition, significant heterogeneity was observed among the studies included in our analysis (ROM: I2 = 92.9%, SMD: I2 = 91.7%). No significant publication bias was detected among these studies, as indicated by Egger’s test (p = 0.278) and Begg’s test (Fig. 5).

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the moderating effects of ethnicity, treatment status, and biological sample source on VEGF levels in MDD (Figs. 6, 7 and 8). Regarding ethnicity, VEGF levels in MDD patients of Asian populations were significantly elevated compared to controls (RoM = 1.16, 95% CI: 1.00–1.34), indicating a 16% increase. Conversely, studies involving Caucasian samples presented a similar point estimate (RoM = 1.16); however, the confidence interval crossed 1 (95% CI: 0.87–1.54), suggesting that the difference was not statistically significant. Thus, the association between elevated VEGF levels and MDD was confirmed solely in Asian cohorts.

Regarding treatment status, treatment-naïve MDD patients exhibited significantly higher VEGF levels than controls (RoM = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.06–1.66), reflecting a 33% increase. No significant differences were observed in studies involving patients who had received treatment (RoM = 1.21, 95% CI: 0.71–2.07) or mixed-treatment groups (RoM = 1.08, 95% CI: 0.91–1.28).

Regarding sample source, when VEGF was measured in plasma, MDD patients demonstrated significantly higher levels than controls (RoM = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.03–1.68), indicating a 32% increase. However, serum VEGF levels did not differ significantly between groups (RoM = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.94–1.35). Despite these subgroup findings, substantial heterogeneity remained across all subgroups, suggesting that these factors did not completely explain the variability observed between studies.

Discussion

VEGF level alterations in MDD

This meta-analysis utilized both the SMD (SMD = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.13–0.77) and the RoM (RoM = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.06–1.38) as effect size measures. Evidence from both approaches indicates that changes in peripheral VEGF levels in patients with MDD are likely associated with neuroinflammatory processes, thereby reinforcing the biological connection between VEGF and MDD. Furthermore, the consistency between the SMD and RoM results suggests that RoM offers advantages in quantifying effect sizes and may facilitate the development of clinically relevant thresholds for future diagnostic or therapeutic use.

Clinical studies of VEGF in MDD patients show mixed results, reflecting complex biology and methodological variations

The results of this meta-analysis indicate that, on average, VEGF levels were elevated in the MDD group relative to the control group. This finding aligns with the results from several previous clinical studies, further supporting the association between elevated VEGF levels and MDD. A study conducted by Li et al. in 2024 utilized astrocyte-derived extracellular vesicles (ADEVs) to investigate brain-derived VEGF. The study identified elevated VEGF levels in ADEVs from untreated patients with MDD compared to controls [38]. This increase in central VEGF was associated with higher plasma levels of Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP), an astrocyte marker, suggesting disruption of the BBB. Similarly, Wallensten et al. (2021) compared isoforms of soluble VEGF in stress-related disorders. They found that plasma levels of VEGF_165, the main isoform, were significantly lower in MDD than in controls, while another isoform, VEGF_121, was higher in patients with stress-exhaustion [43]. The authors concluded that changes in specific isoforms vary by diagnosis. These findings suggest that peripheral VEGF assays may fail to detect underlying alterations in specific VEGF isoforms or brain-localized VEGF.

In contrast, other studies have reported decreased or normal VEGF levels. For example, Rigal assessed plasma VEGF-A levels in patients during a major depressive episode and following remission. The study found that depressed patients exhibited significantly lower VEGF-A levels compared to HCs, with these levels remaining low even after symptom remission [44]. The researchers concluded that VEGF deficiency in depressed individuals might contribute to the pathogenesis of MDD, and suggested that treatments aimed at enhancing VEGF could be therapeutic. In CSF, Kranaster et al. (2020) observed that patients with treatment-resistant depression had significantly lower CSF VEGF levels than matched controls. Neither electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) treatment nor symptom improvement significantly altered CSF VEGF levels in that study. These findings indicate a correlation between VEGF deficiency and MDD, at least in certain contexts [45]. Meanwhile, meta-analyses and several large-scale studies have found no significant differences in serum VEGF levels between patients with depression and HCs [46].

In summary, current research has yet to reach a consistent conclusion regarding changes in VEGF levels in patients with MDD. Variation in study design may significantly contribute to the lack of consensus. Key factors include whether VEGF is measured in serum or plasma (as clotting can release VEGF from platelets), time of day, patient heterogeneity (e.g., first-episode vs. chronic, drug-naïve vs. treated), and assay methodologies. For example, one meta-analysis found that differences in VEGF levels between patients and controls were more pronounced in younger cohorts, whereas variables such as BMI or gender exerted negligible effects. Some inconsistencies may also result from unmeasured confounders such as inflammation, comorbidities, or genetic polymorphisms [47–49]. Therefore, additional large-scale, multicenter, high-quality clinical studies are needed to elucidate the characteristics of VEGF level changes in patients with MDD.

Methodological heterogeneity

This meta-analysis identified substantial heterogeneity, which necessitates cautious interpretation of the findings. Meta-regression and subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate potential sources of heterogeneity. Meta-regression revealed a statistically significant association between the proportion of female participants and effect size, suggesting that sex may be a key contributor to this heterogeneity. Furthermore, subgroup analysis by ethnicity demonstrated a marked reduction in heterogeneity within the Asian subgroup (I² = 70.7%), indicating that ethnic differences may influence VEGF levels and partially explain the observed heterogeneity. Similarly, subgroup analysis based on treatment status showed reduced heterogeneity in the treatment-naïve MDD group (I² = 76.1%). While heterogeneity remained relatively high, this reduction suggests that treatment exposure may affect VEGF levels and the consistency of study outcomes. In addition to sex, ethnicity, and treatment status, methodological differences may also contribute to the inconsistencies across studies. Studies differ in terms of sample type (serum, plasma, CSF, extracellular vesicles [EVs]), VEGF isoform measurement, and antidepressant exposure. For example, depressed patients undergoing treatment with SSRIs may exhibit differences compared to those who are unmedicated. The review by Aksan & Mauceri (2025) emphasizes that VEGF levels can be influenced by numerous systemic factors, such as inflammation and hypoxia [50]. Consequently, these varying experimental methodologies have likely contributed to the mixed results observed. It remains unclear whether a single measure of VEGF can reliably capture changes associated with depression.

The function of VEGF

VEGF extends its functions beyond vascular biology, influencing various aspects of brain function, including neurogenesis, neuronal survival, synaptic plasticity, and the health of the neurovascular unit [51]. VEGF plays key roles in the etiology of MDD as follows: Firstly, adult neurogenesis: VEGFA, the primary isoform, is essential for promoting the birth of new neurons in the adult hippocampus [52]. In rodent models, VEGFA is required for exercise- or environment-induced neurogenesis and mediates the neurogenic effects of antidepressant treatments and electroconvulsive seizures [53]. Conversely, chronic stress reduces VEGFA expression, which correlates with reduced hippocampal neurogenesis [54]. Thus, VEGF appears to link environmental or pharmacologic stimuli to increase hippocampal neuron generation, a region generally associated with MDD. Secondly, synaptic plasticity: Independent of new neuron generation, VEGF significantly modulates synaptic function. It enhances synaptic excitability and plasticity through VEGFR2 signaling [19]. In hippocampal neurons, VEGF enhances NMDA receptor–mediated excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) and increases the frequency of miniature EPSCs [55]. VEGF also promotes long-term potentiation (LTP) in hippocampal slices, suggesting it increases synaptic strength [56]. Notably, VEGFA expression is activity-dependent, with neuronal stimulation inducing VEGF in a feedback loop [50]. These data suggest that VEGF acts as a synapse-modulating neurotrophic factor, potentially complementing factors such as brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in memory and plasticity [19]. This function has been shown to be involved in the etiology of MDD. Thirdly, BBB integrity: VEGF is a potent regulator of blood vessel permeability. In the brain, VEGF–VEGFR2 signaling can increase BBB permeability under stress [57]. Matsuno et al. demonstrated that elevated VEGF mediates stress-induced disruption of the BBB in a chronic stress mouse model. Inhibition of VEGFR2 normalized BBB function and effectively prevented hedonic behaviors [17]. Thus, VEGF appears to be a key node linking stress and vascular changes in the brain; excessive VEGF signaling can compromise the BBB, potentially contributing to neuroinflammation and depression-like behaviors [57]. Moreover, VEGF is sensitive to stress hormones. Chronic psychological stress reduces hippocampal VEGF expression and inhibits neurogenesis, while acute stress may transiently upregulate VEGF as a protective mechanism [58–60]. The net effect of stress on VEGF appears to be context-dependent; however, numerous studies have reported that stress reduces VEGF levels and its trophic support. This stress-sensitivity places VEGF within the neurotrophic pathway of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis and the inflammatory responses linked to depression [61].

Collectively, these findings characterize VEGF as a complex neurotrophic factor. In addition to promoting angiogenesis, VEGF in the CNS facilitates neural growth, synaptic modulation, and vascular–neural interactions that are fundamental to plasticity. This physiological function positions VEGF as a biologically plausible contributor to the pathophysiology of depression.

In summary, current clinical evidence does not show a consistent pattern of VEGF alterations in MDD. While some studies report reduced VEGF levels, others observe increases or no significant changes. These inconsistencies underscore the need for standardized assays and stratification of patient samples. Future studies should rigorously control for confounding variables and prioritize specific measures, such as brain-derived vesicle VEGF or CSF VEGF, to better elucidate the relationship between VEGF and MDD.

Limitations

Our findings have important clinical implications, suggesting that elevated VEGF levels may be biologically associated with MDD and providing a foundation for further investigation into its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. However, several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. Potential confounding factors, including disease stage, sample type, comorbidities, inflammatory status, BMI, smoking, and assay conditions, were not adequately controlled, which may compromise the stability and comparability of the results. Although subgroup analyses indicated that sample type was not a primary source of heterogeneity, differences in pre-analytical handling between plasma and serum could still affect the consistency of VEGF measurements. Future studies should standardize sample types or separately report and analyze data from different sources to improve comparability and interpretability.

Furthermore, substantial heterogeneity was observed in this meta-analysis. While meta-regression and subgroup analyses were conducted to identify the sources of heterogeneity, they could not fully explain it. This suggests that VEGF levels are influenced by multiple factors, including sample processing, genetic background, environmental factors, and disease course characteristics, which have not been consistently reported or controlled in previous studies. Additionally, the limited number of studies included and the small sample sizes in certain subgroups may affect the robustness of these findings. Therefore, these conclusions should be interpreted with caution. In summary, further large-scale, multicenter, and methodologically rigorous studies are necessary to validate and elucidate the patterns and mechanisms underlying VEGF alterations in MDD.

Conclusion

The updated meta-analysis suggests that elevated plasma VEGF levels are associated with MDD, providing important insights into its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms.
