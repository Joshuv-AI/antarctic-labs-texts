Clinical significance of preoperative serum VEGF in predicting metachronous liver metastasis of colorectal cancer: development of a novel nomogram
Authors: Yufei Gu, Jin Du, Yanxin Zhao, Zhongwen Sun, Haijian Yang, Libing Yang, Zhenzhen Cao, Xintao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMetachronous liver metastasis (MLM) after primary resection of colorectal cancer (CRC) substantially worsens patient prognosis. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a highly bioactive proangiogenic protein. However, few studies have used serum VEGF to predict CRC liver metastasis, particularly MLM. This study investigated the predictive value of preoperative serum VEGF levels for the development of MLM in patients with CRC and developed a novel nomogram.MethodsData from 229 patients with CRC who underwent radical resection of the primary tumor at the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University between June 2019 and June 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. Patients were divided into training (n = 153) and validation (n = 76) cohorts. Independent risk factors for MLM were identified in the training cohort and were used to develop and validate the nomogram.ResultsPreoperative VEGF levels > 436.81 pg/mL, preoperative carcinoembryonic antigen levels > 5.0 ng/mL, T stage, N stage, and mismatch repair proficiency were independent risk factors for MLM and were used to develop the nomogram. The concordance index of the training cohort was 0.849 (95% confidence interval: 0.765–0.933). Calibration curve analysis revealed that the standard curve fit well with the predicted curve. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis demonstrated the nomogram’s high efficiency. Decision curve analysis confirmed the nomogram’s significant clinical value.ConclusionWe developed a novel nomogram including VEGF and mismatch repair proficiency in this study which were rarely reported in previous studies and the novel nomogram exhibits good discriminative ability and high accuracy in predicting MLM in patients with CRC. The identification of patients at a high risk of MLM would enable closer monitoring and timely interventions.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a prevalent malignant tumor worldwide, with incidence and mortality rates ranking third and second among all malignant tumors, respectively [1]. In China, approximately 650,000 new CRC cases and 270,000 deaths occur annually, with its incidence and mortality ranking second and fifth, respectively, among all malignant tumors [2]. Because colorectal venous blood directly drains into the portal vein, approximately 50% of patients with CRC will develop liver metastasis (CRC liver metastasis [CRCLM]), which notably affects patient prognosis [3].

CRCLM can be divided into simultaneous liver metastasis (SLM) and metachronous liver metastasis (MLM). Liver metastasis that occurs at or before the diagnosis of CRC is referred to as SLM; MLM refers to liver metastasis that occurs after CRC radical surgery. In China, 15–25% of patients with CRC develop MLM [2]. Most SLMs can be detected preoperatively using imaging, and after radical resection, the 5-year survival rate exceeds 50% [4]. However, 80–90% of patients with MLM are unable to undergo radical hepatic resection [5, 6], and their 5-year survival rate is < 5% [1], significantly affecting the overall prognosis of patients with CRC. Early identification of patients at a high risk of MLM may allow the adoption of more effective intervention and comprehensive treatment, which could greatly improve the prognosis of patients with CRC.

Angiogenesis is fundamental for tumor cell growth, invasion, and metastasis [7]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a highly biologically active protein. VEGF can directly act on vascular endothelial cells, promote the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells into neovascularization [8], provide good conditions for the growth and proliferation of tumor cells, and enhance the permeability of blood vessel walls [9], allowing tumor cells to penetrate the blood vessel wall, reach normal tissue around the blood vessels, and invade and metastasize. VEGF is overexpressed in various malignant tumors, including gastric [10], colorectal [11], ovarian [12], and breast cancer [13]. High serum VEGF levels are associated with tumor recurrence, metastasis, stage, and poor prognosis in patients with CRC [14–17]. However, few studies have reported the use of preoperative serum VEGF levels to detect liver metastasis in CRC, especially MLM. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the predictive value of preoperative serum VEGF levels for MLM in patients with CRC and to develop a novel nomogram.

Methods

Patients

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (2024) and was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University. Clinical data of patients who underwent radical CRC resection at the First Affiliated Hospital of Henan University between June 2019 and June 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. All enrolled patients were followed up for three years in accordance with the relevant guidelines [2], and metastasis was detected using enhanced computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging.

For the definition of MLM, in this study, MLM was defined as liver metastasis that occurred after the curative resection of CRC in accordance with guidelines of CSCO and ESMO [2]. But some studies did use the interval of 3 [18], 6 [19], and 12 [20] months after the primary diagnosis/operation as reference points for MLM. Engstrand et al. [21] summarized previous studies and they support using primary tumor diagnosis/operation as standardized cut-off point to define SLM/MLM, which is also adopted by this study.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years, (2) primary colorectal tumor with a pathological diagnosis of adenocarcinoma, (3) radical resection of the primary tumor, (4) only one primary malignant tumor, (5) no metastasis before resection, (6) complete postoperative pathological data and follow-up data, and (7) administration of postoperative chemotherapy or radiotherapy according to the guidelines of CSCO. The exclusion criteria were: (1) adjuvant therapy administered before resection, (2) SLM, (3) multiple tumors, (4) postoperative distant metastases other than liver metastasis, (5) VEGF not detected before surgery, (6) histopathological results showing positive margins, (7) and unavailable clinical data.

Clinical data collection

Clinical data collected included age, sex, tumor site, tumor size, histological type, differentiation degree, T stage, N stage, lymph nodes examined (LNE), vascular invasion, nerve invasion, mismatch repair (MMR) status, preoperative carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels, cancer antigen (CA) 19 − 9 levels, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels, and VEGF levels. Patients were classified into three groups based on age (≤ 60, 60–75, and > 75 years); tumor site was classified into right colon (cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure, and transverse colon), left colon (splenic flexure, descending colon, and sigmoid colon), and rectum; tumor size was classified into ≤ 5 and > 5 cm; histology type was classified into adenocarcinoma (not otherwise specified) and other adenocarcinoma (special histologic subtypes of adenocarcinoma); and degree of differentiation was classified as I (well differentiated), II (moderately differentiated), III (poorly differentiated), and IV (undifferentiated). The T stage, N stage, LNE, vascular invasion, nerve invasion, and MMR status were determined according to the postoperative pathological data. Preoperative CEA, CA19-9, AFP, and VEGF levels were tested within 1 week before surgery, closest to the scheduled surgery date.

Assay of serum VEGF

Blood for VEGF serum level assessments was collected in plain tubes. Blood samples were centrifuged for 10 min at 3,000 r/min at −4 °C. Serum was subsequently removed and stored at −20 °C for use if immediate testing is not possible. Levels of serum VEGF were measured using a commercially available chemiluminescence kit (Weigao Biochemistry, Shandong, People’s Republic of China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

The original data were analyzed using SPSS software (version 26.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The optimal cutoff values for VEGF were determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the Youden index. Continuous variables were converted into binary variables based on the reference range or cutoff values. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) [22, 23] regression analysis was used to identify candidate risk factors for CRCLM using the “glmnet” package in R software version 3.6.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Non-zero coefficient features were selected from the LASSO regression analysis. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent risk factors, in conjunction with the results of the LASSO regression analysis, using a stepwise method. A nomogram was developed based on these factors using the “rms” package in R software. A concordance index with 1,000 bootstrap samples was used to quantify the discrimination of the nomogram. The variance inflation factor was calculated to measure the multicollinearity of the regression models. Calibration and ROC curves were used to assess the accuracy of the nomogram in the training and validation cohorts, respectively. Decision curve analysis was performed to evaluate the clinical value and net benefit of the nomogram in the training and validation cohorts. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Development and validation of the novel nomogram

Based on the results of the multivariate logistic regression analysis, the preoperative VEGF level, CEA level, T stage, N stage, and MMR status were used to develop a nomogram to predict the risk of MLM in patients with CRC (Fig. 4). The concordance index was 0.849 (95% CI: 0.765–0.933), indicating good predictive ability. Calibration curves of the training and validation cohorts showed that the standard curve fit well with the prediction curve, indicating that the predicted probability was consistent with the actual occurrence probability (Fig. 5). The probability of MLM occurrence was determined from the total score of the nomogram, which was calculated by summing the points for each factor. ROC curve analysis of the total points revealed that the nomogram demonstrated high accuracy in both the training and validation cohorts, with AUCs of 0.849 and 0.817, respectively (Fig. 6). The decision curve analysis revealed that both the training and validation cohorts exhibited a high net benefit across nearly the entire range of threshold probabilities, indicating that the novel nomogram has notable clinical applications (Fig. 7).Fig. 4Nomogram for predicting the risk of MLM in the training cohort MLM, metachronous liver metastasisFig. 5Calibration curves for the (A) training and (B) validation cohortsFig. 6Receiver operating characteristic curves for the prediction of metachronous liver metastasis using the nomogram (A) Training and (B) validation cohorts. Discussion

The comprehensive and holistic treatment of CRC primarily involves surgery, supplemented with radiotherapy, chemotherapy, interventional therapy, and targeted therapy. However, despite active completion of standard treatments, the metastasis rate remains high, with liver metastasis being particularly common [24]. Tumor metastasis is a complex and multistep process. During metastasis, the tumor microenvironment interacts with tumor cells to promote tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis, becoming conducive to tumor cell growth, with tumor neovascularization playing a pivotal role [25, 26]. Since Folkman [27] first proposed the link between neoangiogenesis and tumor growth, the importance of angiogenesis in the growth, invasion, and metastasis of primary solid tumors has been widely recognized. Tumor neoangiogenesis can increase the blood supply to tumor cells, facilitating the transport of oxygen and nutrients, and providing channels for tumor cell metastasis [28, 29]. Recently, angiogenesis has been recognized as a prerequisite for CRC metastasis [30].

Tumor angiogenesis is a dynamic equilibrium process fundamental to tumor progression and is regulated by multiple growth factors, the most significant of which is VEGF [31]. VEGF plays a key role in stimulating both pathological and physiological angiogenesis [32]. Serum VEGF levels are significantly higher in patients with CRC than in healthy individuals [33]. Similarly, VEGF has a higher sensitivity than CEA for diagnosing CRC; serum VEGF levels are significantly higher in CEA-negative patients with CRC, and the combination of the two has strong diagnostic power [34, 35]. Furthermore, elevated postoperative serum VEGF levels in patients with CRC are associated with increased risk of tumor recurrence, suggesting that serum VEGF could serve as a marker for postoperative recurrence [36]. Serum VEGF concentrations are also significantly higher in patients with Dukes C/D stage disease than in those with Dukes A/B stage disease [37]. Patients with CRC with high serum VEGF levels often have shorter survival times, and serum VEGF is an independent risk factor for both disease-free survival (DFS) and overall survival (OS) [38]. Serum VEGF levels are higher in patients with metastatic CRC than in non-metastatic patients [16].

In this study, a preoperative serum VEGF level greater than 436.81 pg/mL was identified as an independent risk factor for MLM in CRC and could be used to predict MLM. Liver metastasis in CRC is an extremely complex biological process with unclear mechanisms. The liver is the first site of colorectal venous drainage, and the hepatic sinusoids, acting as blood filters, are generally considered the organs most susceptible and earliest invaded by CRC. However, explaining this clinical phenomenon solely based on anatomy offers limited guidance for antimetastatic therapy. In patients with CRC who have significantly increased serum VEGF levels prior to surgery, the molecular mechanism of the increased probability of MLM may be related to the following aspects: First, high VEGF expression in tumor tissue promotes endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and lumen formation, thereby creating a rich tumor vascular network that provides ample oxygen and nutrients, which in turn promotes tumor growth and spread [39]. However, compared to normal vessels, tumor neovascularization is often immature, unevenly distributed, tortuous, and dilated, with reduced pericyte coverage. This creates a microenvironment with high vascular permeability, making tumor cells more prone to infiltration and metastasis [40–42]. Second, VEGF can regulate both immune and stromal cells in the tumor microenvironment, indirectly promoting tumor invasion and metastasis through non-angiogenic pathways. Lymphocytes are the primary cell population involved in human immune regulation, and T lymphocytes are a crucial component in tumor immunity. Increased VEGF in the tumor microenvironment can induce endothelial cells to produce prostaglandin E2, which impairs the function of T lymphocytes [43]; VEGF can also reduce T lymphocyte infiltration and inhibit tumor immunity by upregulating apoptosis stimulation fragment ligand in endothelial cells [44]. VEGF can also promote the epithelial–mesenchymal transition process, causing tumor cells to lose intercellular connections, obtain invasion and migration ability, and upregulate the expression of matrix metalloproteinases to degrade the extracellular matrix components of the basement membrane of the blood vessel wall and enhance vascular permeability, resulting in tumor cells entering the blood [45]. Third, VEGF can also directly act on tumor cells that selectively express functional VEGF receptors. The secreted VEGF binds to these receptors, phosphorylates them, activates the corresponding signaling pathways, and forms a VEGF autocrine loop within the tumor cells, promoting the proliferation and infiltration of these cells [46]. When the preoperative peripheral blood VEGF level of patients with CRC increases, the tumor develops rapidly, its invasion ability is enhanced, and tumor immunity decreases, increasing the likelihood of MLM.

Furthermore, postoperative pMMR was an independent risk factor for MLM, a finding that has rarely been reported. Tumor MMR deficiency can be detected immunohistochemically as a loss of MMR protein expression. The association between pMMR status and an elevated risk of liver metastasis (LM) may be attributed to the following mechanisms: First, tumors with dMMR status—owing to defects in DNA mismatch repair machinery—accumulate extensive genetic mutations and generate abundant neoantigens. These molecular alterations serve as potent stimuli for the immune system, recruiting infiltrating immune cells such as CD8 + T lymphocytes and natural killer (NK) cells. This robust immune infiltration enables the killing or suppression of tumor cells, thereby impeding the development of distant metastasis. In contrast, pMMR tumors lack this immunostimulatory characteristic [47]. Second, pMMR status tumors may upregulate the expression of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) via the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process. PD-L1 upregulation facilitates tumor evasion of immune surveillance, while EMT endows tumor cells with enhanced invasive capabilities—two features that collectively promote metastasis [48]. The liver, with its abundant blood supply and distinct microenvironment, becomes the most frequent site of metastasis. Third, pMMR tumors can activate hepatic stellate cells through exosomes (such as miR-188-3p). This activation triggers remodeling of the extracellular matrix and the development of liver fibrosis, ultimately establishing a pre-metastatic microenvironment in the liver [49]. Zhu et al. [50] found that patients with deficient MMR (dMMR) had significantly better OS and DFS than those with pMMR. Dou et al. [51] demonstrated that the probability of MLM was significantly lower in patients with dMMR than in those with pMMR, consistent with the present results. Tumor markers are abnormally produced by malignant cells, and CEA is one of the earliest and most widely used markers. Several studies have indicated that a high preoperative CEA level is significantly associated with MLM [52, 53], which is also consistent with the present results. Several studies have shown that higher T and N stages are significantly associated with CRCLM [54, 55], also aligning with the current results.

Additionally, single indicators have limitations in clinical applications, making the combined detection of multiple indicators a key focus in clinical practice. This study employed a nomogram to develop a predictive model incorporating five indicators: preoperative VEGF, preoperative CEA, T stage, N stage, and MMR status. These indicators have been validated for their good predictive value for MLM in CRC. Several studies have used nomograms to predict CRCLM. Liu et al. [56] developed a nomogram based on tumor location, vascular invasion, T stage, N stage, preoperative CEA levels, and CA-199 levels to predict SLM. Yimin et al. [57] included age, primary tumor site, histologic grade, T stage, N stage, and preoperative CEA level in a nomogram to predict SLM in early-onset CRC. Hao et al. [58] developed a nomogram that included age, CEA, vascular invasion, T stage, N stage, family history of cancer, and KRAS for predicting MLM. However, few studies have included preoperative VEGF and postoperative MMR status in a nomogram specifically designed for MLM prediction.

This study had certain limitations. First, The VEGF-related gene family includes five glycoproteins (VEGF-A, VEGF-B, VEGF-C, VEGF-D, and VEGF-E) and two placental growth factors (PLGF1 and PLGF-2). VEGF-A, commonly known as VEGF [59], was the focus of the present study. However, no other factors were studied owing to the limitations of the detection conditions at this center. Second, most patients with stage II–III disease received postoperative chemotherapy. Differences in chemotherapy regimens or responses are not included in this study, which may have influenced the MLM risk to some extent. We hope to further explore the relationship between MLM risks and differences in chemotherapy regimens or responses in future studies. Third, although the developed nomogram incorporates five preoperative and postoperative indicators, crucial information such as the expression status of other biomarkers (e.g., KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF) was not fully collected, possibly introducing bias. And preoperative biomarkers reported recently, such as Serum Ca2+ [60], inflammatory-related indicators [61] and nutritional-related indicators [62] have shown good predictive value for the prognosis of colorectal cancer, but their predictive value for MLM remains unclear. This study did not include all these indicators, and further multi-center research is needed. Fourth, this was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively short follow-up period.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study suggests that a preoperative serum VEGF level > 436.81 pg/mL is an independent risk factor for MLM in patients with CRC and can be used to predict MLM. A novel nomogram was developed to predict the risk of MLM in patients with CRC, demonstrating good accuracy and clinical application value. The nomogram may help clinicians identify patients with CRC who are at high risk of MLM earlier, enabling close monitoring and timely intervention. However, further validation through larger sample sizes, multicenter studies, and longer follow-up durations is warranted.
