Sleep-related symptoms and cognitive fatigue are prominent in the multidimensional symptom network of children with malignant solid tumors: key targets and subgroup differences
Authors: Shuting Yuan, Lu Yu, Lin Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildren with malignant solid tumors often experience cognitive, emotional, sleep, and fatigue-related problems, but the structural relationships among these symptoms domains remain insufficiently understood. This study used network analysis to characterize the multidimensional symptom network, identify structurally important nodes, and compare network heterogeneity across clinical subgroups to inform stratified supportive care.MethodsUsing convenience sampling, we recruited 230 children aged 6–18 years with malignant solid tumors from a tertiary children’s hospital in Chongqing, China. Cognition, emotion, sleep, and fatigue were assessed using the Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children-Fourth Edition (WISC-IV) Chinese version (short form), the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), the Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC), and the PedsQLTM Multidimensional Fatigue Scale (PedsQLTM MFS), respectively. A Gaussian graphical model was estimated, followed by analyses of centrality, bridge centrality, network stability, and subgroup network comparisons.ResultsChildren with malignant solid tumors showed a substantial multidimensional symptom burden. Sleep problems had the highest prevalence (63.48%), followed by anxiety (51.74%), depression (29.13%), and below-average cognitive performance (44.78%, FSIQ < 90), together with a relatively high fatigue burden. In the symptom network, “Disorders of Excessive Somnolence” (S5-DOES), “Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep” (S1-DIMS), and “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) showed high centrality. Among the bridge metrics, “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) was the most prominent, followed by “Anxiety” (E1-Anx) and “Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep” (S1-DIMS). Subgroup comparisons indicated significant differences in both network structure and global strength between tumor-type groups, whereas cognitive-status groups differed primarily in network structure, underscoring clinical heterogeneity in symptom organization.ConclusionsCognitive, emotional, sleep, and fatigue symptoms in children with malignant solid tumors formed an interconnected multidimensional symptom network. Sleep-related symptoms and “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) showed high structural importance and may be candidate targets for priority monitoring and supportive care. Network heterogeneity by tumor type and cognitive status further suggests that symptom assessment and management may need to begin earlier and be tailored by clinical subgroup. Background

In recent years, the burden of childhood cancer in China has continued to rise. Malignant solid tumors account for 65.8% of newly diagnosed pediatric cancers, with an annual incidence of 79.0 per 1,000,000 children, and are the second leading cause of death in childhood [1, 2]. Although advances in diagnosis and treatment have increased overall survival to approximately 80% [3], the multidimensional symptom burden associated with the disease and its treatment remains a prominent challenge in clinical management [4]. Impairment is evident across cognitive, emotional, and physical domains. At the cognitive level, approximately 40%-60% of affected children develop cancer-related cognitive impairment (CRCI), with deficits in memory, attention, and executive function [5, 6]. At the emotional level, 89.2% of pediatric oncology patients experience anxiety, depression, or related distress, and this burden may persist into adulthood [7, 8]. At the physical level, fatigue affects approximately 73% of children during treatment, including severe fatigue in about 30%, whereas sleep disturbance has been reported in 74%-95% of cases [9, 10]. Importantly, these symptoms do not occur in isolation. Existing evidence suggests that disease- and treatment-related neurotoxicity, inflammatory responses, and neuroendocrine dysregulation may jointly affect cognition, sleep, and emotional functioning [11, 12], while psychosocial factors such as prolonged hospitalization, school interruption, and caregiving burden may further aggravate fatigue and emotional distress [13, 14]. These multidimensional symptoms may therefore be better understood within a unified framework.

However, previous studies [15, 16] have generally examined cognition, emotion, sleep, and fatigue as relatively independent outcomes, reporting prevalence or associated factors separately or analyzing relationships only at the level of total scale scores. Although such work is useful for describing symptom burden, it provides limited insight into the structural relationships among symptoms and makes it difficult to identify central symptoms within the overall system or bridge symptoms linking different symptom domains. By contrast, network analysis can estimate conditional associations after controlling for all remaining nodes and can identify central nodes, bridge nodes, and structural differences between networks, thereby providing a methodological basis for understanding multidimensional symptom structure and prioritizing clinical attention [17]. To date, symptom-network studies in pediatric oncology have focused mainly on specific populations, such as childhood cancer survivors [18] and children with acute leukemia [11], whereas multidimensional symptom networks in children with malignant solid tumors remain insufficiently studied.

Clinical heterogeneity also warrants attention. Children with central nervous system (CNS) tumors and those with non-central nervous system (Non-CNS) tumors differ in tumor location, degree of nervous system involvement, and treatment exposure [19]. These differences may contribute to distinct patterns of connections across sleep, cognition, emotion, and fatigue. In addition, children with lower cognitive functioning may show reduced information-processing efficiency and more limited adaptation to learning demands, and may be more vulnerable to emotional problems and daytime functional difficulties, which may in turn shape their symptom burden and patterns of association [20]. These considerations support comparing symptom networks across tumor-type and cognitive-status subgroups. This study aimed to: (1) describe the overall burden of cognition, emotion, sleep, and fatigue-related problems in children with malignant solid tumors; (2) construct a multidimensional symptom network and identify core nodes, key bridge nodes, and important cross-domain connections; and (3) compare network differences across tumor-type and cognitive-status subgroups to inform stratified symptom management.

Methods

Study participants

Using convenience sampling, we recruited children with malignant solid tumors from the departments of surgical oncology and neurosurgery at a tertiary children’s hospital in Chongqing, China, between September 2025 and February 2026. Eligible participants were aged 6–18 years, had a diagnosis of malignant solid tumor, were conscious, had basic communication and comprehension abilities, and voluntarily agreed to participate. Children were excluded if they had severe physical dysfunction or other major non-oncologic organic diseases that precluded completion of cognitive testing, or if either the child or guardian withdrew during the study period.

Based on sample-size recommendations for network analysis [21], the present study included 17 symptom nodes. The network model therefore involved 17 threshold parameters and 136 pairwise association parameters [17 × (17 − 1) / 2], for a total of 153 parameters. Allowing for a 10% non-response rate, the required sample size was at least 170. A total of 242 questionnaires were distributed. After quality control (exclusion of logically inconsistent and incomplete responses; Supplementary Figure S1), 230 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding an effective response rate of 95.04%. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (approval no. [2025] Ethical Review [Clinical Research] No. 351), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their guardians before testing.

Instruments

General information questionnaire

A general information questionnaire was developed by the research team on the basis of previous literature to collect sociodemographic and disease-related information for children with malignant solid tumors, including age, sex, educational level, disease duration, number of hospitalizations, number of chemotherapy cycles, treatment regimen, and clinical stage.

Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children-Fourth Edition (WISC-IV) Chinese version (short form)

Cognitive function was assessed using the Chinese short form of the WISC-IV revised by Li et al. [22]. The instrument is administered individually by qualified examiners to children aged 6 years and older and includes six core subtests: Similarities, Matrix Reasoning, Comprehension, Block Design, Digit Span, and Coding. Scoring indices include Full Scale IQ (FSIQ), as well as dimension scores such as Verbal Comprehension and Perceptual Reasoning, with FSIQ derived from converted subtest scores. According to the norms, an FSIQ of 90 or above indicates the average range, 80–89 the below-average range, 70–79 the borderline range, and 69 or below the extremely low range. Previous studies [23] have shown that the Chinese short form of the WISC-IV has good reliability and validity, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92; in the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.742.

In addition, on the basis of previous pediatric oncology studies of cognitive outcomes and the normative interpretation of the Chinese short form of the WISC-IV [24], we predefined FSIQ < 90 as a research-oriented cognitive stratification threshold to identify a subgroup with below-average cognitive performance for comparison.

Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7)

Anxiety symptoms were assessed with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder Self-Rating Scale-7 (GAD-7) [25]. The scale comprises seven items rated on a four-point Likert scale (0 = not at all; 1 = several days; 2 = more than half the days; 3 = nearly every day). Total scores are categorized as 0–4 (none), 5–9 (mild anxiety), 10–14 (moderate anxiety), and 15–21 (severe anxiety). The Chinese version of the GAD-7 has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.93 [26]; in the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.861.

Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9)

Depressive symptoms were assessed with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [27]. The scale comprises nine items scored from 0 to 3 using the same response format as the GAD-7 (0 = not at all; 1 = several days; 2 = more than half the days; 3 = nearly every day). Total scores are categorized as 0–4 (none), 5–9 (mild depression), 10–14 (moderate depression), 15–19 (moderately severe depression), and 20–27 (severe depression). The Chinese version of the PHQ-9 has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84 [27]; in the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.784.

Sleep Disturbance Scale for Children (SDSC)

The SDSC developed by Bruni et al. [28] was used to assess sleep disturbance in children aged 6 years and older. It comprises 26 items across six dimensions: Sleep Hyperhidrosis, Sleep-Wake Transition Disorders, Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep, Sleep-Breathing Disorders, Disorders of Arousal, and Disorders of Excessive Somnolence. Items are scored on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always), with higher scores indicating more severe sleep disturbance. A total score of 39 or above is commonly used to indicate sleep disturbance. The Chinese version of the SDSC has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.81 [29]; in the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.709.

PedsQLTM Multidimensional Fatigue Scale (PedsQLTM MFS)

The PedsQLTM Multidimensional Fatigue Scale, developed by Varni et al. [30] and translated into Chinese by Bu et al. [31], was used to assess fatigue over the previous month in children aged 2–18 years with cancer or other chronic diseases. It includes parent-proxy and child self-report forms and comprises three dimensions-General Fatigue, Sleep/Rest Fatigue, and Cognitive Fatigue-with six items in each dimension. Items are scored on a five-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 4 (almost always) and are reverse-scored and linearly transformed (0 = 100, 1 = 75, 2 = 50, 3 = 25, 4 = 0). Mean scores for the total scale and each dimension were used to reflect fatigue severity, with lower scores indicating more severe fatigue. The Chinese version of the PedsQLTM MFS has a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.915 [31]; in the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.721. Given the clinical characteristics of the sample, parent-proxy report was used throughout.

Symptom nodes

The symptom network comprised 17 continuous nodes across four domains: cognition, emotion, sleep, and fatigue. Specifically, the cognitive domain included the six WISC-IV short-form subtests, the sleep domain included the six SDSC subdimensions, the fatigue domain included the three PedsQLTM MFS subdimensions, and the emotional domain included the total scores of the GAD-7 and PHQ-9 as indicators of overall emotional burden. The primary analysis retained this 17-node framework to preserve subtest- or subdimension-level information while controlling network size and improving estimation stability. Statistical analysis

All data processing and analyses were conducted in Python 3.10. Continuous variables are summarized as means and standard deviations (SDs), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Before network estimation, the distributional characteristics of the 17 primary symptom nodes were examined, including univariate skewness, kurtosis, and multivariate normality. Because some nodes showed bounded or skewed distributions, Z-score standardization was used to place variables on a common scale, and a rank-based sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the influence of distributional deviations on the network estimates. Based on the standardized data, two-sample t tests were used to compare node-level scores between tumor-type groups and cognitive-status groups. All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Network model estimation

A Gaussian graphical model (GGM) was estimated to characterize the conditional association structure among symptoms. Graphical LASSO implemented in scikit-learn was used to regularize the partial correlation network, thereby reducing spurious associations and improving model interpretability [32]. The regularization parameter was selected using a custom implementation of EBICglasso. Specifically, the optimal solution was identified by searching 20 candidate alpha values on a logarithmic scale, with gamma set to 0.5, and the model with the minimum EBIC value was retained as the final network. In the network, nodes represent symptom indicators and edges represent unique associations between pairs of symptoms after conditioning on all remaining nodes; edge weights correspond to regularized partial correlation coefficients. Retained edges were those that remained non-zero after L1 regularization, and no additional manual thresholding was applied. Network visualization was performed using matplotlib and NetworkX [33].

Centrality and bridge metrics

The relative importance of nodes was quantified using centrality indices [34]. The primary metrics reported were strength centrality and expected influence, which reflect, respectively, the overall connection strength of a node and its potential influence within the network. To identify key connecting nodes across symptom domains, bridge strength, bridge expected influence, bridge betweenness, and bridge closeness were further calculated on the basis of four prespecified symptom communities. These bridge metrics were implemented programmatically according to the definitions proposed by Jones et al. [35]: bridge strength and bridge expected influence represent the sum of the absolute and signed edge weights, respectively, linking a node to other symptom communities, whereas bridge betweenness and bridge closeness are based on shortest paths across communities. A bridge network retaining only cross-domain edges was also constructed to visualize direct links between symptom domains. All metrics were interpreted primarily on the basis of relative ranking rather than fixed absolute cutoffs.

Network stability and sensitivity analyses

Resampling procedures were used to evaluate the accuracy and stability of the estimated network. A non-parametric bootstrap with 1,000 iterations was used to calculate 95% confidence intervals for edge weights, and a bootstrap with 300 iterations was used to estimate confidence intervals for centrality and bridge centrality metrics [34]. Case-dropping bootstrap was further used to assess the stability of centrality estimates, and correlation stability coefficients (CS-Cs) were reported. According to methodological recommendations, CS-C values should exceed 0.25, and values above 0.50 indicate good stability [35].

In addition, several sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the main findings. First, a covariate-adjusted residual network was estimated to control for potential confounding, with age, sex, tumor type, surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy included as covariates; tumor stage was not included because of the relatively high proportion of missing values. Second, to assess whether asymmetry in node granularity across symptom domains affected bridge centrality estimates, the two emotional total-score nodes in the main network were replaced by item-level GAD-7 and PHQ-9 nodes while retaining the original 15 non-emotional nodes. Because PHQ-9 item 9 (thoughts of being better off dead or of self-harm) showed no variance in this sample, it could not be included in GGM estimation; the final sensitivity network therefore contained 30 nodes. Third, informed by the distributional assessment, the 17 primary nodes were rank-transformed and the network was re-estimated; consistency with the primary network was then evaluated in terms of edge-weight structure and the ranking of key nodes.

Network comparison testing

Subgroup network comparisons were performed by tumor type and cognitive status. A permutation procedure based on the general framework of the Network Comparison Test (NCT) was used to assess differences between two networks in structure (M statistic), global strength (|S|), and specific edge weights, with P < 0.05 indicating statistical significance [36]. In each permutation, group sizes were held constant while group labels were randomly permuted and both networks were re-estimated to obtain the corresponding test statistics and P values. For cognitive subgroup comparisons, FSIQ < 90 was used as the primary grouping threshold, with FSIQ < 85 examined in sensitivity analysis.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Multidimensional symptoms were common, with sleep problems showing the highest prevalence

A total of 230 participants were included, comprising 119 boys (51.74%) and 111 girls (48.26%), with a mean age of 10.80 ± 2.89 years. Of these, 122 (53.04%) had Non-CNS tumors and 108 (46.96%) had CNS tumors. Overall, children with malignant solid tumors showed a marked multidimensional symptom burden, with sleep problems being the most prominent (63.48%). In addition, 51.74% had anxiety symptoms, 29.13% had depressive symptoms, and 44.78% had cognitive performance below the average range (FSIQ < 90), together with a relatively high fatigue burden (mean total score, 53.30). Significant differences in selected symptom nodes were observed across clinical subgroups

Significant differences at the node level were observed across clinical subgroups (P < 0.05). Compared with the Non-CNS group, the CNS group showed more severe “Sleep-Breathing Disorders” (S2-SBD), “Sleep-Wake Transition Disorders” (S4-SWTD), and “Disorders of Excessive Somnolence” (S5-DOES), indicating a more prominent sleep-related burden in this subgroup (Table S3). By contrast, the Non-CNS group had higher scores for “Anxiety” (E1-Anx), “Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep” (S1-DIMS), and “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF), suggesting that emotional distress, sleep-initiation and maintenance difficulties, and cognitive fatigue were more pronounced in this subgroup. Compared with the FSIQ ≥ 90 group, the FSIQ < 90 group had lower scores across cognitive nodes and greater anxiety, depression, and fatigue burden (Table S4), indicating a broader multidimensional symptom burden among children with lower cognitive status.

The overall network showed clear within-domain clustering and cross-domain connections

The symptom network linking cognition, emotion, sleep, and fatigue in children with malignant solid tumors is shown in Fig. 1A. The overall network comprised 17 nodes and 136 possible edges, of which 57 were non-zero (41.91%), with a mean edge weight of 0.026, indicating moderate overall connectivity. Overall, nodes within the same symptom domain tended to cluster closely. For example, strong within-domain edges were observed between “Similarities” (C1-SI) and “Comprehension” (C2-CO) in the cognitive domain, “General Fatigue” (F1-GF) and “Sleep/Rest Fatigue” (F2-SRF) in the fatigue domain, and “Anxiety” (E1-Anx) and “Depression” (E2-Dep) in the emotional domain. Clear cross-domain associations were also observed, with the strongest cross-domain edge between “Depression” (E2-Dep) and “General Fatigue” (F1-GF), suggesting a strong structural association between emotional symptoms and fatigue (Fig. 1B). Because the PedsQLTM MFS is reverse-scored, with lower scores indicating more severe fatigue, negative edges involving fatigue nodes may clinically correspond to positive co-occurrence of symptom burden. Network structures for subgroup analyses were broadly similar and are shown in Figures S2-S3.

Sleep-related nodes showed high centrality, and “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) showed the highest bridge strength

In the overall network, “Disorders of Excessive Somnolence” (S5-DOES) had the highest strength centrality (Fig. 2), followed by “Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep” (S1-DIMS) and “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF), indicating that sleep-related symptoms and cognitive fatigue occupied prominent structural positions in the network. “Comprehension” (C2-CO) had the highest expected influence, followed by “Sleep-Breathing Disorders” (S2-SBD) and “Block Design” (C3-BD), suggesting that some cognition-related indicators also played an influential role in the overall connectivity pattern. Subgroup analyses (Figures S4-S7) showed some variation in centrality rankings across clinical subgroups, but sleep- and fatigue-related nodes generally remained highly ranked in most networks. Bridge centrality analysis further showed that “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) consistently had high bridge strength in the overall sample (Fig. 3) and across subgroup networks (Figures S8-S9), indicating that it may represent a structurally prominent candidate bridge node linking different symptom domains.

The estimated network showed good stability and robustness

Bootstrap analyses showed that, in the overall sample (Figures S10-S14) and across subgroup networks (Figures S15-S18), CS-C coefficients for centrality and bridge centrality were all above 0.50, indicating overall stability in the ranking of key nodes (Table 3). In the covariate-adjusted residual network, edge weights were highly correlated with those of the original network (r = 0.928), and the rankings of highly central and bridge nodes were broadly consistent, supporting the robustness of the main network (Figure S19).

Distributional checks of the 17 primary nodes showed that some variables had skewed or bounded distributions. Mardia’s test further indicated that the assumption of multivariate normality was not fully satisfied (all P < 0.001; Table S5). Accordingly, item-level and rank-based sensitivity analyses were performed, and both were broadly consistent with the primary analysis. In the emotion item-level sensitivity analysis, “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) retained the highest bridge strength in both the item-level emotion network and the rank-based item-level network, and the bridge rankings of the 15 common non-emotional nodes showed high consistency between the main network and the item-level sensitivity network (Tables S6-S8; Figures S20-S23). In the rank-based supplementary analysis, the rank-based network remained highly consistent with the main network in edge-weight structure (r = 0.980), and node rankings for strength and bridge strength also showed high concordance (Spearman rho = 0.949 and 0.956, respectively). “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) remained the node with the highest bridge strength, and the rankings of key nodes such as “Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep” (S1-DIMS), “Anxiety” (E1-Anx), and “General Fatigue” (F1-GF) were broadly stable (Table S9; Figure S24).

Symptom networks differed significantly by tumor type and cognitive status

Network comparison results (Table 4) indicated clear heterogeneity across clinical subgroups. The CNS and Non-CNS groups differed significantly in both network structure and global strength, suggesting differences in both the organization and the overall connectedness of multidimensional symptoms across tumor types. Meanwhile, the FSIQ < 90 and FSIQ ≥ 90 groups differed in network structure but not in global strength, suggesting that the below-average cognitive-performance group was characterized primarily by altered symptom organization rather than greater overall connectivity. Re-grouping with the stricter threshold of FSIQ < 85 yielded results in the same direction (Table S10; Figure S25).

Discussion

Children with malignant solid tumors had a substantial multidimensional symptom burden, with sleep problems most prominent

This study showed that children with malignant solid tumors had a substantial multidimensional symptom burden, with sleep problems most prominent. Anxiety, depression, below-average cognitive performance, and a high fatigue burden were also common [37], consistent with previous reports [6–10]. This burden may reflect the combined influence of biological factors, such as disease- and treatment-related neurotoxicity and inflammatory responses, and psychosocial stressors, including prolonged hospitalization, nighttime care, illness uncertainty, and caregiving burden [38]. Supportive care should therefore move beyond a single-symptom focus and incorporate continued screening and dynamic assessment of sleep, emotion, cognition, and fatigue, particularly during early treatment and hospitalization.

Sleep-related nodes were structurally prominent in the symptom network

In this study, “Disorders of Excessive Somnolence” (S5-DOES), “Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep” (S1-DIMS), and “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) ranked among the highest nodes in strength centrality, and sleep problems had the highest prevalence in the descriptive analysis. Together, these findings suggest that sleep-related symptoms were common and closely connected to other nodes in the network. Because centrality reflects only the relative structural position of nodes within the current network, clinical interpretation should remain cautious [39]. Even so, the findings indicate that sleep problems may warrant priority clinical attention. Clinically, “Disorders of Excessive Somnolence” (S5-DOES) may reflect daytime sleepiness and activity restriction after poor nocturnal sleep, whereas “Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep” (S1-DIMS) may capture difficulty falling asleep, difficulty maintaining sleep, and circadian disruption during treatment, and may also relate to ward environmental disturbance, pain, and heightened emotional arousal [29]. These findings support routine screening for difficulty initiating sleep, difficulty maintaining sleep, and daytime sleepiness during key treatment phases, together with pain management, optimization of nighttime care, sleep-hygiene education, and, when needed, psychological or behavioral support. Whether such strategies lead to broader symptom improvement remains to be tested in future studies.

“Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) may be a candidate bridge node across symptom domains

Bridge centrality analysis showed that “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) had the highest bridge strength in the overall sample and across subgroup networks, followed by “Anxiety” (E1-Anx) and “Disorders of Initiating and Maintaining Sleep” (S1-DIMS). Bridge metrics reflect conditional association patterns between symptom domains and do not, by themselves, establish the direction of symptom spread or the sequence of intervention [39]. Nonetheless, the stable bridge role of “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) suggests that it may be a clinically relevant node in cross-domain symptom associations. Clinically, cognitive fatigue may present as difficulty sustaining attention, reduced thinking efficiency, and greater burden in learning and communication, and may be related to treatment-related neurotoxicity, limited family support, restricted daily activity, and difficulties in emotional coping [40–42]. Brief assessment of cognitive fatigue, daytime executive functioning, and participation in learning may therefore complement routine fatigue screening and be interpreted alongside caregiver observations and neuropsychological performance. Future longitudinal and intervention studies are needed to determine whether cognitive fatigue exerts a stable temporal linking role across symptom domains and whether targeted management of this symptom can also improve sleep and emotional outcomes.

Network heterogeneity suggests the need for stratified assessment and supportive management

Network comparison results indicated significant heterogeneity across clinical subgroups. Compared with the Non-CNS group, the CNS group showed stronger global connectivity. Compared with the FSIQ ≥ 90 group, the FSIQ < 90 group differed mainly in symptom organization. Notably, FSIQ < 90 was used here as a research-oriented stratification threshold, and the direction of findings remained consistent in sensitivity analysis using the stricter cutoff of FSIQ < 85. This provides some support for the robustness of the cognitive-status comparison, although confirmation in larger samples remains necessary.

Taken together, the network comparison results and node-level differences suggest that the stronger overall connectivity observed in children with CNS tumors may reflect the joint effects of tumor-site-related nervous system involvement and treatment exposure on sleep-wake regulation and neurobehavioral functioning, making sleep problems, fatigue, and related daytime functional difficulties more likely to cluster [43, 44]. Meanwhile, in children with FSIQ < 90, heterogeneity appeared more likely to be expressed in symptom organization, with node-level findings also indicating more prominent anxiety, depression, specific sleep dimensions, and fatigue symptoms [45]. These findings suggest that children with CNS tumors may benefit from earlier combined assessment of sleep, fatigue, and related daytime functioning, whereas children with lower cognitive functioning may require concurrent screening for emotional, sleep, and fatigue problems alongside cognitive assessment to enable early supportive care for those with high symptom burden.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single-center cross-sectional design with convenience sampling limits the generalizability of the findings and precludes inference regarding the temporal ordering or causal direction among cognitive, emotional, sleep, and fatigue symptoms. Second, node granularity was not fully consistent across symptom domains, which may affect the clinical interpretation of centrality and bridge metrics. Third, emotional symptoms were assessed primarily by child self-report, whereas fatigue was assessed mainly by caregiver report, so measurement sources were not fully aligned; among pediatric patients with lower cognitive functioning, such multi-informant assessment may introduce both self-report comprehension bias and observer-report bias, thereby affecting the precision of the estimated symptom nodes. In addition, the internal consistency of the Chinese short form of the WISC-IV in this sample was lower than that reported by the developers, suggesting that parameter estimates in the cognitive domain may have been affected by measurement error. Finally, subgroup sample sizes were limited and the age range was broad, which may influence the stability of stratified network comparisons. Some bridge centrality and network comparison analyses were implemented using custom Python scripts; although these scripts are publicly available, their equivalence to established R implementations still requires external validation. Future multicenter studies with larger samples and longitudinal designs, together with objective sleep monitoring and more comprehensive neuropsychological assessment, are needed to further test these findings.

Conclusions

From a network perspective, cognitive, emotional, sleep, and fatigue-related problems in children with malignant solid tumors were not isolated but formed an interconnected multidimensional symptom system. Sleep-related symptoms occupied prominent positions in the overall network, whereas “Cognitive Fatigue” (F3-CF) may link different symptom domains. Symptom networks also showed heterogeneity by tumor type and cognitive status, suggesting that stratified assessment and supportive care should take both factors into account. These findings may help identify highly burdensome symptoms and priority subgroups in this population, although confirmation in larger samples and longitudinal studies remains necessary.
