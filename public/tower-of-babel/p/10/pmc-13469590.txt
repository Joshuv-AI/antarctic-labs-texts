CFD overexpression inhibits CESC progression and enhances TIL therapy efficacy via NOSTRIN and eNOS signaling pathway
Authors: Wei Zhang, Dong Li, Shan Liu, Jiying Tang, Xiaojun Cai, Zhigang Zuo, Chaofu Li, Yuhan Liu, Langhong Zeng, Yi Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC) represents the second most commonly diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide, with substantial incidence and mortality rates, particularly in regions where breast cancer is not predominant. Recent advances in tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy have demonstrated promising clinical efficacy in CESC. This study elucidated the role of complement factor D (CFD) in CESC and evaluated its potential as a therapeutic target. Bioinformatic analysis of the GSE39001 dataset revealed significant downregulation of CFD in CESC tissues, a finding subsequently validated in 43 paired tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples. Functional assays demonstrated that CFD overexpression in CESC cell lines (SiHa and C33A) substantially suppressed cell proliferation, viability, and invasion, while concurrently attenuating CD8 + T cell exhaustion. Mechanistically, CFD overexpression upregulated nitric oxide synthase trafficking inducer (NOSTRIN) and suppressed the endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) signaling pathway, as confirmed through RNA sequencing and co-immunoprecipitation assays. Furthermore, the combination of TIL therapy with CFD overexpression significantly suppressed tumor growth in a PDX model. These findings identify CFD as a potential therapeutic target for CESC and suggest that CFD overexpression may enhance the efficacy of TIL therapy in this malignancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54406-2.Keywords: Cervical squamous cell carcinoma, Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, Complement factor D, NOSTRIN, eNOS signaling pathwaySubject terms: Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Oncology

Introduction

Cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC) ranks as the second most frequently diagnosed cancer among women globally, representing a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality, particularly in regions where breast cancer incidence is not predominant1. In recent years, significant progress has been achieved in the application of tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy for CESC. TIL therapy, an immunotherapeutic approach involving the extraction, ex vivo expansion, and reinfusion of lymphocytes derived from a patient’s tumor tissue, has demonstrated promising clinical efficacy across various solid tumors, particularly in CESC and other human papillomavirus (HPV)-associated malignancies2.

In a landmark clinical trial investigating TIL therapy in patients with recurrent CESC, researchers administered TIL infusion following lymphodepletion preconditioning, eliciting notable tumor responses. The findings revealed that TIL therapy maintained T cell persistence and proliferative capacity even without high-dose interleukin-2 (IL-2), thereby reducing treatment-related adverse effects3. Additionally, another study examined TIL therapy application in HPV-associated epithelial cancers, demonstrating that this approach induced complete tumor regression in some patients, with sustained remission lasting several years in certain cases—suggesting the potential for long-term efficacy of TIL therapy in CESC4. Consequently, investigating the underlying mechanisms of TIL treatment in CESC is of considerable scientific importance.

Previous studies have demonstrated that tumor-infiltrating CD8 + T cells in CESC frequently exhibit an exhausted phenotype characterized by upregulation of inhibitory receptors including PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG35. The interaction between PD-1 and its ligand PD-L1, along with the expression of alternative checkpoint molecules TIM-3 and LAG3, has been implicated in T cell dysfunction and poor response to immunotherapy in CESC6. The infiltration of CD8 + T cells within the immune microenvironment of CESC is also closely associated with patient prognosis. Accumulating evidence indicates that tumor-infiltrating CD8 + T cells serve as critical prognostic biomarkers and are associated with immunotherapy response. First, the expression of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1) is significantly correlated with CD8 + T cell infiltration in CESC. IDO1 is upregulated in CESC and positively correlates with the infiltration of immune cells including CD8 + T cells, CD4 + T cells, and dendritic cells, suggesting that IDO1 may influence CESC prognosis through modulation of immune cell infiltration7. Second, the TIGIT/CD155 signaling pathway plays a crucial role in CESC pathogenesis. Blockade of TIGIT/CD155 signaling restores CD8 + T cell functionality and enhances antitumor activity. High TIGIT expression promotes CD8 + T cell exhaustion, whereas inhibition of this pathway increases cytokine production capacity, thereby augmenting the immune response8. Furthermore, the infiltration of CD103 + T cells in CESC is associated with patient prognosis. CD103, a biomarker labeling intraepithelial T cells, exhibits strong correlation with CD8 + T cell markers and is predominantly localized in the epithelial regions of CESC. High CD103 + cell infiltration is closely linked to improved prognosis, particularly in patients receiving radiotherapy, suggesting that CD103 may serve as a promising marker for assessing immune responses in CESC9. Therefore, identifying novel differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with CD8 + T cell infiltration in CESC is imperative.

Complement Factor D (CFD) is a serine protease synthesized predominantly by adipocytes and macrophages, functioning as the rate-limiting enzyme that cleaves Factor B to initiate the alternative complement cascade. Beyond its classical role in innate immunity, emerging evidence indicates that CFD participates in metabolic regulation and vascular homeostasis. Recent studies have implicated CFD dysregulation in various malignancies10,11; however, its specific expression pattern and mechanistic role in CESC remain undefined. In the present study, we identified that CFD is significantly downregulated in CESC patients and is associated with CD8 + T cell infiltration. Overexpression of CFD inhibited proliferation, invasion, and migration of CESC cells while concurrently attenuating CD8 + T cell exhaustion. Transcriptomic sequencing of cervical cancer cells overexpressing CFD revealed that nitric oxide synthase trafficking inducer (NOSTRIN) is among the most significantly upregulated genes. Enrichment analysis demonstrated that DEGs were significantly enriched in the eNOS signaling pathway. Moreover, in vivo studies demonstrated that the combination of TIL therapy with CFD overexpression significantly suppressed the growth of CESC xenografts.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

SiHa and C33A cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC) and authenticated using short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. The medium was filter-sterilized using a 0.22 μm membrane filter and stored at 4 °C until use.

Differential expression analysis of GSE39001 dataset

The gene expression dataset GSE39001 was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma package in R. A linear model was fitted to normalized expression data, and empirical Bayes moderation was applied to estimate gene-wise variances. Contrasts were defined to compare experimental groups of interest (tumor vs. normal). DEGs were identified based on a log2 fold change (log2FC) threshold of |log2FC| > 1 and an adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05.

Patient samples

A total of 43 paired samples of CESC tissues and adjacent normal tissues were collected from patients undergoing surgical resection. Tissues were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until RNA extraction.

In vitro CD8 + T cell preparation and activation

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from 43 CESC patients using Ficoll-Paque Plus (GE Healthcare, USA). CD8 + T cells were purified by incubating PBMCs with CD8 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and passing the mixture through a MACS Column (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). Purified CD8 + T cells were eluted from the column and collected. For activation, isolated CD8 + T cells (1 × 106) were stimulated with Cloudz CD3/CD28 (R&D Systems, USA) and recombinant IL-2 (Abcam, UK) in vitro. Subsequently, after direct co-culture treatment for 24 h, CD8 + T cells were harvested for subsequent analyses.

Direct co-culture system and supernatant collection

For functional assays involving CD8 + T cell interactions, CESC cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded in 6-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Activated CD8 + T cells (1 × 10⁶ cells/well) were then added directly to the wells to establish a direct co-culture system. After 24 h of co-culture, the supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 2,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cellular debris. The clarified supernatants were subsequently concentrated approximately 20-fold using 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff centrifugal filter units (Amicon Ultra, Millipore, USA) at 4,000 × g. The concentrated samples were then subjected to Western blotting analysis for CFD detection.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered suitable for further analysis. RNA integrity was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis or using a Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara) with oligo(dT) primers, following the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System. The following primer sequences were used: CFD Forward: 5’-ATGCAGCGTTAGCAGCTAGTG-3’, Reverse: 5’-GGCCTGTTAATGGCACGATATG-3’; NOSTRIN Forward: 5’-CAGAAAGACACAGCAGCGTTA-3’, Reverse: 5’-CAGAAGATGCCTTGCTCACAATA-3’; PD-L1 Forward: 5’-GCTGCACTAATTGTCTATTGGGA-3’, Reverse: 5’-AATTCGCTTGTAGTCGGCACC-3’. GAPDH was used as an internal control with the following primers: Forward: 5’-ACAACTTTGGTATCGTGGAAGG-3’, Reverse: 5’-GCCATCACGCCACAGTTTC-3’. The qPCR conditions consisted of an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final melting curve analysis ranging from 60 °C to 95 °C at 0.5 °C increments. Relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blotting analysis

For supernatant samples, proteins were concentrated from conditioned media using 10 kDa MWCO centrifugal filters (Amicon Ultra) as described above. For protein detection in tissues and cells, samples were homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor cocktail. The supernatant was collected after centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit. Subsequently, 30 µg of protein were denatured in Laemmli sample buffer at 95 °C for 5 min and separated by SDS-PAGE on a 10% polyacrylamide gel at 120 V for 90 min. Proteins were then transferred to a PVDF membrane using a wet transfer system at 300 mA for 90 min. The membrane was blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies: anti-CFD (ab204917, dilution 1:2000, Abcam), anti-PD-L1 (PA5-20343, 2 µg/mL, Invitrogen), anti-NOSTRIN (PA5-113121, dilution 1:500, Invitrogen), anti-eNOS (PA1-037, dilution 1:1000, Invitrogen), and anti-phospho-eNOS (MA5-14957, dilution 1:1000, Invitrogen). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (ab6721, dilution 1:2000, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection kit and imaged with a chemiluminescence imaging system.

To validate the interaction between CFD and NOSTRIN in CESC cells, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) was performed. For immunoprecipitation, 500 µg of total protein was incubated with 2 µg of anti-CFD (PA5-47208, dilution 1:100, Invitrogen) or anti-NOSTRIN (sc-365296, dilution 1:50, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) antibody, or control IgG overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Protein A/G agarose beads for 4 h at 4 °C. The beads were washed three times with cold lysis buffer, and bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 2× Laemmli sample buffer. The eluted proteins were subsequently separated and analyzed by western blotting.

Vector construction and cell transfection

For CFD overexpression, the recombinant plasmid vector pENTER carrying CFD or control vector was purchased from Vigene (China). Lentiviral packaging, infection, and puromycin selection were performed as previously described12. To construct the knockdown plasmid, shRNA molecules targeting NOSTRIN were cloned into the pGPU6/GFP/puromycin vector. The designated vectors were transfected into SiHa and C33A cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA).

CCK-8 assay

CESC cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in 100 µL of medium and allowed to adhere overnight. After adherence, cells were subjected to the desired experimental conditions in triplicate. Wells containing only culture medium (without cells) were included as blank controls. Following treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo Molecular Technologies) was added to each well. The plate was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 4 h. Subsequently, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Background correction was performed by subtracting the absorbance of blank control wells from experimental wells. Cell viability was calculated using the following formula: Cell viability (%) = [(Absorbance of treated cells − Absorbance of blank) / (Absorbance of untreated cells − Absorbance of blank)] × 100. Cytotoxicity was calculated using the formula: Cytotoxicity (%) = [(Absorbance of untreated cells − Absorbance of treated cells) / (Absorbance of untreated cells − Absorbance of blank)] × 100.

EdU assay

To assess cervical cancer cell proliferation, cells were incubated with 10 µM EdU for 2 h at 37 °C to incorporate EdU into newly synthesized DNA. Cells were then washed twice with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min. After washing, the Click-iT EdU reaction cocktail (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and cells were incubated with the cocktail for 30 min protected from light. Following the reaction, cells were washed twice with PBS and counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (1 µg/mL) for 10 min to visualize nuclei. Images were captured using a fluorescence microscope at 20× magnification. The number of EdU-positive cells (proliferating cells) and Hoechst-positive cells (total cells) was counted using ImageJ software. The proliferation rate was calculated as the percentage of EdU-positive cells relative to the total cell number.

Transwell assay

For migration assays, cells were trypsinized, resuspended in serum-free medium, and seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell insert (8 μm pore size) at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well, while the lower chamber was filled with medium containing 10% FBS as a chemoattractant. For invasion assays, the upper chamber was pre-coated with Matrigel (diluted 1:8 in serum-free medium) and incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to form a gel layer; cells were then seeded as described for the migration assay. After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h (migration) or 48 h (invasion), non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with a cotton swab. Cells that had migrated or invaded to the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min at room temperature. Images of stained cells were captured using an inverted microscope at 100× magnification, and the number of migrated or invaded cells was counted in five randomly selected fields per well using ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry

Following 3-day experimental treatment, CD8 + T cells were harvested from the co-culture system and subjected to Fc-receptor blocking and Golgi-stop treatment. Single-cell suspensions were incubated with surface markers including CD8 (BD Biosciences, 564526), CD107a (BD Biosciences, 571927), TIM-3 (BD Biosciences, 565558), LAG3 (BD Biosciences, 565719), and PD-1 (BD Biosciences, 561272) at 4 °C for 30 min. Finally, cells were analyzed using a BD FACSVerse Flow Cytometer.

For assessment of tumor-intrinsic PD-L1 expression by flow cytometry, the adherent CESC cells remaining in the wells after T cell removal were gently washed twice with PBS to eliminate residual lymphocytes, then detached using non-enzymatic cell dissociation solution (Gibco) and filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Tumor cells were subsequently incubated with anti-PD-L1 (BD Biosciences, 572226) at 4 °C for 30 min, followed by analysis on the BD FACSVerse Flow Cytometer. Tumor cells were gated based on forward/side scatter properties distinct from lymphocytes, excluding CD8-positive events.

PCR array screening

To investigate the effects of CFD overexpression on gene expression profiles in CESC cells, total RNA was extracted from CFD-overexpressing C33A cells and negative control cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, and samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered suitable for further analysis. RNA integrity was confirmed by agarose gel electrophoresis. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the RT² First Strand Kit (Qiagen, 330401) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The cDNA was subsequently mixed with RT² SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Qiagen, 330529) and aliquoted into the wells of RT² Profiler™ PCR Arrays (Qiagen) targeting cancer signaling pathway genes or specific disease-related gene panels. Quantitative PCR was performed on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with the following thermal cycling conditions: initial activation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s and combined annealing/extension at 60 °C for 1 min. A melting curve analysis ranging from 60 °C to 95 °C at 0.5 °C increments was performed to verify amplification specificity. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Genes with |fold change| > 2.0 and p < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed using DAVID bioinformatics resources and the Qiagen online analysis tool.

Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models and autologous TIL therapy

NOD-SCID IL2Rγnull (NSG/NDG) mice (female, 5–6 weeks old) were purchased from Charles River Laboratories (Beijing, China) and housed in a pathogen-free environment. PDX models were established from a CESC recurrence patient. Fresh tumor fragments (approximately 2–3 mm³, ~ 20–30 mg) were implanted subcutaneously into the right flank under isoflurane anesthesia, and the PDX model was established using first-passage patient-derived tissue without prior in vivo expansion to preserve original tumor characteristics. After tumor implantation, mice bearing xenografts reaching approximately 30 mm3 in volume were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5 per group): (1) Control group; (2) LV-NC (intratumoral injection of negative control lentiviral plasmid, 1 × 10⁸ TU/mL in 100 µL, without TIL); (3) LV-CFD (intratumoral injection of CFD overexpression lentiviral plasmid, 1 × 10⁸ TU/mL in 100 µL, without TIL); (4) TIL group (mice received autologous TILs generated from the same patient), (5) TIL + lv-CFD group (tumor injection with CFD overexpression lentiviral plasmid in TIL group), and (6) TIL + lv-NC group (tumor injection with negative control lentiviral plasmid in TIL group). On day 0, mice in the control group received 0.2 mL cryoprotectant, while mice in the TIL group received 1.64 × 107 TILs suspended in 0.2 mL cryoprotectant. Tumor volume was measured weekly for four weeks. Subsequently, tumor tissues were collected from all mice after euthanasia by isoflurane inhalation.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Tumor tissues collected from the PDX mouse model were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h at room temperature. Fixed tissues were then embedded in paraffin wax (Leica Biosystems) and sectioned into 5 μm thick slices using a microtome (Leica RM2255, Leica Biosystems). Sections were deparaffinized in xylene (Sigma-Aldrich) and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 70%) for 5 min each. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0; Abcam, ab6421) at 95 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubating sections in 3% hydrogen peroxide (Sigma-Aldrich) for 10 min at room temperature. Non-specific binding was blocked by incubating sections in 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min at room temperature. Sections were then incubated with primary anti-CD8 antibody (ab316778, dilution 1:100, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with PBS, sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit IgG, ab150081, dilution 1:200, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Sigma-Aldrich) at a concentration of 1 µg/mL for 5 min. Finally, sections were mounted with coverslips, and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51, Olympus).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tissues were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, 70%), followed by antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 20 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, and non-specific binding was blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin for 30 min. Sections were then incubated with primary anti-PD-L1 antibody (PA5-20343, Invitrogen) overnight at 4 °C, followed by 1-hour incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature. Signal detection was performed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine as the chromogen. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with coverslips.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 24.0. Student’s two-tailed t-test and Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used for comparisons between two groups, while one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons. Two-way ANOVA was employed for analyzing data involving two independent variables. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was generated by plotting the true positive rate (sensitivity) against the false positive rate (1 − specificity) at various CFD expression thresholds. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to evaluate the diagnostic performance of CFD. For all analyses, a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) as indicated.

Results

Downregulation of CFD expression in CESC tissues correlates with reduced CD8 + T cell infiltration and exhibits diagnostic potential

Bioinformatic analysis of the GSE39001 dataset identified marked downregulation of the CFD gene in patients with CESC (Fig. 1A). To corroborate these findings, paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues were collected from 43 cervical cancer patients. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed significantly reduced CFD mRNA expression in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Fig. 1B, fold change ≈ 0.5, P < 0.001). ROC curve analysis further demonstrated that reduced CFD levels exhibit diagnostic potential for CESC (AUC = 0.9200, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1C). Western blot analysis confirmed the downregulation of CFD protein expression in tumor tissues (Fig. 1D and E, fold change ≈ 0.25, P < 0.001). Moreover, immune cell infiltration analysis utilizing the TIMER2.0 database revealed a positive correlation between CFD expression and CD8 + T cell infiltration (Fig. 1F).

Overexpression of CFD inhibits proliferation, viability, and invasion of CESC cells and attenuates CD8 + T cell exhaustion

To investigate the functional role of CFD in CESC, we overexpressed CFD in CESC cell lines (Fig. 2A, fold change ≈ 5, P < 0.001). CCK-8 and EdU assays revealed that CFD overexpression significantly suppressed cell viability (Fig. 2B, P < 0.001) and proliferation (Fig. 2C and D, P < 0.001). Furthermore, Transwell assays demonstrated that CFD overexpression markedly inhibited the invasion and migration capabilities of CESC cells (Fig. 2E and H, P < 0.001). These findings suggest that CFD acts as a tumor suppressor in CESC by regulating key cellular processes associated with tumor progression. Notably, these inhibitory effects were cancer cell-specific, as CFD overexpression in the non-malignant cervical epithelial cell line Ect1/E6E7 did not significantly alter cell proliferation, viability, or migratory/invasive capacity (Supplementary Figure S1, P ˃ 0.05).

(B) CCK-8 assay showing reduced cell viability in CFD-overexpressing C33A and SiHa cells compared to control cells. (C, D) EdU assay demonstrating decreased proliferation in CFD-overexpressing C33A and SiHa cells. Representative images (C) and quantitative analysis (D) are shown. (E-H) Transwell assays showing impaired invasion and migration in CFD-overexpressing C33A and SiHa cells. Representative images and quantitative analysis are presented. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent biological replicates (n = 3). Statistical significance was determined using two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test. P < 0.001.

To verify that CFD is secreted into the culture medium and potentially acts in a paracrine manner, we collected supernatants from the direct co-culture system of CESC cells with activated CD8 + T cells and performed Western blot analysis. CFD protein was detected in the supernatant of CFD-overexpressing C33A and SiHa cells, with levels approximately 4.5-fold and 3.8-fold higher, respectively, than those in vector control cells (Figs. 3 A and 3B, P < 0.001). Subsequently, functional analysis of this direct co-culture system revealed that CFD overexpression significantly enhanced CD8 + T cell-mediated cytotoxicity against CESC cells (Fig. 3C, P < 0.001), indicating that secreted CFD facilitates anti-tumor immune responses. To investigate the mechanism underlying this enhanced cytotoxicity, we harvested CD8 + T cells from the co-culture and performed flow cytometry to quantify established T cell exhaustion markers—specifically PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG3, which characterize terminally exhausted CD8 + T cells with impaired anti-tumor function in CESC. This analysis demonstrated that CFD overexpression significantly increased CD8 + T cell numbers (Fig. 3D, P < 0.001) while markedly decreasing the expression of exhaustion markers PD-1 (Fig. 3E, P < 0.001), TIM-3 (Fig. 3F, P < 0.001), and LAG3 (Fig. 3G, P < 0.001). Finally, because PD-L1 is predominantly expressed on tumor cells as the primary ligand mediating immune checkpoint inhibition, and to distinguish tumor-intrinsic PD-L1 regulation from immune cell-specific expression, we separated CESC cells from the non-adherent T cells by differential adherence, prepared tumor cell lysates. The adherent CESC cells remaining in the wells after T cell removal were subjected to flow cytometry to assess tumor-intrinsic PD-L1 expression. This revealed that CFD overexpression significantly reduced PD-L1 levels on tumor cells (Fig. 3H, P < 0.001), which was further confirmed by Western blotting (Fig. 3I and J, fold change ≈ 0.5, P < 0.001). These results collectively demonstrate that CFD secretion creates a paracrine signal that attenuates CD8 + T cell exhaustion and downregulates tumor-intrinsic PD-L1 expression, thereby enhancing antitumor immunity.

CFD overexpression upregulates NOSTRIN and suppresses the eNOS signaling pathway in CESC cells

To explore the downstream mechanisms of CFD in CESC, we performed PCR array screening on CFD-overexpressing C33A cells. The analysis identified NOSTRIN as one of the most significantly upregulated genes (Fig. 4A). Functional enrichment analysis of the PCR array data revealed that DEGs were enriched in the eNOS signaling pathway, with NOSTRIN being a key component of this pathway (Fig. 4B). NOSTRIN protein expression was significantly decreased in CESC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues, whereas the p-eNOS/eNOS ratio was markedly elevated in tumors (Supplementary Figure S2, P < 0.001). Furthermore, quantitative PCR confirmed that CFD overexpression significantly increased NOSTRIN mRNA levels in both C33A and SiHa cell lines (Fig. 4C, fold change ≈ 5, P < 0.001). Additionally, CFD overexpression upregulated the protein expression levels of NOSTRIN and decreased the phosphorylated eNOS protein levels (Fig. 4D and F, P < 0.001). Co-IP results suggested a physical interaction between NOSTRIN and CFD in CESC cells (Fig. 4G). These findings suggest that CFD modulates the eNOS signaling pathway through NOSTRIN upregulation, potentially contributing to its tumor-suppressive effects in CESC.

To further elucidate the functional relationship between CFD and NOSTRIN in CESC, we knocked down NOSTRIN in CFD-overexpressing CESC cells. The results demonstrated that NOSTRIN knockdown significantly attenuated the inhibitory effects of CFD overexpression on cell viability, proliferation, invasion, and migration (Fig. 5A and H, P < 0.001). Additionally, NOSTRIN knockdown partially reversed the suppression of CD8 + T cell exhaustion induced by CFD overexpression (Fig. 5I and P, P < 0.001). These findings suggest that NOSTRIN serves as a critical downstream mediator of CFD in regulating tumor progression and immune modulation in CESC.

Combination of TIL therapy and CFD overexpression enhances tumor growth inhibition in a PDX mouse model

To evaluate the effects of CFD overexpression alone and in combination with TIL therapy, we established a PDX model from a recurrent CESC patient and randomly assigned mice into six groups. In the LV-CFD group, tumor weight was significantly reduced compared to both the Control and LV-NC groups (Fig. 6A and B, P < 0.001). Tumor volume measurements over four weeks showed consistent inhibition in the LV-CFD group relative to controls (Fig. 6C, P < 0.001). The TIL group also exhibited reduced tumor weight and volume compared to controls (P < 0.01). The TIL + LV-CFD group demonstrated the lowest tumor weight and volume among all groups, with statistically significant differences compared to the TIL group (P < 0.05). Immunofluorescence staining for CD8 showed increased fluorescence intensity in the LV-CFD group compared to the Control and LV-NC groups (Fig. 6D and E, P < 0.001). The TIL group exhibited higher CD8 intensity than controls (P < 0.001). The TIL + LV-CFD group displayed the highest CD8 fluorescence intensity, significantly exceeding that of the TIL group (P < 0.05). The TIL group also showed decreased PD-L1 expression relative to controls (P < 0.001). The TIL + LV-CFD group exhibited the lowest PD-L1 expression among all groups, with statistically significant differences compared to the TIL group (P < 0.001).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that CFD is downregulated in CESC tissues, correlating with reduced CD8 + T cell infiltration. Overexpression of CFD in CESC cell lines inhibited cell proliferation, viability, and invasion while attenuating CD8 + T cell exhaustion. Furthermore, CFD overexpression upregulated NOSTRIN and suppressed the eNOS signaling pathway. The combination of TIL therapy with CFD overexpression significantly inhibited tumor growth in a PDX model established from a recurrent CESC patient, suggesting that CFD represents a potential therapeutic target for CESC.

TIL therapy has demonstrated substantial potential in the treatment of various cancers13,14. This immunotherapeutic approach involves extracting and expanding tumor-specific T cells ex vivo, followed by reinfusion into the patient to enhance antitumor immune responses15. TIL therapy has shown promising results in melanoma and is being actively applied to other cancer types, including CESC16,17. The application of TIL therapy in CESC has received increasing attention. Studies demonstrate that TILs from CESC patients can be effectively expanded, and these expanded TILs exhibit specific cytotoxicity against tumor cells in vitro18. Moreover, given that CESC is closely associated with HPV infection, TIL therapy can mount immune responses against HPV-related antigens, thereby enhancing treatment efficacy19.

The application of TIL therapy in CESC also involves combination with other immunotherapeutic strategies. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have shown promising results in treating CESC, and combining TIL therapy with ICIs may further enhance therapeutic efficacy20. The potential of this combination therapy lies in the fact that TILs can provide specific immune responses, while ICIs can overcome tumor-mediated immune suppression, thus achieving synergistic effects. Additionally, research on TIL therapy in CESC involves modulation of the tumor microenvironment. Studies have identified that tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) play significant roles in CESC development, and TIL therapy can enhance antitumor immune responses by regulating TAM function21. This regulatory effect may improve TIL therapy efficacy and provide new insights for CESC treatment.

CD8 + T cells play crucial roles in TIL therapy. Research indicates that the contribution of CD8 + T cells in TIL therapy extends beyond quantitative increases to encompass improvements in functional states. CD8 + T cells eliminate tumor cells by recognizing specific antigens on tumor cell surfaces and exerting cytotoxic effects. However, in the tumor microenvironment, CD8 + T cells often exhibit functional exhaustion, which limits their antitumor activity22. Therefore, improving the functional state of CD8 + T cells is key to enhancing TIL therapy effectiveness. It has been observed that the clonal composition of CD8 + T cells undergoes significant changes during ex vivo expansion, which may affect the tumor reactivity of TIL preparations. During ex vivo expansion, dominant tumor-reactive T cell clones may be lost while other clones proliferate excessively23. Furthermore, the degree of CD8 + T cell infiltration in the tumor microenvironment is closely related to patient prognosis. The number of tumor-infiltrating CD8 + T cells is positively correlated with patient survival rates, indicating that CD8 + T cell infiltration capacity is a crucial factor affecting TIL therapy efficacy24.

CD8 + T cell exhaustion is a critical factor in the progression and immune evasion of CESC. This phenomenon is characterized by loss of effector functions and sustained expression of inhibitory receptors, ultimately leading to impaired antitumor immunity. In CESC, the tumor microenvironment plays a significant role in promoting CD8 + T cell exhaustion, contributing to disease progression and poor prognosis. One study highlights the expression of CD39 on T cells in CESC patients, which is associated with immune suppression and disease progression. CD39 is an inhibitory checkpoint affecting the ATP-adenosine pathway, and its expression on CD8 + T cells correlates with advanced FIGO stage, lymph node metastasis, and distant metastasis in CESC patients25. This suggests that targeting CD39 could potentially reverse T cell exhaustion and improve immune responses against CESC. Another study investigated the role of SERINC2 in CESC, demonstrating that it negatively affects CD8 + T cell infiltration and function. SERINC2 is involved in serine metabolism, and its high expression in CESC cells creates a competitive advantage over CD8 + T cells for microenvironmental serine, contributing to T cell exhaustion and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment26. Therefore, targeting pathways and molecules that contribute to T cell dysfunction holds promise for enhancing antitumor immunity and improving patient outcomes in CESC.

In this study, we identified a novel gene correlating with reduced CD8 + T cell infiltration and exhibiting diagnostic potential. CFD, a component of the complement system, has been implicated in various physiological and pathological processes10,11. Our study revealed its downregulation in CESC and its correlation with CD8 + T cell infiltration. CFD overexpression not only suppressed CESC cell proliferation and invasion but also enhanced CD8 + T cell cytotoxicity and reduced their exhaustion. Moreover, NOSTRIN was found to be upregulated by CFD in CESC cells in this study. NOSTRIN, or nitric oxide synthase trafficking inducer, is known to regulate eNOS activity, which is crucial for nitric oxide production—a molecule involved in vascular function and immune response modulation27. NOSTRIN’s interaction with eNOS can lead to changes in endothelial cell proliferation, invasion, and angiogenesis, potentially impacting the infiltration and activity of TILs within tumors28.

Furthermore, the eNOS signaling pathway is involved in various cellular processes, including regulation of vascular tone and blood flow, which are essential for maintaining an appropriate tumor microenvironment for TIL therapy. Activation of eNOS leads to nitric oxide production, which can promote angiogenesis and improve blood flow, thereby facilitating the delivery and effectiveness of TILs in targeting tumor cells29. Studies have also demonstrated that the PI3K/Akt/eNOS signaling pathway contributes to oncogenic signaling in multiple malignancies30,31. In the present study, we found that CFD overexpression upregulated NOSTRIN and concomitantly decreased p-eNOS levels in CESC cells. Given that NOSTRIN is established as a negative regulator of eNOS trafficking and activity, these data indicate that CFD suppresses eNOS signaling rather than activating it. We propose that this NOSTRIN-mediated eNOS inhibition reduces protumorigenic nitric oxide production, thereby directly restraining CESC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration, as observed in our in vitro functional assays. This cell-autonomous tumor-suppressive mechanism is further corroborated by the finding that CFD overexpression alone significantly inhibited tumor growth in the immunodeficient PDX model, where adaptive immunity is absent.

Importantly, the immunomodulatory effects of CFD appear to operate through a distinct but complementary mechanism. While eNOS suppression exerts direct anti-tumor effects on cancer cells, CFD overexpression concurrently attenuated CD8 + T cell exhaustion by reducing PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG3 expression, and downregulated tumor-intrinsic PD-L1 levels. These immune-regulatory actions enhance TIL therapy efficacy by improving T cell cytotoxicity and reducing immune checkpoint inhibition, as evidenced by the synergistic tumor suppression observed in the combination treatment group. Thus, CFD elicits dual anti-tumor effects: (i) direct cancer cell-intrinsic suppression via the NOSTRIN-eNOS axis, and (ii) immune microenvironment modulation via attenuation of T cell exhaustion and PD-L1 downregulation.

Our research identified NOSTRIN as a significantly upregulated gene upon CFD overexpression and validated its interaction with CFD through co-immunoprecipitation. The inactivation of the eNOS signaling pathway by CFD overexpression further underscores its potential mechanism in tumor suppression. The combination of TIL therapy with CFD overexpression demonstrated a synergistic effect in inhibiting tumor growth in vivo, highlighting the importance of integrating gene therapy with immunotherapy to enhance therapeutic outcomes in CESC.

While our study provides valuable insights into the role of CFD in CESC and its potential as a therapeutic target, certain limitations exist. The sample size of patient tissues was relatively small, and the in vivo experiments were conducted in a single tumor model. Long-term effects and potential side effects of CFD overexpression require further investigation. Despite these limitations, our findings have significant clinical implications, offering a novel strategy for CESC treatment by combining CFD overexpression with TIL therapy, potentially improving therapeutic efficacy and patient outcomes. Future research should focus on larger clinical trials and exploring other potential targets within the CFD-NOSTRIN-eNOS axis to further optimize treatment approaches for CESC. Additionally, we acknowledge that the precise molecular mechanism by which extracellular CFD regulates intracellular NOSTRIN-eNOS axis remains to be fully defined, and future studies employing subcellular fractionation are required to delineate whether this interaction occurs during CFD biogenesis or via cell-surface receptor complexes.
