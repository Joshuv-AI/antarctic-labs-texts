Does the Age, APACHE II Score, Glasgow Coma Scale, and Creatinine Model Offer Incremental Value over APACHE II for Mortality Prediction in Sepsis?
Authors: Faruk Yildiz
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with interest the article by Gulia et al. describing the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-derived age, APACHE II score, glasgow coma scale (GCS), and creatinine (AAGC) model for predicting in-hospital mortality in sepsis.1 The authors’ prospective effort is commendable; however, we are not convinced that its central claim (that this is a simple and novel prognostic tool) is supported by the data presented.

The AAGC model comprises four variables: age, acute physiology and chronic health evaluation II (APACHE II) score, GCS, and creatinine. Three of these (age, GCS, and creatinine) are themselves constituent inputs of the APACHE II score.2 The model, therefore, largely repackages APACHE II together with its own components. This structural redundancy cannot be dismissed based on the weak pairwise correlations shown in the authors’ heatmap; indeed, their reported APACHE II–GCS correlation of −0.76 directly contradicts the conclusion that each predictor “contributed independently.”

The practical consequence of this overlap is evident in the performance results. The authors report the discriminatory ability of APACHE II as an area under the ROC curve (AUC) of 0.95, indistinguishable from that of the four-variable AAGC model. If this figure reflects APACHE II alone, then adding age, glasgow coma scale (GCS), and creatinine yields no additional discrimination; if it does not, then a head-to-head comparison against APACHE II alone is missing. Either way, the model's incremental value remains unproven. Resolving this would require a direct comparison of AAGC against APACHE II using a DeLong test, along with measures of incremental value, such as the net reclassification index or the integrated discrimination improvement (NRI/IDI).3,4

Beyond the optimism, single-center design, and need for external validation that the authors already acknowledge, the crux is this: Without a demonstration of added value, the “simplified novel model” amounts to little more than a restatement of APACHE II. Meaningful incremental value would most plausibly arise from markers not included in APACHE II (such as lactate or albumin), which the present four variables do not capture.5 Quantifying this would clarify whether AAGC represents a genuine contribution and would strengthen the study.
