t2 occupancy as a descriptor for polysulfide conversion on spinel oxides
Authors: Wen Xie, Zihan Shen, Shibo Xi, Longcheng Zhang, Kai Tang, Zhenhui Ma, Chichong Lu, Zhichuan J. Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Transition metal oxides with partially filled d orbitals serve as effective polysulfide conversion catalysts in lithium-sulfur batteries. Nonetheless, a predictive descriptor to elucidate their structure-activity relationship is still lacking. To fill this gap, here we investigate the occupancy of t2 orbitals as a possible descriptor for the polysulfide conversion activity of spinel oxides. The electrochemical characterizations reveal a volcano relationship between t2 occupancy and the polysulfide conversion activity of spinel oxides. The theoretical calculations indicate that the t2 occupancy effect is associated with the ability of tetrahedral cations to cleave S-S bonds and form Li-S bonds. As such, Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4, with an optimized average t2 occupancy of 2.88, is designed and demonstrates promising polysulfide conversion activity among representative oxides. This work elucidates that t2 occupancy serves as an effective descriptor for evaluating the polysulfide conversion activity of spinel oxides. With the catalytic mechanism proposed, the current study prompts further exploration of the application of t2 occupancy as a descriptor for predicting the polysulfide conversion efficiency of other catalytic systems beyond the spinel structure.

Introduction

Enhancing the energy density of batteries is essential for the future development of electric vehicles. However, due to its modest energy capacity and expensive production, the current lithium-ion battery technology faces challenges in meeting future demands for extended driving range, creating an urgent need for next-generation lithium-ion systems1–4. Among the various alternatives, lithium-sulfur (Li-S) batteries have gained much interest for their cost-effectiveness, high theoretical specific capacity (1675 mAh g−1), and high theoretical energy density (2600 Wh kg−1)5–9.

Despite these advantages of Li-S batteries, their practical implementation is hindered by challenges such as the shuttle effect caused by soluble polysulfide intermediates, sluggish polysulfide conversion kinetics, poor electrical conductivity of sulfur and lithium sulfide, low sulfur utilization, and severe corrosion of the lithium negative electrodes9–13. Among these limitations, the sluggish polysulfide conversion kinetics significantly affect battery performance. The complex 16-electron conversion processes involve a series of phase transformations, and their sluggish kinetics result in incomplete sulfur conversion and exacerbate the shuttle effect14,15. With the ability to both adsorb polysulfides and catalyze the subsequent conversion processes, transition metal oxides are commonly used as catalysts to enhance the polysulfide conversion kinetics of Li-S batteries16,17. In these oxides, the filling of d orbitals in transition metal cations dominates their interactions with molecules18,19. In the case of adsorption, cations with a lower d-band center location that would trigger a higher anti-bonding state occupancy, are usually regarded as unfavorable sites20. During the subsequent charge transport process, electrons occupying the higher-lying orbitals with sufficient energy are more likely to be donated to molecules and drive the conversion of molecular intermediates21. Besides, the geometric environment plays an important role in determining the splitting and filling of d orbitals in transition metal cations. In an octahedral environment, the d orbitals split into two higher-lying eg orbitals and three lower-lying t2g orbitals, giving rise to the eg theory for determining the oxygen electrocatalytic activity of perovskite oxides22,23. Both the d-band center theory and the eg theory highlight the critical role of electron filling in d orbitals in determining the catalytic activity of transition metal oxides. The eg theory, which considers the ligand effect and orbital geometry, places greater emphasis on the filling of the higher-lying eg orbitals of octahedral transition metal cations. It is worth noting that the eg orbitals of transition metal cations in an octahedral coordination are associated with the σ* anti-bonding states in the molecular orbitals of these oxides. To understand the eg theory, we should primarily look at the molecular orbitals of transition metal oxides. First, electrons in the bonding states of molecular orbitals are stable and hardly contribute to interactions with molecular intermediates, shifting attention to the anti-bonding states. Second, electrons in the σ* anti-bonding orbitals of these oxides can interact more strongly with molecular intermediates than those in the π* anti-bonding orbitals23,24. Thus, to explore descriptors for transition metal oxides with different geometries, one should focus on the d orbitals that account for the formation of the σ* anti-bonding states in the molecular orbitals of these oxides. For example, in a tetrahedral coordination environment, the splitting of d orbitals of transition metal cations results in three higher-lying t2 orbitals and two lower-lying e orbitals, which correspond to the σ* and π* anti-bonding states of molecular orbitals of the oxides, respectively. Although the eg theory has been well established as a descriptor for oxygen electrocatalysis due to the more active octahedral sites, recent studies have found that sulfur electrocatalysis on spinel oxides differs from oxygen electrocatalysis, with the tetrahedral sites identified as the most active sites for catalyzing polysulfide conversion24. This distinction makes the study of the t2 occupancy effect on the polysulfide conversion activity of spinel oxides valuable.

In this study, a series of spinel oxides were synthesized and served as catalysts for polysulfide conversion in Li-S batteries. Cr, with a high preference for octahedral sites25, was selected to stabilize the octahedral sites, and its empty eg orbitals eliminate any potential influence from eg electrons. M (M = Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, or Zn) cations at the tetrahedral sites were used to achieve different t2 orbital fillings. With this design strategy, the background contributions from the coordination environment are eliminated, enabling an accurate study of the t2 occupancy effect on the polysulfide conversion efficiency of spinel oxides. The electrochemical measurements suggest that the polysulfide conversion activity of the as-synthesized spinel oxides exhibits a volcano relationship with the t2 occupancy of M cations. Theoretical calculations show that the volcano relationship is attributed to the ability of tetrahedral cations to cleave S-S bonds as well as form Li-S bonds, with both barriers initially decreasing and then increasing as the t2 occupancy increases. With the volcano relationship elucidated, Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 with an optimized average t2 occupancy of 2.88 was designed and served as Li-S catalysts. Further electrochemical measurements indicate that Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 indeed outperforms the other examined samples in catalyzing polysulfide conversion, positioning it at the vertex of the volcano-shaped curve. This confirms the validity of t2 occupancy as an effective descriptor for predicting the polysulfide conversion efficiency of spinel oxides.

Results

Orbital interaction and spinel structure

Before processing any experimental measurements, we first analyzed the orbital interaction between tetrahedral transition metal cations with different t2 occupancies and LiS* or SS* intermediates. Due to the orbital splitting in tetrahedral coordination, only the three higher-lying orbitals which might form strong σ bonding with polysulfide orbitals are considered. These orbitals located at high energy levels also benefit the subsequent charge transfer from transition metal cations to polysulfides. According to molecular orbital theory, the orbital interaction between tetrahedral transition metal cations and LiS* or SS* intermediates results in three lower-lying bonding orbitals and three higher-lying antibonding orbitals (Fig. 1a). Bond order (defined as BO = (number of bonding electrons – number of antibonding electrons)/2) is used to quantify the orbital interaction, and a higher value of BO suggests a stronger orbital interaction. In the case of the orbital interaction between tetrahedral cations and LiS* intermediates, the BO first increases with t2 occupancy from t2 = 1 to t2 = 3, and then decreases as t2 occupancy increases further (Fig. 1b). Similarly, the BO of the orbital interaction between tetrahedral cations and SS* intermediates exhibits a comparable trend: it first increases with t2 occupancy from t2 = 1 to t2 = 2, and then decreases as t2 occupancy increases further (Fig. 1c). Because the polysulfides (Li2S, Li2S2, Li2S4, and Li2S6) are composed of Li-S and S-S bonds, the BO analysis above indicates that the interaction between tetrahedral coordinated transition metal cations and polysulfides might change in correspondence with the t2 occupancy, and the strongest orbital interaction with polysulfides is likely to be exhibited by cations with a t2 occupancy that lies between 2 and 3. Additionally, the filling of t2 orbitals also plays an important role in regulating the electron transport during polysulfide conversion. The half-filled high energy orbitals have been reported to promote spin-selected charge transport during electrocatalysis by constructing a spin channel within the oxide structure26. In contrast, both empty and fully filled orbitals give localized electronic states and blocked spin pathways (Fig. 1d). Besides, electrons in the half-filled polarized t2 orbitals can couple the spin-oriented electrons in polysulfide intermediates through spin-pinning effect, and then be donated to polysulfides to drive polysulfide conversion (Fig. S1)27. t2 Occupancy

To determine the t2 occupancy, we first examined the oxidation states of various M cations with X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectra23. The edge position in XANES spectra, used to determine the oxidation states of transition metal cations, can be calculated by the integral method28. It is clear that each examined cation exhibits a shift in the edge position relative to its standard divalent sample, suggesting a charge deviation (Fig. 2a–f). The Zn K-edge XANES of ZnCr2O4 shows a relatively smaller shift in edge position compared to those of other cations and is more clearly depicted in Fig. S7. Electrochemical behavior of spinel oxides as catalysts for polysulfide conversion

Visual Li2S4 adsorption tests and ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) absorption spectra were performed to examine the polysulfide adsorption behaviors of various spinel oxides. Li2S4 was selected because it represents a key polysulfide intermediate that dictates the overall conversion kinetics30. Samples with identical surface areas were immersed in Li2S4 solutions and left undisturbed for a few hours. Subsequently, the solutions were diluted fivefold with 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME) before being subjected to UV-vis absorption tests. It is evident that the intensity of the remaining S42- species increases with the M cation t2 occupancy (Fig. 3a), consistent with the colorization of the Li2S4 solution (inset of Fig. 3a). The UV-vis absorption spectra, combined with visual adsorption tests, suggest that the polysulfide adsorption ability of spinel oxides may benefit from a lower t2 occupancy when it ranges between 2.53 and 5.99. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were employed to examine the polysulfide conversion kinetics of different catalysts in symmetric Li2S4 cells (Fig. 3b). Peaks A and C are associated with the transformation between long-chain polysulfides and short-chain polysulfides, while peaks B and D correspond to the conversion between short-chain polysulfides and solid Li2S. The intrinsic activity of different catalysts was evaluated by the peak current density (peak current normalized by surface area)32–34. Results suggest that the peak C and D current densities, and thus the polysulfide conversion kinetics, of various spinel oxides first increase with t2 occupancy from t2 = 2.53 to t2 = 3.18, then decrease with a further increase in t2 occupancy from t2 = 3.18 to t2 = 5.99, resulting in a volcano-shaped curve (Figs. S9–S10 and Fig. 3c). Moreover, the absence of redox peaks in the CV curve of Cr2O3 indicates that octahedral Cr3+ exhibits negligible activity toward polysulfide conversion (Fig. S11). Therefore, the volcano behavior is primarily attributed to the different t2 occupancies of the tetrahedral cations. Based on the analysis above, although the polysulfide adsorption ability of spinel oxides may benefit from a lower t2 occupancy, a relatively greater number of t2 electrons is necessary to facilitate the subsequent charge transport process.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations help identify the optimal t2 occupancy and elucidate the source of the polysulfide conversion activity observed in different spinel oxides at the atomic scale. In principle, the conversion of polysulfides (S8 → Li2S6 → Li2S4 → Li2S2 → Li2S) during discharge is accompanied by the cleavage of S-S bonds and the formation of Li-S bonds. Thus, we examined the barriers to S-S bond cleavage and Li-S bond formation on tetrahedral sites of various spinel oxides. The optimized structures for the different steps of S-S bond cleavage and Li-S bond formation processes on the tetrahedral sites of (111) of various spinel oxides are shown in Fig. 3d and Figs. S12–S23. The results suggest that both the S-S bond cleavage barrier and the Li-S bond formation barrier exhibit an inverted volcano relationship with the t2 occupancy of tetrahedral cations (Fig. 3e). Particularly, Fe with a t2 occupancy of 2.82 exhibits the lowest Li-S bond formation barrier, while Co with a t2 occupancy of 3.18 exhibits the lowest S-S bond cleavage barrier among the examined cations. It is worth noting that the strongest orbital interaction with SS* intermediates should be possessed by cations with a t2 occupancy of around 2, rather than around 3, based on the BO analysis in the previous sections of this work. In fact, a higher BO value is not necessarily better for catalyzing polysulfide conversion. For example, cations with a t2 occupancy of 3 exhibit the highest BO value with LiS* intermediates, indicating that the LiS* intermediates should be more stable on these sites than on cations with a lower or a higher t2 occupancy. This stability could, in turn, benefit the formation of Li-S bonds during polysulfide conversion. In the case of interaction with SS* intermediates, although a high BO value provides stronger orbital interaction, the excessive stability of SS* on cations with a t2 occupancy of 2 makes it difficult for the metal sites to cleave the S–S bonds, leading to a shift of the vertex of the inverted volcano toward higher t2 occupancy. Gibbs free energy was then calculated to study the thermodynamics of polysulfide conversion on tetrahedral sites with different t2 occupancies (Fig. 3f and Figs. S24–S29). During the first step, S8 spontaneously reacts with lithium ions to form Li2S6 on all sites. As the reaction proceeds, the conversion of Li2S6 to Li2S4, followed by Li2S2, is accompanied by the cleavage of S-S bonds, which requires energy. The energies required during this step are +0.96 eV, +0.71 eV, +0.16 eV, +0.38 eV, +0.64 eV, and +0.67 eV for the Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn sites, respectively, aligning with the inverted volcano relationship between the S-S bond cleavage barrier and t2 occupancy. After that, the transformation of Li2S2 to Li2S is accompanied by the intercalation of lithium ions into the Li2S2 lattice to form new Li-S bonds. The energies required during this step are −0.28 eV, −0.37 eV, −0.24 eV, +0.56 eV, +2.65 eV, and +3.90 eV for the Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, and Zn sites, respectively, aligning with the inverted volcano relationship between the Li-S bond formation barrier and t2 occupancy. Overall, the energy analysis above suggests that the t2 occupancy of tetrahedral cations is strongly associated with their ability to cleave S-S bonds and form Li-S bonds, contributing to the volcano relationship between t2 occupancy and the polysulfide conversion efficiency of spinel oxides.

Verifying the reliability of the t2 occupancy descriptor

Based on theoretical calculations, the Li-S bond formation and S-S bond cleavage processes on tetrahedral sites of spinel oxides benefit from Fe (t2 = 2.82) and Co (t2 = 3.18), respectively. To achieve a polysulfide conversion ability beyond the upper limit of these spinels, it is reasonable to design materials with a t2 occupancy between 2.82 and 3.18. A simple method involves replacing part of the Co cations in the tetrahedral sites of CoCr2O4 with either Mn or Fe (Fig. 4a). Accordingly, Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 and Fe0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 oxides were synthesized. However, impurities in Fe0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 are considered inevitable after multiple synthesis attempts (Fig. S30a). To exclude the background contributions from impurities and ensure the accuracy of our work, pure-phase Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 was selected to further study the effect of t2 occupancy on the polysulfide conversion efficiency of spinel oxides (Fig. S30b). Since heteroatom doping may introduce oxygen vacancies, which may cause uncertain effects on polysulfide conversion, the electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) test was conducted on Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4. The absence of the EPR signal at g = 2.003 suggests that doping does not generate oxygen vacancies in Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 (Figure S32). The particle morphology and elemental spatial distribution of as-synthesized Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX). The particles are in the nanometer range, and Mn, Co, and Cr in Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 are homogeneously distributed (Fig. 4b). The lattice spacing of 0.476 nm in the high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) corresponds to the (111) plane of the cubic spinel structure (Fig. 4b). The stoichiometry of Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 was confirmed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES), which determined the ratio of Mn:Co:Cr to be 0.49:0.46:1.82 (Fig. 4c). Site occupancy of Mn and Co was investigated by EXAFS fitting (Fig. 4d and Table S1). The results suggest that most of the Mn and Co (both above 95%) occupy the tetrahedral sites in the Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4. The dominant octahedral peaks in the Cr K-edge EXAFS spectra indicate that Cr primarily occupies the octahedral sites in the as-synthesized spinel oxides (Fig. S33). To investigate the reliability of our proposed t2 occupancy effect, Li2S nucleation experiments were conducted to explore the adsorption-catalysis of polysulfides on various spinel oxides. For electrode preparation, to eliminate the influence of different surface areas, the quantity of oxides in each cell was adjusted to maintain a consistent oxide surface area. Conductive carbon was used as a substitute for oxides to achieve a comparable total mass loading. The Li2S nucleation capacities were calculated according to Faraday’s law35,36. Results show that Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4, with the highest capacity of 307.6 mAh/g and the largest current response of 1.06 mA (Fig. 5a), positioned at the vertex of the volcano-shaped curve, indeed outperforms other samples in Li2S nucleation (Figs. S34–S35). Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 and MnCr2O4 electrodes were chosen as representatives to examine the surface evolution during Li2S nucleation using SEM. As shown in Fig. S36, the electrodes consisted of spinel catalysts, porous carbon, and binder. During discharge, polysulfides were initially adsorbed and agglomerated on the electrode surface (Figure S37). A thick and solid Li2S layer was observed on both electrodes after being discharged at a constant voltage of 2.05 V for 1 hour (Fig. S38). After being charged at 2.35 V, the solid Li2S layer dissolved, exposing the active sites (Fig. S39). The Li2S nucleation capacity and the corresponding current response are 217.12 mAh/g and 0.72 mA for MnCr2O4, 253.35 mAh/g and 0.77 mA for FeCr2O4, 274.79 mAh/g and 0.90 mA for CoCr2O4, 217.14 mAh/g and 0.66 mA for NiCr2O4, 165.25 mAh/g and 0.59 mA for CuCr2O4, and 115.80 mAh/g and 0.37 mA for ZnCr2O4, respectively. On the left side of the volcano, the spinel oxides (t2 < 2.88) exhibit relatively large S-S bond cleavage barriers, and their polysulfide conversion efficiency is constrained by the sluggish transformation of Li2S6 to Li2S4, followed by Li2S2. On the right side of the volcano, however, the polysulfide conversion efficiency of spinel oxides (t2 > 2.88) is constrained by the sluggish transformation of Li2S2 to Li2S due to their relatively large Li-S bond formation barriers. It is worth noting that the relatively large deviation of Fe from the volcano-shaped curve can be attributed to its comparatively lower tetrahedral occupancy (89%) in the FeCr2O4, compared to that of other cations (equal to or greater than 94%) (Fig. S35). This is significant because tetrahedral sites are identified as the active sites for catalyzing polysulfide conversion in spinel oxides24. After normalizing the Li2S nucleation capacity by the M cation tetrahedral occupancy, the large deviation of Fe from the volcano-shaped curve is eliminated (Fig. 5b). CV measurements were further conducted to evaluate the role of catalysts on polysulfide conversion. Each positive electrode exhibits two pairs of reversible peaks corresponding to polysulfide redox reactions (Fig. 6b, c). Peak current is an indicator of redox kinetics and can be used to estimate the polysulfide conversion activity of different spinel oxides. To exclude the influence of different site occupancies, the peak currents were normalized by cation tetrahedral occupancy. The results show a clear increase in the normalized peak current with t2 occupancy, from t2 = 2.53 to t2 = 2.88, for MnCr2O4/S, FeCr2O4/S, and Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S (Fig. 6d). Conversely, a decrease in the normalized peak current is observed with a further increase in t2 occupancy for Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S, CoCr2O4/S, NiCr2O4/S, CuCr2O4/S, and ZnCr2O4/S (Fig. 6d), consistent with the volcano relationship. To gain insight into the enhanced kinetics of Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S during the redox process, the evolution of charge-transfer resistance was further investigated through in situ EIS measurements of Li–S batteries at various cycling stages, using Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S and MnCr2O4/S as representatives. As shown in Fig. S40 and Table S2, Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S exhibits lower charge-transfer resistance (Rct) values at all states compared to MnCr2O4/S, thereby facilitating the redox process. Galvanostatic charge/discharge at varied current densities (0.1 C, 0.2 C, 0.3 C, 0.5 C, 1 C, and 2 C) was then performed to examine the rate capability of different electrodes with spinel oxides as catalysts. When t2 < 2.88, a positive correlation between the specific discharge capacity and the t2 occupancy of spinel oxides is observed (Fig. 6e and Figs. S41–S42), while a negative correlation is observed when t2 > 2.88 (Fig. 6f and Figs. S41–S42), following the volcano relationship. Additionally, the relatively large capacity drop with increasing cycle numbers for MnCr2O4/S and FeCr2O4/S is likely attributed to the strong adsorption of polysulfides on MnCr2O4 and FeCr2O4 oxides, which usually results in the decomposition and dissolution of polysulfides in the electrolyte, as well as the blockage of active sites37. Due to the enhanced catalytic activity, Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S delivered the highest capacity of 1280.5 mAh/g among all positive electrodes at 0.2 C (Fig. 6g), and exhibited a capacity retention of 89% after 500 cycles at 1 C (Fig. 6h). The practical application of Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S was further demonstrated by its high-rate performance up to 6 C. As shown in Figs. S43a, b, the Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S delivered a specific capacity of 628.9 mAh/g at 5 C and 513.6 mAh/g at 6 C, and then recovered to 543.2 mAh/g upon returning to 5 C. This capacity decay at 6 C and the incomplete recovery upon returning to 5 C suggest that, at very high rates, the ability of catalyst to maintain optimal redox kinetics is somewhat limited. However, from a commercial perspective, most Li–S battery applications target moderate to high current densities (1–3 C) for real-world use38–40, within which Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S performs reliably and efficiently. Moreover, stable performance is maintained up to 5 C, which is widely considered a high rate for Li–S systems41–43. This demonstrates that Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S effectively enables fast redox kinetics under practical high-rate conditions. Thus, while challenges remain at very high rates (≥ 6 C), the demonstrated performance of Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S at practical rates still represents a promising step forward in the development of advanced Li-S battery materials. The practical viability of Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S was further supported by its cycling performance under high sulfur loading (6 mg/cm2) and lean electrolyte conditions (electrolyte-to-sulfur ratio = 5 μL/mg). The results show a capacity retention of 74% after 1100 cycles at 2 C (Fig. S43c). This cycling performance of Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4/S under high sulfur loading conditions highlights its potential as a promising candidate among reported materials for advancing the commercialization of Li-S batteries (Table S3). Overall, the electrochemical performance of Li-S full cells demonstrates the reliability of t2 occupancy as a descriptor for predicting the polysulfide conversion efficiency of spinel oxides.

Discussion

In this study, we identified the t2 occupancy of tetrahedral transition metal cations as an effective descriptor for predicting the polysulfide conversion efficiency of spinel oxides. The electrochemical measurements reveal a volcano relationship between t2 occupancy and the polysulfide conversion activity of the as-synthesized spinel oxides. The theoretical calculations indicate that this volcano relationship is due to the ability of tetrahedral cations to cleave S-S bonds and form Li-S bonds, with the barriers first decreasing and then increasing as t2 occupancy increases. Namely, the Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 oxide, with an optimized average t2 occupancy of 2.88, sits at the vertex of the volcano-shaped curve, outperforming the other examined samples in catalyzing polysulfide conversion.

To demonstrate the applicability of the proposed t2 occupancy descriptor across a wide range of spinel oxides, the S–S bond cleavage and Li–S bond formation barriers on MAl2O4 (M = Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, or Zn) were further examined. A similar inverted volcano relationship was observed, suggesting that the t2 occupancy descriptor is applicable to MAl2O4 oxides (Figs. S44–S56). Therefore, this work provides an approach for the rational design of catalysts for polysulfide conversion based on t2 occupancy, as well as a deep insight into the catalytic mechanism of the polysulfide conversion activity of spinel oxides. The proposed t2 occupancy descriptor, which exhibits a volcano-shaped relationship with polysulfide conversion efficiency, is also expected to be applicable to other catalytic systems with active tetrahedral sites beyond the spinel oxide structure (e.g., spinel sulfides, cation-doped ZnS), due to their similar electronic configurations. It is worth noting that the optimal t2 occupancy for catalyzing polysulfide conversion may vary depending on the catalytic system. For example, in this work, the optimal t2 occupancy lies between 2.82 and 3.18 for MCr2O4 oxides, while it could shift to a higher or lower value in other catalytic systems, such as spinel sulfides or cation-doped ZnS. Moreover, this work highlights the role of the electron occupancy of d orbitals that contribute to the formation of σ* anti-bonding states in molecular orbitals (specifically the t2 orbitals in this work) in determining the S–S bond cleavage and Li–S bond formation abilities, and thus the polysulfide conversion efficiency of catalysts. This finding offers valuable insights for the development of more diverse catalysts, such as the widely studied single-atom catalysts. For example, in single-atom catalysts, the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{d}}}_{{{\rm{z}}}^{2}}$$\end{document}dz2 orbitals of cations are responsible for the formation of σ* anti-bonding states in the molecular orbitals of these catalysts44. Therefore, adjusting the electron occupancy of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{d}}}_{{{\rm{z}}}^{2}}$$\end{document}dz2 orbitals of cations could be an effective method for manipulating the polysulfide conversion behavior of single-atom catalysts. As such, this work is expected to provide value to the current landscape of catalyst development for polysulfide conversion.

Methods

Preparation of MCr2O4 spinel oxides

Spinel oxides (MnCr2O4, FeCr2O4, CoCr2O4, NiCr2O4, CuCr2O4, ZnCr2O4, and Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4) were synthesized through sol-gel methods. In the case of MnCr2O4, 10 mmol Mn(NO3)2·4H2O (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%) and 20 mmol Cr(NO3)3·9H2O (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%) were weighted and dissolved into 200 ml deionized water to form a colloidal solution. Then, 60 mmol urea (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%) and 60 mmol citric acid monohydrate (Sigma-Aldrich, 99%) were used as gelling agents and dissolved into the solution. The above solution was stirred at 60 °C until dried to form a gel. The obtained gel was heated at 170 °C for 12 h. Finally, the fully dry gel was annealed at 800 °C for 12 h.

FeCr2O4, CoCr2O4, NiCr2O4, CuCr2O4, and ZnCr2O4 were synthesized following the similar procedure with Fe(CO2CH3)2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%), Co(CO2CH3)2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%), Ni(CO2CH3)2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%), Cu(CO2CH3)2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%), or Zn(CO2CH3)2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%) taking place of Mn(NO3)2·4H2O. In the case of Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4, 5 mmol manganese tetrahydrate Mn(NO3)2·4H2O (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%) and 5 mmol Co(CO2CH3)2 (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.9%) were used.

Assembly of symmetric Li2S4 cells

Symmetric Li2S4 cell electrodes composed of catalysts, carbon black (Nouryon) and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Specifically, spinel oxides, carbon black and PVDF in a mass ratio of 4:5:1 were mixed in N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP) solvent (Sigma-Aldrich, 99.5%) to prepare the electrode slurries. The slurries were coated on Al foil and dried in vacuum overnight and then punched into circular pieces, serving as both working and counter electrodes. The mass loading for each electrode was controlled at 1.6 mg/cm2 to ensure that the same quantity of oxides was used in each cell, allowing for subsequent normalization of the CV peak currents by surface area to obtain the intrinsic activity of the oxides. The electrolyte used in these cells composed of 1.0 M Lithium bis(trifluoromethanesulfonyl)imide (LiTFSI, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.99%) and 0.25 M Li2S4 in a 1:1 volume ratio of 1,3-Dioxolane (DOL, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.8%) to 1,2-Dimethoxyethane (DME, Sigma-Aldrich, 99.5%). Li2S4 was synthesized by dissolving S (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 99.5%) and Li2S (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 99.9%) in a molar ratio of 3:1 in DOL:DME at 80 °C in argon glovebox, functioning as the redox active species. Coin cells were assembled in CR2025 cases using the electrodes and Celgard 2500 (single-layer, 25 μm thick, 1.8 cm wide, 55% porosity, average pore size of 0.04 μm) as the separator, and 40 μL electrolyte.

Assembly of Li2S nucleation cells

Li2S nucleation cell working electrodes composed of catalysts, carbon black and PVDF with a mass ratio of around 2:7:1. The slurries were coated on Al foil and dried in vacuum overnight and then punched into circular pieces, serving as working electrodes. Lithium metal (0.4 mm thick, 15 mm in diameter) and Celgard 2500 served as the counter electrode and separator, respectively. The lithium metal was stored in an inert environment for three months at 25 ± 2 °C. Coin cells were assembled in CR2025 cases. The influence of different surface areas is eliminated by controlling the amount of spinel oxides in each cell to achieve the same surface area of 0.004 m2. Furthermore, the influence of varying oxide mass is eliminated by substituting with carbon black to ensure a consistent total mass loading of 1.5 mg cm-2. Li2S8 solution prepared by dissolving Li2S and S (1:7) in a solution of DME:DOL (1:1), serving as the electrolyte.

Disassembly of Li2S nucleation cells

Cells were disassembled at different stages of the Li2S nucleation tests for ex situ SEM characterization of the Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 and MnCr2O4 electrodes. Disassembly was performed inside a fume hood in open lab air at 25 ± 2 °C using a wrench. After disassembly, the working electrode was air-dried for 2 h before being directly transported in a plastic Petri dish to the SEM facility.

Assembly of Li-S cells

The positive electrodes composed of sulfur, spinel oxides, carbon black and PVDF. Sulfur and spinel oxides were first mixed with a mass ratio of 7:3 and heated under 170 °C for 8 h. Then the sulfur/oxide were mixed with carbon black and PVDF (6:3:1). The slurries were coated on Al foil and dried in vacuum overnight and then punched into circular pieces, serving as positive electrodes. Lithium metal (0.4 mm thick, 15 mm in diameter) and Celgard 2500 served as the negative electrode and separator. The lithium metal was stored in an inert environment for three months at 25 ± 2 °C. The electrolyte purchased from DoDochem consists of 1 M LITFSI and 1 wt% lithium nitrate dissolved in a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of DME and DOL. Coin cells were assembled in CR2025 cases. The influence of different surface area is eliminated by controlling the amount of spinel oxides in the cell to have the same surface area of 0.004 m2 present within each group by substituting with carbon black to ensure a similar total mass loading and proportions of active sulfur. For the low sulfur loading cells, a mass loading of 1.5 mg/cm2 and an electrolyte volume of 15 μL/mg were used. For the high sulfur loading cells, a mass loading of 6 mg/cm2 and an electrolyte volume of 6 μL/mg were used.

Material characterization

XRD were performed using Bruker D8 with Cu-Kα radiation. BET analysis was conducted on ASAP Tri-star II 3020. SEM study was conducted on FESEM-EDX JEOL JSM-7600F. TEM-EDX and HRTEM study were done by a JEOL 2200 at 200 kV. XANES and EXAFS data were collected at the XAFCA beamline of the Singapore Synchrotron Light Source. Element concentration in Mn0.5Co0.5Cr2O4 was determined by ICP-OES analysis. The absorption spectra were measured by using a UV-2700 and the S42- species were determined using the absorbance at 425 nm wavelength. The EPR test was conducted using a JEOL JES-X320 ESR spectrometer.

Electrochemical measurement

The CVs and EIS were measured using BioLogic Science Instruments SP-150 potentiate. All EIS measurements were conducted over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 0.1 Hz, with a signal amplitude of 10 mV in potentiostatic mode. A total of 43 data points were collected at a quasi-stationary potential. For the EIS measurements shown in Fig. 6a, the cells were held at open-circuit voltage for 1 hour prior to measurement to ensure stabilization, while the measurements shown in Fig. S40 were performed concurrently with CV, with cycling paused at specific voltages for CV scans. Both the symmetric Li2S4 cells and Li-S full cells CVs were scanned at a slow rate of 0.1 mV s-1, which is necessary for accommodating the sluggish sulfur redox reaction. The potential ranges are from -0.8 V to 0.8 V and from 1.7 V to 2.6 V for symmetric Li2S4 cells and Li-S full cells, respectively. In situ EIS was conducted by pausing the cycling at specific voltages. The Li2S nucleation tests were measured using Autolab potentiostat. Specifically, the cells were first discharged at a constant current of 0.05 mA until the voltage reached 2.1 V to convert long-chain polysulfides (Li2S8 and Li2S6) into short-chain polysulfides (Li2S4). They were then discharged at a constant voltage of 2.05 V until the current decreased to 0.01 mA to facilitate Li2S nucleation. Finally, the cells were potentiostatically charged at 2.35 V to dissolve the Li2S into soluble polysulfides. The Li2S nucleation capacities can be calculated by integrating area under the constant voltage discharge curve, according to Faraday’s law35,36. Galvanostatic Charge–Discharge (GCD) cycles were carried out on a Neware battery from 1.7 to 2.6 V.

Theoretical calculations

DFT was performed using the VASP code with PAW method. (111) plane was applied to all frameworks. The exchange-correlation potential was described by using the GGA-PBE. The plane-wave cutoff energy of 400 eV was used. For the surface adsorption model, a 4 × 4 × 1 k-mesh in the Brillouin zone was employed. The force certification was set to 0.05 eV/Å and the energy convergence tolerance was maintained at 10−5 eV. The Li-S bond formation and S-S bond cleavage on the MCr2O4 oxides surface are evaluated using the CI-NEB method.

The atomic coordinates of the optimized computational models for CI-NEB and Gibbs free energy calculations are provided in Supplementary Data 1 and Supplementary Data 2, respectively. Visualization of the structures was done by using VESTA software.

EXAFS analysis

Data merging, background subtraction, normalization, and fitting of the XANES and EXAFS spectra were performed using the Demeter software package, which includes the Athena, Artemis, and Hephaestus programs. Specifically, data merging, background subtraction, and normalization were conducted using Athena to convert the raw data into a suitable form, while fitting was carried out in Artemis using theoretical standards generated by the FEFE and ATOMS codes. The EXAFS structural parameters are obtained with k3 weighting to emphasize the high-energy part of the spectrum. The k-ranges and R-ranges for the fitting of all the EXAFS data were set as 3-12 Å-1 and 1.0-3.5 Å respectively. The ab initio calculations using FEFE6 were performed to create theoretical standards based on a cubic spinel structure. In the theoretical standards used for the M K-edge (M = Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu, or Zn), the M absorber atoms were placed at the tetrahedral site. For all fittings, the scattering paths are used without phase correction. The S02, used in the fitting for Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu and Zn, were extracted from the analysis of the EXAFS of the standard metallic foil. For each sample, the S02 value was constrained to the same initial value and kept fixed throughout the fitting for each path, while the E0 was applied as a shared parameter for each path and allowed to vary during the fitting. The N and R parameters were varied independently for each path without any applied constraints. The Debye-Waller factors were constrained as σ2 (X_Td-O) = σ2 (X_Oct-O), σ2 (X_Td- M_Td) = σ2 (X_Oct- M_Oct), where X = Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, Cu or Zn for reducing the number of variables. We performed EXAFS curve fitting for the various spinel oxides by referencing a landmark work45.
