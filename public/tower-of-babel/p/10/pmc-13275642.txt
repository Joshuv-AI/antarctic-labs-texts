Speech Neurophysiology in Realistic Contexts: Big Hype or Big Leap?
Authors: Giovanni M Di Liberto, Emily Y J Ip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnderstanding the neural basis of speech communication is essential for uncovering how sounds are translated into meaning, how that changes with development, ageing and speech‐related deficits, as well as contributing to brain–computer interfaces research. While traditional neurophysiological studies have relied on simplified, controlled paradigms, recent advances have shifted the field towards more ecologically valid approaches. Here, we describe the evolving landscape of experimental designs in speech neurophysiology, from discrete to continuous stimuli and from socially isolated listening to dynamic, multiagent communication. Realistic paradigms in that space challenge conventional methods, offering richer insights into neural encoding, functional brain mapping and neural entrainment. At the same time, they introduce significant analytical and technical complexities, particularly when incorporating social interaction. By synthesising findings across studies, we highlight how these ecologically valid speech paradigms have been contributing to refining theories of language processing and open new avenues for research. In doing so, this review critically evaluates of whether the move towards realism in speech neurophysiology represents a technological trend or a transformative leap in understanding the neural underpinnings of speech communication.Keywords: dialogue, neurophysiology, perception, social interaction, speech

Introduction

Speech communication is a fundamental aspect of the human experience, enabling us to express thoughts and emotions, form connections and foster social cohesion (Hari et al. 2015). Understanding the neural machinery at the foundation of speech communication has great importance over numerous dimensions, from the study of development, ageing and speech‐related deficits to the more fundamental understanding of how sounds are translated into meaning and vice versa. Such insights are also particularly valuable in application, as they inform groundbreaking developments that continue fostering a new generation of brain–computer interfaces (BCIs) (Hjortkjær et al. 2025; Metzger et al. 2023; Raghavan, Patel, et al. 2024).

The neurobiology of speech has been extensively investigated through lesion studies, behavioural experiments and measurements of neural activity, whether that be electrical or haemodynamic. Such studies have been made possible by using technologies such as electroencephalography (EEG) (Beres 2017; Kutas and Federmeier 2010), magnetoencephalography (MEG) (Salmelin 2007), functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) (Cusack et al. 2018) and functional near infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) (Chen et al. 2020). While much of that literature probed neural activity using heavily simplified experimental paradigms (e.g., listening to sequences of syllables), research in the last 15 years or so has increasingly shifted towards studying speech and language neurophysiology using more realistic ecologically valid tasks. Typical experiments in that space involve speech comprehension tasks, where participants are presented with audiobooks or other prerecorded audio or audio‐visual material. Recent developments are moving this research towards even more realistic tasks involving human interaction, facilitated by increasingly accessible hyperscanning technology (EEG, OPM‐MEG, fMRI and fNIRS) (Hirsch et al. 2021; Lin et al. 2023; Misaki et al. 2021; Montague 2002; Veneziani et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2025) and even mobile recording technology, such as wireless EEG (Goregliad Fjaellingsdal et al. 2020). As we approach this potential methodological shift, it is important to understand if the motivation and future advances in this area are primarily technological, or if we might be at the frontier of a groundbreaking new understanding of brain functions.

As is often the case, the reality likely lies somewhere in the middle. There is certainly a technological drive behind these innovations, and there is also potential for new scientific discoveries. While these technological developments have already begun to take shape (e.g., hyperscanning technology and mobile EEG technologies), the research vision for exploiting them in speech neurophysiology remains scattered and often blurry. This manuscript attempts to provide some clarity on this line of work and its future directions by discussing two key questions: Have realistic experiments involving continuous speech listening contributed to our understanding of speech neurophysiology? Could the study of more interactive tasks and realistic scenarios contribute to speech neurophysiology with truly novel insights?

After a brief introduction to common experimental designs and the challenges they pose (Section 2), this manuscript discusses those two questions in dedicated sections (Sections 3 and 4), concluding with a perspective on key theoretical questions that have the potential of bringing a considerable shift to our understanding of the neural functions underpinning speech processing.

Common Methods and Designs

Analysis methods and experimental designs typically come as a pair, as experiments are built with particular analyses in mind. Some designs are rigid, in that they are only suited for particular analyses. Others are quite flexible, in that they can be reanalysed in many ways. This section provides a brief description of some key designs and analytical methods, offering a brief overview of a complex experimental landscape, with a focus on receptive speech. This overview serves as a foundation for the discussion on more realistic experiments in Sections 3 and 4.

The Discrete‐to‐Continuous Dimension

Receptive speech involves the processing of a myriad of acoustic and linguistic properties by a complex and distributed neural network. Real‐life speech streams typically involve connected word sequences, with their processing being influenced by their proximal context (e.g., perceptual restoration effect) (Leonard et al. 2016). An additional complexity is that speech units at different timescales are processed simultaneously and in synergy with each other (Gwilliams et al. 2025). As such, methodologies for isolating neural activity relating to different aspects of speech and language processing are crucial for shedding light on the underlying neural infrastructure. To that end, the traditional approach involves the design of tailored experiments that isolate the property of interest from other possible covariates. For example, designs that involve listening to isolated syllables or words can be used to pinpoint cortical areas that are implicated in syllable or word encoding and discrimination (Eichele et al. 2005; Shtyrov et al. 2000), while excluding the impact of other linguistic phenomena (e.g., semantics and syntax) by construction. Methodologies such as event‐related potentials or event‐related fields (ERP/ERF) are common approaches for measuring the average neural activations corresponding to (what is assumed to be) discrete events of interest. A similar experimental rationale can be adopted to isolate contextual effects, by measuring ERP/ERFs in correspondence to discrete speech units that are more or less expected depending on the prior context. This has been done extensively in the N400/P600 literature (Kutas and Federmeier 2010; Osterhout and Mobley 1995; van Herten et al. 2005) that, for example, sheds light on important synergies between bottom‐up and top‐down processes in speech processing and on speech development in the first months of life (Kuhl 2004; Zhao and Kuhl 2022).

Experiments involving such discrete analytical methods span from technically simplistic designs (e.g., syllable listening) to more challenging ones (e.g., observing syntactic and semantic violations), where the linguistic content must be carefully manipulated to maximise and balance the effects of interest (Yamada and Neville 2007). One critical downside of this type of experiment is that its tasks are usually far from real‐world activities, raising questions as to whether our brains operate in the same way ‘in the wild’. This is far from trivial. This manuscript explores how we can study language as it naturally happens in the real world, reviewing the progress and challenges involved. We emphasise that separating the brain's processing of different speech and language properties, as well as isolating speech‐related activity from other overlapping processes, is challenging but essential. This effort continues to reshape our understanding and may reveal surprising aspects of how the brain deals with speech.

How can we study speech neurophysiology with more realistic tasks? From a technical perspective, a simple experimental design involves the listening of prerecorded speech streams from, for example, audiobooks or podcasts. Those speech streams typically consist of a continuous and coherent sequence of semantic concepts across multiple consecutive sentences. However, the concept of discrete and continuous becomes blurry when considering natural speech. Acoustic properties like the sound envelope are continuous signals, and the study of sound envelope‐brain relationships have been extensively explored using methods like the temporal response function (TRF) (Crosse et al. 2016; Crosse et al. 2021), phase‐locking/phase‐tracking measurements (Luo and Poeppel 2007), cross‐correlation (Millman et al. 2015), canonical correlation analysis (De Cheveigné et al. 2018), back‐to‐back regression (King et al. 2020) and linear mixed effects models (Mai et al. 2024). Linguistic units like phonemes and words can also be studied with those methods, which consider such units as part of a time continuum (Figure 1). In these instances, the proximal context can impact the processing of subsequent units, with a likely temporal overlap in their processing. Depending on the specific hypotheses and assumptions, it is also possible to ignore those temporal dependencies and perform analyses (e.g., ERP/ERF) that consider them as discrete isolated events, albeit with some limitations (Khalighinejad et al. 2017).

Experiments involving speech streams can vary greatly in complexity. In its basic form, the experimental design simply requires audio recordings, a device for measuring neural activity (e.g., EEG and MEG) and an accurate temporal synchronisation between the two. On the other hand, methods for analysing the multifaceted nature of those data come with some challenges and have only become established and standardised in recent years (Crosse et al. 2016; Crosse et al. 2021; De Cheveigné et al. 2018; Di Liberto et al. 2024; Di Liberto et al. 2021; Heilbron et al. 2022; King et al. 2020). One of the main difficulties lies in choosing a stimulus, wherein the possibilities of design dimensions are seemingly endless. Such design considerations include number of speakers, scripted versus spontaneous speech, monologue versus dialogue, emotion type and intensity, accent, language, audio quality and the presence of dysfluencies, among many others. The large number of dimensions highlights the importance of being cautious when drawing conclusions from a single study and of considering appropriate controls and variations. Certainly, such challenges have been mitigated by the advancement of computational and statistical analytical techniques, rendering the use of natural language stimuli more commonplace in neuroscience experiments (Hamilton and Huth 2020).

Another clever method has also been proposed, namely the frequency‐tagging paradigm, which offers an analysis that is somewhat in‐between discrete and continuous. This controlled stimulation design involves speech stimuli consisting of continuous sequences of words, played in a precise rhythmic manner, with different timescales of the linguistic structure being associated with distinct frequencies (Ding et al. 2016; Luo and Ding 2024). While the artificial nature of that design makes the listening experience quite unusual (but note the potential link with sung speech, which is also rhythmic), the paradigm associates linguistic structures at different timescales with precise and distinct frequencies (Martorell et al. 2023), which can be elegantly studied with a Fourier transform analysis. Nonetheless, that also leads to a somewhat unnatural experiment, with neural activations that may differ from what is measured with more realistic tasks (Martorell 2024). This trade‐off between experimental manipulation in controlled tasks versus introducing confounds in more realistic tasks lies at the heart of uncovering whether insights from social speech neurophysiology will drive a genuine leap forward.

The Complexity of Speech Interaction

Continuous speech listening experiments have proven effective for studying the multifaceted speech processing network, by enabling the measurement of neural signatures of multiple acoustic and linguistic properties with a single experiment. Two key issues should be noted: First, typical experimental scenarios involve nonrealistic laboratory settings, where participants minimise motor movements and are alone in a dark sound‐proof room (Levy et al. 2025). Second, while controlled experiments afford the ability to isolate specific properties of interest, natural speech in realistic scenarios normally occurs during interactive communication. The main challenge is designing experimental set‐ups to disentangle the concurrent phenomena that complicate interaction, including both exogenous (e.g., background noise) and endogenous processes (e.g., trust, social anxiety and empathy).

The opportunity for studying social speech neurophysiology comes at a cost: the social dimension adds an enormous number of factors, as well as nonnegligible technical complexities. One of the key concerns regards the experimental design: What could be the ideal designs for studying the neurophysiology of speech interaction? Attempts at outlining various design elements in multispeaker scenarios have reached limited success, as considerations in experimental design span across norms of diverse disciplines and modalities (Gregori et al. 2023; Offrede et al. 2021). Still, designing speech interaction experiments is possible and has been explored in specific subfields. For example, one could record neural data from participant dyads as they freely interact (Kinreich et al. 2017; Norton et al. 2022). Such paradigms could illuminate how brains in dyads process speech during listening (from sound to meaning), tease apart the neural processes enabling speech production, quantify brain‐to‐brain synchronisation and derive neural metrics for studying the many other factors that have a role in speech communication. However, a design of this kind comes with its own complexities, for example, regarding how to prompt the conversation, how to ensure that the dialogue goes both ways, how to control for individual biases (e.g., social biases), familiarity (e.g., with the person, accent or topic of conversation) and social anxiety, among other phenomena. The number of agents and their roles are also key factors, as they can lead to different types of social interaction. For instance, consider that an agent might be human or based on artificial intelligence (AI) (Diederich et al. 2022); an agent may play different roles in an interaction, such as an ‘eavesdropper’ (coined as unremarked listener in Figure 1, which we clarify in Section 4.1) or an ‘addressee’ (Bell 1984); a meeting might be in person, virtual or hybrid; an interaction might involve two or more parties (Branigan 2006); the speech might be fully unidirectional (e.g., movie watching in copresence), primarily unidirectional (e.g., one‐to‐many lecture) or bidirectional (e.g., conversation); and the social context may also vary in formality (e.g., job interview vs. casual conversation). All these possibilities pose various degrees of speech accommodation and audience design factors and which must be carefully considered to isolate the neurophysiological signals relevant to the researcher's hypotheses.

The other key concern involves the purely technical obstacles that come with the study of speech interaction in terms of the equipment that is available for such neural recordings. Hyperscanning, which refers to the simultaneous recording of neural data from multiple individuals at once, has been explored for at least 20–25 years (Flexer and Makeig 2007; May 2004; Montague 2002). The simultaneous recording of neural data from multiple individuals at once increases the typical data collection risks, such as participant drop‐out (which typically means cancelling the session for both participants), issues with the recording device (e.g., battery, damaged sensors), as well as introducing challenges such as EEG–EEG synchronisation. Nonetheless, modern devices developed for hyperscanning minimise those risks, providing automation and control to experimental setups in speech neurophysiology and many other domains (Holmes et al. 2023; Kothe et al. 2025). Furthermore, hyperscanning is not a mandatory choice for studying social speech interaction, as that can also be done by recording neural data from a single participant in real or simulated social contexts—albeit with some data analysis limitations.

When considering speech interactions, it is also necessary to consider the challenge of recording and processing the speech sounds. This may arise, for example, in scenarios where two or more participants engage in conversations. Depending on the specific experiment, the first challenge may be the separation of sound mixture into speech streams from each distinct speaker, with methodologies (e.g., beamforming and bling source separation) that should be defined before data collection to ensure that the appropriate equipment is used (e.g., microphone array). Indeed, experiments where participants speak one at a time, without overlap, make stream segregation trivial. Next, speech sounds would typically need to be transcribed and temporally aligned for the purpose of language feature extraction and analysis (Gorman et al. 2011). Depending on the target analysis, those features must also be time‐aligned with the EEG and preprocessed for behaviours that emerge in spontaneous natural speech. For example, dysfluencies are interruptions of the speech flow that can be utilised voluntarily to regulate and guide the course of a conversation (Clark and Tree 2002; Kowal 2009) or involuntarily in cases like stuttering (Mulligan et al. 2001). We address dysfluencies in further detail in the following section.

Processing steps are considerably more complex for speech interactions than for audiobook listening. Prerecorded material can involve professional readers in a quiet environment, providing ideal conditions for accurate feature extraction, unlike spontaneous, interactive speech. Furthermore, speech and language features typically need to be extracted only once for audiobook listening or similar experiments, as the speech material is reused across participants. Speech interaction experiments, instead, would in principle require a dedicated feature extraction for each experimental session and participant, raising concerns on the scalability of this process. Indeed, ongoing work has explored experimental or analytical simplifications that would still allow for the study of speech interaction in reasonably realistic setups, which we discuss below.

Note that, while our discussion focusses on the two continua above (i.e., discrete‐to‐continuous and interaction; Figure 1), there exist other important dimensions of interest that we do not cover in this manuscript. One important dimension is the visual modality, including verbal and nonverbal cues that have a central role in social interaction (Gregori et al. 2023). Another important area of study is the speech production side of the interaction. Furthermore, one very positive trend to mention is the rising interest in sharing anonymised data after publication of the results, which we think will substantially speed up the search for solutions to the complexities and challenges in speech neurophysiology. In addition to increased transparency, data (and code) sharing also provides an opportunity for determining whether a given result replicates across different datasets, or how it is influenced by factors such as neural recording technology, stimulus material, task and participant cohort. Data sharing in this area has also extended to intracranial neural recordings, with publicly available neural data like the Podcast ECoG dataset, which includes neural recordings from nine individuals listening to podcasts, with the corresponding acoustic and linguistic features also provided (Zada, Nastase, Aubrey, et al. 2025). This and other similar contributions producing precious data (Wang et al. 2025) can be very important, as these kinds of rare neural recordings could be reanalysed in numerous manners beyond the original goals of a given study, which could take years (or may not be possible) for a single research team.

Discoveries With Designs Involving Speech Streams

Early neurophysiology research studying the listening of continuous speech explored phenomena such as stimulus‐to‐brain and brain‐to‐brain synchronisation (e.g., correlation between repetitions, cortical rhythms or individuals) (Luo and Poeppel 2007; Peelle and Davis 2012). When recording neural signals during speech listening, the neural traces naturally reflect a myriad of processes, spanning from sound to semantics. One key challenge is to determine what exactly contributes to the neural signal, characterising temporal, spatial and spectral origins of its components. One approach for isolating a neural process of interest involves contrasting neural activations in tailored experimental conditions. For example, comparing neural responses to intelligible and unintelligible speech has elucidated what cortical sites are involved in speech comprehension, besides sound processing (Friederici 2011; Specht 2014; Di Liberto et al. 2015; Di Liberto and Lalor 2017; Di Liberto, Crosse, and Lalor 2018; Di Liberto, Lalor, and Millman 2018; Vanthornhout et al. 2018; Lesenfants et al. 2019).

This section discusses some of the key impacts of speech stream neurophysiology. While we do not intend this section to be comprehensive, we mention some of the most distinctive research contributions to date, aiming to spark reflections on how the study of speech streams has contributed to our understanding of speech neurophysiology. Rather than detailing a chronological list of findings, we focus on key areas of interest in this space: neural encoding of linguistic units, functional brain mapping, neural entrainment and decoding (Figure 2). We then conclude with a section on how speech neurophysiology research has incorporated and contributed to recent breakthroughs in generative AI.

Key areas of interest in speech neurophysiology. Research contributions in speech neurophysiology typically focus on determining what information our brain encodes, where that is encoded in the brain spatially and what neural rates are involved. Altogether, these dimensions inform us on how speech is transformed into meaning. The combination of these dimensions also contributes to research on the decoding of, for example, sound, language or auditory attention, contributing to areas such as brain–computer interfaces.

One challenge in speech neurophysiology is the large number of dimensions, which can lead to a wide variety of experiments, with connections that are not always clear. One advantage (among many) of studying continuous speech is that numerous metrics can be derived from a single participant and task, enabling more direct comparisons between the underlying neural processes. Even so, research in this space is still quite varied, with single studies often focusing on only one (or sometimes two) of the dimensions in Figure 2. Understanding the neural processing of speech will ultimately require amalgamating some of these dimensions into a cohesive view of brain functioning.

For example, let us consider studies relating developmental dyslexia with atypical EEG delta‐band (~0.5–4 Hz) stimulus‐neural synchronisation (Power et al. 2016), or studies relating that same frequency band with speech comprehension (Di Liberto, Peter, et al. 2018). Taken in isolation, such findings provide valuable characterisations of delta‐band activity, but their true impact emerges when considered within the broader literature. The following sections offer a brief review of that and other research in this space, organised into the four key areas of interest.

Neural Encoding of Linguistic Units [WHAT]

How does the human cortex transform speech into abstract meaning? What speech and language properties are explicitly represented in our brain? How does that encoding change over the course of our lives, for example throughout childhood? These and other related questions have been extensively studied via behavioural and neural investigations, generating a large repertoire of results and numerous theories. While many unanswered questions and ongoing debates remain, studying the neurophysiology of speech stream processing has greatly contributed to that research.

To begin, how do we know what representations of speech to search for in the brain? Linguistics has laid the groundwork by defining representational units, providing the conceptual framework that shapes questions about the neurobiological organisation of language (Hasson et al. 2018; Hickok and Poeppel 2007; Poeppel 2014). Linguists have left a trail of feature encoders, for example with the proposal of a hierarchical organisation of language structure, where the smallest units of sound piece together to form increasingly larger units of meaning, up to syllables, words and then sentences. Such encoders have emerged from linguistic theories that enable the interpretability of neural signals, such as phoneme processing (Chomsky and Halle 1968; Eimas et al. 1971), semantic context (Firth 1957), and prediction and surprisal (Levy 2008), to name a few. The in‐depth work on those linguistic concepts and psycholinguistic theories has then led to research providing a neurophysiological validation of some of those constructs. While we do not cover the origin of linguistic units in detail in this manuscript, this section summarises some of the key findings in the speech neurophysiology literature informing us on the neural encoding of specific linguistic units. We expect that such insights will play a vital role in illuminating the neural underpinnings of social speech, allowing scientists to dissect sound and language processing from communication goals and other endogenous factors at the foundation of social speech.

Multivariate analysis methods have proven effective in measuring the neural processing of a multitude of acoustic and linguistic properties when applied to neural data recorded during continuous speech listening. While early research was primarily concerned with the neural encoding of the sound envelope (Ding and Simon 2012; Lalor and Foxe 2010), numerous studies in the last decade or so have shed light on the encoding of linguistic properties at the phonological (Di Liberto et al. 2015; Mesgarani et al. 2014), syntactic (Brennan and Hale 2019), prosodic (Gnanateja et al. 2025; Teoh et al. 2019) and lexical levels (Brodbeck et al. 2018; Broderick et al. 2018; Chalehchaleh et al. 2025). That work primarily involved listening tasks using continuous speech recordings, for example, from audiobooks and podcasts. These tasks are experimentally appealing because they are more pleasant for participants than traditional single‐syllable or word listening paradigms, allow multiple metrics to be extracted from a single EEG recording and enable extensive reanalysis, making data sharing particularly valuable.

One main contribution of speech neurophysiology research using continuous streams is the generalisation of previous findings to more realistic listening tasks, increasing the level of detail and clarity on the neural representation of distinct stimulus properties. For example, the temporal progression of the speech to meaning transformation proposed based on ERP/ERF results (Salmelin 2007) could not clearly resolve the temporal overlap of the various responses to sound, phonology and lexical–semantic analysis. The study of continuous speech streams involves regression methods designed to tackle that temporal overlap, carrying out that separation across different timescales (Di Liberto et al. 2021; Di Liberto et al. 2015). This has led to new data and theories on how our brain might handle that overlap (Gwilliams et al. 2022; Gwilliams et al. 2025).

In the context of phonological processing, an ongoing debate concerns whether and how abstract phonological representations play a role in how the brain encodes and processes continuous speech streams (Daube et al. 2019; Port and Leary 2005; Ramscar and Port 2016; Stilp and Kluender 2010). Previous research using audiobook listening tasks suggested that neural responses to continuous speech reflect the acoustically invariant encoding of phonemic identities (Di Liberto et al. 2015). That work used careful control analyses to isolate neural correlates of acoustically invariant phonological information. Because accounting for all possible acoustic features is not possible, that result initially raised legitimate concerns that the reported phonological neural indices might simply reflect residual acoustic representations rather than an acoustically invariant phonological representation (Daube et al. 2019). Since then, extensive work has addressed this issue by testing whether these indices are sensitive to well‐established phonological manipulations. Evidence shows that they strongly relate to phonological awareness (Di Liberto, Crosse, and Lalor 2018; Di Liberto, Peter, et al. 2018), comprehension (Di Liberto, Lalor, and Millman 2018; Millman et al. 2015), language proficiency (Di Liberto et al. 2021), early development (Di Liberto et al. 2023), hearing impairment (Carta et al. 2024) and speech register (Piazza et al. 2025). Those metrics have also been explored across multiple languages (Carta et al. 2024; Lesenfants et al. 2019) and with distinct modulations from attention (Raghavan et al. 2023; Teoh et al. 2022). That work was also recently validated and extended by a team using intracranial data (Mai et al. 2024) and has been complemented by the finding that phonological processing persists with a substantial overlap between neighbouring units (Gwilliams et al. 2022) and is influenced by predictive mechanisms (Brodbeck et al. 2018; Di Liberto et al. 2023; Di Liberto et al. 2019). Collectively, these results relying on speech streams support the view that phonological processing is central to speech perception in the human brain, offering rich insights into cortical substrates such as the superior and lateral temporal cortex, ventral/dorsal parietal cortex and inferior prefrontal cortex (Gong et al. 2023; Mesgarani et al. 2014). These results have far‐reaching implications for phonological theory and for models of language representation and learning in the brain (Di Liberto et al. 2023).

The study of speech streams has also deepened our understanding of early speech development. Research using sound discrimination tasks provided compelling evidence that, between 6 and 12 months of age, infants begin to specialise their speech processing in response to the language they are acquiring (Kuhl 2004). However, because these studies focus on basic phonetic contrasts, often using isolated sounds, they do not necessarily inform us on how the human brain represents these distinctions during the processing of continuous speech. 1 Studies on the neurophysiology of continuous speech offer that complementary view, confirming and refining previous results. While a large part of that research focusses on neural entrainment metrics (Section 3.3), there is also evidence that an acoustically invariant encoding of phonetic features starts forming between 4 and 7 months of age (Attaheri et al. 2022; Di Liberto et al. 2023), when considering continuous speech streams (in that specific case, in the form of nursery rhymes). Compelling arguments also link the timing of language acquisition to the relatively late maturation of fast brain activity in infants, offering a plausible explanation for their early reliance on slow speech modulations and longer phonological units (Menn et al. 2023a, 2023b). In parallel, research on early development and language acquisition has also drawn comparisons with large language models (LLMs), highlighting notable similarities in stages and schemes of language acquisition observed in the children's brain and in LLMs (Evanson et al. 2023).

Another important property of speech streams involves the temporal regularities of sound and language. These regularities are present at various timescales concerning, for example, phoneme sequences (phonotactics), syntactic units, word form and semantic meaning (Kaufeld et al. 2020; Keitel et al. 2018). The neural processing of those regularities has been studied in‐depth for decades, forming an extensive literature on semantic and syntactic expectedness and violation (Bornkessel‐Schlesewsky and Schlesewsky 2008; Kutas and Federmeier 2010; Kutas et al. 1984; van Herten et al. 2005). While that research typically focussed on expectations at a single linguistic level, studying the processing of speech streams can inform us about the simultaneous unfolding of neural predictions at those multiple linguistic levels. In contrast with the ‘reactive’ perspective, where the focus is the neural consequence of an unexpected event, other research has also found that linguistic predictions shape the early encoding of speech sounds (Broderick et al. 2019; Dou et al. 2024) and even reported anticipatory neural activations reflecting those predictions (Goldstein et al. 2022; Leonard et al. 2016). Once again, studying speech streams presents favourable conditions for studying these phenomena from a single neural trace.

Functional Brain Mapping [WHERE]

The previous section concerns what properties are encoded in the human cortex during speech listening and what processes are involved (e.g., lexical prediction). Another important angle involves the precise identification of the spatial origins and pathways enabling speech listening. Indeed, our field is interested in mapping brain areas to their function, and to identify the corresponding neural pathways. Frameworks like the dual‐stream model have guided neurophysiology research in this area for about two decades (Hickok and Poeppel 2007). The study of speech streams combined with advances in neural recording technology has led to a more detailed brain mapping of the neurophysiology of speech listening.

Previous work often involved assumptions involving, for example, the sequential nature of the pathways transforming speech into meaning. Indeed, that simplification is often carried out for practical reasons, as there is convincing evidence on a complex interplay of bottom‐up and top‐down processes, as well as results pointing to parallel pathways even at the acoustical‐phonological interface (Hamilton et al. 2021). Similar research has been carried out that studies the neural processing of more abstract linguistic constructs. Syntactic structure, for instance, has been associated with various cortical sites, with research on continuous speech streams pointing to a key role of the anterior temporal lobe (Brennan et al. 2012; Brennan et al. 2016). Initially, work using manually labelled predicate argument structures indicated that possibility, paving the way to the more recent use of computational models of parsing effort. Interestingly, combinatory categorial grammars have been shown to be a better model of the neural processing of language structure than context‐free grammars and LLMs (He et al. 2024; Stanojević et al. 2023). Other research has explored the neural encoding of prosody, finding Heschl's gyrus, the superior temporal plane and the superior temporal gyrus to be central to its processing (Gnanateja et al. 2025), with the delta‐band being of particular importance (Teoh et al. 2019).

The spatial resolution has also increased (Chung et al. 2022). Evidence from fMRI and intracranial EEG substantially magnified our previous understanding of the spatial organisation of speech in the human cortex (Cusack et al. 2018; Formisano et al. 2008). More recently, findings from intracranial recordings detailing the neural encoding of speech sounds in the human auditory cortex (Mesgarani et al. 2014) have been revisited by evidence from higher‐density intracranial data, even at the level of single‐neurons (e.g., Neuropixels arrays) (Leonard et al. 2024). Those results are partly confirmatory, as they found neural populations across the depth of cortex to be tuned to a dominant speech feature (Mesgarani et al. 2014; O'Sullivan et al. 2019). On the other hand, the human cortex also shows encoding to other speech features across the depth dimension, indicating a more complex picture than previously observable that highlights the complexity and varied neural encoding across the cortical layers (Leonard et al. 2024).

This brief overview of the spatial dimension of continuous speech neurophysiology is intended to prompt reflection on the future of this domain. Emerging high‐density intracranial recording technologies will soon generate vast datasets—how will we harness them? Could they help resolve ongoing debates and challenge established knowledge? Or will they open distinct dimensions of inquiry while shedding less light than desired on existing questions?

Low‐Frequency Neural Entrainment [FREQUENCY]

Neural signals at different rates align with incoming sounds. This phenomenon, known as neural entrainment in the broad sense or neural tracking, has been associated with various potential underlying mechanisms, such as evoked responses (Oganian et al. 2023), endogenous neural oscillations synchronising to the stimulus (Obleser and Kayser 2019) and other endogenous activations related to auditory predictions and sensory restoration (Brodbeck et al. 2020; Cervantes Constantino and Simon 2018; Leonard et al. 2016; Morillon and Schroeder 2015). Despite being a complex phenomenon with ongoing debates about its contributors (Chalas et al. 2023; Duecker et al. 2024; Haegens 2020; Lalor and Nidiffer 2025; Mai and Wang 2023), researchers have extensively studied its relationship with factors such as comprehension and attention (Broderick et al. 2018; Heilbron et al. 2022; Power et al. 2012), in addition to investigating how it is impacted by brain development, ageing, hearing impairment, neurodiversity and speech‐related deficits (Broderick et al. 2021; Carta et al. 2024; Di Liberto, Peter, et al. 2018; Kries et al. 2024; Przysinda et al. 2025; Wynn Camille et al. 2018). The use of speech streams for these investigations has substantially advanced our understanding of this phenomenon, informing us about the potential functional roles of different neural rates for speech perception. Yet, the large set of results in that domain is at times difficult to reconcile and remains under debate.

Delta (~0.5–4 Hz) and theta (~4–8 Hz) band rates are crucial for neural tracking of the acoustic envelope, potentially aiding speech segmentation and syntactic processing of linguistic units at timescales corresponding to their cycles (Martorell et al. 2023; Peelle et al. 2013). Other studies have shown that brain‐acoustic entrainment in delta and theta covaries with speech intelligibility (Doelling et al. 2014; Mai et al. 2016; Peelle et al. 2013), with delta linked to comprehension and theta to speech clarity (Di Liberto, Crosse, and Lalor 2018; Di Liberto, Lalor, and Millman 2018; Etard and Reichenbach 2019). However, other findings suggest that delta is more related to speech chunking and discourse‐level parsing than meaning (Boucher et al. 2019), and theta is entrained more generally to the processing of acoustic attributes (Boucher et al. 2019; Kösem and van Wassenhove 2017). In fact, delta rates appear to have an important role since birth, with some proposing that they might be central for learning speech segmentation, even before the processing of word meaning (Attaheri et al. 2022). From there, the alpha band (~8–13 Hz) is a hallmark of the higher‐level sensory and cognitive processes involved in speech‐ and language‐related processing and comprehension, such as working memory and attention. This has been demonstrated in active listening tasks measuring speech intelligibility (Dimitrijevic et al. 2017) and comprehending speech under adverse conditions (Obleser and Weisz 2012). Such processes of attention are modulated in EEG responses in studies that investigate selective attention in speech (Näätänen et al. 2002; Power et al. 2012). Other studies highlight the function of alpha rates, alongside beta rates, in other low‐level mechanistic operations facilitating language comprehension (Zioga et al. 2023), as well as the unification of semantic and syntactic information in a ‘desynchronization effect’ (Lam et al. 2016).

A similar heterogeneity can be observed when considering higher neural rates. Beta (~13–30 Hz) rates were reported to be involved in syntactic processing (Bastiaansen et al. 2010), though predictive mechanisms implicate them in the semantic processing of upcoming words when coupled with gamma rates (Meyer 2018). Early studies have suggested gamma rates (30–70 Hz) to also be involved in lexico–semantic retrieval in EEG/MEG (Lutzenberger et al. 1994; Pulvermüller et al. 1997), while a wide range of literature suggests neural signals at gamma rates to be related to sound amplitude modulations at subsyllabic frequencies (Lizarazu et al. 2019; Meyer 2018). Predictive processing has become increasingly adopted in recent years to frame functions of oscillations (Dogonasheva et al. 2025; Giraud and Poeppel 2012), whereby top‐down predictions in the beta rates mediate sentence‐level representation and facilitate comprehension (Lewis et al. 2016; Mamashli et al. 2019). Moreover, gamma power was found to be correlated with an upcoming word's predictability rather than semantic integration (Lam et al. 2016; Wang et al. 2012), further supported by findings that high‐gamma frequency neural signals index bottom‐up propagation of the prediction error (Prystauka and Lewis 2019). This suggests complementary functions at the beta and gamma rates in the predictive processing framework (Meyer 2018; Weissbart and Martin 2024), while the functions of these bands in the frequency domain are posed as distinctly segregated (Bastiaansen and Hagoort 2015).

A wealth of research has also been conducted on the role of phase‐amplitude cross‐frequency coupling (PAC) and has shed light on many facets implicated in speech neurophysiology such as speech processing and behaviour (Arnal et al. 2015; Attaheri et al. 2022; Lakatos et al. 2008; Lizarazu et al. 2019; Mai et al. 2016; Meyer 2018), interaction between memory systems (Fell and Axmacher 2011; Lisman and Jensen 2013) and oscillation‐mediated predictive processing mechanisms (Weissbart and Martin 2024; Zoefel and VanRullen 2015), among others. This has yielded key insights in children's acquisition of speech (Goswami 2019), dynamics of cross‐language communication (Liu et al. 2021) and characterising speech discrimination behaviour of hearing‐impaired individuals (Wang et al. 2023), to name a few.

In addition, research has been substantially augmented by studying neural entrainment in participant cohorts with particular deficits or neuro‐diversities. For example, impaired neural entrainment in delta rates has been associated with difficulties in crucial speech processing tasks, such as phonological awareness in developmental dyslexia (Di Liberto, Peter, et al. 2018; Goswami 2011). A similar parallel is drawn in neurodiverse populations, such as in children and adults with spectrum disorder who experience impaired speech rate entrainment (Wynn Camille et al. 2018), and cohorts that experience abnormal neural entrainment (e.g., attention‐deficit/hyperactivity disorder) and selective attention deficits (Calderone et al. 2014; Obleser and Kayser 2019).

This section briefly covers a small part of the literature, prompting one simple reflection: more work is necessary to reach a consensus on what neural entrainment in the broad sense reflects exactly. The large number of dimensions to consider when analysing that literature (e.g., experimental design, stimulus material, analysis method and neural recording modality) may mean that apparent contradiction might be reconciled. In fact, it is possible that entrainment in a given band (or PAC) reflects numerous phenomena, some of which are more prominent than others depending on the experimental paradigm and analysis. This complex landscape of results highlights the extensive effort to study this phenomenon, with heterogeneous methodologies and results that are complementary and contradictory at times, likely influenced by specific stimuli and tasks. Certainly, more work is needed (Chalas et al. 2023).

One angle is that this line of work might inform theoretical models. For example, the development of the Temporal Sampling Framework of developmental dyslexia (Goswami 2011) was informed by a variety of both behavioural (e.g., finger tapping tasks) (Thomson and Goswami 2008) and neural evidence, including studies examining the neural encoding of speech rhythms, and then further tested with tailored experimentation (Flanagan et al. 2025). This led to a comprehensive model that explains how diversities in the processing of different speech rhythms can lead to phonological and reading difficulties. The study of speech streams and the corresponding neural activity was a crucial component to that research, complementing behavioural evidence by offering a more direct view into how speech and neural rhythms align in different cohorts of participants.

Recent work has also recognised that not all EEG and MEG activity reflects periodic components. These signals contain a nonoscillatory, aperiodic background component that roughly follows a 1/f relationship, where power decreases with increasing frequency. Although this property has long been known, it is now increasingly regarded as a meaningful source of neural information rather than mere noise to be removed or controlled for (Donoghue et al. 2020; Gao et al. 2017). That adds yet another interesting dimension that should be considered when carrying out research in this area, or when interpreting prior work.

Speech Decoding and Brain‐Computer Interfaces

Speech neurophysiology has also a BCI dimension. While developments have been initially slow in this area, the last decade or so has seen a growing interest in auditory attention decoding interfaces. Previous research demonstrated that, given a multitalker scenario, attended and unattended speech streams are encoded differently by our brains (Power et al. 2012; Zion Golumbic et al. 2013). That difference can be detected by neural recording technologies such as EEG, enabling the robust decoding of the focus of attention (Hjortkjær et al. 2025; O'Sullivan et al. 2015; Raghavan, Patel, et al. 2024; Straetmans et al. 2024). The breakthrough has attracted a substantial interest both in academia and industry. Part of that work progressed to investigate the neural underpinnings of attention mechanisms across various cohorts of interest (Carta et al. 2024; Choudhari et al. 2024; O'Sullivan et al. 2019), while other work further investigated attention decoding for BCI development (Han et al. 2023; Raghavan, O'Sullivan, et al. 2024; Raghavan, Patel, et al. 2024). We look forward to seeing future developments in this direction, which will likely involve personalised multimodal systems combining biosignals of various kinds.

Another very exciting recent development involves transcription of brain signals to text or speech. While this research is primarily possible using invasive recordings, for example, in patients that are fully paralysed (Metzger et al. 2023; Metzger et al. 2022; Moses David et al. 2021; Pei et al. 2011), recent developments have also explored with some success the possibility of decoding text from brain signals recorded with MEG (Défossez et al. 2023; Lévy et al. 2025) while EEG was much less accurate in that specific study. This recent development might lead to the most naturalistic brain‐to‐text decoding approach to date, going beyond the well‐known P300 speller paradigm (Farwell and Donchin 1988). The brain‐to‐text decoding may be performed based on various tasks, from attempted hand‐writing movements (Lévy et al. 2025) to imagined speech articulation (Pei et al. 2011). While most noninvasive decoding studies focus on speech listening or text typing recordings, we anticipate growing efforts to demonstrate further proofs‐of‐concept for these methods (both invasive and noninvasive), for example in paralysed patients attempting to speak or type.

Computational Models

The work of linguistics has also proliferated into the field of artificial intelligence, spawning a generation of LLMs that rely on large amounts of data to capture the statistical patterns of human language. These models have been used by relating neural signals with the learned representations of language projected in vector spaces, also known as embeddings, and with other LLM‐derived features of language, such as word entropy and next‐word surprise. Both approaches have been shown to produce remarkably high LLM‐brain correlations across different neural recording modalities, including EEG, intracranial EEG, MEG and fMRI (Goldstein et al. 2022; He et al. 2024).

One remarkable property of these LLM‐based features is that they are built without explicitly including linguistic assumptions, rules or constraints (e.g., phonotactic or syntax rules) when relying on LLMs trained in a self‐supervised manner. 2 This is the case for architectures such as the decoder‐only transformers (Radford et al. 2019; Vaswani et al. 2017), like the well‐known LLM GPT‐x or Mistral, which are trained to minimise next‐token prediction error. Numerous studies have successfully associated LLM‐derived features to neural signals (Chalehchaleh et al. 2025; Heilbron et al. 2022; Mischler et al. 2024; Tikochinski et al. 2023). Interestingly, this relationship has been studied from different angles, with some using LLMs to replace specific features that were used previously, such as semantic dissimilarity derived from word2vec embeddings or lexical surprise from n‐grams (Broderick et al. 2018; Broderick et al. 2021). While those features are typically unidimensional, as they represent distances in the embedding space or prediction surprise, brain activity has also been related to the actual multidimensional embedding values. Interestingly, recent work related LLM embeddings at progressively deeper hidden layers with neural activity, tracing a precise neural pathway for the speech‐to‐meaning transformation in a data‐driven manner (Caucheteux et al. 2022, 2023; Mischler et al. 2024). While that neural pathway was already known, there was no guarantee that LLMs would correlate with that transformation at all. In other words, LLMs may achieve excellent next‐token prediction through some other transformations, different from the human brain. Instead, it is remarkable to see that certain LLMs correlate with human brain activity even with regard to the intermediate steps of this transformation.

Does this mean that rule‐based or linguistic‐driven features are not useful anymore? Not necessarily. First of all, it is important to acknowledge that we know that neural signals (e.g., from EEG) represent certain speech and language properties because that was initially tested with features extracted from linguistic‐informed procedures (Brennan et al. 2012; Di Liberto et al. 2019). Second, simply associating some unknown features (e.g., hidden layer embeddings) with brain activity would leave important uncertainties, unless we can relate those results with what we know on certain brain areas or neural rhythms, or by interpreting those features by relating them with known linguistic constructs. As such, while that approach may not use linguistics‐driven models for feature extraction, linguistics remains fundamental for interpreting the resulting brain representations and dynamics. Indeed, there is no guarantee that those features even carry a linguistic meaning, and that it one consideration that should be kept in mind when choosing the model; using the latest state‐of‐the‐art off‐the‐shelf LLM might not always be optimal choice. It should also be considered that most research relating LLMs with neural activity focussed on text‐based LLMs. Intuitively, speech‐based LLMs or multimodal models might resemble neural activity even more closely. That is far from trivial, however, and new work in the near future will tell us more about opportunity. Similarly, LLM development does not build on linguistic theories and, as such, there is a chance that future developments will lead larger models away from linguistics and neurophysiological relevance, depending on what's optimal for the model objective (e.g., next‐token prediction).

So, one quest might involve identifying a set of most neurophysiologically plausible LLMs to be used in our field. That would at least bring some consistency to an area of research whereby different models are used across studies, even within the same research team. Appropriate data, code and model sharing becomes crucial in this context (Di Liberto et al. 2024; Makeig and Robbins 2024) as results using new LLM models will have to also be compared with other LLM models used in previous studies. Failure in doing do carries a risk for p‐hacking. For example, let us say that we obtain a nice‐looking result with a given LLM after failing with other five LLMs. Failing to acknowledge the negative results when publishing the study would constitute a big problem, as that would lead to a literature with a diverse set of models that succeed, while in reality those models might have failed in other studies. This is a difficult challenge that can be tackled via appropriate data sharing, as other researchers may then evaluate the consistent neurophysiological validity of an LLM across tasks, cohorts and recording modalities. While that issue also exists for linguistic‐driven models, LLMs increased the risk substantially due to the large number of possible models that one might select. In sum, our view is that this is a powerful new tool, where the excitement is matched by the risks. So, the future will probably involve a combination of these data‐driven methods with linguistic‐driven hypotheses and controls. One recommendation that we put forward in this context is to have strong motivations in place before using a certain model (e.g., a certain version of an LLM). For example, a previous study may have used that model, or a particular fine‐tuning was applied that might be relevant to the particular research question at hand. The other recommendation is indeed to report the negative results as well, as they might be important, especially when interpreting a result in the context of the literature or when aggregating results from different studies and investigators.

Summary

Studying speech neurophysiology using continuous streams has led to important scientific advances. Some of the progress involves the generalisation of previous findings to the more realistic context, confirming and extending prior models and results. This line of work produced new insights into how our brains cope with serial relationships and high temporal overlap between linguistic units during continuous speech listening. This is novel in that previous research attempted to avoid having those temporal overlaps in their experiments, due to the analytical challenge that they pose. The study of speech streams also enabled the investigation of how speech processing is supported by more general neural mechanisms, such as neural entrainment. Finally, decoding research in this domain has been attracting quite some interest across academia and industry in recent years, bringing us much closer to real‐world applications (Metzger et al. 2023).

Social Speech Neurophysiology

Having clarified the key advantages of studying the neural processing of speech streams, a natural question arises: Would it be beneficial to explore the neurophysiology of even more realistic scenarios involving social interaction? This section presents early results on the study of social speech and discusses our perspective on which directions might be precursors to scientific breakthroughs.

Beyond Audiobook Listening: The Social Dimension

What could be more realistic than listening to audiobooks? Audiobook listening is a real‐world task, which is also similar to common activities like radio and podcast listening. However, those tasks do not capture the most important use of speech: social interaction.

The social dimension of speech encompasses a wide range of research domains, including anxiety, trust, empathy, communication accommodation and disorders affecting social interaction (Figure 3). While some of these domains have been studied in controlled scenarios, such experiments may be far removed from the way our brains function during real speech interactions (Hung et al. 2024; Rigoulot et al. 2014). Moving from audiobook listening to interactive speech is at least as significant methodologically as the earlier transition from discrete stimuli (e.g., isolated syllables) to audiobook listening discussed in the previous section. These experiments enable the study of a whole range of phenomena that are particular or especially important for social interaction, such as turn‐taking (Bögels 2020), brain‐to‐brain synchronisation (Pérez et al. 2017) and neural encoding alignment (Zada et al. 2024). In turn, investigating speech interaction through neurophysiology could provide fresh insights into ongoing research, as well as open up new research directions.

Opportunities and challenges of social speech neurophysiology. This diagram highlights that social scenarios require considering numerous factors that are typically controlled for when studying receptive speech in social‐isolated lab scenarios. The interaction complexity axis (orange) indicates four different levels of interaction, ranging from no interaction, where the speech material does not account for the listener (unremarked listener), to interaction involving one group with two or more interlocutors (dyads and multiparty, respectively), or multiple groups (cocktail‐party). The green area points to some endogenous processes that are known to have a role in social interaction and have been extensively studied in other research domains. In other words, these are key factors that should be considered when introducing the social dimension in speech neurophysiology research (green).

While designs involving such a multitude of factors and mechanisms have been explored (Goldstein et al. 2024; Xie et al. 2023), previous studies have controlled for some of those complexities by simplifying either the analysis or the experimental paradigm. For example, turn‐taking studies often constrain their analyses to the measurement of ERP/ERFs surrounding the speaker‐switching moments, with a design that can address realistic speech interactions (Bögels 2020; Kuhlen and Abdel Rahman 2023). Another analysis that can be applied to realistic interaction designs is the study of brain‐to‐brain synchronisation (Bevilacqua et al. 2019; Dikker et al. 2017; Montague 2002; Pérez et al. 2017), which can be used to pinpoint similarities between individuals, but is less suited for disentangling the neural processing of different linguistic properties. Other studies opted for design simplifications, for example, involving the recording of only one participant in a dyad during the speech interaction (Goldstein et al. 2025). Another angle is the study of copresence, with tasks where participants are presented with the same stimulus simultaneously while in the same room, without speaking to each other (Dikker et al. 2017).

As we briefly alluded to in Section 2.2, the different roles that one may play in an interaction also increase the complexity of the social dimension (Figure 3). Different language has been used to refer to these role distinctions, where early work established the terminology ‘speaker, addressee, auditor, overhearer and eavesdropper’ (Bell 1984). More recently, the terminology has evolved as such concepts have made its way through other disciplines, including the terms ‘second/third‐person neuroscience’ in the last few years. Findings from second/third‐person neuroscience highlight a unique opportunity for studying speech communication. A ‘coupled two‐source system’ can reveal how speech encoding differs across interaction roles (e.g., speaker/listener) (Li and Zhang 2024), while first‐person and second‐person roles may involve entirely distinct mechanisms (Redcay and Schilbach 2019). We introduce the term of an ‘unremarked listener’ (Figure 3) role to refer to the previously used term ‘eavesdropper’ (Bell 1984). This change reflects that, in current experimental paradigms, the listener is present but not secretly listening in on a conversation. The adoption of this term removes any ambiguity or misinterpretation (and negative connotation) in the ‘eavesdropper’ term. We later highlight the importance of harmonising terminology across related fields in our final reflections (Section 4.3).

Early research on continuous speech raised concerns on whether it would be feasible to study neural responses to such a complex process involving the simultaneous processing of many stimulus features. Appropriate methodologies, like multivariate TRFs, were identified for tackling that challenge, without excessively compromising on the naturalness of the listening task (Hamilton and Huth 2020). While these multivariate methods are expected to extend to speech interaction tasks, the myriad of linguistic and nonlinguistic factors at play raises questions about their robustness in these new scenarios. Because this line of work is still in its early stages, we think now is the ideal time to systematically evaluate the effectiveness of these methods for interactive speech, adapt them to maximise measurable effects and explore new approaches.

The technical challenges of studying the neurophysiology of social interaction have attracted the attention and excitement of some researchers in academia and industry, advancing hyperscanning technology and producing encouraging early results (Hirsch et al. 2021; Holmes et al. 2023; Lin et al. 2023; Misaki et al. 2021; Veneziani et al. 2025; Zhou et al. 2025). Despite important progress, some technical challenges and experimental encumbrance may be hard to avoid; these experiments will likely remain more complex and less accessible than audiobook listening experiments for the time being. Without a doubt, the wide range of possible experimental factors calls for a clear formulation of hypotheses, replication, controls and larger sample sizes, even more so than when studying continuous speech streams. In other words, the risk for false positives or result misinterpretations will be higher than before if the design is taken too lightly. So, are these experiments feasible, and are they worth it?

Before diving into this, it is worth noting that other research directions extend beyond continuous sound and speech listening. One noteworthy idea is the study of sound/speech‐in‐the‐wild, where the neurophysiology of sound and speech listening is studied in out‐of‐lab settings (simulated or real; Daeglau et al. 2025; Haupt et al. 2025; Korte et al. 2025; Straetmans et al. 2024). For example, recent work explored proneness to distraction and mind‐wandering using a hybrid approach involving virtual reality and a real classroom space (Levy et al. 2025). This and other lines of work, which would deserve dedicated reviews, are not further discussed here.

Preliminary Impact

Recent research has explored the possibility of probing the neural processing of speech streams in more socially relevant scenarios. While the literature includes investigations considering a variety of scenarios, from dialogue listening (Ip et al. 2025; Ip et al., in preparation; Zada et al. 2024; Zada, Nastase, Aubrey, et al. 2025) to dyadic interaction (Liu et al. 2019; Neel et al. 2025; Uhrig et al. 2018; Veneziani et al. 2025; Zada, Nastase, Speer, et al. 2025), that body of work remains sparse. Comparing these findings to the broader literature remains challenging due to the diversity of experimental designs and the wide range of social components examined. Here, we attempt to provide some clarity on what has been done and what might be possible to study in the future of social speech neurophysiology, organising some of the key findings according to the social dimension (i.e., complexity of interaction; Figure 1).

A first step into the social dimension involves speech listening scenarios with a social component, like prerecorded dialogues between two or multiple speakers (e.g., from podcasts). Results from our own research indicate that EEG activations for monologue and dialogue listening have comparable spatial and temporal characteristics (Ip et al. 2025). One noteworthy phenomenon was an increased cortical tracking for dialogue listening, possibly due to increased listening engagement. That study also examined the impact of dysfluencies, an important element of speech communication that can serve many functions, such as bringing attentional focus to upcoming speech material (Diachek and Brown‐Schmidt 2023). However, dysfluencies may also pose a technical challenge. For example, lexical processing can be measured by relating neural signals with LLM‐derived features (Chalehchaleh et al. 2025; Heilbron et al. 2022; Tikochinski et al. 2023), but LLMs have been suggested to be impacted by dysfluencies, potentially requiring tailored fine‐tuning strategies (Mousavi et al. 2024). Our study indicates that, despite the presence of dysfluencies, the cortical tracking of speech can be robustly measured using LLM‐derived features using an off‐the‐shelf model to analyse a dataset containing up to 3.3% of dysfluent words. As a result, we can measure cortical speech tracking responses on real‐world, spontaneous podcast dialogues that are compatible with monologue listening (Ip et al. 2025).

Numerous other questions relevant to speech interaction can be explored with relatively controlled tasks involving the listening of prerecorded monologue or dialogue. For example, hypotheses such as the ‘herding effect’, which proposes that a stronger speaker–listener alignment promotes how much the listeners cluster together (Chang et al. 2024), have been preliminarily tested using controlled listening paradigms involving prerecorded speech. Let us now take communication accommodation as an example. Our recent work has investigated communication accommodation by comparing neural signatures of speech listening across different registers, such as native‐directed and foreigner‐directed speech (Piazza et al. 2025). We found that foreigner‐directed speech has a pervasive impact on the neural processing of speech, aiding sound, phonological and lexical encoding, beyond what could be achieved by simply speaking more slowly. Indeed, one limitation of that work is that communication and accommodation are naturally two‐directional phenomena (albeit with varying degrees of accommodation). So, while the ability to measure such phenomena in controlled experimental conditions is encouraging, the effects may be considerably larger in more socially relevant scenarios involving interaction.

The next step brings us to experiments involving social interaction (i.e., interaction complexity in Figure 3). Two‐party (dyadic) conversations have been used to study how neural activity aligns between two interlocutors. A variety of recording technologies have been explored for this research direction, including EEG (Solanki 2017), MEG (Lin et al. 2023), fMRI (Misaki et al. 2021; Montague et al. 2002), fNIRS (Aili et al. 2025; Green et al. 2024; Li et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2019) and even intracranial EEG (Zada et al. 2024). The study of brain‐to‐brain coupling has been at the forefront of this line of research, proposing that interpersonal brain coupling arising via various means (e.g., body movement, breathing and focussing on the same stimulus) impacts the actions of an individual, generating behaviour that could not have emerged in isolation (Hasson and Frith 2016; Hasson et al. 2012; Jiang et al. 2020; Pérez et al. 2017). In line with that idea, previous research has studied social interaction with a focus on brain synchronisation across various social contexts, such as classroom learning (Dikker et al. 2017). Brain‐to‐brain synchrony has been related to similar communicative effort (Green et al. 2024), comprehension difficulties (Li et al. 2024), persuasion and deception (Angioletti et al. 2024; Veneziani et al. 2025), socio‐psychological connectedness (Hinvest et al. 2025), delay in phone conversations (Uhrig et al. 2018) and other factors. One interesting application of these metrics is on the study of parent–child brain‐to‐brain synchrony, which has been investigated with multiple recording modalities across several ages and countries (Lin et al. 2023; Neel et al. 2025; Schwartz et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2025). An issue with those metrics is that they may reflect the simultaneous alignment at a variety of levels, without pinpointing any of them in particular, unless a specific control condition is included.

Furthermore, recent work has used more targeted analytical methods to assess how language encoding is affected by social interaction. This phenomenon has been studied in dyads with fMRI (with participants being tested in neighbouring rooms), finding that strangers converge linguistically and neurally, while friends diverge over time (Speer et al. 2024). This result is compatible with the idea that strangers naturally search for a common ground, while friends explore more diverse topics. Other results on dyadic speech interaction using fMRI found that shared representations for listening and production extend beyond the language network into areas that are typically associated with social cognition (Zada, Nastase, Speer, et al. 2025). Further research was conducted to study what exactly drives the brain alignment during dyadic communication, finding that interlocutors align their neural representation of semantics (Zada et al. 2024) and syntax (Liu et al. 2019). Research using intracranial electrical recordings could go even deeper, finding that contextual embeddings learned by LLMs capture word‐by‐word neural alignment between speaker and listener (Zada et al. 2024). That research found, as expected, that the speaker's brain encodes linguistic content before word articulation, and that the same content re‐emerges in the listener's brain after word articulation, providing precise spatial and temporal maps for that encoding. These findings were obtained despite dealing with the numerous technical complexities of dyadic interaction, supporting the feasibility and value of that experimental paradigm.

Indeed, there is so much more that we could talk about in the context of social interaction, such as multiparty interaction (Zhou et al. 2025), turn‐taking (Bögels 2020), nonverbal cues (Argyle 1972) and even social phenomena beyond speech interaction (e.g., dyadic dance) (Bigand et al. 2024). Eye‐gaze, for example, plays a major role in human communication and was suggested to be instrumental even in infants for bringing brains into mutual temporal alignment, facilitating information transfer and learning (Leong et al. 2017).

Future Outlook

The previous section illustrates that the study of speech neurophysiology during realistic interaction is feasible and valuable. However, numerous challenges have yet to be addressed, both technical and theoretical, with some considerable differences across the social dimension (Figure 1). Nonetheless, the rising interest in this domain, exemplified through research studies (see previous section) and dedicated workshops as fora of discussion (e.g., TRF workshop 2024 3 ; CNSP hackathon 2025 4 ), gives us confidence that technical solutions will be identified as we move forward with this work.

A key risk is that the resulting literature may become too sparse or fragmented, with findings difficult to reconcile across studies due to differences in experimental setup, task design, participant samples or insufficient control of confounding variables. Therefore, defining clear and meaningful objectives that motivate and guide this line of research will be essential, ideally fostering a unifying framework for systematic investigations across this inherently multidisciplinary and multidimensional field. Ultimately, we must ask ourselves: What problems are we truly trying to solve? How will this work advance our understanding of the human brain? And where lies the optimal balance between ecological realism and experimental control when tailoring these studies to its research question?

Measurements of neural entrainment, stimulus‐to‐brain and brain‐to‐brain, have already been explored in this area. As such, we expect more of that work in the near future, with potential for substantially advancing our understanding of the link between neural entrainment mechanisms and social interaction. The possibility of studying brain‐to‐brain synchronisation in scenarios involving interaction adds an important dimension to this line of work. It will also be important to determine how the social element of speech impacts phenomena that are already studied in more controlled scenarios such as audiobook listening, from how speech and language features are encoded in the human brain to auditory attention mechanisms. As this effort originated in linguistics, the study of the social dimension of speech neurophysiology would likely benefit by building on longstanding theories on the use of speech in social contexts. For instance, spontaneous spoken interaction may entail interaction‐specific grammar that is principally different from written language or language from noninteractive contexts (Biber et al. 2021). The dynamics of conversation lead to a distinct ‘online’ processing, whereby common ground between interlocutors is constantly updated and turn‐taking is marked by real‐time interjections, dysfluencies and self‐repairs of speech. While social speech neurophysiology continues to investigate ways to implement such phenomena experimentally, corpus linguistics and the systems of annotations that have been developed for analyses of corpora provide treasure chests of conversations to explore in the interim (Garside et al. 1997; Sacks et al. 1974). In the past two decades, these annotated features have become ripe for picking as resources from which to mine conversational linguistic features, where the expertise of human annotators is complemented with the efficiency of computer taggers through semi‐automatic annotation (Kelterer and Schuppler 2025). In addition, work in conversation analysis has employed the Jeffersonian transcription methodology which is rich with interactional features that convey the social delivery of not only what was said, but also how it was said (Jefferson 2008; Park and Hepburn 2022). Such linguistic insight holds the potential to reveal differences in the interpersonal dimensions of conversation, as we tackle these dynamics specific to social speech.

In parallel, ongoing work in human–machine interaction could yield important developments that lead to other useful features poised for further investigation in social speech neurophysiology (Keitel et al. 2018; Littlejohn et al. 2025; Meah and Moore 2014; Metzger et al. 2023; Metzger et al. 2022; Moore 2012). Challenges in human–machine interaction include how to make forms of embodied communication as natural and close to human communication as possible. Existing dialogue system frameworks and speech language models (SLMs) have architectures that account for the natural flow of conversation and are trained specifically for modelling social speech dynamics, such as incremental processing (Schlangen and Skantze 2011; Skantze and Schlangen 2009) and full‐duplex communication in modern SLMs (Lin et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024) that investigate naturalistic turn‐taking.

Another body of work focusses on interlocutor modelling in the context of personal assistant interactions, conversational agents and other platforms of human–computer dialogue (Clark et al. 2019; Cowan et al. 2015; Doyle et al. 2019; Peña et al. 2023). How are such systems designed to be useful and take cues from human‐human dialogue? What do human interlocutors expect from their machine counterparts? Despite the fundamental limitations of what is termed as the habitability gap between mismatched human and machine interlocutor partners (Moore 2016), there is still so much to learn about the mechanisms underlying spoken language interaction in human conversation partners, where mismatched human interlocutors are critical for investigation (Ip et al. 2025; Piazza et al. 2025). The insights from human–machine interaction on extracting mechanistic accounts of social speech dynamics could yield further psycholinguistic feature sets for social speech neurophysiology to explore. In crossing domains in increasingly interdisciplinary fields, the distinction of such interlocutors or audience roles is one example that highlights the importance of future research in this line of work to move towards harmonising terminology and forming unifying frameworks of investigation across domains. Such efforts ensure a consistency of insights leading to our ability as a field to present truly novel insights and discoveries about functions in the brain, aligned with existing theories and methodologies.

While we have mentioned many potential impacts of social speech neurophysiology, it is unclear if such work will constitute a breakthrough or simply lead to interesting complementary insights into brain functioning. Our view is that studying the social dimension will offer new opportunities for enabling speech neurophysiology research to contribute in new ways to the study of the human brain. Furthermore, the greatest breakthrough may involve the study of phenomena that subserve social speech communication (e.g., communication accommodation, social anxiety, humour, bias and empathy) that have largely eluded speech neurophysiology research so far. Achieving this goal will entail employing paradigms that involve realistic speech interaction, and fine‐grained neural measurements at multiple timescales (e.g., phoneme, word, phrase) (Keitel et al. 2018) in addition to the more common brain‐to‐brain synchronisation. Such work might be transformative for studying how those phenomena develop across the lifespan, and how they differ in particular cohorts with speech‐related deficits, neurodiversity or other conditions that impact social communication. Social speech neurophysiology enables a unique opportunity for scientific advancement, as the sensitivities to the neural differences in these populations may only be tapped into with interactive scenarios, those of which are the most closely related to naturalistic social communication.

Summary

So, big hype or big leap? It is probably a bit of both. The complexity of social speech neurophysiology experiments calls for a clear definition of the specific objectives, guiding technological developments in this area with targeted experimental paradigms and controls, reducing an otherwise enormous space of possible factors that play a role during speech interaction, and reconciling parallel developments and emerging frameworks in related fields. The additional experimental challenges compared with audiobook listening tasks will make standardisation and data sharing even more important here, enabling reanalyses from multiple angles and enabling contributions from teams with limited resources (e.g., multiple EEG systems). This will ultimately accelerate the development of this exciting domain of research, bridging speech neurophysiology with neighbouring research areas, such as pragmatics, perspective taking, human–machine interaction, anxiety, humour and numerous others.

What or who led you to become a neuroscientist?

As I was finishing my studies in Computer Engineering, I asked myself: what are the most important unanswered questions in science that I could contribute to? A few ideas came to mind and neuroscience felt the most exciting. I was, and remain, genuinely curious about how our brains represent complex concepts, how we think, and how we learn across development. Neuroscience also presented important research gaps, especially around neurodiversity and mental health. Neural recordings are among the most challenging types of data, so I thought an entry point for me would be to advance methodologies for neural data analysis. I soon realised the challenge was even greater than I imagined, for example because of the lack of publicly available data at that time, although data sharing has become much more common now.

There is a simpler angle to the story. I had a naïve interest in a field that was new to me. Through a web search I found a professor at Trinity College Dublin with a background similar to mine who was working in sensory neuroscience. A single videocall with him erased any doubts and turned my naïve interest into a scholarship application. He made it clear that getting one of those scholarships was hard, but the conversation convinced me to go for it. So what led me to become a neuroscientist? Meeting Prof. Ed Lalor was certainly the turning point. Everything else evolved organically.

What are the main challenges young neuroscientists face to build an independent and successful career in research? How did you overcome those? Do you have any tips you’d like to share with early‐career scientists based on your personal experience?

Interdisciplinary research can be hard. I was fortunate that my institution has been supportive. My work spans Computer Science, Engineering, Linguistics, Music, Psychology, and Cognitive Neuroscience, and I had to decide whether to build a team primarily reflecting my own background or a more diverse team spanning all those disciplines. I chose the latter, which has been very rewarding and gives me the freedom to investigate complex neuroscience questions as a team with an overall perspective. That decision should not be taken lightly, as it can raise challenges, for example when applying for certain funding calls.

What is your view of Impact Factor as a metric to choose the Journals to publish your scientific work? How do you personally assess the impact of your own work in your field?

In principle, a good study should be noticed eventually regardless of journal impact factor. Indeed, the reputation of a journal (which is not necessarily the same as its impact factor) can influence media attention and provide a bridge to the general public that amplifies a study’s reach, which can be useful in some cases. Nonetheless, specialist or niche journals often publish excellent work that is simply less suited to broad public interest. Ultimately, the impact of a study depends on whether it changes our state of knowledge. For methodological work, we hope others will adopt the tool; citations can be a useful indicator of that, though citations have caveats. For example, it’s not just about the number of citations, but also about the quality and context of the citations.

My preferred way to assess whether my work is going in a valuable direction is to talk with colleagues at conferences, workshops, and during lab visits. Thinking together about research gaps, challenges, open questions, and possible solutions is what works best for me. The connections that arise from those interactions are usually long‑lasting and are positive byproducts of a genuine attempt to learn more about the brain. Since I became a PI, I noticed other strong indicators: getting invited to give talks, the quality of applicants to join my team, and request from strong students to join my team for short or long visits. So, it’s certainly difficult to summarise all this into a single metric (and all this is certainly field‐dependent), but these indicators have certainly helped to reflect on my career trajectory and impact.
