Comparing between face-to-face and mobile learning for improving nurses’ performance in premature infant positioning: a quasi-experimental study
Authors: Fahimeh Sabeti, Azadeh Ranjbar, Mandana Kashaki, Sedigheh Khanjari, Marjan Banazadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundProper positioning is widely recognized in neonatal care. Implementing evidence-based education to improve Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) nurses’ competency in infant positioning performance is essential. Evidence-based educational strategies are critical to enhancing NICU nurses’ competency in infant positioning.ObjectivesTo compare the effect of face-to-face training versus mobile learning (m-learning) utilizing multimedia resources on the positioning of preterm infants among NICU nurses.MethodsThis quasi-experimental pretest-posttest study employed a nonequivalent control group design, with 90 convenience-selected nurses from the NICU assigned to one of three groups: control, face-to-face training, or mobile learning (m-learning). The intervention consisted of face-to-face training (30 min plus 10–15 min of bedside training over 2 weeks) and m-learning (interactive multimedia content via WhatsApp for 2 weeks). Data were collected using the Infant Positioning Assessment Tool (IPAT) at baseline (T1), one week (T2), and one month (T3) post-intervention.ResultsNurses’ performance of infant positioning was in a “need to change” state in three groups preintervention (p > 0.05). Post-intervention, in T2, the control group remained in a “need to change” state (IPAT score = 4.71 ± 0.44), whereas both the face-to-face training group (IPAT score = 8.66 ± 0.55) and the m-learning group (IPAT score = 8.57 ± 0.40) were in an “acceptable” state. In T3, compared to T1, there was a significant difference in IPAT scores among the control, face-to-face training, and m-learning groups, respectively (IPAT scores = 4.66 ± 0.37, 6.63 ± 0.48, 7.88 ± 0.65, p < 0.001). Both intervention groups showed decline from T2, but the pattern differed markedly: all nurses in the face-to-face group (100%) returned to “need to change” status, while 40% of the m-learning group maintained acceptable performance. The m-learning group demonstrated significantly higher scores and better knowledge retention at one month follow-up compared to the face-to-face group.ConclusionFace-to-face and m-learning methods significantly improved nurses’ ability to position premature infants, with m-learning showing better long-term knowledge retention. These findings confirm that each approach is effective on its own, while also suggesting that their unique strengths could be combined. Therefore, we recommend future research explore the effectiveness of blended learning strategies that combine the immediate feedback and practical experience of face-to-face training with the accessibility and reinforcement of m-learning.

Introduction

Approximately 15 million preterm infants are delivered worldwide annually [1]. The incidence of preterm births, as reported in various studies conducted across different cities in Iran, ranges from 5.4% (95% CI, 3.9–6.9) to 19.85% (95% CI, 16-23.7) [2]. Preterm infants necessitate specialized care and often require admission to neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) [3]. Adequate developmental positioning is essential for ensuring their physiological stability and promoting their long-term developmental outcomes [4].

Birth before 37 weeks of gestation deprives infants of the womb’s optimal environment for physical and neurological development. In utero, fetuses maintain a midline position with a straight alignment of the head, neck, and torso, plus flexed limbs [5]. Premature infants exhibit hypotonia due to neurological immaturity, preventing them from maintaining this physiological position against gravity [6]. The facilitated tucking position, in which a nurse supports the infant’s head and buttocks while the legs are flexed, has been shown to enhance physiological indicators, including heart rate and respiratory rate, in addition to improving sucking success and overall comfort levels [7]. Without proper positioning in the NICU, body deformation can occur, impacting both midline orientation and future motor development, including sitting, crawling, and walking [8]. Proper positioning provides multiple benefits: it reduces pain, improves brain oxygenation, and lung function [9], facilitates better sleep [10], supports neuromuscular development [11], decreases gastroesophageal reflux [12], and lowers stress levels [13].

Research indicates that when nurses and physicians receive sufficient education and utilize a standardized positioning tool, there is an enhancement in knowledge and an increase in the application of skills related to developmentally appropriate positioning. This, in turn, leads to improved short-term physiological responses and better long-term neuromotor outcomes [5, 6, 14]. Nurses are essential in the realm of neonatal care [15], particularly in the application of developmental positioning techniques [5]. Despite the recognized importance of proper positioning in neonatal care, standardized training for NICU nurses on developmental positioning is lacking. This highlights the need for evidence-based educational programs to enhance nurses’ competency in this crucial practice [5]. Several studies have examined ways to improve developmental positioning practices in NICUs [16–19]. Louw and Maree showed improved positioning competencies after educational workshops [17]. While face-to-face training provides essential hands-on experience and immediate feedback for NICU staff, its implementation is often challenged by time constraints, staffing shortages, and the intensive nature of the NICU environment [20]. The Internet and mobile technologies have revolutionized educational delivery methods and learning systems [21]. Multimedia and mobile learning platforms enhance education by combining text, images, audio, video, and animations to create engaging, interactive learning experiences [22]. On the other hand, mobile social networks have become popular educational tools due to their accessibility, affordability, and flexibility, enabling users to learn and interact at any time and from anywhere [23].

Recent research has used innovative educational methods to enhance nurses’ competencies in developmental positioning. These studies employed face-to-face training through simulation with human models and dolls, hands-on practice with neonates, and pedagogical techniques like lectures, discussions, PowerPoint presentations, posters, and videos. Some studies also combined traditional instruction with E-learning methods via social media platforms [5, 6, 14, 16, 24–27].

Currently, electronic technology is utilized for delivering educational content and is recognized as an alternative method for enhancing the quality of presenting up-to-date scientific material [28]. One form of electronic learning is mobile learning (m-learning) or smartphone-based education. The integration of mobile phones and communication technologies into healthcare and public health has given rise to a new concept known as mobile health (mHealth), which has the potential to substantially improve healthcare delivery processes [29]. Evidence increasingly supports the effectiveness of m-learning in nursing education, especially in the development of clinical skills. Kim et al. found that a 3-week mobile-based clinical practice education program led to significant improvements in clinical competency and problem-solving abilities among nursing students [30]. Bilgiç et al. showed that the use of mobile-based educational videos throughout the learning period led to a significant enhancement in subcutaneous injection skills among nursing students when compared to traditional instruction alone [31]. In a similar vein, Radmard et al. assessed a mobile ventilator training application with 50 ICU nurses, revealing notable improvements in both knowledge and technical skills, such as graph interpretation and equipment settings, following two weeks of self-directed mobile learning [32]. These studies highlight the potential of m-learning for both fundamental procedural training and intricate technical competencies. Nevertheless, despite the expanding evidence base, there is a scarcity of comparative studies that assess m-learning against face-to-face training for specialized neonatal care competencies. Research focusing specifically on m-learning for the positioning of preterm infants is particularly limited. Given the complex nature of positioning techniques, it is crucial to understand the comparative effectiveness of m-learning versus traditional training methods in this area to enhance NICU nursing education. Therefore, this study aimed to compare face-to-face training, mobile learning utilizing multimedia resources, and standard care (control) on the positioning of preterm infants among NICU nurses.

Methods

Study design

A quasi-experimental pretest-posttest study with a nonequivalent control group design was employed. The study was conducted from January 2021 to July 2022.

Sample and setting

This multicenter study involved 3 groups of NICU nurses from four hospitals (A, B, C, and D) in Tehran, affiliated with the Iran University of Medical Sciences. Participants were eligible if they were willing to participate, had at least 6 months of experience caring for premature infants, had not received any infant positioning training within the past year, owned a smartphone capable of running WhatsApp, and were proficient in its use, particularly in the m-learning group. Participants who missed any face-to-face training sessions or failed to engage with interactive content (e.g., not responding to three consecutive messages) in the m-learning group were withdrawn from the study and classified as dropouts.

A convenience sampling method was used to recruit nurses. The required sample size was calculated based on the estimated standard deviation from a previous study [27], with a 95% confidence level and 80% power. It was assumed that nurses’ performance in positioning preterm infants would differ by at least 1.5 units before and after the intervention. Consequently, the minimum sample size was 29 participants per group. Non-random assignment was necessitated due to staffing limitations and COVID-19-related workload constraints. The decision to use a non-random assignment was based on specific considerations, including the following factors. Logistical challenges associated with coordinating nursing schedules across four hospitals complicated the process of random allocation. Additionally, disparities in classroom facilities among the hospitals hindered the possibility of randomly assigning participants to the face-to-face training group. Furthermore, keeping nurses in similar groups within each hospital allowed senior nursing staff to more effectively organize and manage shift schedules. Participants were assigned to three groups non-randomly, comprising two intervention groups and one control group. A total of 110 nurses were recruited; however, 12 were ineligible, and 8 declined to participate, resulting in 20 nurses (18.2%) being excluded before enrollment. The final sample consisted of 90 nurses: 30 in the control group, 30 in intervention group 1 (face-to-face training), and 30 in intervention group 2 (M-learning). Although the target was 32 participants per group, additional recruitment was limited by the availability of eligible nurses. Allocation to the groups was based on the convenience of nurses and the availability of facilities for classroom instruction. Hospitals A (15-bed level III NICU) and B (13-bed level III NICU) were combined into the control group due to a lack of available classrooms and insufficient NICU nurses. Hospital C (60-bed level III NICU) was designated for intervention group 1 because it had suitable classroom facilities. Hospital D (32-bed level III NICU) was assigned to intervention group 2 due to the absence of an appropriate classroom. Sampling was conducted in parallel, based on the predetermined sampling groups across the four hospitals. To recruit participants, the researcher visited the NICUs on consecutive days and during different shifts, clearly explaining the study’s aims to eligible nurses interested in participating. The control group consisted of 15 nurses from Hospital A and 15 nurses from Hospital B, whereas Intervention Group 1 included 30 nurses from Hospital C, and Intervention Group 2 comprised 30 nurses from Hospital D.

This method of cluster allocation within a hospital setting effectively minimized the potential for intervention contamination, which might arise if nurses from various study groups operated within the same unit or frequently interacted with one another. Moreover, participants were instructed not to share training materials with colleagues from other hospitals. The physical separation of study groups across different hospital sites provided a natural barrier against contamination while maintaining the feasibility of the intervention delivery within each setting. To address potential selection bias, uniform inclusion and exclusion criteria across all hospitals were established, and demographic and professional characteristics (years of nursing experience, educational background, NICU experience, and employment status) between groups before the intervention were compared, revealing no significant differences, as the results will be presented subsequently.

Measures and instruments

Data collection was conducted using two instruments. A 7-item questionnaire was used to gather participants’ demographic backgrounds. The Infant Positioning Assessment Tool (IPAT), developed by Coughlin et al., was utilized. This tool serves a dual purpose: it assesses infant positioning and acts as an educational resource, providing a visual reminder of essential positioning components in an easily understandable format. The researcher completed the IPAT, which took approximately ten minutes.

The IPAT assesses optimal developmental positioning across six anatomical regions: head, chin/neck, shoulders, hands/arms, pelvis, and knees/ankles/feet. Each region is scored from 0 to 2, resulting in total scores that range from 0 to 12. A score of 2 indicates appropriate positioning, 1 represents an acceptable alternative, and 0 signifies unacceptable positioning. The scoring system ranges from 0 to 12. A score below 8 suggests that a change in position is necessary. A score ranging from 8 to 10 signifies that the position is acceptable. Meanwhile, a score between 10 and 12 indicates that the position is ideal. Higher scores reflect superior developmental positioning performance by NICU nurses [33].

Previous studies have confirmed this instrument’s content validity and reliability [6, 16, 25, 33, 34]. The original developers granted permission for use via email correspondence. The questionnaires underwent assessments for both validity and reliability. Content validity was evaluated by a panel of 10 experts (5 nursing faculty members and 5 researchers specializing in instrument development). After the expert evaluation, the reliability of the instrument was assessed using the inter-observer reliability method. Two nurses independently scored 15 infants simultaneously, resulting in a reported correlation coefficient of 0.957.

Data collection and intervention

A protocol was created before starting the study to ensure all steps were followed.

Pre-test

Following consultations with the chief nurses regarding the participants’ work schedules, the researcher made unannounced NICU visits across shifts to observe positioning performance. Each participant was assessed 3 times using IPAT (once per shift), totaling 90 measurements per group and 270 total pre-intervention measurements.

Intervention.

In intervention group 1

Following two weeks of education and engagement between the researcher and the nurses, the nurses no longer had access to training materials after the intervention.

In the intervention group 2

Following two weeks of education and engagement between the researcher and the nurses (Table 1), the training link remained active and accessible; however, the researcher ceased further interaction with the participants.

In the control group

No intervention took place (Table 1). Following the intervention, all educational materials created were provided to the participants.

Post-test

Post-tests were conducted at two time points: one week and one month after the intervention. The researcher visited the four NICUs at these intervals to observe and assess each participant’s positioning performance using the IPAT tool on three distinct occasions. Each participant was assessed once per shift daily, resulting in a total of 90 measurements for each group at each time point. This amounted to 270 measurements one week post-intervention and an additional 270 measurements one month post-intervention.

The evaluator’s role was clearly defined and limited to conducting all positioning assessments using the standardized IPAT checklist. The evaluator (second author, a seasoned NICU nurse with expertise in developmental care and the positioning of premature infants) performed direct observations at all three measurement points. She was tasked with objectively scoring each item according to the IPAT criteria and promptly documenting the results following each observation. Despite the impossibility of blinding to group allocation due to the inherent characteristics of the interventions, the evaluator did not participate in the design or execution of the educational sessions, thereby maintaining independence from the intervention process. before data collection, the evaluator participated in a calibration session to guarantee the consistent application of the IPAT scoring guidelines. As the sole trained evaluator, she ensured that assessment procedures were uniformly applied across all participants. To mitigate the Hawthorne effect, which refers to changes in behavior resulting from the awareness of being observed, several strategies were employed. Specifically, nurses were kept unaware of the precise timing of the observations, and assessments were carried out during various shifts and at different times of the day. Observations were conducted from a suitable distance to avoid disrupting the nurses’ routine activities. Additionally, nurses were informed that the purpose of the study was to assess the effectiveness of the educational program rather than their performance. To further diminish the Hawthorne effect, which is generally short-lived, the observation period was extended to include one week prior to and one week following the intervention. Additionally, to reduce the likelihood of bias in the evaluations carried out by the researchers, several strategies were implemented. The IPAT, the standardized and validated instrument featuring objective criteria for positioning assessment, was utilized to limit subjective interpretation. A statistician, who had no role in the intervention or data collection, carried out the data analysis.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed utilizing SPSS version 24, incorporating both descriptive and inferential statistical techniques. Descriptive statistics were employed to delineate the sample population through frequency distributions, means, and standard deviations. Baseline characteristics and pre-intervention IPAT scores were compared across groups using one-way ANOVA for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables to assess the success of randomization. Given that baseline comparisons revealed no significant differences among groups, indicating successful randomization and baseline homogeneity, subsequent analyses proceeded without covariate adjustment. For inferential analysis, chi-square tests and Fisher’s exact tests were applied to investigate associations among categorical variables, while repeated measures ANOVA was used to evaluate changes within groups over several time points. Post-hoc analyses such as Tukey’s HSD and Bonferroni corrections were implemented to pinpoint specific differences between groups and mitigate the risk of multiple comparison errors.

Results

Participants’ demographic characteristics

The average age (with standard deviation) of participants was 34.37 years (7.05) for the face-to-face training group, 35.97 years (5.57) for the m-learning group, and 36.43 years (6.07) for the control group. Based on Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests and repeated measures ANOVA, no statistically significant differences were observed among the three groups concerning demographic characteristics. Baseline knowledge and positioning skills comparisons revealed no significant differences among groups, indicating successful randomization and baseline homogeneity. Furthermore, we ensured that both face-to-face and m-learning groups received the same educational content, ensuring the learning experiences were comparable across both groups. Pretest: Three observations/assessments of positioning performance (IPAT scores)

The results from each of the first, second, and third assessments of participants’ positioning performance before the intervention in each group demonstrated a need for change, as none of the participants achieved either ideal or acceptable IPAT scores. Furthermore, the analysis of mean scores from each assessment across the three groups revealed no statistically significant differences in the mean scores for all three measurements before the intervention (p = 0.44, 0.874, 0.821) (Table 2).

Additionally, the total mean scores of all three measurements combined within each group needed adjustment, as none of the participants attained either ideal or acceptable IPAT scores. Based on the analysis of variance, there were no statistically significant differences in the total mean scores across the three groups (p = 0.59) (Table 3). Discussion

This quasi-experimental study aimed to compare the effect of face-to-face training and m-learning using multimedia on preterm infant positioning among NICU nurses, compared to the control group. The results showed that both face-to-face and m-learning significantly improved the positioning skills of NICU nurses for preterm infants. Notably, m-learning demonstrated greater long-term efficacy, exhibiting significantly improved knowledge retention during the one-month follow-up.

The large effect sizes observed indicate not only statistical significance but also clinically meaningful improvements in nurses’ positioning performance. The findings of the current study align with some studies but exhibit inconsistencies with others. The positioning scores before intervention were at an acceptable level in Spilker et al.’s research, differing from the current study. This may be due to variations in hospital training systems, as Spilker’s study was conducted in public hospitals affiliated with a top university [6]. In contrast, studies by Charafeddine et al. and Yun and Kim in university hospitals in Beirut and South Korea found that pre-training IPAT scores needed improvement, aligning with the present findings and emphasizing the need for training to enhance nurses’ positioning competencies [5, 25].

Findings of the current study indicate nurses’ IPAT scores significantly improved in both intervention groups one week post-intervention, and this difference was significant compared to the control group. Masri et al. found that a combination of theoretical and practical training significantly improved NICU nurses’ positioning skills for hospitalized infants [34]. Charafeddine et al. structured a training program consisting of 2 theoretical sessions followed by 1 practical session. Pre-intervention IPAT scores indicated a need for change, but post-training assessments showed significant improvements from 3.4 to 6.3 after the theoretical component, and further to 7.3 following the practical training. These results highlight the effectiveness of the training program, with practical training yielding superior outcomes [25]. The current study’s findings align with the referenced studies [25, 34], which showed that the face-to-face training group receiving both theoretical and practical bedside education achieved higher scores one week post-intervention. However, Charafeddine et al.’s study showed that while nurses’ IPAT scores improved after training, they still indicated a need for change [25]. This discrepancy may be attributed to their lower baseline IPAT scores compared to the current study’s pre-intervention scores. Perkins et al. found that while structured training approaches, including workshops, improved NICU nurses’ competency in positioning premature infants, the nurses themselves perceived bedside training as more beneficial [26]. Jeanson’s study found that individualized bedside training using IPAT led to progressive improvements in nurses’ IPAT scores at 3 intervals: before, during, and 4 months post-intervention [16].

In the present study, IPAT scores of the face-to-face training group were significantly higher one week post-intervention. However, a decline in scores was noted one month post-intervention compared to the scores recorded one week post-intervention. This deterioration aligns with the forgetting curve phenomenon, which demonstrates rapid memory decay after learning unless knowledge is actively reinforced [36]. In contrast, the m-learning group demonstrated higher scores than the face-to-face training group one month post-intervention. The results indicate that while both groups experienced a reduction in performance retention one month post-intervention, the m-learning group maintained relatively better scores during this period, suggesting more effective long-term learning. This may be attributed to the continuous on-demand accessibility and repeated engagement with educational materials for the m-learning group. This argument aligns with evidence indicating that in m-learning, learners can access information and educational content anytime and anywhere, utilizing portable devices that free them from reliance on traditional classroom settings. This mode of learning offers several advantages, including equitable access to education, encouragement of lifelong learning, availability of educational resources, and the convenience of learning at one’s own pace [37, 38].

Our findings are consistent with previous research on positioning education. In the study by Yun and Kim, a 7-week educational intervention was implemented, with 3 weeks of education and practice, followed by 4 weeks of motivational messages through social media. This program showed improved IPAT scores, but the researchers did not evaluate the IPAT scores after each phase to determine the impact of the supplementary social media training [5]. Similarly, our m-learning group experienced consistent engagement via text messages, phone calls, and WhatsApp support, which could account for the enhanced one month retention rate in contrast to the singular face-to-face intervention. This alignment supports the hypothesis that continuous reinforcement, irrespective of the mode of delivery, is essential for maintaining competency. Moon et al. conducted a 1-group, pre- and post-test study to evaluate a neonatal positioning training program for nurses. The training included a 30-minute PowerPoint lecture and a 12-minute video presented 5 days later. Results showed the video significantly improved nurses’ knowledge and performance in positioning. However, the study did not assess scores after each training phase, limiting comparative analysis of the 2 methods [14]. This study supports our results, indicating that multimedia strategies that integrate IMLR with videocast are effective in teaching positioning skills. Nevertheless, our research distinctly evaluated various delivery methods and examined long-term retention, uncovering that the continuous accessibility of m-learning provided considerable benefits compared to conventional face-to-face training at the one month follow-up. These results build upon the work of Moon et al. by illustrating not only the significance of multimedia content but also the critical role of delivery format and sustained access in knowledge retention.

Our research focused on a comparative analysis of traditional face-to-face training and M-learning. Since we found no similar studies in this context, we will review existing literature that compares the effects of similar educational methods in other areas of neonatal care. Zolghadr Nasab et al. [39] conducted a quasi-experimental pre-post study to train NICU nurses in neonatal skin care, involving 3 groups: a control group and 2 intervention groups receiving either face-to-face workshops or virtual interactive education. The workshop included 4 h of theory and 2 h of practical training with a neonate manikin. The virtual education, created with Storyline software, was mobile-friendly for smartphone access. Both intervention groups showed improved knowledge and performance compared to the control group, with the multimedia group achieving slightly better outcomes than the traditional workshop group. The findings from this study align with ours; however, our interactive multimedia resource, created with Storyline software, included a wider range of elements such as text, images, audio, and an engaging videocast demonstrating premature infants’ positioning. The use of multimedia learning principles and technological strategies supports the integration of video in medical education [40]. Educational videos present content in a clear, diverse, and accessible way, making them effective for learning and instruction [41].

In the present study, we shared the educational material via the user-friendly WhatsApp, promoting interaction between the researcher and NICU nurses. we facilitated ongoing interaction between the researcher and NICU nurses throughout the intervention period. This continuous engagement, likely reinforced learning and contributed to better retention. In a quasi-experimental study, 80 NICU nurses were divided into experimental and control groups. The experimental group participated in a 2-month training program via a Learning Management System (LMS), which included visual, audio, and text (PDF) materials. The Caring Ability Inventory (CAI) assessed the nurses’ competencies on the first day and 2 months post-intervention. Results showed significant differences in mean scores for the experimental group before and after training, as well as between both groups post-intervention [42], though their longer intervention period limits direct comparison with our 2-week protocol. The use of WhatsApp has increased its adoption among students [43], enhancing classroom material, fostering discussions, encouraging collaboration, and providing opportunities for reflection. This allows students to revisit content until they achieve comprehension [44]. Moreover, although face-to-face training demands considerable time from instructors and substantial physical resources, m-learning offers enhanced cost-effectiveness by minimizing the hours required from instructors and removing the expenses associated with venue costs [45].

In our research, nurses’ IPAT scores in both intervention groups significantly increased after training, reaching an acceptable level, but we did not achieve the ideal score at any point. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Spilker , Charafeddine , and Jeanson [6, 16, 25]. The researchers suggested that an IPAT acceptable score above 8 reflects improved training for nurses in infant positioning, which is satisfactory. However, ideal scores represent the best outcomes achievable through ongoing training efforts over an extended period. The conditions surrounding our research correspond with this understanding in several ways. Our intervention was confined to a duration of 2 weeks, which, although adequate to enhance performance from “need to change” to “acceptable” standards, may not have been sufficient for attaining mastery. Furthermore, even though the m-learning group retained access to resources post-intervention, the active support and involvement of researchers concluded after 2 weeks, thereby restricting opportunities for continuous feedback and skill enhancement. To achieve optimal positioning competency, it is essential to have not only initial training but also ongoing practice, supervision, and feedback in authentic clinical environments over a prolonged period, factors that exceed the limitations of our short-term intervention study. These results indicate that while targeted training can yield significant improvements, the highest level of positioning performance necessitates long-term educational initiatives accompanied by ongoing support.

Furthermore, based on the observed decline in performance in the face-to-face group and the more sustained improvements in the m-learning group, a blended learning approach may optimize both immediate skill acquisition and long-term retention.

Conclusion

Our research revealed that both face-to-face and m-learning methods significantly enhanced nurses’ performance of positioning premature infants, with m-learning exhibiting superior long-term knowledge retention. These results affirm the effectiveness of each approach on its own, while also indicating that their unique strengths could be effectively combined. Consequently, we recommend that future investigations explore the effectiveness of blended learning strategies that merge the immediate feedback and practical experience offered by face-to-face training with the accessibility and reinforcement features of M-learning. Such research would be instrumental in assessing whether an integrated approach could maximize both immediate skill development and sustained knowledge retention in clinical nursing education. We propose an innovative blended model that combines theoretical training with engaging multimedia elements and practical training at the bedside or through simulation. We suggest a pragmatic three-phase blended learning framework designed for NICU positioning training: Phase 1 - Online theoretical modules accessible through mobile platforms that address positioning principles and evidence-based practices (Week 1); Phase 2 - In-person interactive workshops that emphasize hands-on skill demonstration and peer learning (Week 2); and Phase 3 - Mobile-supported reinforcement featuring case-based scenarios and regular competency assessments (Weeks 3–4). This framework could be implemented as a 4-week program in which nurses initially engage with self-paced online modules, subsequently participate in intensive practical sessions, and finally receive continuous mobile-based support and evaluation. Such a systematic approach would capitalize on the convenience of mobile learning for knowledge acquisition while guaranteeing the essential hands-on experience required for procedural competence in neonatal care.

Limitations

This study faced several limitations. The COVID-19 pandemic significantly increased nurses’ workloads and hindered face-to-face training, necessitating careful planning of sessions to align with nurses’ schedules and minimize disruption to clinical duties. Additionally, the workload made it challenging to employ a research assistant as a performance evaluator, leading the researcher to take on this role with approval from the Faculty’s Graduate Education Council. Random assignment of participants to control and intervention groups was also unfeasible due to hospital constraints and workforce shortages. Data collection occurred one week and one month post-intervention; future research should conduct follow-up assessments over a longer duration (3–6 months) to obtain more precise findings, evaluate the sustained effects of training, and analyze the impact of educational courses on nursing practice. Furthermore, the lack of monitoring participants’ engagement with m-learning resources following the study period restricted our capacity to evaluate how the ongoing availability of educational resources influences the maintenance of practice changes. Additionally, there are several potential confounding factors that may influence the results, including variations in the baseline technological proficiency and motivation levels of participants. These confounding factors highlight limitations within our study design that we could not adequately address in our methodology. Future investigations would gain from a more thorough evaluation of these variables, utilizing standardized measures of technological self-efficacy and comprehensive tools for assessing motivation.
