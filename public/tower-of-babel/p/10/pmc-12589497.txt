Bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation reveal that heat HSP90AA1 enhances intestinal ischemia reperfusion induced necroptosis by inducing phosphorylated MLKL
Authors: Mingcan Zheng, Zirui Jia, Puxu Wang, Yuhang Wang, Guo Zu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Intestinal ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury is a serious threat to human health and has high mortality and morbidity rates. Several studies have examined the role of necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury. However, the specific mechanism and signaling pathways involved in necroptosis in intestinal I/R are still unclear. Bioinformatics analysis and experimental validation were utilized to investigate the role of necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury. The dataset of target genes and disease genes was constructed using the R programming language and software environment. An interaction network of genes or proteins was built using GO and KEGG enrichment analysis to investigate the likely mechanism involved. Finally, the potential role of necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury was further verified experimentally. Eighteen differentially expressed genes were identified via the construction of target and disease gene dataset. The core gene heat shock protein 90 alpha family class A member 1 (HSP90AA1) was identified via coexpression network analysis of related protein and genes. The expression of HSP90AA1 and phosphorylated mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (p-MLKL) in human ischemic intestinal tissues were increased in ischemic intestinal tissue (P < 0.05). The same results were also shown in IEC-6 cells subjected to H/R injury, where the expression of HSP90AA1 and p-MLKL was considerably greater in the H/R group than in the normal group (P < 0.05). We further used siRNA to inhibit HSP90AA1 expression in IEC-6 cells, the p-MLKL protein expression level was lower in the H/R + si-HSP90AA1 group than in the H/R group (P < 0.05). HSP90AA1 promotes the development of intestinal I/R-induced necroptosis and plays a crucial role in intestinal I/R injury. HSP90AA1 may be a new therapeutic target for intestinal I/R injury.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22345-z.

Introduction

Intestinal ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury, especially intestinal mucosa injury, is an increasingly prevalent clinical concern. The intestine is considered the most vulnerable tissue to I/R injury1. Intestinal I/R injury is a dangerous complication that may occur in response to a variety of acute situations, including septic shock, acute mesenteric ischemia, liver and bowel transplants, and abdominal aortic surgical trauma2–4. Ischemia can lead to intestinal tissue destruction, which leads to impaired oxygen delivery and ultimately cell necrosis. However, restoring the blood supply can lead to additional cell damage, which exacerbates intestinal damage and cell apoptosis5. Studies have suggested that apoptosis is strongly elevated during I/R in the gut and could play a significant role in the pathophysiology of I/R-induced intestinal damage6–8. Necroptosis is a type of planned inflammatory cell death that was first identified as an alternative to apoptosis following the activation of death domain receptors9. Studies have shown that the activation of necroptosis plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of many diseases, such as CNS diseases, cancer and immune-related diseases10–13. In addition, necroptosis plays an important role in ischemic disease. Chen et al. suggested that necroptosis is involved in the disruption of the blood‒brain barrier in ischemic stroke14. Some researchers have shown that necroptosis is also involved in the ischemia‒reperfusion injury of several organs, such as the liver and heart15,16. Recently, several studies have examined the role of necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury17. However, the specific roles and mechanism remain unclear and require further investigation.

Necroptosis, a type of regulated necrosis, is triggered by the kinase activity of receptor-interacting protein kinase 3 (RIPK3). The mixed lineage kinase domain-like (MLKL) pseudokinase has been identified as a downstream target of RIPK3 and the ultimate effector of necroptosis execution18–20. Wang et al. reported that phosphorylation of MLKL (p-MLKL) may constitute a key step in necroptosis execution21. Wen et al. revealed that HMGB1 signaling is highly involved in the RIP1/RIP3-dependent necroptosis pathway in I/R-induced intestinal injury and that necroptosis is a key mediator of enterocyte loss in intestinal I/R injury22. Shi et al. demonstrated that ursolic acid induced necroptosis through the RIP1/RIP3/MLKL pathway, attenuated morphological damage, and enhanced intestinal barrier function23. Recently, studies have reported the effects of necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury17. However, the specific molecular mechanism and signaling pathway of necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury are still unclear.

In this study, the bioinformatics database was used for systematic bioinformatics analysis to evaluate the roles of necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury. Furthermore, we detected the potential biological mechanism and key genes involved in necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury. In addition, we further verified the expression of the key genes through experimental validation in human ischemic intestinal tissues and IEC-6 cells with hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) injury. Our study may provide a new therapeutic target for intestinal I/R injury.

Materials and methods

Screening and identification of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The European Nucleotide Archive (ENA) provides a comprehensive record of the world’s nucleotide sequencing information, covering raw sequencing data, sequence assembly information and functional annotation. We acquired the PRJEB45362 dataset from the ENA dataset to study intestinal I/R-related mRNAs. We did not include the information of intestinal I/R-related lncRNAs in our study. The expression profile data of the PRJEB45362 dataset included 5 control groups (C1, C2, C3, C4 and C5) and 5 experimental groups (A1, A2, A3, A4 and A5)24. FASTP, developed by OpenGene in China, was used for data quality control and filtering. The expression is calculated using “feature counts” from the R version “Subreads” package. The read counts of each gene are described in the output file, and TPM standardization of the read counts is performed using the “fpkm function” in the “edger” package.

We used the official gene symbol to annotate and preprocess the matrix file produced from PRJEB45362. Afterward, DEGs were identified via the R package “edgeR” when P < 0.05 and |FC|>2.0 (|logFC|>1.0) were reached. The method of Benjamini-Hochberg was used to control for multiple hypothesis testing.

Functional enrichment of DEGs

Through KEGG and GO pathway enrichment analysis25,26, the biological activities of the DEGs were ascertained, and enrichment analysis was carried out with a cutoff threshold of P < 0.05 using the clusterProfiler, org.Hs.eg.db, enrichplot, and ggplot2 packages.

The creation and analysis of a protein-protein interaction network

In our study, a protein‒protein interaction network between DEGs was constructed using the STRING database software (https://string-db.org/), and we used Cytoscape software to visualize the protein interaction network27. The most significant modules in the PPI network were subsequently screened via the Cytoscape application MCODE (a plug-in of Cytoscape software) for identifying key gene modules. The application MCODE was subsequently used to identify closely connected network clusters, and the scoring parameters of cluster analysis followed the default parameters of the application.

Human intestinal tissue samples

Human ischemic intestinal tissue samples were collected from individuals receiving surgery for incarcerated hernias, strangulated intestinal obstruction, or acute mesenteric artery embolism. We designated the marginally normal section of the comparable ischemic tissue as normal intestinal tissue to compare it with the ischemic group. The intestinal samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and fixed in 10% neutral formaldehyde until analysis. The ischemic gut segments were obtained from clinical patients at the Central Hospital of Dalian University of Technology (Dalian Municipal Central Hospital). The families of the patients provided written informed consent. All the experiments were performed in accordance with relevant named guidelines and regulations and were approved by the Ethics Committee of the Central Hospital of Dalian University of Technology (Dalian Municipal Central Hospital) (Approval number: YN2023-042-02).

Histological examination

Normal and ischemic intestinal tissues were fixed in paraffin and cut into 4 μm thick sections. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed, and Chiu’s score was used to assess damage to the intestinal mucosa as described in a previous study28.

Cell culture and H/R injury

Rat small intestinal epithelial cell line IEC-6 (National Laboratory Cell Resource Sharing Platform, China) were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2 humidity in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% glutamine, penicillin/streptomycin and 1% nonessential amino acids. To produce a hypoxic environment, a microaerophilic system (Thermo Scientific) was used to cultivate the cells for 12 h with 5% CO2 and 1% O2 balanced with 94% N2. At the end of hypoxia, reoxygenation was realized by incubation under normal conditions. After reoxygenation for 6 h, the cells were harvested for further use.

RNA interference

HSP90AA1 siRNA was created and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Before transfection, IEC-6 cells were transferred to 6-well plates and grown to a confluence of 40 ~ 50%. Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) was used for transfection according to the manufacturer’s protocol as described in our previous study28. The HSP90AA1 sequence of the siRNA was as follows: 5′-GGUCAAGAAAUGCCUAGAATT-3′; the antisense sequence was as follows: 5′-UUCUAGGCAU UUCUUGACCTT-3′.

Protein extraction and western blot analysis

Total protein extracts from intestinal tissues or IEC-6 cells were prepared via a commercial protein isolation kit (KeyGEN Biotech, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted proteins were subsequently quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (Yunhe, China). An equal amount (20 µg) of each sample was subjected to 10% SDS‒PAGE (Millipore, Bedford, MA) using base electrophoresis power (Bio-Rad PowerPac Basic 1645050). Proteins were subsequently loaded onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes using an electrophoresis machine at 300 mA for 70 min of transmigration. The PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% BSA diluted in TBST buffer for 60 min and then incubated overnight with primary antibodies against β-actin (1:1000, ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China), p-MLKL (1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and HSP90AA1 (1:1000, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Santa Cruz, CA, USA) overnight at 4 °C. The membranes were washed with TBST and then incubated for two hours at room temperature with a secondary antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (HRP). Enhanced chemiluminescence reagents were used to visualize the corresponding western blots. Finally, we used a WD-9423BC automatic chemiluminescence imaging system (LIUYI, China) to image the protein bands.

Statistical analysis

The data are reported as the means ± SDs. The normality of the data distributions was assessed with the Shapiro‒Wilk test. For data that followed a normal distribution, a two-tailed Student’s t test was used to assess the statistical significance of the differences between means across experimental groups. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used to compare more than two groups. All experimental data given are from at least three independent tests. All the statistical analyses were carried out using GraphPad Prism 5.0 (GraphPad Prism Software, La Jolla, CA). P values < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Determination of DEGs in the PRJEB45362 dataset

After standardization of the results of the PRJEB45362 dataset, we identified a total of 1888 DEGs (Fig. 1a), and 18 genes related to necroptosis were identified (Fig. 1b). In contrast to black plaques, red plaques presented 13 upregulated DEGs, and green plaques presented 5 downregulated DEGs (Fig. 1c). The details of expression of 18 genes were listed in Table S1. FASTP, developed by OpenGene in China, was used for data quality control and filtering (Fig. 1d).

Protein‒protein interaction network and enrichment analysis

We built a target protein‒protein interaction network using STRING data to examine the connections between the eighteen DR-DEG target proteins. The 18 target proteins exhibited correlations (Fig. 2a,b). In the protein interaction diagram, the four genes encoding heat shock protein 90 alpha family class A member 1 (HSP90AA1), HSP90AB1, MLKL and NLRP3 were the proteins with the greatest connections and the strongest relationships with the other proteins. A core gene, HSP90AA1, was obtained via coexpression network analysis of related proteins and genes (Fig. 2c,d).

Functional correlation analysis

Eighteen DEGs were expected to have distinct roles in the current study. GO enrichment analysis revealed that enriched biological processes (BPs) were mainly involved in the regulation of the extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway and extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway. The enriched molecular functions (MFs) included cytokine activity and cytokine receptor binding, and the enriched cell components (CCs) included ficolin-1-rich granules and the ficolin-1-rich granule lumen (Fig. 3a,b). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis mostly implicated pathways related to the extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway, positive regulation of cytokine production and regulation of the extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway (Fig. 3c,d).

HSP90AA1 expression in intestinal ischemic tissue and IEC-6 cell H/R injury

To further investigate the role of HSP90AA1 in necroptosis induced by intestinal I/R injury, we detected the expression of HSP90AA1 in human ischemic intestinal tissues and found that intestinal injury was aggravated in intestinal ischemic tissue (Fig. 4a,b). Compared with that in normal tissues, the protein expression of HSP90AA1 and p-MLKL was increased in ischemic intestinal tissue (Fig. 4c–e). The same results were also shown in IEC-6 cells subjected to H/R injury, where the expression of HSP90AA1 and p-MLKL was considerably greater in the H/R group than in the normal group (Fig. 4f–h). These results suggest that the expression of HSP90AA1 is related to necroptosis in intestinal ischemic tissue and in IEC-6 cells subjected to H/R injury.

Experimental validation that HSP90AA1 enhances H/R-induced necroptosis by inducing p-MLKL in IEC-6 cells

We further used siRNA to inhibit HSP90AA1 expression in IEC-6 cells, and the expression level of HSP90AA1 in the H/R + si-HSP90AA1 group was lower than that in the H/R group, which was greater than that in the normal group. Furthermore, after the inhibition of HSP90AA1 expression by siRNA, the p-MLKL protein expression level was lower in the H/R + si-HSP90AA1 group than in the H/R group, which was greater than that in the normal group (Fig. 5a–c). These results suggested that the inhibition of HSP90AA1 expression may ameliorate necroptosis induced by H/R through the inhibition of p-MLKL expression (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Intestinal I/R injury is a common clinical problem. Injury to intestinal tissue is often related to many factors29. Research has indicated that intestinal I/R injury often leads to programmed cell death (PCD) in the intestinal tract, resulting in intestinal dysfunction30. PCD is a common form of active cell death that is dictated by genes and plays an important role in maintaining tissue homeostasis31. Its common types include the following five types: apoptosis, autophagy, ferroptosis, necroptosis and pyroptotic death32. It has been reported that the pseudokinase MLKL plays an important role in the necroptosis. Pseudokinase MLKL is activated by phosphorylation during the execution step of necroptosis. This activation causes MLKL to translocate to the plasma and cytoplasmic membranes, where it modifies ion channel activity to cause necrosis33. Previous researches have demonstrated that I/R can lead to necroptosis and that inhibition of necroptosis can improve the pathological status of mice in a murine intestinal I/R model, these studies mentioned that the occurrence of necroptosis is closely related to the expression of p-MLKL, which can be used to monitor the occurrence of necroptosis34–36, but the specific roles and mechanism has not been studied. Our findings revealed that p-MLKL expression was elevated in ischemic intestinal tissue and IEC-6 cells after H/R injury, indicating that H/R in IEC-6 cells could cause necroptosis of intestinal cells and in human ischemic intestinal tissue. In I/R tissue, p-MLKL expression was greater than that in the normal group, which is in line with the HE staining results and confirmed that intestinal I/R could cause necroptosis in IEC-6 cells.

In this study, a total of 18 DEGs (13 upregulated genes and 5 downregulated genes) were identified by a comprehensive bioinformatics analysis of microarray datasets. The DEGs were subsequently explored using bioinformatics analysis including PPI network construction and GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. The results of the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that these genes were significantly enriched in the “extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway”, “regulation of extrinsic apoptotic signaling pathway” and “interleukin-6 production”. Intracellular signals, including DNA damage, oxidative stress, and food deprivation, cause extrinsic apoptosis, which is in turn caused by extracellular signals that activate the death receptor family. These results suggest that there is a relationship between the differentially expressed genes and necroptosis during intestinal I/R injury. As a core gene, HSP90AA1, was obtained in intestinal I/R injury via coexpression network analysis of related proteins and genes.

HSP90AA1 is among the most abundant heat shock proteins, acting as a chaperone and maintaining protein stability when cells are damaged or stressed, restoring unfolded proteins, or targeting them for degradation. Recently, HSP90AA1 has been shown to be involved in the process of necroptosis associated with p-MLKL expression37. Zhou et al. reported that emodin induced necroptosis in prostate cancer cells via the HSP90 AA1/MLKL/PGAM pathway38. However, its function in intestinal I/R-induced necroptosis has not been studied. In our study, we used the bioinformatics database to perform a systematic bioinformatics analysis and found that there was a close relationship between the occurrence of necroptosis and the expression of HSP90AA1 in intestinal I/R injury. In the necroptosis pathway, a robust association was observed between the expression of p-MLKL and that of HSP90AA1. The same results were obtained through experimental verification. The expression of p-MLKL expression was elevated in ischemic intestinal tissue and IEC-6 cells after H/R injury, and HSP90AA1 expression followed p-MLKL expression in a proportionate manner. Furthermore, following the inhibition of HSP90AA1 expression in IEC-6 cells, p-MLKL expression was decreased compared with that in the non-inhibited group, suggesting that necroptosis induced by intestinal I/R injury could be ameliorated by the inhibition of HSP90AA1 expression. Therefore, HSP90AA1 may promote the progression of necroptosis induced by intestinal I/R injury.

Our study has several limitations. First, in this study, we investigated whether HSP90AA1 is involved in necroptosis-induced damage by promoting the expression of p-MLKL by bioinformatics analysis. Therefore, the exact mechanisms underlying the relationship between HSP90AA1 and necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury must be further elucidated in the future. Second, our study is limited to in vitro cell models and tissue samples, and without in vivo animal validation, our conclusions should be further explored in future studies. Furthermore, the sample size of the dataset is too small, and our study lacked cross-validation with multiple datasets, particularly from independent sources, which may affect the robustness of the results. In addition, there are many factors that may influence the development of necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury, such as environmental exposures or lifestyle factors. It is very important to analysis all the factors that may influence the development of necroptosis and the molecular subtypes of necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury.

Conclusion

In summary, we used bioinformatics analysis and experiments to explore the roles and mechanism of necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury and found that HSP90AA1 plays a significant role in intestinal I/R injury-induced necroptosis and promotes the progression of necroptosis in intestinal I/R injury.
