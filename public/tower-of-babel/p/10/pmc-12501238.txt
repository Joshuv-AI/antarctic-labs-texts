Investigation of the effects of variable process conditions on the production of Apple snacks by explosion puffing drying
Authors: Özcan Bulantekin, Merve Seçil Bardakçı
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The response surface method (RSM) was used to evaluate the conditions for producing apple snacks using explosion puffing drying (EPD). Independent variables included puffing temperature, pressure difference time, puffing pressure, and moisture content. The measured responses were the rehydration ratio (RR), expansion ratio (ER), total phenolic content (TPC), and total antioxidant activity (TAA). The model explained over 85% of the variations in these responses based on EPD conditions. It is crucial to preserve the bioactive components and structural properties of dried products. ANOVA results and response surface curves indicated that the EPD parameters significantly impacted the structural and bioactive properties. Increased puffing temperature and pressure formed more pores in the product, enhancing RR, ER, TPC, and phenolic acid release. EPD showed remarkable advantages over hot air drying in improving both the drying process and product. The optimal conditions for maximizing the structural and bioactive properties of apple snacks were determined as a puffing temperature of 64.44 °C, pressure difference time of 10.55 min, puffing pressure of 2.79 MPa, and moisture content of 48.13%. To our knowledge, no optimization study has examined the effects of system conditions on both structural properties and bioactive components in the production of apple snacks with EPD. Given its benefits for product quality, EPD is recommended for industrial application.

Introduction

Snacks are popular because they are practical to prepare, ready to consume, and easily accessible. However, these products are generally defined as foods with high caloric and low nutritional values. Approximately three-quarters of the snacks produced in the food industry do not contain the nutrients necessary for human health1. In particular, the high fat, sugar, and salt content of these products are considered risk factors. Along with increasing consumer awareness, the demand for healthier products is also increasing. Therefore, the need to preserve nutritional value and provide functional properties while producing snacks has emerged. Drying effectively reduces water activity to enhance shelf life and stability, while maintaining key sensory and nutritional properties2. Drying fruits and vegetables and consuming them as snacks are considered a suitable and feasible way to meet this demand.

The apple is an ideal fruit for snacks because of its high nutritional value. It has been shown to have beneficial effects on vascular function, blood pressure, lipids, inflammation, hyperglycemia, aid to preserve cardiovascular diseases3–5. Fruit chips have gained increasing popularity in contemporary dietary practices due to their desirable crunchy texture6. In addition, their high phytonutrient content and ease of transportation contribute significantly to their expanding market share. A variety of processing technologies are employed in the production of fruit chips, including deep-fat frying, vacuum frying, freeze-drying, hot air drying, steam puff drying, and microwave vacuum drying7–10. Drying is commonly used to produce apple snacks in the food industry11. However, depending on the drying method and processing conditions, many undesirable changes occur in products dried using traditional drying techniques, such as discoloration, low rehydration capacity and nutritional loss12. These limitations have paved the way for the development of new technologies and optimization of currently used drying techniques. Explosion puffing drying (EPD) technology is also one of the developed technologies.

EPD is a technology that offers significant advantages for producing healthy snacks. Preserving the porous structure allows the products to retain high rehydration capacity. An explosion puffing drying (EPD) system typically consists of two interconnected chambers: one pressurized and the other under vacuum. In a conventional EPD process, the product is first subjected to elevated pressures ranging from 0.1 to 0.3 MPa at temperatures between 80 and 130 °C7. This is followed by a rapid decompression step that facilitates moisture removal from the product. However, the effectiveness of puff drying largely depends on the initial moisture content of the product, which should be reduced to approximately 25–50% before the puffing stage. Puff drying alone is generally ineffective for fresh fruits with initial moisture contents above 80%8,13. Upon sudden pressure release, the vapor pressure inside the porous matrix becomes significantly higher than the external ambient pressure, driving rapid moisture evaporation in accordance with Darcy’s Law of Heat Transfer14. The high internal vapor pressure generated during pressurization causes mechanical stress on the cell walls. When the pressure suddenly drops, the boiling point of water decreases sharply, resulting in explosive evaporation and expansion of the product structure (Fig. 1). This leads to the “alveolation phenomenon,” in which a porous network is formed, enhancing water diffusion, accelerating drying rates, and reducing the energy consumption. The expanded porous structure also contributes to desirable textural qualities such as increased crispness and crunchiness15–19. It is also an efficient drying technique that preserves color, flavor, bioactive compounds and consumes less energy8,18,20. EPD is used to produce crispy fruits and vegetables such as peach13, mango, pitaya, and papaya20, pumpkin21, sweet potato22.

The drying method and associated process parameters utilized in the food industry have a significant impact on the heat and mass transfer mechanisms, as well as on the compositional, nutritional, and textural characteristics of the final product. Consequently, optimizing these parameters is essential for enhancing process efficiency, improving product quality, and ensuring maximum benefit from the drying operation. In this regard, optimization of the process conditions in explosion puffing drying (EPD) systems has gained increasing attention due to its potential to improve system performance and product quality. Previous studies have primarily focused on the optimization of EPD conditions for various fruits8,23,24. However, the majority of this research has been limited to evaluating structural changes, with relatively little emphasis on the comprehensive assessment of both the structural and bioactive quality attributes of dried apples23. Preserving bioactive compounds in dried products is equally critical as maintaining favorable structural properties, particularly in health-conscious food markets. Therefore, the present study aims to fill this gap by investigating the influence of key EPD process parameters including puffing temperature, puffing pressure, pressure difference time, and sample moisture content on the quality characteristics of apple chips. These characteristics include both structural integrity and the retention of bioactive compounds. Furthermore, this study employs Response Surface Methodology (RSM) to optimize the drying conditions, thereby providing a more holistic and quantitative understanding of how EPD parameters influence multiple quality aspects of dried apple products. This integrated approach distinguishes the current research from prior studies and contributes to the development of optimized, high-quality apple-based snack products.

Material and methods

Material and sample preparation

The Granny Smith apple variety (Malus domestica Borkh. var. Granny Smith) used in this study was obtained from a local producer (Gelendost, Isparta, Türkiye) and stored at 2.5 °C until processing. Before drying, the apples were washed, and excess surface moisture was removed using a paper towel. In fruit drying applications, fruits are commonly sliced within the 2–6 mm thickness range. Thinner slices accelerate drying by increasing the surface area-to-volume ratio and reducing diffusion distance; however, slices thinner than 3 mm may be prone to structural damage and degradation of heat-sensitive phenolic compounds26–28. In this study, a slice thickness of 5 mm was selected to achieve a balance between effective moisture removal and the preservation of structural and chemical quality. Apples were sliced to a thickness of 5 mm and standardized using a sizing device (35 mm diameter), after which they were subjected to a pre-drying process.

Drying process

Pre-drying methods

Before EPD, the sliced samples were pre-dried by hot-air drying (HAD) at 70 °C under air flow rate of 1.3 ± 0.02 m. s− 1 (flowing parallel to drying surface tray). Apple slices were dried at 70 °C in single and combined systems29,30. Bochnak and Świeca31 found that apple slices dried by HAD at 70 °C had the best color preservation, the highest antiradical capacity, and reducing power. Once the moisture content reached the target value specified in the experimental design (15–55% w.b.) for each trial, the pre-drying process was terminated. The dried samples were then sealed in polyamide/polyethylene bags and stored under controlled conditions. The initial moisture content of the apples to be used for drying was measured as (85.50 ± 0.45%).

Explosion puffing drying process

The self-made EPD apparatus system was used for the drying experiments (Fig. 2). In the EPD equipment, a humidity reader (model Tekon HT-25, accuracy = ± 2% RH, Turkey), 760 W electrical resistance on the bottom surface of the vessel, a digital thermostat (Enda ET4420, accuracy = ± 1 °C, Turkey), resistance and thermostat connected to the Pt100 thermocouple (Isiso, accuracy = ± 0.3 °C, Turkey), an overpressure preventive safety valve (Pakkens, range: 2–12 bar, Turkey), and a manometer (Pakkens, range: ≤40 bar PN x 1.25, Turkey) an electric motor fan (Volt, İzmir, Turkey) to ensure equal and homogeneous temperature in the cabinet during drying of products were used. Sliced at 5 mm thickness apples, pre-dried by hot air, with different moisture contents, were dried at different pressures, times, and temperatures using the puff drying method, and the moisture content was adjusted to ~ 6%.

Analyses

Rehydration measurement

Rehydration measurement was realized by modifying Salehi, Cheraghi and Rasouli32 and Rasooli Sharabiani, Kaveh, Abdi, Szymanek and Tanaś33. Approximately 5 g of the dried sample was treated with 100 mL of distilled water (25 °C) for 5 h and weighed at certain intervals (30, 60, 120, 180, 240, and 300 min). After 5 h of treatment, the samples were left to rehydrate for 24 h and weighed at the end of the treatment period. Before weighing, the outer surfaces of the samples were lightly dried with a paper towel and excess water was removed. The rehydration rate was calculated following Eq. Extraction process

The dried apple slices were ground, 2 g of sample was weighed (solid: solvent ratio of 1:10) and 20 mL of methanol was added35. The mixture was homogeneously fragmented and extracted using a multi-magnetic stirrer (Velp Scientifica, Italy) for 4 h, and then filtered through a filter. The extraction was performed in two parallels. The obtained extracts were used for determination total phenolic content and total antioxidant activity.

Determination of total phenolic content and total antioxidant activity

TPC of apple chips was determined as reported by Singleton and Rossi36. After removing 40 µL of the produced extract, 2.4 milliliters of distilled water were added and vortexed. Folin-Ciocalteu solution (0.2 mL) was then added, and the mixture was vortexed once more. Following this, 0.6 mL of saturated sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) was then added to begin the incubation phase. After adding 0.76 mL of distilled water, the mixture was incubated for two hours in the dark. At a wavelength of 765 nm, sample absorbance was measured using a spectrophotometer (T70 + UV/VIS spectrophotometer, PG Instruments, UK). The result was stated in terms of “mg gallic acid/100 g dry matter.”

Total antioxidant capacity (TAC) of the samples was calculated using the methodology described by Dorman, Peltoketo, Hiltunen and Tikkanen37. A mixture of 50 µL extract and 450 µL reagent buffer (Tris-HCl, 50mM, pH 7.4) was vortexed. After adding 1 mL of a 0.10 mM DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl) solution, the mixture was incubated in the dark for 30 min. Using a spectrophotometer (T70 + UV / VIS spectrophotometer, PG Instruments, UK) set to 515 nm, the absorbance of the samples was determined. Statistical analyses

Creating the experimental design for the optimization of the system, statistical analysis of the effect of the process parameters on the determined responses and determination of optimum conditions were carried out with the Minitab 17.0 package program. In the modeling analysis, the variable backward elimination method was adopted, and model terms were removed from the equation according to p ≤ 0.1. The compatibility of the model obtained with experimental data was evaluated by “lack of fit”, model regression coefficient (R2), adjusted regression coefficient (Adj-R2) and estimated multiple regression coefficient (Pre-R2) values. To compare the differences between the averages of the analysis results obtained from the apple samples according to the application, analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Tukey’s multiple comparison test were used in the Minitab 17.0 package program (p < 0.05).

Results and Discussion

In this study, the electro-puff drying (EPD) process of apple chips was evaluated by examining the effects of key process parameters, namely, puffing temperature, puffing pressure, puffing pressure difference time, and initial moisture content. Statistically significant models were developed for system optimization, as indicated by non-significant lack-of-fit values (p > 0.05), confirming the adequacy of the fitted models. Therefore, all measured responses, including the rehydration ratio, expansion ratio, total phenolic content (TPC), and total antioxidant activity (TAA), were included in the optimization process. The coefficients of the regression equations and the corresponding model performance parameters are presented in Table 4. The developed models explained more than 85% of the variability in the responses (R²>0.85), suggesting a high degree of model accuracy and predictive capability under the evaluated conditions.

Effect of EPD condition on rehydration ratio

During the drying process, heat treatment can lead to increased product hardness, volume reduction due to the collapse of cellular structures and pores, and changes in the final shape of the product. These structural alterations are critical, as they directly affect consumer acceptability. Rehydration is a complex phenomenon that reflects both the physical and chemical modifications induced by drying. Stress resulting from heat and moisture transfer causes various physical changes, such as shrinkage and cell wall degradation38,39. The rehydration ratio (RR) values of apple snacks obtained under different conditions based on the experimental design are presented in Table 3. A predictive model was developed for RR, where the first-order term of moisture content and the interaction terms between puffing temperature and moisture content, as well as puffing pressure and moisture content, were found to be statistically significant (p < 0.05). Other non-significant terms were excluded from the model. The lack-of-fit test indicated no significant deviation from the model (p > 0.05) (Table 4). As shown in Fig. 3a, the RR of apple snacks increased with higher puffing temperature and pressure. This enhancement can be explained by the rapid evaporation of internal moisture caused by high temperature and pressure gradients during the EPD process. The resulting sudden vaporization exerts internal pressure that expands the cellular structure, leading to the development of a more porous microstructure. Such structural changes enhance water absorption during rehydration Varnalis, Brennan, MacDougall and Gilmour40 also reported that higher puffing temperatures provide sufficient energy for this rapid moisture removal, resulting in greater expansion and improved rehydration capacity. Additionally, at relatively lower puffing temperatures, a reduced drying rate leads to a denser structure and lower RR. A positive correlation between volume expansion and RR has been reported in previous studies41. However, at elevated puffing temperatures and pressures, high initial moisture content negatively influenced the RR. This adverse effect may result from the structural damage caused by extreme thermal and mechanical stress during the EPD process, such as the degradation of key cellular components like cellulose and pectin23. Conversely, at lower puffing temperatures and pressures, higher initial moisture content contributed positively to RR (Fig. 3b, c), likely due to better cell integrity and moderate expansion during the drying process.

Effect of EPD condition on expansion ratio

The expansion ratio (ER) values of apple snacks obtained under different experimental conditions are presented in Table 3. In the regression model developed for ER, the first-order effect of moisture content and the interaction terms of puffing temperature-moisture content and puffing pressure-moisture content were statistically significant (p < 0.05). Non-significant terms were excluded from the model, and the lack-of-fit test confirmed that the model adequately fit the data (p > 0.05) (Table 4). As shown in Fig. 4a, ER increased with rising puffing temperature and puffing pressure. This trend can be attributed to intensified moisture evaporation under higher temperature and pressure conditions, which leads to greater internal vapor pressure and pore expansion within the product matrix. Yuan, Zhao, Wang, Xu, Chen, Kong and Wang23 reported similar findings, indicating that elevated puffing temperatures and pressures enhance moisture release and porosity, thereby increasing the expansion ratio in dried apple products. High-temperature processing accelerates heat transfer, causing the internal moisture to rapidly vaporize, thereby promoting the development of a porous structure42. Likewise, Tabtiang, Prachayawarakorn and Soponronnarit43 observed that increasing puffing temperature enhanced puff volume. However, they also noted that excessive thermal exposure negatively impacted product color due to pigment degradation and non-enzymatic browning, leading to undesirable darkening that may reduce consumer acceptability. In the EPD drying process, a rapid increase in temperature or a sudden pressure drop facilitates moisture flash-off from intracellular spaces, which contributes to pore formation and volumetric expansion of the product (Sullivan et al., 1980). While high initial moisture content combined with moderate puffing conditions (low temperature and pressure) was found to enhance porosity and ER, excessive puffing intensity (i.e., high temperature and pressure) may cause structural collapse of cell walls (Fig. 4b, c). Extreme temperature and pressure conditions during explosion puffing drying (EPD) can induce significant microstructural changes, the degradation of essential cell wall components such as cellulose and pectin. This degradation can reduce effective pore expansion, thereby limiting the ER despite high moisture levels prior to drying23.

Effect of EPD condition on total phenolic content

Apples and their by-products are recognized as rich sources of phenolic compounds, which are known for their antioxidant properties and associated health benefits44. The retention of these bioactive compounds during drying is often used as a key indicator of final product quality45. Therefore, optimizing the drying parameters in the explosion puffing drying (EPD) process is critical to minimizing the degradation of phenolic compounds. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has systematically investigated the influence of EPD parameters on the retention of phenolic content in dried apple snacks, indicating a gap in the current literature.

The total phenolic content (TPC) values of the dried apple snacks produced under varying conditions are presented in Table 3. The TPC values ranged between 349 and 725 mg GAE/100 g dry weight, which is consistent with findings from previous studies on dried apple products17,46,47. A regression model was developed to evaluate the effect of EPD parameters on TPC, as shown in Table 4. The model explained 83% of the variation in TPC (R² = 0.83), demonstrating good predictive performance. Among the first-order terms, only the moisture content was statistically significant (p ≤ 0.001), while all other linear terms and quadratic terms were excluded due to lack of significance. However, two interaction terms, puffing temperature-sample moisture and puffing temperature-moisture content were highly significant (p ≤ 0.001), indicating the critical role of thermal and moisture dynamics in phenolic retention. The lack-of-fit test revealed no significant deviation (p > 0.05), confirming the suitability of the model (Table 4). 3D response surface plots were generated to evaluate the influence of puffing temperature, puffing pressure, puffing pressure difference time, and initial sample moisture on the total phenolic content (TPC) of dried apple slices (Fig. 5). As shown in Fig. 5a and b, TPC increased linearly with rising puffing temperature, while puffing pressure and pressure difference duration exhibited comparatively minor effects. The elevated temperatures and pressures applied during EPD are believed to disrupt ester bonds that bind phenolic compounds to cell wall components, thereby enhancing the release and extractability of phenolic acids48–50. As illustrated in Fig. 5d, higher TPC values were observed when the puffing pressure difference time was short (e.g., 0.25 min), likely due to reduced exposure to pressure-induced degradation. In contrast, at extended puffing pressure difference times (e.g., 20.25 min), prolonged exposure to mechanical stress appeared to reduce TPC levels, possibly due to degradation of thermolabile phenolic compounds. The effect of pre-drying moisture content on TPC is presented in Fig. 5e and f. TPC increased linearly with higher initial moisture content in the apple slices, largely independent of puffing time and pressure. This is likely attributable to the shorter exposure of high-moisture samples to hot air during pre-drying. In this study, hot air drying (HAD) was conducted at 70 °C to adjust the initial moisture level before EPD. Samples with higher residual moisture reached target moisture values more rapidly and were thus subjected to thermal stress for a shorter duration. Given the thermal sensitivity of phenolic compounds, prolonged drying of low-moisture samples may result in their degradation, as supported by previous studies on berries51, pears52, and apples45. Notably, this trend reversed at higher puffing temperatures (e.g., 100 °C). At such elevated temperatures, samples with high initial moisture content remained under heat for longer durations to reach the final desired moisture level (~ 6%), which likely contributed to increased phenolic degradation. In contrast, for samples with low moisture content, the shorter drying time may have mitigated the negative effects of high puffing temperatures on TPC (Fig. 5c).

Effect of EPD condition on total antioxidant activity

Total antioxidant activity (TAA) results for the apple snacks are shown in Table 3. TAA values were between 17.50 and 46.49 µmol TE/g dw. The lowest TAA value was obtained at 4.5 MPa at 90 °C for 15.25 min 45% moisture content, and the highest was obtained at 35% moisture content at 3 MPa at 80 °C for 10.25 min. The regression analysis (Table 4) revealed that the developed model explained 92% of the variation in TAA. While first-order terms were not statistically significant, quadratic terms of puffing temperature and moisture content had a significant effect at the p ≤ 0.01 level, and quadratic effects of puffing pressure difference time and puffing pressure were highly significant at p ≤ 0.001. Furthermore, interaction effects between puffing temperature and sample moisture were significant at p ≤ 0.01, while the interaction between puffing pressure difference time and puffing pressure was significant at p ≤ 0.05. The lack-of-fit test indicated no significant deviation, suggesting the model fits well (p > 0.05). As shown in Fig. 6, the TAA response exhibited a clear quadratic pattern, with values increasing up to optimal intermediate levels of the drying parameters, followed by a decline at higher intensities. This behavior aligns with known degradation patterns of thermally sensitive bioactive compounds. Antioxidant activity in apple snacks is closely linked to phenolic content and ascorbic acid, both of which are susceptible to degradation under elevated temperatures and extended processing durations. Specifically, vitamin C, a key contributor to antioxidant capacity, is highly heat-sensitive and degrades rapidly above 60 °C53,54, which likely explains the observed reduction in TAA at higher puffing drying intensities.

Optimization analysis and verification

Optimization of the drying method is essential for enhancing the final product quality, improving process performance, and informing future research. In this context, to identify the optimal drying conditions for apple snacks, specifically puffing temperature, puffing pressure difference time, puffing pressure, and moisture content, key quality indicators such as rehydration ratio (RR), expansion ratio (ER), total phenolic content (TPC), and total antioxidant activity (TAA) were selected as target responses owing to their high relevance to product functionality and nutritional value. Accordingly, these responses were maximized during the optimization process, with the importance levels of all variables set to the first order. The optimal conditions were determined as follows: a puffing temperature of 64.44 °C, puffing pressure of 2.79 MPa, puffing pressure difference time of 10.55 min, and sample moisture content of 48.13%. Under these conditions, the predicted responses were 404.82 for RR, 1.87 for ER, 647.53 for TPC, and 29.67 for TAA. The validation of the model under these optimized conditions yielded a maximum absolute error of less than 10%, demonstrating the reliability and predictive accuracy of the developed model (Table 5). Compared to conventional hot-air drying, the explosion puffing drying (EPD) method exhibited several advantages, including enhanced rehydration capacity, accelerated drying rates, improved volume expansion, and increased retention of bioactive compounds47,55. To further evaluate the effectiveness of the EPD method, a control group of apple snacks was dried using hot air at 70 °C without undergoing EPD treatment. The results clearly demonstrated that the structural properties and bioactive compound content of apple snacks dried using EPD were significantly superior to those of the control group (p ≤ 0.05) (Table 5). These findings are consistent with previous studies on various fruits such as jackfruit17, jujube56, black mulberry, and apple46, where the application of EPD following hot-air drying resulted in improved rehydration characteristics and enhanced levels of bioactive components in the final dried products.

Conclusions

In this study, the response surface methodology (RSM) was employed to systematically evaluate the drying conditions during explosion puffing drying (EPD) of apple slices pretreated with hot-air drying. An experimental design was developed based on RSM, incorporating puffing temperature, puffing pressure difference time, puffing pressure, and moisture content as independent variables. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) results indicated that the rehydration ratio (RR), expansion ratio (ER), total phenolic content (TPC), and total antioxidant activity (TAA) were significantly influenced by the selected drying parameters. Increases in puffing temperature and puffing pressure were found to promote the formation of more porous structures within the apple slices, enhancing the release of phenolic compounds and consequently increasing RR, ER, and TPC. However, TAA exhibited a decline when puffing temperature, puffing pressure, and pressure difference time exceeded their respective intermediate levels. These findings underscore the critical importance of optimizing drying parameters to achieve desirable quality attributes in the final apple snack product. The optimal EPD conditions were identified as a puffing temperature of 64.44 °C, a puffing pressure difference time of 10.55 min, a puffing pressure of 2.79 MPa, and a sample moisture content of 48.13%. The outcomes of this study are expected to contribute meaningfully to the existing body of knowledge, support industrial applications, and guide future research in the field.
