Anti‐Obesity Pharmacotherapy and Emerging Multimodal Interventions for Obstructive Sleep Apnea
Authors: Anish Preshy, Cornelius J. Fernandez, Mohammad Hamid Nedai, Joseph M. Pappachan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a major public health crisis affecting nearly one billion people worldwide and is associated with significant cardiovascular and metabolic complications. The prevalence of OSA is rising steadily due to the obesity pandemic and is contributed by interacting anatomical, inflammatory, and neuro‐respiratory mechanisms. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) remains the gold standard for the management of OSA; however, it does not address underlying obesity or weight‐independent pathophysiology. Obesity is an important modifiable risk factor for OSA, as weight loss is associated with resolution/improvement of the disease. Therefore, growing evidence now supports surgical and medical management of obesity as complementary strategies to improve both body weight and apnea‐hypopnoea index (AHI), prompting a paradigm shift towards integrated, multimodal care. Bariatric interventions typically achieve 25%‐35% total weight loss and yield significant but variable reductions in AHI with remission rates of 50%–75%, driven by mechanical unloading, improved ventilatory control, and favorable metabolic and anti‐inflammatory effects. However, it is constrained by eligibility, cost, and perioperative risks. Alternatively, Incretin‐based therapies, particularly Tirzepatide, achieve 10%–22% weight loss and reduce AHI to 12–30 events per hour, securing the first regulatory approval for OSA based on the largest trial‐level AHI reductions. The recent introduction of several therapeutic agents with excellent weight loss potential has reshaped the management landscape of people with obesity and OSA. This review aims to analyze the literature across surgical, endoscopic, and pharmacological interventions and OSA while proposing an individualized treatment framework integrating weight‐loss pharmacotherapy with device‐based and structured lifestyle strategies and surgical interventions, tailored to disease phenotypes.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a significant public health concern, characterized by ventilatory disruptions from repetitive upper airway collapse during sleep secondary to reduced upper airway muscle tone during sleep, leading to intermittent hypoxia, sleep fragmentation, and substantial cardiovascular, neurocognitive, and metabolic morbidities [1, 2, 3]. OSA severity is defined by the apnea‐hypopnea index (AHI), classified as mild (AHI 5–15 events/hour), moderate (AHI 15–30 events/hour), or severe (AHI above 30 events/hour), however clinical management must integrate the patient's symptom burden, cardiovascular risks, and comorbidities, to ensure nuanced evaluation of disease impact beyond the AHI score and creation of an multimodal management plan [4]. Multimodal therapy in OSA aims to explore sequential or concurrent application of multiple treatment modalities, including structured lifestyle modification, mechanical airway support (continuous positive airway pressure [CPAP], mandibular advancement devices [MADs]), weight loss pharmacotherapy and surgical interventions, individualized to patients' characteristics, disease phenotype, and treatment preferences.

Bidirectionally, OSA promotes weight gain through hormonal and metabolic dysregulation, completing a vicious cycle of mutual exacerbation [5]. Although CPAP remains the gold standard therapy, it does not impact the underlying obesity, and adherence rates have been observed to decline over time. Bariatric surgery is effective but is associated with perioperative risk and is not universally available due to the cost and eligibility criteria [6]. As a result, there is an increasing need for pharmacotherapeutic options that target both excess weight and OSA pathophysiology simultaneously, ideally providing durable effectiveness across the range of obesity‐related OSA.

This narrative review examines the evolving landscape of anti‐obesity pharmacotherapy in OSA compared with bariatric surgical approaches and proposes its role within a multimodal treatment framework to optimize weight loss, OSA symptom burden and AHI, and associated cardiometabolic risk. An appraisal of the evidence heterogeneity, adverse effect profiles, and clinical management strategies also provides a framework for individualized patient selection and treatment integration.

Epidemiology and Burden of OSA

The burden of clinically significant OSA is increasing worldwide, affecting nearly 935 million adults worldwide, of whom nearly 425 million adults suffer from moderate to severe OSA (AHI ≥ 15) [7]. Prevalence is highest among men, older adults, and those with obesity [7]. Obesity, defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2, is the most potent modifiable risk factor for OSA. The increasing prevalence of obesity across the world in recent decades has resulted in an exponential increase in the incidence of OSA globally, increasing its incidence by up to fourfold and being present in over 70% of patients with moderate to severe OSA [8, 9]. In Australian cohorts, 1 in 10 adults is affected by clinically significant OSA (40% men and over 25% of women), with rates increasing sharply with age and obesity [1, 10]. Rural and indigenous communities bear the burden due to poor accessibility and multiple socioeconomic deprivations.

Pathophysiology of OSA

OSA is a chronic multisystem disorder driven by both weight‐dependent (Obesity‐related) and weight‐independent mechanisms. A combination of upper airway compromise, unstable neuromuscular and ventilatory control, and obesity‐related inflammatory and metabolic pathways, primarily driven by recurrent apneas, chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH), hypercapnia, and sleep fragmentation, results in cardiometabolic, vascular, and neurocognitive consequences [11].

Anatomical and Mechanical Factors:

Anatomical factors such as parapharyngeal and upper airway fat deposition, craniofacial abnormalities (e.g. retrognathia, hypoplastic maxilla, narrow maxilla), and conditions associated with enlarged soft tissues, such as hypothyroidism, acromegaly, and cardiac failure, result in reduced airway distensibility, elevated collapsibility, and mechanical loading, causing recurrent obstructions during sleep [12]. Obesity causes further thoracic and abdominal loads, decreasing lung volumes and caudal traction on the upper airways [13].

Neuro‐Respiratory Control and Ventilatory Instability:

Non‐anatomical contributors include reduced responsiveness of upper airway muscles, low respiratory arousal threshold, increased ventilatory loop gain, and pharyngeal neuromuscular dysfunction [14]. CIH and sleep fragmentation further alter chemosensitivity, autonomic balance and cortical arousal circuits, reinforcing a high‐gain, unstable ventilatory system that promotes cycling between obstruction, arousal, and hyperventilation [15, 16, 17, 18].

Cellular and Molecular Mechanisms of Obesity‐related OSA:

At the molecular level, CIH and sleep fragmentation trigger systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, sympathetic activation, and metabolic dysregulation. Expansion of visceral adipose tissue promotes macrophage infiltration and activation, leading to increased pro‐inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor [TNF‐α], interleukin‐6 [IL‐6], and interleukin‐1 beta [IL‐1β)] [19]. Concurrently, CIH and reperfusion cycles increase NLRP3 inflammasome activation in monocytes and macrophages. Thus, generating a self‐perpetuating inflammatory state that leads to impaired pharyngeal muscle function through oxidative stress‐mediated myofibre dysfunction and edema formation, thereby causing further airway compromise during sleep [14]. Adipokine dysregulation, particularly obesity‐associated leptin resistance and reduced adiponectin, impairs hypothalamic appetite suppression, respiratory drive, and insulin sensitivity, thus disrupting neuro‐respiratory balance and further linking OSA to metabolic syndrome [20, 21]. Additionally, CIH drives excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) via NADPH oxidase activation and mitochondrial dysfunction, elevates asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA), and impairs endothelial nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, all of which contribute to endothelial dysfunction and early atherosclerotic changes [22, 23]. CIH, excess adiposity, and gut dysbiosis (causing increased abundance of gram‐negative bacteria and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) production) all result in insulin resistance (via phosphorylation alterations in insulin receptor substrates (IRS1/2) and PI3K/AKT pathway inhibition), ectopic lipid deposition, and toll‐like receptor (TLR)‐ mediated systemic inflammation [14]. The above mechanisms explain how OSA is associated with multisystem involvement, including cardiovascular disease, pulmonary hypertension, metabolic syndrome, neurocognitive impairment, as well as its impact on malignancy, renal, and reproductive domains. OSA often has bidirectional aggravation of comorbidities like metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), type 2 diabetes mellitus, heart failure, stroke, and hypogonadism [11, 14, 24, 25, 26, 27].

Current Treatment Modalities in OSA and Role of Multimodal Therapy

Management of OSA involves a range of evidence‐based strategies, with CPAP remaining the gold standard for most patients, aimed at maintaining upper airway patency. CPAP achieves a mean AHI reduction of approximately 86%, normalizing or near‐normalizing indexes, thus significantly modifying disease severity [28, 29]. Dental appliances, such as MADs, offer an established alternative, particularly for patients intolerant of CPAP or with mild‐to‐moderate disease. MADs reduce AHI by 30%–50% on average, with more than half of patients achieving at least a 50% reduction; complete normalization (AHI < 10) occurs less frequently, particularly in severe OSA [30, 31].

Intensive lifestyle modifications (diet, physical activity, avoidance of alcohol and smoking, and behavioral support) remain foundational in OSA management. Even 5%–10% of weight loss can lower AHI and improve metabolic markers, and ≥ 10% weight loss can reduce AHI by 25%–50% in many patients [32]. However, achieving meaningful weight loss without support proves challenging in the context of obesity‐associated metabolic dysfunction.

Other alternative therapies for the management of OSA include positional therapy (vibrotactic or mechanical positional therapy), hypoglossal nerve stimulation, daytime neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) of the tongue, and various surgical procedures of the nose, tonsils, and uvulopalatopharyngoplasty, as well as maxillomandibular advancement. Positional therapy can reduce AHI by 50%, but only if adherence is high and patients are correctly selected [33, 34]. Hypoglossal nerve stimulation notably achieves sustained AHI reductions of 60%–70%, with benefits lasting at least three to 5 years in eligible patients [35]. Literature surrounding NMES of the tongue shows AHI reductions of 25%–35% for mild OSA and snoring; however, studies are limited by small sample sizes and short duration [36, 37]. Although they contribute to AHI reduction, they have minimal effect on weight reduction. An individualized approach according to the patient's airway anatomy, disease severity, cardiometabolic comorbidities, and preferences/goals should be the evidence‐based standard of care.

Although BPD is associated with the highest percentage of weight loss, it is currently less preferred by many centers due to the risk of multi‐nutrient deficiencies leading to gross malabsorption [39]. Modern Bariatric surgeries (particularly RYGB and SG) achieve a mean weight loss percentage of 25%–35%, with marked efficacy in OSA resolution. Endoscopic interventions, including endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty, duodenal‐jejunal bypass liner, and intragastric balloons, all offer less invasive options that provide moderate weight loss and symptom control [46, 47].

Mechanisms of the Benefits of Bariatric Surgery in OSA

Bariatric surgery provides significant benefit for OSA through complementary weight‐dependent and weight‐independent mechanisms. Weight‐dependent effects include substantial reductions in overall and regional adiposity (parapharyngeal fat), leading to reduced airway collapsibility and mechanical loading. This leads to improved lung function by increasing functional residual capacity and expiratory reserve volume, reducing the work of breathing, and enhancing caudal traction on the upper airway, all of which collectively contribute to significant reductions in AHI [48, 49].

Weight‐independent mechanisms provide additional therapeutic benefits beyond simple mechanical decompression of airway structures, including significant improvements in glycaemic control and dyslipidaemia after bariatric surgery, often preceding substantial weight loss, suggesting metabolic pathways that are independent of weight reduction [50, 51]. Second, early and sustained reductions in systemic inflammatory markers, including C‐reactive protein (CRP), TNF‐α, and interleukin‐6, are observed after surgery [52, 53]. These anti‐inflammatory changes are associated with improved upper airway muscle tone and endothelial function and reduced airway edema, thereby contributing to a lower AHI [52]. Furthermore, bariatric procedures improve respiratory center control through improved insulin sensitivity and reduced leptin resistance, leading to more stable ventilatory patterns and fewer central apneas [39]. Third, bariatric surgery provides improvements in sleep architecture, including an increase in slow‐wave sleep and reduced sleep fragmentation, which contribute to diminished daytime sleepiness independent of AHI changes [54]. Finally, surgery also induced durable remodeling of gut microbiota to resemble the composition of lean individuals, with reductions in branched‐chain amino acids and an increase in gut hormones (GLP‐1, GLP‐2, and PYY) [55].

The varying effects between bariatric procedures reflect varying degrees of these mechanisms. RYGB is associated with greater long‐term weight loss than SG, which may explain the observed differences in OSA remission rates [39, 41, 42]. Furthermore, the influence of RYGB on gut hormones (GLP‐1, PYY, and ghrelin) may confer additional weight‐independent metabolic benefits. However, both procedures significantly outperform ABG, with SG achieving an 83% higher OSA remission rate than ABG [39].

Pharmacotherapy in OSA

Various pharmacological agents, including noradrenergic agents (Atomoxetine, Reboxetine), antimuscarinic agents (oxybutynin, solifenacin, or hyoscine with atomoxetine or reboxetine), acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (Donepezil, Physostigmine), serotonergics (trazodone, mirtazapine), central respiratory stimulants (acetazolamide, theophylline, doxapram, almitrine), and hypnotics (eszopiclone, zolpidem), have been tried in the treatment of OSA, with varying effects on AHI reduction (7–10 events/hour), however with no beneficial effect on weight reduction [56].

Incretin‐Based Therapies

GLP‐1 Receptor Agonists (GLP‐1RAs)

GLP‐1 receptor agonists, such as liraglutide and semaglutide, exert their anti‐obesity effects through central appetite inhibition, delayed gastric emptying, enhanced insulin sensitivity, and reduced systemic inflammation. Experimental models also suggest a role in upper airway muscle control. The pivotal SCALE sleep apnea trial has demonstrated liraglutide's efficacy, with an AHI reduction of 12.2 events/hour and a mean weight loss of 5.7% [58]. There are no randomized controlled trials (RCTs) available specifically investigating the beneficial effects of semaglutide among patients with OSA. A retrospective cohort study that aimed to generate real‐world evidence comparing liraglutide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide found that, in patients with OSA and T2DM, tirzepatide was associated with a lower incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events than liraglutide and semaglutide [65]. Additionally, tirzepatide and semaglutide are associated with a lower incidence of OSA in comparison to liraglutide. Recent meta‐analyses reported a pooled AHI reduction of 14–16 events/hour, mean weight losses approaching 11–12 kg, with improvements in blood pressure and sleep‐related quality of life [66]. However, its cost, gastrointestinal side effects and the requirement for subcutaneous administration are significant limitations for general use.

Dual GLP‐1/GIP Agonist (Tirzepatide)

Tirzepatide, a dual GLP‐1/glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) agonist, represents the most effective anti‐obesity medication to date. The SURMOUNT‐OSA trial discovered that Tirzepatide achieved a mean weight loss of 18%–20% and a mean AHI reduction of 29.3 events/hour in active CPAP users and 25.3 events/hour in non‐active CPAP users [61, 62]. It further demonstrated marked reductions in hypoxic burden, daytime sleepiness, systolic blood pressure, and CRP levels, and supported reductions in cardiometabolic risk and improved patient‐reported sleep outcomes [61]. Based on these results, Tirzepatide was FDA‐approved in December 2024 for the treatment of OSA, making it the first pharmacologic treatment approved for OSA.

Mechanism of Incretin‐Based Therapies Benefits in OSA

Incretin‐based therapies provide dual benefits. Weight‐dependent effects via substantial weight loss of 10%–16% in obese individuals through reducing appetite, slowing gastric emptying, and improving insulin sensitivity. This reduces adiposity, particularly around the upper airways, thereby lowering pharyngeal collapsibility and increasing airway patency during sleep, thereby improving AHI [62]. Significant weight loss also enhances sleep‐related quality of life and cardiometabolic profiles [67].

In addition to their influence on body weight, incretin‐based therapies have weight‐independent effects that may benefit patients with OSA. GLP‐1 agonists modulate central neuroregulatory pathways involved in respiratory control and arousal, thereby enhancing ventilatory stability and reducing the frequency of apnoeic episodes [62]. Studies have also shown that incretin agents can decrease systemic and airway inflammation as well as improve insulin resistance, which may positively influence OSA severity even in those without substantial weight reduction [62]. Additionally, these drugs impact cardiovascular and metabolic risk factors, improving blood pressure, glycaemic control, and lipid profiles, all of which could synergistically reduce overall OSA burden and related comorbidities, irrespective of changes in body mass [68]. Other Anti‐Obesity Medications

Phentermine‐topiramate reduces appetite and promotes weight loss, with RCTs suggesting modest AHI reductions of 5–7 events/hour in OSA [64]. Naltrexone‐bupropion acts via central appetite modulation and is approved for weight management; however, it lacks robust evidence for AHI reduction in OSA, with small studies indicating only mild improvements [63]. Orlistat, a peripheral lipase inhibitor, has limited efficacy on OSA outcomes, although it may be used in selected individuals who are intolerant of other agents [63].

Adverse Effects of Anti‐Obesity Pharmacotherapy

GLP‐1RAs and Tirzepatide, though, achieve substantial weight loss and AHI reduction; it is not without distinct and dose‐dependent adverse effect profiles that require structured monitoring and individualized risk‐benefit analysis. Incretin‐based agents commonly cause gastrointestinal toxicity, including nausea, vomiting, delayed gastric emptying, diarrhea and constipation in approximately 40%–70% patients and are the leading cause for impaired adherence [69]. However, these adverse effects are usually mild to moderate, dose dependent and cluster around the initiation/titration phase, with the majority of obesity trials revealing non‐serious adverse effects [69, 70, 71]. Beyond the classical GI symptoms, emerging data highlight increased risk of gastroesophageal reflux disease, psychiatric complications (including mood changes and other mental health symptoms) and ocular problems (progression of diabetic retinopathy and possible NAION) [71]. Other rare but serious concerns include pancreatitis and gallbladder disease, particularly in individuals with pre‐existing metabolic comorbidities; however, large observational and pharmacovigilance studies provide a reassuring pancreatic safety profile [71]. There are also reports in retrospective studies of association with thyroid malignancy; however, to date, extensive RCTs have not supported this association [71].

Among other anti‐obesity agents, network meta‐analyses indicate that Phentermine‐topiramate, orlistat, and naltrexone‐bupropion are associated with adverse effects, including gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, hepatic, and neuropsychiatric symptoms, particularly with combination regimens and in patients receiving concomitant drugs [63, 64, 72].

Therefore, for OSA management, an individualized risk‐benefit analysis, slow up‐titration, and close monitoring are paramount to identify adverse effects promptly and to aim for early discontinuation in non‐responders to minimize harm.

Comparison and Integration into Clinical Practice

Recent meta‐analyses and systematic reviews demonstrate substantial benefit of both bariatric interventions and incretin‐based therapies on weight loss and AHI reduction; however, the magnitude and durability differ. Historically, surgical interventions have yielded significant weight loss, particularly in severe obesity; however, emerging evidence on pharmacotherapy is promising in approaching the magnitude of bariatric surgery.

A significant limitation of the comparative synthesis in this review is the heterogeneity of treatment study designs, baseline characteristics, endpoints, and follow‐up periods. The AHI reduction quoted for each management is within their study's parameters rather than collective data. It therefore cannot be used to infer a direct superiority of one management over another, nor should it be interpreted as a head‐to‐head efficacy ranking.

Despite this limitation, we can still infer that both bariatric surgery and incretin‐based therapies achieve clinically significant benefits in OSA, including substantial weight loss, reduced symptom burden and AHI, and favorable cardiometabolic effects. Tirzepatide likely achieves weight‐loss and AHI‐reduction outcomes comparable to surgical benefits, with substantially lower procedure morbidity and surgical risk.

Tirzepatide demonstrated better efficacy than other GLP‐1RAs. While GLP‐1RAs have demonstrated the next‐best effect on concomitant AHI reduction and weight loss, making them a viable alternative for patients intolerant of Tirzepatide. Although Phentermine‐Topiramate also demonstrates impressive AHI reduction and weight reduction, its adverse effects make it less preferable, especially in patients with risk factors. It should be reserved for use when Tirzepatide and GLP‐1RAs are unaffordable or contraindicated, in carefully selected, closely monitored patients without significant cardiovascular or psychiatric disease. By contrast, other agents yielded smaller reductions in AHI with modest weight loss. Orlistat was the least effective, with minimal weight loss and no direct effect on upper airway physiology, and thus played only a minimal complementary role in the management of OSA.

Individualized Multimodal Treatment Framework

In practice, most patients require a multimodal approach combining: (1) airway‐targeted therapies (CPAP, MADs, positional devices, upper airway stimulation), (2) Weight loss strategies (lifestyle, pharmacological, and surgical), and (3) symptom‐targeted agents (wake‐promoting agents, neuro‐respiratory modulators) to ensure an individualized strategy. Building on the data above, we can formulate treatment plans based on key factors: (1) OSA severity and endotype (weight‐dependent vs. independent drivers, symptom burden), (2) WHO obesity class and weight trajectory (BMI, central vs peripheral adiposity, prior weight loss attempts/treatments), and (3) comorbidity profile and patient preference (cardiometabolic comorbidities, perioperative risk, mental health history, preference for surgical vs injectable vs oral).

Rather than ranking surgery and pharmacotherapy as mutually exclusive options, a multimodal strategy integrating lifestyle changes, airway device therapy, weight loss pharmacotherapy, and surgical intervention, tailored to the patient's profile, comorbidities, and preferences, should be considered. Emerging Therapies and Uncertainties

Recent advances in pharmacotherapy focus on several novel pathways, including co‐agonists targeting GLP‐1/glucagon co‐agonists or GLP‐1/GIP/glucagon triple agonists, dual amylin and calcitonin receptor agonists (DACRAs), oral incretin agents and other agents that modulate ventilatory control and arousal threshold.

GLP‐1/glucagon co‐agonists and triple agonists (Retatrutide), which target GLP‐1, GIP, and glucagon receptors, work in synergy to stimulate energy expenditure and promote substantial weight loss [73, 74]. They are now under investigation for their direct and indirect effects on OSA severity. Early‐phase results with triple agonists (GLP‐1/GIP/glucagon) are promising, but long‐term durability and direct OSA‐related outcomes remain to be clarified [73, 74].

Other new drugs in development are the dual amylin and calcitonin receptor agonists (DACRAs), which have shown metabolic benefits, appetite suppression, and anti‐obesity effects in early human trials [75]. Additionally, oral versions of incretin‐based medications are in development, providing less invasive options to injectable therapy for weight loss and potentially OSA [76]. There is also research surrounding medications (Atomoxetine/oxybutynin, Atomoxetine/Pimavanserin, Solriamferol, and orexin pathway modulators like Lemborexant) for “lean” OSA phenotypes and potential management targeting upper airway muscle tone, ventilatory control, and arousal threshold to improve daytime symptoms by targeting sleep architecture [77, 78, 79, 80, 81].

The US FDA has approved two stimulants (modafinil and armodafinil) and a non‐stimulant (solriamfetol) as wake‐promoting agents (WPAs) to treat excessive daytime sleepiness associated with OSA [82]. Another drug, pitolisant, which is an FDA‐approved drug for narcolepsy, is being investigated for excessive daytime sleepiness in OSA [83]. Among these four wake‐promoting agents, solriamfetol exhibited the greatest efficacy on the Epworth Sleepiness Scale and the Maintenance of Wakefulness Test [82].

Despite these advances, several gaps remain regarding the long‐term durability of pharmacotherapeutic agents, the effects of withdrawal, optimal patient selection, comparative effectiveness across drug classes, interactions between novel pharmacotherapies and standard OSA treatments, and how to integrate them to achieve sustained benefit. Large‐scale, real‐world data are required to better understand the appropriate use of these therapies beyond the populations selected in pivotal trials.

Conclusions

The rapid evolution of weight loss pharmacotherapy is shaping a precision medicine paradigm in OSA. It is moving beyond just device‐based management to a more integrated multimodal approach, offering holistic care that involves pharmacotherapy alongside lifestyle interventions, CPAP, and oral appliances. These emerging therapies could represent, in the future, an alternative to bariatric surgery as the first‐line treatment for obesity‐related OSA. The FDA's approval of Tirzepatide for OSA proves this paradigm shift. However, substantial research gaps still exist, including long‐term impact on cardiometabolic outcomes, optimal combination with other therapies, and personalized approaches based on OSA phenotype and possibly weight‐independent disease activity. The success of Incretin‐based agents compared to other anti‐obesity agents now shapes clinical decision‐making in obesity‐associated OSA, with AHI reductions by the Tirzepatide serving as a standard of care for OSA pharmacotherapy.
