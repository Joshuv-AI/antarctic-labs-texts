Circumpatellar Denervation With Patelloplasty in Primary Total Knee Arthroplasty Without Resurfacing: Five-year Outcomes by International Cartilage Repair Society Patellar Cartilage Grade
Authors: Thana Bamroongshawgasame, Prangthong Unprasert, Wichayan Chobchai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatellar management in primary total knee arthroplasty (TKA) remains controversial. When the patella is not resurfaced, circumpatellar denervation with patelloplasty (CP + PP) is widely used, but it is unclear whether outcomes differ by International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) patellar cartilage grade.MethodsWe conducted a cohort study of TKAs performed with a standardized non-resurfacing protocol incorporating CP + PP. Intraoperative patellar cartilage was graded ICRS 2, 3, or 4. Four patient-reported outcomes (PROs)—Oxford Knee Score (OKS), visual analog scale (VAS), Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC), and Kujala Anterior Knee Pain Scale (AKPS)—were assessed up to 60 months. Longitudinal change was modeled with generalized estimating equations, and adjusted estimated marginal means (EMMs) at 60 months were compared across ICRS grades. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds were used to calculate the proportion of knees that improved.ResultsWe analyzed 180 knees (26 ICRS 2, 112 ICRS 3, 42 ICRS 4). All PROs improved markedly, with high 60-month OKS and AKPS scores and near-floor VAS and WOMAC values. Every knee exceeded the MCID for all 4 PROs (100%). Adjusted 60-month EMMs were similar across ICRS grades for OKS, VAS, and WOMAC. AKPS showed a small adjusted advantage for ICRS 4 vs 2 (EMM difference +4.35). Complications were uncommon and did not differ significantly by grade.ConclusionsIn non-resurfacing primary TKA, a standardized CP + PP protocol produced durable 5-year improvement, with universal attainment of MCID and only minor differences across ICRS grades. CP + PP appears to be a reproducible non-resurfacing option.Keywords: Total knee arthroplasty, Patellar denervation, Patelloplasty, International Cartilage Repair Society, Long-term outcome

Introduction

Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) reliably alleviates pain and restores function in advanced knee osteoarthritis after nonoperative care fails. However, the optimal strategy for patellar management remains debated, particularly regarding the long-term effectiveness, survivorship, and patient-reported outcomes of resurfacing vs non-resurfacing approaches [[1], [2], [3]]. Beyond global outcomes, patellofemoral decisions may influence recovery trajectories, tracking, and satisfaction, while patellar resurfacing can introduce specific risks such as fracture, component loosening, maltracking, and instability [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Technical factors, including surgical technique and trochlear component design, may exert effects comparable to baseline patellar cartilage quality [8].

Circumpatellar denervation with patelloplasty (CP + PP) has emerged as a pragmatic non-resurfacing option that aims to preserve patellofemoral mechanics and reduce anterior knee pain. Randomized trials and meta-analyses of denervation-based strategies report either superiority for pain relief or clinical equivalence compared with alternative patellar management approaches [9,10]. A contemporary series of non-resurfacing TKA further suggests that unresurfaced patellae, including those with more advanced structural degeneration, do not invariably experience inferior outcomes, challenging the assumption that greater cartilage loss mandates resurfacing [[11], [12], [13]]. Nevertheless, grade-stratified, longer-term evidence using standardized cartilage grading systems—notably the International Cartilage Repair Society (ICRS) classification—remains limited; many prior studies pooled heterogeneous populations, used varied techniques and implant designs, and infrequently reported outcomes according to cartilage grade, thereby constraining tailored decision-making at the time of surgery [[14], [15], [16], [17]].

To address these gaps, we conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study of primary non-resurfacing TKA performed with a uniform CP + PP protocol, stratifying outcomes by intraoperative ICRS patellar cartilage grade (2-4). We hypothesized that CP + PP would yield substantial, durable improvements in pain and function across ICRS grades over 5 years, with only modest attenuation of patellofemoral-specific outcomes in higher-grade disease.

Material and methods

Study design

The institutional review board approved this retrospective, single-center cohort study. Consecutive primary TKAs performed without patellar resurfacing using a standardized CP + PP protocol between January 2012 and December 2018 were identified from a prospectively maintained database. Medical records of 246 patients were reviewed. Forty-one patients had incomplete medical records, 22 had less than 60 months of follow-up, and 3 died from medical conditions unrelated to the index TKA; all were excluded. The final analytic cohort comprised 168 patients (180 knees), including 12 patients who underwent bilateral TKA. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology statement for cohort studies (Fig. 1) [18].

STROBE flow diagram of patient selection. STROBE, Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology

Participants

Inclusion criteria were (1) primary knee osteoarthritis; (2) age 50–85 years; (3) primary TKA with patelloplasty and circumferential electrocautery denervation; and (4) complete clinical follow-up to 60 months. Exclusion criteria were prior ipsilateral knee surgery, inflammatory arthritis, prior ipsilateral patellar fracture, incomplete records, coronal deformity >10° varus/valgus, flexion contracture or hyperextension >10°, uncontrolled systemic comorbidities, and any intraoperative medial or lateral retinacular release.

Patellar cartilage was graded intraoperatively using the ICRS system. Patients were grouped according to the most severe patellar lesion: ICRS 2 (group A), ICRS 3 (group B), or ICRS 4 (group C).

Surgical technique and postoperative protocol

All procedures were performed by a senior orthopaedic surgeon under spinal or general anesthesia at the attending anesthesiologist’s discretion. After standard preparation and draping, a pneumatic tourniquet (350 mmHg) was applied. A medial parapatellar arthrotomy was used. Bone cuts were performed with intramedullary guidance for the femur and extramedullary guidance for the tibia. A cemented posterior-stabilized prosthesis (NexGen LPS Fixed Bearing Knee; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, Indiana, USA) was used uniformly. Patelloplasty comprised osteophyte excision, smoothing of the articular surface, and reshaping to maintain congruence with the femoral trochlea. Circumpatellar denervation was performed circumferentially with monopolar electrocautery (Valleylab Force FXc; Covidien/Medtronic, Boulder, Colorado, USA) at 40 W, targeting approximately 50% of patellar thickness; no retinacular releases were performed. Postoperatively, a suction drain was removed on day 2. Quadriceps-setting and straight-leg-raise exercises began immediately. Continuous passive motion was initiated on day 2, and weight-bearing was as tolerated. Perioperative pathways (analgesia, thromboprophylaxis, and rehabilitation milestones) were standardized, and key intraoperative and postoperative variables were recorded.

Follow-up schedule and assessments

Follow-up was scheduled at 2 weeks; 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 months; and annually thereafter. Outcomes were assessed at baseline and at 3, 6, 12, 24, and 60 months by trained personnel masked to study hypotheses. Validated patient-reported measures, administered in a fixed order, were Oxford Knee Score (OKS) (0-48, higher = better), visual analog scale (VAS) (0-10, higher = worse), Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Osteoarthritis Index (WOMAC) total (0-96, higher = worse), and Kujala Anterior Knee Pain Scale (AKPS) (0-100, higher = better). Standard anteroposterior, lateral, and skyline radiographs were obtained preoperatively and at follow-up. Outcomes

The primary longitudinal objective was to characterize trajectories in OKS, VAS, WOMAC, and AKPS from 0 to 60 months by ICRS grade (2-4). Secondary objectives were: (1) to determine the proportions achieving minimal clinically important differences (MCIDs), and (2) to quantify grade-based differences at 60 months via model-based contrasts. MCIDs were prespecified as: OKS ≥5 points, VAS improvement ≥1.5 points, WOMAC improvement ≥12 points (0-96), and AKPS ≥10 points [[16], [17], [18]].

Statistical analysis

Longitudinal change was analyzed using population-averaged generalized estimating equations (GEE) with a Gaussian family, identity link, and an exchangeable working correlation, clustering on patient ID. Each model included the baseline value of the same outcome as a covariate, a linear time term (months), group (A/B/C), and a time × group interaction. Estimated marginal means (EMMs) at 60 months were derived for each group, holding the baseline at the cohort mean. Prespecified 60-month contrasts were B vs A and C vs A; results are presented as EMM differences with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and two-sided P values. Cohort-level change (Δ) from baseline to 60 months is summarized as the mean change with standard deviation (SD); 60-month levels are reported as the mean (SD). There were no missing data for baseline covariates and all primary outcome measures at each scheduled follow-up; therefore, no imputation was performed. Using the OKS as the primary 60-month endpoint and assuming a clinically meaningful between-group difference of 5 points (δ = 5) and the observed 60-month SD of 5.32, an equal-group comparison would require 18 knees per group for 80% power at a two-sided α = 0.05 (24 per group with Bonferroni adjustment for 3 pairwise comparisons, α = 0.0167). The smallest ICRS-group comparison in this cohort (ICRS grade 2, n = 26 vs grade 4, n = 42) exceeded these thresholds. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05. Analyses were performed using Stata/MP 18.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA), with GEE and robust standard errors. Note on adjusted group-wise comparisons: adjusted comparisons at 60 months reported below are derived directly from the GEE framework as EMMs and pairwise contrasts, ensuring consistency between longitudinal and 60-month inferences.

Longitudinal trajectories and MCID attainment

Across the cohort, all instruments showed rapid early improvement, followed by stabilization from 12 to 60 months (Fig. 4). From baseline to 60 months, mean changes (Δ) were large and clearly exceeded the prespecified MCID thresholds for each scale: OKS +27.25, VAS −9.79, WOMAC −73.67, and AKPS +68.54 (Table 2). Consistent with these large effect sizes, 100% of knees achieved MCID improvement on all 4 instruments (Wilson 95% CI 97.91-100.00; n = 180), and MCID attainment was uniformly high across ICRS grades 2-4 (Fig. 5).

Unadjusted between-grade comparisons at 60 months

At 60 months, unadjusted comparisons among ICRS grades 2, 3, and 4 showed no statistically significant differences in OKS (P = .191), VAS (P = .544), WOMAC (P = .278), or AKPS (P = .324). Knee range of motion at final follow-up was also similar across grades (data not shown). These unadjusted findings are consistent with the adjusted GEE models and suggest convergence of 5-year outcomes despite baseline differences in patellar cartilage severity.

Adjusted group-wise comparisons at 60 months (estimated marginal means)

EMMs at 60 months and prespecified pairwise contrasts (B vs A, C vs A) quantified grade differences within the GEE framework. For OKS (higher = better), EMMs were 42.08 (group A), 40.27 (group B), and 39.95 (group C); the adjusted differences were −0.82 (95% CI −2.97 to 1.34; P = .46) for B vs A and −0.50 (−3.05 to 2.04; P = .70) for C vs A. For VAS (higher = worse), EMMs were 0.12, 0.12, and 0.21, respectively; B vs A yielded −0.06 (−0.27 to 0.15; P = .60) and C vs A −0.03 (−0.30 to 0.24; P = .82). For WOMAC (higher = worse), EMMs were 6.23, 6.41, and 6.86; B vs A showed +0.34 (−0.95 to 1.63; P = .60) and C vs A +0.73 (−2.04 to 3.50; P = .61).

AKPS (higher = better) trended higher with increasing grade: adjusted differences were +2.88 (−0.85 to 6.60; P = .13) for group B vs group A and +4.35 (0.23-8.47; P = .038) for group C vs group A. These adjusted findings are concordant with the longitudinal trajectories (Fig. 4) and are summarized in Tables 3 and 4.

Complications

Through 60 months, superficial infection occurred in 5 of 180 knees (2.8%) and urinary tract infection in 8 of 180 knees (4.4%). Exact Fisher–Freeman–Halton tests across ICRS groups showed no statistically significant differences (superficial infection, P = .057; urinary tract infection, P = .198). No other major complications requiring reoperation were identified beyond those listed in Tables 5 and 6.

Discussion

A standardized non-resurfacing strategy using CP + PP yielded substantial and durable improvements in OKS, VAS, WOMAC, and AKPS over 5 years. Cohort-level change scores were significant (OKS +27.25; VAS −9.79; WOMAC −73.67; AKPS +68.54), and every knee exceeded prespecified MCID thresholds for all 4 instruments, indicating robust clinical benefit. At 60 months, baseline-adjusted EMMs from GEE models showed only minor, mostly nonsignificant differences across ICRS grades 2-4 for OKS, VAS, and WOMAC, whereas AKPS demonstrated a modest adjusted advantage for group C (ICRS 4) over group A (ICRS 2) (EMM difference +4.35; 95% CI 0.23-8.47), with no significant difference between groups B (ICRS 3) and A. Taken together, these findings suggest that, under a standardized CP + PP protocol, baseline patellar cartilage severity exerts at most a limited influence on 5-year pain and function.

Across the broader literature, comparisons of resurfacing vs retention are mixed but increasingly converge on clinical equivalence in many settings, with meta-analyses and long-term series reporting no consistent superiority of resurfacing for pain relief or global function [19,20]. Any potential advantages must be weighed against resurfacing-specific complications—patellar fracture, aseptic loosening, maltracking/instability, and polyethylene wear—that may offset benefits in unselected patients [21,22]. Within retention pathways, circumpatellar denervation (with or without patelloplasty) has yielded improved anterior knee pain or, at a minimum, outcomes comparable to alternatives in randomized and pooled analyses [[23], [24], [25]]. Contemporary retention protocols that incorporate structured patelloplasty (osteophytectomy, contouring, and articular smoothing) likewise report favorable function and low reoperation rates, with results comparable to resurfacing in matched cohorts [[26], [27], [28]]. Several studies also emphasize that implant and technique determinants—trochlear geometry, component rotation, alignment targets, and soft tissue handling—can be as influential as baseline cartilage status in shaping patellofemoral symptoms and satisfaction [6,8,29]. Finally, grade-stratified reports indicate that advanced structural degeneration does not invariably portend inferior outcomes under optimized retention, challenging the assumption that severe cartilage loss mandates resurfacing [14,28,30]. Our 5-year, grade-specific findings under a uniform CP + PP protocol integrate with this body of evidence, showing durable improvements across ICRS 2-4 and only minor between-grade differences at 60 months. To our knowledge, few studies have reported ICRS grade–stratified, 5-year outcomes after primary non-resurfacing TKA using a uniform CP + PP protocol. Our results, therefore, extend the existing non-resurfacing literature by demonstrating durable improvements across ICRS grades 2-4 using a standardized, reproducible technique.

Mechanistically, CP + PP couples nociceptive denervation with patellar reshaping/osteophytectomy to improve congruence and reduce focal load. The small between-grade differences at 60 months suggest effective symptom modulation despite structural severity when tracking and soft tissue balance are maintained. The AKPS signal favoring ICRS 4 over ICRS 2 may reflect regression to the mean, measurement characteristics of patellofemoral-specific instruments, or a true benefit in patients with greater preoperative disease when denervation and reshaping are applied systematically. Strengths of this study include a standardized surgical technique and rehabilitation pathway, prespecified ICRS-based stratification, and population-averaged modeling with transparent reporting of EMMs and prespecified contrasts.

Limitations

However, should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, this was a retrospective, single-center, nonrandomized cohort, and although consecutive cases were identified, selection bias and residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Second, inclusion required a complete follow-up to 60 months; therefore, the observed outcomes may overestimate results achievable in broader practice, where loss to follow-up is expected. Third, the protocol was performed using a single-implant design and a standardized technique by a senior surgeon, which may limit generalizability and reproducibility in settings with different implants, varying surgeon experience, or rehabilitation workflows. Fourth, the study did not include a comparator group (eg, patelloplasty without denervation, denervation alone, or patellar resurfacing), so the incremental contribution of each component cannot be isolated. Finally, structural assessments of the patellofemoral joint were not protocolized (eg, standardized skyline imaging, advanced imaging, or formal assessment of component rotation and patellar tracking), and the study was not designed or powered as an equivalence/noninferiority comparison vs resurfacing.

Clinically, for surgeons favoring or requiring a non-resurfacing strategy, CP + PP appears to deliver durable 5-year improvements across ICRS 2–4 with only minor grade-related differences. Reproducibility hinges on explicit operative steps (osteophyte removal, articular smoothing, congruent reshaping, and circumferential denervation) and standardized rehabilitation emphasizing early quadriceps activation and range of motion milestones. Incorporating patellofemoral-specific metrics (eg, AKPS) into routine follow-up may refine counseling and help identify outliers who could benefit from targeted rehabilitation or secondary intervention. These steps may help improve the protocol's transferability across surgeons and institutions.

Future directions

Prospective, adequately powered comparative studies should test CP + PP vs resurfacing and isolate the incremental contribution of patelloplasty by comparing circumpatellar denervation alone (CP) vs combined CP + PP. Trials should prespecify patellofemoral-focused outcomes (AKPS and validated anterior knee pain measures), include standardized imaging such as skyline views and computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging as indicated, and quantify mechanical factors (component rotation, trochlear angle, patellar tilt/lateralization). Longer follow-up beyond 5 years will clarify late trajectories and whether any divergence emerges by grade or alignment philosophy. Stratified analyses by trochlear design and rehabilitation intensity may identify subgroups deriving maximal benefit from denervation-based strategies.

Conclusions

Using a standardized non-resurfacing protocol (CP + PP), primary TKA yielded durable 5-year improvements in pain and function across ICRS grades 2–4, with high MCID attainment and low complication rates. At 60 months, between-grade differences were small and nonsignificant; AKPS favored grade 4 vs 2. CP + PP is reproducible and merits multicenter trials to refine selection and long-term value.
