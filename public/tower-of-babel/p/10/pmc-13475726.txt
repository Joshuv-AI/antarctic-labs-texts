Cannabis Is Associated With Potentially Proatherogenic Inflammation in Youth
Authors: Theodoros Kelesidis, Leila Fotooh Abadi, Pratik Lama Tamang, Katherine Hampilos, Reece Fong, Joshua Sanchez, Isabelle Ruedisueli, Jeffrey Gornbein, Ziva D. Cooper, Holly R. Middlekauff
Published: 2026

Editorial, see Article by Giannis

Legalization of cannabis use in the United States has contributed to the perception that cannabis is safe. However, it remains unclear whether cannabis increases cardiovascular risk. Large studies have led to inconsistent results due to confounding factors, such as tobacco cigarette smoking among cannabis users.1 Cannabis has opposing effects on inflammation mediated by different cannabinoid (CB) receptors.2 CB-1 receptors exert pro-oxidative and proinflammatory effects, whereas CB-2 receptors have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. The overall net effect and its implications for the development of inflammatory atherosclerosis in people who regularly use combusted cannabis (CAN) remain uncertain. To address this knowledge gap, we have established a mechanistic ex vivo model of vascular inflammation that includes 2 early events: monocyte transendothelial migration (MTEM) into the subendothelial space at sites of endothelial activation and monocyte-derived foam cell formation (MDFCF).3

Using this ex vivo assay, flow cytometry, and primary plasma and monocytes from 3 groups of otherwise healthy young people (aged 21–30 years), including people who (1) chronically (>1-year) use CAN (n=59), (2) chronically co-use both CAN and nicotine electronic cigarettes (CAN-nECIG, n=26), and (3) are nonusing controls (n=49), we tested the hypothesis that chronic CAN use increases 2 early steps in atherogenesis, MTEM and MDFCF, and these changes are further increased in the co-use CAN-nECIG group. The experimental protocol was approved by the institutional review board at the University of California, Los Angeles, and written informed consent was obtained from each participant.

As reported previously,4 groups did not differ significantly in sex, age, race/ethnicity, or hemodynamics. Cannabis use burden was similar between exclusive CAN and CAN-nECIG groups.

As shown in the Figure, both MTEM (Figure [A and B]) and MDFCF (Figure [C through E]) were increased in the CAN group, and these early steps in atherogenesis were further increased in the CAN-nECIG group versus the CAN group.

Plasma from study participants, including people who (1) chronically (>1-year) use combusted cannabis (cannabis; n=59), (2) chronically co-use both combusted cannabis and nicotine electronic cigarettes (CAN-nECIG; n=26), and (3) are nonusing controls (n=49), and autologous peripheral blood mononuclar cells (PBMCs) were used in our ex vivo model of atherogenesis. PBMCs were added to umbilical vein endothelial cell monolayers on collagen gels to transmigrate for 1 hour in the presence of 20% v/v plasma and form foam cells over 24 hours. Flow cytometry was used to measure the number of CD45+ CD11b+ macrophages that had undergone transendothelial migration within the collagen gel (reflecting monocyte transendothelial migration [MTEM]) and the fluorescence of BODIPY (% bright positive cells and median fluorescence intensity [MFI]) as measures of monocyte-derived foam cell formation (MDFCF). To further explore our hypothesis that potentially proatherogenic inflammatory effects of cannabis are mediated through systemic effects on oxidative stress, we determined the associations between cellular oxidative stress (median fluorescence intensity of the fluorochrome CELLROX in CD14+ cells), with the MTEM and MDFCF. Compared with controls, the CAN group had higher CELLROX median fluorescence intensity in CD14 cells, and this effect was amplified in the CAN-nECIG group (Figure [E]). Furthermore, higher cellular oxidative stress levels were strongly associated with increased foam cell formation (Figure [F]), a process in which lipid oxidation is critical.

We provide, to our knowledge, among the first ex vivo evidence in humans that chronic CAN use in a real-world exposure context is associated with potentially proatherogenic inflammatory activation that may contribute to long-term cardiovascular risk. We demonstrated that (1) MTEM and MDFCF are increased in the CAN group, (2) these early steps in atherogenesis are further increased in the CAN-nECIG group compared with the CAN group, and (3) increased cellular oxidative stress is strongly linked with MDFCF but not MTEM in the CAN group. This study adds to the growing evidence that chronic cannabis smoking is associated with proatherogenic changes that may lead to inflammatory atherosclerosis.

Mohammadi et al5 recently reported that healthy, nonsmoking adults aged <50 years who chronically use combusted or oral tetrahydrocannabinol-dominant cannabis exhibit significant endothelial dysfunction as estimated by brachial-artery flow-mediated dilation compared with noncannabis users. Abnormal flow-mediated dilation, driven by endothelial dysfunction, is one of the earliest signs of future atherosclerosis and has been linked to the development of adverse cardiovascular events. Our findings show that monocytes from otherwise healthy young people who regularly use CAN have increased atherogenic properties, including higher migration into the subendothelial space and greater foam cell formation, which correlate with increased oxidative stress and further support this phenotypic evidence of cannabis-induced vascular dysfunction.

The strengths of our study include the inclusion of a healthy, young, and well-characterized population and the use of our novel mechanistic model of atherogenesis. We acknowledge limitations. This was a cross-sectional study, and associations do not confirm causality. Cannabis and nECIG use was self-reported and susceptible to recall bias. Our focus was on individuals who used CAN at least weekly for the past year although non-CAN use was permitted. While most cannabis users reportedly use >1 form, future research comparing individuals who exclusively use a single form, such as edibles, would be valuable. Last, the toxins and constituents found in smoke generated from cannabis and tobacco overlap, except for the cannabinoid and nicotine constituents. Our study was underpowered to detect any sex differences in the measured outcomes. Future acute exposure studies involving research on cannabis cigarettes with 0% delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol, followed by known levels of tetrahydrocannabinol, may help clarify the roles of tetrahydrocannabinol versus nontetrahydrocannabinol constituents in cannabis smoke as mediators of proatherogenic effects. Many of these limitations present opportunities for future investigation.
