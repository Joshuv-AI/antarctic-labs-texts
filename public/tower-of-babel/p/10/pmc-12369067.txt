Predictors of trachoma among children aged 1–9 years in Ethiopia: a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shambel Dessale Asmamaw, Birhan Alemnew
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTrachoma is one of the leading chronic diseases in children 1–9 years old and a cause of death primary in resources limited countries. Even though unlimited efforts have been undertaken to control the disease, it remains a major public health challenge in developing nations. In this comprehensive meta-analysis, we aimed to evaluate the pooled estimate and the factors associated with trachoma among 1–9 years old children.MethodsPotential research articles were retrieved from PubMed/Medline, SCOPUS, EMBASE, and electronic databases. Following PRISMA guidelines, a total of 38 observational studies were included in this study. The pooled single estimate of prevalence and odds ratio (OR) was calculated for each predictor. The data was analyzed using STATA version 14. A random effect model was used if the heterogeneity (I2) was greater than 50% and a p value less than 0.05 was considered a significance level.ResultsIn this meta-analysis, we evaluated a total of 218,002 children aged 1–9 years and found estimate of trachoma prevalence of 20.49% (95% CI: 17.49, 23.49%). We identified several predictors associated with trachoma infection. These include: lack of access to piped drinking water (OR = 3.36, 95%Cl 2.42, 4.65), absence of household latrine (OR = 3.3, 95%Cl 2.35, 4.64), failure to wash face with soap (OR = 2.66, 95%Cl 2.0, 3.54), absence of waste disposal (OR = 2.57, 95%Cl 1.74, 3.79), and taking more than 30 min to fetch water (OR = 3.24, 95%Cl 1.85, 5.67). Furthermore, the presence of animal pens in the living area (OR = 3.28, 95%Cl 2.4, 4.47), inadequate knowledge of caregivers/mothers (OR = 3.56, 95%Cl 2.36, 5.36), family size greater than five (OR = 2.86, 95%Cl 1.39, 5.89), face washing < two times per day (OR = 3.09, 95%Cl 2.05, 4.67), presence of flies on the face (OR = 3.31, 95%Cl 2.09, 5.24) were also identified as significant predictors of trachoma.ConclusionIn overall, the present study revealed that the burden of trachoma among children was aged 1-9 years in Ethiopia 20.49%. The predictors associated with trachoma among children includes absence of household latrines, lack of access to piped drinking water, failure to wash face with soap, taking more than 30 min to fetch water, presence of animal pens in the living compound, inadequate knowledge of caregivers/mothers, family size exceeding five, infrequent face washing, the presence of flies on children’s faces, and insufficient waste disposal. Our findings suggest that, in addition to existing preventative strategies, there is an urgent need for improved personal and environmental hygiene practices, as well as addressing these factors.Keywords: Children, Ethiopia, Meta-analysis, Predictors, Trachoma

Introduction

Trachoma is a debilitating eye disease caused by persist and recurring infections of Chlamydia trachomatis. While the disease affects children globally, its impact is particularly pronounced in resource-limited settings [1–3], which are characterized by repeated infections, inflammation, and scarring can lead to chronic conjunctivitis and worse permanent damage [4].

The transmission of the disease is driven by a combination of various environmental factors, economical, behaviors, sanitation, climate conditions, agricultural practices, livelihoods, farming activities, high-fly densities, hygiene practices, and overcrowded [5–7].

To reduce the burden of trachoma transmission, the World Health Organization (WHO) endorsed the SAFE strategy, a comprehensive approach comprising Surgery, Antibiotic distribution, Face washing, and Environmental improvement [1, 8–10]. This strategy addresses various aspects of trachoma, including health promotion, prevention, treatment, and elimination, aimed at enhancing treatment accessibility and fostering better hygiene practices within affected communities. Furthermore, WHO recommends mass antibiotic distributions where trachoma prevalence exceeds 10% in a population [11, 12].

The burden of trachoma remains significantly high in Ethiopia, which accounts for substantial proportion of the global trachoma cases. WHO was initiated road map (2021–2030) emphasizes the transition of non-communicable disease data into national health information systems to establish country-led, integrated programs [13–15].

While there has been notable progress, trachoma remains a persistent public health challenge in Ethiopia. Studies on the prevalence of trachoma among children have been studied in various regions of Ethiopia, revealing inconsistency in findings (it ranges from 4.3 to 52.4% [16, 17]). Additionally, over the past decades, Ethiopia has faced disasters, including drought, instability, and COVID-19 pandemic, which have further hindered efforts to control the disease. This comprehensive meta-analysis summarized the pooled prevalence of trachoma and identified the main predictors associated with trachoma using existing studies.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This study focused on trachoma among 1–9 years old children in Ethiopia. The protocol of this review was registered and assigned an identification number in PROSPERO (CRD42019141117). The study follows Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guideline [18].

Search strategies

Electronic databases were utilized to identify relevant studies from SCOPUS, EMBASE, PubMed/MEDLINE, and others. These databases were systematically searched for pertinent literature using Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and Boolean operators with keywords such as determinants, associated factors, risk factors, trachoma, trachomatous trichiasis, trichiasis, Chlamydia trachomatis infection, burden, epidemiology, survey, assessment, prevalence, and Ethiopia. Only articles conducted in Ethiopia and published in English language were included.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Observational studies published between 2014 and December 14, 2024, that reported the factors associated with trachoma and/or the prevalence of trachoma among 1–9 years old children in Ethiopia were included. Conversely, studies such as reviews, meta-analysis, policy statements, case reports, and qualitative research were excluded in the present study.

Study selection and quality assessment

Potential studies were retrieved from those electronic databases and imported into Endnote version 21. After importing, duplicate studies were removed. Subsequently, titles and abstracts of the studies were reviewed. Following, full text reading was conducted based on pre-set inclusion and exclusion criteria. Furthermore, the quality of each individual study was assessed using Newcastle-Ottawa Scale [19].

Data extraction

Standardized data extraction tools were developed using Microsoft Excel 2016, which included the following information: surname of the author, publication year, study area, study period, design of the study, total sample size included in the analysis, number of cases, and odd ratio with 95% CI of each predictor. Consequently, data were extracted from each original study. Both authors (SDA and BA) independently undertook literature search, data extraction, selection, and quality assessment, as well as resolved any discrepancies through discussion.

Operational definition

Active trachoma is defined as the presence of at least one of the two signs of active trachoma, either Trachomatis Inflammation follicular (TF) or Trachomatis inflammation intense (TI) in at least one eye. Active trachoma is an inflammatory process that involves the tarsal conjunctiva and tarsal plate, which are seen on eversion of the upper eyelid. TF is characterized by the presence of five or more follicles while TI is more pronounced in inflammatory thickening of the upper conjunctiva [20, 21].

Data analysis

The extracted data from Excel was transferred to STATA version 14 for analysis. The pooled single estimate of the odds ratio for each predictor was then calculated. Additionally, the overall prevalence of trachoma was determined along with its 95% CI. If the heterogeneity (I2) exceeded 50%, subgroup analysis was conducted to tease out the possibility of heterogeneities [22]. Furthermore, publication bias was assessed using Egger’s test, with a p-value of 0.05 considered as the threshold for significance [23].

Results

Characteristics of individual studies

Out of the 1,242 articles gathered from various sources, 258 studies were removed due to duplication. Additionally, 727 studies were excluded because their titles and abstracts did not align with the study objectives. Subsequently, 257 full-text articles were meticulously reviewed based on specific criteria, with only 38 studies selected for the final analysis of the present study, as depicted in Fig. 1.

Pooled prevalence of trachoma among children aged 1–9 years

In this meta-analysis, a total of 218,002 participants were included. Out of thirty-eight included studies, only thirty-five studies report the prevalence of trachoma among children. We found that the overall prevalence of trachoma among children aged 1–9 years in Ethiopia was 20.49% (95% CI: 17.49, 23.49) (as depicted in Fig. 2a). The Egger’s test indicated no publication bias (p = 0.37). The funnel plot also showed symmetry, reinforcing the absence of publication bias (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, the overall prevalence of TF and TI among children aged 1–9 years in Ethiopia was 17.93% (95% CI: 14.51, 21.34) and 3.44% (95% CI: 2.45, 4.42), respectively (See Fig. 2c and d).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis was conducted to tease out sources of heterogeneity by considering geographical area, sample size, and publication year. The highest prevalence was noted in the Amhara region, which was 52.4% (95% CI: 48.41, 56.39), followed by the Somalia region which was, 47.7% (95% CI: 42.66, 52.74). On the other hand, the prevalence of trachoma in studies with less than 600 participants was 22.84% (95% CI: 16.76, 28.93), while in studies with a sample size greater than and equal to 600; the prevalence was 19.32% (95% CI 15.64, 22.99). In addition, the subgroup analysis based on the year of publication showed that a prevalence of trachoma was 21.70% (CI 17.55, 25.85) for studies conducted prior to 2018 and 19.34% (95% CI 14.92, 23.76) for studies conducted after 2018 as shown (Table 2).

Demographic factors

Firstly, we examined the association sex and residence of the children with occurrence of trachoma using data from eight studies [17, 33, 34, 41, 43, 47, 50, 57] and three studies [30, 32,56], respectively. We found no significant difference in trachoma prevalence based on sex (OR = 1.07, 95% CI 0.54, 2.11) or child’s residence (OR = 0.71, 95% CI 0.49, 1.03) (Fig. 3a and b). However, Children from families with six or more members were 2.86 times more likely to have had trachoma compared to those from families with less than six family members [16, 44–46, 53] (Fig. 3c). Similarly, inadequate knowledge of the child’s caregiver/mother was also associated with trachoma (OR = 3.56, 95% CI: 2.36, 5.36) [26, 41, 44, 46, 54, 58, 59] (Fig. 3d).

Hygiene and water access

In our present study, we reviewed hygiene practices and water access related factors associated with trachoma, including the utilization of soap, frequency of face washing, flies on faces, and access to water. Our analysis revealed that children who did not use soap to wash their faces were 2.66 times more likely to develop trachoma compared to their counterparts (OR = 2.66, 95% CI: 2.00, 3.54) [17, 26, 28–30, 32–34, 39–41, 44–46, 49–51, 56, 59] (Fig. 3e). Furthermore, children who washed their face less than twice per day were also at increased risk for trachoma (OR = 3.09, 95% CI 2.05, 4.67) [17, 28, 44, 45, 47, 49, 53, 59] (Fig. 3f). Likewise, the presence of flies on their faces was linked with a 3.31 times higher likelihood of having trachoma than in children without flies [26, 28, 30, 47, 49, 56, 59] (Fig. 3g).

On the other hand, we also evaluated the association between access and availability of clean drinking water with trachoma among children. The pooled results from the available studies indicated that children living in households lacking access piped drinking water had a 3.36-fold increase in acquiring trachoma (OR = 3.36, 95% CI: 2.42, 4.65) [17, 44, 50, 53, 56] (Fig. 3h). Similarly, distance to obtain water for households who had to travel more than 30 min to fetch water was found to be three times more likely (OR = 3.24, 95% CI 1.85, 5.67) of developing trachoma as compared to the counterpart [29, 50, 53, 54] (Fig. 3i).

Environmental and sanitation factors

In this study, it was summarized that children from households without access to latrine facilities had 3.3 times higher odds of developing trachoma compared to those with access to a latrine (OR = 3.3, 95% CI: 2.35, 4.64) [16, 17, 26, 29, 30, 34, 40, 41, 44–46, 50, 51, 53, 55, 58, 59] (Fig. 3j). Additionally, the absence of waste disposal facilities in children’s households was associated with a 2.57- fold increase in the likelihood of trachoma compared to those who had the facilities (OR = 2.57, 95% CI: 1.74, 3.79) [17, 29, 30, 34, 44, 45, 51, 53, 56, 58, 59] (Fig. 3k). Lastly, children residing in households with animal pens in their living area were 3.28 times more likely to acquire trachoma compared to their counterparts (OR = 3.28, 95% CI 2.40, 4.47) [29, 30, 33, 41, 46, 59] (Fig. 3l).

Discussion

Trachoma remains endemic in resource-limited countries such as Ethiopia. Although it is a preventable disease, if left untreated, it can lead to irreversible blindness. This meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of trachoma and identify its associated predictors. Consequently, we found that the pooled prevalence of trachoma among 1–9 year old children was 20.39%, four times higher than the WHO trachoma elimination target < 5% by 2020 [60]. However, the present finding is relatively lower than reported in previous meta-analysis conducted by Gebrie A et al., which estimated prevalence of 26.9% [61]. Despite numerous trachoma prevention and elimination programs carried out over the past several years in collaboration with various stakeholders in Ethiopia, the disease continues to pose a significant public health challenges across multiple regions. The country remains one of those with the highest burden of trachoma globally. This persistent prevalence may be attributed to multiple factors such as lack of access to healthcare services, infrastructure, and improper adoption of SAFE measures.

According to the national data report, which revealed that one in every five households still utilizes open defecation throughout the country [62]. In addition to this, 60–80% of health problems in the country are attributed to unsafe water supplies and unhygienic and unsanitary waste disposal [63]. On top of the prevalence of trachoma, this study identified several predictors associated with the disease. We addressed sociodemographic characteristics, environmental factors, hygiene practices, and access and utilization of key facilities related to factors. Accordingly, we found that Children living in households without access to latrine facilities were 3.3 times more likely to had trachoma than those living in households with latrine facilities. This finding was consistent with a study conducted in Nigeria [64] and Ethiopia [65]. It is known that the existence of open defecation facilitates the breeding of eye-seeking flies (Musca sorbens), which serve as a reservoir for the bacterium C. trachomatis [66]. Conversely, proper use and access of latrine facilities helps to limit the presence and the breading of flies in near dwelling environment, thereby reducing the risk of trachoma infections [65].

Our finding also revealed that children who did not use soap while washing their faces were 2.74 times more likely to develop trachoma compared to those who used soap. This finding aligns with previous studies conducted in Mali [67], Ethiopia [68], and Malawi [69]. This might be washing with soap helps to reduce ocular and facial secretions, thereby limiting the proliferation and attraction of the flies [65, 70]. In agreement with previous reports [23, 71]. In addition, frequent face washing was identified as a separate risk factor for trachoma in children. Children who washed their faces less than twice a day were 2.66 times more likely to have trachoma as compared to those who had frequent practices. Indeed, frequent face washing has reduced the attraction of flies and minimizes the direct spread of the bacteria from eye to eye during close contact. On the other hand, the presence of flies on children’s faces was strongly associated with trachoma infection.

Children with facial flies were 3.31 times more likely to get trachoma infection than those without. A similar research finding has shown that [52, 72]. The fact that eye seeking flies can carry C.trachomatis on their legs and in the contents of their proboscis and facilitating mechanical transmission of the disease [71].

In addition to washing practices, children whose household caregivers/mothers who had poor knowledge about trachoma were approximately 3.56 times more likely to have trachoma compared to their counterparts. This finding was consistent with the previous evidences documented elsewhere [3]. The possible explanation is that a child’s caregiver/mother who had adequate knowledge about the disease would better practice SAFE strategy and prevention of trachoma infection.

The results of this review showed that trachoma infection was linked to the lack of proper waste disposal. Children living in households without waste disposal systems were 2.57 more likely than those who had adequate waste management. Similar reported studies were carried out in Nigeria [64] and Ethiopia [52]. Additionally, children from households that required more than half an hour to fetch water for household use were 3.24 times more likely to be infected with trachoma compared to those who had the easier access. This report was in line with the previous report [73]. It has been known that access to safe water in Ethiopia has been an other public health challenge, noticed more a pronounced problem in several rural areas. Equally, protecting personal and environmental hygiene is key for reduction of trachoma [74]. However, lack of safe drinking water and access to water facilities in the living areas in Ethiopia has long been a public health concern, particularly in many rural areas where nearby water supplies are often unavailable. This scarcity significantly hinders the ability to maintain proper hygiene practices on top of daily consumption needs. Although animals are not directing a vector for C.trachomatis, infection [71], the presence of animal pens within household compounds was associated with a 3.28- fold increase in the likelihood of trachoma. This might be attributed to accumulations of animal settlement, excreta or garbage and droppings, which create favorable breeding conditions and attraction of flies.

Consistent with previous finding [61], this study found that children living in households with more than five family members were 2.86 times more likely to develop trachoma compared to those in smaller households. Living in an overcrowded environment may elevated the risk and transmission of trachoma infections by limiting access to essential facilities and services. Likewise, it might be difficult to practice proper personal and environmental hygiene, thereby increasing the risk of trachoma infection.

Limitations of the study

All studies included in this analysis were observational, one case control, and the rest of the others were cross-sectional. Consequently, the outcome variable could have been influenced by various confounding variables, making it challenging to establish causal relationships. Even though subgroup analysis were conducted to identify the source heterogeneity, substantial heterogeneity was still observed in this study.

Conclusion

Overall, this meta-analysis revealed that the pooled prevalence of trachoma infections among children aged 1-9 years in Ethiopia remains significantly higher than the threshold set by WHO. In addition, a range of trachoma predictors were identified, including demographic, personal hygiene practices, sanitation and waste disposal. Subsequently, absence of piped drinking water, lack of latrine facilities, the presence of animal pens near dwellings, poor waste disposal practices, time taken to fetch water, and poor face washing practices were identified as key predictors for trachoma. It is crucial for all stakeholders and collaborators to effectively address these risk factors through implementing beyond the current trachoma prevention and elimination strategies. This includes, strengthening healthcare access and facilities, increasing resources allocation, capacity building, and fostering multi-sectorial to effectively tackle these risk factors.
