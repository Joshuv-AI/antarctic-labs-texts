From waste to intelligent biochar nanocomposites: unifying advanced oxidation processes, structure–reactivity-guided photocatalysis, and data-driven design for pesticide degradation
Authors: Muhammad Idrees, Zia Ul Haq Khan, Ibrahim A. Alsayer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pesticide residues remain a major threat to ecosystems and food security, warranting novel remediation strategies that extend beyond conventional approaches. Biochar-based photocatalytic nanocomposites serve as a transformative platform, repurposing waste biomass into high-performance catalysts that combine waste valorization, exceptional charge dynamics and tunable reactivity. This review integrates innovations in advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), including hydroxyl radical pathways, UV/H2O2 systems, and Fenton, photo-Fenton and ozone-based strategies, with biochar-supported metal oxide photocatalysts (TiO2@biochar, CuO@biochar, FeOx@biochar, and ZnO@biochar), redox-tunable hybrids, nano zero-valent metals and single-atom catalysts. We discuss pesticide structure–reactivity frameworks that guide degradation efficiency and highlight data-driven predictive modeling approaches as a paradigm shift for the rational nanocomposite design. By linking mechanistic insights with computational intelligence, this perspective lays the groundwork for scalable and sustainable pesticide remediation.

Introduction

Pesticides underpin global agriculture production, yet their extensive use results in persistent environmental and ecological contamination, with over 3.7 teragrams of pesticides used annually across agricultural systems worldwide.1,2 Pesticide contamination occurs mainly from the run-off of agricultural fields or spray drifts,3,4 with the detailed pathways shown in Fig. 1. This figure represents the interconnected pathways of environmental contamination arising from pesticide use, which directly leads to pollution of the air, water, and soil. Additionally, soil further amplifies the contamination of the other two media through cross-compartmental transport processes. Recent analyses have confirmed the presence of various pesticides in water, soil, and the food chain, often at concentrations above the quantification limits.5,6 Sixty-two percent of rice from Pakistan and 85% harvested fruits, pulses and leafy vegetables in Western countries contain pesticide residues. In addition, 2–4% of domestic foods in New Zealand (NZ), China (CN), the European Union (EU), and the USA contain residues exceeding the legal safety limits.1,7 These micropollutants drive ecological disruption, bioaccumulation in non-target species, and endocrine disruption in aquatic life and pose emerging risks to human health, including neurodevelopmental effects.8,9 Thus, addressing pesticide pollution is a key challenge in the development of sustainable agricultural and water management systems. However, conventional remediation methods, such as activated carbon adsorption and biological treatment, face challenges in removing recalcitrant pesticides, as they either produce secondary waste or are hindered by matrix inhibition.10–12 These limitations have led to the development of advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) based on the in situ production of highly reactive oxygen species (ROS). These ROS oxidize persistent pesticides, enabling their degradation into less harmful products. However, their widespread applicability is limited due to challenges such as high energy demands, insufficient catalytic stability and incomplete mineralization pathways.13–15 Overcoming these limitations requires the development of new catalysts that are efficient, environmentally benign, cost-effective and compatible with circular economy principles. As oxidation technologies develop, carbon materials are increasingly utilized as possible candidates for the development of effective environmental remediation applications. Among them, biochar, a carbon-rich material produced via the thermochemical conversion of waste biomass with limited oxygen access, has become widely studied as a promising candidate.15,16

Pathways for pesticide contamination in the environment. After application, pesticides disseminate into the air (air pollution), penetrate the ground (soil pollution), and reach aquatic systems through runoff and leaching.

Biochar has been demonstrated to be a chemically versatile carbon-based support possessing a high surface area, porosity, diverse functional groups, and excellent adsorption affinity. When combined with semiconductor nanomaterials (NMs), it efficiently enhances their photocatalytic performance by acting as an adsorbent, electron mediator, charge separator and structural scaffold.17–21 In recent years, biochar-based photocatalysts (such as TiO2@biochar and ZnO@biochar) have been widely applied for the degradation of pesticides.22,23 These composites are impressively expanding the functional scope of biochar-based photocatalysts by boosting charge separation, visible-light harvesting, and interface reactivity. A promising new direction in this area has been the transition from empirical biochar-based catalyst development to mechanistically motivated and predictive design. In addition to the photocatalyst properties, substrate-specific characteristics such as molecular structure, functional groups, electron distribution and oxidative susceptibility also determine pesticide degradation performance. As such, structure–reactivity relationships are utilized to rationalize degradation behavior and guide design principles for catalyst optimization. Quantitative structure–property relations arising from recent mechanistic insights offer avenues for the predictive design of biochar-based catalysts. Meanwhile, data-driven approaches and predictive modelling are starting to reimagine the way that biochar nanocomposites are conceptualized, screened and optimized, providing pathways for accelerating catalyst discovery and enhancing performance prediction. Recent review articles have extensively discussed biochar-supported photocatalysts, advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), and nanocomposite-based remediation systems for environmental applications. However, most reviews primarily focus on experimental degradation efficiencies and material synthesis, while providing limited mechanistic interpretation of structure–reactivity relationships, interfacial charge-transfer phenomena, and operational parameter dependencies. Furthermore, the emerging role of machine learning (ML) and predictive modeling in photocatalytic catalyst optimization remains underexplored in the context of pesticide degradation. Accordingly, the present review introduces a unified mechanistic-to-predictive framework for intelligent biochar nanocomposites in pesticide remediation. Unlike previous reviews that predominantly summarize degradation studies, this work critically correlates catalyst physicochemical properties, including conductivity, graphitization degree, porosity, surface functionality, and heterojunction engineering, with ROS generation pathways, charge separation efficiency, degradation kinetics, and mineralization performance. In addition, this review comprehensively evaluates the synergistic integration of advanced oxidation processes, semiconductor photocatalysis, and biochar-mediated interfacial electron transport while highlighting emerging machine-learning approaches for catalyst screening, process optimization, and predictive degradation modeling. Practical considerations including catalyst stability, regeneration, metal leaching, reactor scalability, and techno-economic feasibility are also critically discussed to provide future design directions for sustainable water remediation technologies.

Classification, origin, and environmental impacts of pesticides

Pesticides are classified based on target organisms, chemical composition, physical properties, mode of action, and physical properties. Based on the target, they are categorized as insecticides, herbicides, fungicides, rodenticides, and algicides (Fig. 2a).35,36 Chemically, they are divided into organic (organochlorines, organophosphates, carbamates, and pyrethroids) and inorganic compounds (Fig. 2b).37 Furthermore, the emerging class of biopesticides, derived from natural sources such as plants, bacteria, and fungi, is gaining attention as an environmentally friendly alternative and is further classified into microbial pesticides, biochemical pesticides, and plant-incorporated protectants (Fig. 2c).38 The mechanisms of action (e.g., neurotoxins, photosynthesis inhibitors, and membrane disruptors) illustrate the wide diversity of pesticide types and toxic mechanisms (Fig. 2d).

Classification of pesticides based on (a) targets, (b) chemical nature, (c) sources, (d) modes of action, and (e) impacts on human health, the environment, and agriculture.

The use of pesticides has undergone three key historical phases, including the natural pesticide era, in which ancient civilizations utilized organic compounds such as sulfur compounds and organometallics (arsenic and mercury compounds), mainly derived from natural sources such as plants and minerals;39 the inorganic chemical era (19th century), in which copper and sulfur were extensively applied against fungal diseases in crops;40 and the synthetic pesticide era, which began during World War II with the development of synthetic pesticides such as DDT, BHC, aldrin and dieldrin.41 These compounds promoted agricultural productivity but also introduced significant negative environmental and health risks.42

Pesticides negatively impact both target and non-target organisms.43 For instance, in aquatic environments, they reduce dissolved oxygen (DO) levels, disturb metabolic functions, induce oxidative stress, and cause harmful changes in the physiology of aquatic organisms, as illustrated in Fig. 2e.44 These impacts eventually result in biodiversity loss and imbalance in the ecosystem. In humans, exposure to pesticide-contaminated water causes cancer, genetic mutations, disruption of endocrine system, neurological disorders, respiratory, and skin diseases (Fig. 2e).45 Moreover, pesticides may inhibit photosynthesis and electron-transport mechanisms, thereby affecting crop physiology and indicating poor crop health (Fig. 2e).43

Pesticide degradation is highly sensitive to electron-rich aromatic rings, halogen substituents, heteroatoms, and phosphorus-containing functional groups from a photocatalytic perspective as these are determining factors for susceptibility toward reactive oxygen species (ROS) attack. For example, organochlorine pesticides are generally more persistent due to the strong C–Cl bond, while organophosphates and carbamates are much more susceptible to oxidative degradation.46 Structure–reactivity relationships have been used to explain degradation kinetics and, more commonly, guide catalyst selection.47 The structural persistence and toxicity profiles of these pesticide classes significantly influence their environmental fate and degradation behavior. As such, appropriate remediation strategies need to be applicable over a wide spectrum of molecular structures and physiochemical properties. In this context, nanotechnology has emerged as a promising platform for enhancing the efficiency of pesticide removal and degradation.

Role of nanotechnology in pesticide degradation

Nanotechnology has emerged as a prominent approach for addressing environmental pesticide pollution. Nanomaterials (NMs) generally range between 1 and 100 nanometers in at least one dimension. They exhibit characteristic properties, such as high surface-to-volume ratio, enhanced reactivity, and tunable surface functionality.48,49 These characteristics enable nanomaterials to behave as efficient catalysts, adsorbents, and reactive agents in the degradation of pesticides.50 Nanotechnology has broadly evolved across various application fields, such as nano-pesticides, nano-sensors, nano-fertilizers, and nanocarriers for controlled release systems.51,52 Nanomaterials including carbon nanotubes (CNTs), graphene, biochar, and metal oxides (e.g., TiO2, ZnO, MnO2, CuO, WO3, MgO, and Fe3O4) have shown great potential in the field of environmental remediation.53,54 Among them, carbon-based NMs have attracted considerable attention due to their high adsorption capability, porosity, chemical stability, and relatively low toxicity. In contrast, metal oxide nanoparticles (NPs) possess high catalytic activity, and low solubility, while generating minimum secondary pollutants after degradation of targeted pollutants.55,56 Nanomaterials can be synthesized through two different approaches, namely, top-down and bottom-up (Fig. 3a). Their applications primarily include water treatment mechanisms such as filtration (physical separation using membranes/adsorbents), chemical reactions (oxidation, reduction, and catalysis leading to less toxic byproducts such as CO2 and H2O), and the removal of contaminants (e.g., pesticides, heavy metals, drugs, and dyes) from polluted water sources (Fig. 3b). As shown in Fig. 3, the synthesis route governs nanomaterial structure and surface properties, which in turn determine the dominant pollutant-removal mechanism, hence establishing a direct link between material design and performance in pesticide degradation.

Schematic of (a) two primary approaches for nanomaterial synthesis: top-down and bottom-up and (b) fundamental mechanisms in water purification: physical filtration, adsorption, chemical transformation, and catalysis.

Nanomaterials provide high surface area, tunable functions and catalytic activity; however, their environmental remediation performance is often maximized after coupling with advanced oxidation processes. The reactive oxygen species produced in these processes are powerful enough to decompose persistent pesticide molecules, thus establishing a synergistic basis for pollutant removal.

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) for pesticide degradation

Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) have gained a lot of attention among emerging wastewater treatment technologies due to their high removal efficiency in removing POPs. AOPs involve the in situ generation of reactive organic species (ROS), particularly hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), and superoxide anion radicals (O2˙−), which exhibit strong oxidation potential (OP), degrading a wide range of pollutants non-selectively.57 AOPs include photocatalysis, ozonation, Fenton and photo-Fenton-type reactions, sonochemical processes, and electrochemical oxidation.58 These processes depend on photochemical, chemical, electrochemical, or sonochemical pathways for radical generation.59 ROS are highly reactive transient species that oxidize organic contaminants in to simpler and less harmful degradation products, such as CO2, water, and inorganic salts.60,61 However, in some cases, intermediate compounds including organic acids and ketones may form, requiring further advanced treatment for complete mineralization. Among AOPs, photocatalytic and photo-Fenton processes are especially effective for treatment of tertiary waste water effluents because of their capability to degrade persistent and non-biodegradable pollutants.62 Overall, incorporating nanotechnology with AOPs provides a potential and viable framework for the efficient degradation of pesticides in wastewater.13 Some advanced oxidation processes for the degradation of pesticides from wastewater, such as photocatalysis, ozonolysis, photolysis, chemical, sonochemical, and electrochemical AOPs, are summarized in Fig. 4. As shown in Fig. 4, there are various activation methods, but regardless of the reactor type, AOPs are consistently characterized by the generation and utilization of highly reactive oxygen species (especially ˙OH and O2˙−) as the key mechanistic pathways governing pesticide degradation and mineralization.

Hydroxyl radical-based AOPs

Hydroxyl radical-based AOPs are some of the most widely studied processes for degradation, which primarily rely on the generation of hydroxyl radicals, exhibiting an oxidation potential between 2.7 and 2.8 V.63 The eight most defining properties of hydroxyl radical-based AOPs are shown in Fig. 5a. The first stage of AOPs involves the formation of ˙OH radicals, which play a main role in the degradation of organic contaminants. These radicals transform higher molecular weight toxic compounds into lower molecular weight, less toxic intermediates or directly mineralize them into CO2 and H2O. The overall process proceeds through electron transfer, hydrogen abstraction, and radical addition. Hydroxyl radicals degrade organic compounds such as pesticides through four main pathways: radical addition, radical combination, electron transfer, and hydrogen abstraction. During this mechanism, organic compounds are transformed into carbon-centered radicals (R˙ or R˙–OH), which then react with O2 to form peroxyl radicals (ROO˙). The subsequent reactions of these radicals lead to the formation of highly reactive species such as H2O2 and superoxide (O2˙−), thereby enhancing the overall degradation process. Finally, these chain-like reactions result in the breakdown of pollutants into relatively less harmful product.64 Recent studies highlighted the role of hydroxyl radicals in the degradation of pesticides. The use of a scavenger, i.e., 2-propanol (OH˙ quencher), decreased the degradation rate, supporting the participation of hydroxyl radicals (Fig. 5b–e).65

Identification of dominant reactive species in hydroxyl radical-based AOPs for pesticide degradation by radical quenching studies: (b and c) kinetics plots, (d) percentage degradation, and (e) rate constants in the presence of a trapping agent.65

Another study investigated the degradation of lindane using hydroxyl and sulfate radicals in a UV/peroxymonosulfate system. ˙OH radicals, being non-selective, degraded lindane through hydrogen abstraction and hydroxyl addition, with second-order rate constants of 108–1010 M1 s1. Due to the presence of strong electron-withdrawing chlorine atoms in lindane, the observed second-order rate constants are relatively lower, ranging from 5.2 × 108–1.1 × 109 M1 s1.66

UV/H2O2 (ultraviolet/hydrogen peroxide) processes

Ultraviolet (UV)-based advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) are recognized as eco-safe (“green”) technologies because of their effective rapid degradation with trace chemical residues. They have been widely utilized for pesticide removal in recent years.67 UV/H2O2 processes are regarded as some of the most effective AOPs, in which ultraviolet irradiation, in conjunction with hydrogen peroxide, generates highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (˙OH).68 In addition to radical production, UV radiation functions as a natural disinfectant while also contributing to the direct photolysis of organic contaminants in wastewater.69 The underlying mechanism comprises the photolytic breakage of hydrogen peroxide under UV irradiation, producing two hydroxyl radicals, as shown in eqn (1).581H2O2 + hν → 2OH˙.In photocatalytic systems, semiconductors such as titania (TiO2) are commonly used to increase radical generation. Upon UV irradiation, TiO2 produces electron–hole pairs, which take part in the surface redox reactions, as shown in eqn (2).702TiO2 + hν → e(conduction band) + h(valence band).

These charge carriers (e−/h+) react with H2O and O2 to generate additional ROS, further enhancing contaminant degradation. The photolytic interaction of H2O2 with UV radiation entails O–O bond cleavage, which is termed within the framework of Haber–Weiss (HW)-type reactions, resulting in the formation of ˙OH radicals and other reactive intermediates such as hydroperoxyl radicals (eqn (3) and (4), respectively).34

These reactions in combination contribute to the generation of a ROS chain, greatly accelerating the degradation efficacy of organic contaminants.

The efficacy of UV/H2O2 processes has been evidenced for different pesticides. For instance, parathion, an organothiophosphate insecticide, has been efficiently degraded in aqueous systems using UV/H2O2. At neutral pH (pH = 7), comparatively low quantum yields were observed; however, the presence of hydroxyl radicals effectively increased degradation, following 2nd-order kinetics with rate constants on the order of 109 M−1 s−1.71 Similarly, some other pesticides such as metaldehyde, clopyralid, and mecoprop have also been studied in both low-pressure (LP) and medium-pressure (MP) UV systems. Mecoprop exhibited fast degradation (up to 99.6%); however, metaldehyde demonstrated limited degradation under UV photolysis alone but achieved a much higher removal efficiency (≈99.7%) when the system was combined with H2O2. Conversely, clopyralid showed relatively low degradation performance even under UV/H2O2 conditions.72

Ozone-based AOPs

Ozone (O3) is a strong oxidizing agent with a high standard oxidation potential (E° ≈ 2.07 V), making it highly efficient for the degradation of a wide range of contaminants, such as pesticides and dyes.73 Ozone-based AOPs degrade toxic pollutants via two mechanisms: (1) molecular O3 directly attacks as an electrophile and (2) indirect oxidation by hydroxyl radicals generated from ozone decomposition.73 In the direct mechanism, ozone acts as an electrophile and attacks electron-rich centers such as carbon–carbon double bonds (CC) in pollutants. In the indirect mechanism, ozone breaks down in water to generate highly reactive hydroxyl radicals, which then non-selectively oxidize pollutants. The generation of hydroxyl radicals from ozone is affected by water and hydroxide ions, as shown in the following simplified reactions:53O3 + H2O → 2OH˙ + 4O2,67

These reactions show that basic conditions increase ozone decomposition, which increases ˙OH radical production and enhances the overall degradation efficiency.

Catalytic ozonation further increases the performance of ozone-based AOPs. Solid catalysts such as activated carbon (AC), metal oxides, and transition metals (e.g., Fe and Mn) increase ozone breakdown and promote the generation of radicals. Representative reactions are as follows:8Mn2+ + H+ + O3 → Mn2+ + O2 + OH˙,9Fe2+ + O3 → Fe3+ + O2−˙

Moreover, advanced systems, including plasma- and UV-assisted ozonation processes greatly increase ozone decomposition due to the generation of additional ROS, including hydroxyl radicals.74 Ozone-based AOPs have been broadly utilized for the degradation of pesticides in agricultural wastewater systems. For instance, pesticides such as chlorpyrifos, tetradifon, imazalil, malathion, difenoconazole, and chlorothalonil have been efficiently removed under ozonation conditions. Gaseous ozone has also been shown to be more efficient than aqueous ozone for chlorpyrifos removal, while near-complete removal of chlorothalonil (≈100%) has been achieved within minutes in fruit matrices.75 Similarly, rapid degradation of different pesticides has been reported utilizing the ozone-based AOPs. Compounds such as acetamiprid and thiacloprid can be completely degraded, while others, including thiamethoxam, clothianidin, spiromesifen, chlorpyrifos, azoxystrobin, lambda-cyhalothrin, and fenazaquin, show degradation performances ranging from ≈85% to 99% within short reaction times (≈5 min).76

Fenton processes

The Fenton reaction, first reported by H. J. H. Fenton in 1894, is based on the use of Fenton's reagent, a mixture of ferrous ions (Fe2+) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), which generates hydroxyl radicals (˙OH).77 Subsequently, this process has been widely utilized in the treatment of wastewater, especially for the removal of toxic pollutants such as pesticides. Compared with other AOPs, it is widely preferable and in demand because of its rapid degradation ability, simple operation, high mineralization performance, and strong anti-interference ability.78 Its basic mechanism involves the catalytic decomposition of hydrogen peroxide by Fe2+ ions, leading to the formation of hydroxyl radicals, as follows:10Fe2+ + H2O2 → Fe3+ + OH˙ + OH−.

The generated ˙OH radicals, possessing a higher standard oxidation potential than H2O2, initiate the degradation of pollutants into simpler and environmentally benign products such as CO2 and H2O.79 H2O2 plays a dual role in the reaction, working as a scavenger and an initiator of ˙OH radicals.80 In aqueous environments, further multi-step reactions contribute to the generation and regeneration of ROS. For instance, Fe3+ ions formed during the reaction can further interact with H2O2 to produce intermediate complexes, as follows:11Fe3+ + H2O2 ⇆ Fe–OOH2+ + H+,12

These reactions allow partial regeneration of Fe2+ ions, supporting the long-term catalytic cycle. However, radicals such as are less reactive and contribute less to pollutant degradation.

Moreover, under neutral or basic conditions, Fe3+ ions may precipitate as Fe(OH)3, resulting in the formation of sludge and hence limiting process efficiency.

Modified Fenton processes have been developed to address these limitations. In the photo-Fenton process, UV or visible light irradiation enhances the reduction of Fe3+ back in to Fe2+, thereby increasing ˙OH generation, hence enhancing the overall degradation performance.81 Likewise, electro-Fenton processes involve electrochemical techniques to continually regenerate Fe2+ and generate H2O2in situ. These modern processes are regarded as highly efficient and cost-effective for large-scale wastewater treatment applications.

Photo-Fenton process

The photo-Fenton process has recently gained significant attention as an advanced version of the established conventional Fenton process because of its superior efficiency in producing ˙OH under light irradiation. This reaction involves the combination of the conventional Fenton's reagent with UV or visible light (typically wavelength lower than 460 nm), which promotes the catalytic reaction cycle.82 Under light irradiation, H2O2 undergoes photolysis, resulting in the generation of ˙OH radicals due to its absorption in the UV region (210–230 nm):5814H2O2 + hν → 2OH˙.

Concurrently, UV light facilitates the photoreduction of Fe3+ ions back to Fe2+ ions, thus maintaining and promoting the Fenton reaction catalytic cycle. Fe2+ ions react with hydrogen peroxide to produce Fe3+ ions and ˙OH radicals, while these ions primarily exist as hydroxy-complexes such as Fe(OH)2+ under acidic conditions (optimal pH ≈ 2.5–3.0).83,84 The general pathway of the photo-Fenton process is demonstrated in Fig. 6a.

These reactions boost the regeneration of Fe2+ ions and support the continuous generation of hydroxyl radicals, appreciably increasing the degradation efficiency by reducing sludge formation compared to the conventional Fenton reaction.86

Conversely, the reaction of Fe3+ ions with hydrogen peroxide is slower than that of Fe2+ ions, which may limit the overall reaction rate, as follows:18Fe2+ + H2O2 → Fe3+ + OH˙ + OH−,19

The hydroperoxyl radical produced as result is appreciably less reactive than ˙OH, participating less in pollutant degradation.87

Recent studies have explored the efficacy of the photo-Fenton process in pesticide degradation. For instance, Wei Liu et al. synthesized single-atom Fe-doped Bi2WO6 monolayers via the hydrothermal method. This catalyst showed a superior Fenton-based catalytic degradation performance for the degradation of dinotefuran under visible light irradiation (Fig. 6b–f).85

In another study, Huston et al. investigated the degradation of various pesticides using a photochemical reactor under controlled conditions (T = 25 °C, pH ≈ 2.8, [Fe3+] = 5 × 10−5 M, [H2O2] = 1.0 × 10−2 M, and initial pesticide concentration ≈ 2 × 10−4 M). The results showed the rapid degradation of various pesticides (such as alachlor, aldicarb, and azinphos-methyl), with significant degradation (near complete) attained within 30–60 minutes, corroborating the high efficiency of the photo-Fenton process. In contrast, this study revealed that methoxychlor exhibits comparatively slower degradation rates, indicating that organophosphorus pesticides degrade more slowly than carbamates and acetanilids.88

Moreover, the photo-Fenton process has been applied to a broad range of pesticides, such as esfenvalerate, dimethoate, methyl parathion, chlorpyrifos, cypermethrin, and triclopyr, attaining high removal efficiencies under optimized operational conditions, as presented in Table 2. Notably, coupled biological-photochemical systems achieve superior mineralization (>96% COD and TOC removal), highlighting the necessity of developing hybrid approaches for complete detoxification.

Electro-Fenton (EF) process

Electrochemical technology integrating Fenton chemistry has emerged as an efficient approach for the remediation of wastewater.96 Over the past decades, it has shown significant improvement and development for the decontamination of hazardous and persistent organic pollutants, such as synthetic dyes, personal care products, pharmaceuticals, industrial effluents, and pesticides. The EF process was developed to overcome the problems of the conventional Fenton process, such as high operating costs, iron sludge generation, difficulties in chemical handling, and the storage and transportation of reagents. Electrochemical systems improve the Fenton process by enabling in situ production of H2O2 and continuous regeneration of Fe2+ ions, hence enhancing the efficiency and reducing sludge formation.97–100

In the EF process, H2O2 is produced at the cathode through the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), as follows:20O2 + 2H+ + 2e− → H2O2.

Concurrently, Fe3+ ions are electrochemically reduced back into Fe2+ ions, supporting the catalytic cycle, as follows:21Fe3+ + e− → Fe2+.

The generated Fe2+ ions react with H2O2 to produce ˙OH radicals, which are responsible for the degradation of pollutants, as shown in eqn (18). These reactions work together to maintain continuous ˙OH radical generation, improving the degradation efficiency while minimizing the need for external chemical inputs.101,102 Depending on the type of reagent stream and system organization, EF processes are divided into several classes, such as cathodic EF, anodic EF (sacrificial anode), Fe2+ regeneration EF (EF–Fere), and hybrid systems. In conventional EF, H2O2 is generated in situ, while Fe2+ may be added externally or electrochemically regenerated.103–105 In sacrificial anode processes, Fe2+ ions are directly released by iron electrodes into solution, thereby improving the degradation efficiency. However, excessive H2O2 can cause scavenging reactions, decreasing the availability of ˙OH radicals and the degradation efficiency.106,107 Advanced electro-Fenton (EF) systems, exemplified by the EF–H2O2–Fere configuration, adeptly integrate the in situ generation of H2O2 with the reduction of Fe3+ at the cathode, effectively reducing the necessity for chemical additives and the consequent production of sludge. Additionally, hybrid EF processes (e.g., photoelectro-Fenton and sono-electro-Fenton) use additional energy sources to further increase radical production and degradation performance.58 The efficacy of EF processes is affected by characteristics of the electrode materials utilized. Sophisticated cathodes, exemplified by materials derived from metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), graphene-integrated electrodes, and carbon materials enhanced with Fe3O4, have demonstrated superior efficiency in the oxygen reduction reaction and enhanced electron transfer rates.108–110 In Fig. 7, electro-Fenton, along with the reaction mechanism, is discussed.

Reaction mechanism of the electro-Fenton process for the degradation of pesticides.

The EF process has been extensively studied and utilized over recent decades for the degradation of different pesticides. Recently, lindane, picloram, diuron, glyphosate, and 2,4-DPPA have been degraded via the electro-Fenton process. Lindane, an insecticide with a 10 mg L−1 concentration was degraded within 15 minutes.111 Picloram, an herbicide, showed 80% removal after 8 h of electrolysis when degraded using the EF process.112 Diuron and glyphosate, both herbicides, were degraded by 8% and 94%, respectively, at pH 3 within 60 minutes of reaction. Additionally, 99.2% of 2,4-DPPA was degraded with k1 = 0696 min−1.113,114 These results show the efficacy of the EF process for the efficient removal of pesticides from water.

Comparative efficiencies, limitations, and synergistic effects of AOPs

Although photocatalysis, UV-H2O2, ozonation, and Fenton/photo-Fenton processes are all efficient AOPs, their operating windows and radical-generation pathways differ, making them incompatible. Photocatalysis is an attractive approach because it enables light-driven radical generation, while UV-H2O2 systems rapidly produce hydroxyl radicals, ozonation provides both direct oxidation and hydroxyl-radical routes, and Fenton/photo-Fenton processes can produce radicals quickly in acidic environments. The target pollutant structure, pH, oxidant dose, catalyst loading, and irradiation source all have a significant impact on their apparent efficiency.115–118 The drawback of isolated AOPs is that their exceptional performance under laboratory conditions frequently fails with actual wastewater or under more realistic conditions. Charge-carrier recombination, poor visible-light utilization and limited stability can limit photocatalysis; requirement for acidic pH or formation of sludge are drawbacks associated with Fenton chemistry, while in ozonation, mass-transfer limitations and selective oxidation behavior restrict treatment performance. This also explains why certain aspects of a process that appear to work well with a model dye or pesticide may be less compelling when moving into systems that feature dissolved organic matter, inorganic ions, and multiple co-contaminants.115–117,119

Typically, hybrid AOPs exhibit a superior performance because they are designed with complementary functions and not just two (chemical) treatments combined. Specifically, photocatalysis can be coupled with Fenton chemistry not only to improve oxidant activation but also suppress electron–hole recombination, while ozone-assisted systems can be intensified through UV irradiation or catalyst-assisted methods to expand the oxidative window. Nevertheless, synergy must only be claimed for systems in which radical generation, charge transfer or pollutant preconcentration is truly enhanced as a result of coupling; improvements may also be additive or even sub-additive.116,118,120

Although biochar primarily enhances AOPs by coupling adsorption with catalytic activation, the inherent limitations in the parent processes remain unaddressed. Biochar is widely used as a modifier in photocatalytic and oxidant-activation systems, where it improves their electron-transfer capacity, stabilizes active phases, and enriches pollutant concentration around reactive sites. Nevertheless, feedstock variability, surface-chemistry heterogeneity, and reduced performance in real wastewater remain primary concerns, making biochar a promising amendment rather than a universal solution.121,122 Therefore, the most meaningful comparison is no longer which AOP is better overall, but rather which process best matches the contaminant and matrix, considering the constraints of reactor design. Among the AOP technologies mentioned above, photocatalysis has garnered focused interest due to its dual advantages of solar energy utilization and efficient free radical generation. Photocatalytic performance is mainly governed by catalyst properties; among them, metal oxide nanomaterials are the most widely studied class for pesticide degradation applications.

Photocatalytic degradation using metal oxide nanomaterials

Nanostructured metal oxides (NMox) have emerged as highly efficient photocatalysts for the degradation of pesticides because of their characteristic physiochemical properties, such as high surface areas, tunable band gaps, and strong standard redox potentials.123 Upon light irradiation, electrons in the valence band (VB) are excited to the conduction band (CB), producing electron–hole (e−/h+) pairs, as follows:22Semiconductor + hν → e−(CB) + h+(VB).

The photo-generated holes (h+) oxidize water or hydroxide ions to produce hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), while electrons (e−) reduce oxygen to form superoxide radicals (O2˙−). These reactive species subsequently degrade pesticide molecules into CO2 and H2O. Common metal oxide photocatalysts include TiO2, ZnO, Fe2O3, CuO, WO3, and Bi2O3. Among these, TiO2 and ZnO are the most widely used due to their high photocatalytic efficiency, stability, and low cost.21,57 However, NMox suffer from rapid electron–hole recombination, which limits their efficiency. This can be addressed by doping NMox with other elements, thereby enhancing charge separation and photocatalytic activity.4,57,123,124 Numerous operational factors, such as pH, catalyst dosage, pollutant concentration, irradiation intensity, reaction time, and reactor architecture, influence photocatalytic degradation efficiency. As a result, rather than presenting directly normalized values across different studies, the degradation efficiencies listed below should be interpreted as condition-specific results. Because these variables differ among studies, cross-study comparisons should be treated as qualitative unless the operating conditions are explicitly matched or normalized. Higher catalyst loadings increase the number of active sites and radical generation, whereas excessive pollutant concentrations may reduce the degradation efficiency due to active site saturation. Metal oxide@biochar-based photocatalysts for pesticide degradation

Various metal oxide@biochar-based photocatalysts and their applications in the degradation of different classes of pesticides, including herbicides, fungicides, and insecticides, are discussed in detail.

TiO2@biochar-based photocatalysis for pesticide degradation

Titanium dioxide (TiO2) is a widely studied semiconductor photocatalyst for pesticide degradation because of its non-toxicity, chemical stability, low cost, and high standard oxidative potential. Owing to its high durability and optical transparency, it is very suitable for environmental applications in the visible region. At room temperature, TiO2 exists as three main crystalline polymorphs, including anatase, rutile, and brookite.133 Among these, anatase- and rutile-phase TiO2 are the two most widely studied polymorphs. The bandgap of anatase TiO2 is ∼3.2 eV, and its photocatalytic activity is generally higher compared to rutile TiO2 (3.02 eV), which is attributed to its longer charge carrier lifetime and lower electron–hole recombination rate.134,135 Alternatively, the less studied brookite, due to its limited abundance and synthesis complications, has also been shown to be a promising photocatalyst. Mixed-phase TiO2 composites, such as anatase–rutile systems, have been demonstrated to improve photocatalytic efficiency through improved charge separation and increased light absorption.136 When irradiated with photons whose energy is greater than or equal to its bandgap, TiO2 generates e−/h+ pairs. These charge carriers take part in redox reactions to subsequently produce ROS, mainly ˙OH radicals, which are responsible for the degradation and mineralization of pesticide molecules into CO2, H2O, and inorganic ions. TiO2 has been observed to degrade organophosphates, carbamates and pyrethroids with high efficiencies. For instance, the oxidation of organochlorine pesticides such as aldrin proceeds rapidly in aqueous systems with UV irradiation using TiO2,137 forming intermediate products (such as dieldrin and related derivatives). The addition of oxidants such as H2O2 also increases the degradation efficiency by generating more hydroxyl radicals.138 The general mechanism of pesticide degradation using TiO2 is shown in Fig. 9.

Pesticide degradation using the TiO2 semiconductor.

Similarly, isomers of hexachlorocyclohexane (BHC), dicofol and cypermethrin were also effectively degraded using TiO2-coated surfaces in the presence of UV light, resulting in their considerable removal within short irradiation times.139 TiO2-based photocatalysis has also exhibited efficiency in degrading herbicides, such as atrazine and terbuthylazine, through various degradation pathways of dealkylation, dichlorination and deamination.140,141 In many instances, photocatalysis harvests biodegradable intermediates (e.g., cyanuric acid) that can be later eliminated using biological treatment methods. Moreover, complete degradation of compounds (metamitron and pyridaben) under optimized photocatalytic conditions has also been reported.142–144 Several operational parameters such as catalyst loading, pH, irradiation time, and pollutant concentration can influence the efficiency of TiO2 photocatalysis. The overall degradation kinetics are found to follow pseudo-first-order behavior based on the Langmuir–Hinshelwood mechanism, involving adsorption of the pollutant molecules on the catalyst surface. Additionally, the use of TiO2 in conjunction with oxidants (H2O2, persulfates, etc.), or other processes (ozonation), considerably increases the degradation rates and mineralization efficiency.21 Different modification strategies have been developed to address the drawbacks of TiO2 including rapid charge recombination and narrow visible-light utilization. For example, metal doping (Pt, Au, Pd), non-metal doping and the formation of heterojunctions to enhance charge separation and extend its light absorption into the visible spectrum. For instance, some data-driven enhancements can be seen in Au–Pd-modified TiO2 composites, which exhibited higher degradation efficiency for malathion.145

Numerous carbonaceous materials have been found to be especially effective in AOPs, such as activated carbon (AC), carbon nanotubes (CNTs), graphene, and reduced graphene oxide (rGO), due to their large surface area and significant adsorption capacity. However, these substances are expensive and typically extracted from non-renewable fossil resources.146 In contrast, biochar (BC) is a material with a high carbon content obtained by pyrolysis in an oxygen-deficient environment, which has emerged in recent years as a promising sustainable, affordable and eco-friendly option. Biochar-based materials have emerged as attractive candidates in recent years due to their distinctive physicochemical properties, such as highly porous structure, tailorable surface area, numerous oxygen-containing functional groups, and strong sorption capacity.147 The main characteristics and functional role of biochar for environmental remediation are illustrated in Fig. 10. It improves not only pollutant adsorption capacity but also catalytic activity to achieve more efficient degradation. Moreover, full carbonization of biomass can be achieved in combination with metal oxides in composites to enhance surface reactivity, improve charge–transfer properties and create more active sites, thereby improving catalytic performance.

Among different materials, biochar-supported TiO2 composites have been found to possess higher surface area, porosity, and adsorption capacity. These hybrid systems have been reported to enhance photocatalytic performance owing to their improved pollutant adsorption capacity and electron transfer efficiency. For example, magnetically recoverable TiO2-biochar composites degraded up to 94.23% of glyphosate under visible irradiation in a reported study, possessing both remarkably high degradation efficiency and good reusability under specific experimental conditions, indicating the feasibility of practical wastewater treatment applications.148 Although its claimed effectiveness largely depends on the experimental conditions employed in each study, TiO2 is a commonly used benchmark photocatalyst for pesticide degradation due to its stability, affordability, and extensive literature base. Recent advances in material engineering and nanocomposite design have made it a reasonable option for both sustainable large-scale applications in environmental remediation, although its apparent performance remains strongly dependent on the operating conditions.

ZnO@biochar-based photocatalysis for pesticide degradation

After TiO2, zinc oxide (ZnO) is the most widely used semiconductor photocatalyst, which possesses a broad band gap (∼3.37 eV), excellent physicochemical stability, low toxicity, and low cost.149–151 Due to these properties, ZnO can be effectively used for environmental remediation, especially pesticide degradation in aqueous environments. In addition to photocatalytic applications, ZnO has also been investigated in a variety of other areas including sensors, solar cells, antibacterial systems and photochemical devices due to its biocompatibility and versatile functional properties.150,152 The photochemical activity of ZnO in generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) makes ZnO-based AOPs efficient.

Recently, ZnO-biochar hybrid photocatalysts have received considerable attention compared with other emerging materials due to the synergistic combination of semiconductor photocatalysis and a carbon-based adsorption platform. Photogenerated charge carriers in ZnO provide strong oxidative power, high surface area, and high electron conductivity, while the surface functional groups of biochar enable the formation of composites that are particularly effective for degrading recalcitrant agrochemicals. The interaction between ZnO and the biochar matrix is essentially determined by interfacial Zn–O–C bonding, which promotes charge separation via rapid electron donation from ZnO to the conductive carbon shell, effectively inhibiting fast electron–hole recombination processes that normally restrict the performance of pristine ZnO.153 Simultaneously, biochar exhibits a porous structure rich in surface functional groups, which consistently adsorb and preconcentrate pesticide molecules on its reactive sites to enhance local photocatalytic reactions. Under UV-visible irradiation, the photogenerated electrons and holes can participate in redox processes, producing ROS species, such as ˙OH and ˙O2−, while photogenerated h+ are mainly responsible for oxidative degradation routes.154,155 Biochar not only improves ROS generation but also maintains bandgap modulation and visible-light harvesting ability, thus overcoming the main disadvantage of ZnO-based photocatalysts.23 Quantitative studies also indicate a clear synergistic enhancement, given that ZnO alone exhibited relatively low degradation efficiencies (e.g., 43% of atrazine), whereas biochar alone was effective via adsorption (77–80% removal). However, under optimized conditions, ZnO–biochar nanocomposites show significantly improved degradation efficiencies of up to 90% for atrazine and 84% for 2,4-D, deviating from additive behaviour and reflecting synergistic effects.156 In addition, the solvent-free synthesis strategies, such as ball-milling, for preparing maize biochar–ZnO nanocomposites emphasize scalable and environmentally friendly options for catalyst preparation. These systems are highly tunable, yielding degradation efficiencies of 83.5% of Safranin O under visible light, 81.0% under UV, and even reaching 78% under dark conditions in the reported work, suggesting that adsorption-assisted pathways contribute to the observed removal performance. The performance of pesticide-related systems varies significantly depending on the irradiation mode. In the described investigation, one ZnO-based system achieved 56.5% degradation under visible light, compared to 27.5% under UV light and 25.2% under dark conditions, indicating pseudo-first-order kinetics, which confirms the adsorption and photocatalysis mechanism for pollutant removal.157 This dual function also indicates that ZnO-biochar systems may work efficiently under low-irradiation conditions, which is an advantage for actual environmental setups. Beyond batch systems, recent developments in fixed-bed column reactor experiments highlight scalability with these materials, where bed height, influent concentration and flow rate are all critical parameters affecting performance. Results from modelling approaches using segmented-bed data fitted to the Thomas and BDST models and performed in triplicate show that increasing bed height and decreasing flow rate improve sorption capacity and breakthrough time, mainly due to increased residence time and mass transfer dynamics, indicating that hydrodynamic effects dominate in continuous-flow systems. In addition, competing co-ions can alter adsorption behaviour; nevertheless, advanced composites (e.g., BC–GO/ZnO) are robust due to their better surface functionality and electron mobility. These systems also show good mechanical stability and reusability through multiple sorption-desorption cycles, indicating their potential for long-term application in continuous treatment systems. Subsequent strategies, such as the incorporation of graphene oxide, may enhance structural stability and intrinsic electronic conductivity, resulting in hybridized architectures with improved performance.158

Several recent advances have included machine learning methods (SVM, RF, and ANN) to predict efficient degradation and determine optimal operating conditions, with SVM providing the most predictive capability and good experimental agreement. This paradigm of data-driven modelling has begun to harness predictive and optimization frameworks for photocatalytic systems, thereby reducing experimental burden and enabling faster scale-up. However, despite these significant advancements, challenges remain such as incomplete characterization of degradation intermediates, limited validation in complex environmental matrices, and concerns surrounding long-term stability and leaching of Zn.156 Findings revealed that ZnO-biochar-based nanocomposites constitute a robust, scalable, and multifunctional platform for pesticide degradation, driven by synergistic adsorption-photocatalytic mechanisms coupled to emerging reactor-scale and in silico optimization strategies, but sequenced system-level and mechanistic studies are need to realize their implementation in practical environmental scenarios.

Moreover, the impact of operational parameters in the above-reported studies (Table 4) demonstrates that both physicochemical conditions and hydrodynamic features significantly affect degradation efficiency, with pH, catalyst dosage and light irradiation rate playing a major role in batch systems, whereas flow rate and bed height modulate mass transfer phenomena and breakthrough consumption in continuous-flow systems.

Copper oxide–biochar-based photocatalysts for pesticide degradation

Copper oxide (CuO/Cu2O)–biochar-based photocatalysts have recently been reported as a significant class of materials for pesticide degradation due to their inherent visible-light activity, narrow band gap (∼1.2–2.1 eV), and redox-active Cu(i)/Cu(ii) cycling that allows solar-driven AOPs. Unlike isolated Cu-based composites, the addition of copper oxides to biochar enables synergistic coupling between the semiconductor photoactivity and carbon-mediated adsorption–electron transfer capabilities, hence improving the overall photocatalytic efficiency. Biochar is not only favourable for providing a high surface area and hierarchical porosity for pesticide adsorption but also serves as an electron reservoir that promotes charge separation and suppresses recombination losses via interfacial charge-transfer pathways.159 Mechanistically, CuO/Cu2O also creates electron–hole pairs under visible-light irradiation, with conduction band electrons reducing dissolved oxygen to superoxide radicals (˙O2−), and valence band holes either directly oxidizing pesticide molecules or forming hydroxyl radicals (˙OH). Experimental data widely indicate that ˙O2− and ˙OH radicals are the dominant reactive species, while biochar promotes multi-electron-transfer (MET) processes that are particularly important for degrading pollutants.160

A significant challenge is the photo-corrosion and instability of Cu2O in oxidative environments, which may eventually result in the oxidation of Cu(i) over long-term operation.161,162 Although biochar partially addresses this issue through semiconductor-interfacial stabilization,163 its long-term durability is not well elucidated. Moreover, although the results demonstrated promising outcomes, particularly in modelled pollutant systems (e.g., dyes and antibiotics), applications in pesticide degradation are still lacking, thus requiring investigation into newly emerging copper oxide–biochar coupled systems for the photocatalytic degradation of pesticides.

Iron oxide@biochar-based photocatalysts for pesticide degradation

Iron oxides are semiconductor materials that can absorb visible light, which promotes electron–hole generation under solar irradiation. In addition, when biochar, a porous environmentally friendly composite produced from biomass, is blended with these materials, their specific surface area, pollutant adsorption capacity and environmental compatibility frequently improve. Moreover, the presence of Fe3O4 gives rise to magnetic separability, enabling easy retrieval and recycling of photocatalysts and reducing secondary pollution.164 Thus, iron oxide–biochar composites have promising applications for effective pesticide removal from water, including the degradation of pesticides, making them sustainable materials for water treatment applications. Efficient charge separation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation are the factors predominantly influencing the photocatalytic behavior of iron oxide-biochar composites. In sequence, under visible-light irradiation, photon excitation occurs when Fe2O3 or Fe3O4 absorbs photons, promoting electrons from the valence band to the conduction band (leaving behind holes).164 Biochar acts as an electron mediator that accepts photogenerated electrons and decreases recombination rates.165 The corresponding charges react with molecular oxygen and water to generate a variety of ROS, including superoxide radicals (˙O2−), hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), and singlet oxygen (1O2), that attack pesticide molecules, eventually leading to mineralization.166 The classical degradation pathways include hydroxylation and oxidative cleavage of C–P bonds in organophosphates and subsequent generation of low-molecular-weight acids and CO2. Fe–biochar interfacial engineering enables instant electron transfer, promoting ROS generation and the reaction kinetics.167 Compared to pristine materials, various iron oxide phases (α-FeOOH, β-FeOOH, Fe2O3 and Fe3O4) immobilized on biochar exhibit greatly enhanced photocatalytic performance. High degradation efficiencies for a range of pesticides, including endosulfan, ethion and 4,4-DDE, have been achieved (>90%) under solar irradiation.168,169 This catalytic activity occurs due to the higher surface area, negative zeta potential values and low band gap energy levels (∼1.8–1.9 eV), which enhance light absorption and interfacial redox reactions. This degradation mechanism usually follows pseudo-first-order kinetics based on the Langmuir–Hinshelwood model, and reactive oxygen species (˙OH, ˙O2−, and h+) have been proven to be potent agents driving the degradation of pollutants. Biochar is also involved in facilitating electron transfer and sustaining continuous radical generation. However, advanced systems such as α-Fe2O3/MgO@BC not only facilitate pesticide degradation but also enable phosphate recovery, while heterojunction composites (e.g., MgO–CeO2–Fe3O4@BC) augment visible-light absorption and charge separation.167,170 These materials also exhibit favourable stability and reusability (5–8 cycles). However, scaling up and validating performance in real wastewater systems are still challenges.

Cross-comparison of metal oxide-based biochar systems reveals that α-Fe3O4/MgO@BC exhibits higher rate constants than ZnO@BC, attributable to its narrower band gap (1.8–2.2 eV vs. 3.37 eV), enabling visible-light activation and enhanced interfacial electron transfer via Fe–O–C bonding. However, ZnO@BC achieves comparable atrazine removal (93%) at a lower dosage, reflecting its superior adsorption affinity, as shown in Table 5.

Redox tunable metal oxides@biochar-based photocatalysts for pesticide degradation

In addition to conventional metal oxides, emerging metal oxides, such as magnesium oxide (MgO), manganese oxides (MnOx) and cobalt oxide (Co3O4), incorporated with biochar, have shown improved photocatalytic degradation of pesticides owing to their unique redox properties, oxygen vacancy chemistry and synergistic interaction with biochar matrices. These systems possess enhanced charge separation, enlarged light harvesting ability and tunable ROS generation, thus showing great potential for advanced photocatalysis.172 These emerging redox tunable metal oxide@biochar composites along with their enhancement mechanisms in the photocatalytic degradation of pesticides are shown in Fig. 11.

Biochar-supported redox tunable metal oxide composites (MgO, Co3O4, MnOx and multi-oxides), as well as their enhancement mechanisms in photocatalytic pesticide degradation.

MgO is a wide bandgap basic oxide that mainly acts to promote the adsorption and surface activation of polar pesticides. For instance, in biochar-g-C3N4-MgO composites, MgO is beneficial for electron–hole separation and visible-light utilization, enabling efficient pesticide degradation (80.1% removal rate for dinotefuran).173 The efficient degradation performance of the composite can also be attributed to the improvement of surface alkalinity induced by the incorporation of MgO, which results in greater adsorption of acidic pesticide species and promotes ROS attack. MnOx, another emerging metal oxide photocatalyst, has multiple oxidation states (Mn2+/Mn3+/Mn4+) and favors redox cycling to produce catalytic ROS. Furthermore, Mn-based oxides catalyze Fenton-like reactions to produce ˙OH radicals in a mild environment. Their incorporation into biochar may improve the electron transfer pathways, dispersion of active sites, and catalytic stability. While direct studies performed with MnOx@biochar composites for specific pesticides are still emerging, their strong oxidative potential and redox flexibility indicate that they may be highly effective at degrading persistent pesticide structures. These composites significantly increased persulfate (PS) activation for thiamethoxam (TMX) degradation, achieving 99% removal within 90 min.

A recent study by Xuetao Liang et al. was based on promising Co3O4@biochar composites for improvements in the activation of peroxymonosulfate (PMS) to degrade 2,4-dichlorophenoxyacetic acid (2,4-D). The results obtained from this study demonstrate the superior potential of Co3O4@biochar composites for pesticide degradation, where an exceptional degradation efficiency of 95.3% was achieved at a PMS concentration of 0.8 mM.174 Co3O4@biochar composites show considerable promise but have yet to be validated in situ for real-world pesticide removal.

Nano zero-valent metal (nZV)-supported biochar composites for pesticide removal

Nano zero-valent metal (nZV)-functionalized biochar composites have been identified as powerful multifunctional materials for the degradation of pesticides and other organic contaminants in water. These composites synergistically combine the high surface area, porosity and functional groups of biochar with the strong reductive and catalytic nature of nZV metals. This hybridization strategy overcomes the critical inherent shortcomings of pristine nZV particles, including agglomeration, rapid oxidation, and limited mobility, thus greatly improving reactivity, stability, and reusability in environmental remediation. Recent experimental data show that nZV iron@biochar NCs are much more effective at degrading nitrochlorobenzene, a pesticide intermediate, than conventional technologies, with their combined adsorption-reduction-oxidation pathways achieving removal efficiencies above 90% under optimal conditions.175 Another key study by Xiangying Li et al. on the remediation of hydrophobic neonicotinoid pesticides reported the synthesis of a smart composite, namely ball-milled nitrogen-doped biochar linked with zero-valent iron (MNBC-ZVI). Consequently, plain ZVI was replaced with the MNBC-ZVI composite, which effectively degraded 100% of thiamethoxam from a solution containing 10 mg L−1 within 360 minutes (more than twelve times faster). More critically, the electron efficiency nearly doubled (from 30% to 58%), indicating that fewer electrons were wasted on side reactions. In fact, less iron sludge accumulation, which is a common operational problem, was noted as well.176 As a result, this study developed a fundamental framework for nZVI@biochar composites as an effective approach to overcome limitations related to irreversible electron selectivity and surface passivation via nitrogen doping and mechanochemical ball milling. Single-atom catalyst (SAC)-supported biochar composites for pesticide removal

Biochar-supported single-atom catalysts (B@SACs) represent a promising class of composites for addressing environmental pollution and can be employed to remove persistent pollutants from aquatic environments such as pesticides efficiently. These materials combine the large specific area, porosity, and surface functionalities of biochar with the highest atomic efficiency and distinct electronic features of single atoms, resulting in unprecedented catalytic activity compared to conventional nanomaterials. Recent developments indicate that biochar-supported SACs can efficiently activate advanced oxidation processes (AOPs), facilitating the on-demand and selective degradation of pesticide molecules in more challenging environments. Usually, single atoms (such as Fe, Co, Cu and Mn) are stabilized by coordination with heteroatoms (N or O) in biochar to produce structures such as M–N4. These configurations drastically alter the electronic densities and redox potentials of the catalytic sites, which promotes pollutant adsorption and activation processes.177 Biochar@SAC composites effectively activate oxidants, such as, peroxymonosulfate (PMS) or peroxydisulfate (PDS), generating ROS (i.e., 1O2, SO4˙−, and ˙OH), which are highly reactive towards pollutant molecules, allowing oxidative degradation pathways.178

Recent research indicates that non-radical mechanisms are especially important, with direct electron transfer processes occurring across conductive biochar matrices. For instance, the Co–N4 sites embedded in biochar significantly increase electron transport, improving the oxidation of pollutants without generating excess radicals.179 Pesticide molecules are inherently adsorbed onto the biochar surface via π–π interactions, hydrogen bonding and pore-filling interactions.180 The resulting preconcentration effect improves the catalytic degradation performance by positioning the pollutants in close proximity to the active metal sites. Reproducibility, scalability, and practical deployment

Biochar-based photocatalysts face a translation gap, as laboratory results do not always translate into practical performance. Although these materials have demonstrated remarkable pesticide removal in controlled tests, the true barrier is whether they are durable, repeatable, and operationally viable outside of idealized conditions. Many studies rely on single-use batch experiments using model contaminants, which are insufficient to support claims of practical suitability.185,186 Stability and recyclability must be viewed as key performance criteria rather than secondary observations. Repeated cycling experiments are required to establish whether the catalyst retains its structure, charge-transfer characteristics, adsorption capacity, and oxidative activity following regeneration. Short-term reuse data are insufficient because they typically exclude post-reaction structural analysis, active-site loss, and quantitative leaching measures.185–187

Scale-up needs to account for validation in continuous flow, not merely in batch operation. Batch testing tends to overestimate performance due to neglecting hydrodynamics, pressure drop, the possibility of clogging, and breakthrough behaviour. While continuous flow and fixed beds are more pertinent for real-world applications, they impose transport limitations, which have to considered.188–190 Compared with synthetic solutions, real wastewater matrices can significantly reduce performance. Validation in real wastewater is crucial because competing ions, dissolved organic matter, fluctuating pH, and co-contaminants can reduce adsorption and interfere with radical production. Claims of field preparedness should be regarded as preliminary rather than proven until such evidence is becomes widely available.186,191,192 The way forward involves standardizing the methods, documenting regeneration over multiple cycles, measuring leaching, and conducting tests on real wastewater under continuous operation. This is required before any claims of scalability can be made about biochar-based photocatalyst systems.

While traditional biochar-supported photocatalysts have shown desirable degradation efficiencies, the need for greater selectivity, enhanced charge-transfer dynamics and improved catalyst stability has paved the way for the development of more advanced multifunctional architectures. These achievements have given rise to next-generation biochar-based hybrid nanocomposites.

Next-generation biochar-based hybrid nanocomposites for pesticide degradation

Recent developments in photocatalysis have moved toward hybrid and multifunctional biochar-based systems that combine various catalytic components into one architecture to address challenges including charge recombination, low selectivity, and insufficient redox potential. Next-generation hybrid systems such as zero-valent metal (nZV)-metal oxide hybrids, single-atom catalyst (SAC)-nanoparticle hybrids, and photo-assisted/electro-assisted systems enable synergistic oxidative–reductive pathways, leading to efficient pesticide degradation.172 Zero-valent metals (e.g., Fe0) are integrated with metal oxides on biochar to form dual-function systems through reductive and oxidative pathways, where nZV, a strong electron-donor, allows reductive dechlorination of halogenated pesticides. Alternatively, metal oxides generate ROS (such as, ˙OH and ˙O2−) for the oxidative degradation of organic compounds. For instance, a recently reported BC@NiFe2O4 NC exhibited exceptional potential in the degradation of atrazine (AT) and endosulfan (ES) pesticides in actual WW. Moreover, BC@NiFe2O4 showed excellent sustainability and cost-effectiveness by retaining its efficiency for up to eight cycles without significant activity loss. AT and ES degradation occurs via adsorption, along with radical oxidation, while the kinetics are further improved via electron transfer processes.193

Scientific development of hybrid biochar-based photocatalysts is no longer limited to traditional inorganic systems but has extended to organic heterojunctions, hydrogel-immobilized catalysts, Z-scheme architectures and multi-metal-engineered composites with superior activity for the photocatalytic degradation of pesticides due to synergistic mechanisms. Largely dominated by traditional inorganic systems, the recent development of hybrid biochar-based photocatalysts has moved towards adding organic heterojunctions, hydrogel-immobilized catalysts, Z-scheme architectures and multi-metal engineered composites which significantly improve photocatalytic pesticides degradation through synergy. Significant progress has been achieved by the combination of organic donor–acceptor heterojunctions with biochar (i.e., PBDB-T:BTTIC-TT@biochar system), which overcomes the related problems of poor dispersion and limited active sites in organic photocatalysts. The addition of thiophene side chains to the acceptor molecule (BTTIC-TT) increased its visible-light absorption by around 18% and improved its photocatalytic efficiency. This system achieved 97.4% degradation of 2,4,6-trichlorophenol within 60 min, during which ˙O2− was identified as the dominant reactive species driving the oxidation pathway toward small carboxylic acids. More importantly, this composite exhibited superb stability during 15 catalytic cycles and stable performance over a wide pH range, thus showing its environmental adaptability.194 Another promising strategy is the immobilization of hybrid photocatalysts onto hydrogel substrates, for instance, CoMgAl layered double oxide–biochar embedded in a pectin-based hydrogel (CoMgAl LDO@BC/pectin). This template acts as a porous, stable and anti-aggregation matrix for improved light harvesting and charge separation. Under visible light, the hydrogel composite degraded acetamiprid and atrazine with degradation efficiencies of >98% within 60 min due to the main roles played by ˙OH and ˙O2− radicals. This study enables dual functionalities by exploiting both adsorption and photocatalysis, significantly accelerating the degradation kinetics, in addition to excellent reusability and performance in real water matrices, confirming its practical applicability.195 Likewise, multi-component semiconductor–biochar systems, such as biochar@CdS–Fe3O4 nanocomposites, also exhibit superior photocatalytic activity through both magnetic retrieval and improved charge separation. CdS, Fe3O4, and biochar in this composite serve as a visible-light-active semiconductor and charge-transfer mediator, a magnetic separator, and a matrix to suppress nanoparticle agglomeration, respectively. This synergistic arrangement resulted in 97% chlorpyrifos degradation, and the kinetics were well described by the Langmuir–Hinshelwood model, which follows pseudo-first-order kinetics, thereby validating adsorption–reaction coupling.196 One sophisticated design involves the Z-scheme photocatalytic system, exemplified here by an Ag3PO4-WO3@biochar nanocomposite (ABW-10), where biochar serves as a solid-state electron mediator that enables efficient charge separation without sacrificing high redox capability. The Z-scheme mechanism enables the generation of strongly oxidative holes and reductive electrons at the same time, resulting in over 99.9% mineralization of both chlorpyrifos and ciprofloxacin within 60 min.197 Concurrently, bimetallic and persulfate-activated systems have also emerged as robust hybrid platforms. The Fe2SiO4/Zn2SiO4 modified biochar (Fe/Zn@BC) system exhibits dual radical (˙OH and SO4˙−) and non-radical pathways to achieve ∼94.8% imidacloprid degradation under visible-light-driven PMS activation. Mechanistic studies further demonstrate that Fe sites are responsible for PMS activation via mediated electron transfer on Fe–O–Si, while Zn species and PFRs also boost oxidation pathways other than radical ones. The synergistic effect of metal sites and the carbon matrix promote charge transfer and pollutant degradation.198 Finally, carbon@MXene-TiO2 hybrid systems are a new family of multifunctional materials that integrate adsorption and photocatalysis. In TiO2-MXene@derived carbon composites, the high adsorption capacity (qmax ≈ 118–135 mg g−1) of the porous carbon matrix, coupled with the generation of ˙OH/O2˙−/singlet oxygen (1O2) by TiO2 under UV irradiation leads to the in situ degradation of adsorbed organophosphorus pesticides such as chlorpyrifos and diazinon. This material shows robustness and resistance to matrix interference, maintaining >85% efficiency after multiple cycles.199

Overall, these next-generation hybrid biochar nanocomposites comprise a notable advancement in photocatalytic pesticide remediation by combining multi-functional components into a single platform. The highly synergistic advancement in light harvesting, charge separation, ROS generation, and adsorption ability originates from the integration of organic heterojunctions, metal oxides, Z-scheme systems, hydrogel matrices and bimetallic active sites. Notably, the presence of dual radical-non-radical pathways, solid-state electron mediation and structural tunability provides new mechanistic insights into pollutant degradation.

Pesticide structure-reactivity framework in biochar-based photocatalytic systems

The efficiency of pesticide degradation relies on molecular characteristics such as adsorption onto biochar surfaces, susceptibility to reactive oxygen species (ROS), and mineralization pathways. Instead of treating pesticides as generic pollutants, a structure–reactivity lens categorizes them according to motifs that govern photocatalytic behavior in metal oxide@biochar, nZV@biochar, and SAC@biochar composites.200,201 Recent studies show that photocatalytic efficiency is not universal with pesticides but depends significantly on functional groups, electron density and bond strength.193,202 Photocatalytic degradation mechanistically refers to three coupled phases, including adsorption (structure governed), photo-induced radical attack, and bond cleavage, leading to mineralization.203 Studies such as ESR and radical quenching confirmed that ROS (e.g., ˙OH and ˙O2−) primarily govern the degradation pathways within biochar-based composites.173,204 The susceptibility of pesticides to photocatalytic oxidation is mainly related to their frontier molecular orbital (FMO) properties, specifically the HOMO energy level and electron density distribution. Electron-rich moieties (amines, heterocycles, and π-conjugated systems) show increased reactivity to electrophilic oxidants such as ˙OH. Conversely, electron-deficient centers (halogenated carbons and nitro groups) will be less susceptible to oxidative attack but can undergo reductive pathways. For instance, degradation of atrazine starts from electron-rich nitrogen sites, giving rise to the stepwise N-dealkylation and ring opening.193 In contrast, oxidative attack on organophosphates occurs preferentially at electron-dense P–O/PS centers, promoting bond fragmentation.202

Additionally, the intrinsic stability of chemical bonds also plays a critical role in degradation kinetics, as C–N and P–O (low bond dissociation energies) undergo fast cleavage, while bonds with high bond dissociation energies (e.g., C–Cl and C–F) possess persistence and resistance to cleavage. For example, organophosphates and carbamates have labile P–O and C–N bonds that degrade rapidly, while organochlorines have strong C–Cl bonds, which confer persistence.172 Thorough pathways of mineralization comprising CO2, H2O, NH3 and PO43− validate individual bond-breaking mechanisms.205 More importantly, biochar presents an essential adsorption-reaction coupling, which serves as both an adsorption platform (π–π stacking and hydrophobic interactions) and electron mediator (interfacial charge transfer facilitator), enriching pesticides around these reactive sites selectively, in essence functioning as a molecular pre-activation step. In addition, it should be noted that the catalytic activity of biochar-based photocatalysts is greatly determined by their inherent physicochemical characteristics, such as surface functionalities, porosity, degree of graphitization, and electrical conductivity. Using the results of mechanistic investigations through electron spin resonance (ESR), photoluminescence (PL), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), transient photocurrent measurement, and XPS, it was found that surface oxygen functionalities (–OH, –COOH, and CO) affect the formation pathways of free radicals and the adsorption of substrate onto the biochar surface. Polar groups enhance hydrogen bonding with pesticides, while non-polar graphitic groups increase π–π interactions.119,206,207 Moreover, pore architecture has direct implications for both photocatalytic accessibility and mass-transport kinetics. Pores that possess a high specific surface area and hierarchically structured meso- and macro-pores enhance the ability of pesticide molecules to diffuse toward catalytic sites, while excess microporosity can result in steric hindrance and hinder access to ROS. Regarding the mechanism, investigations comparing BET surface areas and kinetic rates have found that hierarchical pores yield higher apparent rate constants because adsorption and diffusion occur simultaneously within the pore structure.172,206 This coupled adsorption-reaction mechanism conforms to the principles of Langmuir–Hinshelwood kinetics, which suggests that adsorption equilibrium governs the degradation rate.208

Conductivity and the degree of graphitization of biochar play a key role in modulating the charge-transfer process within composites. Raman analysis, along with EIS experiments conducted in a few studies, revealed that biochar with a high degree of graphitization exhibits lower charge-transfer resistance and suppress electron–hole recombination.209–211 Conductive biochar is involved in the rapid transport of photogenerated electrons from the conduction band of metal oxide@biochar heterojunctions, thereby enhancing the generation of ˙O2− and ˙OH radicals. The structures of defect-rich biochar with PFRs and quinone groups can undergo redox cycles and produce ROS under light irradiation.212–214

The correlations between catalyst structure and performance have also been demonstrated experimentally. For instance, oxygen-containing biochar can facilitate the oxidative degradation of electron-rich pesticides by generating more ˙OH radicals.215 Conductive graphitic biochar favours electron transfer-based reduction processes in nZV@biochar composites mainly due to its excellent conductivity, especially for halogen-containing pesticides that require dechlorination reactions. For SAC@biochar composites, doped heteroatomic coordination structures such as Fe–N4 and Co–N4 enable the selective activation of the target bond without triggering side reactions. Together, these results indicate that photocatalysis efficiency does not depend solely on the pesticide itself but rather on the interplay between pesticide structure and the physicochemical properties of the biochar matrix. Concerning steric and diffusion effects, larger pesticides with bulky molecular dimensions tend to face severe diffusion limitations through the biochar porous architecture, where steric hindrance directly limits access of target molecules to the embedded catalytic active sites. Supporting this, Shanaah et al.,172 further demonstrated that the introduction of hierarchical porosity (accessibility from meso- and macro-sized pores) significantly enhances molecular accessibility, but too much microporosity can ironically hinder the degradation of larger pesticide molecules because of size-exclusion effects, indicating that careful pore engineering is crucial in catalyst design. In terms of catalyst-specific interactions, metal oxide@biochar systems mainly function via electron-accelerated oxidative processes mediated by reactive oxygen species (ROS), including hydroxyl radicals (˙OH) and superoxide (˙O2−), thus rendering them also effective towards the transformation of electron-rich pesticides and polar compounds. Mechanistically, this improved performance is supported by increased charge separation and efficient radical formation occurring at the metal oxide@biochar heterojunction interface.173 Alternatively, nano zero-valent metal (ZVM)@biochar systems enable reductive degradation pathways in which direct electron transfer from the zero-valent metal center makes them particularly effective for halogenated pesticides where reductive dehalogenation is necessary, such as the cleavage of carbon-chlorine (C–Cl) bonds via R–Cl + e− → R˙ + Cl−. Finally, a unique strategy characterized by the development of single-atom catalyst (SAC)@biochar systems has emerged as the frontier of selective catalysis, where atomically dispersed metal sites provide precisely defined active centers to selectively activate specific chemical bonds such as C–N (amine/triazine linkages) and P–O (organophosphate bonds), thus allowing controlled reaction pathways that minimize the formation of unwanted toxic intermediates. This capacity is demonstrated in the study by Vigneshwaran et al.,202 showing that MOF-derived SAC-like systems exhibit selective catalytic performance with a high efficiency approaching 98% in linearly targeted pesticide degradation.

Although these mechanistic and structure–reactivity relationships offer important insights into catalyst performance, translating these complex interactions into the rational design of catalysts is still highly challenging by conventional experimental techniques alone. As a result, efforts have been directed to data-driven approaches and machine-learning strategies, which can speed up the catalyst optimization process and enable the prediction of degradation performance.

Data-driven design and predictive modelling of biochar nanocomposites toward pesticide degradation

The traditional approach for designing biochar-based nanocomposites includes iterative experimentation, which is resource-intensive and often non-generalizable. Recent progress has shown that ML and AI can find strong relationships among synthesis conditions, physicochemical properties, and degradation performance. However, the conventional design approach for biochar-based nanocomposites used in pesticide degradation depends extensively on empirical trial-and-error processes that are laborious and not always generalizable.219,220 For instance, J. Benavides-Hernandez et al. demonstrated the integration of machine learning and green chemistry principles to foster innovations in wastewater treatment technologies, highlighting the value of interdisciplinary approaches for developing sustainable environmental solutions. This work reviews the synergistic interplay between artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) approaches with high-throughput experimentation (HTE) for heterogeneous catalysis, highlighting strategies for catalyst discovery and optimization,221 as shown in Fig. 15.

Catalytic data analysis using end-to-end machine learning workflow. This workflow includes four stages carried out in succession: (1) exploration and validation, including data pre-processing, such as transformation, normalization, and featurization; (2) training, where ML models are trained; (3) testing, to validate the effectiveness of the model; and (4) application, which refers to deploying the model and its applications in practice.221

In another study, S. Kokonda et al. proposed a unified machine-learning approach that integrates reinforcement learning with a multi-stage Crystal Graph Convolutional Neural Network (CGCNN) funnel to identify photocatalysts optimized over multiple electronic and structural attributes. This study screened 120 000 MOF candidates with respect to multi-variable optimization over 13 key descriptors including band-gap suitability, CO2/H2O selectivity, adsorption energy and structural stability. Importantly, they successfully identified Cr- and Zn-based MOFs that show simultaneous improvements of light absorption, redox energetics, and framework durability relevant to environmental remediation and CO2 conversion.222

Recent research highlights the benefits of ML models over conventional regression methods in describing complex, nonlinear relationships between synthesis parameters and photocatalysis efficiency. For example, ANNs use multi-layer perceptron models with nonlinear activation functions (such as ReLU and sigmoid) and backpropagation-based optimization to model complex interactions between process conditions (e.g., pyrolysis temperature, precursor selection, and metal loading) and pesticide degradation rates. Moreover, Random Forest (RF) models use an ensemble of decision trees that are bootstrapped from the data and provide feature-importance metrics, thereby identifying primary factors such as pH, catalyst dosage, surface area, and pyrolysis temperature. Conversely, XGBoost is a form of ensemble learning using gradient-based optimization for accurate predictions with very high (typically above 90%) efficiency of photocatalytic pesticide degradation.156 A prominent study by Patel et al. overcame this by developing a predictive model for ZnO@biochar nanocomposites, which accurately predicts the degradation of multiple agrochemicals. Three ML models for predicting the % removal of contaminants through the photocatalytic degradation process were evaluated (Fig. 16). Input variables for the models include experimental data such as catalyst type, amount used, pollutant concentration, and pH value. Three parameters, the root mean square error (RMSE), the mean absolute error (MAE), and the coefficient of determination (R2), were utilized to quantify the effectiveness of all the models. The SVM model made very accurate predictions, almost matching the real data, while the ANN model gave very good results, with slight variations that reduced its accuracy slightly compared to the SVM model.156 Machine learning models have shown significant potential for photocatalytic treatments, as evidenced by their successful applications.

In regard to descriptors and feature engineering, most machine learning tools used in biochar@metal oxide systems have been based on experimental physicochemical parameters rather than descriptors computed from first-principles calculations. These descriptors include pyrolysis temperature, metal oxide loading, BET specific surface area, pore volume, pH value, pesticide concentration before treatment, irradiation period, and catalyst dosage.156,223 Nonetheless, these computational models are limited in their capacity to incorporate electronic descriptors from DFT calculations, including adsorption energy, band gap, and interfacial charge transfer barriers. Therefore, intricate phenomena such as the formation of heterojunctions and the separation of charges or radicals involved in pesticide degradation using biochar@metal oxide interfaces are still poorly understood in existing modeling approaches, limiting their application to realistic catalytic processes. The validation strategies used in current ML research include standard supervised learning approaches. Patel et al. employed train-test partitions (such as 70–30 or 80–20) to measure the performance of their models in terms of R2, RMSE, and MAE.156 External validation data sets or temporal validation approaches (validation on future data sets after training on previous experiments) are also used to examine temporal robustness.224 The accuracy of predictive models based on different performance indicators discussed in the literature suggests promising yet immature results for biochar@metal oxide nanocomposites. Nevertheless, these predictions have been achieved using a rather limited number of observations (from tens to hundreds). Furthermore, performance characteristics are usually expressed only in terms of degradation efficiency without considering important catalytic parameters such as turnover frequency (TOF), apparent quantum efficiency, or stability that are critical in real-world application.

While there have been many developments in machine learning technologies for use in environmental photocatalysis, only a few studies have explored this field systematically for application in the development of biochar@metal oxide systems for pesticide degradation. This limitation reveals a broader research gap in the field, as most previous works have been limited to traditional experimental studies of biochar@metal oxide composites for pesticide remediation. Notably, it is important to mention that current studies are mostly empirical correlations and do not include any fundamental physicochemical descriptors from density functional theory (DFT), limiting mechanistic insights into adsorption energetics and interfacial charge transfer processes. Furthermore, complex interfacial phenomena such as the formation of heterojunctions, charge species separation, or radical generation pathways occurring in biochar@metal oxide composites for pesticide removal are still lacking in currently established modelling frameworks, which restrict their applicability for predictive performance on relevant catalytic systems. It is clear that these limitations highlight an urgent need for the development of integrated, data-driven and multiscale modelling strategies involving DFT, ML and experimental validation, specifically focused on biochar@metal oxide nanocomposites, to fully harness their potential toward efficient and predictable pesticide degradation. Outlook and perspectives

Biochar-based photocatalytic nanocomposites have now progressed to a point where practical relevance rather than mere degradation percentages under ideal laboratory conditions must be the benchmark for future advancement. Subsequent steps in research should shift to standardized benchmarking, validation with real wastewater and transparent reporting of catalyst stability, regeneration (if applicable) and leaching data as these are the baseline characteristics required for fair comparison among different systems. Similarly, underexplored aspects such as energy demand, catalyst dosage and oxidant consumption and product coefficients should also be reported for the elucidation of material performance in combination with operational feasibility. Unified evaluation protocols should be a major priority. Future research should utilize similar contaminant concentrations, reaction times, illumination sources, pH levels and mineralization indices, together with repeated-cycle experiments and post-reaction structural characterization of catalysts after decontamination. This would address the existing challenge of fragmented datasets, enabling more reliable comparisons across diverse biochar-supported systems (metal oxide hybrids, redox-tunable composites, nano-zero-valent metals and single-atom catalysts). Simultaneously, routine testing under continuous-flow conditions and in real wastewater matrices should also be adopted, as batch experiments with model pollutants do not establish the practical applicability of these systems.

Another key direction is the coupling of experimental and data-driven approaches. High-quality datasets in which synthesis and reaction parameters are consistently reported allow for machine learning, statistical optimization, and multivariate analysis to identify the most significant factors affecting material performance. By combining these methodologies with operando characterization and mechanistic investigations, a more stringent link among structure, charge transfer and reactivity would be attainable. This is particularly important in the context of designing catalysts that can achieve a trade-off between activity, selectivity, stability and recoverability under realistic conditions. Further studies on life-cycle impacts and circular-economy principles should also be conducted from a sustainability point of view in the future. The desirability of biochar lies in its potential to upcycle waste biomass into useful and functional materials, but its true environmental benefit remains dependent on feedstock choice, pyrolysis conditions, reuse strategies and end-of-life management. A catalyst that has excellent performance but requires energy-intensive synthesis, excessive oxidant input, or high cost due to frequent replacement after only a few cycles is not a viable solution. Thus, a shift in the field to multi-objective optimization that balances performance, durability, cost and environmental burden simultaneously needs to be achieved. Ultimately, the only credible way of thinking ahead is to focus on biochar-based photocatalysts that can be used in practice rather than just being reported in the literature. This means that we need to characterize materials in terms of long-term stability, scalability, regeneration efficiency and reactor engineering compatibility. The field will only advance once mechanistic understanding, quantitative benchmarking and practical feasibility are treated with equal importance.

Conclusion

Biochar-based photocatalytic nanocomposites have evolved into a new class of materials for successful pesticide degradation, as they integrate waste valorization with advanced functionalities including versatile redox properties, enhanced charge separation and optimized structure–reactivity relationships. This review illustrates their versatility across a range of systems, from traditional metal oxide supports (TiO2@biochar, CuO@biochar, etc.) to complex redox-tunable hybrids, nano-zero-valent metal composites and single-atom catalyst frameworks, while putting them with the broader context of advanced oxidation processes. The elucidated pesticide structure-reactivity frameworks also provide a predictive lens for degradation pathways, and together with data-driven modeling, they accelerate rational catalyst design beyond empirical trial-and-error approaches. However, these materials cannot be directly compared across studies because they rely on standardized evaluation protocols to translate performance claims out of the laboratory. The next frontier will be integrating computational intelligence, high-throughput synthesis, and real-world deployment. Key challenges still exist to scale these waste-derived materials for field applications, increase visible-light efficiency and ensure long-term stability under complex environmental matrices. Therefore, unified benchmarking criteria should be adopted in future studies such as standard pollutant concentrations, reaction conditions, kinetic reporting and TOC mineralization, along with catalyst recovery, leaching and regeneration testing to allow meaningful comparison among different systems. Concurrently, hybrid experimental validation coupled with data-driven approaches (e.g., machine learning) and multi-objective optimization would facilitate accelerated catalyst discovery while minimizing the reliance on independent trial-and-error studies. These platforms have strong potential to enable circular-economy solutions to global pesticide contamination by aligning sustainability imperatives with innovative photocatalysis. There should also be a greater consideration of environmental and economic viability, especially regarding cost, energy use, catalyst reuse, and life-cycle implications. To be useful in typical analytics and models, future evaluations should include energy-per-order or similar efficiency metrics; quantify material and regeneration costs, (including the constituents of key synthesis) at scale; and identify links with environmental trade-offs across synthesis, operation and disposal stages.
