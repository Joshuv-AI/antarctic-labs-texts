Integration of customised LLM for discharge summary generation in real-world clinical settings: a pilot study on RUSSELL GPT
Authors: Chun En Chua, Ngoh Lee Ying Clara, Mohammad Shaheryar Furqan, James Lee Wai Kit, Andrew Makmur, Yih Chung Tham, Amelia Santosa, Kee Yuan Ngiam
Published: 2024

Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT (Open AI, USA) have received widespread attention and hold promise to revolutionize clinical medicine. One potential area for LLM application is to reduce administrative tasks in healthcare. Discharge summaries are essential in facilitating continuation of patient care by subsequent healthcare providers. At present, generation of a discharge summary is a manual, time consuming process with the burden usually falling on junior doctors. Discharge summaries are also of varying quality, depending on the seniority of the doctor and their level of involvement throughout the patient's admission, thus presenting a potential safety issues in care transition from hospital to primary care.1

While preliminary studies have demonstrated that ChatGPT-based discharge summaries perform well in accuracy of facts such as past medical history and presenting complaints,2,3 time savings4 and is of comparable quality to physician generated notes,4 deployment in clinical practice has not been possible in Singapore. This is because there is no ChatGPT/GPT-4 model deployed locally. The Singapore government as with many governments worldwide exercises great caution when managing healthcare data of citizens.5,6 Strict regulations govern where and how identifiable data is stored. Identifiable data must be stored within accredited infrastructure such as the healthcare data centre, local data centre or virtual private cloud (known as healthcare commercial cloud—residing in Singapore data centers). The use of overseas commercial cloud in an overseas data center requires data declassification. There are also further assessments if there is any integration with healthcare systems.

Based on these regulations, there are two options: either deploying a LLM within Singapore in healthcare commercial cloud, or anonymizing all outgoing medical records to a LLM housed in an overseas data center. We hence developed an in-house LLM application named Russell-GPT 1.0, leveraging on Anthropic Claude 2.1 LLM technology as the backbone.7 Russell-GPT 1.0 and its Claude model backbone (serviced via Amazon Web Service (AWS) Bedrock) are hosted locally within the Singapore Healthcare Commercial Cloud, an internet separated AWS virtual private cloud located in Singapore.7 The methodology for Russell-GPT 1.0 model development and validation can be found in Supplementary A.

In this pilot, we evaluated Russell-GPT 1.0's performance in comparison to discharge summaries manually generated by junior doctors in a medical department, in terms of time utility and medical accuracy. Additionally, we evaluated user perspectives and readiness for adoption by conducting qualitative surveys. Our findings offer preliminary insights into Russell-GPT 1.0's efficacy and its pitfalls as a tool for automated clinical documentation.

On average, a junior doctor took 10 min to write a traditional discharge summary, compared to 6 min when aided by Russell-GPT 1.0 (30 s for a summary to be generated, and 5 min 30 s to vet its contents). 19,300 discharges were completed by junior doctors in our hospital in 2023, which would have translated into 1285 man-hours saved annually. Interestingly, during the validation period, we saw an increase in user traffic growth accessing Russell-GPT 1.0 for purpose of trying the tool, but we advised most to continue with the traditional method of discharge summary writing (Supplementary D).

Rusell-GPT 1.0 and human written discharge summaries were compared in 30 patients admitted to 10 different medical subspecialties. The evaluators assessed that 100% of Russell-GPT 1.0 generated discharge summaries had accurate primary diagnoses, in comparison to 83% of human written summaries (p = 0.020) (Table 1). However, Russell-GPT 1.0 performed less favorably in clinical narrative, with only 50% of evaluators deeming AI generated documentation to have good clinical flow and reasoning, in comparison to nearly 100% of human written summaries (p = 0.05) (Table 1). One of the chief issues was that Russell-GPT 1.0 erroneously omitted listing 40% of treatments performed during the admission (Table 1). Evaluators rated about 80% of the missing or omitted content by the AI tool to have moderate clinical significance. In addition, evaluators felt that Russell-GPT 1.0 failed to convey clinical uncertainty and diagnostic challenges in patients with convoluted problems.

Two evaluators commented that there were 3 instances when the AI-generated discharge summaries produced text generations that were non-aligned with local cultural and societal practices, resulting in depersonalization of patient summaries. For example, the AI-generated note for a hypoglycaemia due to insulin-caloric intake mismatch by a Muslim patient during the Ramadan season who had been performing religious prayers, was summarized as hypoglycaemia due to inadequate oral intake.

The quantitative survey was completed by 9 junior doctors (Supplementary E). All of them exercised a cautious approach to adoption of this new tool and asserted that they would carefully vet any AI output. 90% opined that Russell-GPT 1.0 was inadequate to generate good quality discharge summaries and felt that physician supplementation was essential for clinical narrative. Nonetheless, they agreed that Russell-GPT 1.0 had several benefits, which included the provision of a standard template for discharge summaries and time savings.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first in Asia to deploy a customized and data-secure LLM for conducting quick pilot testing in a real-world hospital setting of heterogenous medical admissions. Russell-GPT 1.0 demonstrated significant time savings and improved accuracy in tabulation of primary and secondary diagnoses in automating discharge summaries. Physicians were cautiously optimistic that integrating this AI tool into operative workflows could improve the efficiency of their work. The time savings may allow physicians to dedicate more time to higher value clinical work.

However, this study revealed limitations with the Russell-GPT 1.0 model, as there were erroneous omissions of key information such as treatments received during admission. Such omissions can have great potential clinical implications and could lead to faulty decision making. Its performance was also inferior to clinicians in clinical reasoning. Other groups have started reporting methods of aligning LLMs to the safety-critical medical domain and clinical reasoning, such as instruction prompt tuning.8 These efforts have significantly improved the Flan-PaLM model's performance to medical assessments that employ long-form questioning, though it continues to remain inferior to medical experts in comprehension, knowledge retrieval and reasoning.8 Savage et al. have taken this one step further by reporting the use of diagnostic prompts to study GPT-4 in imitation of a physician's clinical reasoning process, which would mitigate ‘black-box’ limitations and explainability of LLMs.9

Curiously, we observed omissions in Russell-GPT 1.0's text generations but did not encounter frequent hallucinations leading to factually inaccurate statements. Nonetheless, there is an apparent need for the AI tool to communicate uncertainty estimates along with its responses. This is still an area of ongoing research. Preliminary data by Singhal et al. have suggested that selection prediction tasks (where the machine is given decodes matching a given answer as a measure of uncertainty, and told to withhold answer if the model is not appropriately confident), together with machine self-consistency, may make it possible to encode uncertainty for LLMs in the medical domain.8 Finally, clinicians must continue to exercise full responsibility and individual discretion for AI generated content.

Our study is the first to use real-world evaluation of a customized LLM for clinical summarisation. Medical admissions through the emergency department are heterogeneous (unlike elective surgical admits, which often follow a defined pathway). Studying the performance of an LLM model in such a setting allows us to truly test its potential. Preliminary data generated from this pilot study will furnish our product roadmap which develops Russell-GPT as a LLM aggregator platform that orchestrates and processes live event-driven and batch job data flow from our electronic medical record. We acknowledge however that the study has a small sample size, and that it would have benefited from a more comprehensive evaluation framework for evaluating AI output including potential for clinical harm and bias.

In summary, this real-world pilot study shows the clinical utility of Russell-GPT 1.0 for clinical administrative use cases, in particular discharge summaries. Much work remains to be done to create safe and accurate LLMs that can truly complement a physician's work. Nonetheless, we believe that the question, is how, and not if, we adopt this technology in clinical medicine.
