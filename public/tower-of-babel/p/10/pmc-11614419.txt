Effect of Preoperative Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index on Prognosis in Patients after Surgery for Lower Gastrointestinal Perforation
Authors: Makoto Hasegawa, Yohei Sanmoto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionGeriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) is a reliable index derived from serum albumin levels, height, and weight. Although various prognostic factors have been studied, the effect of preoperative nutritional status on surgical outcomes remains unexplored. This study aimed to evaluate the efficacy of the GNRI in predicting postoperative outcomes of lower gastrointestinal perforation.MethodsEighty patients treated at our institution between January 2016 and December 2022 were retrospectively analyzed. This study primarily focused on the correlation between preoperative GNRI and two key outcomes: postoperative hospital stay duration and 1-year mortality rate.ResultsOur findings revealed a significant association between low GNRI scores and increased 1-year mortality (odd ratio 4.0, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.1–16, p = 0.025). Kaplan-Meier analysis and log-rank test showed that patients in the low GNRI group had markedly poorer overall survival rates than those in the high GNRI group (12-month survival rate 0.88 [95% CI: 0.75–0.95] vs. 0.65 [95% CI: 0.47–0.78]; p = 0.018). Additionally, both univariate and multivariate analyses indicated that lower GNRI scores were associated with prolonged hospital stays.ConclusionWe showed that a low GNRI score was associated with high mortality and prolonged hospital stay after emergency surgery for lower gastrointestinal perforation.

Introduction

Lower gastrointestinal perforation is a hole or rupture in the lower part of the gastrointestinal tract and is caused by colon and rectal cancers or diverticulitis. It continues to be a critical condition with a high mortality rate of 16.0–19.6% [1–5]. Accurate and timely diagnosis and surgical control of sepsis caused by intestinal perforation are important [2, 6]. Factors associated with poor prognosis have been reported [1, 3, 7], such as older age, American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status (ASA-PS) 3 or higher, high preoperative Physiological and Operative Severity Score for the Enumeration of Mortality and Morbidity score, perforation combined with peritonitis, and large amount of intraoperative blood loss, low levels of total protein, albumin, hemoglobin, various blood cell ratios. A low skeletal muscle index and the prognostic nutritional index are significant indicators of poor prognosis in patients with gastrointestinal perforation. Previous studies reported that sarcopenia is associated with high mortality and long hospital stay after surgery [8].

Malnutrition is defined as inadequate nutritional status [9] and contributes to impaired immune function [10]. In the context of highly inflammatory states, such as in perioperative patients, specific nutritional challenges arise, leading to an increased risk of malnutrition [11]. This highlights the importance of conducting nutritional assessments for all patients regardless of body mass index (BMI) [9]. Once malnutrition is identified, patients can benefit significantly from tailored nutritional interventions [9, 12–14].

Among various indicators available, appropriate indicators for assessing nutritional status in acute settings have been debated [9, 15]. The Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI) is a simple and accurate tool for predicting the risk of morbidity in hospitalized older patients [16] and is calculated from serum albumin, height, and body weight, the lower the serum albumin and the lower the body weight relative to the ideal body weight, the lower the GNRI. Four levels of nutrition-related risk are defined based on the tool: major risk (GNRI <82), moderate risk (GNRI 82–<92), low risk (GNRI 92–98), and no risk (GNRI >98) [16]. In the surgical setting, GNRI has been reported to be useful in predicting prognosis after surgeries for multiple traumas, cholangiocarcinoma, and rectal cancer [15, 17–19]. However, little is known about the relationship between GNRI and prognosis after surgery for lower gastrointestinal perforation.

This study aimed to investigate the association between preoperative GNRI and survival outcomes in patients with lower gastrointestinal perforation who underwent emergency surgery. We hypothesized that low GNRI is a poor prognostic factor for lower gastrointestinal perforation.

Methods

Patients and Data Collection

This study included all adult patients who underwent emergency surgery for lower gastrointestinal perforations at Takeda General Hospital between January 2016 and December 2022 with no other eligibility criteria. At our hospital, patients with lower gastrointestinal perforation receive immediate intervention from the nutrition support team upon admission. These patients undergo daily nutritional assessments throughout their hospital stay. Discharge criteria include the ability to consume oral nutrition and the absence of a requirement for intravenous antimicrobials or inpatient wound care. The treatment plan for lower gastrointestinal perforation has remained consistent throughout the observation period.

The following data were recorded for each patient: age; sex; ASA-PS; height; weight; BMI; mean arterial pressure; preoperative vital signs such as body temperature, mean arterial pressure, pulse rate; and blood biochemistry test results. In addition, we recorded intraoperative and postoperative information such as operation time, intraoperative blood loss, whether the perforation was associated with malignancy, perforation site (cecum: ascending: transverse: descending: sigmoid: rectum), grade III or higher complications based on the Clavien-Dindo classification, postoperative hospital days, and death within 1 year after surgery. GNRI was calculated in each patient as described below. The short-term outcome focused on evaluating the relationship between the GNRI score and the number of postoperative hospital days. The long-term outcome was the relationship between the GNRI score and death within 1 year of surgery. For patients who discontinued their outpatient visits to our hospital within 1 year, we contacted the patients or their family members by telephone to ascertain their status 1 year later.

Calculation of GNRI

We used the following formula: GNRI = (14.89 × serum albumin [g/dL]) + (41.7 × present body weight [kg]/ideal body weight [kg]). The ideal weight was calculated using the Lorentz equation (WLo) as follows:

For men: height (cm) − 100 − {[height (cm)−150]/4}.

For women: height (cm) – 100 − {[height (cm)–150]/2.5}.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians and interquartile ranges for nonparametric data. Univariate relationships were analyzed using the χ2 test for categorical variables, but the Fisher test was used when the expected value of the subject in a single category was less than five and within the Mann-Whitney U test for nonparametric data.

Overall survival after the emergency surgery was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, and survival curves were compared using the log-rank test. Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to examine factors associated with prolonged hospital stays. In both univariate and multivariate analyses, continuous explanatory variables were converted into nominal variables using the median as the cut-off point. Postoperative hospitalization days were converted into logarithmic format for the multivariate analysis to ensure normality. For multivariate analysis, we selected independent variables including the GNRI, which is the primary focus of this study, and C-reactive protein (CRP) to assess inflammation. Basic demographic factors such as age and sex were also included. The ASA-PS, which has been identified as a poor prognostic factor in previous studies, was also included. In this study, we classified GNRI into two groups, using a cut-off value of 92, distinguishing moderate from low nutritional risk, based on prior studies that classified GNRI into 2–3 categories [17–19].

All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan), a graphical user interface for R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Specifically, EZR is a modified version of R commander and was designed to add statistical functions commonly used in biostatistics. The threshold for significance was set at p values <0.05.

Results

GNRI and Patient Characteristics

Eighty patients were included in the study, and 35 were in the low GNRI group and 45 were in the high GNRI group. The study sample included more males than females (46:34), with a median age of 77.5 years (interquartile range: 66–83). Seventeen patients (21.3%) had a preoperative ASA-PS of 3 or higher. Malignancy was the cause of perforation in 22 (27.5%) patients. The most common site of perforation was the sigmoid colon (53 cases, 66.3%), followed by the rectum, ascending colon, transverse colon, descending colon, and cecum. There were no significant differences in age, sex, ASA-PS, body temperature, mean atrial pressure, pulse rate, white blood cell count, serum blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, serum sodium, serum potassium, operation time, intraoperative blood loss, grade III or higher complications based on the Clavien-Dindo classification. However, compared with the high GNRI group, the low GNRI group had fewer obese patients (p < 0.001), higher rates of tumor-related perforation (p = 0.010) and hypoalbuminemia (p < 0.001), higher CRP levels (p = 0.0017), higher rates of hypohemoglobinemia (p < 0.001), lower hematocrit levels (p < 0.001), higher platelet counts (p = 0.011), longer postoperative hospital stay (p = 0.034), and more deaths within 1 year of surgery (p = 0.014) (Table 1).

Univariate Analysis for Death within 1 Year of Surgery

Factors associated with death within 1 year of surgery included older age (odd ratio 6.4, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.5–38, p = 0.0051), low albumin level (odd ratio 7.3, 95% CI 1.8–44, p = 0.0019), low serum potassium level (odd ratio 3.7, 95% CI 1.0–18, p = 0.030), lower intraoperative blood loss (odd ratio 6.4, 95% CI 1.5–38, p = 0.0051) (p = 0.026), grade III or higher complications based on the Clavien-Dindo classification (Odd ratio 412, 95% CI 2.9–60, p < 0.001), and low GNRI (odd ratio 4.0, 95% CI 1.1–16, p = 0.025) (Table 2). Due to the small number of deaths at 1 year in 17 patients, multivariate analysis could not be performed.

Kaplan-Meier and Cox Regression Analysis for Overall Survival

The median follow-up period was 12 months. Kaplan-Meier analysis and the log-rank test showed that patients in the low GNRI group had significantly worse prognosis in terms of overall survival than those in the high GNRI group (12-month survival rate 0.88 [95% CI: 0.75–0.95] vs. 0.65 [95% CI: 0.47–0.78]; p = 0.018) (shown in online suppl. Univariate and Multivariate Analyses for Hospital Stay after Surgery

Factors associated with a prolonged hospital stay after surgery included low BMI (p = 0.0029) and low GNRI (p = 0.034). Furthermore, multivariate regression analysis highlighted low GNRI as a significant predictor of mortality within 1 year of surgery (p = 0.012) (Table 3).

Discussion

The main study finding was the significant association between a low GNRI and 1-year postoperative mortality. Additionally, a lower GNRI score was linked to longer postoperative hospital stays.

A patient’s nutritional status is related to their prognosis [11–13]. However, in patients with severe conditions, assessing the nutritional status using a single indicator is often challenging because of the impact of severe inflammation and volume overload. Conversely, the GNRI is a tool that evaluates nutritional status by combining multiple parameters. Jia et al. highlighted that the combination of multiple parameters minimizes the confounding effect of hydration status and accurately predicts postoperative outcomes in older adult patients undergoing emergency surgery [15]. Furthermore, its components, such as albumin levels, height, and weight, are easily obtainable, making it a practical and effective method for nutritional assessment. Previous studies have similarly reported that low GNRI is associated with increased mortality in various medical conditions [15, 16, 18–21]. However, the relationship between a low GNRI score and adverse outcomes following emergency surgery for lower gastrointestinal perforation has not been well established. This study is the first to report the prognostic impact of GNRI scores in these patients.

Other factors significantly associated with mortality within 1 year of surgery include older age, low albumin level, low serum potassium level, and grade III or higher complications based on the Clavien-Dindo classification, consistent with previous reports [1, 3, 7, 8]. In contrast, our findings differ from those of a previous study that reported high intraoperative blood loss was associated with high mortality following surgery for colorectal perforation [1]. High blood loss destabilizes hemodynamics, leading to increased perioperative intravenous fluid and blood transfusions; this exacerbates the body’s inflammatory response, ultimately worsening patient prognosis [1]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the difficulty in accurately measuring blood loss during surgery for lower gastrointestinal perforation, where intraoperative peritoneal lavage, presence of ascites, and gastrointestinal contents leaking into the abdominal cavity complicate precise measurement. Additionally, the small sample size of this study prevented us from performing a multivariate analysis of mortality and could have introduced confounding factors.

In this study, patients with low GNRI, indicating poor nutritional status, had significantly longer hospital stays. Additionally, low BMI was associated with prolonged hospital stay, which is consistent with previous reports [22, 23]. Malnutrition leads to impaired immune function, increased susceptibility to infection, poor wound healing, and muscle weakness [10]. These conditions can further escalate to more serious outcomes, such as falls and fractures in older individuals who are particularly vulnerable to malnutrition [10]. In our study, patients with more severe malnutrition experienced prolonged hospital stays owing to further decline in activities of daily living and the occurrence of complications. Importantly, nutritional assessment and intervention are recommended for patients regardless of their weight or height, especially during periods of high inflammation such as the perioperative period [11]. Once malnutrition is identified, patients can greatly benefit from tailored nutritional interventions [9, 12–14].

The proportion of tumor-related perforations was significantly higher in the low GNRI group than in high GNRI group (42.9% vs. 15.6%). Considering that intestinal obstruction and gastrointestinal perforation due to gastrointestinal cancer are mainly observed in advanced cancer, the prolonged cancer-bearing state is potentially associated with the progression of malnutrition in these patients. Cancer patients often experience malnutrition because of various factors, including chronic systemic inflammation. This inflammation can lead to severe, involuntary loss of skeletal muscle mass, with or without loss of fat mass, by enhancing catabolic reactions [24]. In our study, patients with tumor-related perforation experienced progression of cancer-associated malnutrition, leading to hypoalbuminemia and weight loss. This may lead to decreased GNRI and ultimately contribute to poorer patient outcomes.

This study had some limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study, which could introduce bias; thus, the results may not represent the general population. Second, the smaller number of patients analyzed prevented us from performing a multivariate analysis on mortality, leading to potential confounding factors. Last, the CRP values in this study were significantly higher in the low GNRI group, potentially influencing the lower albumin levels, which are a component of the GNRI [10, 12, 25]. However, GNRI is a nutritional indicator that combines multiple parameters, which may help minimize this influence [15].

In conclusion, GNRI is a reliable and simple index. It is derived from serum albumin levels, height, and weight. We showed that a low GNRI score was associated with high mortality and prolonged hospital stay after emergency surgery for lower gastrointestinal perforation. Determination of the GNRI score before surgery for lower gastrointestinal perforation may contribute to appropriate nutritional interventions. Further research, ideally prospective, is warranted to confirm the results of this study.
