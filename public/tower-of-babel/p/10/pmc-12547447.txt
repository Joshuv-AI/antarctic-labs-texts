Impact of Boarding Time on In-Hospital Mortality in Patients Presenting to an Oncologic Emergency Department of a Comprehensive Cancer Center
Authors: Patricia A. Brock, Denise M. Langabeer, Valda D. Page, Ziyi Li, Aiham Qdaisat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesLonger boarding time in emergency departments (EDs) has been associated with negative clinical outcomes, including higher mortality rates. Studies of boarding to date have been conducted in the general population but not in the cancer population. In this study, we evaluated the association between mortality and boarding time in patients presenting at an oncologic ED.MethodsThis retrospective study included all ED visits for patients aged ≥19 years who presented to The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center ED between March 3, 2016 and December 31, 2022, with a disposition of hospital admission. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed to determine the associations between boarding time and in-hospital mortality.ResultsThe final patient cohort included 43,308 patients. The median boarding time was 2.7 hours (interquartile range = 1.5-5.2 hours), with an in-hospital mortality rate of 7.0% (n = 3029). Death occurred in 6.9% of patients (n = 2997) within 14 days of ED presentation and increased to 13.7% (n = 5941) within 30 days. Patients with boarding times ≥5.2 hours had a 24% higher odds of in-hospital mortality compared with patients with <1.5 hours (odds ratio = 1.24; 95% confidence interval = 1.11-1.38; P < .001) after controlling for common clinical and cancer-related factors.ConclusionLonger ED boarding times were associated with higher in-hospital mortality rates after hospital admission. These results emphasize the need for improved strategies to expedite the admission process and optimize care transitions for cancer patients in the ED to reduce mortality rates, especially for patients with complex medical needs and high-acuity presentations.

The Bottom LineThis research studied the effect of emergency department (ED) boarding times on patients with cancer admitted to the hospital. Those who had longer boarding times had a higher risk of short-term mortality when compared with patients with shorter boarding times, as evidenced by a 24% higher in-hospital mortality among patients with boarding times ≥5.2 hours versus those with <1.5 hours. The findings highlight the urgency of improving the efficiency of the admission processes and enhancing care transitions for patients with cancer to improve boarding times and reduce mortality after ED visits.

Introduction

Background

Emergency Departments (EDs) serve as a critical link for addressing unplanned or unscheduled acute patient care needs. The importance of safe care transitions has been recognized as an essential element of health care quality and patient safety.1 In the ED setting, a common transition is to an inpatient unit, especially for cancer patients, who have high hospital admission rates.2 When this transition time is excessive, it is known as “boarding,” which is defined by the American College of Emergency Physicians as the time a patient remains in the ED after an admission disposition has been made to the time of transfer to an inpatient bed.3

Importance

Prolonged boarding in the ED is a critical issue that significantly compromises patient care and adds significant pressure on the healthcare system.4 It has been associated with poor patient outcomes, including delayed treatment,5 higher mortality rates,6, 7, 8 longer hospital stays,8,9 and increased complications in patients with already acute presentations. Several professional medical associations wrote to the US President in 2022, outlining ED boarding as a worsening national public health crisis that is believed to result in patient harm, including death.10

Patients with cancer have a high symptom burden; thus, it is not surprising that they use ED services at a higher rate, as noted in the National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey.11 Unfortunately, for all patients who present in the ED, long boarding times continue to be a public health issue across the United States. Research over the last 20-plus years has shown that extensive boarding times are associated with negative patient outcomes, including mortality, and contribute to longer hospital and intensive care unit stays.6, 7, 8,12, 13, 14 It has been estimated that between 4% and 5.4% of patient encounters in the ED involve patients diagnosed with cancer.11,15 Patients with cancer have complex clinical presentations and high acuity; thus, they are more likely to experience high rates of hospital admission when presenting to the ED.16

Goals of This Investigation

Although research has demonstrated that extensive ED boarding times can negatively impact patient outcomes in the general population, there is a deficit in understanding the influence of boarding time on patients with cancer presenting to the ED. Therefore, this study was conducted to determine the association between boarding time and mortality in an oncologic ED.

Methods

ED Setting, Patient Cohort, and Data Collection

The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (Houston, Texas) is an urban, academic National Cancer Institute–designated comprehensive cancer center with 760 inpatient beds; it provides care to nearly 180,000 patients a year, including several surgical specialty services. MD Anderson includes a fully functional 41-bed ED, which specializes in emergent and urgent medical needs in patients diagnosed with cancer. Almost all patients visiting the ED have an existing cancer diagnosis or a high suspicion of cancer.2,17 Most patients get either admitted, discharged, or placed in observation, with less than 0.3% of the patients being transferred to another institution/facility. The ED admits nearly 50% of all hospital admissions, with nearly all admissions presumed to be related to the patient’s oncology diagnosis. This ED is staffed by the Department of Emergency Medicine faculty, moonlighters, registered nurses, patient care technicians, and service coordinators. Structured clinical data were extracted using SAP Business Objects BI Platform 4.2 Version: 14.2.9.4473 (SAP SE), which links to the institution’s electronic medical record system (Epic, Epic Systems Corporation). No manual chart review or individual patient record abstraction was performed.

Data were collected on all consecutively treated patients who were at least 19 years of age and who visited MD Anderson’s ED between March 3, 2016 and December 31, 2022, with a disposition of being admitted. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) age ≤18 years; (2) patients who did not have an admission decision in the ED; (3) patients who were placed in the observation unit; (4) patients who had other dispositions, including leaving against medical advice, transferring to another hospital, and dying in the ED; (5) patients who were admitted directly to the intensive care unit; (6) patients who had no recorded inpatient stay; (7) patients for whom admission orders were placed prior to ED presentation; (8) patients with missing boarding time data; (9) patients with boarding times >48 hours (mainly due to identified discrepancies in recorded time stamps); and (10) preventive or follow-up presentations including advice only, follow-up, research, review of laboratory results only, travel consult, medication refill, dressing change, and annual examination. Patients admitted directly to the ICU or placed in the observation unit from the ED were excluded to prevent potential confounding and to have a homogenous cohort. Patients placed directly in the ICU have critical conditions that often necessitate immediate and intensive interventions, whereas those placed in the observation unit are typically expected to be discharged within 24 to 48 hours without any complications. By focusing on patients who were ultimately admitted to general care units minimized the influence of varying levels of care on boarding times.

The study was approved by the institution’s Institutional Review Board and was granted a waiver of informed consent.

Data Exploration and Statistical Analysis

Patients’ demographics and clinical characteristics were examined using standard descriptive statistics. As some patients had multiple visits, one visit for each patient was selected randomly as the indexed visit for further analysis. In accordance with the American College of Emergency Physicians, boarding time for each visit was defined as the difference measured in hours between the time the physician made a “disposition to admit” order to the patient’s ED departure. Sensitivity analysis using different randomly selected indexed visits was conducted for the main analysis to examine the robustness of the findings. Missing data were uncommon in this study, and data points with missing values (ie, race, ethnicity, and emergency severity index) were labeled as unknown or unspecified. The chi-square test was applied to compare categoric data, whereas the nonparametric Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test was employed for continuous variables, given the lack of normality assumption across all examined continuous variables. A univariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify associations between clinical factors, including boarding time in hours, Charlson comorbidity index, acuity levels, and in-hospital mortality rate. The analyses of this study incorporated clinical and cancer-related predictor variables that are known to be associated with short-term mortality following ED visits, along with other patient characteristics. Boarding time was categorized into 4 quartiles (quartile model) based on the 1st quartile (25th percentile), median (50th percentile), and 3rd quartile (75th percentile)—to facilitate group comparisons across its distribution. Additionally, boarding time was examined as a dichotomous measure (dichotomous model) defined as short (≤4 hours) or long (>4 hours) boarding time. To further examine the association between boarding time and in-hospital mortality, potential predictor variables that are known to be associated with in-hospital mortality following hospital admission from the ED alongside variables with P value < 0.10 in the univariate analysis were included in multivariable logistic regression models serving as the primary analysis, reporting the odds ratio (OR) and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI). The association between boarding time and 30-day mortality was also examined using a multivariable logistic regression model as secondary analysis. In addition to boarding time, the final multivariable models incorporated the following variables: age, sex, race, Charlson comorbidity index, cancer type, acuity level/emergency severity index, and recent chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or surgery/invasive procedures. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.3.1 (The R Foundation). A 2-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Limitations

A key limitation of this study is its retrospective nature. As the analysis is based on electronic health data, it may lack some relevant clinical information not routinely recorded in the ED, including performance status and cancer stage at the time of the ED visit. Additionally, ED admitting diagnoses were not included due to their preliminary and often broad nature, which are frequently revised during hospitalization, which could introduce misclassification. Future chart reviews focusing on specific diagnoses may help clarify how ED boarding time impacts outcomes across different clinical conditions. Further, the study design did not allow to control for potential biases related to admission prioritization, which may vary by patient condition or treatment status. This unmeasured variable and other confounders that were not controlled for may have influenced the observed association between boarding time and mortality, warranting further investigation in future studies. Although missing data were relatively limited, it is acknowledged that certain clinical parameters that are documented in the ED, such as the diagnosis, may still lack complete or detailed information and therefore were not collected or analyzed. Deaths that occurred outside the institution, such as at home or in different hospitals, may also not have been recorded. Although the institution routinely tracks the vital status of patients who receive care within the cancer center, it is possible that some cases were not captured, potentially leading to an underestimation of mortality rates. Similarly, cancer treatment administered outside at other institutions may not have been accounted for, limiting the accuracy and completeness of patient treatment records. Additionally, due to the specialized nature of the ED, which primarily serves patients with cancer, establishing an appropriate control group of noncancer patients was not feasible, limiting the ability to compare outcomes between these populations. Only one ED visit per patient was also included to reduce the influence of intra-patient correlation and avoid overrepresenting frequent ED users, which could bias population-level estimates of boarding risk; however, it is acknowledged that repeat visits may reflect evolving behaviors or clinical needs, and future studies should examine how repeated ED exposure affects both boarding risk and care-seeking trends over time. Moreover, the analysis included patients who were under hospice care or receiving comfort measures prior to ED presentation. For these patients, hospice care is revoked upon ED presentation to manage acute conditions, and thus, they were included in the analysis. Although it is acknowledged that this may have impacted treatment decisions and outcomes, this reflected the actual clinical workflows. Further, this study was conducted at a single institution with a specialized oncologic ED, which may limit the generalizability of these study findings to other healthcare settings or patient populations.

Discussion

Optimizing throughput processes that equate to improved boarding times in an emergency setting is essential for delivering timely and comprehensive care to patients with highly acute illnesses, especially those diagnosed with cancer. The results of this study demonstrate that patients with a boarding time of >5.2 hours are at a 24% higher risk (95% CI, 1.11-1.38; P < .001) of in-hospital mortality than are those with a boarding time of <1.5 hours, after controlling for age, sex, race, Charlson comorbidity index, cancer type, cancer therapy, and acuity level. The odds of 30-day mortality were also higher among patients with longer boarding times. These findings demonstrate a significant association between longer boarding time and short-term mortality.

With over 350 million acute care visits annually,18 including nearly 140 million ED visits,19 EDs, and acute or urgent care centers form the foundation of the acute care delivery system. However, they face challenges in meeting increasing patient care demands across the United States.20 This has also been observed globally, where EDs are not capable of meeting patients’ increasing acute care needs.21,22 Furthermore, rising hospital occupancy rates and constraints on hospital resources have created a harmful cycle, resulting in longer boarding times in the ED and, as a consequence, extended overall ED length of stay, which is defined as the time from ED presentation to ED departure (note: ED boarding time is sometimes mistakenly used interchangeably with ED length of stay, but it is a component of the latter; the two are strongly correlated). Length of stay is considered one of the main critical metrics that provides valuable insights into ED efficiency and operational bottlenecks.23, 24, 25, 26 Longer ED length of stay has been associated with worse patient outcomes, heightened risks of complications, patient dissatisfaction, and increased healthcare costs for both patients and institutions.27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32 In 2022, the Emergency Department Benchmarking Alliance reported an average ED length of stay of 211 minutes, with an upward trend from previous years.33 This finding aligns with data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services, which reported a median ED length of stay of 162 minutes.34 The data also highlight significant variations in the median length of stay across the United States, with North Dakota reporting a median of 108 minutes and Washington, DC, showing a median of 320 minutes.35

Along with laboratory and imaging investigations and consultations,36,37 boarding time is an important independent variable that influences ED length of stay. Prolonged boarding in the ED indicates an overloaded health system, which is often closely linked with a high inpatient census, further reflecting a system-wide capacity strain.4,20,38,39 In the ED, this system failure impacts the quality of care, timeliness of care, patient and provider satisfaction, and patient safety concerns.40 Excessive boarding times can contribute to ED overcrowding and cascade to other concerns,4 including an increase in left without being seen (ie, ED walkaway) rates, an inability to accept appropriate ED to ED transfers due to overcapacity issues, and ultimately diversion in the inpatient setting, which prevents patients from being accepted in the hospital.

In the general population, several studies have examined the impact of extended boarding time and its association with different outcomes. Patients with prolonged boarding times had longer hospital stays,8,9 higher mortality rates,6, 7, 8 longer intensive care unit,12 increased treatment costs,12 and experienced delays in treatment that exacerbated their acute presenting conditions.5

Comorbidities and cancer-related factors among cancer patients lead to a variety of complications, causing cancer patients to be frequent visitors to the ED.2,15,41 Their care and outcomes in the ED differ from those of the general population.42, 43, 44 However, the impact of boarding time on their outcomes, including in-hospital mortality, has not been thoroughly studied in the existing literature.

In this study, the investigators found that longer boarding time was a significant predictor of in-hospital mortality, particularly when considered alongside other factors, including patients’ comorbidities and acuity levels. Although acuity levels and comorbidities are known predictors of poor outcomes in cancer patients visiting the ED, the findings from this study indicate that increased boarding times lead to greater risks, with a clear trend in which the longer boarding times are associated with higher in-hospital mortality rates. Additionally, the findings highlight that boarding times in cancer patients visiting the ED are not only a measure of quality of care and resource utilization but also a significant predictor of patient outcome.

In conclusion, this study found an association between boarding time and in-hospital mortality in patients who presented to an oncologic ED. Patients with longer boarding times were more likely to experience mortality during their hospital admission. High comorbidity scores and high-risk emergency severity index scores were also identified as risk factors. Although the effect size for the association between boarding time and in-hospital mortality was modest in this study, the authors believe this is the first study to explore the relationship between emergency department (ED) boarding time and short-term survival in cancer patients, offering valuable initial insights into this issue within a uniquely vulnerable population. These results emphasize the need for improved strategies to expedite the admission process and optimize care transitions for cancer patients in the ED to reduce mortality rates, especially for patients with complex medical needs and high-acuity presentations. Further research and interventions targeting a reduction in boarding times are warranted to improve outcomes for this vulnerable population.
