Hemoglobin Oxygen Affinity Does Not Change in the Canine Model of Moderate Acute Normovolemic Hemodilution
Authors: Ming Sheng, Weidan Li, Guoxing You, Ying Wang, Penglong Li, Shiyu Tao, Daoyuan Gao, Hong Zhou, Lian Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objective: As the amount of hemoglobin (Hb) decreases during moderate acute normovolemic hemodilution (ANH), it is unclear whether the Hb-oxygen affinity (HOA) changes accordingly. Thus, this study aimed to explore changes in HOA during moderate ANH. Methods: A moderate ANH model of canine was established. Hemodynamic, blood gas, biochemical, oxygenation, and HOA-related parameters were measured. P 50 was obtained using three methods. One was derived from the oxygen dissociation curve (ODC) by the Bloodox-2018 analyzer, and the other two were based on blood gas analyzers. Results: With decreased Hb and hematocrit (HCT) levels during ANH, the temperature (T) and base excess (BE) decreased, while Cl– increased. Compared with baseline (BL), in hemodilution 1 (HCT decreased to 75% of the BL, H1) and hemodilution 2 (HCT decreased to 50% of the BL, H2), pH and HCO3 – decreased. Methemoglobin (MetHb) levels in H2 were significantly higher than those in BL and H1. HOA-related parameters did not change significantly in moderate ANH. Factors that increase or decrease the P 50 offset each other, leaving the P 50 unchanged in moderate ANH. The P 50 obtained based on the two blood gas analyzers was consistent, but neither was consistent with P 50 derived from the ODC.

Introduction

Autotransfusion is a safe and reliable alternative for patients who undergo blood transfusions during surgery. Acute normovolemic hemodilution (ANH) for autotransfusion is widely used in clinical settings. ANH is a blood conservation strategy used to reduce the need for allogeneic blood transfusion during the perioperative period and the risk of complications associated with perioperative allogeneic blood transfusions in both cardiac and noncardiac surgeries. During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) epidemic, blood donation was limited, and surgical operations became difficult. , ANH plays a critical role in reducing the demand for allogeneic blood transfusions.

ANH refers to withdrawing a specific amount of whole blood from a patient and replacing it with crystal/colloid mixtures to maintain the blood volume. Extracted blood will be infused back into the same patient if needed during the operation, and the remaining blood will be completely returned to the patient by the end of the operation. According to the hematocrit (HCT) level, ANH can be classified as mild (HCT >30%), moderate (20% <HCT <30%), severe (10% <HCT <20%), and extreme (HCT <10%), and moderate ANH is commonly used in the clinical setting.

In a previous study, rats in a septic shock model had significantly different oxygen delivery capacity compared to normal rats. Hill coefficient decreased from 2.30 to 1.99 and P 50 increased from 41.40 to 51.17 Torr. Rats in the hemorrhagic shock model showed increased P 50 with increased severity. As the amount of hemoglobin (Hb) decreases during ANH, it is unclear whether Hb functions, especially its oxygen-carrying–releasing capacity. The oxygen-carrying–releasing capacity of Hb is expressed by its oxygen affinity (HOA). When the oxygen saturation of Hb reached 50%, the partial pressure of oxygen at this point was termed as P 50, reflecting the HOA. The P 50 value can be obtained from the oxygen dissociation curve (ODC), which plots the relationship between the oxygen saturation of Hb and partial pressure of oxygen. The cooperative effect of Hb results in an S-shaped ODC. The increase and decrease in P 50 represent the decrease and increase in HOA, respectively.

HOA has been found to be involved in many pathophysiological processes. Low HOA with high P 50 was found to enhance oxygen supply in isolated cardiac muscle, increase survival time in sepsis mice, improve exercise abilities of mice with heart failure, and enhance radiosensitivity of mice with RIF-1 sarcoma. High HOA with low P 50 was found to increase arterial oxygen saturation during hypoxia without affecting exercise ability.

Therefore, this study aimed to explore whether HOA changes during moderate ANH. Beagles were used to investigate HOA because they have relatively stable biological characteristics and minimal differences between individuals. They also share many characteristics with humans, especially regarding the cardiovascular and immune systems. In terms of model selection, moderate hemodilution is more closely related to the clinical setting, which helps explore changes in HOA. Therefore, we established a canine model of moderate ANH and monitored a series of related parameters, including hemodynamic, blood gas, biochemical, and oxygenation parameters. The P 50 derived from the ODC and blood gas analysis at different phases was obtained, and correlations between them were analyzed. The theoretical oxygen-release capacities and Hill coefficients in different phases were also calculated.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Ten nontransgenic male beagles (9.94 ± 0.76 kg) aged 11–15 months old were obtained from Beijing Rixin Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All animals were kept under the same housing and husbandry conditions, and man-made disturbances such as noise and unfavorable temperature were avoided to ensure that animals were in good conditions to ensure the reliability of repetitive experiments in individual animals. All beagles were kept in the animal facility (Laboratory Animal Use Permit: SYXK 2017-0023; issuing agency: Health Administration of the Logistic Support Department of the Central Military Commission). Animals were fed twice a day with 200 g of beagle feed provided by the Research Animal Center of the Academy of Military Medical Sciences. Automatic drinking machines were used in this study for the water supply. Tools were sanitized once a week, and cages were washed twice daily. Animals were housed individually in separate cages (length: 90 cm, wide: 70 cm, height: 80 cm). The environmental conditions were as follows: temperature of 20–24 °C, humidity of 40–70%, and 24 h simulated day–night cycle. Before the study, no animals were included in other studies. All animals were fasted for 12 h before the experiment with free access to water. The Laboratory Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of the Laboratory Animal Center of the Academy of Military Medical Sciences approved this study (Approval No. SYXK-2017-0023).

Anesthesia and Surgery

All animals were anesthetized with propofol (0.8 mL/kg, Libang Pharmaceutical, Xi’an, China). After endotracheal intubation, anesthesia was maintained with 2–3% isoflurane (Zhongshidichuang, Beijing, China). The gas flow was controlled at 1–2.5 L/min.

The limbs were immobilized in the supine position, and the right jugular vein, left femoral vein, and bilateral femoral arteries were separated. Both femoral arteries were catheterized: one for monitoring and the other for arterial blood collection. A catheter was inserted into the femoral vein for fluid infusion. The right jugular vein catheter was inserted approximately 7 cm to collect samples and injected with 4 °C saline (No.4 Pharmaceutical, Shijiazhuang, China).

Canine Model of ANH

This study was an independent replication experiment. The experimental flowchart is shown in Figure . After intubation, the animals were stabilized for 15 min. The approximate blood volume was 8.5 mL/100 g body weight. Blood was drawn until blood loss was 20% of the total blood volume. The time required for blood collection was 15 min. At the same time, the same volume (blood loss) of the crystal-colloid mixture (hydroxyethyl starch 130/0.4 sodium chloride injection, 6% hydroxyethyl starch 130/0.4 and 0.9% sodium chloride, Shandong Weigao Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.) was reinfused into animals at a speed of 1 mL/min/kg body weight. The time when HCT decreased to approximately 75% of the BL was marked as H1. Later, with the same rate of blood drawing and reinfusion, HCT decreased to 50% of the BL as H2.

Physiological and Hemodynamic Measurements

Temperature (T), mean arterial pressure (MAP), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), pulse rate (PR), central venous pressure (CVP), cardiac output (CO), global end-diastolic volume (GEDV), and systemic vascular resistance (SVR) were continuously recorded by using a monitor (Infinity Delta XL, Dreger, Germany). By injecting 5 mL of 4 °C saline into a central venous catheter (CVC), it rapidly dispersed within the pulmonary and cardiac volumes. The resulting temperature change was detected using a thermistor on the proprioceptive arterial line, and the cardiac output was calculated. PiCCO index results were collected in all stages (BL, H1, and H2).

Blood Gas Measurements

Arterial and central venous blood was collected using a 1 mL syringe. Blood samples were analyzed using a blood gas analyzer (ABL90 FLEX; Radiometer, Copenhagen, Denmark). The following parameters were measured: total Hb, HCT, oxygen saturation in arterial blood (SaO2), oxygen saturation in central venous blood (ScvO2), partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (PaO2), partial pressure of oxygen in central venous blood (PcvO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood (PaCO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide in central venous blood (PcvCO2), methemoglobin (MetHb), base excess (BE), pH, HCO3 –, K+, Na+, Ca2+, Cl–, glucose (Glu), and lactate (Lac).

Calculation of Oxygenation Parameters

Femoral arterial and central venous blood samples were analyzed to obtain Hb, SaO2, ScvO2, PaO2, and PcvO2. Combined with CO detection in hemodynamics, the following formulas were used to calculate the oxygen content of arterial blood (CaO2), oxygen content in central venous blood (CcvO2), oxygen delivery (DO2), oxygen consumption (VO2), and oxygen extraction ratio (O2ext). − CaO2=(Hb×1.34×SaO2+PaO2×0.0031)×10(mLL−1) CcvO2=(Hb×1.34×ScvO2+PcvO2×0.0031)×10(mLL−1) DO2=CO×CaO2 VO2=CO×(CaO2−CcvO2) O2ext=VO2/DO2

Biochemical Measurements

Arterial blood biochemistry was analyzed using a Blood Biochemistry Analyzer (Pointcare V2; MNCHIP POINTCARE, Tianjin, China). Biochemical parameters included blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (Cr), amylase (AMY), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and creatine kinase (CK).

HOA-Related Measurements

The ODC for each blood sample was recorded using a Bloodox-2018 analyzer (Softron Biotechnology, Beijing, China) at 37 °C. The central venous/arterial blood was diluted to 0.75 mg of Hb/mL using a buffer solution (30 mM TES, 130 mM NaCl, and 5 mM KCl, pH 7.4). The P 50 values were obtained directly from the curve.

The theoretical oxygen-release capacity of red blood cells (RBCs) was calculated from the difference (ΔSO2) between the oxygen saturation corresponding to 100 mmHg (oxygen tension in the lung) and the oxygen saturation corresponding to 40 mmHg (oxygen tension in the tissue).

The Hill coefficient is the slope of the ODC. The Hill coefficient was calculated according to the best-fitting range (oxygen saturation from 20% to 80%) using the following equation: log⁡y1−y=nlog⁡p+k y = fractional O2 saturation and k = −nHlogP 50.

The P 50 value using the ABL90 blood gas analyzer was determined as follows: P50=P1+FCOHb0.5×(1−FCOHb−FMetHb) P was associated with the values of pH, PCO2, diphosphoglycerate concentration (cDPG), and fractions of carboxyhemoglobin (COHb), MetHb, and fetal Hb (HbF).

The reference value for P 50 measurements was 26.8 mmHg, with standard conditions defined at a temperature of 37 °C, pH of 7.4, and partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PCO2) of 5.33 KPa, fractions of COHb, MetHb, and HbF were all 0, and cDPG was 5 mmol/L. The actual P 50 values were calculated based on measured parameters, such as PCO2, PO2, SO2, COHb, MetHb, and HbF. The other method was based on the Roche blood gas analyzer for detecting P 50. The calculation formula is as follows: P50=26.7×10(lgPO2−lgPO2k)withlg⁡PO2k=lg⁡Q+4.1722.9andQ=SO21−SO2

SO2 and PO2 levels were obtained from blood samples of the central venous tissue and detected using an ABL90 blood gas analyzer.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Based on baseline physiological parameters and referring to Veterinary Experimental Animal Science, while also complying with the range of related studies, we included animals with systolic blood pressure (110–160 mmHg), diastolic blood pressure (60–90 mmHg), pulse rate (70–130 bmp), and respiratory rate (10–30 times/min) within reasonable ranges.

Measurements were screened based on the normal distribution theory in statistics within a range of mean ±2 × standard deviation (SD).

Statistical Analysis

All data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 26.0, IBM, USA). Analysis and comparison of the time lag effect was performed using the mixed effect model in repeated measurements, and the covariance structure was the scaled identity. The mixed-effect model can analyze data with an approximately normal distribution, which is in good agreement with our study. Differences were considered statistically significant if the p-value was <0.05. Analysis of HOA-related parameters and linear correlation analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8.3.0; GraphPad Software LLC, USA).

HOA-Related Parameters of the Canine Model at Different Phases in ANH

P 50 was different in BL (35.59 ± 6.99 mmHg), H1 (34.44 ± 6.44 mmHg), and H2 (36.27 ± 7.32 mmHg) (Figure A). The theoretical oxygen-release capacity increased from BL (37.42 ± 9.43%) to H1 (37.73 ± 10.22%) and H2 (38.70 ± 10.31%) (Figure B). Hill coefficient decreased from BL (2.54 ± 0.21) to H1 (2.50 ± 0.25) to H2 (2.46 ± 0.18) (Figure C). There were no statistically significant changes in the P 50, theoretical oxygen-release capacity, or Hill coefficient obtained from Bloodox-2018 during ANH (p > 0.05). However, the theoretical oxygen-release capacity exhibited an increasing trend, whereas the Hill coefficient exhibited a decreasing trend.

As shown in Figure D, P 50 obtained by the ABL 90 blood gas analyzer was similar in BL (33.19 ± 2.30 mmHg), H1 (33.70 ± 3.40 mmHg), and H2 (33.78 ± 4.55 mmHg). There was a strong positive correlation between the P 50 values measured using the ABL 90 and Roche analyzers (p < 0.0001, r = 0.9963) (Figure G).

Discussion

Canine Model of Moderate ANH

ANH is a controlled blood conservation strategy used in cardiac surgery, , major liver resection, and major orthopedic surgery. Although tolerable limits are unknown, the target for blood withdrawal in ANH is to reach Hb of 8.0 g/dL. , Simultaneously, the intraoperative transfusion trigger is set at Hb <7.0 g/dL , or HCT <18%. In the current study, Hb was controlled to reach approximately 7.0 g/dL in H2 to test whether HOA changes during moderate ANH.

In the present study, relatively stable CVP and GEDV values indicated that euvolemia was maintained. With a stepwise decrease in Hb and HCT from BL to H2, T, MAP, DBP, and CO changed significantly, indicating physiological and hemodynamic changes in moderate ANH. As products of Hb and CO, CaO2, CcvO2, and DO2 decreased in a stepwise manner from BL to H2, and significant changes in acid–base (pH, HCO3 –, BE) and ion (K+, Ca2+, Cl–) concentrations reflect disturbed homeostasis. The increase in MetHb levels in H2 suggests oxidative stress.

As mechanical ventilation was not used in this study, PaCO2 was approximately 49 mmHg in the BL. As a result, the pH (7.28 ± 0.07) and BE (−3.42 ± 1.83) were below the reference level. The PaCO2 of canines decreases to below 45 mmHg immediately after recovery from anesthesia.

HOA-Related Parameters Did Not Change in the Canine Model of Moderate ANH

In this study, three methods were used to obtain P 50. One is derived from the ODC using the Bloodox-2018 analyzer. The other two were based on blood gas analysis results. Regardless of the method used, P 50 did not change significantly from BL to H2 during moderate ANH.

The increase in Cl– (Figure A) as well as the decrease in pH (Figure B), BE (Figure C), and HCO3 – (Figure D) resulted in an increase in P 50. Bohr et al. demonstrated that a decrease in the pH caused an increase in P 50 due to H+ binding to Hb and facilitating oxygen release. The effects of BE and HCO3 – for the ODC are similar to the above principle. Cl– binds to Hb as an allosteric effector and increases the P 50. In addition, Cl– and HCO3 – move inside and outside the RBCs by anion exchange, and the change in HCO3 – is pH-dependent. Thus, the change in Cl– is affected by pH and HCO3 –.

In contrast, there is a decrease in body temperature (Figure E) and an increase in MetHb (Figure F) levels led to a decrease in P 50. An increase in body temperature promotes the unloading of oxygen from oxygenated Hb, whereas oxygen binds more tightly to Hb at lower body temperatures. Low perfusion of the microcirculation caused by ANH led to a decrease in the body temperature. The reaction between hemoglobin and oxygen is exothermic, resulting in a decrease in hemoglobin oxygen affinity with an increase in temperature. This adaptation of temperature sensitivity of hemoglobin oxygen affinity is considered beneficial for oxygen unloading in warm tissues (such as exercising muscles) with increased oxygen demand. This is also reflected in the periodic reductions in oxygen demand and increased hemoglobin oxygen affinity of mammalian hibernators during winter to help match oxygen supply and demand. The formation of MetHb is due to Hb oxidation with a shift from Fe2+ to Fe3+. MetHb increases HOA of the remaining normal Hb, leading to the decrease in P 50. ,

As a result, factors that increase or decrease P 50 offset each other, leaving P 50 unchanged (Figure G). Although there were no statistically significant changes in the theoretical oxygen-release capacity or Hill coefficient, the theoretical oxygen-release capacity showed an increasing trend, whereas the Hill coefficient showed a decreasing trend during ANH. Whether they change with severe ANH or hemorrhagic shock should be further investigated.

Comparison of P 50 Derived from the ODC and Calculated by Blood Gas Analyzers

The Bloodox-2018 analyzer measures the ODC by adding Hb or RBCs to a standard buffer solution at pH 7.4, and the final concentration of Hb is approximately 1 mg/mL. Samples are oxygenated by blowing air to increase the partial pressure of oxygen, and then deoxygenated with nitrogen gas, similar to the Hemox-Analyzer. The Clark oxygen electrode detects changes in oxygen tension and is recorded on the x-axis of the ODC. The resulting oxyhemoglobin fraction was simultaneously monitored by using dual-wavelength spectrophotometry and is displayed on the y-axis. This method can be applied to Hb and RBCs from different species. However, the influence of carbon dioxide is not considered. Therefore, the resulted P 50 may differ from the actual values in vivo.

Blood gas analyzers can be applied to fresh human blood samples. P 50 values obtained by the blood gas analyzer are based on the reference P 50 value for humans under standard conditions. , The reference value for the P 50 measurements is 3.578 kPa (26.8 mmHg). The P 50 values are calculated based on parameters including PCO2, PO2, SO2, COHb, MetHb, and HbF. If the blood gas analyzer fails to obtain some of these parameters, the calculation of P 50 would be inaccurate.

Only blood gas results with SO2 < 97% are reliable when measuring P 50 using a blood gas analyzer. − The precision of the P 50 calculation decreases rapidly with an increase in oxygen saturation because even a small absolute error in oxygen saturation causes a large error in ln⁡SO21−SO2 when SO2 approaches 1. Consequently, an SO2 value ≥97% may result in erroneous calculations of the extreme values of the ODC and inaccurate P 50.

It has been reported that P 50 values of human blood measured using different blood gas analyzers are consistent. Zongtang Chu et al., Vogel DJ et al., and Balcerek B et al. reported the P 50 value of 25.42 ± 0.81 mmHg (ABL90 FLEX, Radiometer), 25.70 ± 6.00 mmHg (calculated by Roche blood gas analyzer formula), and 27.97 ± 1.54 mmHg (ABL800 flex, Radiometer), respectively. Moreover, previous study found that P 50 of human blood measured by the blood gas analyzer (ABL90 FLEX, Radiometer) (25.42 ± 0.81 mmHg) and Bloodox-2018 analyzers (23.83 ± 2.96 mmHg) had no statistical difference. However, for beagles, P 50 values varied dramatically between the blood gas analyzer (28.21 ± 1.32 mmHg) and Bloodox-2018 analyzer (46.47 ± 1.20 mmHg).

In the present study, there was a strong correlation between the P 50 obtained by ABL90 and Roche blood gas analyzers, while neither of them correlated with Bloodox-2018.

P 50 in Chronic Anemia and Hemorrhagic Shock in Different Species

Normovolemic hemodilution is only acute in clinical operations, and ANH can be divided into different degrees according to the amount of blood loss. ANH is a blood conservation strategy used to reduce the need for allogeneic blood transfusion, and moderate ANH is more common in clinical practice. Normal (chronic or gradual) normovolemic hemodilution is not a proactive clinical intervention measure. Generally, normal (chronic or gradual) normovolemic hemodilution refers to anemia. Anemia shares the same characteristics with ANH with regard to low Hb and HCT levels but normal blood volume. However, anemia is a chronic disease, and ANH is a treatment.

According to studies investigating diseases similar to ANH, the increase in P 50 in patients with chronic anemia is mainly due to an increase in 2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG) levels. Previous studies showed that HOA decreased with a right shift of the ODC, and P 50 increased in almost all types of anemia, including iron deficiency anemia (P 50 = 31.1 mmHg), cirrhosis anemia (P 50 = 30.6 mmHg), hemolytic anemia (P 50 = 30.1 mmHg), and aplastic anemia (P 50 = 28.6 mmHg) compared to normal subjects (P 50 = 27.2 mmHg). , The increase in the 2,3-DPG concentration is the major reason for the increase in P 50 and oxygen delivery to tissues. This is beneficial for promoting oxygen offloading in tissues and compensating for the decrease in Hb levels. ,

Changes in HOA differed between species during hemorrhagic shock. In mongrel canines, P 50 remains unchanged when MAP reaches 60 mmHg during hemorrhagic shock. However, in rhesus monkeys, when the mean Hb decreased from 12.5 to 9.2 g/dL in hemorrhagic shock, P 50 increased from 33 to 36 mmHg.

2,3-DPG concentrations are higher in canines than in humans. However, there is no significant correlation between P 50 and 2,3-DPG levels in canines, indicating that 2,3-DPG is not a major regulator of P 50 in canines. The effects of T, pH, and PCO2 on P 50 are similar in humans and canines.

In this study, we established a canine ANH model of acute anemia. We speculate that HOA remained unchanged during moderate ANH because of the following: (1) the P 50 of canines is not sensitive to acute anemia, (2) P 50 changes differently in acute anemia and chronic anemia, and (3) P 50 does not change in moderate ANH but may change in severe ANH.

Limitations and Future Studies

This study has several limitations. First, although CO2 retention is reversible after anesthesia, the possibility of complications must be investigated. Second, the Bloodox-2018 analyzer has no control on partial pressure of CO2 and cannot simulate the in vivo environment and requires further refinement. Third, the P 50 of different breeds of the same species is different, indicating that effects of ANH on different species may also differ. Fourth, in our study, the MAP in H2 was notably lower. However, in clinical settings, norepinephrine is often used as a treatment for ANH but was not used in our study. This discrepancy in the method used between our study and the real clinical setting might affect our results.

Conclusions

In the present study, a canine model of moderate ANH was established, with Hb and HCT reaching nearly 50% of the BL. P 50 was obtained using three methods, one derived from the ODC and the other two based on blood gas analyzers. Physiological, hemodynamic, oxygenation, homeostasis, and oxidative stress parameters changed during the ANH. P 50 did not change significantly from BL to H2 during moderate ANH. There was a strong positive correlation between the P 50 obtained by the ABL90 and Roche blood gas analyzers, but neither was correlated with Bloodox-2018. The theoretical oxygen-release capacity and Hill coefficient exhibited increasing and decreasing trends, respectively. Whether they change with severe ANH or hemorrhagic shock should be investigated.
