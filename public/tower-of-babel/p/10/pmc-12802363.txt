An explainable artificial intelligence framework reveals mutations associated with drug resistance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis
Authors: Hui Cen, Peng Zhang, Yunchao Ling, Guoping Zhao, Guoqing Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights Scientific questions: The emergence of drug resistance in Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) has significantly increased the complexity of tuberculosis treatment and transmission risks, underscoring the critical need to elucidate resistance mechanisms for rapid diagnostics, optimized therapeutic strategies, and global tuberculosis burden mitigation.Evidence before this study.While genomics-based statistical association studies have identified many resistance-associated mutations, MTB continues to develop new resistance mutations under the persistent selective pressure of anti-tuberculosis drugs, emphasizing the necessity to further refine existing catalogues of resistance mutations. Although deep learning models have been applied to explain resistance-associated mutations at the population level, individual-level resistance mechanisms remain underexplored. New findings: We propose an explainable artificial intelligence framework called xAI-MTBDR. It combines multiple machine learning models with the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method, aiming to identify new drug resistance-associated mutations and predict the drug resistance of MTB. To our knowledge, this is the largest study utilizing the MTB dataset (39,145 isolates) to assess the performance of similar models for drug resistance prediction. Compared to existing methods, xAI-MTBDR not only significantly improves the prediction accuracy of drug resistance but also provides detailed explanations of each mutation’s contribution to resistance. The predictions of xAI-MTBDR are highly consistent with the MTB drug resistance mutation catalogue published by the World Health Organization (WHO), validating its broad applicability and reliability. At the individual level, xAI-MTBDR can deeply analyze the unique drug resistance mechanisms of each isolate. We revealed 27 potential resistance markers, some of which are closer to the corresponding drug targets in protein structure than known resistance mutations. Significance of the study: xAI-MTBDR not only predicts the drug resistance of MTB but also provides the rationale for each isolate’s prediction, helping researchers decide how to utilize the results. This framework can also be used to identify new drug resistance-associated mutations for the study of resistance mechanisms.Keywords: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Antimicrobial resistance, Drug resistance mechanisms, Explainable artificial intelligence, Machine learning

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB), is the world’s leading bacterial infectious disease [[1], [2], [3]]. In 2022, approximately 10.6 million people developed TB, resulting in 1.3 million deaths [4]. Anti-TB drugs are traditionally classified into first-line and second-line agents [5]. First-line drugs consist of four main agents: isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol, which are primarily used to treat drug-susceptible TB [6]. These drugs are characterized by their strong bactericidal activity, favorable safety profile, and substantial clinical evidence supporting their use. In contrast, a second-line TB drug is an agent reserved for the treatment of drug-resistant TB [7]. For example, the 6-month bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid and moxifloxacin (BPaLM) regimen is the World Health Organization (WHO) preferred recommendation for patients with multidrug-resistant TB [8]. The rise of drug-resistant TB has worsened this crisis, with treatment success rates for drug-resistant TB being 25 % lower than for drug-susceptible TB [4,9]. This highlights the urgent need to understand resistance mechanisms for rapid detection of drug-resistant MTB, guiding effective treatment and reducing the global TB burden [10].

The majority of drug resistance in MTB isolates is attributed to chromosomal mutations [11]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have been successfully applied to investigate novel resistance mechanisms in MTB by identifying nucleotide variants associated with drug resistance [12]. This approach has not only detected known resistance loci but also uncovered new resistance-related loci, such as specific sites in the ald gene correlated with increased resistance to D-cycloserine, novel codons in the ethA gene associated with ethionamide resistance, and variations in the promoter region of thyX related to para-aminosalicylic acid resistance [[13], [14], [15]]. The WHO-endorsed catalogue of MTB resistance mutations, based on whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data, categorizes mutations according to their association with resistance and serves as a high-quality reference [[16], [17], [18]]. Pre-determined mutation catalogues, which are essential for detecting drug resistance, form the foundation upon which popular prediction tools such as TB-profiler [19], Mykrobe [20], and SAM-TB [21] infer drug resistance. With the rapid accumulation of WGS data, coupled with the enhanced representation of genome sequences from diverse geographic regions [15,[22], [23], [24]], these catalogues need further refinement [9,12], particularly for low-frequency mutations in natural populations [12].

Deep learning models have enriched mutation catalogues by identifying complex patterns in genomic data [25], as demonstrated by A.G. Green et al. [26]'s convolutional neural network, which identified 18 sites not previously associated with resistance. However, this study did not explore deeper individual-level interpretations, which are essential for understanding how specific mutations contribute to resistance in individual cases. The need for models that provide both global (population-level) and local (individual-level) explanations is critical. Global explanations help identify broad mutation contributions, while local explanations are essential for understanding individual mutations that drive resistance in specific isolates. The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method is well-suited for this purpose, offering both global and local insights by evaluating the contribution of each mutation to drug resistance predictions [27]. SHAP’s dual capability enhances mutation catalogue completeness and improves the understanding of resistance mechanisms, making it a valuable tool for research on pathogenic microorganisms.

In this study, we present xAI-MTBDR, an explainable artificial intelligence framework that integrates machine learning with SHAP to identify potential resistance-associated mutations and predict drug resistance in MTB. Leveraging public WGS data from nearly 40,000 MTB isolates, xAI-MTBDR outperforms state-of-the-art methods in predicting resistance for all first-line anti-TB drugs. By providing both population-wide and individual-level insights, xAI-MTBDR enables revelation of potential biomarkers, grouping of drug-resistant isolates, and reflection of varying resistance levels. It is helpful for advancing the understanding of TB resistance mechanisms. The code and data are available at https://github.com/BioMedBigDataCenter/xAI-MTBDR.

Materials and methods

Sequence and drug susceptibility testing (DST) data

The raw WGS data and corresponding phenotypic DST data for MTB isolates were obtained from three studies: CRyPTIC [28], T.M. Walker et al. [17], and GenTB [29]. The CRyPTIC dataset consists of clinical isolates from 23 countries across five continents, with WGS and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) data for 13 anti-TB drugs generated using a standardized assay. The T.M. Walker et al. dataset aggregates global isolates from 45 countries, sourced from academic consortia, public health laboratories, and published studies, including both population-representative and resistance-enriched samples. The GenTB dataset comprises publicly available clinical isolates with raw sequencing data, representing a broad geographical and genetic diversity. The original datasets contained 12,288, 38,223, and 20,379 isolates, respectively. The processing workflow for these datasets is shown in Fig.S1. After manual curation to remove unavailable and duplicate isolates, a total of 41,790 isolates were retained. To balance the scale of the datasets, isolates were randomly selected with proportional distributions close to 1:1:1, resulting in 12,285 isolates from CRyPTIC, 14,324 from T.M. Walker et al., and 15,181 from GenTB.

Drugs with fewer than 250 isolates in any dataset were excluded [26]. Given the potential overlaps and inconsistencies among the datasets, the DST data were validated and reconciled based on the following principles: (1) consistent DST results from multiple sources were retained; (2) phenotypes from one source were adopted when another lacked data; and (3) inconsistent results were marked as “conflicting”. The DST categories included susceptible, resistant, and conflicting. Only isolates with a definitive DST label (susceptible or resistant) for a given drug were taken as the ground truth for model training and evaluation, while those with conflicting labels were excluded. Notably, 12,006 isolates from the CRyPTIC dataset included MIC data, offering a more detailed resistance level beyond the binary resistance phenotype. Variant calling and feature extraction

All raw WGS data were processed using Clockwork (v0.11.3, https://github.com/iqbal-lab-org/clockwork), a WHO-recommended bioinformatics pipeline for variant calling in Illumina paired-end sequencing data. Quality control filters were applied, retaining only isolates with an average sequencing depth greater than 15, aligned to the H37Rv reference genome (NC000962.3), and with less than 5 % of reads mapped to nontuberculous mycobacteria genomes [18]. After filtering, 12,006 isolates from CRyPTIC, 13,750 from T.M. Walker et al., and 13,389 from GenTB remained (Fig.S1). To ensure high-quality mutation data, additional quality control criteria were applied: (1) a minimum sequencing depth of 5 ×, (2) a fraction of supporting reads of 0.4 or higher, and (3) a genotype confidence percentile (GCP) of 0.5 or greater [28]. Mutation annotation was performed using SnpEff (v5.1d) [30].

We categorized the mutations identified within 49 regions associated with drug resistance (45 genes and 4 promoters; Table S1) [19,20,[31], [32], [33]] into common (>30 isolates) and rare (≤30 isolates) mutations, following a previous study [33]. For rare mutations, derived predictors were created by grouping them based on both their genomic locus and mutation type. Specifically, for each coding region, rare variants were categorized into three subtype groups: single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), frameshift insertions/deletions (indels), and non-frameshift indels. For each non-coding region, rare variants were grouped into two subtypes: indels and SNPs. For each isolate, if at least one mutation within a given derived predictor was present, the predictor was encoded as “1”; otherwise, it was encoded as “0”. Only common mutations and derived predictors present in at least 30 isolates were retained for the final features. In total, 788 features were extracted, including 717 common mutations and 71 derived predictors, from 39,145 isolates. Each drug-specific model was trained on the same 788 features.

Model construction and explanation

We combined the strengths of eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), logistic regression, and random forest to construct a soft voting ensemble model using Python’s Scikit-Learn library (v0.24.2) [[33], [34], [35]], with voting weights set to 2.0, 1.5, and 1.0. The Leave-One-Out (LOO) strategy was applied to train and test the ensemble model for each drug, where one dataset was iteratively selected as the test set while the other two were used for training. Hyperparameters were tuned using RandomizedSearchCV with five-fold cross-validation, followed by manual optimization.

Our framework employs the SHAP method, a post-hoc interpretability tool based on game theory, to evaluate each feature’s contribution to the prediction [27]. SHAP is implemented via the TreeExplainer for the XGBoost model, which plays the largest role in predicting drug resistance within the ensemble model. SHAP values were calculated for the test dataset of each drug, providing local explanations by quantifying the contribution of each feature in every sample to the model’s resistance prediction, thereby identifying mutations that influence drug resistance at the individual level. Global explanations were derived by averaging the absolute SHAP values of each feature across all samples, highlighting mutations broadly associated with resistance at the population level.

Identifying potential resistance markers

For every correctly predicted resistant isolate, we recorded the mutation with the largest SHAP attribution and then aggregated and deduplicated mutations across isolates to obtain the candidate mutations (Table S2). Mutations annotated as category 1 (associated with resistance) or category 2 (associated with resistance – interim) in the WHO mutation catalogue were treated as known resistance-conferring mutations [16,18,26]. Mutations that are not listed in the WHO mutation catalogue, specifically those not classified in category 1, category 2, category 4 (not associated with resistance – interim), or category 5 (not associated with resistance), but classified in category 3 (uncertain significance) or as unknown mutations, were considered as potential resistance markers. We used Mann-Whitney test to compare differences between the known mutations group and potential mutations group.

Subgroup the MTB drug-resistant isolates

To investigate the role of SHAP values, we performed principal component analysis (PCA) for each drug using the PCA method from sklearn.decomposition for data visualization and compared it with PCA using the original features (where 0 indicates missing mutations and 1 indicates presence).

To subgroup the drug-resistant isolates based on SHAP values, we chose the top principal components which account for more than 90 % of the cumulative explained variance to represent the isolates. Then we determined the optimal number of clusters using the sum of squared errors (SSE) plot (Fig.S2) and performed clustering using the AgglomerativeClustering method from sklearn.cluster.

To identify the key driving mutations within different groups, we applied the Kruskal-Wallis H test, and visualized the top 10 statistically significant mutations associated with resistance (P < 0.001) in a heatmap.

Assessing relationships between SHAP values and resistance levels

To assess the relationship between SHAP values and drug resistance levels, we calculated the sum of SHAP values for each isolate and classified them into three groups: susceptible, low-level resistant, and high-level resistant, based on established criteria [[36], [37], [38]]. For rifampicin, MIC ≤ 0.5 μg/mL indicated susceptibility, 0.5 < MIC ≤ 2.0 μg/mL indicated low-level resistance, and MIC > 16.0 μg/mL indicated high-level resistance. For isoniazid, MIC ≤ 0.1 μg/mL indicated susceptibility, 0.1 < MIC ≤ 3.2 μg/mL indicated low-level resistance, and MIC ≥ 6.4 μg/mL indicated high-level resistance. For ethambutol, MIC < 4.0 μg/mL indicated susceptibility, 4.0 ≤ MIC ≤ 16.0 μg/mL indicated low-level resistance, and MIC ≥ 32.0 μg/mL indicated high-level resistance. We used the ggplot2 R package to create violin plots of SHAP values across different resistance levels and employed Spearman analysis to investigate correlations. The Mann-Whitney test was used to compare differences among the three groups.

We developed a ternary XGBoost model (susceptible, low-level resistant, and high-level resistant) using MIC data and trained it separately with the original features and SHAP values from the CRyPTIC dataset. The data was split into training and testing sets with an 8:2 ratio to evaluate model performance.

Results

The framework and datasets characteristics

The framework is consisted of four main stages: dataset collection, feature extraction, model construction and explanation (Fig. 1, detailed in Methods). In stage 1, we compiled 39,145 high-quality MTB isolates from three datasets, each providing WGS and DST data for 11 anti-TB drugs [17,28,29]. In stage 2, features were derived from diverse mutations, including insertions, deletions, and SNPs, based on 49 preselected antimicrobial resistance (AMR) regions (genes or promoters) [33]. Stage 3 involved training and testing for each drug by the LOO strategy. In the final stage, we applied SHAP to provide interpretable insights, identifying key mutations driving resistance and exploring resistance mechanisms at both population and individual levels.

Proportions of drug-resistant isolates across different drugs are summarized in Table 1. Each drug is represented by at least 250 isolates per dataset [26]. Rifampicin and isoniazid have the highest numbers, with 36,552 and 36,240 isolates. In contrast, capreomycin has the fewest DST entries, with only 11,246 isolates. The proportion of resistant isolates also varies, ranging from 8 % for amikacin to 34 % for isoniazid.

xAI-MTBDR outperforms state-of-the-art methods in predicting resistance

Based on the xAI-MTBDR framework, we trained individual models for each drug to predict MTB resistance and compared it with four state-of-the-art methods, including machine learning models (random forest and logistic regression), deep learning models (WDNN), and catalog-based methods [TB-profiler (v4.3.0)] [19,33,35]. As shown in Table 2, our models improved area under the curve (AUC) by 0.271 %-1.439 % for first-line drugs, with an average improvement of 0.564 %, and by 0.045 %-3.060 % for second-line drugs, with an average improvement of 1.135 %; while sensitivity and specificity are detailed in Table S3, our model achieves improved specificity compared to other machine learning models with only minimal sensitivity loss. The lower performance for amikacin and capreomycin may be due to the limited number of isolates. These results demonstrate that our framework outperforms existing methods in predicting drug resistance.

Population-level explanations of SHAP values aligned with WHO mutation categories

The WHO MTB resistance mutation catalogue classifies mutations into five categories based on their confidence level of association with resistance [16,18]. To assess the relationship between SHAP values and the WHO categories, we performed a statistical analysis between the categories of each drug using the Mann-Whitney test and adjusted the P-values using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction to control the false discovery rate (FDR) (Table S4). The results revealed a strong correlation between mutation importance (Fig. S3A) and these categories. For rifampicin, category 1 mutations (associated with resistance) had the highest SHAP values, ranging from 4.62 to 6.29, while category 5 mutations (not associated with resistance) had the lowest (−0.02 to 0.09) (Fig. 2A). Similar trends were observed for isoniazid and ethambutol (Fig. 2B,2C). In category 3 (mutations with uncertain resistance associations), some mutations overlapped with category 1, particularly for isoniazid (InhA I194T, InhA I21V, and KatG S315G) and ethambutol (EmbB D1024N), whose SHAP values were above the median of category 3, aligning more closely with category 1, consistent with their reported resistance associations [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. These findings suggest SHAP values may help reclassify uncertain category 3 mutations (Fig. 2B,2C). For pyrazinamide, as individual mutations associated with its resistance are both found less frequently and distributed more broadly among genes such as pncA, the WHO mutation catalogue adopted a “relaxed” grading criteria [16]. As a result, we observed some overlap in SHAP values across categories (Fig. 2D), but category 1 mutations still showed relatively higher SHAP values. The SHAP values for second-line drugs also exhibit similar category differences (Fig. S4). Overall, SHAP values aligned well with the WHO mutation catalogue, effectively distinguishing resistance-associated mutations at the population level.

Individual-level explanations identify potential resistance markers

The individual-level explanations revealed variations in mutation contributions among isolates (Fig. S3B), enabling the identification of 99 important mutations for the four first-line drugs, with 27 were not included in the WHO category 1 or 2 and were considered potential biomarkers (Table S2). Further literature review identified four mutations among these 27 potential biomarkers that are consistent with reported drug resistance: isoniazid-related mutations (InhA I194T, InhA I21V, and KatG S315G) and the ethambutol-related mutation (EmbB D1024N) [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. Additionally, three other variants (AhpC −52C > T, InhA I21T, and AhpC −48G > A) are considered potential resistance biomarkers [[43], [44], [45]]. For isoniazid and ethambutol, we identified potential resistance markers, excluding rifampicin due to the absence of novel markers in RpoB, and pyrazinamide as its markers overlapped with known mutations in PncA. The potential markers I194 (isoniazid) and D1024 (ethambutol) were located 3.7 Å and 6.1 Å from their respective drugs, compared to 4 Å and 8.6 Å for the known markers S94 and M306 (Fig. 3A, 3B, Table S5) [46]. The median distances of known and potential markers to ethambutol were 12.6 Å and 9.1 Å, respectively, with no significant difference (Fig. 3C), suggesting these mutations may influence the interaction between EmbB and ethambutol.

Individual-level explanations subgroup MTB drug-resistant isolates

We performed a PCA on the SHAP values of the isolates. For rifampicin, the susceptible and resistant isolates were clearly separated, and the resistant isolates were clustered into three distinct groups (Fig. 4B, Fig. S2 and Fig. S5). In contrast, the PCA of the original features (where 0 indicates missing mutations and 1 indicates presence) failed to achieve this separation (Fig. 4A). Similar patterns were observed for other first-line drugs and second-line drugs (Fig. S6 and Fig. S7). We further visualized the top 10 rifampicin resistance-associated mutations across these three groups of resistant isolates, which may reflect the underlying mechanisms driving their differentiation (Fig. 4C). Group 1 is primarily driven by the RpoB D435V mutation within the 81-base pair (bp) rifampicin resistance-determining region (RRDR, corresponding to codons 426–452 in MTB). Group 2, which contains the most isolates, is predominantly associated with the RpoB S450L mutation within the RRDR. In contrast, group 3 displays a more diverse mutation pattern, including non-RRDR RpoB V170F and I491F. These mutations are positioned near or directly interact with rifampicin (Fig. 4D), potentially altering the structure of the active center or the interactions between ribonucleic acid polymerase (RNAP) subunits, leading to resistance, consistent with previous research [36]. These results suggest that SHAP values not only capture key characteristics of drug-resistant isolates, but also offer insights into potential resistance mechanisms, emphasizing the importance of non-RRDR mutations in resistance detection.

The framework reflects drug resistance level of isolates

When low-level resistance to first-line drugs occurs, increasing the dosage may help overcome this resistance [47]. We analyzed the relationships between SHAP and MIC values for three first-line drugs using the CRyPTIC dataset (excluding pyrazinamide due to missing data). The isolates were classified into three categories based on MIC value: susceptible, low-level resistant, and high-level resistant following previous studies [[36], [37], [38]]. A significant positive correlation between SHAP values of isolates and resistance levels is observed, with the coefficient of determination (R2) ranging from 0.44 to 0.69 (Fig. 5A-C). Notably, the explainable results from binary classification models appear to contain information related to resistance levels. To further evaluate this, we constructed a ternary classifier using the original features and the SHAP values separately. The classifier trained with SHAP values outperformed the one using original features, showing over 1 % improvement (Fig. 5D), indicating that SHAP values provide a more effective representation of resistance levels.

Discussion

xAI-MTBDR predicts MTB drug resistance and identifies resistance-associated mutations at both population and individual levels. To our knowledge, this is the largest study utilizing the MTB WGS dataset (39,145 isolates) to assess the performance and applicability of similar models for drug resistance prediction. The framework achieved state-of-the-art performance in predicting resistance to 11 anti-TB drugs (Table 2), with similar performance observed when models were trained and tested within each dataset (Table S6). This framework was then used to identify potential resistance biomarkers, subgroup drug-resistant isolates, and capture resistance levels. Notably, the framework’s ease of deployment and low resource requirements facilitate rapid identification of drug-resistant patients, potentially enabling earlier clinical interventions. Furthermore, the SHAP-based interpretation of key mutations provides transparent rationale for model predictions, which may support clinical reasoning when integrated with established diagnostic protocols. While further validation is required to assess impact on patient management, this approach offers a pathway toward data-informed decision support in antimicrobial resistance scenarios.

The potential resistance biomarkers identified in this study contribute to a deeper understanding of mutations not yet definitively classified by the WHO [16,18]. Importantly, these potential biomarkers are computational predictions, and structural proximity to a drug target alone does not necessarily imply functional impact. Accordingly, their relevance to drug resistance requires further confirmation through energetic modeling (e.g., docking, molecular dynamics) and experimental validation, even for those with prior literature associations. Pending such validation, these biomarkers may provide a foundation for the development of diagnostic assays for drug resistance detection and could serve as potential targets for drug discovery.

A key strength of xAI-MTBDR is its ability to detect resistance mutations at the individual level, enabling the clustering of isolates with similar resistance mechanisms. For instance, rifampicin-resistant isolates were grouped by distinct mutation patterns, offering a more detailed understanding of resistance. This individual-level insight sets the foundation for more precise prediction models tailored to specific mutation profiles.

Although the same model is used, prediction performance varies across different drugs. This may be attributed to the following factors: (1) Data heterogeneity: The data were integrated from multiple publicly available datasets, which differ in culture media, DST methods, and definitions of resistance. Additionally, the resistance information in these public datasets is often incomplete, with most datasets lacking MIC data. Since MIC is critical for calibrating resistance, its absence increases the risk of prediction bias [48]; (2) Data quality: The accuracy of DST data for each sample is a key factor affecting model training and testing performance [49]. For example, the reliability of DST data for pyrazinamide is relatively low, as its antimicrobial activity depends on specific acidic pH conditions, making the testing process complex. Improper handling may lead to poor reproducibility, thereby affecting data quality; (3) Data volume: Sufficient phenotypic data for certain drugs, especially second-line drugs, would help improve the predictive performance of the model [49]. Furthermore, the geographic origin of the isolates is a crucial factor. As resistance patterns are shaped by local treatment practices and transmission dynamics, the predictive power of models will be significantly enhanced as future datasets become more comprehensive and geographically representative [9,50]. It is also important to note that our framework is inherently designed to accommodate this evolution. xAI-MTBDR trains a separate model for each drug, ensuring that newer drugs (e.g., bedaquiline, linezolid) can be seamlessly integrated once sufficient genomic and phenotypic data become available.

Despite progress achieved in drug resistance prediction, the proposed framework still has certain limitations that merit further exploration. First, complex genetic interactions in MTB, including both epistasis between mutations in different genes and strong linkage among SNPs within the same genomic regions [26], may not be fully captured by SHAP. Because SHAP evaluates each mutation independently, they may underrepresent effects that arise from co-occurring or interacting mutations. Further research into these interactions could enhance prediction accuracy and provide deeper mechanistic insights. Second, the framework has not been evaluated for its performance under the influence of strain mixtures, as the WGS data used in this study were derived from pure-culture isolates and subjected to stringent quality control via multiple bioinformatic procedures to minimize potential confounding effects from mixed-strain infections in MTB. Given that mixed-strain infections and heteroresistance represent well-established challenges in MTB diagnostics, future work will involve the use of in silico mixed samples and, where feasible, the inclusion of clinically characterized mixed samples to assess and further refine the model’s predictive performance.

Our framework adopts a per-drug modeling strategy, in which a separate model is trained for each drug to generate accurate resistance predictions. These individual predictions can then be used to derive resistance profiles, such as mono-resistant, multidrug-resistant (MDR), or extensively drug-resistant (XDR). We have performed an illustrative analysis on the CRyPTIC dataset, showing that the framework can distinguish these categories, although the mono-resistant group was very small. The corresponding code and results are available in our GitHub repository, providing a resource for further validation as larger datasets with more complete DST data become available.

Although xAI-MTBDR currently focuses on MTB, it is adaptable to other pathogens by substituting resistance-associated genes and reference genomes. This flexibility makes it applicable to a broad range of pathogens, including Mycobacterium abscessus [51], Staphylococcus aureus [20], Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumoniae [52], broadening its scope in antimicrobial resistance research.

In summary, we developed an explainable and high-accuracy drug resistance prediction framework designed to facilitate the discovery of potential resistance-associated mutations, offering a new perspective on resistance mechanisms at both population and individual levels. As WGS and resistance phenotype data continue to improve, integrating explainable artificial intelligence methods will enhance prediction accuracy, deepen our understanding of resistance mechanisms, and optimize treatment strategies. This approach has the potential to reduce morbidity and mortality from drug-resistant tuberculosis and other pathogens.
