Low population genetic structure in five Chilean Nothofagus species and high fluctuating asymmetry in Nothofagus pumilio x N. antarctica putative hybrids
Authors: Riley Yewon Jung, Luana S Maroja, Shaw N Lacy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFollowing historic natural disturbances, interspecific hybridization has been documented between several Nothofagus species in Chilean forests. While hybridization can contribute to survival by enhancing genetic diversity, it may lead to developmental instability, reflected in higher leaf fluctuating asymmetry. Our goal was to investigate if hybrid individuals between N. pumilio and N. antarctica had developmental instabilities observable through high leaf fluctuating asymmetry and to examine evidence of potential hybridization and population genetic structure across five Nothofagus species.ResultsWe collected N. pumilio, N. antarctica, and putative hybrid leaves for morphological analysis and chloroplast DNA sequencing. We also performed population genetic analysis on additional Nothofagus species (N. pumilio, N. antarctica, N. betuloides, N. dombeyi, and N. nitida) using microsatellite markers (eight loci). The putative hybrids with intermediate phenotypes displayed significantly higher fluctuating asymmetry than morphologically unambiguous N. pumilio and N. antarctica. Yet despite clear phenotypic differences, N. pumilio and N. antarctica shared cpDNA and had almost identical microsatellite composition. Our microsatellite analysis of five Nothofagus species found high allelic variation within species and subpopulations but low genetic structure between species and populations.ConclusionsOur findings reveal possible developmental instability in putative N. pumilio and N. antarctica hybrids characterized by leaf morphology. Though nuclear ITS sequences have phylogenetically differentiated pure Nothofagus taxa, we found little to no genetic differentiation between sympatric or allopatric Nothofagus species from microsatellite data, particularly N. pumilio and N. antarctica. This is a surprising result given the clear morphological and biogeographical differences between the Chilean Nothofagus. While N. pumilio and N. antarctica are named species, matching the ecological species concept, whole genome analyses is needed to test if they are distinct enough to be classified as separate species under the phylogenetic species concept.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-025-02435-2.Keywords: Nothofagaceae, Nothofagus, Fluctuating asymmetry, Hybridization, Introgression, Population structure

Background

Interspecific hybridization can lead to the transfer of alleles and increase the genetic diversity within species [28]. Experimental evidence suggests that while hybrid progeny can display outbreeding depression, certain combinations of genes might lead to hybrid vigor or heterosis–hence the importance of studying the extent of hybridization occurring in areas co-occupied with several species capable of hybridizing [3]. Morphologically, interspecific hybrid offspring across various taxonomic groups tend to have intermediate phenotypes between those of the parental species [19] and can be significantly different from one or both parental types, as exemplified by Arabidopsis, Allium, and Solanum [5, 7, 26, 53].

Another consequence of plant interspecific hybridization is the increase in leaf fluctuating asymmetry. Leaf fluctuating asymmetry measures the degree of random deviations from perfect bilateral symmetry in leaves and is assumed to be caused by genomic disruptions due to hybridization, inbreeding, or directional selection [23, 37]. Interspecific hybridization between Betula pubescens/Betula nana/Betula pendula, Salix sericea/Salix eriocephala, and Quercus magnoliifolia/Quercus resinosa showed higher fluctuating asymmetry in hybrid offspring [2, 23, 52]. Such results support the coadaptation hypothesis–that the change in the genetic composition of an individual through hybridization leads to the disruption of coadapted gene complexes within a population’s gene pool, resulting in developmental instability [8].

In Chile and Argentina, interspecific hybridization has been documented between several pairs of species within Nothofagus, commonly referred to as the Southern Beech [1, 9, 10, 13, 15, 32, 38, 46, 50]. Ten Nothofagus species grow in Chile [12], including the deciduous N. pumilio, N. antarctica, and N. obliqua and the evergreen N. betuloides, N. dombeyi, and N. nitida. Their distribution has a northern limit of 33°S, bound by the arid conditions in the north, and a southern limit of 56°S, constrained by the absence of land in the south (Fig. 1) [34]. Notably, the temperate forests in the southernmost part of Chile (53–56°S) are inhabited by just three of the six Nothofagus species–N. pumilio, N. antarctica, and N. betuloides. This species scarcity is assumed to be due to the population bottlenecks that occurred during the Last Glacial Maximum, as well as extreme temperatures and precipitation changes in the region [22, 27, 33, 47]. The hybridization of Nothofagus is frequently observed in latitudes above 42°S, where there is higher species diversity [46]. Nothofagus nitida x N. dombeyi hybrids are found at 40°S [38], as are hybrids of N. nitida x N. betuloides at 40°S [10], (Premoli, 1994), N. alpina x N. obliqua at 35°S [11], N. nervosa x N. obliqua at 40°S [13, 15, 32], N. glauca x N. obliqua at 36°S [9], and N. dombeyi x N. antarctica at 40–41°S [45, 50].

Map of Nothofagus distribution (blue dots) in South America from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility [17]

Nothofagus pumilio x N. antarctica hybrids have been documented at both central and more southern latitudes, mostly on the Argentine side of the continent. The putative hybrids found in N. pumilio and N. antarctica forests in the Tierra del Fuego region (54°S) had leaf morphologies differing from those of pure N. pumilio and N. antarctica in size and the distance between the widest width to base [6]. Evidence of N. pumilio and N. antarctica hybridization was also observed in mid- and southern-latitudinal regions (36°S to 44°S and 51°S to 55°S) in Argentina [6, 40, 42], [

46]. The hybrid zones of N. pumilio and N. antarctica at 42–44°S in particular are thought to be due to the West–East direction of the wind in the area contributing to pollen dispersal from N. pumilio, growing at higher altitudes in the west, to N. antarctica at lower altitudes in the east. A clear north–south division of haplotype distribution between N. pumilio and N. antarctica was observed at 42°S, suggesting that the northern and southern Nothofagus populations form separate and segregated lineages within both species [46]. According to paleoecological data, the hybrid populations at 42°S diverged from the pure ancestral population about 26,700 years ago [48]. However, other studies also suggest that the hybridization and introgression events of Nothofagus occurred far before the last glacial maximum [1, 39]. Marine transgressions that took place during the Eocene-Miocene led to a paleogeographic segmentation preventing gene flow, as well as the divergence of Nothofagus chloroplast lineages at 42–43°S [1, 25, 39, 40].

Having been detected in a multitude of genera, including those of Quercus, Betula, Argyroxiphium, and Brassica, the introgression of chloroplast DNA (chloroplast capture) in Nothofagus is facilitated by the fact that N. pumilio and N. antarctica share a common pollen type (Nothofagus dombeyi) [4, 20, 44]. It is possible that after large-scale disturbance events, N. antarctica, characterized by their small population size and ability to thrive across diverse and disturbed habitats, acted as the maternal parent and received pollen from N. pumilio. This could have led to the formation and colonization of hybrid progeny that contain N. antarctica cpDNAs but largely N. pumilio nuclear DNA, leading to chloroplast capture [1, 39]. Such chloroplast capture seems to be widespread, as Acosta & Premoli [1] documented 10 locations in which all species present shared chloroplast haplotypes. However, not all studies show unidirectional introgression–Soliani et al. [48] present evidence for bi-directional backcrosses in some populations with a contribution ratio that is close to equal for both N. pumilio and N. antarctica species in Argentina.

In this study, we analyze Nothofagus hybridization and introgression in Chile by characterizing the morphological and genetic variation in N. pumilio, N. antarctica, and putative hybrids in the field. Though past research has found that N. pumilio and N. antarctica hybrids have different leaf dimensions compared to pure parental species, they did not evaluate if the hybrids display higher leaf fluctuating asymmetry [6]. Here, we examine whether putative N. pumilio x N. antarctica hybrids (identified as having distinct leaf morphology from the pure species, [6]in Southern Chile (51–53°S) have differing leaf ridge, vein, and area ratios and higher leaf fluctuation asymmetry than pure species. Since identifying plant species through morphological analysis alone may be inaccurate due to environmental or epistatic effects [50], we complement our morphological study with molecular analyses using nuclear genetic markers and chloroplast DNA. Specifically, we conducted phylogenetic and population genetic analyses of N. pumilio and N. antarctica species at 51–53°S and extended the analysis to include additional samples of N. betuloides, N. dombeyi, and N. nitida from 51–53°S and the 41–42°S regions.

Methods

Nothofagus collection

We collected leaf samples from the forests near Estancia Pingo Salvaje, Chile (−51.5359, −72.5669) in December 2022. A total of 10 N. pumilio, 11 N. antarctica, and 23 putative hybrid individuals were identified among healthy living trees with leaves within reach. Detailed information on the collection locations can be accessed via Dryad. 10 leaves were collected from the side branches of each tree, flattened with a clear sheet of plastic on 0.7 cm × 0.7 cm gridded paper, and photographed under natural lighting.

During the following March, October, and November, we collected additional samples from 125 trees from 14 populations in northern (41–42°S) and southern regions (51–53°S; Fig. 2A) of Chile for DNA analyses. 33 N. pumilio, 29 N. antarctica, 29 N. betuloides, and 6 N. dombeyi trees were sampled in the southern regions, and 1 N. pumilio, 2 N. antarctica, 16 N. dombeyi, and 9 N. nitida were sampled in the northern regions (see Fig. 2. for numbers in each population). The plant species were identified without accounting for the possibility of hybrids (Fig. 2B), and the leaves were preserved in individual tea bags with Drierite prior to DNA extraction. A previously deposited voucher specimen in the Royal Botanic Garden Edinburgh herbarium catalog [Voucher Number: E00031947 (N. pumilio), E00027301 (N. antarctica), E00112612 (N. betuloides), E00031945(N. dombeyi), E00031989 (N. nitida)] was used to confirm the species identifications.

Morphology analysis

A total of 440 N. pumilio, N. antarctica, and putative hybrid leaves collected from 44 trees during December 2022 were used for morphology analysis. We counted the number of veins and ridges on the right and left sides of the leaves and measured the dimensions of the leaves on ImageJ using the 0.7 cm × 0.7 cm grids as a size standard (Figs. 3 and 4). The leaf length was measured from the apex to the base, and the maximum leaf width was measured perpendicularly from the midrib to the outer margins on the right and left sides of the leaf. We then calculated the fluctuating asymmetry value for the length and width of each leaf using the equation Fluctuating Asymmetry =L-R(L+R)2 where L is the length or width of the left side of the leaf, and R is the length or width of the right side of the leaf [52]. The area of each leaf was also determined using the particle analysis function on ImageJ.

Leaf width and length measurements used to calculate fluctuating asymmetry on a putative hybrid leaf. The leaf veins (outlined in white) and ridges are also shown

Leaves of (A) N. pumilio, (B) N. antarctica, and (C) putative hybrids. They were collected at 52°S and photographed on 0.7 cm × 0.7 cm gridded paper

We performed the Shapiro test using R [43] to evaluate each variable's normality and took the non-normal variables’ square root for normal transformation. We then compared the means of the ridge-to-vein ratio, ridge-to-area ratio, and fluctuating asymmetry values by leaf width and length using p-values from ANOVA and the Tukey test. Linear regression models were also constructed to investigate the relationship between the leaf area and the fluctuating asymmetry values between N. pumilio, N. antarctica, and putative hybrids.

DNA extraction and chloroplast sequencing

We extracted DNA from the leaves of 125 Nothofagus trees using the DNeasy® Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), following the standard procedure for plant material. A single leaf or half a leaf (< 20 mg) was used for DNA extraction, depending on leaf size; the tissue was frozen at −20 °C before being fragmented by metal beads using the Tissuelyser II prior to extraction. We confirmed DNA quantity and quality with Nanodrop and the Quantus™ DNA Fluorometer.

For cpDNA sequencing, we used 5 phenotypically N. pumilio and 7 phenotypically N. antarctica individuals from three different populations (S1, S2, and S3) at 52°S. For each individual, the matK, rbcL, and the atpB-rbcL spacer region were amplified using primer mixes [29–31, 54] (Table 1). The PCR mix included 1 μl of DNA, 1 μl of a mix of 2 primers (2 μM per primer), 2 μl OneTaq® Standard Reaction Buffer (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Massachusetts, USA), 0.05 μl OneTaq® Hot Start DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Massachusetts, USA), 0.25 μl dNTP (10 mM) (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Massachusetts, USA), and RNase-free H2O in a total reaction volume of 10 μl. After PCR, we confirmed the amplification of DNA with gel electrophoresis. We removed unincorporated primers and dNTPs with Exo-SAP-ITTM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and sent samples to Retrogen, Inc. for Sanger sequencing. We cleaned, aligned, and generated consensus sequences for each individual with Geneious Prime 2023.2.1 (https://www.geneious.com). We then pairwise-aligned the sequences to known Nothofagus matK, rbcL, and the atpB-rbcL spacer region reference sequences on GenBank with Fagus crenata as an outgroup (Data available on Dryad). We used Bayesian analysis in Mr.Bayes [21]to create phylogenetic trees (Total Chain Length was 1,100,000 with a subsample frequency of 200 and a burn-in length of 110,000).

Microsatellite sequencing and analysis

A total of 125 Nothofagus individuals were genotyped for the 5 Nothofagus species (34 N. pumilio, 31 N. antarctica, 29 N. betuloides, 22 N. dombeyi, and 9 N. nitida) (Table 2). We used 8 different microsatellite loci (Npum1, Npum3, Npum10, Npum13, Npum9, Npum11, Npum18, and oak_pum43) [35]. The PCR mix included 1 μl of DNA, 2.5 μl of primer mix, 12.5 μl of Type-IT Master Mix (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), 0.25 μl of Recombinant Albumin (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Massachusetts, USA), and RNase-free H2O with a total volume of 25 μl. DNA amplification was confirmed after PCR using gel electrophoresis with 1% agarose gel. We diluted each sample two-fold and mixed 2 μl of the dilution with 18 μl of Hi-DiTM Formamide (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) and 0.3 μl of GeneScanTM 500 LizTM Size Standard (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The samples were genotyped by the Cornell Institution of Biotechnology in an Applied BioSystems 3730xl, and the microsatellite peaks were read and analyzed with Geneious Prime 2023.

Population genetic analysis

STRUCTURE [41]was used to analyze population structure and plots were created with Genetic-Structure-Tools (https://github.com/DrewWham/Genetic-Structure-Tools). We did runs with K numbers ranging from 1–7, both with and without the locprior model, assuming species admixture using phenotype, geographic location, and collection site data [24]. For each K, we performed ten independent runs with a 500,000 burn-in period and 500,000 repeats. We determined the optimal number of populations (K) based on the mean estimate of the posterior probability of the data for a given K (LnP(D)). The population analysis results were plotted using R [43]. We also calculated the FST and FIS values for populations within each species and between N. pumilio and N. antarctica specifically using the hierfstat package [18].

Results

Leaf morphology and fluctuating asymmetry

We compared the ratio of the number of ridges to the number of veins on each leaf of N. pumilio, N. antarctica, and the putative hybrids collected at 52°S (Fig. 4). Nothofagus antarctica had the highest mean ratio (5.726 ± 0.111), while N. pumilio had the lowest mean ratio (2.170 ± 0.0141, Fig. 5A). The putative hybrids had a mean ratio similar to that of N. antarctica (5.562 ± 0.0879). The mean ratios differed significantly across groups (ANOVA; F2, 437 = 363.8, P < 0.0001). The Tukey post-hoc test showed a significant difference in the means of the ridge-to-vein ratio between N. pumilio and N. antarctica (P < 0.0001) and N. pumilio and hybrids (P < 0.0001). However, there was no significant difference between the mean ridge-to-vein ratio between N. antarctica and the putative hybrids (P = 0.437).

For the ratio between the number of ridges in each leaf to the leaf area, N. antarctica had the highest square-root transformed mean (4.067 ± 0.0688, Fig. 5B). Nothofagus pumilio had a lower ratio (3.285 ± 0.0559), and the putative hybrids had an intermediate ratio (3.811 ± 0.0438). The ratios differed across groups (ANOVA; F2, 437 = 38.7, P < 0.0001) and all three groups differed from each other, as a Tukey post-hoc test found significant differences between N. pumilio and N. antarctica (P < 0.0001), N. pumilio and putative hybrids (P < 0.0001), and N. antarctica and putative hybrids (P = 0.00302).

The square-root transformed mean values for leaf length fluctuating asymmetry were highest for the putative hybrid leaves (0.269 ± 0.00565), lowest for N. pumilio (0.0597 ± 0.00791, Fig. 6A), and intermediate for N. antarctica (0.117 ± 0.00835). Chloroplast DNA analysis

Analysis of the chloroplast DNA sequence of rbcL (1,290 bp), the atpB-rbcL spacer region (962 bp), and the matK gene (2,435 bp) of 5 N. pumilio and 7 N. antarctica samples collected at 52°S did not provide evidence for differentiation between species (Fig. 9, Bayesian phylogeny). All of the samples (irrespective of species) had identical sequences for all three chloroplast genetic markers, but each gene had a different species match to Genbank entries (Genbank accession numbers PV239901-PV239906, one individual for each species/gene). For the atpB-rbcL spacer region, our samples (phenotypically N. pumilio or N. antarctica) were in the same clade as N. pumilio and N. betuloides GenBank sequences (with 6 and 1 differences, respectively), and the single N. antarctica GenBank sequence was a sister species to our samples (with 4 differences). For the rbcL gene, our samples were in the same clade as the N. dombeyi GenBank sequence (1 difference) and sisters to a clade containing N. antarctica, N. pumilio, N. betuloides, and N. nitida (10, 17, 5 and 4 differences, respectively). Finally, for the matK gene, our samples were in the same clade as the N. antarctica GenBank sequence (2 differences in 1,014 bp–the Genbank sequence was shorter than our matK sequence) with N. nitida as a sister species (11 differences in 1,019 bp); no matK sequences from N. pumilio, N. dombeyi, or N. betuloides were available in GenBank at the time of our analyses (Fig. 9).

All of the N. dombeyi individuals were placed in the same population as N. betuloides and N. antarctica. Discussion

While the various Nothofagus species and their putative hybrids differ substantially in morphology, there were no diagnostic genetic differences within our samples across the three chloroplast genes and eight nuclear microsatellite DNA markers. In particular, N. pumilio and N. antarctica possess considerable overlap in cpDNA and nuclear microsatellite population composition. The other three species of Nothofagus (N. betuloides, N. dombeyi and N. nitida) also had a substantial overlap in population structure.

Nothofagus pumilio and N. antarctica have distinct phenotypes

Nothofagus pumilio and N. antarctica have distinct leaf phenotypes that match the descriptions in the existing literature [16]. While they are similar in leaf area, phenotypically pure N. pumilio leaves have crenated edges with round lobes that are separated by numerous veins, while N. antarctica leaves have serrated edges and fewer veins (Fig. 2B and Fig. 4). Such phenotypes explain why the N. pumilio leaves have a significantly lower ridge-to-vein ratio and ridge-to-area ratio than N. antarctica leaves (Fig. 5).

Putative Nothofagus hybrids have intermediate leaf phenotypes

We identified both parental species and their putative hybrids by morphology. We thus start with a caveat that while we did not use fluctuating asymmetry to assign individuals as putative hybrids (we focused on selecting trees with leaves that had a distinct morphology from N. pumilio and N. antarctica), fluctuating asymmetry could have influenced our decisions. If the species are indeed overlapping genetically as microsatellite and chloroplast DNA sequences suggest, their morphological differentiation would be potentially caused by few loci or by phenotypic plasticity.

We found no significant differences in the ridge-to-vein ratios between N. antarctica and the putative hybrid leaves. However, the mean leaf ridge-to-area ratio of the putative hybrids is significantly higher than that of N. pumilio and significantly lower than that of N. antarctica (Fig. 5). Putative Nothofagus hybrids therefore have an intermediate leaf morphology distinguishable from both parental species. This observation is consistent with previous observations of the N. pumilio and N. antarctica hybrids at 41ºS displaying intermediate numbers of leaf marginal lobes as well as the findings in other plant genera, such as Arabidopsis and Quercus [5, 7, 19, 26, 51]. Our characterization of intermediate leaf morphology adds data to the morphological classification of Nothofagus hybrids, complementing previous findings that demonstrated intermediate characteristics in bark roughness and color, stem straightness, and crown form in putative hybrids of N. pumilio and N. antarctica [47].

Putative Nothofagus hybrids have higher leaf fluctuating asymmetry

Although the leaf morphology of putative hybrids was intermediate between the two parental species, their leaf fluctuating asymmetry was significantly higher than that of the parental species, N. pumilio and N. antarctica (Fig. 6). Such high fluctuating asymmetry was present for both the leaf length and leaf width. The higher fluctuating asymmetry of hybrid individuals has been attributed to developmental instability caused by the disruption of coadapted gene complexes during hybridization [8, 23]. Indeed, our linear regression between the fluctuating asymmetry values of leaf length and leaf width showed a positive correlation for only the putative hybrids, indicating that the factors that drive asymmetry operate on width and length simultaneously (Fig. 7) and suggesting that the two could be caused by underlying developmental disruptions in hybrids.

We also showed that the leaf area of hybrids had a negative correlation with the fluctuating asymmetry of leaf length and width: larger leaves were less likely to be asymmetrical (Fig. 8). In contrast, the N. pumilio leaf area was negatively correlated only with the fluctuating asymmetry of leaf width, while the N. antarctica leaf area had a positive correlation only with the leaf length fluctuating asymmetry. In other words, larger N. pumilio leaves are likely to be less asymmetrical in width, while larger N. antarctica leaves are likely to be more asymmetrical in length. However, since the adjusted R2 values for all of the models were very low (the highest R2 value being 0.2009), there may be external factors that significantly affect both leaf size and the fluctuating asymmetry of leaf length and width for all of the study species and their hybrids.

Nothofagus pumilio and N. antarctica have identical chloroplast DNA

While cpDNA sequences in GenBank show differences between N. pumilio and N. antarctica species, our N. pumilio and N. antarctica samples (populations S1-S3, 52°S) possessed identical cpDNA sequences for all genes (Fig. 9). Furthermore, our samples differed from the conspecific GenBank cpDNA sequences (see results for details). A possible explanation for the identical chloroplast sequences between phenotypically distinct N. antarctica and N. pumilio individuals in the 52°S region is chloroplast capture through introgression. Indeed, introgression events have been documented in Argentine and Chilean Nothofagus populations near our collection sites [1].

Studies have hypothesized that backcrosses to N. pumilio or N. antarctica might have caused such introgression [1, 40, 48]. Our putative hybrids (if correctly identified) resembled N. antarctica morphologically, perhaps suggesting backcrosses towards N. antarctica (no significant difference in leaf ridge-to-vein ratio between N. antarctica and putative hybrids,Fig. 5). This supports the findings of Quiroga et al. [42], where no difference was found for maximum width, total and minimum length, and the ratio of total and minimum length between N. antarctica and hybrid leaves. Similar introgression patterns have also been observed between N. nervosa and N. obliqua, which exhibit asymmetric late-generation backcrosses towards N. obliqua at high altitudes and backcrosses in both directions at lower altitudes [13]. El Mujtar et al. 13 suggest that the preference towards N. obliqua backcrossing at high altitudes might be due to a selective advantage over other hybrids. Considering that N. antarctica is a generalist species with high adaptive capacity, while N. pumilio prefers well-drained soil, the backcrossing at 52°S might have given hybrids an adaptive advantage, perhaps during unfavorable or variable climatic periods such as continental glaciations [40, 47].

However, these results should be taken with caution, as it has been proven that the use of chloroplast DNA to study phylogenetic relationships between plants can lead to inaccurate conclusions–nuclear DNA analysis as well as ample taxon sampling is needed to properly elucidate the relationships between the Nothofagus species [49].

Low nuclear genetic differentiation between species

Previous studies of nuclear ITS regions from sympatric Nothofagus populations identified N. antarctica as a sister clade to the evergreen species N. betuloides, N. dombeyi, and N. nitida, while N. pumilio diverged earlier [1, 39]. These results suggest that the 5 morphologically distinct species also have distinguishable genomes and well-supported phylogenetic relationships based on nuclear gene data. However, our analyses using eight unlinked variable loci revealed unexpectedly low population structure between species and across most populations. In every STRUCTURE analysis, the calculated values of K [14] were lower than the number of morphologically defined species or populations. The best value of K for all populations/species was 4 rather than 5 (the total number of species) (Fig. 10). K was 3 for N. pumilio, N. antarctica, N. betuloides, and N. dombeyi within the southern populations (Fig. 11) and only 1 for the southern N. pumilio and N. antarctica populations (Fig. 12). While the morphological differences in our samples might be due to phenotypic plasticity, we find this unlikely since many of the Nothofagus species are sympatric, and we collected samples from different species within the same tree stands.

While there was variation within and between populations, STRUCTURE showed that every species had individuals assigned to populations in common with other species (Figs. 10, 11 and 12). Moreover, though past studies classify N. pumilio and N. antarctica as clearly distinct species [48], we were unable to tell sympatric (51–53°S) N. pumilio and N. antarctica apart with microsatellite markers (STRUCTURE, Fig. 12, best K = 1) despite high genetic diversity within each species. The FST between N. pumilio and N. antarctica was lower than the FST calculated within populations of any of the Nothofagus species (Table 3). As found by Premoli et al. [40], such results could be explained by the recurrent hybridization and divergence between N. pumilio and N. antarctica through glacial–interglacial cycles. Another possible explanation is that phenotypic differences between these otherwise genetically homogenous populations could be driven by only a small proportion of the Nothofagus genome. For example, this surprising genome homogeneity between morphologically different species has been observed in the seed finch species Sporophila plumbea and S. beltoni, which differ in plumage and beak color but have only six highly differentiated loci [36], suggesting that assortative mating within morphotypes is not strong enough to prevent genome mixing. Our analyses suggest that a similar scenario might be true for N. pumilio and N. antarctica, particularly within the 51–53°S region. Future studies with more extensive genetic sequencing will be needed to clarify the extent of genetic differentiation between these Nothofagus species.

While it is possible that introgression mixed up sympatric populations, it is notable that even distant collection sites (41–42°S, 51–52°S, 53°S) of N. pumilio and N. antarctica had very low differentiation (Table 3), with large overlaps in STRUCTURE population assignment. Although we sampled few individuals from the northern populations (1 N. pumilio and 2 N. antarctica from 41–41°S), this result was unexpected because prior studies showed clear population differentiation between the northern and southern Nothofagus populations [33, 46, 48]. Therefore, sequencing more northern N. pumilio and N. antarctica individuals would improve the accuracy of our analysis and confirm whether the lack of genetic differentiation between the two species is limited to the more southern regions near 51–53°S.

While we did not quantify morphological differences for the three other Nothofagus species used in our study–the evergreen N. betuloides, N. dombeyi, and N. nitida–their leaves are easily identifiable. Nothofagus betuloides has leaves much thicker and smaller than those of N. pumilio and N. antarctica, and both N. dombeyi and N. nitida have thick yet bigger and more pointed leaves than N. betuloides. Nothofagus nitida leaves were collected exclusively on Chiloé Island (population N3) and were larger than N. dombeyi leaves. However, we cannot exclude the possibility that some N. dombeyi individuals were mistaken for N. nitida since the two species were less morphologically distinct, and STRUCTURE analyses overlap in population assignment for N. dombeyi and N. nitida (Figs. 2B and Fig. 10). Previous studies have also reported evidence of hybridization between the deciduous N. antarctica and the evergreen N. dombeyi at 42°S, which could explain the genetic similarities observed between N. antarctica and N. dombeyi in our analysis at 51–53°S [50]. If the similarity between species was indeed caused by introgression, then our data also supports evidence of a previously undocumented hybridization between N. antarctica and another evergreen species, N. betuloides, explaining the substantial genetic overlap between N. antarctica, N. betuloides, and N. dombeyi (Fig. 11).

Hybridization between tree species may serve as an evolutionary strategy for successful recolonization following habitat disruptions. In South America, Nothofagus forests have experienced repeated incidents of environmental change–such as marine transgressions and glaciations–during which hybrid individuals may have possessed advantageous traits that enhanced their survival. Today, similar large-scale disturbances continue to affect Chilean forests, particularly through recurrent wildfires since the Last Glacial Maximum. In this context, assessing the extent of Nothofagus hybridization and understanding how species admixture affects an individual’s developmental instability or the genetic health of the population may offer insights into the adaptive capacity and resilience of these species under changing environmental conditions.

Conclusions

Our data show higher fluctuating asymmetry in putative N. pumilio x N. antarctica hybrids and very low differentiation between Nothofagus species despite clear morphological differences. In particular, N. pumilio and N. antarctica shared identical chloroplast sequences and had no distinct population structure in either sympatric or allopatric populations. While more comprehensive sequencing data and wider taxon sampling are needed to confirm if this lack of differentiation extends across the genome, our results suggest either widespread hybridization and introgression or a lack of assortative mating and homogenous genomes with only a few loci responsible for the morphological variation in these species.
