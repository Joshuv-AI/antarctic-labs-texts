Noninvasive Hemodynamic Characterization of Cardiohepatic Syndrome in the Cardiac Intensive Care Unit
Authors: Ahsan Butt, Mitchell Padkins, P. E. Miller, Jason N. Katz, Dustin B. Hillerson, Drew N. Rosenbaum, Marc D. Samsky, Maan Jokhadar, Jacob C. Jentzer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients with cardiohepatic syndrome are at higher risk of adverse outcomes in the cardiac intensive care unit. We hypothesized that cardiohepatic syndrome phenotypes would exhibit differences in their clinical and transthoracic echocardiogram hemodynamic profiles, portending a higher risk of mortality.MethodsWe included unique CICU patients with an admission diagnosis of heart failure from 2007 to 2018 with available data for 1 or more admission liver function tests. Echocardiographic variables were extracted from patients who had a transthoracic echocardiogram within 1 day of admission. We assigned patients to 1 of 4 mutually exclusive cardiohepatic syndrome phenotype groups: normal, hepatocellular, cholestasis, and combined.ResultsWe included 3992 CICU patients; 2483 had an available transthoracic echocardiogram. Patients with cholestasis had more right ventricular systolic dysfunction and congestion, whereas those with hepatocellular injury had more left ventricular systolic dysfunction and poor forward flow; hemodynamics and biventricular function were worst in the combined group. In‐hospital mortality occurred in 14.6% and was higher for patients with cholestasis or hepatocellular injury, especially those with greater extent/severity and worse biventricular function or poor hemodynamics. After adjustment, only the group with hepatocellular injury (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 1.35 [95% CI, 1.04–1.75], P=0.03) and the combined group (adjusted OR, 1.75 [95% CI, 1.30–2.34], P<0.001) remained at higher risk. One‐year mortality was highest in the combined group (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 1.29 [95% CI, 1.10–1.51], P=0.002).ConclusionsCardiohepatic syndrome phenotypes are associated with distinct echocardiographic profiles. Patients with the combined cardiohepatic syndrome phenotype were at highest risk of mortality, particularly if this was combined with poor cardiac function.

Heart failure (HF) is a leading cause of hospitalization in the United States, and HF hospitalization is associated with high rates of morbidity and mortality particularly for critically ill patients requiring admission to the cardiac intensive care unit (CICU). 1 , 2 There has been increased recognition of the consequences of noncardiovascular end‐organ dysfunction, especially of the kidneys or liver, complicating decompensated HF. 3 Patients with acute HF and abnormal hepatic biomarkers, termed “cardiohepatic syndrome” (CHS), may be at higher risk of adverse outcomes. 3 Different patterns of abnormal hepatic biomarkers (commonly termed “liver function tests” [LFTs]) may distinguish hepatocellular injury (eg, aspartate aminotransferase [AST] and alanine aminotransferase [ALT]) from cholestasis (eg, alkaline phosphatase [ALK] and total bilirubin [TB]). Abnormal LFTs reflecting these disease processes are frequently observed in patients with acute HF, with prior studies noting a prevalence of 11% to 62%. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7

Prior studies of patients with acute HF have found variable associations between abnormal LFTs and increased mortality, providing limited insights into the underlying pathophysiology and causes of these associations. 5 , 6 We recently reported similar findings in CICU patients with and without HF. 8 Recent studies of patients with acute HF and abnormal LFTs have led to the classification of CHS into different proposed subtypes, including Type I CHS, with hepatocellular injury attributed to decreased cardiac output (ie, hypoxic hepatitis) and Type II CHS, with cholestasis attributed to increased congestion (ie, congestive hepatopathy). 3 Although these CHS subtypes have been proposed and associated with prognosis, the specific hemodynamic profile underlying each pattern of LFT abnormalities has not been studied. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 9

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is an increasingly used noninvasive tool with the ability to assess cardiac physiology and hemodynamics and has previously been shown to inform prognosis in CICU patients with HF. 10 , 11 , 12 Accordingly, we sought to evaluate the clinical profile, survival outcomes and hemodynamic TTE findings associated with each CHS phenotype among critically ill patients with HF admitted to the CICU. We hypothesized that distinct CHS phenotypes would exhibit differences in their clinical and TTE hemodynamic profiles and that CHS would portend a higher risk of in‐hospital and 1‐year mortality.

METHODS

The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article and its Supplementary Material.

Study Design and Population

The Institutional Review Board of Mayo Clinic approved this retrospective observational cohort study as minimal risk to patients who had already consented to use their medical records for research. We identified consecutive unique adult CICU patients with an admission diagnosis of acute or chronic HF from 2007 to 2018 and included those with available data for 1 or more of the admission LFT values of interest (AST, ALT, ALK, TB); patients who were missing all these laboratory values were excluded. Admission diagnoses were defined as all International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes documented within 1 day of CICU admission and were not mutually exclusive.

Data Sources

Demographic, clinical, laboratory, and outcome data were extracted from the Mayo Clinic database. 13 Admission laboratory values were defined as those closest to CICU admission or the first value after CICU admission; time stamps were unavailable. The Charlson Comorbidity Index, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment score, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation‐III score, and Mayo CICU Admission Risk Score were calculated using established algorithms. 14 , 15 The Mayo CICU Admission Risk Score is a novel CICU‐specific in‐hospital mortality prediction score that was derived and validated in this Mayo Clinic CICU population, outperforms Sequential Organ Failure Assessment and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation in this cohort, and does not include any LFTs. 16

Echocardiographic variables of interest were extracted electronically from existing Mayo Clinic databases for patients who had a TTE within 1 day of CICU admission (Table S1). 12 The severity of left ventricular systolic dysfunction (LVSD) was classified according to sex‐specific American Society of Echocardiography guidelines based on LV ejection fraction. 17 Right ventricular systolic dysfunction (RVSD) was assessed on a semiquantitative scale based on the overall assessment of the interpreting physician based on available measurements. Spectral and tissue Doppler measurements were used to calculate right and left heart hemodynamic variables (Table S2). 18 Right atrial pressure (RAP) was estimated based on the size and collapsibility of the inferior vena cava in the majority of patients, with an invasively measured RAP substituted when available. Congestion was defined as elevated RAP (right sided) or elevated estimated left atrial pressure (left sided) based on the medial mitral E/e′ ratio.

Exposures and Outcomes of Interest

Abnormal LFTs were defined as values above the upper limit of the normal range for our laboratory: AST, 48 U/L; ALT, 55 U/L; ALK, 129 U/L; TB 1.2 mg/dL. 8 We defined hepatocellular injury as abnormal AST or ALT and cholestasis as abnormal ALK or TB. We assigned patients to 1 of 4 mutually exclusive CHS phenotypes: normal (ie, all LFTs below the upper limit of normal), hepatocellular only, cholestasis only, and combined (ie, both hepatocellular and cholestasis). To determine the CHS group, missing LFTs were assumed to be normal. Due to the presence of complete outcome data for in‐hospital mortality and potential for loss to follow‐up after hospital discharge, in‐hospital mortality (including CICU deaths) was selected as the primary outcome. The secondary outcome was all‐cause 1‐year mortality (including in‐hospital deaths), with survival duration determined based on Mayo Clinic databases. 16

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range), with groups compared using the Kruskal–Wallis rank‐sum test. Categorical variables were summarized as numbers (percentage), with groups compared using the Pearson hi‐square test. Due to complete outcome data for in‐hospital mortality, logistic regression was selected as the main analysis method. Odds ratio (OR) and 95% CI values for in‐hospital mortality were calculated using logistic regression before and after multivariable adjustment. Discrimination of logistic regression models was evaluated with area under the receiver‐operator characteristic curve C‐statistics. Survival right‐censored at 1 year was evaluated using Kaplan–Meier curves, with groups compared using the log‐rank test. Hazard ratio (HR) and 95% CI values for 1‐year mortality were calculated using Cox proportional‐hazards regression before and after multivariable adjustment. The proportional‐hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residual tests and plots, and violations of the proportional‐hazards assumptions were identified justifying our use of logistic regression as the primary analysis. Multivariable regression models were adjusted for the following variables selected a priori based on clinically relevant and known associations with mortality in the CICU population: age, sex, race, Charlson Comorbidity Index, history of liver disease, Mayo CICU Admission Risk Score, admission diagnosis of acute coronary syndrome (ACS), number of vasoactive drugs, use of positive‐pressure ventilation (PPV), and receipt of cardiac catheterization or percutaneous coronary intervention. 15 To avoid potential misclassification of patients with elevated hepatocellular injury markers due to cardiac myocyte necrosis, we performed a sensitivity analysis excluding patients with ACS. Statistical analysis was performed using BlueSky 13.1 Pro (BlueSky LLC, Chicago, IL).

RESULTS

Study Population

Out of a data set of 12 428 unique CICU patient admissions, 6008 (48.3%) had an admission diagnosis of HF. Among these, 3992 (66.4%) had available data for 1 or more LFT values on admission and were included in the final study population (Figure 1). Patients who were excluded due to missing LFT data had lower severity of illness scores, fewer critical care admission diagnoses, and lower CICU (4.7%) and in‐hospital (7.6%) mortality (all P<0.001). The median age of the study population was 70.3 (60.2, 79.8) years, and 37.4% were women (Table 1). ACS was present in 36.0%, including ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction in 18.4%. Critical care admission diagnoses included respiratory failure in 42.2%, shock in 27.6%, and cardiac arrest in 15.0%. Critical care therapies included vasoactive drugs in 42.6%, PPV in 44.8%, dialysis in 9.5%, and temporary mechanical circulatory support in 15.1%. The median Mayo CICU Admission Risk Score was 3 (2–5).

Admission LFT Values

LFT values were abnormal in 1995 (50.0%) patients, including hepatocellular injury in 1569 (39.3%) patients and cholestasis in 947 (23.7%) patients (Figure 1). Hepatocellular injury reflected isolated AST elevation in 40.2%, isolated ALT elevation in 7.3%, and combined AST/ALT elevation in 52.5%. Cholestasis reflected isolated ALK elevation in 26.7%, isolated TB elevation in 53.2%, and combined ALK/TB elevation in 20.1%. Hepatocellular injury was more common in patients with ACS or shock, whereas cholestasis was less common in patients with ACS and more common in patients with shock (Figure S1). Most patients classified as hepatocellular injury (51.5%) had ACS, including 56.6% of those with isolated AST elevations. Patients were assigned to the following CHS phenotypes (Figure 1): normal, 50.0%; hepatocellular only, 26.3%; cholestasis only, 10.7%; and combined, 13.0%. Substantial differences were observed across CHS phenotypes in clinical characteristics (Table 1) and admission laboratory values (Table S3), reflecting greater overall severity of illness in patients with CHS (especially the combined phenotype).

Echocardiographic Findings

A total of 2483 (62.2%) patients had a TTE within 1 day of admission. The median LV ejection fraction was 37 (25–54) %; 617 (26.2%) patients had preserved LV ejection fraction, and 781 (33.2%) patients had severe LVSD. Among patients with available data, 702 (40.0%) had moderate or severe RVSD. Admission TTE findings differed substantially across the CHS phenotypes (Table 2). Patients with cholestasis had more RVSD and congestion, whereas those with hepatocellular injury had more LVSD and the combined group had the highest prevalence of biventricular dysfunction (Figure S2). Doppler estimates of forward flow were lower in patients with either hepatocellular injury or cholestasis and were lowest in patients with a combined phenotype. Estimated RAP was higher in patients with either hepatocellular injury or cholestasis, and estimated left atrial pressure was highest in patients with cholestasis. Biventricular systolic function and forward flow worsened, and biventricular filling pressures increased, in patients with a greater extent of either hepatocellular injury or cholestasis. After excluding patients who received PPV within 24 hours, markers of RV function and RV congestion remained worse in patients with cholestasis (including those with isolated cholestasis or a combined phenotype).

In‐Hospital Mortality

A total of 583 (14.6%) patients died during hospitalization, including 323 (8.1%) CICU deaths. Before and after adjustment, patients with either hepatocellular injury or cholestasis were at higher risk of in‐hospital mortality (Figure 2A), with a stepwise increase in mortality with each abnormal LFT (Figure 2B). Both the extent and severity of individual LFT abnormalities and cholestasis/hepatocellular injury predicted in‐hospital mortality (Figure 3). In‐hospital mortality varied according to specific individual LFT abnormalities and their combinations (Figure 4). In‐hospital mortality varied according to CHS phenotype (Figure 5), being highest in the combined group; similar patterns were observed in patients with and without shock and among those who did and did not receive PPV; patients with shock or PPV had substantially higher mortality (Figure S5). After adjustment, the hepatocellular injury and combined groups remained at higher risk, but the isolated cholestasis group was not (P=0.21). Differences in in‐hospital mortality were observed according to the CHS phenotype and the presence and severity of both LVSD (Figure 6A) and RVSD (Figure 6B), as well as for Doppler hemodynamic variables reflecting biventricular filling pressures and systolic function, with the highest mortality for patients in the combined phenotype who had worse hemodynamic abnormalities (Figure S6).

One‐Year Survival

A total of 1420 (35.6%) patients died by 1 year, including in‐hospital deaths; 387 (15.0%) of the 2572 hospital survivors had follow‐up duration less than 1 year but were alive at last follow‐up (lost to follow up). One‐year mortality was higher in patients with cholestasis or hepatocellular injury, with differences according to the severity, extent, and pattern of LFT abnormalities (Figures S7 through S9). One‐year mortality differed according to the CHS phenotype, being lowest in the normal group and highest in the combined group (Figure 7). Despite higher early (eg, in‐hospital) mortality, the hepatocellular group had slightly lower 1‐year mortality than the cholestasis group. Compared with the normal group, 1‐year mortality increased incrementally in each CHS group. After multivariable adjustment, only the combined group was at higher risk, but the hepatocellular and cholestasis groups were not.

Sensitivity Analysis—Patients Without ACS

A total of 2554 patients did not have an admission diagnosis of ACS, including 371 (14.5%) in‐hospital deaths. Patients with and without ACS were generally comparable but differed in several important baseline characteristics (Table S4). Among patients without ACS, hepatocellular injury was present in 32.5% and cholestasis was present in 29.1%, resulting in the following CHS phenotypes: normal, 53.0%; hepatocellular only, 14.5%; cholestasis only, 17.9%; and combined, 14.6%. Among those with an early TTE, the presence of either hepatocellular injury or cholestasis was associated with worse markers of LV systolic function and forward flow, and cholestasis was additionally associated with worse markers of RV systolic function and right‐sided congestion (Tables S5 through S7). Accordingly, the majority of TTE measurements varied by CHS phenotype and were worst in the combined group (Table S5). In patients with and without ACS, in‐hospital mortality was higher in those with either cholestasis or hepatocellular injury with variability according to the pattern of abnormal LFTs (Figure S10) and a stepwise increase in mortality was observed with a greater number of abnormal LFTs (adjusted OR, 1.14 per each [95% CI, 1.02–1.27], P=0.020) in those without ACS. As in the overall cohort, in‐hospital mortality varied across CHS profiles in those with and without ACS (Figure S11), but only the combined phenotype remained associated with higher in‐hospital mortality after adjustment (adjusted OR, 1.54 [95% CI, 1.08–2.17], P=0.016), whereas isolated hepatocellular injury or cholestasis were not. Among patients without ACS, 1‐year mortality was higher in those with either cholestasis or hepatocellular injury with variability according to the pattern of abnormal LFTs and a stepwise increase in mortality was observed with a greater number of abnormal LFTs (Figure S12). Both isolated cholestasis (adjusted HR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.00–1.47], P=0.047) and the combined CHS phenotype (adjusted HR, 1.34 [95% CI, 1.11–1.62], P=0.002) were associated with higher adjusted 1‐year mortality among patients without ACS, whereas isolated hepatocellular injury was not.

DISCUSSION

This is the first large‐scale study to examine the associations between different phenotypes of CHS with hemodynamic TTE findings and in‐hospital and 1‐year mortality among critically ill CICU patients admitted with HF. We found associations between RVSD and right‐sided congestion with cholestasis and associations between LVSD and low forward flow with hepatocellular injury. Although both hepatocellular injury and cholestasis were associated with LVSD and reduced forward flow, the combined CHS phenotype had the worst hemodynamics. In‐hospital mortality varied according to the CHS phenotype and was highest in the combined phenotype, persisting after adjustment for the higher illness severity in this group. One‐year mortality increased incrementally in the hepatocellular, cholestasis, and combined groups; however, following multivariable adjustment, only the combined group remained at higher risk. Both in‐hospital and 1‐year mortality varied according to the extent, severity, and pattern of LFT abnormalities. These data imply that a simple hepatocellular versus cholestatic CHS dichotomy, as previously described, does not capture the true spectrum or pathophysiology of CHS in critically ill patients with HF. Furthermore, our findings emphasize the incremental prognostic importance of CHS combined with TTE findings in CICU patients with HF (particularly a combined phenotype) and underscore the distinct yet overlapping hemodynamic mechanisms underlying hepatocellular injury and cholestasis in these patients.

Prior studies have identified associations between hepatic biomarker derangement and mortality in patients with HF. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 In our prior analysis from this CICU population, the severity and extent of LFT abnormalities were positively associated with in‐hospital and 1‐year mortality. 8 Prior studies have similarly shown associations between elevated LFTs and mortality among patients admitted with HF. 4 , 9 In prior analyses from this CICU cohort, we have identified strong associations between elevated TB (the liver Sequential Organ Failure Assessment subscore), and mortality in the broader CICU population. 19 , 20 Collectively, these studies emphasize that LFT abnormalities reflecting CHS are prognostically important in patients with HF across the spectrum of illness severity. Although the association with hepatic biomarker derangement and mortality has been increasingly recognized, our data suggest the need for considering CHS phenotype rather than single biomarker assessment when determining the effect of liver dysfunction on mortality. This is supported by our finding that in‐hospital mortality varied according to CHS phenotype and was significantly higher in the combined phenotype who had more extensive TTE abnormalities and greater severity of illness. We must emphasize that, although we and others have used the term CHS to denote the presence of abnormal LFTs in the setting of HF for convenience, LFT abnormalities can result from a multitude of noncardiac processes that may not represent true CHS. Accordingly, it is likely an oversimplification to label all LFT abnormalities in patients with HF as “CHS,” pending formal consensus definition of this condition. Although this may be of particular concern when considering potential hepatocellular injury (especially isolated AST elevation) in patients with myocardial infarction, our sensitivity analysis findings in patients without ACS generally supported our main findings in this large and heterogeneous cohort with HF.

Previous studies have not systematically investigated the underlying pathophysiology of CHS, with disagreements about how best to classify patients. Instead, prior studies focused on cardiohepatic interactions have primarily investigated mortality risk in relation to hepatic biomarker abnormalities. For example, in a post hoc analysis of 1134 patients from the SURVIVE (Survival of Patients With Acute Heart Failure in Need of Intravenous Inotropic Support) trial, Nikolaou et al. found abnormal ALK was associated with higher 180‐day mortality and abnormal transaminases were associated with greater 31‐day and 180‐day mortality. 4 Indeed, we have found similar trends in the analysis of hepatic biomarker abnormalities among our CICU population. 8 Prior studies proposing this dichotomy between hepatocellular (Type I CHS) and cholestasis (Type II CHS) have led to uncertainty about how to classify the high‐risk minority with a combined phenotype. In this analysis, we differentiated CHS phenotypes into 3 primary groups, including a combined hepatocellular and cholestasis group. This diverges somewhat from prior conventions, which have created a dichotomy between Type I CHS (hepatocellular injury attributed to decreased hepatic perfusion) and Type II CHS (cholestasis attributed to elevated central venous pressure). 3 We included the combined phenotype as a third CHS phenotype given the frequency of mixed hepatic biomarker elevation, which was justified by our observation that patients with the combined CHS phenotype had the worst hemodynamics, were at highest risk of mortality and outnumbered those with isolated cholestasis.

Our Doppler TTE hemodynamic data call into question the assumptions that hepatocellular injury is purely due to a low‐output state and that cholestasis is purely due to systemic congestion, although these may be the dominant mechanisms in many patients. Indeed, we found that a low output state and systemic congestion were more common in patients with either LFT abnormality, and those with a combined CHS phenotype had the worst LV function and systemic congestion. However, a notable association was observed between cholestasis and either RVSD or elevated RAP, emphasizing the importance of the RV in CHS pathophysiology. Systemic venous congestion and elevated RAP from RV dysfunction has been implicated as an important mechanism underlying multiorgan injury (including hepatocellular injury) in patients with acute HF and cardiogenic shock, including the hemometabolic cardiogenic shock phenotype that is characterized by hepatocellular injury. 21 , 22 , 23 An elevated hepatic venous pressure from systemic congestion will lower the pressure gradient for liver perfusion, particularly during hypotension or shock, potentially resulting in hepatocellular injury. Accordingly, mild hepatocellular injury in acute HF may be more likely due to congestive hepatopathy than overt ischemic hepatitis per se, with a contribution from myocardial release in patients with ACS who comprised most of our patients with isolated hepatocellular abnormalities. Indeed, relatively few patients in this cohort would meet conventional criteria for ischemic hepatitis with transaminases >10 to 20 times above the upper limit of normal. Further studies using invasive hemodynamic assessment as the gold standard would be important to clarify these issues further.

Current risk‐prediction tools are not inclusive of echocardiography in predicting mortality in critically ill patients. The inclusion of hepatic biomarker assessment in risk‐prediction tools is also limited, primarily the TB as in Sequential Organ Failure Assessment or Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation. 24 We argue for the classification of CICU patients with acute HF and abnormal LFTs into different phenotypes of CHS, allowing for improved prognostication. Although only 13% of our final study population were found to have combined CHS, this portended a substantially worsened prognosis compared with the normal group even after adjustment for known risk markers. Furthermore, given our observed associations between different CHS phenotypes and ventricular dysfunction/congestion, we speculate that CHS phenotyping may provide additional guidance in the management of CICU patients. Similarly, altered hemodynamics found on TTE may provide guidance on patients at risk of developing CHS, which may have important prognostic implications. In prior analyses from this CICU cohort, we have demonstrated the utility of TTE for mortality risk stratification. 10 , 12 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 The use of hepatic biomarkers with better specificity for different underlying disease processes may facilitate CHS phenotyping in the future rather than simple labels based on LFT patterns. The present analysis provides additional evidence of the utility of TTE and LFT evaluation to inform valuable prognostic and mechanistic information on CICU patients admitted with HF. Given the relatively routine acquisition of LFTs and TTE among CICU patients with HF, their inclusion into risk‐prediction tools would both be cost effective and easily accessible.

Limitations

This study has several relevant limitations that apply to retrospective cohort analyses, particularly the potential for additional unmeasured confounding variables that have influenced the analysis. Although inclusion of a heterogeneous CICU cohort with HF allowed us to provide broad insights into the epidemiology of CHS, the inclusion of diverse patients (eg, with acute and chronic HF, ACS, and shock) could have affected the specificity of our findings. Notably, the hepatocellular CHS phenotype was the most common CHS phenotype we observed, which in part may be explained by AST release from cardiac muscle in this cohort with 40% ACS—this contention is supported by the substantially higher troponin T levels in this group. Accordingly, not all cases of transaminitis were truly due to hepatocellular injury, and these terms are not truly synonymous. Furthermore, isolated AST elevations did not appear to confer the same adverse prognosis in patients with ACS, suggesting that the cause of AST elevation may influence outcomes and true liver injury is more sinister. We included only patients with the admission diagnosis of HF and available LFT data, resulting in selection bias as LFTs were obtained at the clinician's discretion rather than uniformly. The availability of data for LFTs and TTE may be associated with outcomes based on patterns of clinical testing, and patients with available LFT data had higher mortality risk than those with missing LFT data reflecting higher severity of illness. We focused on admission laboratory values, and we could not assess LFT trends or discriminate acute and chronic hepatic biomarker elevations. TTE variables in our study reflect only a single time point during the hospitalization and therefore do not capture the evolving hemodynamics of critically ill patients. We did not have invasive hemodynamic measurements to validate the accuracy of our noninvasive TTE hemodynamic assessment, which is operator dependent and can be affected by a variety of patient‐specific factors affecting imaging quality. In particular, we used TTE estimates of RAP based on the inferior vena cava size/collapsibility to assess RV congestion, and this can be affected by the frequent use of PPV in our cohort. Interreader variability could have affected TTE variables that have a subjective component, such as the semiquantitative assessment of RV function, which was often reported in the absence of specific RV measurements. Our postdischarge mortality analysis relies on electronic health records to determine patient death, which may underestimate postdischarge mortality if patients have died outside of our health system. This is particularly salient, as we observed violations of the proportional‐hazards assumptions in our Cox models—accordingly, our HR values should be interpreted with caution. Without data on cause of patient death, it is difficult to know whether CHS directly contributed to mortality or rather was an epiphenomenon of the overall severity of illness. In contrast from prior retrospective analyses on CHS, we opted to not analyze gamma‐glutamyl transferase levels due to the paucity of available data as gamma‐glutamyl transferase is not routinely obtained among CICUs. Additional hepatic synthetic biomarkers, such as albumin and ammonia, were less frequently obtained in our population and therefore were not included in our classification system; prothrombin time/international normalized ratiowas avoided due to the frequent use of warfarin. 30 Finally, hepatic imaging data were not available, limiting our ability to comment on acute or chronic structural liver pathology.

Conclusions

CHS phenotypes appear to be associated with distinct echocardiographic profiles. LVSD and reduced forward flow were characteristic of the hepatocellular group, RVSD coupled with poor forward flow and congestion was seen in the cholestasis group, and biventricular dysfunction and poor hemodynamics in the combined group. Patients with the combined CHS phenotype were at highest risk of mortality, particularly if this was combined with poor cardiac function. This large‐scale analysis of CICU patients admitted with HF emphasizes the utility of TTE and liver function testing as prognostic tools, and advocates for consistent CHS phenotyping in patients with HF. Future research is needed on how to best integrate these risk markers into prediction tools among CICU patients admitted with HF.
