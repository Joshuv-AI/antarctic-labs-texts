Ideal cardiovascular health and its associated factors among young people in Zhuhai, China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Fengyi Jiang, Song Zhou, Tao Liang, Jiao Jiang, Xiaoyue Yang, Yanxia Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveTo investigate the status of ideal cardiovascular health (CVH) among young individuals in Zhuhai, China, and analyse the factors that affect it, providing a basis for formulating prevention strategies for cardiovascular diseases in young people.DesignA cross-sectional study.SettingThe Health Management Centre of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University in Zhuhai, China.Participants944 young individuals aged between 18 and 40 years were included. Inclusion criteria: at least a primary school education, the ability to communicate effectively in the local language and willingness to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria: a history of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, cognitive impairment, previous tumour diagnoses, prior mental illnesses, severe liver, kidney and lung diseases and pregnancy.Outcome measuresIdeal CVH Scale scores and factors associated with ideal CVH among young populations.ResultsThe median score for ideal CVH among young individuals in Zhuhai was found to be 10 (9, 11), with 0.5% having all seven ideal CVH items, 1.6% exhibiting four ideal CVH behaviours and 45.1% possessing three ideal CVH physiological and biochemical factors. The proportions of seven CVH factors in an ideal state were as follows: physical activity, 13.3%; healthy diet, 30.9%; Body Mass Index, 54.6%; smoking, 81.1%; blood pressure, 56.2%; total cholesterol, 76.7% and blood sugar, 96.6%. Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that gender (β=−0.328, p=0.001, 95% CI −1.447 to −0.996), education level (β=0.068, p=0.046, 95% CI 0.004 to 0.451) and per capita monthly household income (β=0.092, p=0.007, 95% CI 0.050 to 0.320) were the main influencing factors on ideal CVH, explaining 12.3% of the variance.ConclusionsThe ideal CVH status of young individuals in Zhuhai is not optimistic. Young males with low education levels and lower per capita family income are particularly lacking in ideal CVH behaviours. Therefore, these individuals should be targeted for interventions in the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular diseases.

Introduction

According to the China Cardiovascular Health and Disease Report 2021, cardiovascular diseases in China currently affect approximately 330 million people, including 13 million cases of stroke, 11.39 million cases of coronary heart disease, 8.9 million cases of heart failure, 45.3 million cases of lower extremity arterial disease and 245 million cases of hypertension.1 The prevalence of cardiovascular diseases is steadily increasing, making them a significant public health concern. In 2010, the American Heart Association introduced the concept of ‘ideal cardiovascular health (CVH)’, which encompasses four healthy behaviours and three health factors: non-smoking, maintaining a healthy Body Mass Index (BMI), engaging in physical activity, following a healthy diet and maintaining healthy blood pressure, total cholesterol and fasting blood glucose.2 Studies have shown that each point increase in the ideal CVH score is associated with a reduction in the relative risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.3

Given the need to enhance the management of CVH throughout the lifespan, it is essential to prioritise CVH from an early age and focus on primary prevention strategies, such as preventing hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes, obesity and smoking.1 Cardiovascular diseases and related risk factors in young individuals not only impact their own health but also pose a substantial potential threat to the development of cardiovascular diseases in middle-aged and elderly populations. Previous studies have shown that the prevalence of ideal CVH among rural populations in Northeast China is extremely low (at 0.1%).4 A study of community residents in Wuxi City revealed that only 2.9% maintained all seven factors at ideal levels.5 However, research on CVH status in this age group remains limited. As an immigrant city, Zhuhai attracts residents from various regions across China and possesses a certain degree of representativeness. Currently, there is a dearth of research on the status of ideal CVH in Zhuhai. The young population aged 18–40 years is at a critical juncture for CVH, and their health behaviours, along with accumulated risk factors, will have a decisive impact on the future burden of cardiovascular disease. However, research attention in this area is insufficient. This study aims to evaluate the current CVH status among young individuals in Zhuhai by assessing their smoking status, dietary habits, BMI, physical activity, total cholesterol levels, blood pressure and fasting blood sugar levels. Furthermore, the study seeks to identify the key determinants of CVH among young individuals, thereby providing a scientific basis for optimising prevention and treatment strategies for cardiovascular diseases.

Methods

Study design and sampling

The study adopted a cross-sectional design and conveniently sampled young individuals in Zhuhai, China, as the research cohort. The sample was recruited from those who underwent physical examinations at the Health Management Center of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University between July and August 2022 (the institution, being the largest hospital in Zhuhai, offers the highest volume of physical examination services). The inclusion criteria specified individuals aged 18–40 years who had completed at least primary school education, could communicate effectively in the local language and were willing to provide verbal consent. Exclusion criteria consisted of a history of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, cognitive impairment, previous tumour diagnoses, prior mental illnesses, severe liver, kidney and lung diseases and pregnancy. All procedures followed established ethical guidelines and principles. Given the variability in individual examination items, this study excluded data from examinees who did not complete the ideal CVH items or had incomplete general information. Based on the requirements of multifactorial analysis, the sample size should be at least 10 times the number of variables.6 In this study, there are a total of 21 independent variables; considering a 30% invalid questionnaire rate, the required sample size should be at least 300.

Measurement scales and scoring

Data collection involved the acquisition of general information and ideal CVH indicators. General information encompassed age, sex, education level, nationality, birthplace, residence, marital status, occupation, per capita monthly income of the family, medical payment method, past medical history, family medical history, medical treatment history in the past 2 weeks, BMI, smoking history, alcohol consumption history, physical activity, dietary habits, total cholesterol, blood pressure and fasting blood sugar levels.

Ideal CVH indicators included four ideal health behaviours (smoking, BMI, physical activity and dietary habits) and three ideal health factors (blood pressure, total cholesterol and fasting blood sugar level). Each indicator was classified into three categories: ideal, average or poor. Individuals were assigned 2 points, 1 point or 0 points for each indicator based on the respective classifications. Consequently, a CVH score could be calculated for individuals. Attaining all seven ideal CVH indicators simultaneously defined an ideal CVH state.7 The ideal CVH score was categorised into three levels: low (0–6), medium (7–9) and high (10–14)8 (table 1).

Data collection

Well-trained investigators administered a standardised questionnaire to gather data on participants’ socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle behaviours, history of chronic diseases and medication usage. Physical examinations, including measurements of height, weight and blood pressure, were conducted. Laboratory tests involved obtaining fasting venous blood samples in the morning to assess biochemical markers such as blood cholesterol and fasting blood glucose levels. After data collection, research group members performed immediate checks for data completeness and physical examination details. Incomplete questionnaires were excluded. A total of 1212 questionnaires were collected; however, 268 physical examination reports were incomplete, primarily due to the absence of blood pressure measurements and were, therefore, excluded. The final dataset comprised 944 valid questionnaires, resulting in an effective response rate of 77.89%. The questionnaires encompass a diverse range of occupational and educational backgrounds within the local area and possess strong representativeness.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics V.26. Descriptive statistics, including frequency distributions, percentages, means and SD, were used to summarise sample characteristics. When applicable, the mean and SD were used for normally distributed data, while the median and quartiles were used for non-normally distributed data. Univariate analyses employed independent samples t-tests or analysis of variance if the data met normal distribution assumptions; otherwise, non-parametric tests (Mann-Whitney and Kruskal-Wallis tests) were conducted for categorical variables. Multivariate analysis employed the significant variables identified in univariate analyses as independent variables in a stepwise linear regression analysis.

Results

Ideal CVH status of young individuals

Of the 944 young participants, the average age was 29.44±5.12 years, comprising 444 females (47.0%) and 500 males (53.0%). The ideal CVH score ranged from 3 to 14 (out of 14), with a median score of 10 (9, 11). Among the participants, 48 (5.1%) had low scores, 292 (30.9%) had moderate scores and 604 (64%) had high scores. Five individuals (0.5%) achieved all seven ideal CVH indicators, 15 (1.6%) met four ideal behaviours and 426 (45.1%) satisfied three ideal health indicators (table 2).

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that the young participants had a median ideal CVH score of 109 10, which is generally considered an ideal level. However, 36% of individuals achieved moderate or lower scores, with 5.1% obtaining low scores. Therefore, the overall ideal CVH behaviour among young people needs improvement. Furthermore, only 0.5% of participants met all seven ideal CVH indicators. The research results are lower than those of Shen et al11 (2.9%) on 7804 community residents in Wuxi City and Wang et al10 (1.5%) on 10 520 urban residents in the Xinjiang region. The discrepancy may be attributed to the fact that the research focused specifically on young individuals who may not have fully recognised the importance of adopting a healthy lifestyle for preventing and treating cardiovascular diseases. It is important to note that the incidence of cardiovascular diseases is increasing among younger populations. This study’s innovation lies in its examination of young individuals as a distinct group to identify vulnerabilities in the prevention and treatment of cardiovascular diseases, thereby providing a foundation for subsequent intervention research aimed at reducing the occurrence of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases.

Only 1.6% of individuals simultaneously exhibited four ideal CVH behaviours, which is lower than the results reported by Cao et al (2.9% among 1075 college students in Baoding) and Wang et al (3.6% among the urban population of Xinjiang comprising 10 520 individuals).10 12 Conversely, 45.1% of participants satisfied three ideal CVH indicators, which is higher than Wang et al’s findings among the urban population of Xinjiang (25.9%).10 These results suggest that while physiological and biochemical factors, such as blood pressure, fasting blood glucose and total cholesterol, are at an ideal level for a significant proportion of the young population, behavioural indicators, such as smoking, healthy diet, BMI and physical activity, are not satisfactory. To improve the ideal CVH of young individuals, it is imperative to promote the adoption of a healthy lifestyle, focusing particularly on zero-level preventive measures aimed at reducing the risk of cardiovascular diseases. This study’s additional innovation lies in separately analysing the ideal CVH behaviour index and physiological and biochemical factor index, which reveal weaknesses in CVH behaviours among young individuals, particularly their limited awareness of zero-level prevention of cardiovascular diseases. These findings provide essential guidance and a basis for subsequent intervention strategies.

The study identified the lowest rate of achievement for ideal physical activity, at only 13.3%, which was lower than that reported by Shen et al, involving 76.6% of 7804 community residents in Wuxi.11 This disparity might be attributed to young individuals’ heavy work and study demands, resulting in limited awareness and engagement in physical activities. Another area for improvement is a healthy diet, with only 30.9% of participants meeting this ideal indicator, indicating a higher rate compared with Shen et al’s findings among 21.3% of 7804 community residents in Wuxi.11 Therefore, increased awareness campaigns are necessary at the societal level. Regular health education lectures, brochures, WeChat videos and other methods should be provided by hospitals and communities to raise public, especially young individuals’, awareness of physical activity and healthy eating practices in order to enhance the overall health literacy of the population.

The results of this study highlight gender, educational level and per capita monthly income as the main factors influencing ideal CVH among young individuals. Specifically, men exhibited lower CVH scores compared with women, consistent with Pompeo et al’s research.13 This difference may be attributed to young men often perceiving themselves as being young and, therefore, lacking awareness regarding the impact of lifestyle on their health, coupled with limited access to relevant health education and guidance. Additionally, individuals with a lower educational level demonstrated significantly lower CVH scores compared with those with higher education levels, consistent with findings reported by Wang et al and Fang et al.14 15 This suggests that individuals with higher education levels are more likely to engage in ideal CVH behaviours and enjoy better health outcomes. This disparity can be attributed to lower educational attainment leading to a lack of health knowledge, insufficient active engagement in learning, an inability to verify knowledge accuracy and a lack of self-regulation. Furthermore, individuals with lower per capita monthly incomes had significantly lower CVH scores, aligning with the findings of Bucholz et al and others.16 The underlying cause may stem from lower-income individuals prioritising livelihood concerns, resulting in a relative neglect of personal health needs. To promote ideal CVH among young individuals, particular attention should be paid to men, those with lower educational levels and individuals from low-income families.

To achieve the ideal CVH goals for residents, it is crucial to enhance their awareness of proactive health management. Relevant studies have shown that ‘active health’ can change residents’ health concepts and promote the formation of healthy behaviours by enhancing their awareness of active health and health literacy, improving the utilisation of health resources.1719 Therefore, the following strategies need to be adopted for improvement: on the one hand, use new media and social platforms, as well as campus and workplace health lectures, to conduct CVH education tailored to the characteristics, language and interests of young people. On the other hand, cardiovascular risk assessment should be integrated into routine physical examinations, such as those for college students during enrolment, new employees during onboarding and the creation of health records for community youth. The assessment should extend beyond BMI and blood pressure to include evaluation of lipids, blood sugar and lifestyle. Additionally, employ digital health tools to offer online health consultations, risk assessments and personalised feedback. Encourage collaboration between primary-level medical and health institutions/community health service centres and schools, enterprises and social organisations to bring ICVH screening, basic intervention and health education to the ‘last mile’ of young people’s lives and work.

This study has several strengths. It used ideal CVH indicators among young individuals to effectively assess the status of ideal CVH in this demographic and analyse the factors that influence it. Furthermore, the large sample size, which includes 944 participants, provides a robust foundation for insights that are generalisable specifically to young individuals. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. first, as a cross-sectional study, it cannot establish a causal relationship between ideal CVH and the development of actual cardiovascular disease. Second, the research was confined to young individuals in a specific area of Zhuhai City, China, which limits the generalisability of the results. Additionally, all data in this study were obtained from self-reports at a single source and a single time point, which may pose a risk of common method bias. Future research should consider including larger and more diverse samples from multiple locations, conducting longitudinal studies and adopting objective measurements or reports from others to clarify the directionality and underlying mechanisms of the observed associations.

Conclusions

The study’s findings indicate that the ideal CVH status of young individuals in Zhuhai is not optimistic, with only a small percentage meeting all seven of the ideal CVH indicators. This disparity is largely due to the suboptimal adherence to four behavioural indicators, especially concerning physical activity and diet. Gender, educational attainment and per capita monthly household income significantly influence CVH scores, with men scoring lower than women, and higher education levels and incomes correlating with better CVH behaviours. This study concentrates on young individuals aged 18–40 years, who are in a crucial window period for CVH. Their health behaviours and accumulated risk factors significantly influence the future burden of cardiovascular disease. The findings of this study offer a solid foundation for the development of precise and youth-oriented CVH promotion strategies.
