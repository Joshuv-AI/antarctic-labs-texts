An unbiased whole-genome open reading frame overexpression screen identifies B3GALT2, a novel inducer of cellular ASO activity
Authors: Julia Hesselmann, Carolina Hager, Liza Malong, Jean-Philippe Fortin, Roland Schmucki, Ted Lau, Scott Martin, Mike Costa, Zora Modrusan, Benjamin Haley, Filip Roudnicky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) are a promising class of therapeutics made of chemically modified synthetic single-stranded nucleic acid molecules, yet their clinical translation is often hindered by challenges in cellular uptake and delivery. Identifying cellular factors that modulate ASO activity is crucial for overcoming these limitations. Utilizing a whole-genome open reading frame (ORF) overexpression screen with a genetic splice reporter system to identify genes that can increase or decrease ASO activity, we identified B3GALT2 as the most significant hit for increasing ASO activity. Subsequent validation in HEK293 and U2OS cell lines demonstrated that B3GALT2 overexpression consistently and significantly enhances the activity of both splice-switching and mRNA-degrading ASOs across different targets (CD81, MALAT1, and CERS2). Transcriptomic analysis of B3GALT2-overexpressing cells showed upregulation of endocytic scavenger receptors CUBN and SCARA5, and gene set enrichment analysis indicated an enrichment of proteins involved in clathrin-mediated endocytosis. Mechanistic investigations revealed that B3GALT2 overexpression leads to a significant increase in ASO uptake. These findings highlight B3GALT2 as a modulator of ASO cellular entry and offer an avenue for potentially improving ASO-based therapeutic strategies.Keywords: MT: Oligonucleotides: Therapies and Applications, anti-sense oligonucleotides, ASOs, functional genomics, B3GALT2, ASO uptake

Introduction

Antisense oligonucleotides (ASOs) can modify the expression and/or splicing patterns of target RNAs. ASOs are short, synthetic single-stranded nucleic acid molecules,1 often incorporating chemical modifications such as phosphorothioate internucleosides (PSs), 2′-O-methoxyethyl, or locked nucleic acid sugars to enhance their stability, activity, and/or bioavailability.2

PS-ASOs can enter cells without assisted uptake in a process termed gymnosis.3 PS linkages confer high-affinity interactions with cell surface proteins and receptors4 (e.g., stabilin-1 and -25 and ASGR16), enabling endocytic internalization. In contrast, unmodified phosphodiester ASOs show minimal binding and uptake in most cell systems; therefore, all experiments in this study employed PS-ASOs due to their prior establishment for gymnotic delivery. As ASOs generally lack specific sorting signals, they largely remain within the endosomal system as it matures from early to late endosomes, a journey during which most ASO molecules are ultimately trafficked to lysosomes for degradation.

Unbiased screens have been performed to reveal genes involved in nucleic acid therapeutic (small interfering RNA,7,8 mRNA,9 and ASOs10,11,12) entry, trafficking, and activity. Among these, gain-of-function screens have demonstrated success13 in identifying cellular factors involved in various biological processes. Here, we deployed a genomically integrated splice-switch reporter system (EGFPsr10,14) as a functional, real-time readout of ASO activity via corrective splicing of an EGFP transcript. This approach enabled identification of an extensive set of genes that influence ASO activity, in particular, genes involved in glycosylation and/or have known localization within the Golgi compartment. Among these, beta-1,3-galactosyltransferase 2 (B3GALT2) emerged as the most significant positive modifier of ASO activity. In this study, we sought to validate and mechanistically characterize the role of B3GALT2 in modulating ASO activity. Conditional overexpression of B3GALT2 in HEK293 and U2OS cell lines confirmed its robust, enhancing effect on splice switching and RNase H-dependent knockdown of ASOs that target various mRNAs (i.e., CD81, MALAT1, and CERS2). Furthermore, transcriptomic analysis of B3GALT2-overexpressing cells indicated an upregulation of endocytic receptors, notably CUBN and SCARA5, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) highlighted an enrichment of proteins involved in clathrin-dependent endocytic transport, pointing to enhanced receptor-mediated uptake as a plausible mechanism. Our subsequent investigations revealed that B3GALT2 overexpression led to significantly increased cellular uptake of ASOs. This work provides insights into B3GALT2’s impact on ASO cellular entry and activity and may inform strategies for enhancing ASO-based therapies.

Results

ORF screening identifies genes that modify the activity of ASOs, with B3GALT2 having the strongest effect

We engineered HEK293 cells with a genomically integrated EGFP splice-switching reporter system (HEK293-EGFPsr) based on a previously published design10,14 (Figure 1A) to quantitatively measure the activity of splice-switching ASOs (SS-ASOs). This system exhibits minimal background signal and allows for direct assessment of correct EGFP splicing upon SS-ASO treatment (Figure 1A). Nuclear activity of SS-ASOs leads to the exclusion of a β-globin cryptic exon within intron-2 sequence of the EGFP sequence, leading to correct splicing and resulting in a functional EGFP protein. To identify genes that modify cellular activity of ASOs, we first transduced HEK293-EGFPsr cells with a whole-genome lentivirus open reading frame (ORF) library (Table S1) and followed this by treatment with 25 nM SS-ASO for 3 days (Figure 1B). Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) for the approximately top 15% of GFP-positive (GFP+) and GFP-negative (GFP−) populations (Figures S1A and Figure 1B, Round 1) was performed in two consecutive rounds allowing for 1 week of recovery after ASO treatment (Figure 1B, Round 2) to further enrich for the positive and negative GFP cells (Figure 1B). With this approach, we successfully identified ORFs (Table S2) that, upon overexpression, increased (SORT1 GFP+, Figure 1C, left; SORT2 GFP+, Figure 1C, right) or decreased SS-ASO activity (SORT1 GFP−, Figure 1D, left; SORT2 GFP−, Figure 1D, right).

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of the genes with fold change >1.5 and p adjusted value <0.01 that increased the cellular activity of SS-ASO showed significant enrichment for molecular function of enzymes involved in glycosylation (SORT1 GFP+, Figure 1E, left; Table S3) and Golgi and membrane cellular components (SORT1 GFP+, Figure 1E, right; Table S3). Analysis of the second sort (SORT2 GFP+) using the same parameters showed enrichment for similar molecular functions and cellular components (Figure S1B; Table S4). Using the same cutoff for the genes that decreased the cellular activity of SS-ASO upon overexpression, we found activity connected to neuropeptide signaling and extracellular processes (SORT1 GFP−, Figure S1C; Table S5). Analysis of the second sort of the GFP− cells (SORT2 GFP−) found enrichment in several enzyme families and enrichment in cellular components such as extracellular space, cytoplasm, and endoplasmic reticulum (SORT2 GFP−, Figure S1D; Table S6). Overall, these results were consistent with mechanisms related to intracellular trafficking, post-translational modification, or organelle sorting as having an influence on ASO/reporter function.

We performed technical validation of the primary hits from our screen via lentiviral transduction (Table S7) of HEK293-EGFPsr cells with individual ORF expression constructs in an arrayed screening format (Figure 1F). Because our primary interest was in genes that increase ASO activity when overexpressed, and our sorting methods repeatedly pinpointed genes with a significant positive effect, we prioritized the validation of ORFs that demonstrated this enhanced ASO activity. Consistent with the effects in the primary pooled screen, we were able to validate enzymes involved in glycosylation such as glycosyltransferases B3GALT2, GCNT2, FUT1, MGAT2, GALNT16, GCNT1, UGT1A4, B4GALNT2, and UGT1A1; carbohydrate sulfotransferase CHST6; transport SLC22A18; and metabolism of glycosaminoglycans UST.

B3GALT2 overexpression reproducibly increases activity of ASOs across cell lines and targets

Having the most consistent, positive effect on ASO activity as shown in our pooled screens (first and second sort) and arrayed validation, we decided to further investigate B3GALT2, MGAT2, and PLOD3 through further validation with an expanded ASO target set and cell line (U2OS, Figure 2). To ascertain the specificity and dose dependency on activity of these genes in relation to ASO activity, we engineered HEK293 and U2OS cells with doxycycline (Dox)-regulated transgenes that uniquely expressed B3GALT2, MGAT2, or PLOD3. ORFs of the selected genes were tagged with hemagglutinin (HA) tag and a downstream IRES driving mCherry to confirm transgene expression. We could validate the inducibility of B3GALT2 upon Dox addition in U2OS and HEK293 by RT-qPCR (Figure S2A) and with an antibody against the HA tag via western blot (Figures S2B, S5, and S6) and immunofluorescence (Figure S2C) or with an antibody against B3GALT2 in immunofluorescence (Figure S2D). We could observe that B3GALT2 was not expressed in wild-type (WT) HEK293 or U2OS cells. This was confirmed by further studies using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (Tables S8 and S9) in HEK293 WT cells. According to the public expression data (Protein Atlas15) we have chosen a cell line NCI-H72716 that shows expression of B3GALT2 (Figure S2E). B3GALT2 overexpression significantly induced the activity of ASOs against CD81 (Figure 2A), MALAT1 (Figure 2B), and CERS2 at 100 nM (Figure 2C) and at 1,000 nM (Figure 2D). B3GALT2 knockdown using small hairpin RNA in NCI-H727 resulted in an ∼50% reduction of B3GALT2 mRNA expression (Figures S3A, left, and S3B, left); this did not result in any significant effect on the activity of the ASOs against MALAT1 (Figure S3A, middle and right) or CERS2 (Figure S3B, right). MGAT2 overexpression showed a dose-dependent increase of ASO activity with increase of expression only for MALAT1 (Figure 2B), and it downregulated CERS2 without ASO treatment (Figure 2C); therefore, MGAT2 was deprioritized for further experiments. PLOD3 overexpression decreased the activity of ASOs targeting CD81 (Figure 2A) and CERS2 when treated with 1,000 nM ASO in a Dox dose-dependent manner (Figure 2D), but it had no effect on 100 nM dose of ASOs targeting CERS2 (Figure 2C) and MALAT1 (Figure 2B) and was, therefore, deprioritized from further studies.

B3GALT2 overexpression induces increased uptake of ASO

Using the process of gymnosis, non-formulated ASOs freely enter cells via endocytosis (Figure 1A). Given that multiple surface receptors are implicated in the gymnotic uptake of ASOs,2 we employed whole-transcriptome RNA-seq to explore if B3GALT2 overexpression altered the expression profiles of known gymnosis and endocytosis-related receptors. For this, we compared transcriptomes of HEK293 cells overexpressing B3GALT2 to empty vector controls (Figure 3A; Table S8). Similarly, HEK293 cells with control (empty) vector overexpression were compared against parental (unmodified) HEK293 cells (Table S9).

Differential expression analysis of the whole transcriptome RNA-seq identified nine genes with significant upregulation (|logFC| > 1, false discovery rate [FDR] <0.05; Figure 3A) exclusively in the B3GALT2 overexpression condition, including synaptotagmin like 5 (SYTL5), scavenger receptor class A member 5 (SCARA5), and cubilin (CUBN). Many of the significantly downregulated genes such as EDA2R, AR, DTX4, CHRDL1, NPR3, ZNF875, and ADCYAP1R1 were also found to be downregulated in the comparison of WT HEK293 to control (empty) vector, and these were excluded from follow-up experiments (Figure S4A).

We specifically focused on the two aforementioned receptors observed to be upregulated as a consequence of B3GALT2 overexpression. SCARA5 is a scavenger receptor that is able to bind extracellular ferritin and mediates endocytosis of ferritin-captured iron.2,17,18 Cubilin is a receptor for vitamin B12 but is also responsible for endocytic uptake of different lipoproteins, vitamin-binding proteins, carrier proteins, hormones, enzymes, and drugs in epithelial cells.19 Given its established role as a receptor involved in the uptake of macromolecules, it is plausible that cubilin could also play a role in the internalization of ASOs.

We have used a Dox-inducible system with 1,000 ng/mL of Dox to induce CUBN and SCARA5 individually and compared them to B3GALT2 expression in terms of efficacy of three different ASOs: an SS-ASO targeting the EGFP splice reporter construct, a knockdown ASO targeting CD81 (Figure S4B), and a knockdown ASO targeting CERS2 mRNA (Figure S4C) in HEK293 and U2OS cell lines. In all tested ASOs and cell lines, overexpression of B3GALT2 had the strongest effect on increasing ASO activity in comparison to SCARA5 and CUBN. Expression of SCARA5 induced ASO activity in all the cases, except for that targeting the EGFP splice reporter (Figure S4B, left). CUBN overexpression significantly increased the activity of ASOs targeting both the EGFP splice reporter and CD81 (Figure S4B) but did not affect the potency of ASO-mediated knockdown of CERS2 (Figure S4C).

Next, we performed GSEA to identify coordinated changes in gene expression by comparing B3GALT2-overexpressing cells to the empty vector control line. Using FDR values to prioritize gene sets, we found two with significant enrichment that related to cellular components: clathrin-coated pit and anchored component of the membrane. This observation motivated a further investigation into ASO uptake. Here, we designed an assay for direct measurement of ASO uptake by way of imaging our target cells in the presence of a fluorescently labeled ASO. The ASO can be detected with conventional fluorescent microscopy, in which ASO vesicles show up as “spots” in the cell. Additionally, the “spot intensity” of the vesicles can be measured to estimate the amount of ASOs uptaken. To infer the kinetics of ASO uptake, we performed a time-course experiment over 0–24 h ASO incubation in Dox-inducible U2OS cells, comparing inducible B3GALT2 and empty vector control. Prior to the ASO treatment, these cells were induced with Dox and FACS sorted based on the mCherry reporter intensity to isolate populations representing the 15% of cells with the highest and the 15% with the lowest mCherry expression, which were then used for subsequent uptake analysis.

Analysis of spots per cell (Figure 3C, left) revealed that ASO uptake in high-mCherry-expressing B3GALT2 cells was significantly increased at several time points (2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 24 h) compared to high-mCherry-expressing control (empty) vector cells. The number of spots per cell in high-mCherry B3GALT2 cells appeared to plateau around 40–45 spots from 6 h onward (representative images Figure 3D).

A similar trend was observed for spot intensity per cell (Figure 3C, right). High-mCherry-B3GALT2-expressing cells exhibited significantly increased spot intensity at 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, and 24 h compared to high-mCherry empty vector cells. These data demonstrate that B3GALT2 enhances both the rate and extent of ASO internalization into cells.

Discussion

The effective delivery of ASOs into target cells and their subsequent trafficking to the appropriate subcellular compartment remain critical determinants of therapeutic success. The processes by which ASOs enter cells via gymnosis and escape the endosomal compartment to reach their targets in the cytoplasm or nucleus remain largely unknown, although some protein modulators have been identified,20,21,22,23,24,25,26 such as SIDT2,27,28 Rab5C,29,30 EEA1,29 Rab7A,29 STX5,23 PRKCA,22 TSG101,26 VPS28,26 GCC2,31 ANXA2,32 AP2M1, AP1M1,10 TBC1D23,10 WDR91,12 and GOLGA8.2,25,32 Our study utilized an unbiased whole-genome ORF overexpression screen employing a sensitive EGFP splice-switching reporter system (EGFPsr10,14) (Figures 1A and 1B). This approach led to the identification of several genes capable of increasing ASO activity, with a notable Gene Ontology enrichment for enzymes involved in glycosylation and proteins localized to the Golgi apparatus and membranes (Figures 1E and Figure S1B). Among the identified hits, beta-1,3-galactosyltransferase 2 (B3GALT2), an enzyme known for its role in protein glycosylation,33 emerged as the most significant and consistent gene capable of strongly increasing ASO potency across all rounds of pooled ORF (Figure 1C, SORT1 GFP+ and SORT2 GFP+) and arrayed ORF screening (Figure 1F). The robust effect of B3GALT2 on ASO activity was further validated using conditional overexpression, in which a correlation between B3GALT2 expression levels and ASO activity was observed. This effect was consistent across different ASO modes of action (splice switching and RNase H-dependent degradation), various gene targets (CD81, MALAT1, and CERS2), and in two distinct human cell lines, HEK293 and U2OS (Figures 2A–2D, S2A–S2D, S3B, and S3C). Notably, B3GALT2 is not endogenously expressed in WT HEK293 or U2OS cells, as confirmed by RT-qPCR and RNA-seq (Figure S2A; Tables S8 and S9). RNAi experiments in NCI-H727, aimed at reducing the native levels of B3GALT2, did not result in an observable difference in ASO activity (Figures S3A–S3B). This suggests that a higher knockdown is needed to have an opposite effect of the overexpression, that B3GALT2 function is compensated by another gene/gene network in this context, or that the effect of B3GALT2 can be only observed upon overexpression. Mechanistically, B3GALT2 overexpression leads to a marked increase in the cellular uptake of ASOs. This was directly demonstrated through time-course experiments using fluorescently labeled ASOs in U2OS cells with conditionally activated B3GALT2 expression. Both the number of ASO-containing spots per cell and the spot intensity were significantly elevated in cells with high B3GALT2 expression (Figures 3C and 3D). Further supporting a mechanism involving increased ASO uptake, our whole-transcriptome RNA-seq analysis of HEK293 cells overexpressing B3GALT2 (compared to an empty vector control) revealed significant upregulation of several genes implicated in endocytosis and vesicular trafficking (Figure 3A; Table S8). Plausible receptors mediating ASO uptake include the endocytic receptor cubilin (CUBN) and scavenger receptor class A member 5 (SCARA5), along with SYTL5, which were also among the highly upregulated genes (Figure 3A). SCARA5 is a scavenger receptor34 that has been implicated in the uptake of negatively charged nanocomplexes with oligonucleotides.17 Previous studies have indicated that scavenger receptors4 of class H (e.g., stabilin-1 and -25) and class A subtypes (e.g., SRA,35 SCARA1,36 SCARA3, and SCARA517) can mediate the uptake of naked ASOs or ASOs protected by nanoparticles. SCARA5 is an N-glycosylated protein,37 where an increased expression and subsequently increased number of glycosylated receptors, due to B3GALT2 overexpression, could be one mechanism of action to increase ASO uptake.37 Cubilin functions by binding a variety of ligands and internalizing them.19 Cubilin shows N-linked glycosylation of at least four amino acids, which is required for membrane trafficking of cubilin and appearance on the cell surface.38 It has been shown that mutations of the N-glycosylated amino acids reduce membrane trafficking.38 Receptor candidates for ASOs such as CUBN and SCARA5 are likely to be cell type biased, particularly given their enrichment in kidney epithelia and our use of HEK293 cells in the primary screen. While overexpression of CUBN or SCARA5 alone did increase ASO activity, the effect was generally less pronounced than that observed with B3GALT2 overexpression (Figures S3B and S3C), suggesting that B3GALT2 may act through multiple effectors or a more complex mechanism than simply upregulating individual receptors. Moreover, GSEA showed enrichment of clathrin‑coated pit components and other membrane‑anchored factors under B3GALT2 overexpression, consistent with clathrin‑mediated ASO uptake. SYTL5 was also upregulated; this synaptotagmin‑like protein harbors tandem C2 domains and an N‑terminal Slp‑homology domain that binds Rab27A and promotes regulated exocytosis,39 raising the possibility that B3GALT2 modulates both endocytic entry and Rab27‑linked exocytic/recycling steps that influence ASO processing. Mechanistic dissection beyond the transcriptomic signatures—such as defining the direct cell surface glycoprotein substrates of B3GALT2, establishing which receptors physically engage ASOs under B3GALT2 activation, and quantifying endosomal escape—remains to be completed.

We recognize several limitations of our study. First, the discovery paradigm is based on ORF overexpression. Accordingly, we refrain from extrapolating beyond the demonstrated in-cell overexpression context. Second, while our imaging and expression data favor an uptake-centric mechanism, we have not directly quantified endosomal escape or mapped specific glycan modifications on candidate receptors. Third, although we observe consistent effects across distinct ASO sequences in two cell backgrounds (HEK293 and U2OS), broader generalizability across additional chemistries, primary cells and tissues remains to be determined. Finally, the possibility that B3GALT2 influences other facets of cellular physiology warrants attention in translational applications.

In conclusion, this work identifies B3GALT2 as a novel and potent positive modulator of ASO activity, primarily by enhancing ASO cellular uptake. These findings contribute to a better understanding of the complex cellular mechanisms governing ASO efficacy and suggest that targeting pathways influenced by B3GALT2 could be a viable strategy for improving the therapeutic window and efficacy of ASO-based medicines.

Material and methods

Cell lines and culturing

The human GripTite HEK293 line was obtained from Thermo Fisher, later referenced as HEK293. HEK293 cells were cultured in DMEM Glutamax (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% PenStrep (Gibco), 600 μg/mL hygromycin B (Invitrogen), and 5 μg/mL blasticidin (Invitrogen). U2OS cells were obtained from the Roche Biorepository (HTB-96, Lot No. 58078676, ATCC). U2OS cells were cultured in McCoys 5A (Gibco) and supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% PenStrep (Gibco). Cells were split twice a week using TrypLE Express (Gibco), with a passage ratio of 1:10. NCI-H727 were obtained from Roche Biorepository (CRL-5815, Lot. No. BA141119) and cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco), 10% FBS (Gibco), 1% PenStrep (Gibco), and 2 mM L-Glutamine (Gibco). All the cell lines were cultivated in an incubator at 37°C, 5% CO2, and 95% humidity and were split twice a week using TrypLE Express (Gibco) with a passage ratio of 1:10.

HEK293 splice switch HBB EGFP reporter construction

Described in detail in Byrnes et al.14 and Malong et al.10

Pooled ORF vector library

The lentiviral library has been produced as in Malong et al.10 and obtained from Sigma (MISSION TRC3 Human Whole Genome Lentiviral ORF Pool). Lentiviral production is described in supplemental information.

ORF lentiviral screen

Forty-four million HEK293-EGFPsr cells per biological replicate were seeded and were transduced the next day with a whole-genome ORF human lentiviral library (library sequences are available in Table S1) at an MOI of 0.3. Three individual transductions were performed and handled separately in all consecutive steps. On day 3, a reference sample was collected, centrifuged (610 × g, 10 min), supernatant removed, and cell pellets snap frozen. On day 4, ORF-expressing cells were selected by adding Zeocin (500 μg/mL) throughout the study. On days 11 (replicate 1), 12 (replicate 2), and 13 (replicate 3) cells were treated with 25 nM SS-ASO for 72 h. After 72 h, cells were dissociated from plates using Accutase (StemCell Technologies, Catalog No. AT104) and filtered through 30-μm filters to create a single-cell suspension (Miltenyi Biotec, Catalog No. 130-098-458). Next, 38 million cells were centrifuged (610 × g, 10 min), supernatant removed, and cell pellets snap frozen; the rest of the cells were prepared in DMEM (phenol free, Thermo Fisher, Catalog No. 21063045) media for sorting. Sorting was performed in parallel on several BD FACSAria III equipped with a 70-μm-size nozzle (BD Biosciences) using a 4-way purity precision mode. Cells were sorted into two bins based on GFP expression and lack of cell death staining by SYTOX Red Dead Cell Stain (Thermo Fisher, Catalog No. S34859): low (bottom ∼15%) and high (top ∼15%) with approximately 6 million cells per bin (from each high and low gate). Sorting was performed in cooled collection tubes with complete DMEM with 20% FBS and 25 mM HEPES. Ninety percent of the sorted cells were centrifuged (610 × g, 10 min), supernatant removed, and cell pellets snap frozen (Round 1). Ten percent of the sorted cells were re-seeded and allowed to proliferate and recover from SS-ASO treatment to days 23 (replicate 1), 25 (replicate 2), and 26 (replicate 3) and then re-treated with 25 nM SS-ASO for 72 h. Exactly the same FACS procedure as described above was repeated—cells were sorted into two bins based on GFP expression and a lack of cell death staining SYTOX Red Dead Cell Stain (Thermo Fisher, Catalog No. S34859): low (bottom ∼15%) and high (top ∼15%). Approximately 6 million cells per bin were sorted, cells were centrifuged (610 × g, 10 min), supernatant removed, and cell pellets snap frozen (Round 2). FACS plots were generated using the Flowjo_V10 software (Flowjo).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with Prism 7 (GraphPad) or Microsoft Excel (Microsoft) or as stated in the Methods. For statistical analysis, an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t test or one-way or two-way ANOVA was used. Statistical significance was corrected for multiple comparisons using Holm-Sidak method with α = 0.05 or using Dunnett post hoc test. Data in graphs are shown as mean ± SD. p values <0.05 are considered significant (∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001).
