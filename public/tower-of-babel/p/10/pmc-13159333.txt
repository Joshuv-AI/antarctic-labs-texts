Red blood cell transfusion in neurocritical patients: an updated systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yuxuan Fu, Tianyuan Wang, Ruiqi Lu, Weihua Cui, Yunzhen Wang, Qing Zhang, Ruquan Han, Yun Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnemia is a prevalent complication in neurocritical patients and contributes to secondary brain damage by diminishing the oxygen supply to the brain. Although red blood cell transfusions may enhance cerebral oxygen delivery, whether this intervention improves patient outcomes remains unclear.MethodsWe searched the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Embase, and MEDLINE (PubMed) from inception to January 26, 2025. All randomized controlled trials (RCTs) assessing liberal versus restrictive RBC transfusion strategies in neurocritical patients were included in this analysis. The primary outcome was mortality at discharge.ResultsA total of 5579 bibliographic citations were identified. After reviewing the title, abstract, and full text, 8 studies were deemed eligible for inclusion. The combined results showed no significant difference in mortality at discharge (20.5% for restrictive strategy vs. 20.8% for liberal strategy, RR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.76 to 1.26, I2 = 21%). There was no significant difference between the restrictive and liberal transfusion groups in terms of ICU mortality (RR: 1.36, 95% CI: 0.52 to 3.52), six-month mortality (RR: 1.05, 95% CI: 0.90 to 1.22) or long-term mortality (RR: 1.04, 95% CI: 0.91 to 1.18). However, the combined results showed that the incidence of unfavorable Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) outcomes at 6 months (RR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.20) and long-term unfavorable outcomes (RR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.19) was significantly lower in the liberal transfusion group compared to the restrictive transfusion group. The long-term unfavorable outcomes were significantly lower in the liberal transfusion group compared to the restrictive group in traumatic brain injury subgroup (RR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.19). Additionally, the incidence of sepsis or septic shock was lower in the liberal transfusion group than in the restrictive transfusion group (RR: 1.37, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.80).ConclusionsCombining our findings, this systemic review suggests that while both transfusion strategies had similar impacts on mortality outcomes, the liberal strategy may offer a better prognosis in terms of long-term recovery and a reduced risk of sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03796-x.Keywords: Red blood cell transfusion, Neurocritical, Meta-analysis, Systematic review

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI), subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) are common neurocritical diseases. In the neurocritical patient population, the incidence of anemia is notably increased, with rates as high as 69% in traumatic brain injury (TBI) patients and 47% in subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) patients [1–6]. A large-scale cohort study revealed that nearly one-quarter (24%) of ICH patients presented with anemia [7]. The human brain, being a highly oxygen-dependent organ, is particularly vulnerable to hypoxia [8]. Anemia exacerbates secondary brain damage by reducing oxygen supply to the brain [3]. As a result, even mild anemia can lead to cerebral hypoxia [9, 10]. Although red blood cell (RBC) transfusions may improve cerebral oxygen delivery, it remains unclear whether this treatment leads to better patient outcomes [4]. Additionally, blood transfusions carry risks, including an increased likelihood of infection and transfusion-related acute lung injury [11].

A previous meta-analysis conducted by our team found no significant difference between restrictive and liberal RBC transfusion strategies in terms of mortality, unfavorable outcomes, neurological adverse events, and most major non-neurological complications [12]. However, due to the potential bias, heterogeneity, and the small sample size of earlier studies, the overall quality of evidence was considered low. Recently, the completion and publication of three large-scale randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have provided us with a substantial amount of additional data [3, 4, 13]. In light of these new findings, we have conducted an updated systematic review and meta-analysis to further investigate the effects of different RBC transfusion strategies in neurocritical patients.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis adheres to the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items of Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA). The review protocol has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD42021225043.

Search methods for the identification of studies

We conducted a comprehensive search of the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), Embase, and MEDLINE (PubMed) on January 26, 2025, using optimized search terms for sensitivity and precision. The search, performed by YY and YF, adhered to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (version 6.3) [14]. The full search strategy is provided in the supplementary material (Supplemental Digital Content: Search Strategies). To ensure completeness, we screened all eligible articles and reviews for additional relevant studies, applying no geographical restrictions.

Selection criteria

We included all RCTs that evaluated liberal versus restrictive RBC transfusion strategies in neurocritical patients. We considered all relevant documents published in English, regardless of their publishing status. Non-randomized studies were excluded. Only adult participants aged 18 years or older, diagnosed with TBI, ICH, and SAH, were included in the analysis, irrespective of whether they underwent surgery.

The intervention group received a liberal RBC transfusion strategy, while the control group followed a restrictive strategy, with well-defined transfusion thresholds in each study. The primary outcome was discharge mortality. Secondary outcomes included ICU mortality, six-month mortality, long-term mortality, poor Glasgow Outcome Score (GOS) at six months, neurological events (vasospasm, stroke, and intracranial pressure treatment), severe non-neurological complications, other adverse events, and ICU and hospital length of stay. The Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS) was selected as the assessment tool in our study due to its well-established simplicity, validity, and reliability in evaluating patients with neurological disorders. As the most widely adopted functional outcome measure in numerous intervention trials, GOS provides standardized assessment suitable for comparative clinical research [15]. Long-term mortality was defined as the sum of 60-day mortality and 12-month mortality. Vasospasm was defined as a middle cerebral artery flow velocity > 120 cm/s and a Lindegaard index > 3 [16]. ICP treatment included hypertonic agents, vasopressors, hyperventilation, sedation, analgesia, neuromuscular blockers, cerebrospinal fluid drainage, diuretics, hypothermia, barbiturates, or craniotomy [16]. Major non-neurological complications included deep vein thrombosis (DVT), myocardial infarction, hypotension, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and urinary tract infection (UTI).

Data collection and analysis

YY and YF independently conducted the search. YY and TW independently screened the results to identify studies meeting the inclusion criteria, documenting reasons for inclusion/exclusion. YF and RL independently extracted data using electronic tables, with discrepancies resolved through discussion between YY and TW. A third reviewer (RH) was consulted for further clarification. WC and YW independently performed the data analysis, and YY contacted study authors via email if additional information was needed.

YY and QZ independently assessed the risk of bias in the included studies using the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (version 6.3) [14]. They evaluated potential biases such as selection bias, performance bias, and attrition bias, covering areas like randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, missing data, and selective reporting. Studies were classified as low risk if all areas were adequate, or high risk if any area was inadequate or unclear. A risk-of-bias graph was created to illustrate the assessment.

Data were analyzed using Review Manager (RevMan 5.4). Mean differences (MD) were used for continuous data, while dichotomous data were assessed via a random-effects model to estimate risk ratios (RR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Given the expected heterogeneity, a random-effects model was applied. Meta-analyses synthesized participant, intervention, and outcome data, while studies with insufficient numerical data underwent narrative analysis.

Heterogeneity was assessed based on clinical (e.g., disease type, RBC transfusion thresholds) and methodological differences. Subgroup or sensitivity analyses were performed to address clinical heterogeneity, using forest plots and the I² statistic [14]. An I² > 50% indicated substantial heterogeneity, warranting further investigation. Eight studies were included, and funnel plots were not generated due to the absence of publication or reporting bias concerns.

“Summary of findings” table and GRADE

The GRADE system principles were applied to assess the quality of evidence regarding the primary outcome. GRADEpro software was used to create the “summary of findings” Table [17]. A “summary of findings” table was generated for “mortality of patients with severe neurological conditions” (see Supplement Table 1).

Results

Study selection

Following a systematic search based on the predefined strategy, a total of 5579 bibliographic citations were identified. After removing duplicates, 4,791 potentially relevant records remained. Based on the title, abstract, and full-text review, 8 studies were deemed eligible for inclusion. The identification flow chart is presented in Fig. 1.

Flowchart. RCT, randomized controlled trial

Characteristics of the included studies

The eight included RCTs were published between 2006 and 2024. Among them, five studies focused on patients with TBI [3, 16, 18–20], two involved patients with SAH [4, 21], and one study included patients with TBI, aneurysmal SAH, or ICH [13]. Detailed characteristics of the included studies are presented in Supplemental Table 2.

Risk of bias and quality assessment

Seven items detail specific procedures for generating random sequences [3, 4, 13, 16, 18–20]. In six studies, postoperative adverse events were evaluated by investigators who were blinded to the randomized outcome [3, 4, 13, 16, 19, 21]. The source of funding was not stated in the four of the studies, leaving it unclear whether any commercial sponsors were involved [16, 18–20]. The remaining three studies provided clear details regarding their funding sources [3, 4, 13]. The risk of bias summary is presented in Fig. 2 and Supplemental Table 3.

Synthesis of results

This review evaluated mortality, adverse outcomes, neurological complications, major non-neurological complications, as well as the length of ICU or hospital stay under both restrictive and permissive strategies.

Primary outcome: mortality at discharge

Four studies, involving 1,579 patients, assessed patient mortality at the time of hospital discharge (Fig. 3) [3, 4, 16, 20]. Overall, the combined results of these studies showed no significant difference in in-hospital mortality (20.5% for restrictive strategy vs. 20.8% for liberal strategy, RR: 0.98, 95% CI: 0.76 to 1.26, I2 = 21%).

Secondary outcomes

Mortality at ICU

Three of the eight studies, evaluated mortality during ICU stay (Fig. 4) [3, 16, 20]. The combined results showed no significant difference in ICU mortality between the two groups (15.8% for restrictive strategy vs. 15.7% for liberal strategy, RR: 1.36, 95% CI: 0.52 to 3.52, I2 = 48%).

Mortality at ICU. ICU, Intensive care unit

Mortality at six months

Four studies assessed mortality at six months (Fig. 5) [3, 13, 16, 19]. The combined results showed no significant difference in 6-month mortality between the liberal and restrictive transfusion groups, with negligible heterogeneity (27.7% for restrictive strategy vs.26.2% for liberal strategy, RR: 1.05, 95% CI: 0.90 to 1.22, I2 = 0%).

Mortality at six months

Long‑term mortality

Long-term mortality was defined as the sum of 60-day mortality and 12-month mortality across a total of 2557 patients. The combined results of the six studies showed no significant difference in long-term mortality between the two groups, suggesting negligible heterogeneity (27.3% for restrictive strategy vs. 26.1% for liberal strategy, RR: 1.04, 95% CI: 0.91 to 1.18, I2 = 0%) (Supplemental Fig. 1) [3, 4, 13, 16, 19, 20].

Unfavorable outcomes

Four out of the eight trials reported data on unfavorable GOS outcomes at 6 months (Supplemental Fig. 2) [3, 13, 16, 19]. The pooled results indicated no significant difference between the liberal and restrictive transfusion groups (71.1% for restrictive strategy vs. 64.9% for liberal strategy, RR: 1.07, 95% CI: 0.95 to 1.21, I2 = 55%). Long-term unfavorable outcomes were defined as patient dependence at 3 months and unfavorable GOS outcomes at 6–12 months. The combined results revealed a statistical difference between the two groups, with the liberal transfusion group showing a lower incidence of long-term unfavorable outcomes (60.5% for restrictive strategy vs. 55.0% for liberal strategy, RR: 1.09, 95% CI: 1.00 to 1.18, I2 = 27%) (Supplemental Fig. 3) [3, 4, 13, 16, 19, 21]. Although the pooled results suggested that a restrictive transfusion strategy may be associated with a small increase in the risk of long-term unfavorable outcomes (approximately 9%), it should be noted that the statistical evidence for this effect was weak (P = 0.05, with the lower confidence interval limit approaching 1). While the low heterogeneity among the included studies indicates good consistency of the results, the effect size was modest, and the conclusion was largely dependent on a few large-scale studies. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting this finding, and its clinical significance requires further validation in future studies.

Neurological adverse events

We calculated the incidence of cerebral vasospasm (Supplemental Fig. 4), the incidence of stroke (Supplemental Fig. 5), the incidence of intracranial hypertension requiring treatment (Supplemental Fig. 6), the risk of brain tissue hypoxia (Supplemental Fig. 7), delayed cerebral infarction (DCI) (Supplemental Fig. 8), and central nervous system infection (Supplemental Fig. 9). No statistical differences were observed for these outcomes between the two transfusion strategies.

Major non-neurological complications

Five studies measured the incidence of sepsis or septic shock, and the combined results showed a higher incidence in the restrictive strategy group than in the liberal strategy group (8.7% for restrictive strategy vs. 6.2% for liberal strategy, RR: 1.37, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.80, I2 = 0%) (Supplemental Fig. 10). Furthermore, we assessed the incidence of DVT, acute myocardial infarction, hypotension, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, ARDS, urinary tract infection, any adverse event related to transfusion, bacteremia, cardiac arrest, infections, venous thromboembolic events, renal insufficiency or failure, and duration of mechanical ventilation. No difference was found between the two groups (Supplemental Figs. 11–24).

Patients transfused

The number of patients who received RBC transfusions, the number of RBC units transfused per patient, the mean hemoglobin concentration of patients was showen in Supplemental Figs. 25–27.

ICU and hospital lengths of stay

The results indicated that there was no significant difference in the hospital length of stay (95% CI: -1.35 to 2.45, I2 = 0%) (Supplemental Fig. 28) and ICU length of stay (95% CI: -1.31 to 1.38, I2 = 45%) (Supplemental Fig. 29) between the two groups [3, 4, 13, 16, 20].

Sensitivity analyses

We also conducted a subgroup analysis based on the primary disease, examining patients with TBI and SAH. The pooled results showed no significant difference between the liberal and restrictive transfusion groups (Supplemental Fig. 30).

A study by Robertson et al. compared the effects of erythropoietin and two hemoglobin transfusion thresholds (7 and 10 g/dL) on neurological recovery after TBI [19]. To account for the impact of erythropoietin, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding the study by Robertson et al. The pooled results indicated that the incidence of unfavorable GOS outcomes at 6 months (72.5% for restrictive strategy vs. 64.6% for liberal strategy, RR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.20, I2 = 1%) (Supplemental Fig. 31) and the incidence of long-term unfavorable outcomes (60.7% for restrictive strategy vs. 54.0% for liberal strategy, RR: 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05 to 1.19, I2 = 0%) (Supplemental Fig. 32) were significantly lower in the liberal transfusion group compared to the restrictive group, with very low heterogeneity. A subgroup analysis of TBI patients showed similar results (70.5% for restrictive strategy vs. 63.6% for liberal strategy, RR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.02 to 1.19, I2 = 0%) (Supplemental Fig. 33).

Summary of findings

Based on the GRADE system, we found low-quality evidence regarding ICU mortality, in-hospital mortality, 6-month mortality, and long-term mortality (Supplemental Table 1).

Discussion

Our meta-analysis revealed no significant differences in ICU mortality, in-hospital mortality, 6-month mortality, or long-term mortality between the restricted and liberal transfusion groups. However, the incidence of adverse outcomes on the 6-month GOS and long-term GOS was significantly lower in the liberal transfusion strategy group compared to the restrictive transfusion strategy group. Additionally, the restrictive transfusion strategy group had a higher incidence of sepsis or septic shock when compared to the liberal transfusion strategy group.

Anemia is prevalent among neurocritical patients, particularly those with moderate to severe cases, partly due to hemodilution from perioperative bleeding and fluid resuscitation [6]. According to Roh et al., inflammation’s multimodal effects on erythrocyte survival and erythropoiesis significantly contribute to anemia in patients with cerebral hemorrhage [22]. These processes can worsen secondary brain injury, as hemoglobin levels correlate with ischemic lesions detectable via magnetic resonance imaging [23]. A hemoglobin level below 9 g/dL independently predicts brain hypoxia and cellular dysfunction [24]. The severity of anemia directly affects brain hypoxia, with brain hypoxia increasing by 70% for each 1 g/dL decrease in hemoglobin [25].

Cerebral oxygen supply depends on both arterial oxygen content and cerebral blood flow, with hemoglobin’s oxygen-binding capacity being a key determinant of arterial oxygen content. In acute brain injury, impaired brain autonomic regulation can lead to iadequate oxygen delivery [26]. Acute anemia increases the risk of cerebral vasospasm in SAH patients, with the degree of hemoglobin reduction (rather than its absolute value) correlating with a higher risk of delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI), MRI ischemic lesions, and poor outcomes [27, 28]. In TBI patients, RBC transfusion may worsen brain autoregulatory dysfunction, potentially due to increased blood viscosity or endothelial dysfunction [29]. A large retrospective study found that red blood cell and plasma transfusions were linked to higher mortality in severe TBI cases, though it remains unclear whether higher mortality drives more transfusions or vice versa [30]. Additionally, pro-inflammatory mediators in blood products could exacerbate secondary neuroinflammation following TBI [29]. Clinicians must carefully balance cerebral oxygen demand and potential transfusion complications before administering blood products to patients with acute brain injury [11].

Anemia can subtly but significantly impact neurological function, and common outcome measures (such as mortality, Glasgow prognostic scores, and functional assessments) may not fully capture the complex physiological processes in TBI or clinical regression [26]. Our observation found no significant difference in near- and long-term mortality between patients receiving restrictive versus liberal transfusions. TBI, SAH, and ICH each involve complex pathophysiology, with various factors (including primary and secondary brain injuries) influencing outcomes. Beyond anemia, which disrupts the balance of cerebral oxygen supply and demand, other factors (such as cerebral perfusion pressure, DCI prevention, and organ function preservation) also contribute to secondary brain injury [31–34]. A recent retrospective study identified two types of intracranial hemorrhage and preoperative anticoagulant use as independent mortality risk factors following craniotomy in elderly TBI patients [35]. Additionally, extracranial complications (e.g., ARDS, kidney injury, myocardial injury, and coagulopathy) play a multifactorial role in clinical regression [36].

A recent trial in patients with mixed acute brain injury found that maintaining hemoglobin levels > 9 g/dL with liberal RBC transfusions improved long-term neurological outcomes [13]. Acute anemia increases the risk of ischemic secondary brain injury, so RBC transfusions may reduce ischemic injury in cerebral hemorrhage patients and improve clinical regression [27]. Previous studies suggest that anemia following ICH is linked to inflammation, which induces microthrombosis [22, 37]. However, the exact role of anemia, inflammation, or other risk factors in ischemic brain injury remains unclear. This clarification is vital, as RBC transfusions carry risks. Robertson et al. found that maintaining hemoglobin > 10 g/dL did not improve 6-month neurological outcomes but was associated with higher adverse event rates, such as DVT [19]. The factorial design of the study introduces limitations, such as potential treatment delivery errors that could affect results. Interactions between the two interventions may also complicate outcome assessments, requiring careful interpretation [38]. Interestingly, excluding this study in a sensitivity analysis showed a significant reduction in heterogeneity and a lower incidence of long-term adverse regression in the liberal transfusion group. For adverse events like DVT, pooled results showed no significant difference between transfusion strategies.

Sepsis is relatively rare in neurocritical care patients but is linked to higher in-hospital mortality, making prevention vital for survival [39]. Anemia exacerbates tissue hypoxia and increases the risk of bacterial infections, especially gram-positive ones [40]. Higher oxygen saturation and hemoglobin levels, along with lower lactate levels, reduce hypoxia and significantly lower death risk in sepsis or septic shock patients [41]. Hu et al. identified anemia as an independent predictor of secondary sepsis in ICU TBI patients, with those having anemia at higher risk [41]. We observed a higher incidence of sepsis or septic shock in the restrictive transfusion group compared to the liberal group. Chen et al. found that hemoglobin levels were negatively correlated with 28-day mortality in sepsis patients (41–104 g/L), with each 1 g/L increase in hemoglobin reducing the risk of death by 10% [42]. Several potential mechanisms may explain the close association between hemoglobin levels and sepsis. First, in patients with sepsis, increased tissue oxygen demand coupled with reduced hemoglobin levels exacerbates hypoxia, leading to organ dysfunction and worse clinical outcomes [43]. In addition, hemoglobin plays a key role in defending against pathogens, supporting leukocyte function, and promoting nonspecific immunity by sequestering iron, thus depriving bacteria of essential nutrients. It also helps process carbon dioxide, maintain hemodynamic stability, and transport antibiotics [42, 44]. Furthermore, hemoglobin modulates antibiotic pharmacokinetics through its effects on drug metabolism and absorption, with declining hemoglobin levels substantially compromising antimicrobial effectiveness [43]. Increasing evidence suggests hemoglobin also contributes to sepsis-related organ failure through mechanisms such as cellular dissociation, inflammation regulation, nitric oxide scavenging, and free radical damage [45].

Post-transfusion cardiopulmonary complications, such as transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) and transfusion-related circulatory overload (TACO), are major causes of morbidity and mortality [46–48]. TACO typically presents as cardiogenic pulmonary edema, while TRALI manifests as noncardiogenic pulmonary edema. Identifying risk factors (such as a history of TACO, advanced age, renal failure, and cardiac insufficiency) is crucial before transfusion [46]. For high-risk patients, strategies like managing fluid overload, slowing the transfusion rate, and increasing monitoring should be considered. In our study, there was no significant difference in transfusion-related adverse events between the two groups. However, limited studies focused on TRALI and TACO, suggesting that controlling the transfusion rate and carefully managing the process do not increase the incidence of complications, regardless of transfusion volume.

This systematic evaluation has several limitations. First, the thresholds for liberal and restrictive transfusion vary across studies, with a significant gray area between them. This requires caution when interpreting and generalizing the findings, and clinicians should make individualized decisions based on the risks and benefits of RBC transfusion. Second, there is a limited number of large, high-quality studies, with many having small sample sizes, resulting in a low level of evidence. Furthermore, our focus on the overall population of neurocritical illness limited access to subgroup data, preventing a more effective analysis of how transfusion strategies impact specific subgroups such as TBI, SAH, and ICH.

Conclusions

To sum up, our systemic review suggests that while both transfusion strategies had similar impacts on mortality outcomes, the liberal strategy may offer a better prognosis in terms of long-term recovery and a reduced risk of sepsis.
