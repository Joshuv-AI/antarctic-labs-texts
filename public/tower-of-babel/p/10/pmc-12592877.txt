Cultural Adaptation and Validation of the Hindi Version of Malocclusion Impact Scale for Early Childhood for Assessing Oral Health-related Quality of Life in Indian Preschool Children
Authors: Gauri Kalra, Tanu Nangia, Varinder Goyal, Apurva Mishra, Ruchi Nagpal, Anubhav Sharma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA specialized tool was originally developed in Brazilian–Portuguese to assess how malocclusion in primary teeth affects the oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) of preschool children and their parents.AimTo translate the Malocclusion Impact Scale for Early Childhood (MIS-EC) into Hindi, evaluate its reliability and validity, and determine its usefulness in assessing the impact of malocclusion on the OHRQoL of Indian preschool children and their parents.MethodsThe Hindi MIS-EC was adapted using international translation guidelines and administered to 110 preschool children (3–5 years). Reliability was tested through intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and test–retest methods, while validity was examined using convergent and discriminant analyses.ResultsThe Hindi MIS-EC demonstrated good reliability, with an overall Cronbach's α of 0.71 and an ICC of 0.87. Discriminant validity (4.83) was significant, effectively distinguishing between children with and without malocclusion. Convergent validity was also confirmed, as the Hindi MIS-EC showed a significant correlation with the general health status of the child.ConclusionThe Hindi MIS-EC scale is a reliable and valid tool for assessing the effect of malocclusion on the OHRQoL of children aged 3–5 years in India.How to cite this articleKalra G, Nangia T, Goyal V, et al. Introduction

Management of oral health-related problems holds prime importance for protecting primary dentition during childhood. Dental problems in children not only cause loss of function and esthetics but also psychological issues, sleep disturbances, low nutrition, missed school, and poor performances.1 Often, parents fail to understand or notice significant issues with the primary dentition. This could be due to insufficient awareness and inadequate recognition of the role of healthy primary teeth in overall well-being. Certain oral problems, such as eruption disturbances, minor tooth malpositioning causing bite problems in children, or caries in young children, may cause chewing difficulties, compromised esthetics, and function.2 Besides dental caries, malocclusion is often present in primary dentition. A recent study by Sharma et al.3 found spacing in maxillary teeth in 81.9% children, overbite (30.9%), and open bite in 1.9% children in India. Such malpositions compromise esthetics, function and also affect the oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) in children.4,5 Thus, diagnosing malocclusions in the primary dentition and evaluating future orthodontic risks can guide timely interventions, enabling appropriate treatment while minimizing overall costs and complexity.

Many generic assessment tools have been available to examine the effect of dental caries, traumatic injuries, and malocclusion in various populations.6–8 However, general assessment tools are not able to assess conditions specifically, as they lack sensitivity and are unable to measure specific changes associated with a specific condition in individuals.9 Thus, there is a lack of solid evidence assessing the effect of malocclusion on the OHRQoL of children and adolescents, especially in preschoolers with primary dentition. A specific malocclusion impact scale may be able to identify malocclusion at an early age and prevent problems related to psychological issues. The Malocclusion Impact Scale for Early Childhood (MIS-EC) was developed in Brazilian–Portuguese and English by Homem et al. in 2021, consisting of 8 items.10 Thus, to make its applicability in other languages and cultures, there is a dire need to translate, validate, and test among the Indian population. To the best of our knowledge, the Hindi-translated version of the MIS-EC is not available, and hence, in this context, this study aimed to translate the questionnaire into Hindi, validate its reliability and validity, and assess its use among Indian preschool children. It was hypothesized that the Hindi version would be comparable to the original MIS-EC and demonstrate acceptable psychometric properties.

Methods

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) and was conducted in the Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry of a private dental hospital and research center in Haryana, India. Prior informed consent was taken from the parents of participants of the study, and they were provided with detailed information regarding the questionnaire and study.

Settings

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted in adherence with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Participants

Sample size estimation was based on the guidelines from previous studies suggesting a minimum of 10 respondents per questionnaire item.11 The current scale consisted of eight questions with two general health questions, making a total of 10 questions; therefore, a sample of 100 individuals was estimated to be included for the study. According to the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), a minimum of 100 participants in the sample size is required for quantitative instrument validation studies.12 A pilot sample of 110 preschool children (3–5 years old), exclusively in the primary dentition stage, reporting to the Outpatient Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry in a Private Institute in Haryana, India, were recruited by convenience sampling method. Children were excluded if they had experienced dental pain from caries or traumatic dental injury in the month before examination, or if they had any previous history of orthodontic treatment or appliance use. Either of the parents available with the child was given the 8-item questionnaire before dental examination.

Malocclusion Impact Scale for Early Childhood

The MIS-EC scale consisted of 8 items across two domains—six items in the child impact domain and two in the family impact domain. Scoring of the responses was done from 0 (never) to 5 (very often) and 6 (do not know), a Likert scale was used, with the higher the score, the greater the impact. In addition, parents were asked two questions about their child's overall oral health to assess convergent validity, with responses recorded on a five-point scale as very good = 1, good = 2, fair = 3, poor = 4, and very poor = 5.

The original 8-item Brazilian–Portuguese version of MIS-EC was translated into the English language first, following translation into Hindi language based on a standardized linguistic exercise by two bilingual translators with dental experience. Both the experts were fluent in English and Hindi, and they independently produced two initial Hindi drafts of the questionnaire. Only minimal revisions were required, after which the Hindi questionnaire was back-translated into English by two dental professionals who had no prior exposure to the MIS-EC scale. The final version was sent to a panel of five dental experts for expert opinion, assessing the quality of translation and comprehension of the scale. Pretesting of the questionnaire was done on 10 parents who were excluded from the main study. The translated questionnaire was understandable by the parents, and no changes were suggested.

Clinical Examination

Children were made to sit on the dental chair in the clinical area and were examined orally with the help of a mouth mirror and dental explorer for any malocclusion, dental caries, and traumatic injury to the primary teeth. During clinical examination, dental caries was evaluated using the World Health Organization criteria (dmft index), in which no caries was expressed as 0, and the presence of caries was expressed as numerical data >0. Traumatic dental injuries (TDI), if any, were determined using the criteria established by Andreasen and Lovschall,13 dichotomized as the absence or presence of at least one type of trauma. Malocclusion was assessed using the criteria of Foster and Hamilton.14 A precalibrated single investigator examined all the children and recorded the findings.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS software version 24.0 (SPSS for Windows, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA), with the level of significance set at 0.05. Sociodemographic and questionnaire variables were analyzed descriptively and presented as mean, standard deviation (SD), range, and frequencies (numbers and percentages). Reliability and validity of the scale were assessed through internal consistency using Cronbach's α, along with test–retest reliability. Discriminant validity with malocclusion and its types was evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Results

A total of 110 children aged 3–5 years (mean age: 4.17 years) were included in the study. Among them, 54.5% presented with dental caries and 10.9% with traumatic dental injuries. Malocclusion was observed in multiple forms, with an overall prevalence of 63.6% (n = 70) (Table 1). Table 2 presents the mean, SD, and range for the overall MIS-EC and its two domains. Caregivers' perception of their child's oral health varied: 46.4% rated it as poor, while 32.1% considered it very good/good. Only 12 mothers (10.7%) reported that their child's oral health had never negatively affected overall well-being, whereas 42.8% of parents indicated some degree of impact on OHRQoL (MIS-EC ≥ 1). Validity and Reliability

Convergent reliability of the Hindi MIS-EC scale was established, as the scores showed a significant correlation with overall health and general well-being measures (p < 0.001). Internal consistency of the scale items was assessed using Cronbach's α coefficient, while test–retest reliability was evaluated by calculating the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) after a 2-week interval in a subset of 20 children. The total Cronbach's α value was 0.71 for the sample, and ICC was 0.87, which was significant (Table 4). Discriminant validity was assessed by comparing the mean scores of preschool children with and without malocclusion. The findings revealed that children with malocclusion had significantly higher total Hindi MIS-EC scores, indicating a greater negative impact on OHRQoL compared to those without malocclusion. Mann–Whitney U test is significant for total MIS-EC scores; family impact domain and child impact domain (p < 0.001) (Table 5). According to the type of malocclusion present in children, overjet was most prevalent (61.8%); crossbite and rotation (14.5% each), and anterior open bite was 3.6%, respectively. In the current study, rotations present in anterior primary teeth were found to be associated with OHRQol of preschoolers using Hindi MIS-EC (p < 0.0001) (Table 6).

Discussion and Conclusion

Unlike dental caries or traumatic dental injuries (TDIs), malocclusion in primary dentition is often undetected, largely because parents are unaware of its potential esthetic, psychological, and financial consequences, and they only take their children to a dentist in case of dental pain/swelling. The present study described the validation of the Brazilian–Portuguese version of MIS-EC in Hindi for the assessment of the effect of malocclusion on the OHRQoL of preschoolers and their primary caregivers in India, and to the best of our knowledge, this is the first pilot study to be conducted among the Indian population using this scale. The translated scale was easy to comprehend for the caregivers of the preschool children. The hypothesis proposed that the Hindi MIS-EC would demonstrate good reliability and validity for assessing the OHRQoL in the Indian preschool population. Zaror et al., in 2018, developed a Chilean version of ECOHIS to assess the impact of dental caries, TDIs, and malocclusion on OHRQoL in preschool children and found it an effective tool for dental caries but insignificant nonspecific results for malocclusion.15 However, the original scale of MIS-EC was thus developed and validated to specifically demonstrate the effect of malocclusion on the OHRQoL in primary dentition.

For the reliability test, the Cronbach's α value was 0.71 in the current study, which is near to the original scale value (0.87). However, it was higher in the cross-cultural variation of the English version of the MIS-EC into Chinese conducted by Chen.16 This is due to the fact that our study included a lesser sample size as compared to the other two studies. Test–retest ICC values for the current validation study were 0.87, which is slightly lower than that of the original scale study (0.94); however, this shows good repeatability. Such value differences are due to cultural and social factors.

We also evaluated the discriminant validity of Hindi MIS-EC for children with and without malocclusion and it was higher and significant for the current study. The results are concurrent with the original study and the Chinese validation of MIS-EC scale study.7,16 In the present study, rotations present in anterior primary teeth were found to be associated with OHRQol of preschoolers using Hindi MIS-EC; however, Ramos-Jorge et al.,17 using the ECOHIS scale, reported that anterior open bite was linked to a negative impact on OHRQoL. Similarly, Homem et al. observed that an anterior open bite significantly affected the OHRQoL of younger children.10

The limitations were that the sample size was less and the findings of the study were restricted to a specific population, the children aged 3–5 years. Clinical, social, and demographic factors were considered potential confounders, as they may negatively influence the OHRQoL of Indian preschoolers. The Hindi MIS-EC demonstrated good reliability and validity and can be effectively used to assess OHRQoL in preschool children with malocclusion in the primary dentition. However, further sensitivity analyses on larger longitudinal samples are needed to strengthen these findings.

Thus, Hindi MIS-EC is a reliable and valid assessment tool to evaluate the effect of malocclusion in primary dentition on the OHRQoL in Indian preschool children. However, the findings of the current study are preliminary and restricted to a specific population; future studies are required to evaluate the responsiveness and sensitivity of MIS-EC on different populations and varied languages.

Clinical Implications in the Field of Pediatric Dentistry

This study introduces a validated Hindi version of the MIS-EC questionnaire, providing pediatric dentists in India and other Hindi-speaking regions with a reliable tool to assess the impact of malocclusion on the oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL) of preschool children.
