Influence of droplet size and surface hydrophobicity of soybean protein-based nanoemulsion fillers on the quality of silver carp myofibrillar protein gels
Authors: Chenxing Du, Ge Zhu, Hanwen Hu, Zhangqun Duan, Shuizhong Luo, Lin Lin, Jianfeng Lu, Zhi Zheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the mechanisms underlying the influence of droplet size and emulsifier wettability on gel properties when oil-in-water (O/W) emulsions serve as fillers in myofibrillar protein (MP) gels. Pickering emulsions with varying droplet sizes were prepared using soybean protein isolate (SPI) and SPI-curcumin nanoparticles, then used to construct composite gels. Findings showed that decreased droplet size and increased emulsifier surface hydrophobicity enhanced hydrophobic interactions in the gel, increasing the β-sheet content of MP molecules. Upon the introduction of SPI-Cur-NPs stabilized nanoemulsion (SCNE), the hydrophobic force in the gel was approximately 2.6-fold more remarkable than that of the control, and the β-sheet content increased to 16.51 %. This resulted in a denser mesh framework and more uniform oil droplet distribution, increasing the hardness value from 26.993 g to 41.847 g. Moreover, SCNE addition improved gel antioxidant properties, reducing carbonyl and peroxide levels to 31.82 % and 24.15 % of the control, respectively. These findings offer insights for improving MP-based gel products.Keywords: Nanoemulsion, Surimi gel, Soybean isolate protein, Droplet size, Hydrophobic interaction

Introduction

Surimi gel is an important food ingredient in today's global market, particularly in Asia (Gani et al., 2018). The industry has observed a considerable trend in creating surimi products that contain critical fatty acids, driven by customers' increasing need for food with full nutritional content. Nevertheless, directly incorporating fats and oils into surimi and the unequal spread of these fats and oils might disturb the framework of the protein network and hinder the interconnection of molecules (Lu et al., 2022). To address this issue, utilizing O/W Pickering emulsions derived from edible oil as fillers has been proposed as a potential solution in recent years. Wang, Luan, et al. (2023) effectively improved the texture of MP gels by incorporating OSA starch-stabilized Pickering emulsions as fillers. Similarly, Wang, Zhao, et al. (2023) significantly enhanced the water-holding capacity and viscoelasticity of the MP gels via the addition of Pickering emulsions stabilized by Tenebrio molitor protein. However, the exact mechanism by which Pickering emulsions impact surimi gels still needs to be fully understood due to variations in preparation methods and emulsifier properties. Specifically, the impact of two critical factors, droplet size and emulsifier hydrophobicity, on gel properties is yet to be thoroughly investigated.

The size of droplets in Pickering emulsions can have various effects on the quality of composite gels (Lv et al., 2024). Micron-sized droplets can increase the gel's volume and water-holding capacity by filling up space (Zeng et al., 2023). On the other hand, nanosized droplets can be more evenly distributed, resulting in a finer gel structure. Additionally, droplet size can influence how they interact with protein molecules (Cai et al., 2022). Larger droplets primarily affect gel properties through physical filling, while nanodroplets can form more interfacial interactions with proteins, potentially changing their shape and function. These interactions between droplets and proteins of different sizes ultimately determine the stability and gelling properties of the filler in surimi gels (Lee et al., 2023). Consequently, they also affect the texture and mouthfeel of the final product.

Currently, a common method for improving the quality of surimi gels is strengthening the non-covalent forces between molecules in the myofibrillar protein gel, particularly the hydrophobic interaction forces (Chen, Chen, et al., 2020; Chen, Chen, et al., 2020). These forces have been shown to contribute to up to around 90 % of the non-covalent forces in the gels. Cen et al. (2022) increased the strength of the hydrophobic interaction forces in the MP gels by including quinoa protein-stabilized Pickering emulsions. This resulted in a significant 80 % increase in the hardness of the gels. Nevertheless, most existing research primarily concentrates on enhancing the characteristics of surimi gels by utilizing Pickering emulsions as additives. However, a more comprehensive investigation is still needed into the precise impact of variations in the surface hydrophobicity of emulsifiers on the non-covalent interaction forces within the gels. The enhanced hydrophobicity of the microenvironment promotes structural changes in MP, thereby altering the formation process and final structure of the gel network (Du et al., 2024). In a prior investigation, we fabricated nanoparticles using curcumin and SPI that exhibited superior surface hydrophobicity compared to SPI alone (Du, Xu, et al., 2022). These nanoparticles were then utilized as emulsifiers to create nanoemulsions of the O/W type, which displayed excellent resilience to environmental stress (Du, Wang, & Zheng, 2023). Significantly, incorporating curcumin in the emulsifier not only enhances the gel's structure but also improves the oxidative stability of surimi proteins and lipids. Therefore, the SPI-curcumin-stabilized nanoemulsion has the potential to serve as a versatile filler in the production of surimi gels.

Based on the above considerations, the present study was conducted to prepare SPI-based nanoparticles with varying surface hydrophobicity and use them as emulsifiers to construct Pickering emulsions with varying droplet sizes. These emulsions were then used as fillers to prepare fish-derived myofibrillar protein gels. The study analyzed the impact of droplet size and emulsifier hydrophobicity on surimi gel quality by examining hardness, rheological characteristics, water distribution, and protein structure. Furthermore, a thorough assessment was conducted to determine the composite gel's stability under oxidative stress. This research aims to establish a theoretical foundation and provide technical support for developing high-quality surimi gel products with enhanced nutritional content.

Materials and methods

Materials

SPI with a protein content of 92.13 % was obtained from low-denatured defatted soybean flour (Shandong Wandefu Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shandong, China) (Du, Xu, et al., 2022). The preparation process of SPI was previously described by our group (Du, Wang, & Zheng, 2023). Grade AAA frozen silver carp surimi was sourced from Jiangsu Tianyi Surimi Production Co. (Xuzhou, Jiangsu, China). Curcumin (98 % purity) was acquired from Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Additive-free walnut oil, produced by COFCO Corporation (Beijing, China), was stored at 4 °C prior to use (Table S1). Tris-HCl buffer was purchased from Solebo Biotechnology Co. (Beijing, China). All other chemicals were of analytical grade unless otherwise specified.

Preparation of emulsion

SPI-Cur-NPs were synthesized following the method described by Du, Xu, et al. (2022), using curcumin at a concentration of 50 μmol/L. Nanoemulsions were prepared based on the technique reported by Du, Wang, and Zheng (2023), with several modifications. Initially, 4 % (w/w) SPI-Cur-NPs powder was dissolved in deionized water and stirred for 2 h at room temperature. The solution was then kept at 4 °C overnight to ensure complete hydration. The emulsions were prepared in two stages: i) A mixture of SPI solution and walnut oil was prepared at a 9:1 (v/v) ratio. This mixture was homogenized using a high-speed shear at 13,000 rpm for 3 min to create micron-sized emulsions. These emulsions were stabilized by SPI (SE) with SPI-Cur-NPs (SCE). ii) Further homogenization was performed using an ultra-high pressure homogenizer at 100 MPa. The micron-sized emulsions were subjected to three cycles to achieve nanoemulsions. All homogenization processes were conducted at 4 °C.

Isolation of MP

MP was extracted from surimi using a modified method of Zhang et al. (2023). Frozen surimi was thawed at 4 °C and mixed with five volumes of Tris-HCl buffer (0.05 mol/L KCl, 0.02 mol/L Tris-HCl, pH 7.0). The resulting suspension was homogenized using a stirrer at 500 rpm for 2 h at 4 °C to facilitate protein solubilization. Following homogenization, the sample was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to separate the solid fraction. The resulting precipitate was re-suspended in Tris-HCl buffer (0.6 mol/L KCl, 0.02 mol/L Tris-HCl, pH 7.0) to enhance protein solubility and allowed to dissolve completely at 4 °C. The solution was then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C to remove any remaining insoluble material. MP was isolated by diluting the supernatant with a 10-fold volume of deionized water, inducing protein precipitation. The protein content of the extracted MP was determined to be 56.21 ± 2.22 mg/mL using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) method, while the protein purity was determined to be 97.56 % ± 1.86 % using the Kjeldahl method (Karimi et al., 2022).

Preparation of composite gel

The composite gels were prepared following a modified version of the method described by Cen et al. (2022). Initially, the MP concentration was adjusted to 44 mg/mL using Tris-HCl buffer (0.6 mol/L KCl and 0.02 mol/L Tris-HCl, pH 7.0). Subsequently, a 7.5 % (v/v) emulsion was added to the MP solution and homogenized using high-speed shear mixing. The mixture was centrifuged at 500 rpm to remove air bubbles, and then transferred into 10 mL gel vials. A two-step heating process was then applied to the samples. In the first step, the samples were heated at 40 °C for 40 min. Immediately following this, the second step involved heating the samples at 90 °C for an additional 15 min. After the two-step heating process was completed, the samples were rapidly cooled in an ice-water bath and stored overnight at 4 °C prior to further analysis.

Texture analysis

Gel samples were cut into 2 cm × 2 cm squares and equilibrated at room temperature for 1 h Texture analysis was performed using a TA-XT texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, Surrey, UK) equipped with a P36R probe. The experimental parameters were set according to the method described by Xu et al. (2022). All measurements were conducted in sextuplicate.

Rheological measurement

The rheological properties of the composite gels were assessed using a DHR-3 rheometer (TA Instruments, Delaware, USA). Changes in storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were measured during temperature scanning from 20 °C to 95 °C. A parallel plate geometry with a 40 mm diameter was employed. The experimental parameters were set according to the method described by Li, Wang, Cui, Chen, Zhao, & Bai, (2024).

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

The water composition and distribution in the composite gel were determined using an NMI20-040H-I low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrometer (Niumag Corporation, Jiangsu, China). A 0.5 g sample of the composite gel was accurately weighed and placed in an NMR tube. The instrument parameters were set as follows: spectral width of 200 kHz, echo time of 0.2 ms, waiting time of 5000 ms, and RF delay time of 0.08 ms. Data were collected using 12,000 echoes and 32 scan repetitions for subsequent analysis.

Microstructure observation

The microstructures of the composite gels were analyzed following the methods described by Cao et al. (2022). Observations were conducted using Confocal Laser Scanning Microscopy (CLSM; Olympus, Japan) and Cryo-Scanning Electron Microscopy (Cryo-SEM; QUORUM, UK). Each sample was examined in triplicate.

Circular dichroism (CD)

The circular dichroism (CD) spectra of the composite gels were acquired using a Jasco-815 spectrophotometer (Tokyo, Japan). The protein concentration in the composite gel was adjusted to 0.3 mg/mL using a 0.6 mol/L potassium chloride solution. Spectral measurements were recorded over the range of 200–260 nm. The secondary structures of all samples were analyzed using Young's equation. All measurements were performed in triplicate for each sample group.

Chemical interaction

The intermolecular forces within the composite gels were evaluated using the methodology outlined by Chen et al. (2021). This process involved the preparation of five distinct protein-denaturing solutions: SA (0.05 mol/L KCl), SB (0.6 mol/L KCl), SC (0.6 mol/L KCl + 1.5 mol urea), SD (0.6 mol/L NaCl +8 mol/L urea), and SE (0.6 mol/L NaCl +8 mol/L urea +30 mmol/L DTT). Subsequently, 2 g of each composite gel sample was precisely weighed and combined with 20 mL of the respective protein-denaturing solution. The mixtures were then homogenized and fragmented, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min. After centrifugation, the samples were gently stirred at 4 °C for 1 h. Finally, the intermolecular forces in the gels were determined by measuring the protein concentration in the supernatant using the BCA method (Bainor et al., 2011).

Oxidative stability

The oxidative stability of the composite gels was evaluated following the method described by Xu et al. (2021), with slight modifications. Samples were cut into 2 cm × 2 cm squares and placed in open petri dishes. These dishes were then incubated at 50 °C for 12 h. After incubation, the gels were fragmented for subsequent analysis of relevant parameters.

Determination of carbonyl and dityrosine content

The carbonyl content was quantified using the method described by Chen, Chen, et al. (2020). The fragmented composite gel was dissolved in 30 mL of KCl solution (0.6 mol/L), and the protein concentration was adjusted to 5 mg/mL. 1 mL of this protein solution was mixed with an equal volume of 10 mmol/L 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNPH) solution and incubated for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Subsequently, 3 mL of trichloroacetic acid (TCA) solution (0.096 mol/L, w/v) was added, mixed thoroughly, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm. The precipitate was washed with a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of ethyl acetate and ethanol. The washed precipitate was then dissolved in 6 mol/L guanidine hydrochloride and incubated for 1 h. After dissolution and centrifugation to remove insoluble material, the absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 370 nm.

The dityrosine content of the composite gel was quantified using the methodology described by Du et al. (2024). 1 mL of the protein solution was combined with 10 μL of TCA solution (0.096 mol/L, w/v) and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The resulting precipitate was re-dissolved in 20 mmol/L Tris-HCl buffer (pH 6.0) containing 0.6 mol/L KCl. Following complete dissolution, the fluorescence intensity of the solution was measured at an excitation wavelength of 325 nm and an emission wavelength of 420 nm.

Determination of peroxide value (PV) and TBARS content

Peroxide value (PV) and thiobarbituric acid reactive substances (TBARS) in the composite gel were determined using previously established methods by Zhang et al. (2024). PV content (nmol/kg gel) was quantified using a standard curve of iron (III) chloride, described by the equation: A = 0.4486C + 0.0041 (R2 = 0.999), where A represents absorbance and C represents the concentration of Fe3+ ions (μg/mL). TBARS content was quantified using a standard curve of curcumin-ethanol solutions, following the equation: A = 0.5309C + 0.0077 (R2 = 0.999), where A represents absorbance and C represents the TBARS concentration (nmol/kg gel).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, with each experiment performed in at least triplicate. Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS 27.0 software. Differences between groups were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test for post-hoc comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Texture analysis

Table 1 presents the textural properties of the composite gels, showing that the incorporation of different emulsions generally altered the hardness, springiness, cohesiveness, and resilience of the gels compared to the control, with SCNE exhibiting the highest hardness value. The composite gel containing SCNE exhibited the highest hardness (41.847 ± 1.545 g/kg), significantly higher than the control (26.993 ± 1.083 g/kg), while the gel with SE showed only a slight increase (28.045 ± 0.871 g/kg). These findings suggest that the effect of Pickering emulsions as a filler in MP gels depends on the emulsifier's surface properties. The observed differences in hardness can be attributed to variations in the emulsifiers' surface hydrophobicity and the emulsion droplet size (Chen, Chen, et al., 2020; Chen, Zhou, et al., 2020). Curcumin, a flavonoid polyphenol, likely enhanced the binding of MP and filler by strengthening hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding, consequently increasing the hardness of the composite gels (Hu et al., 2023).

Furthermore, nanoscale Pickering emulsions exhibited enhanced fluidity and greater specific surface area than micrometer-sized emulsions. These features of emulsion fillers facilitate their effective embedding inside the gaps of the gel network while enabling strong binding to the MP matrix through various physicochemical interactions, including electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interaction. Additionally, the ultra-high pressure homogenization treatment effectively expanded the structure of SPI-Cur-NPs, revealing an increased number of hydrophobic areas (Yang et al., 2022). The resulting structure could facilitate the cross-linking of SPI with MP through hydrophobic interactions, forming a more compact and rigid gel network structure.

Rheological properties

Dynamic rheology is frequently employed to ascertain the mechanism of protein thermogel production. Fig. 1A illustrates the impact of various emulsions on the storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G“) during the MP thermal process. Across all samples, the values of G' and G" exhibited a similar upward trend at temperatures between 40 and 50 °C, but showed a contrasting trend at 60 °C. Subsequently, these values declined within the temperature range of 50–60 °C, aligning with the findings reported by Wang et al. (2024). The incorporation of Pickering emulsions led to an increase in both G' and loss modulus G" for all composite gels compared to the control (Fig. 1A), following the order: SCNE > SCE > SNE > SE > control. This trend corresponds to changes in droplet size and surface hydrophobicity of the emulsifiers. The enhanced rheological properties can be attributed to several factors: i) Nanoscale emulsions (SCNE, SNE) provide a larger interfacial area for MP molecule adsorption and interaction compared to microscale emulsions (SCE, SE); ii) SPI-Cur-NPs (in SCNE and SCE) exhibit increased surface hydrophobicity, promoting stronger interactions between MP molecules and oil droplets. This enhancement in hydrophobicity leads to stronger interactions between MP molecules and oil droplets, resulting in a more rigid gel structure (Gani & Benjakul, 2018).

Temperature sweep curves of MP and the composite gels. G′ (solid lines) represents the elastic (storage) modulus, whereas G″ (dashed lines) represents the viscous (loss) modulus.

The thermal gelation behavior, characterized by changes in G' and G“ between 40-60°C, indicates MP molecule unfolding and aggregation. Higher G' and G" values for emulsion-containing samples during this process suggest that emulsion droplets influence protein network formation, potentially acting as junction points within the network. Furthermore, curcumin's role in increasing SPI's surface hydrophobicity enhances the adsorption and aggregation of MP molecules onto oil droplet surfaces (Zhang et al., 2022). These rheological findings correlate with the previously observed changes in droplet size and surface hydrophobicity, suggesting that both the physical presence of emulsion droplets and their surface properties play crucial roles in modifying the gel network structure and enhancing its rheological properties.

LF-NMR

The LF-NMR technique is exceptionally effective in characterizing the status and distribution of water molecules in myofibrillar protein gels. Fig. 2A shows that the T2 relaxation time profile reveals a significant impact of different emulsifiers on the gel system's bound, fixed, and free water content. Fig. 2B further visualizes these findings through color intensity maps, where blue (low intensity) to red (high intensity) correspond to signal strength in different gel samples. The introduction of Pickering emulsions substantially augmented the amount of immobilized water within the gel matrix compared to the control. Such a phenomenon can be explained by restricting water molecule movement due to emulsion droplets scattered throughout the protein network as active fillers (Fei et al., 2024). Further comparison showed that the signal intensity of immobilized water in the composite gels made of nano-emulsions was greater than that of regular emulsions (MP-SC, MP-SCE) with the same type of emulsifier. The observation indicates that nanoscale droplets can create a filler network that is evenly distributed and has a greater surface area, effectively binding water molecules and enhancing the gels' ability to retain water.

(A) Typical curve of T2 relaxation time for MP and the composite gels. (B) MRI images of MP and the composite gels.

Furthermore, utilizing the SPI-curcumin complex as an emulsifier allows for producing composite gels (MP-SCE, MP-SE) that possess a more significant amount of bound water and a lesser amount of free water. Two possible explanations for these results are: i) The addition of curcumin hydrophobic groups increased the hydrophobic nature of the SPI molecules, creating a more compact adsorption layer at the emulsion interface. The resulting interfacial membrane, modified for increased hydrophobicity, forms stronger connections with the myofibrillar protein matrix, promoting the formation of a denser three-dimensional network structure. In this configuration, water molecules mainly reside within bound and stationary water, with a considerably diminished amount of free water (Jia et al., 2019). ii) The hydrophobic SPI-curcumin complexes can also cause protein molecules in the gel system to bind due to hydrophobic forces, leading to structural alterations in the MPs. Consequently, the holes and flaws in the protein network structure decrease, and the resistance to water molecules at inevitable locations increases, restricting free water's movement and clustering (Li, Wang, Cui, Chen, Zhao, & Bai, 2024). By decreasing the emulsion's size and modifying the gel's hydrophobic conditions, the composite gel's overall quality can be improved by influencing the distribution and condition of water within the gel, as confirmed by the TPA and rheological analysis results.

Microstructure of the composite gels

Direct observation of the gel microstructure provides the most straightforward approach to examining alterations in the gel network. Fig. 3 illustrates that the control group's gel network exhibited a relatively unstructured configuration. The CLSM image revealed sparsely dispersed large oil droplets, while the Cryo-SEM image showed a non-uniform network structure with large pores. The resulting structure, characterized by weak intermolecular forces between protein molecules and a loose network, typifies the degradation of MP gels upon direct incorporation of oil droplets. Emulsion incorporation notably altered the gels' microstructure (Lu et al., 2022). The introduction of SE resulted in a higher quantity and more even dispersion of oil droplets, attributed to SPI-encapsulated oil droplets acting as fillers within the MP network. These fillers created additional interfacial regions for MP molecule adsorption. The SCE group demonstrated higher density and homogeneity than the SE group, primarily due to increased SPI surface hydrophobicity from curcumin addition. This enhanced hydrophobicity facilitates stronger hydrophobic contacts between MP molecules and oil droplet surfaces, resulting in a more secure attachment of oil droplets within the MP gel network and a more compact arrangement of MP molecules around them. The SCNE group exhibited the highest level of density and homogeneity in its network structure. The CLSM image revealed a homogeneous distribution of numerous minuscule oil droplets throughout the gel network. This phenomenon results from the combined influence of the nanoscale emulsion's increased surface area and improved hydrophobic properties. Nanoscale oil droplets offer numerous interfacial areas, while heightened surface hydrophobicity enhances their affinity with MP molecules. During thermal gelation, MP molecules are more likely to adhere and reorganize at these widely spread and highly hydrophobic interfaces, forming a more organized and compact network structure. The mechanism of this microstructure evolution can be attributed to two factors: i) The increased interfacial area as emulsion size decreases, providing additional adsorption sites for MP molecules. ii) Enhanced surface hydrophobicity, facilitating MP molecule binding at oil droplet interfaces. These factors result in a more homogeneous and dense gel network connecting localized MP molecule structures around oil particles.

CD spectroscopy

The circular dichroism results indicated a substantial influence of emulsion addition on the secondary conformation of MP molecules. The α-helix content in the control group was 62.51 %, which corresponds to the results reported by Du, Yang, et al. (2023). Upon the addition of emulsion, all samples (SE, SCE, SNE, SCNE) exhibited a substantial reduction in α-helix content, with the highest decrease recorded at 19.48 %. On the other hand, there was an increase in the content of β-structures and random coils. The transition from SE to SNE revealed that nanoscale emulsions induced more significant conformation alterations than microscale emulsions. The α-helix content declined from 32.93 % in SE to 25.02 % in SNE, while the β-folding concentration increased to 14.47 %. The disparity may arise from the increased interfacial area offered by nanoscale emulsions, which provide more adsorption sites for MP molecules (Du, Hu, et al., 2022). At the same time, smaller oil droplets can spread more evenly in the MP network, causing the MP molecules to change shape across a wider range and making it easier for β-folded structures to develop. Additional analysis comparing SE and SCE, as well as SNE and SCNE, demonstrated that including curcumin (SCE, SCNE) resulted in a more significant augmentation of β-structure content and a greater reduction in α-helix content. The observed alteration can be ascribed to the effect of curcumin on increasing the hydrophobicity of SPI, hence facilitating hydrophobic interactions between MP molecules and oil droplets at the interface. The heightened hydrophobic contacts can trigger additional unfolding of MP molecules, revealing more hydrophobic regions, thus facilitating intermolecular hydrophobic connections and generating an increased number of β-folded structures (Dai et al., 2021). The conformational changes directly impact the creation and characteristics of the gel network, accounting for the previously noted alterations in microstructure and enhancements in macroscopic properties.

Molecular mechanisms of enhanced gelling properties of the composite gels

Improving the intermolecular forces of gels is a critical method for enhancing their quality. As shown in Table 2, adding emulsion increased intermolecular forces in all composite gels, with the most notable improvement observed in hydrophobic interactions. The maximum value surpassed the control by a factor of more than 2.6, a phenomenon also observed by Lv et al. (2024) in their investigation. This significant enhancement can be attributed to the introduction of emulsion, which induced the reorganization of MP molecular structure and increased the number of β-folded structures with hydrophobic groups, leading to stronger intermolecular hydrophobic interactions. As illustrated in Fig. 4B, adding the SCNE group further optimized the gel's microstructure and intermolecular interactions. The synergistic effect of nano-sized oil droplets' high specific surface area and the enhanced surface hydrophobicity due to curcumin provided more adsorption sites for MP molecules, promoting stronger hydrophobic interactions. Moreover, when evenly distributed as fillers, the nano-oil droplets not only increased the interfacial area but also optimized stress distribution throughout the network. This multi-scale structure optimization and interaction enhancement resulted in the SCNE group achieving a composite gel intermolecular hydrophobic force of 33.16 g/L, significantly higher than other treatment groups. Consequently, the SCNE group exhibited a more compact three-dimensional network structure than other experimental groups.

Oxidative stability

Oxidative stability is essential for assessing the quality of surimi gels, especially after the inclusion of oil. Fig. 5 shows notable variations in the oxidative stability of composite gels over 24 h of storage at 50 °C. This high-temperature short-term storage condition can be considered an accelerated aging test. Based on van't Hoff's rule, this condition is equivalent to approximately 6–10 days of storage at room temperature (25 °C). All emulsion-added samples demonstrated improved antioxidant capacity compared to the control. The SCNE group exhibited the best performance, with the lowest levels of oxidation markers: dityrosine content (478.67 a.u.), carbonyl content (7.16 nM/mg protein), PV (4.83 nmol/kg gel), and TBARS (13.21 nmol/kg gel). These values represent significant reductions of 71 %, 39 %, 76 %, and 72 % respectively, compared to the control. Other emulsion groups (SE, SCE, SNE) also showed improved oxidative stability, but to lesser extents. These indicators comprehensively reflect protein oxidation and lipid peroxidation processes, with SCNE consistently demonstrating the most significant protective effect.

(A) Carbonyl and dityrosine contents of MP and the composite gels. (B) Peroxide and TBARS values MP and the composite gels.

This exceptional antioxidant performance can be attributed to the synergistic effect of multiple mechanisms (Lv et al., 2024): i) The nanoscale emulsion creates a more homogeneous physical barrier and a larger interfacial area; ii) Curcumin possesses potent free radical scavenging ability and may form an antioxidant complex with protein (Chen et al., 2016); iii) The reduction in emulsion droplet size and increased hydrophobicity due to curcumin promote the formation of a denser gel network structure in the composite gel, thereby reducing the frequency of oxidation in the composite gel (Du, Xu, et al., 2022). These findings indicate that SCNE can serve as a highly effective additive to enhance the oxidative stability of MP-based gels.

Conclusion

This investigation illustrated that the gelation properties of composite gels comprising SPI-based emulsions and MP were substantially improved by decreasing the emulsion droplet's size and increasing the emulsifier surface's hydrophobicity. The enhancement was mainly attributed to strengthened hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonding. When SCNE was used as a filler, the hardness of the composite gels increased by 55 %, and intermolecular hydrophobic interactions were enhanced by about 2.6-fold compared to the control group. CD spectroscopic analysis and microstructural observation revealed that the α-helix content in the composite gels decreased by up to 19.48 % due to the larger specific surface area and higher surface hydrophobicity of SCNE, promoting the rearrangement of MP molecules and resulting in a denser and more uniform three-dimensional gel network structure. Moreover, the SCNE-constructed composite gels exhibited excellent antioxidant properties. After incubation at 50 °C for 12 h, these gels' carbonyl and peroxide levels were reduced to 31.82 % and 24.15 % of the control levels, respectively. In conclusion, the study provides an innovative approach to improve surimi gels containing essential fatty acids, addressing current production limitations and offering new perspectives for developing functional aquatic products.
