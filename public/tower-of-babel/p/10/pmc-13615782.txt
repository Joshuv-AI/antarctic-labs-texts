Assessing the performance of remotely sensed functional traits and spectral metrics for estimating multidimensional plant diversity in wetlands using UAV data
Authors: Long Ren, Zhaoju Zheng, Paolo Villa, Ping Zhao, Cong Xu, Ying Fu, Zhaofu Zhou, Jinchen Wu, Xinsheng Zhao, Wei Li, Hui Lu, Sufan Liu, Dan Zhao, Yuan Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant diversity underpins wetland ecosystem stability and functional sustainability, and its reliable assessment is vital for effective conservation and management. Remote sensing provides an efficient mean of plant diversity monitoring, however the potential of remotely sensed functional traits (RS-traits) and spectral metrics for plant diversity estimation in wetland ecosystems has not been fully investigated. In this study, we integrated UAV hyperspectral and LiDAR data to extract spectral band metrics, Rao’s quadratic entropy and principal components, texture features, and retrieve physiological and morphological RS-traits. We then used these features to predict multi-dimensional (species, functional, and phylogenetic) plant diversity using multiple stepwise regression (MSR), generalized additive models (GAMs), and random forest regression (RF). The results demonstrated that the retrieved RS-traits were generally consistent with field measurements (R2 = 0.36–0.78 for physiological, R2 = 0.47–0.87 for morphological traits). Among the different models, MSR performed best for species diversity, GAMs for functional diversity, and RF for phylogenetic diversity. The highest predictive performance was achieved for species diversity (R2adj = 0.62–0.73), followed by functional (R2adj = 0.41–0.83) and phylogenetic diversity (R2adj = 0.55–0.64). Models based on RS-traits consistently performed better than those based on spectral metrics, while combining spectral metrics and RS-traits did not lead to statistically significant improvements. While our results provide preliminary evidence towards a unified RS-trait framework for the multi-dimensional monitoring of wetland plant diversity, further work is needed to generalise these findings to other sites and wetland types.

Introduction

Wetlands, often called the “Kidneys of the Earth”, provide essential services such as water purification, flood control, climate regulation, and biodiversity support (Zedler and Kercher, 2005; Bridgham et al., 2006). However, due to human activities and climate change, wetlands are disappearing rapidly around the globe, making their protection and restoration an urgent priority (Tickner et al., 2020; Fluet-Chouinard et al., 2023). As primary producers, aquatic plants are a fundamental element of these ecosystems due to their morphological and physiological characteristics, as well as their high phenotypic plasticity (Villa et al., 2025). Species diversity provides a direct measure of community composition (Hill, 1973), but alone cannot fully capture ecosystem complexity; other facets of diversity are also important for understanding wetland biodiversity and its role in sustaining ecosystem processes and resilience, such as functional diversity, which reflects the diversity of traits among individuals within a community (Dí;az and Cabido, 2001; Petchey and Gaston, 2006), and phylogenetic diversity, which quantifies the evolutionary distance among species within a community (Faith, 1992). Accurate monitoring wetland plant diversity across dimensions is therefore essential for developing effective wetland conservation strategies. Yet the highly heterogeneous and dynamic nature of wetlands limits the efficiency of traditional field surveys, underscoring the need for accurate, timely, and spatially explicit monitoring approaches, e.g. based on remote sensing data and techniques (Sun et al., 2024).

In the context of advancing multi-dimensional assessments of plant diversity, plant functional traits, namely physiological and morphological characteristics associated with plant growth, survival, and reproduction, have be widely used to quantify plant diversity and to elucidate how environmental filters and biotic interactions shape community assembly and ecosystem functioning (Adler et al., 2013; Li et al., 2017; McGill et al., 2006; Violle et al., 2007). Similarities of functional traits can be reflected into phylogenetic relatedness (Winter et al., 2013; Zhao et al., 2021). A number of studies have been developed during the last decade for estimating remotely-sensed functional traits (RS-traits) from spectral data to characterize plant diversity across ecosystems (Hauser et al., 2021; Homolová et al., 2013; Ma et al., 2019; Schneider et al., 2017; Villa et al., 2021; Zheng et al., 2021). However, the high heterogeneity of optical, structural, biochemical and phenological properties of wetland plants, driven by diverse plant functional types, water–salinity gradients, microtopography, and seasonal inundation (Räsänen et al., 2020), poses unique challenges for retrieving RS-traits accurately.

Plant diversity monitoring using remote sensing has so far primarily relied on species classification or regression models using spectral metrics, and most applications have focused on terrestrial vegetation (e.g. Fassnacht et al., 2022; Frye et al., 2021; Pinto-Ledezma et al., 2025; Schweiger et al., 2018; Torresani et al., 2024; Wang and Gamon, 2019); more recently, few works have been carried out on aquatic and wetland plant communities, taking into account their specific spectral features (Taddeo et al., 2019, 2021; Tan et al., 2022, 2023; Villa et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2022). However, remote sensing applications in this field are highly context-dependent, and existing studies have largely focused on a single dimension of diversity — most commonly species diversity, less frequently functional diversity and — while comprehensive, multi-dimensional assessments remain rare.

Although satellite imagery can support wetland plant diversity assessment when detailed field surveys for large plots (e.g. mangrove) are available (Rocchini, 2007; Taddeo et al., 2019, 2021; Wang et al., 2022), satellite data with coarser resolution are usually less sensitive to spectral heterogeneity and spatial complexity of wetland plants at community level due to the mixed pixel issue. Many wetland plants tend to be relatively small and to exhibit complex canopy structures, so their monitoring often requires ultra-high resolution data that conventional satellite imagery cannot provide. Unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) can be deployed flexibly with various sensors and collect centimetre-level spatial resolution imaging data, thereby they hold great potential for advancing fine-scale plant diversity monitoring in wetland ecosystems. High-resolution UAV hyperspectral imagery can effectively reduce background effects and improve the estimation of physiological traits (Sun et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2022). For instance, Zhuo et al. (2022) mapped the chlorophyll content of mixed vegetation in a tidal-flat wetland using UAV hyperspectral imagery. However, optical signals cannot penetrate through foliage and are therefore inherently constrained to two-dimensional representations of the canopy surface. In contrast, UAV-mounted Light Detection and Ranging (LiDAR) systems can capture the three-dimensional structural metrics of the canopy, providing a powerful tool for extracting morphological traits, such as canopy height, canopy cover, and structural complexity (Fu et al., 2024a; Li et al., 2023; Song et al., 2025; Valbuena et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2023). Hyperspectral and LiDAR data, complemented by image-based texture features can provide information that characterizes canopy-level spectral and structural heterogeneity (Taddeo et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2025), which constitute promising input data for plant diversity estimation. Yet how the complementarity of hyperspectral and LiDAR data can enhance trait retrieval and support multidimensional plant diversity estimation in wetlands remains underexplored.

Models linking spectral metrics and RS-traits with plant diversity can be broadly categorized into linear, nonlinear, and machine learning approaches. Linear models can offer interpretability and variable-selection advantages through procedures such as multiple stepwise regression (Zhao et al., 2025a). Nonlinear approaches better accommodate the complex spectral–diversity relationships characteristic of wetland plant communities, although most applications have focused on species diversity (Sun et al., 2024). Recent research has shown that machine learning models, such as random forest (RF), can improve the estimation accuracy of functional diversity in aquatic plants, outperforming nonlinear models like generalized additive models (GAMs) (Villa et al., 2025). Nevertheless, the optimal modelling approaches applicable to multi-dimensional plant diversity monitoring in wetlands remain unclear.

To address these gaps, we established a case study investigating the potential of using UAV data to retrieve plant functional traits and predict species, functional, and phylogenetic diversity in wetland plant communities. The objectives of this study are threefold: (1) to assess the capability of UAV hyperspectral imagery and LiDAR data for retrieving canopy-scale functional traits of wetland plants, (2) to evaluate the performance of different modelling approaches for monitoring species, functional, and phylogenetic diversity, and (3) to identify key spectral metrics and RS-traits that consistently serve as key predictors across different dimensions of diversity.

Materials and methods

Study area

Our study was conducted at the Yeyahu Wetland Nature Reserve (40°24.73′ N, 115°50.91′ E), located upstream of the Guanting Reservoir in northwestern Beijing, China (Fig. 1). The region has a continental monsoon climate, with a mean annual temperature of approximately 9 °C and mean annual precipitation exceeding 400 mm (Wang et al., 2015). As the largest and most biodiverse wetland reserve in Beijing, Yeyahu hosts extensive aquatic vegetation, featuring different community types. The dominant species vary along a water-affinity gradient, ranging from emergent/floating plants (EF; e.g., Nymphaea alba, Nelumbo nucifera, Nymphaea mexicana), to helophytes (H; e.g., Phragmites australis, Typha angustifolia, Bolboschoenus planiculmis), and grasses (G; e.g., Hemarthria sibirica, Artemisia scoparia, Plantago major) (see Table S1).Fig. 1Geographic location of the study area and field plots. Field measurements

We selected representative 40 wetland plant plots (4 m × 4 m) across the study area from 3 to 5 August 2024, covering EF, H, and G community types with 9, 26, and 5 plots, respectively. We measured the four corner coordinates of each plot using a Real-Time Kinematic (RTK) GNSS receiver. We took downward hemispherical photographs using a Nikon camera with a fish-eye lens approximately 1 m above the canopy (upward in tall Phragmites australis dominated plots), which were subsequently processed in CAN-EYE v6.495 software (Weiss et al., 2004) to estimate the leaf area index (LAI). We also recorded the species composition and coverage within each plot and measured the maximum canopy height. In addition, we collected healthy mature leaves from dominant species (coverage ≥ 5%) within the plots, and substitute leaf samples from rare species (coverage < 5%) near the plots, to measure leaf spectra and physiological traits. For each species sample within a plot, we generally collected 3 leaves with total fresh mass larger than 3 g, while few species sample only had 1 or 2 leaves depending on species presence. The collected leaves were immediately placed in sealed bags and stored in insulated containers with ice to prevent dehydration and wilting. Leaf spectra were measured using an ASD FieldSpec 4 spectroradiometer (Analytical Spectral Devices, Inc., Boulder, CO, USA) in the laboratory on the same evening as field sampling. For each species sample, spectra from three leaves (each measured three times at different non-vein positions) were averaged to minimize spectral noise. Then, we measured the leaf dry matter content (LDMC), specific leaf area (SLA), leaf mass per area (LMA), equivalent water thickness (EWT), chlorophyll-a (Chla), chlorophyll-b (Chlb), and carotenoids (Car), following the protocol described in Zhao et al. (2016) and eventually calculated the total chlorophyll (Chlab) and the chlorophyll a/b ratio (Chla/Chlb). A summary of the number of leaf samples and spectral measurements for each species is provided in Table S2.

Plant diversity indices

To characterize wetland plant diversity across species, functional, and phylogenetic dimensions, we calculated nine commonly used diversity indices for the sampled plant communities using field-measured species composition, abundance (coverage), and trait variation.

Species diversity

We quantified species diversity using three indices: (1) species richness (SR), (2) Shannon–Wiener index (SW; Shannon, 1948), H=−∑piln(pi), and (3) Simpson’s index (Simpson; Simpson, 1949), D=1−∑pi2, where pi is the coverage fraction of species i in the plot. All indices were calculated in Python v3.12.7 using the NumPy package (v.1.26.4).

Functional diversity

We calculated functional diversity from leaf-level trait measurements using the Trait Probability Density (TPD) framework (Carmona et al., 2016, 2019), which incorporates inter- and intraspecific variation and integrates traits across scales. We selected three low-collinearity traits (i.e. Chla, Chla/Chlb, EWT) that were representative of photosynthesis, photoadaptive regulation and water-use strategy. Species-level TPDs were then generated using trait means and variances of each species based on all leaf samples of that species across plots. These were aggregated into community-level TPDc by weighting species TPDs by their relative abundance within each plot. Functional diversity was then quantified using three complementary indices: functional richness (FRic), reflecting the volume of TPDc trait space; functional divergence (FDiv), capturing the divergence of trait values around the centroid; and functional evenness (FEve), describing the evenness of distribution within that space. Functional volumes and indices were calculated using TPD v.1.1.0 package in R (Carmona et al., 2019).

Phylogenetic diversity

We standardized species names using the R package U.Taxonstand (Zhang et al., 2025) and constructed a regional phylogeny (Fig. S1) with Phylomatic-awk v.1.1.0 (Webb et al., 2008) based on the megatree of Zanne et al. (2014). The phylogeny and community abundance matrices were then imported into Phylocom v.4.2 (Webb et al., 2008) to compute plot-level phylogenetic diversity. Phylogenetic diversity was quantified using three indices: Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (PD), representing total branch length; the net relatedness index (NRI), evaluating deviations in mean pairwise phylogenetic distance from a random expectation; and the nearest taxon index (NTI), measuring the mean distance to each species' closest relative within the community.

UAV hyperspectral and LiDAR data

UAV hyperspectral imagery was acquired in late August 2024 under cloudless conditions using a Cubert X20P snapshot hyperspectral imaging spectrometer (Cubert GmbH, Ulm, Germany) mounted on a DJI Matrice 300 RTK multirotor UAV (DJI, Shenzhen, China). The X20P sensor acquires 164 spectral bands from 350 to 1000 nm. Flights were conducted at 110 m above ground level, yielding panchromatic imagery with a ground sampling distance of approximately 3.25 cm and hyperspectral imagery with native spatial resolution of approximately 17 cm. Preprocessing included radiometric calibration, mosaicking using Cuber Utils Touch (v.2.9.1) as well as geometric registration and pan-sharpening based on ENVI 5.3 software. The pan-sharpening hyperspectral imagery had a spatial resolution of approximately 3.25 cm. Non-target pixels (e.g., non-photosynthetic vegetation, water, and shadows) were masked using empirically thresholds: NDVI > 0.6, NDWI < 0, WAVI > 0.25, and reflectance at 860 nm > 0.06 (McFeeters, 1996; Rouse et al., 1974; Villa et al., 2014). For each plot, the proportion of valid vegetation pixels was calculated. Plots with less than 70% valid vegetation pixels were excluded from subsequent modeling. Following the criteria, plot A34 was removed from further analysis due to an insufficient proportion of valid pixels.

UAV LiDAR data were collected on the same day of the hyperspectral imagery acquisition using the LiAir X3C–H system (Green Valley Inc., Beijing, China). The instrument operates at a wavelength of 905 nm and records up to three returns. Flown at approximately 90 m above ground level, it yielded an average point density exceeding 1400 points/m2 and an elevation accuracy of about 5 cm. After noise filtering and classification into ground and non-ground points, a 0.1-m digital elevation model (DEM) and digital surface model (DSM) were generated from ground returns and first returns, respectively, using kriging interpolation. For plots dominated by emergent/floating vegetation types, additional manual adjustments were applied to the DEM to impose uniform elevation values over water-covered areas. The canopy height model (CHM) was obtained by subtracting the DEM from the DSM (Zhao et al., 2013). Subsequently, the LiDAR-derived CHM was used as the spatial reference to co-register the UAV hyperspectral imagery based on spline transformation with 178 control points, achieving overall registration accuracy of 0.08 cm.

Multidimensional plant diversity estimation framework

An overview of the study’s workflow is presented in Fig. 2. Firstly, we extracted a suite of spectral metrics, texture features and retrieved RS-traits (physiological and morphological) by integrating field measurements, UAV hyperspectral imagery and LiDAR data. Specifically, spectral metrics were computed from band statistics, quadratic entropy and principal components (PCs); physiological RS-traits were retrieved from hyperspectral imagery via partial least squares regression (PLSR) and linear regression based on normalized difference spectral indices (NDSI); morphological RS-traits were derived from normalized LiDAR point cloud data; and image texture features – as additional RS-traits - were computed from the gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM). Next, based on these variables, we estimated species diversity, functional diversity, and phylogenetic diversity utilizing multiple stepwise regression (MSR), generalized additive models (GAMs), and random forest regression (RF). Finally, we conducted a multi-dimensional comparison to identify effective approaches for UAV remote sensing–based plant diversity monitoring in wetlands encompassing variables, models and diversity dimensions using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, paired t-test, and Pearson’s correlation analysis, respectively.Fig. 2Flowchart of the methodological framework for this study.Fig. 2

Spectral metrics extraction

Spectral band metrics (SBMs) were used to characterize the spectral variability of plant communities, including the convex hull area, the mean coefficient of variation of all bands (CV_mean), the mean and standard deviation of NDVI (NDVI_m and NDVI_sd), the mean reflectance of individual bands (band_mean), and the coefficients of variation of individual bands (band_cv). All calculations were limited to the spectral range of 450–950 nm to minimize edge effects and sensor noise. To reduce redundancy among hyperspectral bands, principal component analysis (Jolliffe and Cadima, 2016) was applied separately to band_mean and band_cv variables, and the spectral metrics with the highest loadings in the top five principal components (PC1–PC5) of each group were retained as the most contributive variables (Fig. S2). In total, 14 SBMs variables were incorporated as inputs for plant diversity modeling.

Spectral Rao’s quadratic entropy (SRaoQ; Rocchini et al., 2017), an indicator of local spectral heterogeneity, was calculated for each target plant community based on three synthetic features, namely the first spectral principal component (SPC1), NDVI and WAVI (Villa et al., 2014). For each synthetic feature, SRaoQ was first computed using a moving 9 × 9-pixel window, and then the mean (m), standard deviation (sd), and coefficient of variation (cv) of the resulting SRaoQ values, together with the SRaoQ scores computed over all pixels within each community, were extracted, yielding a total of 12 SRaoQ variables for plant diversity modeling.

The first five spectral principal components (Rocchini et al., 2011), which explained over 98% of the variability of spectral response signals, were calculated from the hyperspectral imagery. The mean (m), standard deviation (sd), and distance from the multidimensional centroid (SPCdist; Möckel et al., 2016) were then extracted from the SPCs images of the target plant communities, resulting in 11 SPCs variables used as input variables for plant diversity modeling.

Plant traits retrieval from hyperspectral and LiDAR data

Physiological RS-traits, reflecting the biochemical and biophysical properties of plants, were retrieved from hyperspectral imagery. First, the feasibility of retrieving nine leaf-scale physiological traits was evaluated using PLSR (Haaland and Thomas, 1988) based on 80 leaf samples and 10-fold cross-validation. These traits included five biochemical traits related to photosynthetic capacity and photochemical efficiency (Chla, Chlb, Chlab, Car, Chla/Chlb) and four biophysical traits related to leaf structure and resource allocation strategies (LMA, EWT, LDMC, SLA). Then, leaf traits were scaled-up to the canopy level through community-weighted means (CWMs; Garnier et al., 2006; Zheng et al., 2023). Both PLSR and normalized difference spectral indices (NDSI; Thenkabail et al., 2000; Villa et al., 2021) were applied to UAV hyperspectral data to retrieve canopy-scale traits. For the NDSI, all possible two-band combinations were tested, and significance was assessed based on p-values adjusted using the Bonferroni correction (padj) to control for multiple comparisons, with padj < 0.05 considered significant (Villa et al., 2021). Canopy-scale models’ performance was evaluated via leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) of 40 plots based on the coefficient of determination (R2), root mean square error (RMSE), and normalized RMSE (nRMSE), and the best retrieval model for each physiological RS-trait was selected accordingly. Finally, the mean (m) and standard deviation (sd) were extracted from physiological RS-traits of target plant communities. A total of 18 physiological RS-traits were collected as input variables for plant diversity modeling.

Morphological RS-traits were derived from LiDAR data, as descriptors of vertical height, horizontal openness and internal complexity of canopy structure - influencing light availability, plant growth, ecosystem productivity and habitat conditions (Valbuena et al., 2020). LiDAR point clouds were first normalized by subtracting the DEM at each point location and were subsequently used for deriving 19 height-related (central tendency, variability, and percentiles) and 11 density-related (DM0–DM9 and canopy cover) parameters (Table S3) using LiDAR360 v8.0 software (Green Valley Inc., Beijing, China). The reliability of the LiDAR-derived morphological parameters was evaluated by correlating the plot-level maximum canopy height from the normalized LiDAR point clouds with field measurements. In addition, the mean and standard deviation of LAI were retrieved from hyperspectral data using PLSR, with performance evaluated via LOOCV. A total of 32 morphological RS-traits were collected as input variables for plant diversity modeling.

Additional RS-traits comprised image texture features extracted from WAVI, CHM, and SPC1 using five metrics (dissimilarity, entropy, sum average, sum variance, and correlation), representing three texture categories of contrast, orderliness and descriptive statistics, based on the GLCM to reflect spectral-spatial canopy heterogeneity (Hall-Beyer, 2017), totaling 15 texture-based RS-trait variables.

Regression and machine-learning based estimation of plant diversity

Starting from an extensive pool of 102 variables preselected as outlined in Section 2.6–37 spectral metrics – and Section 2.7–18 physiological RS-traits, 32 morphological RS-traits, 15 texture features (see Table S3), we removed highly correlated variables within and across feature sets (∣r∣ > 0.8). Using the remaining variables as initial inputs and nine diversity indices (for species, functional and phylogenetic dimensions) as response variables, we tested the performance of three models, i.e. MSR, GAM, and RF, to estimate wetland plant diversity from cm-resolution UAV data. Given that plot A34 was excluded due to insufficient valid pixels, a total of 39 plots were retained for modelling. All models were evaluated using LOOCV, with adjusted coefficient of determination (R2adj), RMSE, and nRMSE used as performance metrics.

Multiple stepwise regression

Linear multiple stepwise regression (MSR; Efroymson, 1960) models, which are able to address multicollinearity among variables and reduce model overfitting, were applied to identify the optimal subsets for different diversity indices within each set by bidirectional (F-test p: ≤ 0.05 for entry, ≥ 0.10 for removal) stepwise regression, implemented in IBM SPSS Statistics 2025 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Since the software fits models directly using the selected subsets without internal cross-validation, model performance was subsequently assessed in Python.

Generalized additive models

Nonlinear generalized additive models (GAMs; Hastie and Tibshirani, 1986) were developed using predictors preselected from generalized linear models (GLM; Nelder and Wedderburn, 1972) that showed moderate effects (p < 0.1). In the GAM framework, all predictors were modeled using the same penalized smoothing function (thin plate regression spline), and the non-linear predictors were selected based on the model significance (p < 0.05). Separate GAMs were first calibrated using spectral metrics and RS-traits. Then, all significant variables identified in these models were combined to calibrate an integrated GAM. The GAMs were tested in R using the mgcv package (Wood, 2020).

Random forest

To explore the performance of machine learning models, we employed the widely used random forest regression (RF; Breiman, 2001). The model hyperparameter ntree was set to 2000, and mtry (the number of variables randomly sampled at each split) was tested using sqrt(n)/2, sqrt(n), and sqrt(n)∗1.5, where n is the number of predictors, with the optimal mtry selected via LOOCV. Subsequently, the model was tested using all input variables within each set (spectral or RS-traits) to obtain variable importance scores, and the top five most important variables were selected to recalibrate a parsimonious RF model. The RF models and variable importance analyses were performed in R using the randomForest package (Liaw and Wiener, 2002) and the caret (Kuhn, 2008) package.

Model evaluation across input features and diversity dimensions

We first evaluated the predictive performance of the different models for each diversity index and identified the optimal models for subsequent analyses. To further assess the contribution of RS-traits, we applied a paired sample t-test (Montgomery and Runger, 2010) to compare the optimal models based solely on RS-traits with those based on combined spectral metrics and RS-traits, thereby testing whether RS-traits provide unique contributions to multidimensional diversity estimation. We then used the Kruskal–Wallis H test (Kruskal and Wallis, 1952) to examine whether diversity indices and their corresponding optimal variable inputs differed significantly across community types (EF, H, G). Finally, we ran Pearson’s correlation analysis of different diversity dimensions, together with their optimal variable inputs, to identify potential common patterns in multidimensional diversity estimation.

Results

Plant diversity at the plot scale

Multi-dimensional plant diversity indices from a total of 39 target plots (Table S4) were used for analysis. Significant pairwise correlations were observed among species diversity indices (r > 0.7, p ≤ 0.001), with the Simpson index showing particularly strong correlation with the SW index (r = 0.97). The phylogenetic diversity indices NRI and NTI were likewise highly correlated (r = 0.91, p ≤ 0.001). In contrast, only FDiv and FRic exhibited a moderate negative correlation within functional diversity (r = −0.60, p ≤ 0.001). Inter-dimensional correlations were mainly observed between species diversity indices, FRic, and phylogenetic diversity indices (r ≥ 0.45, p ≤ 0.01) (Fig. S3). Remotely sensed functional traits

For physiological RS-traits, models based on leaf spectra exhibited moderate to good performance (R2 = 0.28–0.83; nRMSE = 7.8%–19.2%) (Fig. S5), setting the basis for calibrating the canopy-level models. At the canopy scale, PLSR achieved better performance for water/dry matter content traits and Chla/Chlb (Fig. S6), while NDSI performed better for pigments, which were strongly associated with green-wavelength combinations (Fig. S8), and structural traits (Fig. S7). However, t-tests comparing physiological RS-traits retrieved from PLSR and NDSI models did not highlight significant differences between the two approaches (p > 0.77; Fig. S9). Given the heterogeneity of target plant communities typical of wetland vegetation, the overall retrieval accuracies of best performing models between those based on PLSR and NDSI for each trait at canopy scale were considered satisfactory (R2 = 0.36–0.78; nRMSE = 11.5%–18.7%) (Fig. 5a–i). Multi-dimensional plant diversity estimation

A total of 23 spectral metrics and 37 RS-traits (|r| ≤ 0.8) were retained as input variables (Table S6) for estimating species, functional, and phylogenetic diversity with MSR, GAM, and RF.

Discussion

The potential and limitations of RS-trait retrieval

We evaluated canopy-level physiological and morphological trait retrieval against field measurements and found overall acceptable agreement. Pigments were retrieved most accurately, which is consistent with their strong absorption features (Blackburn, 2006; Ustin et al., 2009; Wu et al., 2008), whereas the Chla/Chlb ratio was more challenging to estimate spectrally due to pronounced spectral overlap. The retrieval accuracy of biophysical traits varied: EWT and LDMC were modelled reliably, while LMA and SLA models were less accurate; this can be attributed to the aerenchymatous, low-density leaf structures characteristic of aquatic plants (Jung et al., 2008; Villa et al., 2024). Retrieval performance differed among community types, probably due to the unbalanced sampling representation in our dataset. The H community, which was the dominant and most extensively sampled type (26/39 plots), showed the best accuracy across types, except for LMA and SLA. Several factors may influence the retrievability of physiological traits from spectral response at canopy scale in wetland vegetation, e.g. loss of within-species variation when using CWMs (Zhang et al., 2022), spectral mixing caused by water-surface scattering, and reflectance variability associated with structural heterogeneity, including anisotropy (Asner and Martin, 2008; Piaser et al., 2025). Reliable, physically based radiative transfer models are needed to quantitatively assess and potentially compensate for these biases, but this topic has been understudied for aquatic vegetation so far (Zhou et al., 2015).

Among morphological traits, wetland canopy height was reliably derived from LiDAR data, despite relatively high errors for shorter vegetation (Homolová et al., 2013). In contrast, LAI retrieval remained quite challenging, primarily due to strong variations in leaf angle distribution, foliage overlap, and canopy architecture across and within community types (especially in helophytes). Nevertheless, we should acknowledge a non-negligible level of uncertainty in the reference LAI data, due to the difficulty of standardizing hemispherical images processing for such heterogeneous vegetation.

In our study, linear regression based on optimized NDSI generally outperformed PLSR for physiological trait retrieval. Both methods showed limited accuracy for LMA and SLA, underscoring the need for improved strategies for retrieval of biophysical traits - such as hybrid machine learning–physical models (Danner et al., 2021), optimal upscaling approaches (Shan et al., 2025), and again radiative transfer models specifically tailored to aquatic vegetation (Zhou et al., 2015).

Although our study was conducted at the UAV scale, we calibrated the RS–trait models using average canopy spectra from 4 m sampling plots and identified the sensitive bands for trait retrieval. So it could be extended to larger areas with high-resolution satellite observations, such as 2 m resolution WorldView imagery. However, the transferability of these models to open-access satellite multispectral data (e.g., 10 m Sentinel-2 imagery) still needs to be tested, especially considering the complex canopy structure and heterogeneous backgrounds in wetlands (Gallant, 2015; Kingsford et al., 2016). One practical way could be aggregating the plot measurements to larger areas to align with the pixel size of satellite data, which has recently been applied in estimating functional traits for grasslands (Zhao et al., 2025b). Another potential way could be using existing very high resolution commercial satellite as a bridge between ultra-local UAV data and mid-resolution satellite data.

Estimation of multi-dimensional plant diversity

Our study calculated nine diversity indices across three dimensions and observed strong correlations among most of them. This is expected because species and phylogenetic diversity indices jointly reflect species identities and relative abundances in the community, and do not account for intraspecific variability. In wetland ecosystems, strong environmental filtering can drive evolutionary convergence, with unpredictable consequences in terms of coupling/decoupling of different diversity dimensions (E-Vojtkó et al., 2023). By contrast, FDiv and FEve, the metrics less correlated with the others in our data, do emphasize within-community trait distribution and are less influenced by the number of species present (Villéger et al., 2008). The variability in model accuracies among plant community types appear to be associated with the degree of separability among these types, suggesting that community-level differences exert a strong influence on diversity estimation performance, possibly due to variability in hydrology, light conditions, and species composition. However, a definitive conclusion cannot be reached here. This is because the number of plots within each community type was uneven and quite limited for the EF and G communities. Also, the dataset was collected under a specific set of environmental conditions, which may not be representative of other wetland systems.

Among the three diversity dimensions, species diversity showed the strongest associations with remotely sensed variables. In particular, SW, which is sensitive to the rare species commonly found in wetland communities, achieved good modelling accuracy based on RS-traits (nRMSE = 15.5%). Phylogenetic diversity also showed meaningful associations with RS-traits, albeit weaker than those scored for species diversity metrics; however, the absence of traits more directly linked to evolutionary history in our dataset, such as root traits and reproductive strategies (Cadotte et al., 2009), implies that phylogenetic differentiation cannot be fully captured, which hampers comparison. While models for functional diversity performed as well or better than those for other dimensions when purely spectral features were used as input, optimal models based on RS-traits eventually exhibited weak to moderate associations, particularly for FRic and FDiv; in contrast, models for FEve consistently achieved the best accuracy across all dimensions and input variable sets (nRMSE = 11.4%). This is inconsistent with the findings of Villa et al. (2025), who based their study on data from similar plant communities sampled in wetlands across Italy; however, differences in sample size, community type balance and basic hyperspectral data, as well as the spectral features tested, mean that a direct comparison of our results with theirs is very difficult to establish. Robust conclusions on this topic require more data, collected from different sites and wetland plant communities/species, and a set of spectral features that are as standardized as possible.

The performance of different model types tested varied across both diversity dimensions and community types. These differences likely reflect the intrinsic characteristics of each dimension. Species diversity tends to respond more in a linear way, which MSR can efficiently portray this (Meynard and Quinn, 2007; Romero et al., 2016), while functional diversity involves nonlinear interactions and trait redundancy, which is possibly captured by GAMs structure (Guisan et al., 2002; Kosicki, 2020); phylogenetic diversity, on the other hand, is a product of hierarchical evolutionary patterns that can be easily incorporated into ensemble methods such as RF (Pichler and Hartig, 2023). There was a degree of variation in the performance of diversity models among community types (Fig. 7 and Table S7), suggesting that hydrology-driven differences in community composition may modulate the relationship between plant diversity and features derived from cm-resolution remote sensing data. However, similarly to the previous points, reaching conclusion on this topic would require more data to be collected that is evenly distributed across different community types and sites.

Key predictors for wetland plant diversity monitoring

Unlike a few recent studies on this topic (Fu et al., 2024b; Taddeo et al., 2021; Tan et al., 2023; Villa et al., 2025), our findings did not reveal a strong association between purely spectral metrics and wetland plant diversity. This discrepancy may result from the similarity in diversity levels within certain community types and the strong correlations between spectral metrics and RS-traits (Table S8) in our dataset; such situation can be due to the specificity of our study site and/or the peculiar characteristics of our remote sensing data sources. For example, the spectral data is a product of pansharpening hyperspectral imagery, which still has several shortcomings (Ciotola et al., 2024). The common assumption that spectral diversity represents canopy-level physiological and structural variability linked to community composition (Palmer et al., 2002; Sun et al., 2024), has indeed been contrasted in empirical and theoretical works (see Fassnacht et al., 2022, and references therein). In our case, possible functional redundancy between spectral metrics and RS-traits may explain this pattern. Nevertheless, spectral metrics directly derived from remote sensing optical data remain a cost-effective tool for assessing plant diversity (Rocchini et al., 2010), especially when reference trait data for target species and sites is unavailable.

Overall, model input variables categorized as RS-traits showed stronger impacts on predictive ability. Physiological RS-traits were especially effective, ending up as major contributors to optimal models for all target diversity metrics due to their direct responsiveness to environmental gradients in wetlands. In particular, the Chla/Chlb ratio, which reflects plant acclimation to light availability (Lichtenthaler, 1987), effectively distinguished the shade-adapted characteristics of G communities. Meanwhile Chla, EWT, and LDMC respectively captured differences in photosynthetic efficiency (Chou et al., 2020), water regulation (Wright et al., 2004), and resource-use strategies (Akram et al., 2023) across community types. Structural traits such as LMA and SLA also reflect trade-offs in leaf construction costs and resource acquisition (Wright et al., 2004), but their relatively low retrieval accuracy in our data affected their reliability as predictors.

Morphological RS-traits were associated with plant diversity as well, though less directly than physiological RS-traits. Among these, LAI was the most influential, as it integrates canopy structure and photosynthetic capacity (Li et al., 2025). For LiDAR-derived metrics, canopy cover contributed little due to near saturation in most communities, while point density metrics in lower part of the canopy (DM1–DM3) served as useful proxies for biomass and structural heterogeneity (Cao et al., 2019). On the other hand, texture-based traits did not play a significant role in predicting diversity dimensions, and their contribution may be complementary at most to other spectral and spectro-functional variables.

However, since the retrieval of physiological RS-traits and computation of functional diversity metrics estimation relied on the same field-measured traits in our case, the performance of some diversity models based on RS-traits can be affected by a degree of circularity and mutual dependency. This constitutes an inherent limitation of our results, and future work based on independent data collected from heterogeneous sites covering different wetland vegetation types and groups will be essential for validating the robustness of trait–diversity relationships and identifying more stable predictors, not only of functional, but also of species and phylogenetic diversity. The integrated use of plant trait databases such as TRY (Kattge et al., 2011), alongside continuous remote sensing–derived trait maps (Cimoli et al., 2024; Ma et al., 2019), could provide a feasible approach to developing generalizable RS-trait retrieval models across different scales, decoupling applications of remote sensing for estimating functional diversity from the need for local, site-specific data collection.

Conclusions

This study presented an in-depth case study demonstrating the effective integration of UAV hyperspectral imagery and LiDAR data for retrieving key plant functional traits and estimating species, functional, and phylogenetic diversity in wetland ecosystems. RS-traits, especially those linked to physiological plant characteristics, outperformed purely spectral metrics as input variables for diversity modelling, providing more precise and ecologically meaningful estimates of plant diversity. For the first time, our results identified a subset of traits that could be used to predict multiple diversity dimensions jointly, offering a basis for developing a unified trait-based framework for monitoring wetland biodiversity via remote sensing. This may facilitate deriving an integrated diversity indicator for conservation applications. To achieve greater applicability of this approach, future research should broaden sampling across seasons and regions, incorporate traits more directly linked to evolutionary history, and evaluate model transferability across spatial scales to test generalization. Meanwhile, spatially continuous mapping of multidimensional plant diversity for wetland communities over large areas with UAV data or very-high-resolution satellite data represents a promising future direction. Taking these steps will enable the rapid, effective, large-scale monitoring of wetland plant diversity, contributing to improve conservation and ecosystem management.
