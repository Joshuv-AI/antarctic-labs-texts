Prevalence of orthorexia nervosa and association with mental health among Palestinian physical education students - cross sectional study
Authors: Fatima Masoud Al-Amouri, Rania Abualhaija, Mahmoud Al Atrash, Reem Abu Alwafa, Mohammad AlTamimi, Manal Badrasawi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEating disorders such as orthorexia nervosa (OrNe) are expected to be prevalent among athletes due to the pressure of high physical performance. Consequently, this will have an impact on their mental health. Therefore, this study aimed at evaluating the prevalence of OrNe among student athletes and its relationship with mental health.MethodsA sample of 272 physical education students was randomly selected to participate in the study. The Arabic Teruel Orthorexia Scale (TOS) was used for OrNe assessment. Mental status was measured using the Arabic version of the 12-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12).ResultsThe responses of 272 individuals aged 20.93 ± 3.26 were included in the final analysis. Males represented 45.6%; most of the participants were healthy, non-smokers, not on a diet and having normal weight. Males’ mean OrNe scores were higher and significantly different (p = .013) from females’ at 9.34 ± 5.21 and 7.86 ± 5.96, respectively. The healthy orthorexia (HeOr) scores for all individuals in this study were 10.81 ± 5.45. While the mean GHQ-12 scores for all participants were 11.55 ± 5.96, with no significant differences between males and females. Moreover, sociodemographic factors have no significant association with OrNe except for sport type, number, and years of playing sports (p < .01). On the other hand, dietary behaviors and BMI scores were significantly associated with OrNe scores (p < .05).ConclusionA relatively low prevalence of OrNe was found among the physical education students, with males representing a significantly higher prevalence of OrNe, while mental distress showed no significant link with OrNe. Also, BMI and dietary behaviors were significantly related to OrNe. The findings of this study are essential for informing prevention strategies and targeted interventions within universities and sports communities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01343-8.Keywords: Orthorexia nervosa, Sport, Mental health, BMI, Sociodemographic

Introduction

Eating disorders (EDs) are disabling, dangerous, and costly mental illnesses that affect physical health and psychosocial functioning. They impact the quality of life of affected individuals, their caregivers, and society in the present and future [35]. Mental health issues such as depression, anxiety, and obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) are common in people with EDs [19]. EDs affect a notable portion of the general population. Athletes have a higher risk due to the ideal body image that is believed to enhance performance, along with pressure from coaches, parents, and peers [28]. Prevalence among athletes is estimated at 13.5%, with a higher rate among female athletes (20.1%) than male athletes (7.7%) [19].

However, EDs may negatively affect physical fitness and sport performance due to low energy availability, dehydration, disturbance in electrolytes [19], and extreme loss of body fat and lean mass [13]. In order to maximize athletic performance, athletes have a lot of control over their diets, which increases the risk of having orthorexia nervosa (ON) [16]. ON has been defined as “obsessive focus on dietary practices believed to promote optimum well-being through healthy eating (with inflexible dietary rules, recurrent and persistent preoccupations related to food, compulsive behaviors), with “consequent, clinically significant, impairment (e.g., medical or psychological complications, great distress, and/or impairment in important areas of functioning” [7]. ON is characterized by extreme obsession with food sources, manufacturing methods, and labeling. People with ON exclude what they consider “unhealthy” food, which often leading to social and physical consequences [20]. It is important to distinguish between ON and healthy orthorexia (HO). While both involve healthy eating interest, HO represents non-pathological interests, unlike ON, which is a rigid way of eating and represents a pathological preoccupation with a healthy diet [4].

Several studies link ON risk to sports engagement [22]. The prevalence of ON was 28% among Italian athletes [31], 37% among UK university athletes [16], and 74.5% among Lebanese university students [15]. Predictors of ON included age, previous dieting [31], BMI and body composition [33], and high exercise volume [9]. Moreover, mental health conditions such as OCD, perfectionism, and having autistic characteristics have also been associated with ON [18].

Despite these findings, identifying risk factors of EDs in vulnerable populations is necessary for designing effective intervention and prevention efforts [18]. However, data on ON in Arab athletes or university athletes remain limited. For instance, orthorexic tendency has been assessed among nutrition students and nutritionists in Jordan [1], and across various population groups in Lebanon, including adolescents [25], female university students [3], and male university students [23]. In Palestine, however, this is the first study of its kind to examine the prevalence and determinants of ON and to address a notable gap regarding ON in both Arab and local populations, particularly among university athletes, making it a unique contribution to both regional and international literature in disordered eating behavior. Accordingly, this study aims to investigate the prevalence of ON among physical education college students in Palestine, assess its impact on their mental health status, and explore related risk factors including sociodemographic, lifestyle, medical history, and diet.

Method

Study design

This cross-sectional study was done in February 2022 through face-to-face interviews using a structured paper-based questionnaire by trained researchers. Wording and response options of each questionnaire were standardized to preserve integrity and validity. Researchers filled out a structured questionnaire for randomly selected students from the physical education department at An-Najah National University, the largest university in Palestine. The physical education department is located at the university campus in Nablus city. A total of 313 students were invited to participate in the study; the response rate was 86.9%, and students who agreed to join the study signed a consent form. The sampling was done using simple random sampling. Based on prior population-based research [36, 37], the collected data include sociodemographic information, medical history, lifestyle, ON, and mental status. Palestinian physical education students from An-Najah National University were included in the study. Participants who self-reported using psychiatric medication or experiencing psychological problems were excluded from the study. In addition, participants who were not registered for the summer semester were excluded to ensure the sample reflected currently engaged students in their academic and athletic activities. A remark at the outset of the questionnaire made it clear that participation was entirely voluntary. Participants were those who voluntarily agreed to participate in the study.

Sample size

The sample size was determined using a single proportion for a finite population. Sample size calculations using G Power software, for prevalence study, the prevalence of ON assumed 30% from previous study conducted among University students [12].

Assuming 5% margin error, with a significance level of 0.05 (two-sided) and a power of 80%, indicated that a minimum of 203 participants was needed to determine a prevalence of ON. To determine the association between ON and mental health, the sample size was recalculated assuming the proportion of ON is 300% and the proportion for prevalence of mental health issue (40%) assuming 5% level of significance, and 80% power, giving a sample size of 280 participants [29].

Collected data and study instruments

The questionnaire of this study included information on students’ sociodemographic, sport profession, medical history, lifestyle, and diet. Additionally, the questionnaire included the Teruel Orthorexia Scale (TOS) [4] and General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) [11] (see supplementary material).

Referring to previous literature focusing on mental health [8, 38], the questionnaire included questions on sociodemographic characteristics (age, gender, area of living, family income, academic grading, academic year, sport type, sport, sport number, sport experience, commitment to play sport), medical history (chronic diseases, medications, surgery), and lifestyle characteristics (smoking, type of smoking, weight, height, diet, diet time, reason for diet, nutritional information source, nutritional supplements, ergogenic, sleeping hours).

Orthorexia Nervosa measurement was done by the Arabic-validated Teruel Orthorexia Scale (TOS) [24]. TOS is a 17-item tool used for distinguishing the two orthorexia dimensions from each other (healthy orthorexia (HeOr) and orthorexia nervosa (OrNe)). TOS originally had 31 items, however it is latest revised version had 17 items [24]. 9 out of these 17 items are for assessing HeOr, and the other 8 items measure OrNe. Items’ answers have a zero to three 4-point scale where zero indicates “strongly disagree” and three indicates “strongly agree.” Summing up answers scores generates the HeOr and OrNe dimension scores [4]. The HeOr score is calculated by the sum of the first 9 items in the TOS questionnaire, and the OrNe score is calculated by the sum of the last 8 items [24]. Higher scores indicate higher HeOr and OrNe, respectively [4].

The mental health status of students was evaluated using the Arabic-validated 12-item General Health Questionnaire (GHQ-12) [10]. GHQ-12 is a self-rated tool that has been created in order to detect people with diagnosable psychiatric disorders in consulting settings. Originally, GHQ contained 60 items, but it has been reduced to 30, 28, and 12-item versions. GHQ-12 is the most popular screening tool for common mental disorders. Furthermore, it is a broader measurement for mental wellness [11]. Each of the GHQ-12 twelve items evaluates the severity of a psychological problem during the previous past weeks by a zero to three 4-point scale. The total score of the GHQ-12 ranges from 0 to 36, where the higher the score, the worse the condition is [17]. A score higher than 15 suggested mental stress. Additionally, GHQ-12 scores produce 3 mental health measures: social dysfunction (items 1, 3, 4, 7, 8, and 12), anxiety and depression (items 2, 5, 6, and 9), and loss of confidence (items 10 and 11) [14].

Validity and reliability

Previous research has confirmed and proven the validity of the Arabic versions of the TOS and GHQ-12 questionnaires, demonstrating that they are appropriate for Arab samples [10, 24]. In the current sample, internal consistency was acceptable to good, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.803 for HeOr, 0.829 for OrNe, and 0.824 for GHQ-12.

Data analysis

Data was coded and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 23. Frequency and descriptive analyses were used to explore continuous and categorical variables. The normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Median and interquartile range (IQR) scores are reported to reflect central tendency and dispersion, given the non-normal distribution of OrNe scores. The Mann-Whitney U test and the Kruskal-Wallis test were used to evaluate the relationship between the dependent variable (OrNe score) and the categorical independent variables, including sociodemographic characteristics, medical history and lifestyle, and mental health status. The Spearman’s correlation test was applied to evaluate the relationship between the OrNe score and the continuous independent variables (BMI and GHQ-12 scores). The level of significance was set at p-value < 0.05.

Discussion

The OrNe and its association with sociodemographic, lifestyle, and mental health were successfully assessed in a sample of 272 Palestinian physical education students (mean age = 20.93 ± 3.26). The mean OrNe score for the entire sample was 8.53 ± 5.52 (range: 0–24), with 9.34 ± 5.21 and 7.86 ± 5.96 for males and females, respectively. In addition, male students had significantly higher mean OrNe scores than female students (p < .05). According to previous findings, it’s unclear whether OrNe is more common in women or men [30]. In a study conducted in the United Kingdom, there is no significant variation in OrNe level between male and female university athletes [16]. Similarly, a study of exercise and sport sciences students at an Italian university found no significant differences in OrNe characteristics between males and females [6]. Also, according to a Polish study, there was no significant variation in OrNe levels between male and female elite athletes [33]. However, research conducted in the United States stated that OrNe was more common among female athletes than male athletes [34]. It is possible that the difference is related to the OrNe measurement technique since limited studies have used TOS to assess the prevalence of OrNe [26]. It’s worth noting that the TOS distinguishes between pathological (OrNe) and non-pathological (HeOr) healthy eating behaviors [4], which help to assess OrNe tendencies more precisely and allow more in-depth comprehension of sex differences regarding OrNe tendency. Furthermore, differences in OrNe prevalence between men and women could be influenced by the gender distribution of those who took part in the investigations [22]. Nevertheless, the high prevalence of OrNe among male participants in our sample might be attributed to factors like perfectionism and muscle dysmorphic disorder (MDD). This is supported by a previous study among male university students, which found that higher levels of perfectionism and MDD were significantly associated with higher OrNe [23]. In addition, the observed association may be related to the diet-related control behavior among male students, such as efforts to increase muscle mass, higher protein intake, or supplement use.

OrNe & mental health

In the current study, mental health showed a mean GHQ-12 score of 11.55 ± 5.96, with female athletes presenting a higher mean score than male athletes. However, the GHQ-12 score showed no significant association with OrNe, and neither did the GHQ-12 domains. Our findings contradict previous studies, which reported that OrNe was shown to be associated with poorer mental health [32] and that psychological issues like depression and anxiety were positively correlated with OrNe [2]. Differences in sample size and characteristics could explain the inconsistency between our findings and prior literature. In addition, the GHQ-12 is a general screening tool for psychological distress [14], not tailored specifically to detect mental health conditions associated with OrNe, such as perfectionism and obsessive behaviors. This limitation may help explain the lack of association observed in our results.

OrNe & BMI

BMI categories were significantly associated with OrNe only among the female subgroup in this study (p < .05). As their BMI category increased, the OrNe score increased. For instance, obese female students had a significantly greater OrNe (median = 12) than underweight female students (median = 3). Furthermore, our findings demonstrated that in females, BMI was substantially linked with OrNe scores (p < .05). Previous findings regarding the relationship between BMI and OrNe have been mixed. For example, a study among Italian endurance athletes showed that BMI was not associated with OrNe symptomatology [5], while a Polish study reported that BMI was linked to OrNe levels in male athletes but not in females. Other studies found no link between OrNe and BMI [33]. however, it remains questionable whether a higher BMI increases the risk of OrNe, as current research contradicts this theory [22]. In contrast, Oberle et al. found that obese and overweight people are more likely to develop orthorexic tendencies, as they try to lose weight and improve their physical health by following a diet [27]. These discrepancies and mixed findings might be attributed to several factors, including sample differences such as age and sex variation and athletes vs. the general population. Also, cultural differences and social pressure among studied populations regarding body image and health, and methodological variations in OrNe and BMI assessment and categorization, might contribute to these discrepancies.

OrNe & diet and diet time

A significant relationship between dieting and OrNe score was observed among the whole sample (p < .001) and female and male subgroups (p < .05). Those who have been on a diet had a significantly higher OrNe score (median = 10) than those who have not (median = 7). Similarly, higher OrNe scores (median = 9) were found among females who had been on a diet than those who did not (median = 7). As well as males who have been on a diet (median = 11) than those who did not (median = 8). Furthermore, being currently on a diet was associated with OrNe in all study participants (p < .05) and female students (p < .05). the results indicate that those on a diet currently have higher OrNe scores (median = 12) compared to those who are not currently dieting (median = 9). Likewise, female students who are currently on a diet had higher OrNe scores (median = 13) compared to those who previously dieted (median = 8). Previous studies stated that previous dieting has been associated with OrNe among athletes [5, 31, 34]. The relationship between having a diet experience with OrNe risk and tendencies is consistent in the literature [22]. One possible explanation for this relationship is that weight concerns may lead to abnormal behaviors and obsessive practices in order to maintain a strict food regimen [31]. Interestingly, a study from Croatia suggested that professional athletes are more concerned about their diet and therefore more adherent to the Mediterranean diet due to its well-documented health benefits, which was linked to the tendency of OrNe [21]. The Mediterranean diet may contribute to the development of OrNe by serving as a healthy, clean, and restrictive eating behavior, which aligns with orthorexic attitudes among athletes.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design limits the causality of the findings. Second, the self-reported instruments, which may result in recall bias or over/under reporting. Third, mental health assessment limitations and the lack of diagnostic evaluation for eating disorders before starting data collection. Fourth, the limited use of TOS in previous literature makes it difficult to directly compare results. However, the strengths of this study include the use of a nuanced and validated tool (TOS), exploring multiple associated factors, and focusing on an understudied population.

Conclusions

This study addresses a notable gap regarding OrNe in Arab populations, particularly among university students. It highlights the differences in OrNe tendencies between male and female athletes and their link to numerous factors. The findings of this study revealed that male athletes are at higher risk of OrNe compared to female athletes, while dietary-related behaviors were significantly related to OrNe among both sexes. In addition, BMI was significantly linked to OrNe among female athletes only. Although mental health, which is measured by the GHQ-12, did not show a significant link with OrNe tendencies, such an association might be linked to its limited ability to detect mental health conditions related to OrNe. However, the use of TOS to assess OrNe provided insights into literature that distinguishes between healthy and unhealthy orthorexia. The findings of this study point out the importance of early detection of OrNe and highlight the need to evaluate physical, dietary, and behavioral factors related to orthorexic tendency among young athletes. In addition, understanding OrNe in this population is essential for informing prevention strategies and targeted interventions within universities and sports communities.
