Racial and ethnic health inequities caused by extreme U.S. weather events: A nationally representative study
Authors: Carl A. Latkin, Kelsie Parker, Melissa L. Walls, Lauren Dayton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•All groups had greater odds of health issues from extreme weather than non-Hispanic Whites.•Indigenous respondents had the greatest health inequities from extreme weather events.•It is important to incorporate health equity in federal policies for climate change.

Introduction

Extreme weather events are increasing in frequency and magnitude due to climate change and the lack of effective mitigation strategies. Moreover, exposure and health impacts of these events are not equally experienced; specific racial and ethnic groups and those in particular geographies of the United States are most impacted by these events and their harmful health consequences [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Extreme weather events such as heat waves, tornadoes, flooding, and wildfires can impact every dimension of health, leading to increased respiratory, cardiovascular, and infectious diseases as well as injuries [5]. Extreme weather events also pose a significant threat to mental health, leading to both short and long-term effects such as anxiety, post-traumatic stress, and depression [6]. This study assesses the health consequences of extreme weather events by racial and ethnic group and geographic location using a national sample of U.S. residents.

Extreme weather event vulnerability is impacted by three primary factors: exposure, sensitivity, and ability to adapt [7]. A population’s likelihood of exposure to any extreme weather event is influenced by environmental characteristics such as geospatial location and social or political characteristics such as the built environment and land use [8,9]. For instance, Black and Hispanic/Latino populations consistently have greater exposure to extreme heat/heat waves at national, regional, and metropolitan levels [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. This disparate exposure to extreme heat has been linked to the effects of systematic policies that propagate neighborhood disinvestment, such as redlining [7,14]. Furthermore, racial and ethnic populations in the U.S. often cluster in specific geographic areas, which can influence their exposure to climate-related risks. For example, White households along the eastern and western coasts of the United States are more vulnerable to sea level rise and coastal flooding, while Black, Asian, Hispanic/Latino, and Indigenous populations are more vulnerable to the extreme weather events along the Gulf Coast [15].

These communities are at greater risk due to structural barriers to resources and responses that may mitigate the deleterious effects of extreme weather [8,9]. Pre-existing medical conditions impact sensitivity or the degree to which one is negatively affected by the adverse consequences of extreme weather events [16]. Resiliency, or the ability to adapt to extreme weather events, is often directly related to material and social resources [17,18]. Historically marginalized communities often have access to fewer individual and community-level resources to help them escape or reduce their exposure to the harmful effects of these events. Poverty, low levels of social capital, and the burden of chronic health conditions may also impede communities’ ability to prepare for extreme weather events as well as to respond to them [19,20]. For instance, following Hurricane Sandy, Ma and colleagues identified that lower socioeconomic status and stronger perceived impacts from the hurricane significantly contributed to the disproportionate mental health outcomes experienced by Black and Hispanic survivors compared with White survivors [21].

While there is existing evidence of racial and ethnic disparities in exposure to extreme weather events and vulnerability to their associated health outcomes, the majority of studies have focused on Black and Hispanic populations and geographic areas that have experienced frequent or particularly devastating extreme weather events [22]. Few studies have examined the impact of extreme weather events on other marginalized groups, such as Asian or Indigenous populations, or investigated health impacts at a national or regional level. Further, some extreme weather events have been studied more than others. Few studies have focused on racial and ethnic disparities related to tornadoes, flooding, drought, and severe cold weather/storms. Studies examining the health impact of extreme weather on health disparities and inequities often use data such as emergency department visits, hospital records, and death certificates. However, it can be challenging to attribute morbidity and mortality to extreme weather events as there may be time lags and multiple causes. There have been few national studies on the health impacts of extreme weather events.

In this nationally representative study, which includes an assessment of exposure to extreme weather events and their health impacts, we assessed the health and well-being impacts of these exposures. Next, we examined the influence of geographic region on disparities. Finally, we examined whether individual-level sociodemographic indicators and geographic region altered any disparities in the health impacts of extreme weather events.

Methods

Participants and procedures

The study data for these secondary analyses came from the NPR/Robert Wood Johnson Foundation/Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health: Climate Change Survey, which was a sample of 2646 representative U.S. adults conducted from March 31 to May 8, 2022 [23]. Respondents were recruited through mailed invitation letters and completed the survey online or via phone [23]. Participants were sampled through a stratified random sampling method to obtain a nationally representative sample of U.S. adults by race/ethnicity and geographic region. To meet representation goals for race/ethnicity and region, the survey team sampled a small number of respondents with two auxiliary methods: an advanced cellular frame to reach more Asian American and Indigenous respondents and a probability-based web panel to reach certain racial/ethnic groups in specific disaster areas. The sample was weighted to match the national distribution based on demographic and geographic factors [23].

Measures

Exposure to extreme weather events. To assess exposure to extreme weather events, respondents were asked: “Have you been personally affected by any of the following extreme weather events in the last five years?” The events included: (1) Tornadoes, (2) Hurricanes or severe tropical storms, (3) Extremely hot weather or heat waves, (4) Wildfires, (5) Major flooding, (6) Major droughts or water shortages, (7) Rising sea levels or coastal flooding, and (8) Severe cold weather or winter storms. Response options were “Yes,” “No,” “Refused,” or “Don’t know.” Respondents could check multiple options for exposure to extreme weather events.

Adverse health outcomes from extreme weather events. Participants who reported experiencing one or more extreme weather events were asked about adverse physical and mental health outcomes after experiencing extreme weather: “After experiencing any extreme weather events in the last five years, did you or anyone else in your household experience any of the following problems as a result?” The problems included (1) Serious health problems due to lack of air conditioning, (2) Serious health problems due to smoke or lack of clean air, (3) Serious health problems due to lack of heating, (4) Serious health problems due to poor water quality or lack of water, and (5) Serious mental health problems. Response options were “Yes,” “No,” “Refused,” or “Don’t know.” Respondents could check multiple options for adverse health events from extreme weather events.

Sociodemographic characteristics. Demographic factors included race, Hispanic ethnicity, geographic location, gender, education level, age, and income. Race/ethnicity was assessed with the questions: (1) Are you, yourself, of Latino or Hispanic origin or descent, such as Mexican, Puerto Rican, Cuban, or some other Latin American background?” (Response options: “Yes,” “No,” “Don’t know,” and “Refused/blank”) and (2) “What is your race?” Response options for the latter included: “White, African American or Black, Asian, American Indian or Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, Some other race, Don’t know, and Refused to answer.” These two ethnicity and race variables were combined to create race/ethnicity categories: Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, Asian, American Indian or Alaska Native, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, and Other. There were no Native Hawaiians or other Pacific Islanders in the sample, and we combined the American Indian or Alaska Native into an Indigenous category. Geographic regions reflected the U.S. Census Bureau’s Northeast, North Central, South, and West regions. Income categories were: (1) Less than $30,000, (2) $30,000–$100,000, and (3) Over $100,000.

Data analysis

The sample was weighted to provide nationally representative estimates of the U.S. adult population aged 18 and older [23]. We compared affirmative responses to the other response options for reports of extreme weather events and health outcomes. Three individuals reported “don’t know” for the health impact questions, and <3 % reported “don’t know” or refused to answer questions about extreme weather events. Models with imputed responses did not meaningfully change the magnitude of associations. Respondents who did not report any extreme weather events and hence were not queried about the health impact of the events, we coded as ‘no’ to the questions on serious health impacts of the extreme weather events.

After we excluded 77 individuals who responded “don’t know,” “refused,” or response missing, or “other” for the race question, the study sample consisted of 2569 respondents. We recalculated sample weights after excluding these individuals. To assess associations between race/ethnicity and serious health problems related to the extreme weather events, we used weighted bivariate and multivariable logistic regression models to calculate odds ratios and identify significant differences in self-reported serious health problems between non-Hispanic White individuals and Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, Asian, and Indigenous. We ran multiple multivariate logistical regression models using region, age, gender, education level, and income as covariates. We used three model types for each extreme weather event-related cause of serious health problems. To assess the independent association between extreme weather events and racial/ethnic group, Model A was a bivariate logistic regression model. To account for the impact of geographic location on the relationship between extreme weather events and racial/ethnic group, Model B was a multivariate logistic regression model that adjusted for region. Model C was a multivariate logistic regression model that adjusted for region and socioeconomic characteristics (age, gender, education level, and income).

Among the respondents, approximately 26 % reported that someone in their household experienced serious physical or mental health problems caused by an environmental consequence of a recent extreme weather event (e.g., lack of adequate air conditioning, poor water quality), and 7.1 % reported more than one adverse consequence. Household serious health problems resulting from a lack of adequate air conditioning were reported by 5.6 %. Other serious health problems were those resulting from poor water quality or a lack of water (3.9 %), lack of adequate heating (2.7 %), smoke or a lack of clean air (3.1 %), and any serious problems related to mental health were reported by 9.6 %.

Race/ethnicity remained significantly associated across all five extreme weather-related causes of serious health problems, even when adjusting for region and sociodemographic characteristics. Compared with non-Hispanic Whites, each marginalized racial or ethnic group studied had significantly higher odds of reporting having a serious health problem resulting from lack of adequate air conditioning, lack of adequate heating at home, and poor water quality or lack of water.

Reporting that a member of the household had a serious health problem resulting from a lack of adequate air conditioning was heightened among Hispanic, non-Hispanic Black, Asian, and Indigenous individuals even after controlling for geographic region and sociodemographic characteristics. Hispanic, and non-Hispanic Black, Asian, and Indigenous individuals had adjusted odds of, respectively, 2.79 (95 % CI: 1.81 – 4.29), 1.84 (95 % CI: 1.06 – 3.20), 2.26 (95 % CI: 1.06 – 4.79), and 3.53 (95 % CI: 1.74 – 7.14), compared to non-Hispanic White individuals.

Regarding serious health problems from smoke or lack of clean air, Hispanic, Asian, and Indigenous individuals had significantly higher odds of reporting serious health problems compared with non-Hispanic White individuals across all models. Controlling for all covariates, Hispanic, Asian, and Indigenous individuals had the adjusted odds of, respectively, 2.47 (95 % CI: 1.38 – 4.41), 2.61 (95 % CI: 1.18 – 5.77), and 3.33 (95 % CI: 1.39 – 7.97) of reporting serious health problems resulting from smoke or a lack of clean air.

In the multivariable logistic regression model of reported serious health problems among household members resulting from a lack of adequate heating in their home, Asian individuals had the highest regional- and sociodemographic-adjusted odds (6.39, 95 % CI: 2.26 – 18.11) compared with non-Hispanic White individuals, followed by Indigenous (4.72, 95 % CI: 1.68 – 13.19), Hispanic (4.12, 95 % CI: 2.14 – 7.93), and non-Hispanic Black individuals (3.03, 95 % CI: 1.49 – 6.15).

Indigenous individuals’ odds were 12.33 (95 % CI: 6.11 – 24.89) greater to report serious health problems among household members resulting from poor water quality or a lack of water compared to non-Hispanic Whites in the fully adjusted model. Asian, Hispanic, and non-Hispanic Black individuals’ odds were, respectively, 3.95 (95 % CI: 1.56 – 10.01), 3.61 (95 % CI: 2.05 – 6.34), and 3.46 (95 % CI: 1.85 – 6.45) for reporting these serious health problems compared to non-Hispanic Whites in the fully adjusted model.

The analysis that examined the outcome of reporting any of the four reported causes of health issues revealed a similar pattern. In the fully adjusted model, Indigenous individuals’ odds were 4.95 (95 % CI: 2.86 – 8.55) greater in reporting any of the four health conditions among household members compared to non-Hispanic Whites. In the fully adjusted model, Asian, Hispanic, and non-Hispanic Black individuals’ odds were, respectively, 2.44 (95 % CI: 1.36 – 4.36), 2.85 (95 % CI: 2.04 – 3.99), and 2.12 (95 % CI: 1.40 – 3.21).

Only Indigenous individuals had significantly higher odds (2.65, 95 % CI: 1.48 – 4.75) of experiencing any serious problems related to mental health compared with non-Hispanic Whites when adjusting for regional and sociodemographic characteristics. Notably, non-Hispanic Black individuals had significantly higher odds of experiencing any serious problems related to mental health in the bivariate analysis and adjusted analysis, including region only; however, this association was attenuated after adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics.

Discussion

The study findings are consistent with existing evidence that marginalized racial and ethnic groups face health inequities related to extreme weather events. By employing a nationally representative sample, this study shows that findings on climate-exposure disparities can be generalized to a national level—underscoring an urgent need for consideration to integrate health equity considerations into federal policies on emergency management, disaster preparedness, and strategies for climate change adaptation and resilience-building. Study findings also suggest that health-related climate change inequities are not simply due to geographic residential location, as most of the associations remained statistically significant even after adjusting for geographic region. However, the study results should be tempered by the inability to link specific extreme weather events to specific health outcomes. However, for some extreme weather events, such as flooding, there may have been health-related events, such as traffic accidents, that were not attributed to the extreme weather events. Moreover, extreme weather events may also cause serious health problems or injuries long after the initial event.

This study both supports existing evidence on related racial and ethnic health inequities in serious health problems due to extreme weather events and introduces new evidence of additional inequities. This study highlights severe health problems caused by inadequate air conditioning during extreme heat events, aligning with existing national evidence of heat-related health disparities between non-Hispanic Black individuals and non-Hispanic White individuals [24,25]. The findings identify potential extreme heat-related health inequities among Hispanic, Indigenous, and Asian populations. These groups may face disparate health outcomes related to extreme heat for several reasons, including elevated exposure to extreme heat events [12], distinct sensitivity to their harmful effects due challenges of heat refuge accessibility [26,27], structural racism, and other systemic policies and practices related to the built environment of low-income communities across the country [28,29].

Results of this study also provide evidence of racial and ethnic health inequities related to wildfires at a national level, indicating that Indigenous, Asian, and Hispanic individuals were significantly more likely than non-Hispanic Whites to report serious health problems from smoke or lack of clean air following recent exposure to wildfires. This study builds on prior evidence from studies in California that identified health disparities related to wildfires among Asian and Native American populations [30,31]. Systemic and structural factors, such as living in a redlined area, low socioeconomic status, higher rates of outdoor work, poor-quality housing, limited ability to evacuate, and a greater risk of cardiovascular and respiratory heat events, have been identified as perpetuating these inequities in exposure and vulnerability to wildfire and related health outcomes [7,14,32]. We found that the Black population differed from the other three minority groups in the lack of a significant difference compared to White participants in the frequency of reported serious health issues caused by smoke or lack of clean air. One possible explanation for this finding is that Black individuals are more likely to live in neighborhoods with higher levels of air pollution and hence did not attribute extreme weather events to health outcomes [7].

Regarding inequities from a lack of adequate residential heating following severe cold weather, this study identifies that when compared individually with non-Hispanic Whites, each racial and ethnic group studied was more likely to report experiencing serious health problems. This finding is significant as severe cold weather/winter is among the most frequent and deadly extreme weather events in recent U.S. history [2,3]. Yet, studies examining racial and ethnic disparities are sparse. Further research is needed to rigorously identify and describe disparate health outcomes related to severe cold weather, winter storms, and extreme rain events. Further, qualitative investigations may help describe structural or systemic drivers of racial and ethnic inequities in cold weather-attributed morbidity.

This study also suggests racial and ethnic health inequities related to poor water quality or lack of water from extreme weather events. When compared individually with non-Hispanic Whites, every marginalized group studied was significantly more likely to report experiencing a serious health problem resulting from poor water quality or lack of water. Some of this may be attributable to racial and ethnic inequities in clean water access [[33], [34], [35]] that may be exacerbated by extreme weather events such as tropical storms or hurricanes. However, more research is needed to identify specific health disparities resulting from poor water quality and limited water access and conceptualize determinants of these inequalities.

Indigenous individuals were the only marginalized racial/ethnic group found to be at significantly higher odds compared to non-Hispanic Whites of experiencing serious mental health problems following a recent extreme weather event, after adjusting for region and sociodemographic characteristics in this study. Other studies have previously identified a disproportionate burden on mental health among Black and Hispanic communities compared to Non-Hispanic White communities following specific extreme weather events, including communities impacted by Hurricane Katrina [36], Hurricane Sandy [21], and Winter Storm Uri in Texas [37]. While this was not the case in this study examining mental health inequities at a national level, it certainly highlights the regional impacts of climate change. Moreover, within regions, marginalized and minoritized communities and individuals tend to be more vulnerable to both extreme weather events and their health impacts due to fewer resources, segregation, less access to health care, and lower health status [38]. These modern social determinants of health are rooted in historical policies and practices of dispossession, forced migration, and segregation that carry forward today.

While not all extreme weather events can be directly attributed to climate change, climate change has increased the frequency and severity of such events. For instance, a study of the extreme heat wave in the Northwest in 2021 concluded that such an event would be approximately 150 times less likely without climate change [39]. Regardless of the direct influence of climate change on any specific extreme weather event, marginalized groups will be disproportionately affected by the resulting health impacts. Even if we cannot fully or partially attribute current extreme weather events to climate change, the broader trend suggests that such events will become increasingly severe and consequential for health unless we spend the resources and enact policies to address climate change and racial and ethnic inequities.

One of the strengths of this study is the inclusion of Native American populations, and the results highlight the significantly higher rates of extreme weather-related health problems in this population. In the multivariable model, we adjusted for education and income, yet the health disparities still held. It is possible that education and income do not fully account for the resources necessary to buffer extreme weather events. It is also possible that even if individuals have personal resources, they live in communities that are more vulnerable to extreme weather events. A third possibility is prior health disparities among Indigenous Peoples, which make them more vulnerable to extreme weather events.

Indigenous Peoples in the U.S. are culturally and geographically diverse, representing nearly 600 Federally recognized Tribes with unique cultures and histories and heterogeneous exposures to a range of extreme weather events. Moreover, traditional knowledge and practices of promoting pollinators, fire management practices, agricultural biodiversity, and seed banking may assist in adapting to some amount of climate change. Furthermore, Indigenous participants in this study experienced the highest odds of adverse health consequences related to weather events relative to other groups. Fundamental causes of modern health inequities for American Indians and Alaska Natives are linked to the collective and cumulative experiences and impacts of colonization on Indigenous health [40]. This includes disconnection from traditional cultural practices known to be protective of health and relocation of Indigenous Peoples from homelands to reservations and urban areas [41]. Indigenous Peoples globally are stewards of a small fraction of planetary lands yet protect an overwhelming majority of biodiversity on earth; a cruel irony in our findings is that the Indigenous knowledge systems critical for addressing climate change are most at risk and affected by severe weather events [41].

There are limitations in this study. Using self-reported exposure to extreme weather events introduces potential recall and misclassification bias, as prior studies have shown variation in how people classify extreme weather events. Although we adjusted for some of these biases in the multivariable models, it is likely that we did not account for others. Also, there is no evidence that bias in reporting extreme weather events is associated with race/ethnicity. Self-reported health conditions may not accurately reflect clinical diagnoses. However, healthcare data are unlikely to document health conditions due to extreme weather events that do not result in the use of health services. There are also study limitations due to weighting; these include the assumptions of correct weights. Some cases might have large weights, and the standard error may be inflated, which can reduce statistical significance.

The findings of this study indicate that consideration of racially and ethnically marginalized communities in climate change adaptation and extreme weather planning and policymaking should occur at national, regional, and local levels. Further, these plans should include strategies to address underlying economic and health challenges contributing to the disparate exposure to and the effects of extreme weather events. These strategies could be informed by much-needed further research on structural and systemic factors perpetuating these inequities. Qualitative and mixed-methods research, in particular, could help provide a greater understanding of the lived experiences of such systemic inequalities and their role in preventing successful adaptation and recovery from extreme weather events. Future health research on climate change must do more than document health inequities. Action research should focus on enacting evidence-based equitable solutions as well as methods to amplify and sustain collective action to address climate change.

Conclusion

In this national sample, we found strong and consistent racial and ethnic disparities in serious health problems related to climate change-related extreme weather events due to inadequate air conditioning, poor water quality or a lack of water, lack of adequate heating, and smoke or a lack of clean air as well as serious problems related to mental health. Both climate change mitigation and adaptation policies need to address these disparities.
