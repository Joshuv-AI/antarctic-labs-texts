Efficacy and safety of methylene blue for postoperative pain of haemorrhoids: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yu-Jia Zhu, Yu-Ting Shi, Sai-Ya Shi, Li Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPain is a common complication after haemorrhoid surgery. In addition to anaesthetic methods, surgical techniques and postoperative interventions, surgeons also use intraoperative adjuncts to alleviate postoperative pain. This study aims to evaluate the effectiveness and safety of methylene blue in reducing postoperative pain in haemorrhoid patients.MethodsA search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library and Web of Science for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) up until 9 December 2024, involving methylene blue use for pain relief after haemorrhoid surgery. Two reviewers independently assessed the study eligibility, extracted data and evaluated the risk of bias. Data analysis was performed using Stata 18 software. The primary outcome was the visual analogue scale (VAS) pain scores at different time points within 14 days postoperatively. Secondary outcomes included the number of patients requiring analgesics and the occurrence of complications.Results358 articles were retrieved and six studies involving 598 patients were included after screening. Compared with the control group, the use of methylene blue reduced pain within 12 h postoperatively(WMD −1.582, 95% CI −2.834 to −0.330, p = .013), as well as on day 2 (WMD −1.441, 95% CI − 1.874 to −1.008, p < .0001), day 3 (WMD −0.975, 95% CI −1.309 to −0.641, p < .0001), day 5(WMD −0.786, 95% CI −1.094 to −0.478, p = .028) and day 7 (WMD −0.679, 95% CI −1.257 to −0.101, p = .021), decreased the number of patients using analgesics(RR 0.293, 95% CI 0.203 to 0.422, p < .0001) and did not increase the incidence of complications(RR 0.65, 95% CI 0.39–1.10, p = .111) such as urinary retention, secondary haemorrhage, pruritus, temporary incontinence, wound infection, or local skin reactions.ConclusionsThis study shows that methylene blue can reduce postoperative pain, decrease analgesic use and does not increase complications after haemorrhoid surgery.

Introduction

Haemorrhoidal disease is one of the most common diseases in the United States, leading to over 1.9 million office visits or emergency department visits each year [1]. The main symptoms include bleeding, swelling, prolapse, pain, pruritus and anal discomfort, all of which severely impair quality of life. Some patients have their symptoms effectively alleviated following conservative treatments, such as dietary modifications, the use of phlebotonics and laxatives, or office treatments like banding, sclerotherapy and infrared coagulation (IRC) [2]. However, a portion of patients still depend on surgical procedures for treatment. Pain is one of the most common postoperative complications. In addition to anaesthetic methods, surgical techniques and postoperative interventions, surgeons also use intraoperative adjuncts to alleviate postoperative pain, such as intrasphincteric injection of botulinum toxin, intradermal injection of methylene blue and intrasphincteric injection of ketorolac [3].

Methylene blue is a type of phenothiazine salt, commonly used in colorectal surgery to treat intractable idiopathic pruritus ani [4], trace the path of fistulas to locate the internal opening, or mark the excision area during surgery for pilonidal cysts. Tan et al. [5] were the first to find that methylene blue significantly reduces pain following lateral anal sphincterotomy. Methylene blue primarily alleviates pain through three mechanisms: anti-inflammation, reduction of sodium currents and denervation [6]. A recent evidence-based review has concluded that methylene blue can effectively control pain within the first three days after haemorrhoid surgery [7]. Recently, randomized controlled trials regarding the use of methylene blue for relieving postoperative pain following haemorrhoid surgery have increased, but no relevant meta-analysis has been published so far.

The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to investigate the effects of methylene blue in alleviating postoperative pain following haemorrhoid surgery and its associated complications.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [8]. This study protocol was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (CRD42023494362).

Information sources and search strategy

Two authors (Zhu YJ and Shi YT) conducted a comprehensive search of English-language databases, including PubMed, Cochrane Library, Embase and Web of Science, covering the period from database inception to 9 December 2024. We also searched Google Scholar to identify eligible studies. The search strategy included the terms: ‘Methylene Blue’ AND (‘Hemorrhoids’ OR ‘Hemorrhoidectomy’ OR ‘Postoperative Period’ OR ‘Postoperative Pain’). The search strategy for PubMed, for example, is provided in Supplementary File S1. Additional eligible studies were identified by reviewing the reference lists of fully assessed articles.

Eligibility criteria

Trials were eligible if they compared the pain relief of methylene blue to other treatments after haemorrhoid surgery, were randomized controlled trials and included visual analogue scale (VAS) pain scores at various time points. Studies involving perianal abscesses and anal fistulas were excluded.

Selection process

After removing duplicate articles, two independent researchers (Zhu YJ, Shi YT) screened the titles and abstracts to exclude studies that did not meet the criteria. They then obtained the full texts for further screening. Any disagreements between the two authors regarding study selection were resolved by consulting a third author (Shi SY), with a senior author (Xu L) providing the final decision when required.

Data collection process and outcomes

Two independent researchers (Zhu YJ, Shi YT) extracted data from the included trials using a standardized data collection form, including the first author, year, journal, country of publication, patient characteristics, type of anaesthesia, type of surgery, postoperative management, intervention and control characteristics, data about the VAS pain scores, number of patients requiring analgesics, postoperative complications (urinary retention, secondary haemorrhage, pruritus, temporary incontinence, wound infection, local skin reaction), length of hospital stay, pain requiring readmission and excessive pain requiring unscheduled visit. When a randomized controlled trial included more than two groups, we selected the methylene blue group and the corresponding control group. If the mean or standard deviation (SD) of results was not reported in the text, we estimated these values based on sample size, median, interquartile range (IQR) and minimum and maximum values [9]. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion.

The primary outcome is the VAS pain scores at different postoperative time points, and the secondary outcomes include the number of patients requiring analgesics and postoperative complications (urinary retention, secondary haemorrhage, pruritus, temporary incontinence, wound infection and local skin reactions).

Study risk of bias assessment and quality assessment

Two researchers (Zhu YJ and Shi YT) independently assessed the risk of bias with version 2 of the Cochrane Risk-of-Bias tool. They also graded the overall quality of evidence based on the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) approach [10].

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using Stata software. Mean difference (MD) or risk ratio (RR), with their respective 95% confidence intervals (CI), were used to assess study outcomes, considering results statistically significant if p < .05. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the I2 statistic. Specifically, a p value > .10 and I2 < 50% indicated low heterogeneity and good homogeneity among studies, while a p value ≤ .10 and I2 ≥ 50% suggested substantial heterogeneity. Regarding model selection, a fixed-effects model was used when I2 ≤ 50% and a random-effects model was applied when I2 > 50%. Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding each study and recalculating the pooled mean difference estimates iteratively to assess the robustness of the results. Subgroup analyses were performed, guided by particular clinical questions, to identify potential sources of heterogeneity. Notably, as the number of included studies was fewer than 10, funnel plots could not be used to evaluate possible publication bias.

Results

Literature search

A literature search in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library and Web of Science identified 358 relevant articles (Figure 1). A total of 199 duplicate articles were excluded. An additional 153 articles unrelated to the study topic were excluded after screening titles and abstracts. We conducted a full-text review of the remaining 6 articles, 1 article was a preprint that had been retracted, but its officially published version was retrieved through a subsequent Google Scholar search. 6 trials with a total of 598 patients were included in the present meta-analysis.

Flow chart of the literature search according to the PRISMA guidelines.

All studies reported the basic characteristics of the patients (Table 1), showing no statistical differences in gender, age, or haemorrhoid grade. Four studies used Milligan-Morgan haemorrhoidectomy [11–14], 1 used haemorrhoidectomy [15] and 1 used closed haemorrhoidectomy, doppler haemorrhoidal artery ligation and lauromacrogol injection therapy [16]. Regarding anaesthesia, two studies used general anaesthesia [13,14], two used subarachnoid anaesthesia [12,16] and two did not report the type of anaesthesia [11,15]. Different studies used different medications to relieve postoperative pain. Ramin Azhough’s study [12] used Ketorolac, Naproxen and acetaminophen with codeine, H.-L. Sim’s study [14] used Paracetamol and diclofenac and Xu, S’s study [16] used ropivacaine and parecoxib. 3 other studies [11,13,15] did not report postoperative pain management.

Quality assessment and risk of bias

The risk of bias assessment is shown in Figure 2. Five studies showed low risk across all bias criteria (randomization process, deviations from the intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of the outcome and selection of the reported result). One study showed some concerns in the randomization process and high risk in the Measurement of the outcome (Figure 3). Meta-analysis

Maximum VAS pain scores within 12 h

Five RCTs [11–13,15,16] reported VAS pain scores within 12 h post-haemorrhoid surgery. Using a random effects model, high heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 97.6%). The meta-analysis revealed a significant reduction in VAS pain scores for the experimental group compared to the control group within 12 h (WMD = −1.582, 95% CI: −2.834 to −0.330, p = .013; Figure 4), indicating a clinically meaningful pain reduction in the experimental group.

Forest plot diagram showing pain scores within 12 h.

VAS pain scores on day 2

Three RCTs [12,15,16] provided VAS pain scores two days after haemorrhoid surgery. A random effects model was applied, showing moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 70.3%). The meta-analysis demonstrated a significantly lower VAS pain score in the experimental group compared to the control group on day 2 (WMD = −1.441, 95% CI: −1.874 to −1.008, p = .000; Figure 5), indicating statistically significant pain relief.

Forest plot diagram showing pain scores on day 2.

VAS pain scores on day 3

Four RCTs [11,12,15,16] reported VAS pain scores on day 3 post-surgery. A random effects model was used, revealing moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 72.6%). The meta-analysis showed that the experimental group had significantly lower VAS pain scores than the control group on day 3 (WMD = −0.975, 95% CI: −1.309 to −0.641, p = .000; Figure 6), highlighting a statistically significant pain reduction.

Forest plot diagram showing pain scores on day 3.

VAS pain scores on day 5

Four RCTs [11,12,15,16] provided data on VAS pain scores five days after haemorrhoid surgery. A random effects model was applied, with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 66.9%). The meta-analysis revealed significantly lower VAS pain scores in the experimental group compared to the control group on day 5 (WMD = −0.786, 95% CI: −1.094 to −0.478, p = .000; Figure 7), reflecting a significant pain reduction.

Forest plot diagram showing pain scores on day 5.

VAS pain scores on day 7

Four RCTs [12,13,15,16] reported VAS pain scores on day 7 after haemorrhoid surgery. Using a random effects model, high heterogeneity was noted (I2 = 89.5%). The meta-analysis showed a significant decrease in VAS pain scores in the experimental group compared to the control group on day 7 (WMD = −0.679, 95% CI: −1.257 to −0.101, p = .021; Figure 8), suggesting a meaningful and statistically significant reduction in pain.

Forest plot diagram showing pain scores on day 7.

VAS pain scores at half a month (on day 14 or 15)

Three RCTs [11–13] reported the pain scores at half a month after haemorrhoid operation. A fixed effect model was adopted and the analysis revealed low heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%). The meta-analysis showed that the difference in pain scores between the two groups at half a month after haemorrhoid operation was not statistically significant (WMD = −0.243, 95% CI: −0.510 to 0.024, p = .074; Figure 9).

Forest plot diagram showing pain scores at half a month.

The number of individuals requiring analgesic medication

Three RCTs [11,13,16] reported the number of individuals requiring analgesic medication after a haemorrhoid operation. A fixed effect model was adopted and the analysis revealed low heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%). Meta-analysis demonstrated that the experimental group exhibited significantly lower number of individuals requiring analgesic medication after the operation (RR = 0.293, 95% CI:0.203 to 0.422, p = .000; Figure 10).

Forest plot diagram showing the number of individuals requiring analgesic medication.

Complications

Five RCTs [11–14,16] reported the complications after haemorrhoid operation. Five studies [11–14,16] reported the number of cases of urinary retention postoperatively. One study [13] reported the number of cases of wound infection postoperatively. Two studies [11,14] reported the number of cases of local skin reactions. Three studies [11,12,14] reported the number of cases with secondary haemorrhage. Three studies [11,12,14] reported the number of cases with pruritus. Four studies [11–14] reported the number of cases of temporary incontinence. The complications in the methylene blue groups compared to the control groups showed a relative risk (RR) of 0.65 (95% CI: 0.39 to 1.10, p = .111, I2 = 0.0%; Figure 11). There were no differences between the subgroups based on the type of complications, urinary retention (RR 0.56, 95% CI: 0.25 to 1.22, p = .142, I2 = 0.0%), wound infection (RR 0.50, 95% CI: 0.05 to 5.26, p = .564), local skin reaction (RR 0.33, 95% CI: 0.01 to 8.01, p = .498), secondary haemorrhage (RR 0.36, 95% CI: 0.08 to 1.59, p = .176, I2 = 0.0%), pruritus (RR 1.31, 95% CI: 0.27 to 6.47, p = .738, I2 = 0.0%), temporary incontinence (RR 1.17, 95% CI: 0.36 to 3.79, p = .798, I2 = 0.0%).

Forest plot diagram showing the complications.

Subgroup analyses

The heterogeneity for ≤12 h was 97.6% and for day 7, it was 89.5%. Due to the limited number of included studies, we were unable to perform subgroup analyses on surgical methods, anaesthesia types, or methylene blue concentrations. However, this does not mean that we can exclude these factors as potential sources of heterogeneity.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis revealed that the effect size remained within the 95% confidence interval of the overall effect size after excluding each individual study. This indicates that the included studies did not significantly impact VAS pain scores, the number of patients requiring analgesics, urinary retention, secondary bleeding, itching, or temporary incontinence. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary File 1.

Discussion

Our systematic review and meta-analysis show that the use of methylene blue following haemorrhoid surgery can relieve pain within the first seven days postoperatively and reduce the consumption of analgesics. It does not increase the risk of complications such as urinary retention, secondary haemorrhage, pruritus, temporary incontinence, wound infection and local skin reactions. This is the first meta-analysis on this topic, primarily based on low-quality randomized controlled trials and provides level Ic evidence supporting the wider use of methylene blue during haemorrhoid surgery.

Methylene blue effectively relieves early postoperative pain and pain during defecation [16], thereby reducing the use of analgesics and unplanned consultations [12,14] or readmissions [11,12,14] caused by pain. Unlike nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which can lead to gastrointestinal issues, kidney damage and cardiovascular risks, or opioids, which may cause dependence, respiratory depression and other side effects [17], methylene blue offers a safer way. According to studies by Xu et al. [16] and Tato et al. [13], the use of methylene blue reduces the incidence of nausea and vomiting in patients. Urinary retention, a common complication after haemorrhoid surgery, was not found to be increased by methylene blue use in our study. Additionally, methylene blue does not induce secondary haemorrhage, pruritus, temporary incontinence, wound infection, or local skin reactions, further contributing to patient comfort [16]. However, due to the limited number of included studies and patient sample sizes, larger-scale real-world studies are still needed in the future to further validate its safety.

Methylene blue (C16H18N3ClS) is the first dye used in clinical treatment, commonly used for treating methemoglobinemia [18], restoring vascular tone to treat vasoplegic syndrome [19], as a sentinel lymph node tracer in cancer surgeries [20] and has anti-infective and antiviral properties [21]. In colorectal medicine, methylene blue is commonly used to treat intractable idiopathic pruritus ani [4], locate the internal opening of perianal abscesses or fistulas [22,23] and in colorectal cancer patients, methylene blue improves lymph node collection, which helps to better classify the disease by improving tumour pathological staging [24]. In pilonidal sinus surgery, marking the excision area helps promote the complete removal of the pilonidal sinus [25], reducing the long-term recurrence risk by half [26].

Most researchers suggest that methylene blue induces analgesia by hindering or damaging the neural connections in tissues, a process referred to as denervation. Eusebio et al. applied methylene blue to treat intractable pruritus ani and conducted electron microscopy on the perianal skin of patients. They found no distinct nerve endings in the samples even after a follow-up of up to seven years [27]. Methylene blue plays an important role in the anti-inflammatory process, with its mechanism involving the downregulation of inducible NO synthase (iNOS) to block the iNOS/NO signalling pathway, as well as inhibiting the expression of P2X receptors and lncRNA, NF-κB activation and inflammasome formation, thereby reducing inflammatory cytokine levels and effectively alleviating pain. Methylene blue can also block voltage-gated sodium channels, reduce sodium currents and decrease neuronal excitability, thereby hindering pain transmission [6].

Methylene blue has a latency period of 4 to 6 h before it takes effect, during which it damages the patient’s nerve myelin, causing a burning-like pain in the perianal wound area. Therefore, in clinical practice, methylene blue is often used in combination with local anaesthetics to block the nerves and alleviate the burning pain produced during the latency period of methylene blue [16,28].

At present, most studies [13–15,28–31] use concentrations of methylene blue ranging from 0.1% to 0.25%. A study by Long et al. [31] discovered that after haemorrhoidectomy, perianal injections of 0.1% and 0.2% methylene blue concentrations showed similar analgesic effects, but the 0.1% concentration of methylene blue was safer.

This meta-analysis has some limitations. Some results show high heterogeneity and due to the limited number of included studies, we were unable to perform subgroup analysis to explore the sources of heterogeneity. It is speculated that the heterogeneity may arise from differences in anaesthetic methods, surgical techniques, methylene blue concentration and postoperative adjunctive pain management strategies. One study [11] indicated some concerns regarding the randomization process and a high risk in the outcome measurement. Future studies should focus on increasing the sample size and improving the study design quality to enhance the reliability and persuasiveness of the results.

Currently, the application methods of methylene blue include intradermal injection, subcutaneous injection and topical application on the wound surface. To determine which method is more effective and safer, further experimental research is needed. Additionally, the analgesic effects of combining methylene blue with different local anaesthetics need to be further studied to identify the optimal combination. Due to considerable heterogeneity among studies, further multicentre, large-sample randomized controlled trials including diverse patient populations are needed to provide high-quality evidence. It is important to note that, at present, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved methylene blue products only for the treatment of acquired methemoglobinemia. Therefore, we recommend developing detailed usage guidelines and creating specialized methylene blue products for the treatment of colorectal diseases. This would help ensure that physicians use the drug in a compliant and effective manner.

Conclusions

This systematic review and meta-analysis indicate that the use of methylene blue after haemorrhoid surgery effectively reduces pain within the first seven days postoperatively, decreases the use of analgesics and does not increase the risk of urinary retention or cause secondary haemorrhage, pruritus, temporary incontinence, wound infection, or local skin reactions. However, there are limitations to the study, including high heterogeneity in some outcome measures and a limited number of studies. Furthermore, further experimental investigation is needed to explore the optimal administration method, the combination with local anaesthetics and to improve methylene blue products and their usage guidelines.
