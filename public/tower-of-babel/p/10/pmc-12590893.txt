Neonatal malnutrition, body composition, and childhood obesity in critically ill infants
Authors: Allison D Ta, Ting Ting Fu, Gillian R Goddard, Kera McNelis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are expert-agreed upon malnutrition indicators for infants, but the meaningfulness of these indicators in hospitalized neonates is unknown.MethodsEighty-four term infants hospitalized in a level IV neonatal intensive care unit had body composition measurements and other anthropometric data extracted from the medical record. Linear regression using maximum likelihood estimation was performed to evaluate the relationship of malnutrition status (assessed at time of body composition assessment) with each body composition metric (body fat percent z-score, fat mass z-score, fat-free mass z-score) and with toddler body mass index (BMI) z-score. Linear regression was also performed to evaluate the relationship between each body composition metric and toddler BMI z-score.ResultsThere was a statistically significant negative association between neonatal malnutrition diagnosis with a -0.83 change in fat-free mass z-score (95% CI -1.61 to -0.05, p = 0.04). There was no statistically significant association between body fat percent or fat mass and neonatal malnutrition diagnosis. There was no statistically significant relationship between each infant body composition metric and toddler BMI z-score in unadjusted linear regression models.ConclusionsNeonatal malnutrition diagnosis is associated with lower fat-free mass in critically ill infants with a variety of conditions.Keywords: Anthropometry, Body composition, Body mass index, Infant, Malnutrition

Background

Infancy is a critical time of rapid growth and development that sets the stage for later health. An expert committee recognized the unique demands of this stage and recommended indicators of malnutrition in preterm and term infants [1]. Such indicators by Goldberg et al. included weight and length for age z-score [1]. These malnutrition indicators are specific for the neonatal age and differ from the pediatric indicators, possibly allowing for earlier detection [2–4]. Early diagnosis of malnutrition could allow earlier treatment or prevention of associated morbidity and mortality [5, 6].

These neonatal malnutrition indicators include severity classification: mild, moderate, or severe malnutrition [1]. There are primary indicators, which allow the diagnosis with a single criterion (e.g. decline in weight-for-age z-score), or a secondary indicator that requires multiple criteria to make the diagnosis. Five of the six indicators are based on anthropometric measurements and the respective z-scores for age or growth velocity gain. Although these criteria are practical and can be easily used in many settings, they are expert consensus based. The sensitivity of these indicators for nutritional risk or predicting later childhood health is unknown as they have not been validated [7].

Body composition measurement is another evaluation of health and nutritional status [8]. Different models describe body composition, with the simplest two component model evaluating fat versus fat-free mass [9]. Air displacement plethysmography (ADP) is a valid, non-invasive and safe technology that measures fat and fat-free mass [10]. Normative growth references for infant body composition are published, allowing for interpretation of an ADP measurement and comparison to a healthy peer cohort [11, 12]. High fat gain in infancy is a risk factor for later childhood obesity in healthy infants [13], but it is unclear if this relationship is true in sick infants. In addition, multiple studies have described the association between increased fat-free mass and improved cognition, improved neurodevelopment, and greater brain size in preterm infants, and there is some limited evidence for this link in term infants [14–20].

Although ADP and other types of body composition technology are available, they are not as widely used as simple anthropometric measurements in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) due to constraints of equipment cost and personnel resources [10]. Thus, determining an anthropometric proxy for body composition is useful for bedside clinical practice. While previous studies have explored the relationship between anthropometric measures and body composition in healthy term and preterm infants at birth, [21–23] there remains a knowledge gap in understanding how anthropometric indicators of malnutrition relate to body composition in hospitalized term infants. Infants who require neonatal intensive care may have a higher energy demand due to illness and disordered nutrient accretion compared to their healthy counterparts. This retrospective study explored the association between anthropometric-based malnutrition indicators and body composition in term and late preterm infants in the NICU. We hypothesized that a malnutrition diagnosis would be associated with low fat-free mass. The cohort was followed until toddler age, and later growth was described.

Methods

Study population

This is a retrospective study of late preterm and term infants admitted between 2017 and 2023 to Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center NICU, a regional referral Level IV NICU within a freestanding children’s hospital in Cincinnati, Ohio, USA. Routine use of an ADP device for body composition measurement was integrated into clinical practice at this site; details are described elsewhere [24]. ADP measurement data was exported from the machine to derive the study cohort. Body composition measurements were excluded if identifiers were missing and the measurement was unable to be linked to a patient. Only late preterm and term infants were included, defined as being born at or after 34 weeks completed gestational age [25]. A limited manual retrospective chart review yielded additional relevant variables including anthropometric measurements at birth, head circumference at time of body composition measurement, and anthropometric measurements at toddler age (2–4 years of age). Data were entered into a secure Research Electronic Database Capture [26]. Institutional review board approval was sought, and this study (#2023–0152) was determined to be exempt.

Body composition assessment

The ADP device is approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for clinical use for infants between 1–8 kg (PEAPOD, COSMED, Rome, Italy) [24, 27]. The data exported from the machine included ADP measurement data (test date, fat percentage, fat-free mass percentage, fat mass, fat-free mass, body mass, and body volume) and manually entered variables (sex, gestational age, and length on the day of measurement). Fat mass, fat-free mass, and body fat percentage were converted to z-scores and centiles for age and sex per the Norris et al. reference growth curves [28].

Anthropometric-based assessment

Weight, length, and head circumference at birth were converted to z-scores using the Fenton growth reference [29]. Study subjects were evaluated for malnutrition based on the Goldberg et al. [1] indicators using decline in weight z-score of 0.8 or greater and decline in length z-score of 0.8 or greater from birth to time of the first body composition measurement. Notably, the Goldberg malnutrition indicators do not include head circumference as a primary independent marker of malnutrition, as abnormal head growth is considered more reflective of morbidity related to developmental immaturity rather than nutritional status. Infants were excluded from length-based malnutrition evaluation if their length was missing or implausible (e.g., recorded length shorter than birth length). Infants were categorized as malnourished if they met either criterion. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from toddler clinic visits and converted to z-scores and centiles per the Center for Disease Control and Prevention reference growth curves [30–32]. For patients with more than one toddler-age time point, the first timepoint was used for statistical analysis. Nutritional status at the toddler visit was classified using the Center for Disease Control and Prevention BMI-based definitions: underweight < 5 percentile, normal 5- < 85 percentile, overweight is 85- < 95th percentile and obesity is ≥ 95th percentile for age and sex [33].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SAS software (version 9.4, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC), and additional graphics were generated with R version 4.3.2 (R Core Team) and Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, 2018). Linear regression modeling was performed to evaluate the relationship of malnutrition status (diagnosed yes/no from any criterion) with each body composition metric (body fat percent z-score, fat mass z-score, fat-free mass z-score) as the dependent variable. Linear regression was also performed to evaluate the relationship between each body composition metric and later toddler BMI z-score. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

There was body composition data available for one hundred thirty infants. Forty-six infants were excluded for prematurity < 34 weeks gestational age. Eighty-four infants fit the gestational age inclusion criterion and were included in this analysis. Descriptive characteristics are represented in Table 1. There was a slight over-representation (i.e. greater than an expected 10% for each) of small- and large-for gestational age infants (Table 1). The most common admission diagnosis was a congenital gastrointestinal disorder, such as intestinal atresia or gastroschisis (44% or n = 37 of 84). The next most common diagnosis was major congenital anomaly, such as congenital diaphragmatic hernia or bladder outlet obstruction (15%, or n = 12 of 84).

Ten of the mothers/birthing parents of the 84 infants had diabetes mellitus, including eight parents with gestational diabetes, and two with type I diabetes mellitus. Eight mothers/birthing parents had a diagnosis of chronic hypertension (10%), seven had a diagnosis of gestational hypertension (8%), and four had a diagnosis of pre-eclampsia (5%). Four mothers/birthing parents (5%) had a diagnosis of obesity recorded. Seven mothers/birthing parents (8%) had a history of opiate use during pregnancy, three (4%) had a history of cocaine use, eight (10%) had a history of marijuana use, and there were no parents with reported amphetamine use.

Due to missing length data, 24 infants were only able to have weight-based malnutrition indicators applied. 23 of 60 (39%) of infants with accurate length available met the length-based malnutrition criterion. The majority (n = 53 or 63%) of the 84 infants in the study cohort met at least one criterion for malnutrition diagnosis (Table 1).

Distribution of body composition outcomes are described in Table 1. A statistically significant negative association was observed between a diagnosis of malnutrition and fat-free mass (Table 2), with malnutrition linked to a −0.83 change in the fat-free mass z-score (95% CI −1.61 to −0.05, p = 0.04). No significant associations were found between a malnutrition diagnosis and either fat mass or body fat percentage.

Discussion

This study examines the meaningfulness of expert-based malnutrition indicators in a heterogenous NICU cohort of sick term infants that is reflective of a free-standing level IV NICU. [35] These patients pose a unique challenge for nutritional assessment and growth monitoring, [34] and to our knowledge this is the first study evaluating the performance of these malnutrition indicators with other well-studied markers of growth in term or late preterm infants. We identified a significant association between meeting malnutrition diagnostic criteria and having reduced fat-free mass. This is particularly important as lower fat-free mass in preterm infants has been linked to worse neurocognitive outcomes [13–19]. Although this link has not yet been established in our study population of late preterm or term infants, it warrants further investigation through long-term neurodevelopmental studies.

This study additionally has strengths in its practicability and contributes meaningful support for the use of the neonatal malnutrition indicators proposed by Goldberg et al. These indicators, which rely primarily on anthropometric measurements, are advantageous due to their ease of use and applicability across diverse clinical settings [1, 2]. However, a key limitation is the current lack of high-level evidence supporting their validity. Our findings help address this gap by providing empirical data that reinforces their clinical relevance.

Interestingly, we did not detect a relationship between body fat and malnutrition, nor body fat and later overweight and obesity. A cohort study demonstrated that term singleton infants with lower in the lowest fat mass had higher blood pressure trajectories at 3–6 years of age, but did not have a higher overweight risk [35]. Other studies have demonstrated that high body fat at birth and greater than expected fat gain in infancy confer later obesity risk [13, 36]. Nutritional status of infants peripartum and postpartum have long term impacts on their later growth and development [37]. Healthy infants with greater adiposity as young as 2 months, based on weight-length or BMI, has been correlated with obesity in early childhood [38–40]. Other studies have shown that greater weight gain velocity during 0–6 months was correlated with higher adiposity in childhood [41]. This suggests that complex endocrine pathways and oxidative stress as an infant may modulate the metabolism which continues into childhood. Other factors associated with childhood obesity include genetics, parental weight status, and lifestyle factors (physical activity, sleep, and diet) [42, 43].

These well-done studies intentionally evaluated the growth trajectories of healthy infants and children. It is unknown if the same relationship between early body composition and later obesity risk is true in critically ill infants. ADP and dual x-ray absorptiometry describe body composition in a number of components, but do not evaluate the type of adipose tissue that is present [9]. Brown adipose tissue has an important function for thermogenesis [44]. The physiology of adipose tissue, the function of adipose tissue during critical illness and the plasticity or “browning” of white adipose tissue is only beginning to be understood in an adult population [45]. There is still much to be learned about the physiology of adipose tissue and whether there are protective effects. This raises the question of whether all body fat is equal, and whether it is harmful to have greater body fat for a term infant in the NICU. This is by no means a definitive study, and it is possible this study was underpowered to detect a relationship between nutritional status and body fat that does, in fact, exist. Further research is needed to understand the adaptive role adipose tissue plays in critically ill infants.

This study has several limitations, including the retrospective and single-center design. Due to limited sample size in this pilot study, we bundled all malnutrition indicators together into one category, instead of delineating the relationships between each malnutrition indicator and body composition. Future directions of larger studies should include assessing the performance of each criterion with important growth or health outcomes.

Although body composition measurement via ADP has been implemented into routine care in some NICUs, there are resource- and patient-specific reasons this has not been universally implemented [24]. Free-flowing oxygen cannot be delivered while an infant is inside the machine, so infants requiring respiratory support are unable to have this assessment performed. The exclusion of infants who never liberated to room air may introduce bias in this study cohort. However, the intent of this study is to demonstrate if the neonatal malnutrition indicators by Golberg et al. can help fill the gap when ADP is unavailable. Due to its practicality and ease of use, neonatal malnutrition indicator usage can easily become widespread, especially if aided by improvements to the electronic medical record or other tools [2, 46–48].

Conclusion

Malnutrition diagnosis by the indicators proposed by Goldberg et al. is associated with lower fat-free mass in critically ill infants with a variety of conditions, adding to the usefulness and value of these indicators. Further research is needed to understand the pathophysiology of fat tissue in infants during times of stress and relate these malnutrition indicators to other important health outcomes.
