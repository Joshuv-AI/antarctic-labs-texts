Biomechanical deficits in chronic ankle instability: a comparative study of landing strategies on a laterally inclined surface
Authors: Usef Mohammadi Yaghoubi, Nader Farahpour, Gholamreza Ghorbani-Amjad, Gabriel Moisan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals with chronic ankle instability (CAI) exhibit altered biomechanics during landing. However, no studies have characterised the effects of varying landing heights during unilateral landings on inclined surfaces. Gaining this knowledge is essential for improving our understanding of biomechanical landing strategies in individuals with CAI, with the overarching goal of better characterising their biomechanical deficits in various sports-related contexts. The objective of this study was to determine the differences in landing biomechanics from low and high heights on a laterally inclined surface in individuals with CAI compared to healthy controls.MethodsMidfoot, ankle, knee, and hip angles, moments, and power of 16 participants with CAI and 16 healthy controls were collected during a unilateral drop landing on a 15o laterally inclined surface from landing heights of 20 and 40 cm. Two-way ANOVAs with repeated-measures on the Height factor using statistical parametric mapping was used to determine the Group (control vs. CAI), Height (20 cm and 40 cm), and Group X Height interaction effects for the kinematic and kinetic variables.ResultsIndividuals with CAI exhibited greater ankle inversion angles (1 to 11% of the landing phase (%LP), P = 0.002; 56 to 100%LP, P = 0.002) and less ankle eversion moments (50 to 66%LP, P = 0.002; 95 to 99%LP, p = 0.002) compared to healthy controls. When landing from a greater height, both groups displayed greater midfoot dorsiflexion (5 to 51%LP, P = 0.006), midfoot inversion (17 to 39%LP, P = 0.007), ankle dorsiflexion (33 to 63%LP, P < 0.001), knee flexion (0 to 77%LP, P < 0.001) and hip flexion (8 to 100%LP, P = 0.002) They also exhibited greater ankle plantarflexion (0 to 50%LP, P < 0.001) and eversion (0 to 16%LP, P = 0.002) moments, knee extension (0 to 3%LP, P = 0.008; 5 to 51%LP, P < 0.001) and hip flexion (0 to 3%LP, P = 0.006) moments as well as greater ankle (0 to 19%LP, P < 0.001;) and knee power absorption (from 0 to 3%LP, P = 0.007).ConclusionIndividuals with CAI demonstrate altered lower limb biomechanics, particularly greater ankle inversion angles and reduced ankle eversion moments, which may increase the risk of recurrent lateral ankle sprains. Our results revealed a movement pattern adjustment when landing from greater heights in individuals with and without CAI.

Background

Lateral ankle sprains (LAS) are among the most common musculoskeletal injuries in athletic populations [1, 2]. Lateral ankle sprains represent more than 15% of all injuries in athletes from the National College Athletic Association¸ in the United States of America [1]. After sustaining an initial sprain, up to 80% of individuals [3] will experience a recurrent LAS, and 46% of these individuals will develop chronic ankle instability (CAI) [4]. Chronic ankle instability is characterised by a propensity for recurrent LAS at least one year after the initial sprain, with frequent episodes of ankle “giving way” [5]. Persistent symptoms include pain, swelling, limited range of motion, weakness, and diminished self-reported function [5, 6]. According to the Hertel and Corbett [5] model, a spectrum of motor-behavioral, sensory-perceptual, and pathomechanical impairments, from copers to individuals with CAI can be observed after the initial LAS. All these impairments and symptoms predispose these individuals to sustain multiple LAS throughout their lifetime and develop long-term joint sequelae, namely post-traumatic ankle osteoarthritis [7] and decreased health-related quality of life [8] and physical activity level [9]. Among the physical impairments exhibited by individuals with CAI, biomechanical deficits during locomotion [10] and sports maneuvers [11, 12] are well described in the current knowledge base.

Chronic ankle instability significantly alters lower limb biomechanics during specific sport-related maneuvers such as unilateral jump landing [11, 12], a commonly reported task that imposes large and rapid impulse loads on the ankle complex, potentially triggering the mechanism of LAS in individuals with CAI [11]. During unilateral jump landing, individuals with CAI exhibit greater ankle dorsiflexion [13] and inversion [14], greater knee flexion [13, 15], ankle plantarflexion moments [15], lower peroneus longus and vastus lateralis preactivation [14, 16] and greater peak vertical ground reaction forces [17] compared to healthy counterparts. Most previous studies related to landing biomechanics in individuals with CAI have focused on deficits observed on even surfaces [11, 18].

However, in recent years, greater attention has been directed toward landing biomechanics on more challenging surfaces, such as inclined ones, as these conditions may place individuals with CAI at a higher risk of sustaining recurrent LAS [16, 19–21]. During unilateral drop jump landing on an inclined surface, individuals with CAI exhibit a longer peroneus longus activation latency [20, 22], reduced peroneus longus activation [16, 22], reduced gluteus medius muscle activation [16], greater ankle inversion angles [20, 22] and greater ankle internal rotation angles [19] compared to healthy counterparts. However, biomechanical deficits in individuals with CAI during unilateral jump landing on inclined surfaces vary across studies. For example, Moisan et al. [16] found no differences in ankle inversion angles between individuals with CAI and healthy controls, whereas Simpson et al. [20] reported greater ankle inversion angles in individuals with CAI during landing on a laterally inclined surface. Differences in initial heights and surface inclinations across studies may perhaps explain these differences and thus further exploration is needed to refine previous findings. Also, most studies on landing biomechanics in CAI focused on the ankle, knee, and hip joints biomechanics. De Ridder et al. [23] showed that individuals with CAI exhibit a more inverted midfoot position relative to the rearfoot compared to healthy controls during a side jump and Sagawa et al. [24] observed smaller inversion/eversion excursion angles between the forefoot and rearfoot during a unilateral lateral drop landing task in individuals with CAI. However, no studies investigated the foot biomechanics deficits in CAI during landing on inclined surfaces. Further exploration is needed to better understand the foot biomechanical deficits in CAI during unilateral jump landings on inclined surfaces, which will allow to better understand the biomechanical adaptations exhibited by this population using a biomechanical framework that includes all the key lower limb joints. This could also help understand why individuals with CAI are at more risk of sustaining recurrent LAS during landing.

Most studies investigating unilateral drop landing biomechanics in individuals with CAI used only one initial height [11, 18]. However, greater loads to dampen and higher landing heights may increase the risks of sustaining LAS in individuals with CAI. As the initial landing height increases, there is a notable increase in knee and hip flexion angles, along with greater ankle plantarflexion moments. Additionally, a decrease in ankle inversion angle and inversion angular velocity during landing are observed [15, 25]. To date, no studies have characterised the effects of varying landing heights during unilateral landings on inclined surfaces. Gaining this knowledge is essential for improving our understanding of biomechanical landing strategies in individuals with CAI, with the overarching goal of better characterising their biomechanical deficits in various sports-related contexts.

Thus, the primary objective was to assess the deficits in landing biomechanics on a laterally inclined surface from both low and high heights in individuals with CAI and healthy controls. The secondary objective was to compare landing biomechanics when landing from low and high heights. The main hypothesis was that individuals with CAI would demonstrate more at-risk lower limb biomechanics (e.g., greater ankle inversion angles) during landing from low and high heights compared to control participants and that individuals with and without CAI would exhibit more protective lower limb biomechanics (e.g., smaller ankle inversion angles and greater knee and hip flexion angles) when landing from a higher height.

Methods

Participants

Given that no previous study has evaluated the landing biomechanics of inclined landings at different heights in individuals with and without CAI, we determined our sample size based on Simpson et al. [19, 20]. Their findings indicated that 13 participants per group (CAI and controls) were sufficient to compare landing biomechanics during inclined unilateral drop landings and obtain the following parameters: β = 0.80, effect size = 0.50, and α = 0.05. To account for differences in experimental designs and analyses, we recruited 16 individuals with CAI (age: 26.6 ± 3.2 years; height: 179.4 ± 5.7 cm; body mass: 82.8 ± 14.4 kg; BMI: 25.3 ± 3.8 kg/m²) and 16 age-, height-, and mass-matched healthy controls (age: 28.8 ± 3.9 years; height: 178.6 ± 5.1 cm; body mass: 83.1 ± 12.3 kg; BMI: 25.9 ± 3.0 kg/m²) (see Table 1). Participants were recruited from local orthopedic clinics, provincial sport federations, and advertisements on social media between September 2022 and May 2024 to participate in this laboratory-based case-control study. Before enrolling in this study, the protocol was explained to potential participants to allow them to self-determined if they had sufficient athletic abilities to undertake the biomechanical analyses on the inclined platform. Individuals with CAI scored 68.3 ± 8.3% on the Foot and Ankle Ability Measure- Sport subscale (FAAM-S), 79.7 ± 6.7% on the Activity of Daily Living subscale (FAAM-ADL) [26], and 15.1 ± 4.9 on the Cumberland Ankle Instability Tool (CAIT) [27]. They reported a history of 6.2 ± 2.9 LAS, with 5.2 ± 3.5 years since their first sprain and 1.3 ± 0.5 years since their last episode.

The inclusion criteria were based on the standards provided in the International Ankle Consortium statement [28]. Participants with CAI were included if they: (a) were aged between 18 and 45 years, (b) had a history of at least one LAS occurring at least 12 months before the experimentation onset, and experienced ankle giving way and/or a feeling of ankle instability, (c) scored less than 90% on the FAAM-ADL and less than 80% on the FAAM-S subscales, and (d) scored 24 or less on the CAIT. Individuals were included in the control group if they had no history of LAS. Exclusion criteria for all participants included a history of lower limb musculoskeletal injury in the three months prior to the study, previous surgery on lower limb musculoskeletal structures, a history of lower limb fractures requiring realignment, and neurological conditions affecting walking biomechanics. Only individuals with unilateral CAI were included. The protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hamedan University of Medical Sciences (IR.BASU.REC.1401.034). All participants agreed to be enrolled in the study and signed an informed consent form prior to their participation.

Experimental protocol

Before the biomechanical analysis, all participants completed the FAAM-S, FAAM-ADL, and CAIT questionnaires. Then, synchronised kinematic and kinetic data were obtained using a Qualisys system with 8 Micus cameras (200 Hz) (Version: 2020.3, Qualisys AB, Kvarnbergsgatan2, 41105, Gothenburg, Sweden), and two Kistler force plates (1 000 Hz) (Kistler, Winterthur, Switzerland) during the barefoot drop-landing tasks. Cameras were mounted on tripods around the laboratory at different heights of between 1 and 2 m. Cameras were calibrated using a cubic space (2 m height × 2 m width × 4 m length). The force plates were embedded in the floor in the middle of the calibrated space. Both systems were monitored synchronously via QTM software. Using a combination of the Rizzoli foot model [29] (for the shank and foot segments) and full-body lumbar spine model (only the thigh and pelvis markers were used in this study) [30], 33 markers were placed on the evaluated limb and pelvis to identify the foot (12 markers), shank (9 markers), and thigh (8 markers), as well as the pelvis (4 markers). Of these, four markers on the pelvis, six on the thigh, seven on the shank, four on the hindfoot, four on the forefoot, and three on the hallux (one from the hallux, and two from the forefoot) were used for tracking each segment.

Participants then performed barefoot drop-landing from two heights of 20 cm and 40 cm onto a surface inclined at 15 degrees (with the medial side higher than the lateral side) in a randomly decided order (Fig. 1). Participants were aware of the initial height of the landing task. The platform heights were selected to introduce a progressively greater challenge while maintaining participant safety. The platform inclinations were also chosen to elicit a substantial landing challenge, while remaining within the ethical guidelines approved by our ethics committee to ensure participant safety. It is also aligned with previous inclinations used in this population in previous studies. To incline the surface, a cubic concrete platform with a base matching the dimensions of the force plate (0.4 m × 0.6 m) and heights of 3.00 cm and 13.72 cm for the right and left walls, respectively, was used. This concrete platform was mounted on top of the force plate.

Unilateral drop-landing on a laterally inclined surface

To perform the task, participants had to stand on 20 cm and 40 cm high take-off platforms, propel themselves with the contralateral leg, and land on the testing leg on the centre of the inclined platform. For each task, participants were asked to always face forward, put their hands on their waist and carefully avoid occluding pelvis markers, and remain balanced on the landing surface for 10 s. Trials were rejected and immediately retaken if the foot moved after landing or if participants lost their balance or used their hands to restore balance. Participants completed four trials of each task for a total of eight trials with one minute of rest between trials. Prior to the main trials, they had five minutes of warm-up exercises, which included drop-landing practices.

Data processing

All data were digitised and exported as C3D files using QTM software. Then, C3D files were imported and processed using Visual 3D software (Has-motion, Canada). Tracking markers’ trajectories were filtered at 6 Hz and force plate data at 50 Hz with a zero-lag, fourth-order, low-pass Butterworth filter [16, 21, 31, 32]. Local coordinate systems for the hindfoot, forefoot, ankle, knee and hip were defined according to the Rizzoli multisegmented and lower body models [29, 33]. Not all markers from the full-body lumbar spine kinematic model were used due to the reliance on traditional kinematic analyses instead of the originally planned musculoskeletal modeling. Hallux, midfoot (forefoot related to hindfoot segments) [32, 34], ankle, knee and hip joints angles were computed with an X-Y-Z order of rotations (only the X axis for the hallux). Positive values for the rotation around the X-axis was defined as flexion for the hip, extension for the knee, dorsiflexion for the ankle and the midfoot. Positive rotation around the Y-axis was defined as adduction for the hip and the knee, inversion for the ankle and the midfoot. Positive rotation around the Z-axis was defined as internal rotation for the hip, the knee, the ankle and the midfoot. Ankle, knee and hip joints’ internal moments and power were computed using inverse dynamics, resolved in the proximal segment coordinate system, and normalised to body mass. Then, all variables were normalised to 101 points (from initial foot contact to full knee extension) [32]. Initial contact was determined with the force plate using a 10 N threshold [16, 31]. The primary outcomes are ankle angles, moments and power. The secondary outcomes are foot, knee, and hip angles, moments and power.

Statistical analysis

Shapiro-Wilk’s tests were used to evaluate the normality of the descriptive data distribution. Since the data were normally distributed, independent t-tests were used to compare both groups. Descriptive analyses were implemented in SPSS-27 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Then, two-way ANOVAs with repeated-measures on one factor using statistical parametric mapping (SPM) was used to determine the Group (Control vs. CAI), Height (repeated-measures; 20 cm and 40 cm) and “Group × Height” interaction effects for the kinematic and kinetic variables. Group, Height and Interaction effects were individually analysed. Since there are no normality tests for this ANOVA using SPM, both the parametric (spm1d.stats.anova2onerm function) and the non-parametric (spm1d.stats.nonparam.anova2onerm function) versions were conducted. When both tests qualitatively agreed, the results of the parametric version were reported; when the results qualitatively disagreed, the results of the non-parametric version were reported. When significant interaction effects were observed, independent t tests (SPM(t); spm1d.stats.nonparam.ttest2 function) were used to compare both groups for both heights and dependent t-tests (SPM(t); spm1d.stats.ttest_paired function) were used to compare both heights in each group. However, when data were not normally distributed using spm1d.stats.normality.ttest2 and spm1d.stats.normality.ttest_paired functions, respectively, the non-parametric versions were used (spm1d.stats.nonparam.ttest2 and spm1d.stats.nonparam.ttest_paired functions). The level of significance was set at α ≤ 0.010, rather than the 0.050 gold standard, to compensate for multiple statistical comparisons. All SPM analyses were implemented in MATLAB R2023a (The Mathworks Inc., Boston, MA, USA) using open-access scripts (www.spm1d.org).

Results

Demographic data

No between-group differences were observed for age (P = 0.707), height (P = 0.857), body mass (P = 0.696), and BMI (P = 0.466).

Group X Height interaction effects

Significant interaction effects were observed for hip sagittal (P = 0.002) and transverse (P = 0.002) plane angles. Post-hoc tests revealed no between-group differences inhip sagittal and transverse plane angles for the CAI and the control groups when landing from both heights.

During landing from a 40 cm height compared to 20 cm, greater hip flexion angle was observed for the control (0 to 2%LP, P = 0.002; 11 to 100%LP, P = 0.002) and CAI (0 to 1%LP, P = 0.004; 8 to 100%LP, P = 0.002) groups. Greater hip external rotation angle was observed for the control (0 to 23%LP, P = 0.002) and CAI (0 to 6%LP, P = 0.002) groups.

Group effects

Regardless of landing height, the CAI group exhibited greater ankle inversion angle (1 to 11%LP, P = 0.002; 56 to 100%LP, P = 0.002), as well as less ankle eversion moment (50 to 66%LP, P = 0.002; 95 to 99%LP, p = 0.002) and ankle external rotation moment (86 to 100%LP, P = 0.002).

Height effects

Regardless of the group, when landing from a 40 cm height compared to 20 cm, participants exhibited greater midfoot dorsiflexion (5 to 51%LP, P = 0.006), midfoot inversion (17 to 39%LP, P = 0.007), ankle dorsiflexion (33 to 63%LP, P < 0.001), knee flexion (0 to 77%LP, P < 0.001), hip flexion (8 to 100%LP, P = 0.002), hip abduction (0 to 100%LP, P < 0.001), hip external rotation (0 to 12%LP, P = 0.002) and hallux extension (3 to 59%LP, P < 0.001) angles. Less midfoot internal rotation (9 to 42%LP, P = 0.008), ankle eversion (0 to 2%LP; P = 0.002), ankle internal rotation (6 to 57%LP, P = 0.003), knee external rotation (0 to 60%LP, P = 0.002) and hip flexion (0 to 3%LP, P = 0.002) angles were also observed. No height effects were observed for knee frontal angle.

When landing from a 40 cm height compared to 20 cm, participants also exhibited greater ankle plantarflexion moments (0 to 50%LP, P < 0.001), ankle eversion moments (0 to 16%LP, P = 0.002), ankle external rotation moments (3 to 47%LP, P = 0.002), knee extension moments (0 to 3%LP, P = 0.008; 5 to 51%LP, P < 0.001), knee abduction moments (4 to 26%LP, P < 0.001), knee external rotation moments (0 to 2%LP, P = 0.008; 4 to 7%LP, P = 0.008), greater knee internal rotation moments (11 to 53%LP, P < 0.001), hip flexion moments (0 to 3%LP, P = 0.006), hip extension moments (17 to 20%LP, P = 0.007; 24 to 100%LP, P < 0.001), hip abduction moments (12 to 14%LP, P = 0.008; 20 to 26%LP, P = 0.002) and hip external rotation moments (0 to 3%LP, P = 0.009; 4 to 56%LP, P < 0.001).

Participants also exhibited greater ankle power absorption (0 to 19%LP, P < 0.001; 32 to 78%LP, P < 0.001), knee power absorption (from 0 to 3%LP, P = 0.007; 6 to 21%LP, P < 0.001), knee power generation (31 to 61%LP, P < 0.001), hip power generation (from 1 to 2%LP, P = 0.002), hip power absorption (37 to 94%LP, P < 0.001).

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the biomechanical differences during unilateral drop landing on a laterally inclined surface in individuals with CAI and healthy controls, focusing on the effects of varying landing heights. The main findings highlight significant interaction effects in hip kinematics, along with notable group and height-related effects, particularly regarding ankle biomechanics.

Our study confirmed that individuals with CAI exhibit distinctive biomechanical patterns compared to healthy controls. Consistent with previous findings [20], the CAI group displayed greater ankle inversion angles throughout much of the landing phase, which supports the hypothesis that these individuals are predisposed to at-risk mechanics that may increase the likelihood of recurrent LAS. This excessive inversion likely compromises the stability of the lateral ankle ligaments and places additional stress on the peroneal muscles, both of which are critical in preventing further injury. This is worrisome given that individuals with CAI exhibit lower peroneus longus muscle preactivation during unilateral drop landing on a laterally inclined surface [16], delayed peroneus longus reaction time and eversion strength deficits [35] and lower supination resistance [36]. The reduced ankle eversion and external rotation moments in the CAI group further highlight their impaired ability to counteract inversion forces. These findings are consistent with previous studies that reported deficits in dynamic stability in individuals with CAI during both static balance tasks and dynamic maneuvers [11, 18]. The reduced eversion moments may indicate a diminished capacity for the peroneal muscles activation to limit excessive ankle inversion and thus predispose individuals with CAI to recurrent LAS. Our results suggest that the main biomechanical deficits in CAI are located at the ankle, which was highly expected considering the injury to the ankle joint complex [5].

Regardless of the group, the effects of increased landing height on biomechanical variables were significant across all lower limb joints. As landing height increased, participants adopted active movement pattern adjustments to dissipate higher impact forces. Increased midfoot dorsiflexion, inversion, and external rotation led to reduced ankle inversion and internal rotation as well to increased ankle dorsiflexion angles. These adjustments indicate a more pronated foot and ankle posture when landing from a greater height. These results are particularly interesting given that individuals with CAI are at risk of excessive ankle plantarflexion, inversion, and internal rotation during landing, which can lead to LAS [37]. Our results suggest that, in a controlled landing environment, individuals with CAI demonstrate movement pattern adjustments which could potentially reduce the risk of LAS. Ankle dorsiflexion during landing is considered a protective joint position. Given that the anterior part of the talar trochlea is wider than the posterior part [38], ankle intra-articular pressure increases in a dorsiflexed position [39], thereby enhancing joint stability. This close-packed position of the ankle is believed to protect individuals with CAI during jump landing tasks [11]. Furthermore, the observed increase in ankle eversion moments with increased landing height suggests that, despite the greater loads, participants were able to generate stabilising forces at the ankle to counteract potential inversion forces. The kinematic changes were observed in the foot and ankle resulted in greater knee and hip flexion, hip external rotation, and reduced knee external rotation. Our kinematic results are consistent with those of Zhang et al. [25], who studied the landing biomechanics of a bilateral drop jump onto a laterally and forwardly inclined surface in individuals with CAI. They also observed greater ankle inversion and plantarflexion angles, along with reduced knee and hip flexion angles when landing from a lower height. Additionally, these kinematic changes resulted in greater knee and hip extension moments, as well as increased hip external rotation moments. Consequently, there was increased ankle and knee power absorption and hip power generation during the initial phase of landing. In the later phase, ankle power absorption continued, while the knee began generating power and the hip shifted to power absorption. Previous studies suggested that individuals with CAI tend to adopt a landing strategy relying more on proximal joints (i.e., knee and hip) rather than distal joints (ankle and foot) [25, 40]. This strategy would allow them to rely on more stable lower limb joints to dampen impact loads rather than on their unstable injured ankle. However, during an expected landing on a laterally inclined surface, our results suggest that the landing strategy is highly similar between individuals with CAI and healthy controls when landing height increases, and the between-group differences are mostly located at the distal joints, which is inconsistent with previous findings. Additionally, several biomechanical differences between individuals with CAI and healthy controls were observed during the early post-impact phase. This may suggest alterations in feedforward motor control strategies as an adaptive attempt to better cope with the task demands [16]. These neurologically driven adaptations should be further investigated in future research by integrating electromyographic assessments with kinematic and kinetic analyses.

Limitations

The first limitation of this study is that participants were aware of the inclined landing surface, and biomechanical data were collected in a secured and controlled environment. Since LAS are typically sustained during unexpected perturbations, our results should be interpreted with caution. Notably, Simpson et al. [19, 20] demonstrated that landing biomechanics differs in individuals with CAI when landing on an unexpectedly inclined surface. For instance, when anticipating a laterally inclined surface, ankle inversion increases at initial impact but decreases during the latter part of the landing phase [19], most likely due to a feedforward protective mechanism. Second, the heights of the initial platforms were the same for all participants, and the landing height was not adjusted to account for individual differences in height or leg length. Future research should aim to normalise the height of the initial platform for each participant, especially since our results revealed significant biomechanical differences with changes in landing height. Third, only men were recruited and thus our results may not be generalisable to women. The first 16 participants with CAI were recruited, regardless of their sex. Only male participants responded to our call for participants. The control group was sex-matched with the CAI group. Fourth, the relatively small sample size may have lacked sufficient power to detect significant interaction effects during landing. Fifth, all participants performed the landing tasks barefoot. Although this approach is optimal for isolating biomechanical deficits in individuals with CAI without interference from external supports (e.g., footwear), it does not fully reflect the conditions experienced by most athletes in real sports environments. For instance, sports shoes might attenuate the biomechanical deficits observed during jump landing. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting these results.

Conclusions

This study provides important insights into the biomechanical deficits exhibited by individuals with chronic ankle instability during unilateral drop landings on inclined surfaces from varying heights. Our findings revealed that individuals with chronic ankle instability demonstrate altered lower limb biomechanics, particularly greater ankle inversion angles and reduced ankle eversion moments, which may increase the risk of recurrent lateral ankle sprains. Our results also revealed movement pattern adjustments when landing from greater heights in individuals with and without chronic ankle instability. Future research should continue to explore these biomechanical patterns across various landing conditions and heights to inform rehabilitation strategies aimed at mitigating the risk of recurrent sprains and long-term joint sequelae, such as post-traumatic ankle osteoarthritis.
