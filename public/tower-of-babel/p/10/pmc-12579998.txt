Introduction

Posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) is a commonly employed surgical technique for treating lumbar degenerative disorders, offering favorable clinical outcomes in the majority of cases. However, rare complications such as interbody cage migration may arise, posing significant clinical challenges. Traditionally, surgical retrieval of migrated cages has been performed via open or laparoscopic transperitoneal approaches, both of which provide adequate visualization of the foreign body.

In this report, we present a rare case of anterior cage migration to the posterior aspect of the inferior vena cava (IVC) following PLIF. The cage was successfully retrieved using a laparoscopic retroperitoneal approach, a technique that has not, to the best of our knowledge, been previously described for this indication. This case highlights the feasibility and safety of the retroperitoneal laparoscopic route as a minimally invasive alternative for managing such complex complications.

Case Presentation

An 80‐year‐old female underwent PLIF for lumbar spinal stenosis at our orthopedic department. During the procedure, one 20 × 10 mm titanium cage was found to have migrated anteriorly. Despite this intraoperative finding, the orthopedic surgeon proceeded with the placement of a second cage without retrieving the migrated implant, based on an assessment that the risk of subsequent complications was low.

However, contrast‐enhanced computed tomography (CT) performed on postoperative day 1 revealed that the migrated cage was located anterior to the spine, positioned between the IVC and the two right renal arteries. (Figure 1) The patient was promptly referred to our department for further evaluation and management. At the time of referral, she exhibited no neurological deficits, hemodynamic instability, or radiological evidence of vascular injury. Nonetheless, given the potential risk of delayed vascular complications due to the cage's proximity to major vessels, we elected to proceed with emergency laparoscopic removal.

Under general anesthesia, the patient was placed in the right lateral decubitus jackknife position. The retroperitoneal space was accessed via a small open incision, followed by expansion using a preperitoneal dissecting balloon. Three laparoscopic ports were inserted according to the configuration shown in Figure 2. After removing the flank pad, we incised the lateral conal fascia to expose the iliacus muscle. A localized protrusion on its surface, suspected to be the migrated cage, was identified (Figure 3a). Careful dissection of the surrounding connective tissue revealed the entire cage (Figure 3b,c). Following confirmation that there was no vascular injury or significant adhesion to adjacent structures, the cage was successfully and safely extracted (Figure 3d). Intraoperative fluoroscopy and ultrasound were available, and conversion to open surgery was considered but ultimately unnecessary.

The total operative time was 63 min, with minimal blood loss and no intraoperative complications. The patient's postoperative recovery was uneventful. She was discharged after completing rehabilitation and remained asymptomatic at her 6‐month follow‐up, with no evidence of surgical or vascular complications.

Discussion

In this case, we successfully performed laparoscopic retroperitoneal removal of an anteriorly migrated interbody cage located posterior to the IVC, without intraoperative or postoperative complications.

PLIF is a well‐established surgical technique for treating lumbar spinal stenosis and degenerative spondylolisthesis. The procedure involves decompression of the spinal canal, followed by insertion of a titanium cage between the vertebral bodies and stabilization using pedicle screws and rods. Although cage migration is a recognized complication, it typically involves posterior displacement or subsidence. Anterior migration is exceedingly rare, with an incidence reported to be approximately 0.26% [1].

When anterior displacement of a cage is identified intraoperatively, it is generally recommended to leave the additional cage in place and proceed with the surgery, unless changes in vital signs suggest a major vascular injury. However, postoperative CT imaging should be obtained as soon as possible, and close monitoring is essential. Ceylan et al. reported three cases, and Kumar et al. reported one case that were managed conservatively, all without long‐term complications [2, 3]. In contrast, Murase et al. advocated removal of the dislodged cage because of the potential for serious long‐term risks. In their series of 12 cases of anterior migration, three cages were removed intraoperatively through the same surgical field, seven were removed postoperatively via reoperation, and two were managed conservatively [1].

Delayed intervention may lead to serious complications, including life‐threatening hemorrhage due to adhesions between the cage and major blood vessels. Several reports have documented massive bleeding in patients who underwent removal procedures months after the initial surgery [4, 5]. These findings underscore the importance of early surgical removal of migrated cages, even in asymptomatic cases.

Various surgical approaches have been described for cage removal, involving orthopedic, urologic, and general surgical teams. Most reported cases have been managed via open laparotomy. In 2017, Okuyama et al. [6] reported the first successful laparoscopic transperitoneal removal of a migrated cage. In that case, the cage had migrated below the bifurcation of the aorta and IVC and was extracted using a five‐port technique by general surgeons. However, since then, there have been no further reports of laparoscopic cage removal, suggesting that this approach is not yet widely adopted.

The retroperitoneal laparoscopic approach, commonly employed in nephrectomy and adrenalectomy, offers several advantages over the transperitoneal route. These include reduced postoperative pain, shorter recovery time [7], and direct access to retroperitoneal vascular structures, including the renal vessels [8]. In our case, the migrated cage was situated behind the IVC. A transperitoneal approach would have required extensive retraction of intra‐abdominal organs, increasing surgical invasiveness and the risk of complications. In contrast, the retroperitoneal approach provided a direct and minimally invasive route to the foreign body, allowing safe removal without the need for conversion to open surgery. Thus, in situations where foreign bodies are located posterior to the IVC, renal vessels, or other retroperitoneal structures, a retroperitoneal approach is considered useful.

Nonetheless, the retroperitoneal approach has certain limitations. The confined working space and absence of prominent anatomical landmarks make the procedure technically challenging [9]. In the event of intraoperative hemorrhage, it may be difficult to maintain adequate visualization, potentially necessitating the abandonment of the laparoscopic approach. Therefore, this technique may be contraindicated in patients with suspected vascular injury (e.g., hematoma on imaging) or those presenting long after the initial surgery, when adhesions are more likely. In such cases, laparotomy remains the preferred option.

Conclusion

For urologists and surgeons experienced in retroperitoneal procedures, laparoscopic retroperitoneal removal of a migrated interbody cage can be a useful alternative to conventional open or transperitoneal approaches in selected cases. This technique may offer reduced surgical morbidity and faster recovery when appropriately applied in selected cases.
