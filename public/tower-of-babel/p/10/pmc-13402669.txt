Entanglement area law in interacting bosons from the Bose-Hubbard model to ϕ4 theory and beyond
Authors: Donghoon Kim, Tomotaka Kuwahara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe entanglement area law is a universal principle that characterizes quantum many-body phases and underpins tensor network algorithms. Traditionally, its validity has been limited to systems with short-range interactions and bounded local energy. Achieving a complete generalization that removes both of these constraints has been a longstanding goal in quantum many-body theory, especially for interacting boson systems where unbounded energy presents intrinsic difficulties. In this work, we rigorously prove the area law for one-dimensional interacting boson systems with long-range interactions, covering broad models including the Bose-Hubbard and ϕ4 classes. Furthermore, we establish an efficiency guarantee for Matrix-Product-State approximations of the ground states, offering a practical route to numerical simulation. One of our main technical contributions is a general method for Hilbert space dimension reduction, whose applicability extends to arbitrary spatial dimensions. These results address two major challenges simultaneously and provide important foundations for simulating long-range cold atomic systems.Subject terms: Quantum information, Condensed-matter physics, Information theory and computation, Computational science

Introduction

In modern physics, one of the biggest challenges is figuring out how to accurately simulate systems with many interacting particles. This problem shows up in various fields, like condensed matter physics, high-energy physics, and statistical mechanics. To tackle this, researchers study a field called Hamiltonian complexity1–3, which tries to reveal general rules that explain how particles in these systems possess complex structures from the information-theoretic viewpoint. A main focus in this field is the ground state, which is the system’s lowest-energy state at absolute zero temperature, where quantum effects are most pronounced. One of the key discoveries about the ground state is the entanglement area law4,5. It states that when a system is divided into two parts, the entanglement entropy scales with the surface area of the partition rather than the volume. The area law is deeply related to the structural complexity of ground states6 and is a fundamental ansatz in tensor network algorithms7,8, which are some of the most widely used tools for running simulations. Proving the area law and understanding the complexity of ground states have become landmark achievements in quantum information theory.

Over the past two decades, there has been significant progress in our understanding of one-dimensional (1D) ground states. The first rigorous proof of the entanglement area law in 1D systems was provided by Hastings in 2007, marking a major milestone9. Since then, qualitative improvements have been made, particularly by Arad and colleagues, who refined the approach10–12. In addition, Brandão and collaborators made notable attempts to prove the area law based solely on the exponential decay of correlations in these systems13,14. Currently, the proof of the area law for gapped ground states is predominantly achieved through the formalism known as Approximate Ground State Projection (AGSP)11,12. This formalism became a crucial stepping stone, leading to the development of a polynomial-time algorithm for computing ground states15,16. As of now, 1D systems represent the most successful application of these advancements.

Previous proofs of the area law have primarily relied on two basic conditions9,11,12,17,18: (i) the short-range interactions, and (ii) bounded local energy, as summarized in Fig. 1. While these conditions are universally valid in common spin and fermion systems, it is well-known that physical systems violating these conditions are also ubiquitous. As the violation of condition (i), long-range interactions are characterized by a power-law decay of the interaction strength, which, for instance, has been experimentally observed in cold atomic systems19–26. Understanding how these interactions increase complexity compared to short-range interactions has been a fundamental challenge in Hamiltonian complexity27–33. On the other hand, as the violation of condition (ii), systems with unbounded local energy are well-represented by interacting boson systems. These systems remain largely unexplored, though recent advancements have been made in understanding them from the perspective of information propagation34–37. The most representative example of interacting boson systems is the Bose–Hubbard model, which serves as the minimal model for describing cold atomic systems38. Another notable example is the ϕ4 model, a fundamental model in lattice quantum field theory39–41. This model is equivalent to anharmonic oscillators, including nonlinear terms42–44, and is one of the most well-studied models in high-energy theory and statistical mechanics.

Entanglement area law for one-dimensional quantum systems: limitations and generalization.

Inner box: Short-range interactions and bounded local energies. Previous rigorous results on the entanglement area law have been limited to systems with short-range interactions and bounded local Hilbert space dimensions or local energies. Paradigmatic examples include spin chains and fermionic models. In these settings, the area law ensures that the entanglement entropy of ground states scales with the boundary, not the volume, of a subsystem. Outer box: Long-range interactions and unbounded local energies. Lifting these foundational constraints is crucial for understanding a broad class of physically relevant systems, such as bosonic quantum fields in cold atom experiments and lattice quantum field theories, where both long-range interactions and unbounded local Hilbert spaces naturally arise. In this work, we rigorously establish the entanglement area law for one-dimensional bosonic systems with power-law decaying interactions and unbounded local energies (Result 2 below), providing a rigorous step toward addressing this longstanding challenge. Our results also provide a theoretical foundation for the efficient and accurate representation of ground states using Matrix Product States (MPS), even in these general settings (Result 3 below).

Proving the area law in systems that break the aforementioned limitations has long been a major goal, and various studies have been conducted in this direction. For example, in one-dimensional spin systems, generalization to long-range interacting systems has been achieved29,45. However, many unresolved aspects remain when it comes to boson systems. This is partly due to the fact that there exist counterexamples that break the area law conjecture, such as the Bose–Hubbard model with attractive interactions. For example, by considering a Bose–Hubbard model such that H=J(b1†b2+h.c.)−V(n^1+n^2)2+∑i≠1,2Un^i(n^i−1) (V, U > 0), all the bosons concentrate on the sites 1 and 2. Hence, as long as the total boson number is proportional to the system size n, the entanglement entropy between the site 1 and the other sites can be as large as log(n)/2. Previous research on the boson area law has been largely limited to non-interacting boson systems46,47. Recent breakthroughs have demonstrated that interactions between bosons and other bounded fields (but excluding boson-boson interactions) can be efficiently handled36, from which the area law can be proven under finite-range interactions48. Still, a unified understanding of generic interacting bosonic systems, encompassing key models such as the Bose–Hubbard and ϕ4 classes, remains a completely open problem.

In this study, we resolve the one-dimensional area law conjecture for general interacting boson systems, including the Bose–Hubbard and ϕ4 classes. Our results hold even in the presence of long-range interactions, where the interaction decay rate must be faster than r−2 for the area law to hold. This allows us to establish the area law in the most general cases without the two primary conditions—short-range interactions and bounded local energy. Our results quantitatively show how the boson parameters and long-range interaction parameters, such as the power-law decay rate, influence the entanglement structures (see Eq. (13) below). In addition, we provide a general upper bound on the bond dimension required to describe these ground states using Matrix Product States (MPS). Our result serves as a theoretical foundation for simulating long-range interacting cold atomic systems, which constitute a major platform for modern quantum technologies49,50.

A central challenge in analyzing interacting bosonic systems is the infinite dimensionality of the local Hilbert space. Indeed, as highlighted in several prior works34,36,37,39, providing a rigorous and general framework for dimension reduction—with explicit control over the induced error—has long been recognized as one of the most fundamental and difficult tasks in the field. To rigorously establish the area law, it is essential to quantitatively control the ground-state boson number distribution and to implement dimension reduction in a controlled way. Although truncating the local Hilbert space is a standard prescription in both numerical and theoretical studies, it has traditionally been applied without rigorous guarantees on its validity or accuracy. One of our main technical contributions is to develop a general framework for dimension reduction that can accommodate different types of boson statistics and interaction structures. In particular, we establish analytic tools tailored to both the Bose–Hubbard and the ϕ4 models (Results 1.1 and 1.2 below), each of which requires distinct theoretical approaches due to their differing physical and mathematical structures.

Crucially, when combining long-range interactions with bosonic degrees of freedom, an additional layer of difficulty arises. In such systems, a straightforward dimension reduction fails because preserving the ground state demands an excessively large cutoff. As a result, energy and entanglement entropy grow logarithmically with the cutoff, making the area law inapplicable. To address this, our framework implements a site-dependent and carefully controlled dimension reduction scheme. We further reconstruct and generalize the effective Hamiltonian theory developed in ref. 51, adapting it to account for the non-uniformity and correlations induced by long-range interactions. This allows us to rigorously maintain the validity of the area law even for bosonic systems with power-law decaying interactions, overcoming obstacles that are absent in either short-range or spin systems.

Our work opens the unified theoretical framework capable of addressing the combined challenges of long-range interactions and unbounded local energy in quantum many-body systems. By integrating these two sources of complexity, our approach enables rigorous analysis and simulation techniques that were previously out of reach.

We emphasize that our analysis is restricted to the repulsive regime, where on-site interactions suppress clustering tendencies and keep the boson number suitably controlled. In contrast, attractive interactions can favor the accumulation of particles on a single site, leading to collapse- or clustering-type behavior. Such a gathering qualitatively changes the boson number distribution, preventing the effective Hilbert space dimension from remaining small, and may invalidate the concentration bounds underlying our proof. Consequently, the attractive regime admits ground states with a qualitatively different entanglement structure from the repulsive case, and there exist ground states that violate the entanglement area law.

Notably, our model includes interaction-induced tunneling terms of the form bi(n^i+n^i′)bi′†, where the tunneling amplitude depends explicitly on the occupation numbers. Such interactions are known to violate the Lieb–Robinson bound (Theorem 3 in ref. 52), meaning that information propagation can, in principle, occur at infinite speed. Surprisingly, however, our results show that even in the presence of these interactions—where dynamical locality is lost—the ground state can still satisfy the entanglement area law. This highlights a fundamental distinction between the locality of information propagation (dynamical locality) and the static locality properties of ground-state entanglement. In fact, the Lieb–Robinson bound evaluated with respect to a specific state, such as a gapped ground state, can exhibit much slower effective propagation than the state-independent bound, and its violation is determined by a different criterion. This state-dependent perspective on dynamical locality helps explain why a distinction emerges between the loss of general dynamical locality and the possibility that the ground-state entanglement can still remain at an area-law scale.

By forbidding odd-power terms, it prevents bosonic degrees of freedom from concentrating around large-field configurations. This allows uniform control of boson-number expectation values, which is essential for establishing the concentration bounds used in our proof. This assumption is compatible with standard realizations of ϕ4-type systems, including superconducting Kerr oscillators, symmetric double-well optical-lattice potentials, and the conventional lattice ϕ4 field theory, where the effective potential remains even unless an external bias or magnetic field induces a linear term that breaks the symmetry. In the ϕ4 classes with the parity symmetry, the gapped phase exhibits the physical character of a massive disordered (paramagnetic) state: the field fluctuates around the symmetric configuration and exhibits no long-range order, so correlations remain short-ranged.

As an analytical challenge, we can see that the term ϕi4 allows an infinite number of bosons to sit on a single site at the lowest-energy state. Indeed, even with an infinitesimally small perturbation, the ground state of the ϕ4 model becomes unstable in the sense that an infinite number of bosons sit on a single site. For example, if we consider a single-site model (n = 1) with the Hamiltonian ϕ4+ϕ2+π2+ϵ(b†n^b†+bn^b) for an arbitrary ϵ > 0, the ground state is not well-defined due to the instability. This prohibits any perturbative approaches to analyze the boson number distribution in the ϕ4 class, and hence we employ completely different analyses from the case of the Bose–Hubbard class.

As a nontrivial feature of the ϕ4 model, only the existence of a spectral gap guarantees low boson density at each site, but only in the presence of parity symmetry. In contrast, without parity symmetry, a spectral gap alone cannot prevent the accumulation of an extensive number of bosons on a single site. This underlines the crucial role of symmetry in ensuring the stability and localization properties of the ground state (see also the discussion below Theorem 2 in Supplementary Note 5).

Bosonic concentration bound

As our first main result, we show the concentration bound on the boson number distribution at an arbitrary site, where the tail of the distribution is characterized by (sub)exponential decay. In practical numerical simulations of bosonic systems, it is unavoidable to truncate the local boson number at each site, based on the empirical observation that the occupation number distribution decays rapidly. However, such truncations have traditionally lacked rigorous error bounds or theoretical justification. The concentration bounds established in this section provide a systematic and quantitative foundation for dimension reduction in bosonic models: they ensure that the numerical truncation is both justified and accurate.

We stress that all results presented in this section apply to bosonic systems of arbitrary spatial dimension and remain valid even when the interactions are long-range. Thus, our concentration bounds provide rigorous guarantees for a wide class of quantum lattice models far beyond conventional settings.

Discussion

In this work, we have established the entanglement area law and MPS efficiency guarantees for general interacting bosonic systems, including those with long-range interactions and unbounded local energies. Our results provide theoretical foundations for the validity of existing numerical methods in studying the Bose–Hubbard models53,54 and related quantum field theories55,56.

There are still several open questions. In the Bose–Hubbard class, we have focused on repulsive interactions, leaving the entanglement structure in the attractive case largely unknown. For models with conserved total boson number (e.g., the standard Bose–Hubbard model), there is a trivial logarithmic violation of the entanglement area law. However, it is unclear whether (sub)volume entanglement scaling can occur under a spectral gap assumption. In the ϕ4 class, it remains an open problem to remove the parity symmetry assumption. As shown in Supplementary Information (Theorem 2 therein), a finite bound on the position operator {ϕi}i∈Λ enables the derivation of a concentration bound (Eq. (9)). A key challenge lies in proving this boundedness under only the spectral gap assumption.

Furthermore, the concentration bounds on the boson number probability apply to systems in arbitrary dimensions. Although the area law conjecture is still intractable in high dimensions, our results provide an essential foundation for future resolutions of the area law in high-dimensional boson systems. At the moment, by imposing additional assumptions—such as the existence of an adiabatic path to non-interacting models—we expect to prove a bosonic area law in higher dimensions by employing the Small-Incremental-Entangling (SIE) theorem57.

Finally, developing a quasi-polynomial-time algorithm for simulating interacting boson systems with long-range interactions is also an important goal. Currently, efficient algorithms for one-dimensional ground states exist only for Hamiltonians with short-range interactions and bounded local energy15,16. Extending these algorithms to the general setting and resolving the time complexity for generic interacting boson systems remains a major open problem in quantum many-body physics.

Among the open directions, the most tractable with current techniques appears to be establishing an area law in higher dimensions under the assumption of an adiabatic path to a trivial gapped system. Since our work already proves suppression of boson number distributions in arbitrary dimensions, combining this with the SIE theorem provides a concrete route toward such a generalization. Furthermore, beyond the entropy itself, analyzing the dynamics of the full entanglement spectrum may reduce the quasi-polynomial complexity of long-range interacting boson systems to polynomial complexity58, providing a potential pathway toward the development of polynomial-time algorithms for interacting bosonic systems.

Proof sketch of Result 2 (entanglement area law)

In this section, we provide a brief summary of the proof of the area law for one-dimensional bosonic systems with long-range interactions (see Fig. 4). We consider a one-dimensional lattice defined on Z, where each site i is labeled by its position x∈Z. The system is bipartitioned into left and right semi-infinite subsystems, L = ( − ∞, 0] and R = [1, ∞), and we study the entanglement entropy of the ground state Ω across this bipartition.

Proof strategy for the one-dimensional bosonic area law with long-range interactions.

The diagram outlines how the proof of Result 2 reduces a long-range bosonic Hamiltonian with unbounded local Hilbert spaces to a bounded effective model amenable to the approximate ground-state projector (AGSP) method. The top box recalls the AGSP principle, namely that a sufficiently accurate AGSP with controlled Schmidt rank implies an entanglement area law. The proof begins from a general k-local bosonic Hamiltonian with a subexponentially decaying local boson-number distribution and power-law interactions. First, a site-dependent boson-number truncation H→H¯ is introduced, with cutoffs Nx growing polylogarithmically with the distance from the bipartition boundary; this controls the local boson number while preserving the ground state and the spectral gap up to controlled error. Second, an interaction truncation H¯→Ht reduces the Hamiltonian to block-local terms and nearest-neighbor inter-block couplings, with the induced error controlled by the block number q, the block length l, and the interaction decay. Third, a block-wise multi-energy truncation Ht→H~t makes the effective Hamiltonian bounded while preserving the low-energy sector up to a subexponentially small error in the cutoff τ. A Chebyshev-polynomial AGSP construction can then be applied to H~t, yielding the quantitative area law in Result 2.

Our approach is based on the framework of approximate ground state projectors (AGSPs), originally introduced for systems with short-range interactions11,12, and subsequently extended to long-range interacting systems29. An AGSP operator K approximates the ground state projector ΩΩ, exhibiting a trade-off between its Schmidt rank and approximation accuracy (see Supplementary Note 7.A). The central objective is to construct an AGSP with low Schmidt rank and high fidelity, which enables the construction of a low-entangled approximation to the ground state (see Lemma 24 in the Supplementary Information). In this work, we further extend the AGSP framework to encompass unbounded bosonic Hamiltonians, under the assumption that the boson number distribution exhibits subexponential decay (Eq. (10)).

For this purpose, we perform a sequence of controlled truncations that systematically reduce the original long-range, unbounded bosonic Hamiltonian to a bounded, quasi-local, and gapped form suitable for the application of standard area law techniques. Concretely, we transform the original Hamiltonian H through the following stages:

First, a boson-number truncation is performed to bound the local particle number on each site, effectively rendering the local Hilbert space finite-dimensional. Next, we apply an interaction truncation to remove long-range couplings beyond adjacent blocks near the bipartition boundary, thereby localizing the relevant interactions. Finally, a multi-energy truncation is used to constrain the intra-block energies at a prescribed cutoff, ensuring that the total Hamiltonian becomes bounded. At each step, the truncation parameters are carefully chosen to control the approximation error and to preserve a positive spectral gap, as rigorously quantified in Propositions 25–26 and Theorem 4 in the Supplementary Information.

Once this reduction is achieved, the resulting Hamiltonian H~t is both bounded and quasi-local, allowing us to apply the standard Chebyshev-polynomial-based AGSP construction developed for short-range systems. This ultimately establishes the one-dimensional entanglement area law for the original class of long-range, unbounded bosonic Hamiltonians.

A major difficulty arises from the fact that the unbounded Hamiltonian Ht renders the imaginary-time evolution eβHtOe−βHt ill-defined due to divergence. For bounded local Hamiltonians, robustness of the ground state under truncation is typically proven by controlling imaginary-time evolution, bounding expressions of the form eβHtOe−βHt via convergent expansions in multi-commutators51. This approach crucially relies on the fact that adHtm(O) grows at most factorially (or more generally, in a way that still yields a finite bound after summing ∑mβmadHtm(O)/m!). In bosonic systems, however Ht is unbounded, and multi-commutators exhibit super-factorial growth of the form adHtm(O)≲(Tmm)m with Tm increasing in m, so the imaginary-time dressing eβadHt(O) fails to be well controlled and may diverge. To overcome this difficulty, we follow the alternative strategy of ref. 60, which avoids imaginary-time evolution altogether. Instead of using eβHt, the analysis employs a polynomial energy filter (E+θ−Ht)m, where θ > 0 denotes the energy separation between the low-energy sector Πt,≤E and the excited sector under consideration. Expanding this filter in multi-commutators and optimizing over the degree m yields explicit bounds on matrix elements of the form Πt,[E+θ,E+θ+T0]OΠt,≤E. These bounds decay subexponentially in θ. They are sufficient to show that the multi-energy truncated projector Π~ captures almost all of the ground-state weight. Consequently, the effective Hamiltonian H~t=Π~HtΠ~ preserves the ground state and the spectral gap up to a subexponentially small error in the cutoff parameter τ.

We point out that ref. 48 directly applied the robustness theorem from ref. 12—originally proved for bounded Hamiltonians—to the case of unbounded bosonic systems. However, such an application does not directly extend to the unbounded setting: the key technical assumptions and estimates used in the bounded case do not necessarily hold for unbounded Hamiltonians, which require different analytical tools (see Supplementary Note 10.B for a detailed discussion). This is because the proof techniques for bounded Hamiltonians rely crucially on norm estimates and operator inequalities that can break down in the unbounded setting, necessitating a different analytical approach. In our work, we resolve this issue by employing a framework tailored to unbounded Hamiltonians60, which allows us to rigorously control the ground state and spectral gap under energy truncation in our setup. As a consequence, the truncation error ϵ′ in our setting exhibits subexponential decay in the energy cutoff τ, in contrast to the exponential decay observed in bounded cases. This difference reflects the essential analytical distinction between bounded spin models and unbounded bosonic models and underscores the necessity of a refined treatment in the present setting.

Finally, with the effective Hamiltonian H~t at hand, we follow the same strategy as in refs. 12,29 to establish the area law by constructing an AGSP based on Chebyshev polynomials. The approximation error of the AGSP scales as e−mΔ/∥H~t∥ (see Lemma 27 in the Supplementary Information), where m is the degree of the polynomial and Δ denotes the spectral gap. The corresponding Schmidt rank is also shown to remain within acceptable bounds (see Lemma 28). By optimizing all relevant parameters—including the boson-number cutoff profile, the number and size of the blocks, and the polynomial degree—we construct an AGSP that satisfies the desired approximation properties (see Lemma 24 and Proposition 29). Iterating this AGSP yields an upper bound on the entanglement entropy (Lemma 33) and establishes a quantitative bound on the Schmidt rank required to approximate the ground state (Proposition 30), thereby completing the proof of the entanglement area law for one-dimensional bosonic systems with long-range interactions.
