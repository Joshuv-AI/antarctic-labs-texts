Possibility of brewery wastes application to soil as an organic improver of biological and chemical properties
Authors: Jakub Kostecki, Andrzej Greinert
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil degradation, marked by declining organic matter, threatens global food security. The impact of brewer's spent yeast (BSY) on clay and sand was analysed at varying application rates to assess its effectiveness in improving soil quality. A randomized complete block design with three replicates was employed. One kilogram of soil were mixed with BSY at application rates of 2 t/ha and 5 t/ha. The samples were incubated at 26 °C for 5 months with daily watering. We analysed pH, total nitrogen, organic carbon, total phosphorus, and electrical conductivity (EC); microbial activity (total heterotrophic bacteria, actinobacteria, and fungi) and soil enzyme activity (dehydrogenase, catalase, protease). BSY application improved soil quality, particularly in clay. Clay showed increased in pH, EC, N and C. BSY significantly boosted microbial populations (bacteria, fungi) in clay with a lesser effect in sand. Enzyme activity and a fertility index also improved in BSY-treated clay, while sand displayed increased activity of a different enzyme. Results suggest BSY holds promise as an organic fertilizer, especially for clay soils. Further research is needed to optimize application, understand long-term effects, and evaluate economic feasibility and social acceptance. This study contributes to the search for sustainable, local solutions to improve soil health and agricultural practices.Keywords: Brewery wastes, Brewer's spent yeast (BSY), Soil fertilisation, R10 process, Circular economySubject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental chemistry, Environmental impact

Introduction

Soil degradation is a pressing global issue, affecting approximately 15% of Earth's ice-free land surface1. In regions such as Asia, nearly 40% of soils are degraded. This results in a loss of soil fertility, structure, water-holding capacity, and organic matter content, rendering the soil more susceptible to erosion. Furthermore, wind and water erosion contribute to the problem, resulting in the loss of vital topsoil and a decline in agricultural productivity (Lal2).

A significant contributor to soil degradation is the reduced availability of organic fertilisers traditionally used to improve soil quality. This is due to declining livestock populations and changes in breeding practices, particularly in areas with decreased profitability of livestock farming3. Furthermore, the intensification of agricultural practices, characterised by monoculture cropping systems and the excessive use of inorganic fertilisers, can deplete soil organic matter over time4. This creates a vicious cycle, as healthy soil with adequate organic matter content is crucial for retaining nutrients applied through inorganic fertilisers (Dondini, 2008). It has been shown that organic fertilisers have lower environmental impacts than mineral fertilisers in several key categories (including impacts on climate change, nutrient pollution, fossil fuel use and water use), suggesting potential benefits for sustainable agriculture5.

Interest in organic fertilization has fluctuated in Poland over the past decade, with recent increases in the area fertilized. Unfortunately, there has been a parallel decline in the availability of manure (Fig. 1). The rising cost of traditional fertilisers further complicates the issue. In Poland, for instance, there has been an upward trend in the consumption of fertilisers in recent years, despite public awareness and changes in eating habits that might lead to reduced demand (GUS6). This highlights the need for cost-effective local alternatives that can address soil degradation and support sustainable agricultural practices.The R10 process, outlined in the EU Waste Management Framework Directive7, offers a promising solution. R10 refers to land treatment involving various waste materials, including organic ones, applied for agricultural benefit. This aligns perfectly with the concept of seeking local alternatives to manure fertilization, as advocated by FAO3,8.

The expansion of the craft and industrial beer production sectors has led to an increase in the generation of yeast slurry, a by-product of the fermentation process. This waste stream, known as Brewer's Spent Yeast (BSY), presents a potentially valuable resource for agricultural use under the R10 process. BSY is a complex waste product, with its structure and condition varying depending on the fermentation process employed by the brewery10,11. Despite this complexity, BSY holds promise as an organic and mineral fertilizer due to its high content of mineral compounds12,13.

Early research indicates that BSY may offer several potential benefits for soil health and plant growth. Studies have demonstrated that BSY can act as a slow-release energy source for soil microorganisms, thereby promoting overall soil health by enhancing microbial activity11. Additionally, BSY contains essential plant nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, which directly contribute to plant growth and development14. Furthermore, BSY contains valuable vitamins and growth factors, including B vitamins and auxins, which can significantly enhance plant health and stress tolerance11,15, 16.

However, research on BSY as a fertilizer is still in its early stages. Although studies by Qiu et al.17, Amenkhienarr and Isitekhale18, and Assandri et al.15 indicate that BSY can enhance crop yields and positively impact soil conditions, significant knowledge gaps remain. Determining the optimal application rates for BSY on various soil types is essential for its effective and sustainable use as a fertilizer. Furthermore, investigations into the potential long-term effects on soil ecosystems and interactions with other agricultural practices, such as herbicide or pesticide application, are of great importance.

The European Union places a high priority on the implementation of sustainable waste management practices, as outlined in the Framework Directive on Waste7. This directive emphasises the importance of waste prevention, reuse, recycling, and agricultural use over disposal in landfills. A recent study by Nuss et al.19 has demonstrated the positive environmental impact of effective municipal waste management, highlighting a reduction in greenhouse gas emissions, particularly methane from landfills.

A number of organic materials, including manure, compost, and sewage sludge, have demonstrated the potential to enhance soil fertility20–22. They offer benefits such as increased nutrient levels, enhanced water retention, and reduced availability of harmful metals in contaminated soils23–25. The application of biodegradable waste to degraded soils can improve soil quality, increase plant growth and yield, and sequester carbon dioxide26,27.

However, challenges exist for these established organic fertilizers. Manure availability can be limited due to declining livestock numbers, while concerns regarding potential contamination with pathogens or antibiotics remain. Compost production requires infrastructure and careful management to ensure optimal quality and avoid odor issues. The application of sewage sludge is subject to stringent regulations due to the potential presence of heavy metals.

BSY represents a potentially more manageable alternative. Breweries are often concentrated in urban or peri-urban areas, which makes BSY transportation to agricultural land potentially more efficient than transporting bulky manure or compost. Additionally, the controlled environment of a brewery allows for better control over BSY composition, which may minimise contamination risks.

The development of clear guidelines for BSY use, including quality standards and application protocols, will be crucial for its widespread adoption. It is clear that collaboration between researchers, policymakers, the brewing industry, and agricultural communities will be essential for navigating regulatory hurdles and facilitating the implementation of BSY as a sustainable fertiliser solution.

The aim of this research was to experimentally demonstrate the viability of brewery waste as an organic fertiliser by examining its impact on diverse soil types at varying concentrations. In order to achieve this, a number of factors were closely monitored, including soil fertility, microbial activity, carbon and nitrogen content, and soil pH. The objective was to demonstrate the potential for a more sustainable and environmentally responsible agricultural future. While some challenges remain, the potential benefits for soil health, waste management, and affordability make this pursuit worthwhile.

Materials and methods

Experimental design

The experiment was conducted in a randomized complete block design using pots filled with 1 kg of each treatment mixtures replicated three times. We’ve use 2 types of soil: sand and clay taken from suburban area of Zielona Gora (city located in western part of Poland 51° 56′ 07′′ N, 15° 30′ 13″ E). All samples were mixed with BSY at a rate of 2 t/ha and 5 t/ha (S-2 and S-5 for sand and C-2 and C-5 for clay respectively). Additionally, for comparison, a control sample was also prepared, containing only soil without the addition of BSY (S-0 for sand and C-0 for clay). All samples were prepared in triplicate (3 blank samples, 3 samples with 2t/ha addition, 3 samples with 5t/ha addition for each soil type, 18 samples in total).

All samples were incubated at temperature 26 °C for 5 months, the pots were daily watered with distilled water to maintain 60% of their water holding capacity. Upon completing the research, further samples were extracted from the pots for laboratory analysis.

The obtained results of laboratory tests were subjected to statistical analysis (basic descriptive statistics and ANOVA analysis of variance) with Statistica (13.3 by TIBCO Software Inc.).

Analytical methods

Air-dried soils were sieved through a sieve with a mesh diameter of 2 mm and their particle size distribution was determined using the Casagrande method (Mocek et al.28). To determine the chemical properties and composition, the samples were ground in a porcelain mortar. The thus prepared samples were determined as follow: the pH values of soil solution were measured by the using a pH-meter WTW inoLab pH/ION 7320 (Xylem Analytics Germany GmbH, Welheim, Germany). Soil solution was prepared in the ratio of 1:2.5 with distilled water. Total nitrogen was determined following to the standard ISO 11261:2002 and organic carbon—TOC-V CSN Shimatzu with SSM-5000A. Total phosphorus was determined with colorimetric method by the using a Hach Lange DR 3900 (Germany).

In the soil samples collected, the abundance of the following groups of microorganisms was analysed in the beginning and at the end of cultivation, using the serial dilution method29: total heterotrophic specific bacteria—Merck standard agar medium; total Actinobacteria—Pochon's medium30, total moulds—Martin medium. All groups of microorganisms were expressed in colony forming units (cfu) after conversion to 1 g of soil dry matter (cfu g−1 soil DM) at a dilution of 105 cfu for total bacteria and 104 cfu for Actinobacteria and fungi. The incubation time was 5 days for bacteria, 5 days for fungi and 7 days for Actinobacteria.

The following were also determined in the samples analysed: dehydrogenase activity—spectrophotometric at 485 nm (Novospec spectrophotometer), catalase activity—by manganometry (Johnson and Temple 1964), protease activity—spectrophotometric at 578 nm (Novospec spectrophotometer). Results

Effects of brewery waste on soil chemical properties

The pH of the BSY samples was acidic (pH 5.64) and did not affect the pH of the soils analysed, regardless of the dose applied. The electrolytic conductivity of the tested soils was similar (average conductivity of the clay was 437 uS/cm and sand—390 uS/cm). Noticeable differences were observed in sand, both at a dose of 2t/ha and 5 t/ha. In clay, a dose of 2t/ha caused no visible changes.

The samples analysed were characterised by varying nitrogen contents (Table 1). The average nitrogen content of the clay was 0.22%, with a minimum of 0.11% and a maximum of 0.28%. For sand it was 0.05%, with a minimum of 0.03% and a maximum of 0.07%. In the samples analysed, there was a marked increase in the nitrogen content of the clay after the application of the BSY. There were no differences in the increase due to the fertiliser application rate. In the sand, the nitrogen content remained similar to the control samples, which may be related to the soil texture.

The samples analysed (both soil and BSY) were characterised by different carbon contents (Table 1). The average carbon content of clay was 2.8%, with a minimum of 2.6% and a maximum of 3.1%. For sand it was 0.25%, with a minimum of 0.16% and a maximum of 0.32%. In the samples analysed, there was a slight increase in the organic carbon content of clay after application of the BSY. Slight differences in the increase due to the fertiliser application rate can be seen (higher content after application of 5 t/ha; differences were 0.06–0.4%). In sand, the carbon content remained similar to the control samples, which may be related to the soil texture. The carbon/nitrogen ratio in the analysed samples decreased in clay (from 21 to 10–11), in sand the decrease was less pronounced (from 4.3 to 4.9 up to 5.1, depending on the application rate).

The samples analysed were characterised by varying phosphorus contents (Table 1). The average content of this element in clay was 3.8%, with a minimum of 1,5 and a maximum of 6.0%. For sand it was 0.8%, with a minimum of 3% and a maximum of 15%. In the samples analysed, there was an apparent increase in the phosphorus content of the clay after application of the BSY. A dose of 2t/ha resulted in a twofold increase in phosphorus content and a dose of 5t/ha resulted in a threefold increase. In sand, the phosphorus content also increased and the effect of fertiliser dose became apparent, maintaining the trend found in clay—the 2t dose resulted in a twofold increase and the 5t/ha dose resulted in a threefold increase in the phosphorus content of the soil analysed.

Abundance of selected groups of micro-organisms

The following graphs illustrate the changes in abundance of selected microbial groups in the soil over time (Figs. 2 and 3). A two-factor analysis of variance revealed a highly significant effect (α = 0.001) of the yeast density on the abundance of the microbial groups studied, including total bacteria, Actinomycetes, and fungi.

Following the application of the BSY, a notable rise in the overall bacterial count was observed on the clay, particularly on the second analysis date, irrespective of the 2t or 5t dose. In sand, a minor increase in the relevant microorganisms was observed after the application of 5t of BSY (Fig. 2).

The application of BSY resulted in a significant increase in the number of actinomycetes in clay soil, with counts ranging from 200 to over 1000·104 cfu g−1 DM soil (Fig. 3). It is worth noting that higher abundances were observed when a higher dose of yeast was applied (5t). In contrast, no such increase was observed in the group of microorganisms discussed in soil samples represented by sand.

The clay soil samples exhibited a higher abundance of fungi. A significant increase in this group of microorganisms was observed with the addition of 5t of BSY. Similarly, an increase in the number of fungi was recorded after the addition of 2t of BSY to the sand compared to the control.

Biochemical activity of the soils

Figures 4 and 5 illustrate the changes in soil biochemical activity resulting from the application of yeast densities. The abundance of the microorganisms studied is also presented. The two-factor analysis of variance also revealed a highly significant effect (α = 0.001) of the yeast density on the biochemical activity of the soils. Specifically, the levels of dehydrogenases, catalase, proteases, and the fertility index BIF were affected.

The results suggest that the BSY application significantly increased the level of dehydrogenase activity in the clay soil (Fig. 4), while no such relationship was observed for the sand samples. It is worth noting that the enzyme activity in clay increased after the application of 5t of BSY, compared to 2t.

When analysing the results of protease activity, it was observed that there was a significant increase in the level of protease activity in sand after the application of BSY. However, no such relationship was observed for clay. It is important to note that there was a significantly higher activity of the enzyme in question in the sand during the first term of analysis, immediately after the application of the BSY (Fig. 4).

Upon analysing the results of the catalase activity level, a significant increase in catalase activity was observed after the application of BSY for both clay and sand, in comparison to the control variants. For clay, a higher level of the enzyme was obtained after the application of 5t of BSY, and for sand, after the application of 2t. The enzyme level in the sand samples was significantly higher in the second analysis term than in the first, as shown in Fig. 5.

Saviozzi's BIF (Biological Index of Fertility), determined from the activity levels of dehydrogenases and catalase, was found to be higher only for soil samples representing clay. The highest BIF was observed after the application of 5t of BSY. A slight increase in the index was recorded for sand (Fig. 5).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis showed a significant overall effect (p < 0.05) of BSY application on the measured parameters, although the effect size based on eta-squared values was small to moderate for fertiliser dose (around 0.03). Soil type itself and the interaction between soil type and BSY dose also exerted some influence, but to a lesser extent.

BSY fertilisation showed a slight decrease in soil pH with increasing dose. At the same time, there was an increase in electrical conductivity (EC), which was particularly pronounced in clay compared to sand. However, BSY application had no significant effect on organic matter (Corg), nitrogen (Norg) or phosphorus (P).

The results for total bacteria and actinomycete counts are mixed.

Bacteria showed a dose-dependent increase in the clay analysed, even without fertilisation. A much lower number of bacteria was found in sand, but the principle was similar (dose-dependent growth, also without fertilisation). In the case of fungi, the effect is only visible after the application of BSY, with an optimum dose of 2t/ha (in both clay and sand). In the case of actinomycetes, an increase was observed at both 2t/ha and 5t/ha in both clay and sand.

Protease showed an increase in sand and clay, although its activity decreased in the longer term (in both soils).

A similar behaviour was found for dehydrogenase (in both soils), with a much greater increase in clay. In the longer term, however, a decrease in activity was observed. Catalase activity increased in both sand and clay, but there was a long-term decrease in activity in clay at the higher dose.

Discussion

The environment's quality is affected by various factors, such as moderate water consumption, reduced use of conventional fertilizers, and minimization of soil pollution and waste generation. Finding new, easily accessible organic fertilizers is a significant challenge in today's world. The brewing industry has experienced growth in both small-scale craft breweries and larger conglomerates, resulting in an increase in brewery waste volume. The three main types of waste in the brewery industry are brewer's spent grain, hot trub, and residual brewer's yeast. This waste is classified as nuisance waste due to its high density and logistical challenges associated with transportation to waste water treatment plants. To improve sustainability, breweries are exploring ways to repurpose this waste. Proper waste disposal is crucial. Some waste groups are rich in organic matter and micro and macro elements, making them ideal for a circular economy33. Other waste groups can be used for natural purposes, such as fertilising soil, cultivating plants for fodder production, and growing energy crops. The Waste Act34 allows for the use of these waste groups for natural purposes. The R10 recovery process is particularly relevant in this context as it involves treating waste on the ground to benefit agriculture or improve the environment. This process has enormous potential for managing waste from beer production.

Recycling of BSY has potential environmental benefits, such as reducing waste and promoting sustainable resource management in agricultural settings. By diverting BSY from landfills and integrating it into the soil, the organic matter and essential nutrients within the slurry become valuable components that enhance soil health35,36. This reduction in organic waste helps to reduce the environmental impact of landfill disposal by decreasing the release of greenhouse gases and potential leachate production, which can have a negative impact on water systems37.

All waste generated can be reused through a recovery cycle. This involves activities aimed at replacing waste materials that would typically be used for a given task, such as fertilizing degraded soils. The process prepares waste to fulfill functional roles within a plant or the wider economy, in line with the regulations stated in the Waste Act34. To achieve agricultural benefits through soil rehabilitation, follow the recovery process outlined in Regulation of the Minister of the Environment38 item 132). This regulation specifies the necessary conditions and methods. For further details, refer to the regulation. Waste used for agricultural purposes must meet the standards and guidelines outlined in the regulation. The BSY (ex 02 07 80) is classified as group III waste due to its significant organic content, excluding sewage sludge. The utilization of BSY in agriculture presents a significant opportunity to enhance agricultural practices. It can be incorporated into soil amendments or fertilizers to support plant growth and soil health by enriching the soil with organic matter, essential plant nutrients, vitamins, and growth factors. This organic matter acts as a slow-release energy source for soil microorganisms, promoting their activity and subsequently improving soil health. In addition, plant growth and development are directly influenced by essential nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium11,14.

In our experiment the addition of BSY did not have a significant effect on the pH of the soils studied, which remained relatively high despite the slightly acidic nature of the densities. However, there was a difference in electrolytic conductivity, with the clay having a higher conductivity than the sand. Significant differences were only observed at the highest application rate for sand, where an addition of 5t/ha resulted in a more than twofold increase in conductivity compared to the control. No significant differences were observed between the applied doses of BSY for carbon, regardless of the soil type. In sand, the nitrogen content did not change significantly, while in clay, the addition of slurry to soil resulted in a more than twofold increase in nitrogen content. The largest discrepancies in phosphorus content were observed in both clay and sand, where there was a noticeable increase in this element.

Many works point to the positive aspect of the application of yeast dens from beer production to the soil11,14. Some studies highlights the potential nutrient imbalances due to BSY application39. The use of BSY presents several challenges and requires careful management to ensure proper application and avoid negative impacts on soil and plant health. One significant concern is the risk of overapplication, which can cause an imbalance in soil nutrient levels, particularly nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium. Improper application of BSY can lead to the accumulation of salts in the soil, which poses a risk to plant growth. To avoid this, it is crucial to consider the carbon-to-nitrogen (C:N) ratio. Excessive nitrogen from the slurry can result in nitrogen leaching and potential groundwater contamination. Therefore, it is essential to balance the rate of application with the nutrient requirements of the plants and the soil's capacity to absorb the slurry40. Mata et al.41 draws attention that potential nitrate leaching and increase of GHG emissions induced by yeast application to soil have to be carefully monitored to avoid environmental problems.

Soil microorganisms play a crucial role in maintaining soil health and productivity. It has been demonstrated that the addition of organic matter can significantly impact the microbial community and its activities. Although the application of organic matter can initially enhance microbial activity and diversity, the long-term effects are subject to various factors, including climatic conditions, time, and interactions among different microbial groups. The introduction of organic matter can cause rapid changes in nutrient availability, resulting in shifts in microbial populations and activities42,43. Furthermore, the production of toxins and antibiotics by certain microorganisms can affect the composition of the microbial community. Protozoan grazing on bacteria and fungi can also impact the microbial balance.

Our study showed that BSY application had a significant impact on the abundance of different microbial groups in soil. Specifically, applying BSY, particularly at a higher dose of 5t, resulted in an increase in the abundance of total bacteria, actinomycetes, and fungi in clay soil. However, the effect on sand was less significant, with only a minor increase observed in certain groups. The results indicate that the application of BSY has the potential to promote specific soil microbial communities, which may influence soil health and nutrient cycling. However, the observed effects seem to be dependent on soil type, with clay exhibiting greater responses than sand, and the rate of yeast application.

The study found that yeast application had a significant impact on enzymatic activities, indicating potential benefits for soil health. However, the effects varied depending on the soil type and enzyme function—in clay, dehydrogenase activity and the Biological Index of Fertility (BIF) increased after yeast application, especially at a higher dose of 5 tons. In contrast, the yeast application groups showed increased catalase activity in sand compared to the control, while protease activity significantly increased only immediately after yeast application. It is worth noting that clay did not exhibit any significant changes in catalase or protease activity, and sand did not show any significant changes in dehydrogenase activity or BIF. It is quite surpriseing as Protease and Catalase as essential commercial enzymes are used in fermented foods/beverages production44,45. Kizilkaya and Bayrakh46 demonstrated that soil dehydrogenase activity, an enzyme involved in organic matter degradation, is highly sensitive to changes in soil conditions, including oxygenation levels. Although the application of N-rich sewage sludge can initially increase dehydrogenase activity, this effect may diminish over time. Prasanna et al.47 showed that dehydrogenase enzyme activities are higher in soil amended with organic manure and respective substrate than control. Santhanarajan et al.22 indicate that soil conditioners based on organic food products such as spent coffee ground, rice bran, biochar etc. can increase microbial activity in soils.

One of the reasons for the better results obtained in the clay samples could have been the fact that clay particles have a much larger surface area compared to sand particles. This increased surface area provides more space for microbes to attach and colonize, leading to a more significant boost in microbial populations after BSY application in clay soil. Sandy soils with less surface area may limit this microbial growth. Clay particles have a higher cation exchange capacity compared to sand which allows clay to retain the nutrients from BSY, such as nitrogen and phosphorus, more effectively, making them available for microbes and plants for a longer period. In contrast, sandy soils with lower CEC might lose these nutrients through leaching more readily. Similar efect was found by Alkharabsheh et al.48 for soil fertilised with biochar. Due to the high specific surface area of the fine particles found in clays, they have a higher water holding capacity than sandy soils. This allows the clay to retain moisture for longer, which is crucial for microbial activity and the decomposition of organic matter from BSY. Sandy soils can dry out more quickly, limiting these processes.

Beside of that clay minerals can adsorb some of the organic matter from BSY onto their surfaces, which was confirmed by other works49,50. This protects the organic matter from decomposition by other soil microbes, allowing it to persist longer and provide a slow-release source of nutrients for beneficial microbes in clay soil. In sandy soils, this protection might be less effective.

Our findings demonstrate the intricate relationship between BSY and soil type, which affects the response of particular microbial communities and their associated enzymatic activities. Further research is necessary to uncover the underlying mechanisms behind these observations, assess the long-term effects on soil health and plant growth, and optimize application strategies based on soil type, desired biochemical responses, and potential environmental considerations. When considering the possibility of using organic fertilisers for soil improvers, it should be borne in mind that organic fertilisers, such as solid manure, poultry manure, liquid manure, and slurry, may be contaminated with heavy metals51,52. However, spent BSY, which is a by-product of food processing, is free from hazardous heavy metals by definition.

Conclusions

Our experiment shows a significant increase in beneficial microbial groups such as bacteria, actinomycetes and fungi in clay soils treated with BSY. This suggests that BSY can promote a healthy and active soil microbiome. Clay treated with BSY also showed increased activity of dehydrogenase, an enzyme crucial for organic matter degradation, and a higher BIF. This indicates improved soil health and nutrient cycling. The application of BSY resulted in a significant increase in the nitrogen content of the clay, which may improve plant growth. Phosphorus levels also increased in both sand and clay.

Clay showed a greater response to BSY than sand, suggesting that BSY may be more beneficial for certain soil compositions. Higher doses of BSY generally resulted in more significant improvements.

Our experiment provides promising evidence that BSY can be a valuable organic fertiliser for improving soil quality. As the study only focused on short-term changes, further research is needed to address the missing aspects and to refine BSY application strategies for sustainable agricultural practices. In particular, long-term effects on soil health, plant growth and potential environmental impacts need to be further investigated.
