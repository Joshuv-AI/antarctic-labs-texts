The effect of manufacturing technique on marginal gap in inlay, onlay, veneer, and endocrown restorations: a systematic review and metaanalysis
Authors: Osayd Alawawda, Ensar Binici, Ilgınsu Akıncı, Funda Bayindir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to compare the marginal fit of indirect partial restorations, such as inlays, onlays, and endocrowns, fabricated using milling (subtractive manufacturing) and 3D printing (additive manufacturing) techniques.Materials and MethodsThis review was conducted according to the PRISMA guidelines and utilized the PICO framework to define the research question. A comprehensive literature search was performed on PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases for studies published in English between January 1, 2015, and April 1, 2025. The search targeted studies that directly compared milled and 3D-printed indirect partial restorations and reported marginal gap values in a numerical format. Statistical analysis was performed using a random-effects model, and outcomes were synthesized using the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) with 95% Confidence Intervals (CI).ResultsA total of 16 studies, comprising 257 milled samples and 287 3D-printed samples, were included in the meta-analysis. The overall pooled analysis found no statistically significant difference in marginal gap values between milled and 3D-printed partial restorations (p = 0.62). However, subgroup analyses revealed varying trends. For occlusal veneers, milled restorations showed a smaller marginal gap than 3D-printed restorations; however, this difference was not statistically significant (SMD = –1.86, 95% CI: –18.24 to –14.53, p = 0.39). Conversely, inlays and onlays showed a non-significant trend favoring 3D printing (SMD = 0.87, 95% CI: -0.26 to 2.00, p = 0.11).ConclusionThis systematic review and meta-analysis conclude that there is no overall statistically significant difference in marginal fit between milled and 3D-printed indirect partial restorations.Keywords: dental marginal, cad/cam, 3d printing, dental restorations, inlay, onlay, veneer

Introduction

The integration of digital technologies into dentistry has gained remarkable momentum in recent years. The digital dental workflow involves the high-accuracy intraoral scanning of oral structures, the planning of restorations using computer-aided design (CAD) software, and the subsequent fabrication of physical restorations through either subtractive manufacturing (milling) or additive manufacturing (3D printing) processes (1).

The inception of CAD/CAM (Computer-Aided Design / Computer-Aided Manufacturing) technology in the field of dentistry dates back to the 1980s, subsequently leading to the emergence of two predominant manufacturing methodologies: subtractive manufacturing (milling) and additive manufacturing (layered fabrication or 3D printing). The techniques encompassed within additive manufacturing comprise a range of methodologies including stereolithography (SLA), digital light processing (DLP), and selective laser sintering/melting (SLS/SLM) (2). While CAD/CAM systems offer significant advantages—such as improved marginal and internal adaptation, faster production times, convenient digital storage, and aesthetic results—they also exhibit limitations, including inefficient material usage, progressive wear of milling burs, and geometric constraints that hinder the fabrication of complex structures (3). Conversely, 3D printing systems are advantageous in terms of material efficiency, recyclability of unused materials, and the ability to fabricate intricate geometries with high precision (4, 5). In fixed prosthodontics, the fully digital workflow represents a paradigm shift in restorative protocols. Within this context, partial coverage restorations such as partial crowns have gained increasing attention due to their conservative approach. These restorations preserve sound tooth structure by targeting only the defective area, in contrast to full-coverage crowns, which require extensive tooth preparation and result in greater tissue loss (6). Inlays and onlays are typically used to restore Class I and II cavities involving occlusal and proximal surfaces; onlays cover one or more cusps, while inlays do not. Occlusal veneers are limited to the occlusal surface, and laminate veneer crowns are applied to the labial surface of anterior teeth (7, 8).

To ensure the long-term clinical success of such restorations, their marginal adaptation must remain within clinically acceptable limits. The literature suggests that acceptable marginal gap values generally range between 50 and 120 µm (9, 10, 11, 12). Some studies extend this range to 100–150 µm, while others consider gaps of 110-200 µm to be clinically tolerable (13).

A variety of methods are used to assess marginal gaps in dental restorations (14). Common techniques include stereomicroscopy, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), optical microscopy, and profile projectors (14). Stereomicroscopes provide three-dimensional imaging by combining light from two optical channels, enabling high depth of field and image clarity (15). CBCT generates high-resolution volumetric images from multiple two-dimensional radiographs (16). SEM utilizes high-energy electrons instead of light to visualize surface structures at very high magnification (17). Optical microscopes offer magnification between 4× and 25× and are suitable for directly evaluating marginal adaptation (18). Profile projectors optically magnify the restoration's geometry and project it onto a screen for precise measurements (14).

Inadequate marginal fit has been associated with complications such as cement degradation, microleakage, secondary caries, and periodontal issues (14, 19) . Yuce et al. (20) indicated that there were no statistically significant variances in marginal or internal fit between restorations produced through milling and those created using 3D printing methodologies. Conversely, additional research has proposed that restorations generated via 3D printing may provide enhanced adaptation (19, 21). Meta-analyses focusing on provisional restorations fabricated from resin materials, have indicated a potential superiority of additive manufacturing in terms of marginal adaptation (22). Nevertheless, these investigations have primarily focused on full crowns and fixed dental prostheses, and limited data are available regarding indirect partial restorations. This underscores the need for systematic evaluation of the impact of manufacturing technique on the marginal fit of partial restorations such as inlays, onlays, and endocrowns.

The aim of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to assess the marginal fit of indirect partial restorations fabricated through milling and 3D printing techniques. The null hypothesis presented in this analysis suggests that there is no statistically significant difference in marginal adaptation between restorations constructed through additive and subtractive manufacturing methods.

Materials and Methods

PICO framework

This systematic review and meta-analysis were developed using the PICO framework to clearly define the clinical question and guide the eligibility criteria. The population (P) of interest included partial-coverage indirect dental restorations, such as inlays, onlays, veneers, occlusal veneers, and endocrowns. The intervention (I) was the use of 3D printing for the fabrication of these restorations, while the comparison (C) involved milling manufacturing methods. The primary outcome (O) assessed was the marginal gap or marginal fit of the restorations.

Research question

Based on this framework, the focused research question was: Among partial-coverage dental restorations, does additive manufacturing (3D printing) result in different marginal gap values compared to subtractive manufacturing (milling) and aligned with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (23).

Search strategy

A thorough literature review was executed across three principal electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science, and this was further enhanced by manual exploration of the reference lists from pertinent publications. The search was directed at studies disseminated in English within the timeframe of January 1, 2015, to April 1, 2025, with the exclusion criterion being that only articles with accessible full text were deemed eligible.

To ensure a thorough retrieval of relevant studies, a comprehensive search strategy was developed using Boolean operators (AND, OR) to combine synonymous terms across four key domains: marginal adaptation ("marginal adapt" OR "marginal gap" OR "marginal fit" OR "marginal discrepancy"), manufacturing techniques ("3D print" OR "additive manufactur" OR "digital light process" OR "stereolithograph" OR "milled restoration" OR "subtractive manufactur" OR "CAD/CAM"), restoration types ("inlay" OR "onlay" OR "veneer" OR "endocrown" OR "occlusal veneer"), and accuracy metrics ("digital dentist" OR "accura" OR "fit" OR "precision"). The search was adapted for each database (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science) using appropriate field tags (MeSH terms for PubMed, TITLE-ABS-KEY for Scopus, and TS for Web of Science) while maintaining consistent core search concepts across platforms. The procedure for selecting studies was illustrated through a PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1), which meticulously outlined the quantity of studies that were identified, screened, evaluated for eligibility, and ultimately incorporated into the analysis. A trio of independent reviewers (O.A, E.B, I.A) executed the screening and data extraction processes to mitigate bias and guarantee precision.

Bias evaluation

The potential for bias was evaluated utilizing the Quality Assessment Tool for In Vitro Studies in Dentistry (QUIN) (24). This instrument encompasses 12 criteria, with each study being evaluated independently by both reviewers. The findings were subsequently compared, and any discrepancies were deliberated upon to achieve consensus. The conclusive scores are documented in Supplementary Table 1.

Data synthesis

The criteria for inclusion and exclusion are presented in Table 1. When deemed necessary, unit conversions were employed to standardize the data presented. In instances where a study expressed outcomes as confidence intervals (CIs) rather than standard deviations (SDs), the SD was estimated using the following formula: SD ≈ (Upper CI − Lower CI) / 3.92. In studies that provided median and interquartile range (IQR), the methodology proposed by Wan et al. (25) was utilized to derive the mean and standard deviation (SD). In instances where numerous marginal gap values were reported within a single study, the mean value was computed to aggregate the data for the purpose of meta-analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical evaluation was performed utilizing RevMan Web (Version 1.44). Given the expected heterogeneity among the included studies, a random-effects model was applied. The results were synthesized employing the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) alongside 95% Confidence Intervals (CI). Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I² statistic, and the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman (HKSJ) method was adopted for the robust estimation of confidence intervals.

Results

A total of 16 studies were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis (Table 2), comprising 257 samples in the milled group and 287 samples in the 3D-printed group. The meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate and compare the marginal gap of milled versus 3D-printed restorative materials across various restoration types.

Characteristics and main findings of included studies.

In the inlay and onlay group (9 studies), 3D-printed restorations exhibited a trend toward smaller marginal gaps compared to milled ones; however, the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.11) (Figure 2). The heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 92%), indicating notable variability among the studies. For laminate veneers, data from four studies showed no significant difference between milled and 3D-printed restorations in terms of marginal adaptation (SMD = −0.24, 95% CI: −1.60 to 1.13, p = 0.62) (Figure 2), with moderate-to-high heterogeneity (I² = 78%).

Forest plot of the meta-analysis comparing the marginal gap of milled and 3D-printed dental restorations across different restoration types (inlays/onlays, laminate veneers, occlusal veneers, and endocrowns).

Occlusal veneer restorations (2 studies) showed that milled restorations presented smaller marginal gaps than 3D-printed restorations; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.39)(Figure 2). However, this finding was associated with extreme heterogeneity (I² = 95%) and unusually wide confidence intervals, possibly reflecting inconsistencies in the data or small sample sizes. Regarding endocrowns, only one study was included, which found no significant difference between the two fabrication methods (p = 0.93) (Figure 2); as expected, heterogeneity was not applicable in this single-study subgroup. A subgroup analysis was conducted to determine whether the type of restoration influenced the marginal gap outcomes. The test for subgroup differences yielded Chi² = 5.44, df = 3, p = 0.14, suggesting that the differences between restoration types were not statistically significant. However, the I² value for subgroup differences was 44.9%, indicating that a moderate portion of heterogeneity could be attributed to variation among restoration types. Despite the absence of statistically significant subgroup effects, the direction and magnitude of effect sizes varied: inlays/onlays tended to favor 3D printing (though not significantly), laminate veneers showed no meaningful difference, occlusal veneers favored milled restorations, but no statistical significance was observed, and endocrowns showed a neutral effect. These findings highlight the potential influence of restoration type on marginal fit performance between manufacturing methods, warranting further investigation.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis sought to assess the marginal fit of dental restorations produced via subtractive (milling) and additive (3D printing) manufacturing methods. The null hypothesis posited that there would be no significant disparity in marginal gap measurements between the two fabrication techniques. The findings did not reject this hypothesis (p=0.14).

The pooled analysis indicated a lack of statistically significant difference in marginal gap measurements between milled and 3D-printed restorations (P=0.64). Nonetheless, subgroup analyses revealed notable trends. Specifically, occlusal veneers produced through milling demonstrated reduced marginal gaps when contrasted with their 3D-printed equivalents; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p=0.39). In contrast, inlays/onlays presented a non-significant trend that appeared to favor 3D printing (p=0.11). Laminate veneers and endocrowns exhibited no statistically significant differences between the two methodologies. These results imply that the influence of the fabrication method on marginal fit may be specific to the type of restoration, rather than applicable to all varieties of indirect restorations.

The literature reflects conflicting evidence regarding the superiority of one method over the other. Some studies report improved marginal adaptation with 3D-printed restorations (19, 37, 38, 39, 40), possibly due to the layer-by-layer additive process that can enhance contour precision and reduce cumulative machining errors (41, 42). In contrast, other studies consistently demonstrate superior adaptation in milled restorations, potentially due to the mechanical precision of advanced milling systems (43, 44, 45). Dimensional accuracy can be influenced by a multitude of variables, including the restorative material used, the specific fabrication technique, and the quality of tooth preparation (46).

Moreover, studies have shown that restorations derived from digital impressions tend to exhibit lower marginal discrepancies compared to those made from conventional impressions, suggesting that digital workflows contribute to improved marginal accuracy (47). Additive manufacturing has shown high potential for producing ceramic inlays and veneers with intricate geometries, yet current evidence in this domain remains limited (48). Although fixed dental restorations fabricated via additive manufacturing demonstrate clinically acceptable marginal fit, internal fit, and trueness, comparable to those produced by subtractive methods, 3D printing of dental zirconia remains under active development and requires further technological advancements (49).

Zirconia is known for its favorable mechanical properties, therefore, it is often selected for inlays and onlays (50). Emerging studies have indicated that 3D printing may be a viable alternative for fabricating zirconia crowns, achieving comparable levels of trueness and marginal quality to subtractive manufacturing methods (51). Furthermore, the capability of additive manufacturing to produce ultra-thin restorations some of which can be as thin as 0.1 mm, demonstrates the high-resolution potential of modern 3D printing systems (52).

From a clinical standpoint, the dimensional accuracy and marginal adaptation of a restoration are critical determinants of long-term success (53, 54). Several factors can influence the fit of milled restorations, including the material of the CAD/CAM block, the type of milling equipment, the size and wear condition of the milling burs (2, 55, 56, 57). Similarly, in additive manufacturing, the interaction between the printer and the print orientation can significantly affect the final accuracy of the restoration, especially for complex preparations (58).

It is also important to consider that the method used to evaluate marginal fit might not significantly impact the reported outcomes. Multiple studies have shown consistent results across various assessment techniques, including stereomicroscopy, CBCT, and micro-CT (59, 60, 61). Nonetheless, the implementation of multiple measurement methods within a single study is recommended to increase robustness and reproducibility (62). Future research should prioritize the development of standardized, validated protocols for the assessment of internal and marginal fit to enable meaningful comparisons across studies (63).

Despite the comprehensive methodology of this review, several limitations must be acknowledged. The considerable heterogeneity among the included studies, particularly those within the inlay/onlay and occlusal veneer subgroups, limits the generalizability of the findings. This heterogeneity likely stems from variations in study design, restorative materials, layer thickness, printer or milling calibration, and evaluation methods. Moreover, most of the included studies were conducted in vitro, and thus may not replicate intraoral conditions such as humidity, thermal cycling, and occlusal loading. Another limitation lies in the limited number of studies available for specific restoration types, such as endocrowns and occlusal veneers.

Future investigations should focus on methodological approaches, including standardized testing protocols and clinically relevant simulation environments. Longitudinal clinical trials are especially warranted to assess the long-term performance and durability of restorations fabricated through additive versus subtractive techniques.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis showed no statistically significant differences in marginal fit between milled and 3D-printed partial restorations. Further standardized in vitro and clinical studies are needed to clarify whether specific restoration types may respond differently to each manufacturing technique.
