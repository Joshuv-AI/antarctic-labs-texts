AbstractBackgroundPass-fail decisions in clinical examinations must be defensible, yet traditional standard-setting approaches often report a single cut score without explicitly quantifying uncertainty-an issue amplified in small cohorts and mixed-format assessments (e.g., OSCE plus written). Station cut scores were estimated using (a) Borderline Regression Method (BRM) for comparison and (b) Bayesian regression , giving posterior at borderline B=2. Borderline semantics were represented via a trapezoidal fuzzy set with centroid , producing fuzzy-adjusted cuts . A hybrid OSCE standard was mapped to the mixed-format cut . Introduction

Background and rationale

Performance-based clinical assessments such as the objective structured clinical examination (OSCE) are widely used to evaluate applied competence, often alongside written components (e.g., MCQs, SAQs) in mixed-format examinations. A core requirement of such assessments is a defensible pass-fail standard that is credible to stakeholders and appropriately reflects the intended level of minimal competence. Standard setting remains challenging because no single method is universally optimal across assessment designs, cohort sizes, and institutional constraints, and standard-setting outcomes can meaningfully affect learners and programs [1, 2].

In OSCE contexts, commonly used absolute methods include borderline group and borderline regression approaches that leverage examiner global judgments as an external criterion for station checklist scores. For instance, in the borderline regression method (BRM), station checklist scores are regressed on global ratings, and the estimated checklist score corresponding to the “borderline” global category is taken as the station cut score. This is widely used due to transparency and operational simplicity and has been compared with Angoff-type approaches in health professions education settings [3–6].

However, small cohort size-a frequent reality in many programs-can destabilize classical regression-based cut scores and their station-level diagnostics (e.g., weak checklist-global relationships at particular stations). Recent work has examined when BRM can remain usable in smaller cohorts and emphasized the need to quantify uncertainty rather than relying on point estimates alone [7, 8]. The practical problem becomes more acute for mixed-format examinations, where institutions may wish to combine written and performance-based evidence into a single decision rule and still communicate uncertainty coherently.

Why a Bayesian-fuzzy hybrid for small cohorts with global ratings

Two features of OSCE standard setting motivate a hybrid approach.

First, standard setting is inherently an inference problem under uncertainty. Bayesian modeling naturally represents uncertainty using posterior distributions and credible intervals, which can be particularly valuable when sample sizes are small and parameter estimates are volatile. Bayesian perspectives on standard setting highlight the ability to formally update beliefs with observed data and to integrate additional sources of information beyond what is used in conventional methods [9].

Second, the concept of “borderline” is partly linguistic and can be interpreted differently by raters, even with training. This introduces semantic vagueness that is not fully captured by treating global ratings as crisp categories. Fuzzy set theory provides a principled way to represent such linguistic constructs through membership functions, allowing “borderline” to be modeled as a graded set rather than a sharp point [10]. Interval/type-2 fuzzy sets further extend this idea by representing uncertainty in the membership function itself-useful when there is variability in how raters interpret performance descriptors [11, 12].

Methods

Study design and assessment context

This methodological study specifies and evaluates a Bayesian and fuzzy hybrid standard-setting framework for a mixed-format clinical examination comprising an OSCE component and a written component. The approach is intended for settings where cohort sizes are small, global ratings are available for OSCE stations, and decision-makers require transparent uncertainty quantification around pass-fail thresholds. The design follows established guidance that defensible standard setting should (i) match the assessment purpose, (ii) use an appropriate method for the exam format, and (iii) report uncertainty and consequences of the standard where possible.

Real-world assessment context: the intended use case is an end-of-course or end-of-rotation clinical skills examination, where candidates perform multiple OSCE stations rated by trained examiners while simultaneously completing a written component. In practice, an examination board must choose which candidates go through as black, white ones that need to be reviewed further. It is therefore positioned as a decision-support tool at board level: it does not replace examiner judgement but converts station scores global ratings, and written scores into an explicit standard expressed as a mean with an associated uncertainty band to inform practical defensible decisions for borderline candidates.

Model diagnostics, sensitivity analysis, and practical gains

In posterior predictive evaluations, replicated station means and standard deviations were similar to the observed values, while residual checks indicated that there were no systematic patterns in result by global-rating category. Because scores from the same candidate across stations are usually positively correlated, we also considered a candidate-random-effect sensitivity model. This extension did not change the qualitative classification pattern since it produced a very similar central total cut score, but is recommended for operational datasets where intraclass correlation is high.

Discussion

Principal findings and what they mean mathematically

This study demonstrates a Bayesian-fuzzy hybrid approach to standard setting in a mixed-format clinical examination under small-cohort conditions with station checklist scores and global ratings. Using the collected mixed-format dataset (), station-level borderline-anchored cut scores clustered tightly (BRM point cuts ), while the Bayesian model produced station cut-score posterior distributions with interpretable uncertainty bands (95% credible intervals typically spanning points depending on station). This directly addresses a practical limitation of conventional borderline regression approaches, which often report a point cut score without explicit quantification of uncertainty in the threshold itself.

Mathematically, the key advantage arises from treating the cut score as a random variable:

with inferred via a Bayesian regression. The posterior

naturally yields credible intervals that quantify uncertainty due to finite cohort size, rating dispersion, and station-specific noise. In small cohorts this is valuable because the frequentist standard error estimates can be unstable, whereas posterior uncertainty integrates both sampling variability and prior regularization.

At the OSCE level, aggregation smooths station-level noise:

and in our results the Bayesian OSCE cut score had a relatively narrow posterior interval around a mean of approximately , indicating that much of the station-to-station variability averages out when stations are appropriately blueprint-weighted.

Why the Bayesian component helps in small cohorts

Small cohorts amplify three statistical issues in BRM-type standard setting: (i) noisy slope estimates when global ratings are sparse in some categories, (ii) station-level instability when checklist-global association is weak, and (iii) instability of the overall cut score when a few borderline cases strongly influence the regression line. The Bayesian formulation mitigates these through regularization and (where implemented) hierarchical pooling:

which shrinks extreme station parameters toward an overall mean, reducing variance while preserving genuine station differences. This is aligned with practical concerns for small-scale OSCE standard setting highlighted in the literature.

In the demonstration results, posterior mean slopes ranged approximately 13.96-18.09 checklist points per global-rating step, and stations with lower slope contribute less information about the borderline anchor. Under Bayesian estimation, such weaker stations tend to show wider posterior uncertainty for , which is precisely the signal exam boards need when reviewing station quality and defensibility.

What the fuzzy component adds beyond Bayesian uncertainty

Bayesian uncertainty captures statistical uncertainty in the regression relationship given observed global ratings. It does not, by itself, address the semantic issue that the term “borderline” is intrinsically fuzzy raters may apply the label differently even with training.

Fuzzy set theory models this by representing borderline as a graded concept with membership function .

In the demonstration, we used a trapezoidal membership (Fig. 3) and computed a centroid-based fuzzy borderline anchor:

slightly above the crisp borderline category B=2. This produced a small upward adjustment in cut scores through

The main practical contribution, however, was not the tiny shift in the central cut score; it was the ability to define a decision band around the hybrid threshold that incorporates both Bayesian credible uncertainty and fuzzy “borderline semantics” uncertainty. Fuzzy uncertainty can be widened further using interval/type-2 fuzzy sets, which represent uncertainty in the membership function itself via a footprint of uncertainty.

Conclusions

This methods study describes a hybrid standard-setting framework based on the combination of Bayesian and fuzzy logic for small-cohort mixed-format clinical examinations. For the demonstration dataset, this method presented a central standard that resembled BRM and provided posterior uncertainty estimates alongside a structured borderline visual review band. While these additions may protect against bias through increased transparency and defensibility of pass-fail decisions, the method should still be validated in larger and multiple examination cohorts, with explicit tests for model fit, within-candidate dependence, distributional assumptions, lambda sensitivity, and institutional feasibility prior to widespread clinical adoption.
