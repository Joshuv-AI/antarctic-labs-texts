Survival of a potential bacterial biocontrol agent in the soil and its impact on microbial communities
Authors: Amélie Polrot, Jérémie Béguet, Marie‐Christine Breuil, Marion Devers‐Lamrani, Fabrice Martin‐Laurent, Nadine Rouard, Aymé Spor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND Sclerotinia sclerotiorum is a major crop pathogen commonly managed using fungicides. Efficient biological alternatives are needed but it is essential to determine whether a biocontrol agent persists in soil and affects non‐target microbiota. Here, we assessed the environmental fate and the impact of a novel Bacillus sp. strain (SCL1) on soil microbial communities from three contrasted agricultural soils. The fate and effects of SCL1 applied at an agronomical (LD) or elevated (HD) dose were compared with a commercial biocontrol product, tebuconazole, and an untreated control.RESULTSSCL1 persistence was monitored by selective plate counts and strain‐specific quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analysis. After inoculation, SCL1 declined rapidly but remained detectable up to day 168; at late time points the SCL1 population stabilized at low levels [~103 colony‐forming units (CFU) g−1 in the high‐dose treatment], supporting its long‐term persistence. Treatment effects on microbial community composition were limited: differentially abundant operational taxonomic units (OTUs) represented up to 9.9% of bacterial and 3.8% of fungal communities. Fungal taxa associated with soil functioning increased under SCL1 treatment in some soils; e.g., Mortierellomycetes in Trugny soil (5.7% in control versus 10.7% under low‐dose SCL1 at day 56).CONCLUSIONSCL1 persisted for at least 6 months in soil microcosms and induced small shifts in bacterial and fungal communities, comparable with reference products. These findings support further field studies to evaluate the efficacy of SCL1 against S. sclerotiorum in rapeseed crops, as well as its persistence and potential non‐target effects on soil microbiota. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Sclerotinia sclerotiorum is a phytopathogenic fungus responsible for Sclerotinia stem rot, which affects a broad range of hosts across various plant families, leading to significant economic losses in crops such as rapeseed, soybeans, chickpeas and lentils. 1 The pathogen's elusive behavior, ability to form resilient sclerotia, and broad host range complicate forecasting and management efforts. 2 The management of Sclerotinia stem rot most often relies on fungicide application and the implementation of appropriate cultural practices. 3 However, these strategies can exhibit limited effectiveness, particularly due to the difficulty in predicting optimal fungicide application timing. 3 Recently, growing concerns over the environmental impact of chemical pesticide residues on health have motivated adoption of the Greendeal policy by the European Commission. Within this framework, biobased solutions, such as the use of microbial inoculants with biocontrol properties, appear as a sustainable way to replace chemical pesticides. 4 Although several biocontrol agents against S. sclerotiorum have been identified and commercialized, their efficacy remains inconsistent. 3 Ongoing research into novel biocontrol solutions against S. sclerotiorum is therefore critical. In this context, the biocontrol potential of a new bacterial strain, Bacillus sp. SCL1, is currently under investigation.

A fundamental aspect of the development of biocontrol solutions, besides evaluating their efficacy, is their potential side effects on the environment. Being of natural origin, biocontrol agents are often considered environmentally friendly solutions that are less harmful than chemical pesticides. 5 Nonetheless, biocontrol solutions contain an active ingredient that may have side effects on non‐targeted living‐organisms and for this reason, have to undergo comprehensive ecotoxicological assessment. 5 This evaluation is done in part by the chemical firms themselves to obtain regulatory approval and market application in the European Union. 6 To obtain regulatory approval, biocontrol products are subject to the same regulation as plant protection products and therefore chemical pesticides. This includes assessing the dissipation of the active ingredient (the biocontrol agent) and the ecotoxicological effect on the mineralization of C and N. 7 However, this is insufficient to properly assess the effects of the active ingredient of biocontrol solutions in soil‐living organisms as underlined previously. 5 , 8 , 9 The European Food and Safety Authority (EFSA) has published several scientific opinions: one proposed assessing the ecotoxicity of pesticides on soil ecosystemic services and microbial communities as specific protection goals, 10 another listed the endpoints that should be monitored to assess pesticide effects on in soil‐living organisms. 11 However, the recommendations made by the EFSA have not been taken into account when assessing the side effects of plant protection products. In the absence of appropriate regulations, several scientists have assessed the fate and impact of these products on indigenous microbial communities. 12 , 13 , 14 In terms of survival, studies on bacteria applied to soil have generally focused on short time scales and have reported varied outcomes. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 However, one study documented the stable survival of bacteria in soil over a period of 3 years. 19 Regarding the impact of biocontrol products, numerous studies have been published, with earlier research often relying on fingerprinting techniques such as Denaturing Gel Gradient Electrophoresis (DGGE). Although fingerprinting approaches have provided valuable insights into overall changes within microbial communities following pesticide application, there are limitations in terms of the depth of interpretation. More recent studies based on soil DNA extraction, amplification of gene markers and amplicon sequencing utilizing next‐generation sequencing have offered a deeper understanding, although functional inference remains challenging. The majority of these studies have reported minor changes in microbial communities, leading to the conclusion that the introduced microorganisms had a negligible impact on indigenous microbial communities. 20 , 21 However, even though it is important to not consider all variations in the microbial community negatively, because they may be neutral or transient, a recent review has argued that these impacts are usually overlooked. 20

This study evaluates the persistence and non‐intentional effects of the biocontrol strain SCL1 in soil microcosms. Sclerotinia sclerotiorum was not introduced, because the aim was not to test efficacy but to assess the environmental fate and potential impacts of SCL1 on resident bacterial and fungal communities. The fate of SCL1 was estimated by monitoring its survival in soil following its application by selective plate count and quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) analyses over a 6‐month period. The ecotoxicological effects of SCL1, applied at agronomical and elevated doses, on soil microbiota were evaluated using Illumina MiSeq sequencing of 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and internal transcribed spacer (ITS) genes. These effects were compared with those induced by the application of a formulated commercially available biocontrol agent and the active ingredient of a chemical fungicide, both used to control the development of S. sclerotiorum in rapeseed cultures. A control treatment involving the application of water was also included.

Despite potential biotic and abiotic stresses that could lead to dissipation of the biocontrol agent on soil application, SCL1, as a spore‐forming bacterium, may demonstrate enhanced persistence in soil compared with non‐spore‐forming strains. Its antifungal properties are anticipated to influence the structure and composition of soil fungal communities, while exerting minimal effects on bacterial communities.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Experimental design

The experimental design is shown in Supporting Information, Fig. S1 and is described hereafter.

Soils

Soil was collected from three different sites in Burgundy, France and displayed various physicochemical parameters (Supporting Information, Table S1). Soils were sieved at 4 mm and microcosms were prepared in glass vials with 80 g equivalent of dry soil.

Bacterial strain

Biocontrol bacterial strain SCL1 belonging to Bacillus sp. was provided by Éléphant Vert. This strain is naturally resistant to rifampicin at 100 mg L−1 but a rifampicin‐resistant variant was obtained by plating large amount of a preculture on Luria–Bertani (LB) agar plates containing 100 mg L−1 of rifampicin. Preculture of this antibio‐resistant variant of the biocontrol strain SCL1 was performed for 72 h at 28 °C a rich culture medium also provided by Éléphant Vert (France). The optical density of the culture was then measured at 600 nm, and the cells were centrifuged and resuspended with an appropriate volume of supernatant to reach the high‐dose concentration [1 × 108 bacteria/g soil dry weight (dw)]. Supernatant was used for this resuspension to mimic the final biocontrol product, which could be formulated using unwashed bacterial culture. This high‐dose solution was then diluted with 0.9% NaCl to make a low‐dose concentration (6.25 × 103 bacteria/g soil dw).

Microcosms

Microcosms were subjected to five different treatments: (i) inoculation of SCL1 at a target density of 6.25 × 103 bacteria/g soil dw (agronomical dose, LD); (ii) inoculation of SCL1 at 108 bacteria/g soil dw (HD); (iii) a chemical fungicide [1.56 × 10−7 g/g soil dw tebuconazole (CF)]; (iv) the commercial biocontrol Rhapsody® at its recommended dose [1.25 × 10−9 L/g soil dw (B)]; and (v) a control (C) treated with sterile water only. Sclerotinia sclerotiorum was not inoculated into the microcosms. After application of the treatments, the humidity of the soil was adjusted by adding sterile water to reach 60% of the water‐holding capacity of the soils, it was then readjusted to this value every 2 weeks until the end of the experiment. Five microcosms of each treatment were prepared for each of the first three sampling times. Only the treatments of SCL1 and the control were prepared for the last sampling time, for a total of 240 microcosms. All the microcosms were incubated at room temperature (21 °C) in the dark. They were then sampled on days 0, 7, 56 and 168. Soil humidity was measured straight after sampling, fresh samples were processed immediately for plate counting of SCL1 and the remainder was stored at −20 °C until DNA extraction.

Sampling times (0, 7, 56 and 168 days) were selected to capture immediate, short‐term, mid‐term and long‐term dynamics following treatment application.

At each sampling time, fresh soil was used for selective plate counts of SCL1 (C, LD and HD), and for direct DNA extraction to quantify SCL1 using a strain‐specific qPCR assay (C, LD and HD). Amplicons of 16S rRNA and ITS2 generated by PCR from soil DNA extracts were sequenced to assess bacterial and fungal community responses at days 0, 7 and 56 for all treatments; day 168 was used to assess SCL1 persistence only, because prolonged closed microcosm incubation may increase time/enclosure‐driven drift and reduce the interpretability of treatment effects on microbial community composition.

Biocontrol strain plate counting

At sampling, a suspension of 1 g of soil in 5 mL of sterile water was prepared for each microcosm corresponding the control or the strain SCL1 inoculation treatments. Serial dilutions of this suspension up to 10−7 (using up to 1 mL of the initial suspension) were plated on LB agar containing rifampicin (100 mg L−1) and sulfamethazine (100 mg L−1) as selective agents for the SCL1 strain, as well as benlate (10 mg L−1), natamycin (10 mg L−1) and cycloheximide (100 mg L−1) to prevent fungal growth. Plates were incubated at 28 °C. After 48 h no colonies grew from the control soil, and all colonies that developed from the inoculated soil displayed the expected morphology of strain SCL1. The colony‐forming units (CFUs) were therefore attributed to strain SCL1 and counted accordingly.

DNA extraction

Approximately 250 mg of soil from each microcosm were processed using the DNeasy 96 PowerSoil Pro kit (Qiagen, France) according to the manufacturer's instructions. DNA extracts were then quantified using the Quant‐iT PicoGreen dsDNA Assay Kit (Invitrogen, France) and normalized to 0.5 ng μL−1.

qPCR quantification of the biocontrol strain SCL1

SCL1 gene copy number abundance was measured by qPCR using strain‐specific primers targeting a single‐copy gene. 22 qPCR was performed on a Viia7 in 15‐μL reactions comprising 7.5 μL of master mix (Takyon lowROX SYBR, Eurogentec, France), 0.15 μL of each primer (10 μm), 3.85 μL of MilliQ water, 0.05 μL of T4 Gene 32 protein (Mpbio, France) and 2 μL of DNA (0.5 ng μL−1). Reactions were initiated for 5 min at 95 °C, followed by 35 cycles of 15 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 60 °C, 30 s at 72 °C, 20 s at 70 °C for amplification, and 15 s at 95 °C, 1 min at 70 °C, 15 s at 95 °C for melting curve generation.

16S and ITS rRNA amplicon sequencing

Bacterial and fungal diversity and structure were analyzed via Illumina sequencing of the 16S and ITS rRNA amplicons generated from all DNA extracts using a two‐step PCR procedure as previously described. For 16S rRNA, the first step was PCR amplification of the V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene using the following primers: U341F (5′‐CCTACGGGRSGCAGCAG‐3′) and 805R (5′‐GACTACCAGGGTATCTAAT‐3′). For ITS, the first step was PCR amplification of the ITS2 region with ITS3F (5′‐GCATCGATGAAGAACGCAGC‐3′) and ITS4R (5′‐TCCTCSSCTTATTGATATGC‐3′). The thermal cycling conditions for the first PCR step were as follows: 98 °C for 3 min followed by cycles of 98 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s (25 cycles for 16S rRNA and 30 cycles for the ITS2 region) and a final extension for 10 min at 7 °C. First‐step PCRs were performed twice for each sample and pooled. In the second step, 6 μL of pooled amplicons was used as template for a second PCR analysis with NextEra‐indexed sequencing primers to add multiplexing index sequences. Thermal cycling conditions for this second PCR step were as follows: 98 °C for 3 min followed by 98 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s (eight cycles for 16S rRNA and ten cycles for the ITS2) and a final extension for 10 min at 72 °C. A SequalPrep Normalisation kit (Applied Biosystems, USA) was used to normalize amplicon concentrations before pooling. Sequencing was performed on Illumina MiSeq (2 × 250 pb paired‐end reads) using the MiSeq reagent kit v2.

Sequence processing and bacterial community structure

Sequences obtained from the 240 samples were processed using in‐house developed Python pipelines (https://forgemia.inra.fr/vasa/illuminametabarcoding). Briefly, 16S and ITS sequences were assembled using PEAR 23 with default settings. Further quality checks were conducted using the QIIME pipeline 24 and short sequences were removed (<400 bp for 16S and <300 bp for ITS). Reference‐based and de novo chimera detection, as well as operational taxonomic units (OTUs) clustering, were performed using VSEARCH 25 and adequate reference databases (SILVA representative set of sequences 26 for the 16S rRNA gene and UNITE's ITS2 reference dynamic dataset 27 for the ITS2 region). Representative sequences for each OTU were aligned using Infernal 28 and the phylogenetic tree for the 16S rRNA gene was constructed using FastTree. 29 Taxonomy was assigned using UCLUST 30 and the SILVA database (138.1/2020) 31 for the 16S sequences. For ITS, the taxonomy assignment was performed using BLAST and the UNITE reference database (v.7–08/2016). 27 Alpha‐diversity metrics were calculated based on rarefied OTU tables (8700 ITS2 sequences and 5550 16S sequences).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using R statistical software v.3.4.1. Differences in qPCR quantifications or plate counts of SCL1 were tested using a non‐parametric Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's test for pairwise comparisons. Differences in microbial alpha‐diversity and gene abundance were tested for each soil using analysis of variance, followed by Tukey's HSD test. The normality and homogeneity of the residual's distribution were inspected, and log‐transformations were performed when necessary. Bray–Curtis and weighted UniFrac dissimilarity matrices were computed on rarefied OTU tables to detect variations in the structure of fungal and bacterial communities, respectively. The effects of soil type, inoculation, sampling time and their interactions were evaluated using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations (adonis2 function, vegan package). The homogeneity of group dispersions was tested using the betadisper function followed by a permutation test. When dispersion heterogeneity was detected pairwise ANOSIM tests were conducted with 999 permutations. P‐values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure when multiple comparisons were performed.

Before comparing the differential abundance of OTUs, low‐abundance OTUs were filtered, keeping only OTUs with an average relative abundance above 0.5% for ITS and 1% for 16S across all samples. For each treatment, OTUs present in fewer than 60% of the samples were also excluded. These filtering steps reduce the zero counts in the OTU table, which can increase the number of false positives in the differential abundance analysis. Such a model combines a generalized linear model and a mixed model. The generalized linear model allows us to infer linear regression from data that do not follow a normal distribution because abundance data typically follow a Poisson distribution. The mixed model includes both fixed effects (treatment effects) and random effects (sampling effects). Each soil and sampling time point was analyzed separately. Pairwise comparisons were carried out between control and treatments. Comparisons were considered significant when P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Survival of SCL1 in the three different soils

The survival of SCL1 in the three different soils was assessed using two techniques: a count on selective agar plates and qPCR quantification of a strain‐specific DNA sequence (Fig. 1). Owing to its higher limit of detection (3 × 104 copies/g soil dw), qPCR could only detect SCL1 in the samples inoculated with 108 cells/g soil dw. Using the selective plate count, however, the strain could be detected at both inoculation doses. At the time of inoculation, SCL1 plate counts averaged 6.57 × 103 CFU/g soil dw across the three soils for the LD treatment and 6.2 × 107 CFU/g soil dw for the HD treatment. Using qPCR quantification, the high dose averaged 1.8 × 107 CFU/g soil. Across all doses, both methods showed a rapid decline of SCL1 in the first few days of the experiment, with a more pronounced decrease observed using plate counting (four logs) compared with qPCR (one log). Following this initial decline, SCL1 appeared to persist at low levels over the long term. From 56 to 168 days, SCL1 levels appeared to stabilize, with plate counts ranging from 4.6 × 100 to 5.8 × 100 CFU/g soil dw for the LD treatment and from 2.2 × 103 to 4 × 103 CFU/g soil dw for the HD treatment. qPCR quantification in the HD treatment ranged from 3.0 × 104 to 6.0 × 104 copies/g soil dw over the same period. Note that for the low dose, in the Auxonne soil, SCL1 was not detected at all at 56 days, although a few colonies were observed at 168 days, suggesting survival close to the detection limit. In the Trugny soil, SCL1 was not detected at all at 168 days.

Impact on microbial communities

Ecotoxicological impact of SCL1 inoculation on the abundance of bacterial and fungal communities

The abundances of the bacterial and fungal communities were assessed by qPCR (Fig. 2). After 7 days, an increase in the abundance of bacteria was observed in soil microcosms inoculated with SCL1 at both the tested doses. A slight increase was also noted in microcosms treated with the chemical fungicide in one of the three soils (Auxonne). The extent of the increase in response to SCL1 inoculation at both low and high doses was lower in Trugny than in the two other soils. By 56 days in the Auxonne soil, bacterial abundance in the treated microcosms was not significantly different from the control, but was higher in the HD treatment than in the LD and B treatments. In the Bretenière soil, bacterial abundances in the HD, B and CF treatments were lower than in C and LD treatments. In the Trugny soil, bacterial abundances in the LD, B and CF treatments were higher than in the C and HD treatments. By contrast, at both 7 and 56 days, fungal abundances recorded in non‐treated soil microcosms were not statistically different from those measured in treated soils (Fig. 2B).

Ecotoxicological impact of SCL1 inoculation on alpha‐diversity of bacterial and fungal communities

Observed species and Simpson's reciprocal indices of both bacterial and fungal communities are shown in the Fig. 3. For the bacterial community, at day 7, a significant decrease in the observed species index was recorded in Bretenière soil microcosms inoculated with SCL1 under both LD and HD treatments compared with the control. In Auxonne soil, only the HD treatment showed a significant difference from the control, whereas in Trugny soil, only the LD treatment differed significantly. In all three soils at day 7, observed species in the B and CF treatments did not differ significantly from the control.

Observed species and Simpson's reciprocal index measured at 7 and 56 days in Auxonne, Bretenière and Trugny soil microcosms treated (LD, biocontrol strain SCL1 at low dose; HD, biocontrol strain SCL1 at high dose; B, commercial biocontrol Rhapsody®; CF, chemical fungicide tebuconazole) or not (C, control) are shown. At day 56, in Bretenière soil microcosms, observed species counts across all treatments were comparable with the control. However, in Auxonne soil, the observed species index was significantly lower in the LD, B and CF treatments than in the control. In Trugny soil, only the LD treatment remained lower than all other treatments, including the control.

Simpson's reciprocal index for bacterial communities followed a similar trend to the observed species index for the LD and HD treatments. Specifically, these treatments led to a reduction in diversity across all three soils at day 7. At this time point, the CF treatment was significantly different from the control in both Auxonne and Trugny soils. By day 56, the HD treatment aligned with the control, whereas the LD treatment remained lower. In Auxonne soil, both B and CF treatments resulted in lower alpha‐diversity at day 56 compared with the control, whereas in Trugny soil, the B treatment had alpha‐diversity values similar to the control, whereas the CF treatment showed higher diversity than the control. In Bretenière soil, Simpson's reciprocal values in the CF and B treatments were similar to the control.

By contrast, fungal alpha‐diversity remained largely unaffected by the different treatments in all three soils. Notable exceptions included a short‐term increase in observed species in the LD, HD and B treatments in Bretenière soil at day 7, a decrease in observed species in the CF treatment in Auxonne soil at day 56, and an increase in Simpson's reciprocal index in Bretenière and Trugny soils following the CF treatment at day 56.

Ecotoxicological impact of the treatments on the microbial community structure and composition

To assess variations in bacterial and fungal beta‐diversity in response to the treatments applied to soil microcosms, principal coordinates analyses (PCoAs) of weighted UniFrac and Bray–Curtis were made for bacterial and fungal communities respectively (Figs 4 and 5). A clear clustering of the samples by soil type can be observed (Fig. 4(A)) and confirmed by PERMANOVA. Within each soil, some degree of clustering bacterial communities is also visible in relation to sampling time (Fig. 4(B)) and treatments (Fig. 4(C)), although these factors had a less‐pronounced effect than soil type. Pairwise PERMANOVA results comparing treatments at each time point (Supporting Information, Table S2) indicate that the bacterial community structure was significantly affected by the LD and HD treatments at day 7 in the Bretenière soil, by the LD, HD and CF treatments in the Auxonne soil, and by all treatments compared with control in the Trugny soil at this time point. At 56 days, the bacterial community structure was affected by all treatments across all soils compared with the control (Supporting Information, Table S2).

The fungal community also exhibited strong clustering by soil (Fig. 4(A)), with weaker clustering by time except for the Auxonne soil (Fig. 5(B)) and treatments (Fig. 5(C)). Overall, the fungal community was more resistant to the different treatments than the bacterial community. The pairwise ANOSIM results (Supporting Information, Table S3) indicate that at 7 days, only the LD treatment significantly affected the fungal community in the Trugny and Bretenière soils, whereas both LD and HD treatments had an effect in the Auxonne soil. By 56 days, all treatments except B affected the fungal community in the Auxonne and Trugny soils, whereas in the Bretenière soil, only the LD treatment had a significant impact (Supporting Information, Table S3).

The number of affected OTUs represented only a relatively small portion of the overall communities, being more substantial for bacterial (up to 9.9%) than for fungal (up to 3.8%) communities (Figs 6 and 7). The impact of treatments varied across soils, with more OTUs affected in the Auxonne soil at 7 days, whereas a greater number of OTUs were affected at 56 days in the Trugny soil.

Heatmaps showing the bacterial OTUs that displayed statistically significant changes between at least one treatment and the control are presented in Fig. 6. The full taxonomy of OTUs appearing in these heatmaps is given in Supporting Information, Tables [Link], [Link]. As expected, the heatmap profiles are consistent with the corresponding PCoA plots. Each soil displayed different community compositions, and the taxa responding to the treatments varied, except for the higher abundance of OTU‐2 in the HD treatment, which likely represents the inoculated strain.

In the Auxonne soil at 7 days, the relative abundance of many OTUs related to the Bacilli class decreased in response to HD treatment. The abundances of several also decreased in microcosms exposed to LD treatment. By 56 days, the heatmaps of the HD and control treatments were similar, whereas the abundances of many OTUs related to the Actinobacteria and Alphaproteobacteria classes decreased in the LD, B and CF treatments. At this time point, the abundances of several Bacilli OTUs increased in the B and CF treatments.

In the Bretenière soil, OTUs related to various taxa responded similarly to the LD and HD treatments at 7 days, whereas the heatmaps of B and CF treatments remained similar to that of the control. At 56 days, the heatmaps of all treatments diverged from that of the control, showing similar variations among various bacterial taxa, such as an increase in the abundances of several OTUs related to Bacilli and a decrease in the abundances of different Thermoleophilia, Alphaproteobacteria and Vicinamibacteria.

In the Trugny soil at 7 days, the heatmap of the microcosm exposed to the LD treatment was very different from that of the control, with a marked increase in the abundance of several OTUs related to Bacilli and a decrease in the abundances of several OTUs related to Alphaproteobacteria, Acidobacteria, Clostridia and Actinobacteria among others. A similar trend was observed for the HD treatment, although to a lesser extent, in terms of number OTUs showing a decrease in abundance. At 56 days, the heatmap of samples exposed to LD treatment remained different from that of the control, with a persistence of an increase in the abundance of OTUs related to Bacilli and a decrease in the abundance of OTUs related to Nitrospira, Verrucomicrobiae, Gammaproteobacteria or Acidobacteria as well as other Bacilli. At 56 days, the heatmap of soils exposed to HD treatment was similar to that of the control, whereas the heatmaps of soils treated with B and CF were similar to each other, with an increase in the abundances of a range of OTUs related to Alphaproteobacteria, Actinobacteria and Thermophilia, and a decrease in several OTUs related to Bacilli.

On the fungal side, more OTUs were affected by the treatments. Heatmaps representing the 30 most abundant OTUs with statistically significant variations between at least one treatment and the control are shown in Fig. 7. The full taxonomy of OTUs appearing in these heatmaps is given in Supporting Information, Tables [Link], [Link]. In the Auxonne and Trugny soils, many of the fungal OTUs that increased in response to LD and HD treatments belonged to the classes Mortierellomycetes and Tremellomycetes. OTUs from the class Sordariomycetes were often found to decrease in response to the treatments. Morteriellomycetes OTUs were particularly abundant in these soils, representing 5.7% of the sequences in the Trugny control soil at 56 days and 10.7% in the same soil inoculated with LD SCL1. In the Bretenière soil, an increase in Tremellomycetes, Leotiomycetes and Spizellomycetes OTUs was observed in the LD treatment at 7 days. These Tremellomycetes OTUs are also abundant in this soil with 4.1% of the total sequences in the control at 7 days and 10.6% the corresponding soil inoculated with the low dose SCL1.

DISCUSSION

Survival of the biocontrol strain SCL1 in soil microcosms

The survival of bacterial inoculants in soils following application is a key parameter that should be assessed because it influences bacterial efficiency, environmental fate and possible side effects on non‐target organisms. To the best of our knowledge, most studies have examined the mid‐term survival (typically up to 3 months) of bacterial inoculants applied to soil. 15 , 16 , 17 , 33 , 34 Some researchers have explored longer‐term bacterial survival, with outcomes varying from a decline below the detection limit after 120–168 days, 35 , 36 to stabilization at relatively high population levels for up to 120 days. 37 A few other studies have reported a rapid decline below the detection limit. 38 , 39

This study is among the few that report prolonged survival of a biocontrol strain, which may be attributed to the sporulation capability of SCL1. The erratic detection of SCL1 at low doses in some replicates at 56 and 168 days in certain soils, coupled with its absence in the Auxonne soil at 56 days followed by its reappearance at 168 days, suggest that the strain can stabilize in soil microcosms at low levels (below the detection limit of the two methods used here), and might evade reliable detection with the used quantification methods.

Interestingly, soil type did not significantly influence the survival of SCL1, which contrasts with findings from a study on the survival of Listeria monocytogenes in 100 arable soils, where persistence was influenced by soil properties: chemical properties affected short‐term survival, and texture influenced long‐term survival 40 . This discrepancy might be due to the fact that only three soils that differed in their properties were used to test the survival of SCL1. Two of the three tested soils, Trugny and Auxonne, are relatively similar in chemical composition and mostly differ in texture with Auxonne being sandier. By contrast, the Bretenière soil has higher carbon, nitrogen, organic matter and CaCO3 content than the two other soils. SCL1 might, therefore, exhibit different survival capacities if a broader range of soils were tested.

Limitations of qPCR and selective plate counts to detect and quantify specific bacteria in soil

Two methods were used to assess the survival of strain SCL1 in soils: selective plate counts and qPCR quantification of a strain‐specific DNA sequence. Plate counting was the only method efficient enough to detect the strain in microcosms treated with the lower dose, because of the rather high limit of detection imposed by the DNA extraction and purification steps required for qPCR analysis (3 × 104 specific sequences/g soil dw compared with 6 × 100 CFU/g soil dw for plate counting). Nonetheless, following their inoculation, bacteria can enter a viable but non‐culturable state, as observed in several studies, 41 , 42 which may contribute to underestimates of the population size of SCL1 in soil using plate counting.

Conversely, the substantial differences in the size of SCL1 populations estimated using plate counting or qPCR (e.g. significantly higher numbers of SCL1 detected by qPCR than by plate counting), which was particularly noticeable on day 7 for the highest dose applied, likely result from qPCR detecting DNA from dead SCL1 cells. This can lead to an overestimation of the population size of living SCL1 in the soil. 43 Both methods have drawbacks, plate counting, however, offers a more precise evaluation of survival because of its lower detection limit. A combination of the two methods is of interest to estimate the survival of a bacterial inoculant in soil following its application.

Alternative techniques could also be used, although all have limitations. One technique is RNA quantification by Reverse Transcription–qPCR (R‐qPCR), which, in principle, reflects metabolically active cells. 44 For example, qPCR primers designed on specific genes could be used after extraction and reverse transcription of soil RNA. Nonetheless, RNA copy number for a given gene can vary between active cells, which complicates accurate quantification. In addition, RNA‐based RT–qPCR analyses are challenging because RNA is highly unstable, prone to degradation by abundant RNases, and often co‐extracted with soil‐derived inhibitors. 44 Other methods such as Fluorescent In Situ Hybridization (FISH), CAtalyzed Reporter Deposition‐FISH (CARD‐FISH) or Flow‐FISH are particularly promising because they use a specific fluorescent probe that targets the cells of interest and quantifies them through microscopy or flux cytometry. 45 , 46 Although these techniques are highly sensitive, their main limitations remain laborious sample preparation and the requirement for specialized equipment and expertise. 45 , 46 , 47

Biocontrol strain SCL1 had a minor impact on microbial communities

Soil dependency of the impact of fungicidal treatments on bacterial and fungal communities

The response of microbial communities to the different treatments was highly soil dependent. The lower overall impact of the treatments in Bretenière soil compared with Trugny and Auxonne soils could be attributed to its higher organic matter content and its greater clay content. Higher organic matter may buffer the soil against changes induced by the addition of nutrients in the LD and HD treatments, and the greater clay content could reduce the immediate availability of these nutrients to microorganisms, 48 which in turn reduces the visible effects.

The same mechanisms may explain why commercial biocontrol (Rhapsody®) and chemical (tebuconazole) fungicides have a prolonged effect on bacterial alpha‐diversity in the Auxonne soil only. This soil, being sandy and low in organic matter, may allow greater bioavailability of the applied pesticides, potentially enhancing their toxicity toward the resident microbial community. 49 , 50

Abundance of bacterial and fungal communities

Despite the different fungicidal treatments being applied to soil microcosms, none led to significant changes in the abundance of the fungal community, which was stable throughout the experiment. This is in accordance with previous reports. 51 , 52 However, the abundance of the bacterial community was affected by the fungicidal treatment applied. The increase in 16S rRNA gene copy number in both the LD and HD treatments 7 days after inoculation may be due to bacterial proliferation following the addition of nutrients present in the remaining culture medium added together with the inoculant. Such proliferation upon nutrient addition is not surprising, because bacterial growth in soil is notoriously limited by nutrient availability and can be triggered by nutrient addition. 53 , 54 This proliferation was only temporary because 16S rRNA gene copies returned closer to control levels by 56 days after inoculation in the Bretenière and Trugny soils, possibly due to complete consumption of the extra nutrients.

Composition and diversity of bacterial and fungal communities

As observed for bacterial abundance, the composition and diversity of the bacterial community were more responsive to the different fungicidal treatments applied than the fungal community.

Both LD and HD treatments had a noticeable impact on bacterial alpha‐diversity; although HD always led to resilience at 56 days, the impact of LD was more persistent, which is rather surprising. Insights into the underlying mechanisms by which the LD treatment may be more impactful than the HD treatment are discussed below.

In terms of beta‐diversity, the strong negative response of many Bacilli OTUs in the HD treatment in one of the soils may be attributed to competition with the inoculated biocontrol strain in high amounts. Indeed being a Bacillus sp., the SCL1 inoculant may compete with indigenous Bacilli for similar ecological niches, particularly in oligotrophic environments such as soil in which resources and space are limited, despite the fact that this genus is known to occupy a variety of niches. 55

The commercial biocontrol and chemical fungicides exhibited similar variations in the composition of bacterial communities, particularly after 56 days, whereas the biocontrol strain SCL1 mainly induced a very different response. The observed effects were not, however, necessarily stronger in the HD treatment; in fact, the HD treatment appeared to exhibit resilience after 56 days in the Auxonne soil, with the bacterial profile returning to a state similar to the control. By contrast, the B and CF treatments remained or started to be significantly different from the control at this time point. It should be noted that the effects on microbial communities described in this study remain relatively minor.

Although the effects on fungal communities were even more modest, they are still noteworthy. The increase in different abundant taxa of Mortierellomycetes and Tremellomycetes attributed to Mortierella and Solicoccozyma, observed in the LD treatments, could be beneficial in an agricultural context, given their well‐known plant growth‐promoting abilities. 56 , 57 Promotion of other plant growth‐promoting microorganisms following the introduction of a biocontrol Bacillus amyloliquefaciens strain has already been reported in a field experiment with an antagonist of tobacco wilt disease. 58 Another important effect in this biocontrol context is the decrease of Sordariomycetes further assigned to the renowned plant pathogens Fusarium or Colletotrichum. 59 , 60 These decreases were observed not only in the LD treatment, but also in the HD treatment in the Auxonne soil. Other OTUs promoted in this soil, time and treatment are known for their role in contaminant biodegradation and plant material degradation. 61 , 62 By contrast, the commercial biocontrol and chemical fungicide had minimal impact on the fungal communities; if their effectiveness is confirmed, this could indicate a strong specificity against their target, S. sclerotiorum, which was not inoculated in our experiment and did not appear to be naturally present in the soils used, because it was not identified in the sequencing data.

The effects of microbial inoculants on soil microbial communities have been reported with varying outcomes; some studies highlight changes in structure, composition and diversity with small effect sizes, whereas others report only transient effects or no effects at all. 20 , 21 A recent review argued that the effects of microbial inoculants are often overlooked and wrongly considered unimportant. 20 However, these studies often based their interpretations on the fact that the effects of time, seasonal variations or plant growth stages are more pronounced than the effects of the inoculants. 58 , 63 , 64 , 65 In our study, although some variations in the microbial communities were detected and discussed, they only affected a small portion of the both bacterial and fungal communities. In fact, temporal variations were at least as significant as those induced by the treatments. Moreover, the few changes detected in the fungal communities could be regarded as beneficial in an agricultural context. The implications of the variations in bacterial composition are more challenging to interpret. The low‐dose inoculation of the biocontrol strain SCL1, the treatment mimicking application of the biocontrol product at an agronomical dose, displayed an impact without resilience on several components of the bacterial community analyses, in contrast to the HD treatment. This impact is difficult to explain because the only difference between the LD and HD treatments is that LD exhibits a smaller number of viable biocontrol cells. Difficult mechanisms of interaction may be in play leading to these counterintuitive results. Two studies discovered quorum‐sensing mediated feedback inhibition effects in different bacteria. 66 , 67 This type of mechanism could lead to the inhibition of antifungal compound production by SCL1 in high populations (HD treatment), but the ecological advantage of such behavior remains obscure. Another hypothesis could be that the antifungal compounds are fixed to the SCL1 biomass when added in high concentration (HD treatment) and are therefore not bioavailable in the soil and cannot exhibit their effect on the resident microbial communities. For instance, lipopeptides are very well known for their amphiphilic properties and therefore their ability to interact with bacterial membranes. 68 In addition, several members of the Bacillus genus are known to produce antifungal lipopeptides, 69 , 70 and it is likely that the mode of action of SCL1 also relies on production of these types of molecules. Although the LD inoculation of biocontrol strain SCL1 is the treatment that induced the most changes in the microbial communities, its impact on fungal communities might still be positive. Indeed, the mechanisms by which biocontrol microorganisms and products act are not limited to direct inhibition of pathogens. They can also work indirectly by promoting other Plant Growth Promoting (PGP) microorganisms that enhance plant health and strengthen their defenses, or inhibit other taxa that pathogens depend on, for example. 71 , 72 Therefore, some authors have suggested that changes in community structure and composition should not necessarily be regarded as negative, especially when reporting on the increase in PGP microorganisms. 73 It is consequently necessary to improve our understanding on the true implications of these changes, notably by looking at the functional aspects, rather than relying solely on microbial community structure and composition. 21

Further to investigating the effect of SCL1 on microbial functions, another important perspective would be to consider its combined impact with other intrants or co‐contaminants. Agricultural environments are typically exposed to a wide range of pollutants (e.g. different pesticides, microplastics, antibiotics), and mixtures have been shown to have stronger effects on microbial communities than individual compounds alone. 74 , 75 In addition, because the efficacy and ease of use of biocontrols does not fully match that of chemical pesticides, they are frequently applied together with reduced chemical doses, 76 making the ecotoxicological impacts of such mixtures a key point to investigate.

CONCLUSION

This study provides an initial ecotoxicological assessment of the side effects of a biocontrol strain antagonistic to Sclerotinia sclerotiorum compared with those of biological and chemical fungicides already available on the market. The strain demonstrated its persistence in soil microcosms for at least 6 months at both tested doses, with only minor impacts on bacterial and fungal communities; similarly to the commercial biocontrol agent and the chemical fungicide. The potential promotion of plant growth‐promoting microorganisms suggests that SCL1 culture‐derived metabolites co‐applied with bacterial inoculant could have indirect benefits beyond pathogen control. Further research at a field scale is needed to assess the efficacy of this biosolution to control S. sclerotiorum in rapeseed crops and to assess its possible side effects on soil‐living organisms and supported ecosystemic functions under an agronomical exposure scheme.
