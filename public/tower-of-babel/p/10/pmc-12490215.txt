Addressing clinical needs in NSCLC immunotherapy: Mechanisms of resistance and promising combination strategies
Authors: Kai Kang, Shanghai Liu, Zhuoran Yao, Jianxin Xue, You Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryPD-(L)1 monoclonal antibody-based immunotherapy has become the cornerstone of treatment for non-small cell lung cancer without driver gene alterations. However, resistance, including primary and acquired resistance, remains a major clinical challenge. In this review, we summarize the delayed separation of progression-free survival curves observed in randomized clinical trials, which reflects the emergence of resistance at different treatment stages and the dynamic evolution of anti-tumor immune responses. The development of effective combination strategies should therefore be guided by a thorough understanding of the complex biological mechanisms governing tumor-immune interactions. We review the underlying mechanisms of immunotherapy resistance and propose potential combination strategies. Finally, we emphasize several key considerations for future clinical trial design, including the sequencing of combination therapies, biomarker-guided treatment selection, and optimized management of treatment-related toxicities.Keywords: non-small cell lung cancer, immunotherapy resistance, combination treatment

Introduction

Lung cancer is among the most common and lethal cancers, and non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases.1 In recent years, immune checkpoint blockade, represented by programmed death 1 (PD-1) and programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) inhibitors, has shown the potential to induce profound and durable responses in patients with lung cancer. Several pivotal trials, such as KEYNOTE-024 and IMpower110, have shown that, for patients with advanced NSCLC without epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) or anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) genetic alterations, immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) monotherapy can improve clinical efficacy endpoints in patients with high PD-L1 expression.2,3 Furthermore, combining ICIs with chemotherapy demonstrated significantly better efficacy than chemotherapy alone, regardless of PD-L1 expression status, highlighting the potential of combination immunotherapy strategies.4,5 This significant therapeutic advancement has led to the rapid incorporation of ICIs into the treatment paradigm for most patients with advanced NSCLC.

Unfortunately, despite the overall improvement in efficacy observed in clinical trials, some patients remain completely unresponsive to ICIs and experience rapid disease progression owing to primary resistance, even when ICIs are combined with chemotherapy.6,7 Furthermore, more than half of patients who initially respond to ICIs eventually experience disease progression, indicating the challenge of acquired resistance.8 As a result of both primary and acquired resistance, most patients ultimately fail to benefit from ICI treatment, with only a minority of patients achieving the remarkable benefit of durable responses.8 These resistance patterns underscore the complexity of anti-tumor immunity and the need for more refined therapeutic strategies to improve the clinical outcomes of patients with NSCLC.

In this review, we aimed to summarize recent advances in numerous clinical trials of immunotherapy for NSCLC, integrating them into the resistance characteristics of immunotherapy. This emphasizes the distinctive mechanisms underlying various combination approaches used to enhance ICI efficacy. Finally, this review aimed to discuss the challenges and future directions of these combination strategies, including optimizing treatment sequencing, identifying reliable biomarkers, and ensuring the safety of therapeutic regimens.

Characteristics of immunotherapy resistance

Classifications and clinical patterns of resistance

The concepts of primary and acquired resistance to ICIs originate from other anti-tumor therapies such as chemotherapy and targeted therapy.9 According to the consensus of the Society for Immunotherapy of Cancer, resistance to PD-1 pathway blockade can be categorized into three types: primary resistance (also known as innate resistance), acquired resistance (also known as secondary resistance), and disease progression occurring after treatment discontinuation for any reason.10 Primary resistance was defined as evidence of disease progression after receiving ICI therapy or combinations for at least 6 weeks (two cycles) and no more than 6 months. In contrast, acquired resistance refers to disease progression that occurs after achieving clinical benefits, such as a sustained objective response or stable disease lasting 6 months or longer.10 The distinction between these two types of resistance can be mirrored in the progression-free survival (PFS) curves of randomized clinical trials. Primary resistance corresponds to a rapid decline in the survival curve during the early stages of treatment, often overlapping with the survival curve of the control group, which did not receive immunotherapy. The tangling survival curve indicates that around 40%–60% of patients receiving ICI monotherapy or 5%–15% of patients receiving ICI + chemotherapy combinations may not benefit from ICI compared to chemotherapy alone, suggesting primary resistance (Figure 1A; Table S1). The difference between these two types of clinical trials suggests that ICI combined with chemotherapy can partially overcome primary resistance; however, cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted with caution due to methodological limitations. In contrast, acquired resistance was characterized by a gradual and intermittent decline in the survival curve after 6 months of treatment, implying a subpopulation of patients would experience disease progression with initial ICI benefit (Figure 1B).

Mechanisms of primary resistance

Resistance to ICIs differs from chemotherapy and targeted therapy, as it involves both tumor-intrinsic factors and the external tumor microenvironment (TME). Effective immunotherapy depends on a functioning cancer-immune cycle in which tumor cells release antigens, antigen-presenting cells activate and recruit cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), and CTLs recognize and eliminate tumor cells.11 Although considerable heterogeneity in resistance mechanisms exists among patients, primary resistance is generally attributed to intrinsic dysfunctions in the cycle, such as low tumor antigen load, impaired antigen presentation, and defective T cell activation or infiltration. Additionally, an abundance of immunosuppressive cells within the TME and the overexpression of immune checkpoints unrelated to immunotherapy targets can further exacerbate resistance.

Tumor-intrinsic factors of primary resistance

The immunogenicity of tumor cells is largely influenced by their mutation burden and neoantigen load, both of which are critical in shaping effective anti-tumor immune responses.12 Genetic instability, often driven by defective DNA repair mechanisms, further contributes to increased tumor immunogenicity by promoting the accumulation of somatic mutations and expanding the repertoire of potential neoantigens, ultimately facilitating immune recognition and response.13 In contrast, tumors with a low neoantigen burden and proficient DNA repair mechanisms tend to exhibit intrinsic resistance to ICIs. Most driver mutations in non-squamous NSCLC, such as EGFR and ALK, result in non-immunoreactive tumors characterized by low mutational burden and limited T cell infiltration—both recognized as negative predictors of ICI efficacy.14,15 Moreover, alterations in STK11 and KEAP1 can promote an immunosuppressive TME and, collectively, may account for half or more of the primary resistance to anti-PD-(L)1 therapy in patients with non-squamous NSCLC when used as monotherapy.16

In addition, genetic alteration in signaling pathways, such as mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), and interferon-gamma (IFNγ), affects T cell recruitment and antigen presentation through downstream signaling, ultimately contributing to resistance to immunotherapy.7,9 Specifically, oncogenic activation of the MAPK pathway induces the production of various immunosuppressive factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and interleukin (IL)-8, which suppress the infiltration and function of effector T cells within the TME.17 Similarly, loss of PTEN, a common event across multiple cancer types, leads to enhanced phosphoinositide 3-kinase signaling and promotes the secretion of transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) and CXCL10, thereby facilitating the conversion of CD4+ T cells into regulatory T cells (Tregs) and reducing tumor sensitivity to ICIs.18

Tumor-extrinsic factors of primary resistance

Pre-existing tumor-extrinsic factors, including immunosuppressive cells, aberrant angiogenesis, and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), have also been identified as contributors to primary resistance. Myeloid-derived suppressor cells, Tregs, and M2-like tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) suppress ICI efficacy by secreting immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β and by expressing inhibitory immune checkpoints such as cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen-4 (CTLA-4) and T cell immunoglobulin mucin 3 (TIM-3).19,20,21

With advances in research and single-cell transcriptomic technologies, analyses of immunosuppressive cells have progressed from the cellular to the molecular level, revealing significant functional heterogeneity even within the same cell type. Compared to Tregs in normal tissues, those within the TME are more likely to exhibit a CCR8+ phenotype. This subpopulation displays higher expression of immune checkpoints than CCR8− Tregs and is negatively correlated with immunotherapy response in patients with NSCLC.22,23 Moreover, preclinical models have demonstrated that selective depletion of CCR8+ Tregs can synergize with PD-1 blockade, highlighting the potential for translational therapeutic strategies.24

Aberrant tumor vasculature, primarily driven by excessive VEGF, promotes immune evasion through multiple mechanisms: inhibiting CTL trafficking and effector functions, suppressing dendritic cell (DC) maturation and antigen presentation, and facilitating the recruitment and proliferation of the aforementioned immunosuppressive cells.25,26 These immunosuppressive cells further reinforce both angiogenesis and immunosuppression by releasing VEGF, IL-10, and TGF-β.26 In a study on metastatic lung adenocarcinoma, a distinct INSR+ endothelial cell subpopulation was identified as being specifically enriched within tumor tissues. This subpopulation exhibited elevated expression of genes involved in VEGF and Notch signaling pathways, while genes associated with immune activation were markedly downregulated.27

Additionally, CAFs act as physical and functional barriers to T cell recognition of tumor cells, not only hindering T cell infiltration through deposited matrix but also promoting T cell exhaustion via TGF-β signaling and enhancing immune checkpoint expression.28 Further studies have identified distinct CAF subpopulations with stage-specific roles in immune exclusion. MYH11+ CAFs and FAP+ CAFs are implicated in excluding T cells from the tumor nest in early-stage and more advanced-stage tumors, respectively.29 LRRC15 has been recognized as a marker of TGF-β-driven CAFs, which suppress CD8+ T cell function and reduce tumor responsiveness to ICIs.30,31

Mechanisms of acquired resistance

Acquired resistance arises more rapidly after an initial clinical benefit, whereas drivers of primary resistance are more likely rooted in years of co-evolution between the tumor and the host’s immune response.32 This highlights a sharp contrast between the two resistance patterns in terms of their underlying mechanisms and evolutionary dynamics. The mechanisms of acquired resistance can also be divided into tumor-intrinsic and tumor-extrinsic processes.

Tumor-intrinsic factors of acquired resistance

Tumor-intrinsic factors of acquired resistance primarily contribute to defective antigen presentation. Loss of neoantigens during immunotherapy, through either the elimination of tumor subclones or deletion of chromosomal regions, has been linked to acquired resistance.33 Genetic alterations resulting in loss of function of β2-microglobulin, a critical component of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I complexes, and epigenetic modifications that downregulate cell surface MHC class I molecules expression can directly impair antigen presentation.34 Additionally, mutations in Janus kinase (JAK)1, JAK2, STAT1, and IFNγ-related pathways can indirectly disrupt antigen presentation through dysregulation of signaling pathways.32,35

Interestingly, in addition to pathway inactivation caused by relevant mutations, persistent activation of the tumor-intrinsic IFNγ signaling can also lead to acquired resistance. Unlike primary resistance, tumors with acquired resistance often retain or even enhance inflammatory features rather than exhibiting a non-inflamed phenotype. The marked upregulation of IFNγ suggests a persistent yet insufficient anti-tumor immune response, where chronic IFNγ signaling promotes immune evasion by inducing T cell dysfunction and disrupting antigen presentation, thereby underscoring the therapeutic potential of reprogramming this pathway.36

Tumor-extrinsic factors of acquired resistance

Tumor-extrinsic factors also play a crucial role in acquired resistance, with exhaustion of T cell function and reduction in T cell numbers being the primary contributors. In addition to the primary targets, PD-1 and PD-L1, T cell dysfunction is further exacerbated by the cumulative expression of multiple inhibitory receptors. Following the failure of the PD-1 pathway blockade, alternative immune checkpoints, particularly TIM-3, are upregulated in T cells.37,38 Furthermore, after acquired resistance, not only is the number of CTLs in the TME reduced, but the distance between CTLs and tumor cells also increases.35 Collagen proteins COL3A1 and COL6A1 produced by tumor cells can form a barrier that prevents T cell infiltration and impedes their ability to reach and eliminate tumor cells.39 These immunophenotypic changes suggest that the loss of T cell-mediated anti-tumor immune responses contributes significantly to the development of acquired resistance.

Strategies to overcome primary resistance

Radiotherapy: Promoting antigenicity and overcoming immune desertification

Radiotherapy has long been a key component of cancer treatment. In recent years, in addition to its direct cytotoxic effects, radiotherapy exerts immunomodulatory effects on the TME.40 This indicates that the rational application of radiotherapy in combination with immunotherapy holds the potential for synergistic enhancement.

Primary resistance to immunotherapy often stems from insufficient tumor antigenicity and a suppressive TME. Radiotherapy can exert immunostimulatory effects that specifically target these mechanisms, thereby helping to overcome primary resistance to immunotherapy through multiple mechanisms (Figure 2). It enhances the antigenicity of tumor cells by inducing mutations that generate tumor neoantigens, upregulating neoantigen-encoding genes, and increasing the presentation of neoantigens to MHC class I molecules.41,42 These changes improve recognition by CTLs, thereby reducing immune evasion by tumor cells. Additionally, radiotherapy induces immunogenic cell death (ICD), accompanied by the release of diverse damage-associated molecular patterns and the secretion of immunostimulatory or chemotactic cytokines.43,44 These effects contribute to remodeling the immunosuppressive TME and enhancing anti-tumor immune responses.

Overview of primary resistance mechanisms to immunotherapy in NSCLC and potential combination strategies to overcome primary resistance

The potential of radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy has been validated in multiple preclinical NSCLC models, yet the transition to clinical research presents increased complexity.45,46 For patients with early-stage NSCLC who are unable to tolerate or refuse surgery, a phase 2 randomized controlled trial observed that high-dose stereotactic ablative radiotherapy (SABR) combined with nivolumab significantly improved the 4-year event-free survival (EFS) from 53% to 77%, compared to SABR alone.47 However, a recently reported phase 3 randomized controlled trial, KEYNOTE-867, which had a similar design, was terminated early after an interim analysis showed that stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT) combined with pembrolizumab did not improve EFS or overall survival (OS) compared with SBRT alone, resulting in greater toxicity.48 Future phase 3 randomized controlled trials such as PACIFIC-4 and SWOG/NRG S1914 may provide more evidence for the application of radiotherapy combined with immunotherapy in early-stage lung cancer. Notably, these studies varied in the duration of ICI administration in their designs, underscoring the importance of optimizing the timing and sequencing of immunotherapy when combined with radiotherapy.

For patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC, the PACIFIC trial demonstrated that maintenance treatment with durvalumab following concurrent chemoradiotherapy significantly improved survival outcomes compared with placebo.49 Building on this, the PACIFIC-5 trial expanded indications to include maintenance immunotherapy after sequential chemoradiotherapy.50 However, the PACIFIC-2 trial, in which immunotherapy was advanced to the concurrent chemoradiotherapy phase, failed to achieve the primary endpoint of PFS.51 For patients with limited metastatic NSCLC, the phase 2/3 trial NRG-LU002 observed that, for patients without disease progression after four cycles of first-line immunotherapy with or without chemotherapy, consolidative local therapy with radiotherapy or surgery did not significantly improve PFS or OS but was associated with increased toxicity.52

These clinical trials suggest that the combination of immunotherapy with radiotherapy does not automatically mean that it will work well. Radiotherapy has long focused on maximizing tumor cell eradication while minimizing toxicity to healthy tissues, often overlooking its immunomodulatory effects. Considering the dual nature of radiotherapy in immunomodulation, in addition to the immunostimulatory effect, immunosuppressive effects are also available including the accumulation of M2-like TAMs, Tregs, CAFs, and other immunosuppressive cells under the support of TGF-β, as well as lymph node dysfunction and reduction of immune effector cells caused by radiation.44,53 A reasonable combination therapy should consider how to optimize the timing, regimens, and target volumes of radiotherapy based on the mechanisms of immunotherapy, thereby adapting radiotherapy to synergize with immunotherapy.53

Implementing the concept of adaptive immunoradiotherapy involves refining radiation doses, fractionation schedules, and radiation fields to minimize damage to immune effector cells. Optimization of the sequencing of radiotherapy and immunotherapy is also required to enhance the synergistic effects while limiting toxicity to normal tissues. Several studies have explored the feasibility of this concept. In patients with metastatic NSCLC, a strategy combining SBRT for small lesions aimed at releasing tumor antigens to prime CTLs and low-dose radiotherapy (LDRT) for larger lesions designed to reshape the TME along with immunotherapy has been investigated.54,55 Given the inherently weak anti-tumor effects of LDRT, the response observed in larger lesions is likely attributable to immune activation resulting from the combined therapy. For patients with resectable NSCLC, a single-arm phase 2 clinical trial, SACTION01, explored the use of SBRT (24 Gy in three fractions) as an immunomodulator, featuring a shorter course, lower dose, and avoidance of lymph node irradiation (even in cases with lymph node metastasis). This study discovered that incorporating immunomodulatory SBRT followed by sequential immunochemotherapy as neoadjuvant therapy resulted in a higher major pathological response rate of 76% and a higher pathological complete response rate of 52% than previously reported neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy regimens.56 Additionally, spatially fractionated radiotherapy, originally designed to create an uneven dose distribution within the tumor to ensure effective tumor control while minimizing radiation exposure to the surrounding normal tissues, exhibits remarkable immunostimulatory effects.57,58

Taken together, the inconsistent results observed across clinical trials may stem from heterogeneity in study designs, including variations in radiation dose, fractionation, target volume, and the timing or duration of immunotherapy.59 Therefore, the key to future progress lies in the rational design of combination strategies that amplify the immune-enhancing properties of radiotherapy, such as enhancing antigen release, while minimizing lymphocyte depletion and unintended immune suppression. Additional clinical trials are required to validate the feasibility and optimize the implementation of such combination therapies.

Anti-angiogenic therapy: Normalizing vasculature to facilitate immune cell infiltration

Dysregulation of pro- and anti-angiogenic factors leading to abnormal vasculature is a hallmark of tumors. In addition to promoting tumor invasion and metastasis, an abnormal tumor vasculature induces an immunosuppressive TME.26 Anti-angiogenic therapy, primarily through drugs targeting VEGF-dependent signaling, can normalize the tumor vasculature, enhance the infiltration of immune effector cells into the TME. Moreover, vascular normalization reduces the recruitment of immunosuppressive cell populations, ultimately synergizing with immunotherapy to overcome primary resistance (Figure 2).26

The combination of anti-angiogenic therapy and immunotherapy has been a long-standing area of development, with multiple phase 3 randomized controlled trials exploring the efficacy of combination treatments in different populations of patients with NSCLC. For patients with driver mutation-positive NSCLC whose disease progresses while receiving targeted therapy, optimal treatment options remain limited. Both the CheckMate 722 and KEYNOTE-789 trials failed to demonstrate that adding immunotherapy to platinum-based doublet chemotherapy provides PFS or OS benefits for patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC who progressed to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI) therapy.60,61 This suggests that the combination of chemotherapy and immunotherapy cannot overcome the primary resistance to immunotherapy conferred by driver mutations. The IMpower150 trial showed that adding atezolizumab to bevacizumab plus platinum-based doublet chemotherapy significantly improved both PFS and OS in the overall population, as well as in a subpopulation of patients with EGFR mutations or ALK translocations.62 However, this result was not validated in the subsequent IMpower151 trial, which included numerous patients with EGFR mutations or ALK translocations.63 The ATTLAS trial demonstrated that, in patients with EGFR mutations or ALK translocations who had progressed after prior TKI therapy, adding atezolizumab and bevacizumab to platinum-based doublet chemotherapy significantly improved PFS, with a median PFS of 8.48 months compared to 5.62 months in the chemotherapy group. However, this PFS benefit did not translate into an OS advantage, as the median OS in both groups was approximately 20 months64

Interim analyses of the ORIENT-31 and HARMONi-A trials provided promising results for patients with EGFR-mutated non-squamous NSCLC who progressed with EGFR-TKI therapy. The ORIENT-31 study showed that the combination of sintilimab, the bevacizumab biosimilar IBI305, and chemotherapy significantly improved PFS, yielding a median PFS of 5.5 months, compared to 4.3 months with chemotherapy alone.65 Similarly, the HARMONi-A trial demonstrated that adding ivonescimab, a bispecific antibody (BsAb) targeting PD-1 and VEGF, to chemotherapy significantly improved PFS in patients with advanced or metastatic NSCLC after EGFR-TKI failure, with a median PFS of 7.1 months compared to 4.8 months66 However, whether these two trials demonstrate OS benefits requires a longer follow-up period. The heterogeneity in results across these clinical trials may stem from differences in the baseline characteristics of the enrolled populations, particularly the patterns of resistance to targeted therapy, and the variability in therapeutic agents used. Nevertheless, combining immunotherapy and anti-angiogenic therapy represents a highly promising approach for overcoming immunotherapy resistance in patients with driver mutation-positive NSCLC.

The combination of immunotherapy and anti-angiogenic therapy also holds substantial promise for patients with advanced NSCLC lacking EGFR or ALK genetic alterations. While the previously mentioned IMpower150 and IMpower151 trials showed inconsistent benefits for patients who progressed to targeted therapies for driver mutations, the subgroup of patients without driver mutations consistently demonstrated a trend toward survival benefits.62,63 Furthermore, preliminary analysis from the HARMONi-2 trial revealed that ivonescimab monotherapy significantly prolonged PFS compared to pembrolizumab monotherapy, with a median PFS of 11.1 months versus 5.8 months for patients with PD-L1-positive NSCLC.67

In summary, the divergent outcomes observed across clinical trials of anti-angiogenic therapy combined with immunotherapy may be attributed to two main factors. First, patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC often exhibit a more immunosuppressive TME, which limits the efficacy of immunotherapy. Second, differences in drug design also play a critical role. In particular, BsAb such as ivonescimab may achieve better spatiotemporal synergy by co-targeting VEGF and PD-1, thereby enhancing therapeutic specificity while minimizing toxicity and efficacy dilution.68 However, the precise mechanism requires further investigation.

Antibody-drug conjugates: Inducing ICD and bystander killing

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) have recently emerged as a focal point for lung cancer treatment. ADCs typically comprise three key components: an antibody that targets tumor cell surface antigens, a cytotoxic payload, and a chemical linker that connects them. Once ADC binds to the surface of a tumor cell, it becomes internalized and transported into the cell, where the cytotoxic payload is released within lysosomes, exerting its anti-tumor effects.69 Meanwhile, the released drug can passively diffuse across the cell membrane to the neighboring cells, inducing a bystander cell death. ADCs have demonstrated the potential for synergistic enhancement of immunotherapy by promoting ICD, increasing T cell infiltration, and inducing DC maturation (Figure 2).70,71 These effects contribute to the transformation of immunologically inert tumors into immune-inflamed phenotypes, thereby overcoming key mechanisms underlying primary resistance to immunotherapy. In preclinical studies, combining ADCs with immunotherapy has successfully overcome the primary resistance to immunotherapy in NSCLC models.71

The most commonly targeted antigens for ADCs in treating NSCLC are trophoblast cell surface antigen 2 (TROP2), HER2, and HER3, which are widely overexpressed in the tumor cell membranes of patients with NSCLC. ADCs targeting TROP2 have been extensively explored in combination with immunotherapy. Several early clinical trials have mutually validated the safety and feasibility of ADCs targeting TROP2, with various designs, as first-line treatments in combination with immunotherapy for NSCLC.72,73,74,75 The OptiTROP-Lung01 study, a phase 2 trial, evaluated different dosing regimens of the TROP2 ADC (sacituzumab tirumotecan) combined with tagitanlimab (anti-PD-L1) in patients with advanced NSCLC. The objective response rates (ORRs) were 48.6% and 77.6% for the every-2-week group. The 6-month PFS rates were 69.2% and 84.6%, respectively, with manageable safety profiles.74 In the cohort A of another phase 2 trial, EVOKE-02, the combination of TROP2 ADC (sacituzumab govitecan) and pembrolizumab was investigated in patients with advanced NSCLC with PD-L1 expression ≥50%. Among the 30 patients, the ORR was 67% and the median PFS was 13.1 months.75

ADCs are emerging as rising stars in precision chemotherapy with significant potential for combined application with immunotherapy. However, the current diversity and heterogeneity of ADCs pose challenges. Large-scale clinical trials are essential for evaluating the efficacy of different targets, ADC designs, and bispecific ADCs in combination with immunotherapies.

Small-molecule drugs: Targeting tumor-intrinsic pathways

Small-molecule drugs and antibody-based therapies, based on immunoglobulin frameworks, are two distinct classes of therapeutic agents. Compared with small-molecule drugs, monoclonal antibodies exhibit higher specificity for binding to their targets. However, owing to their prolonged half-life, immune-related adverse events associated with antibodies are difficult to mitigate through dose adjustments and often require treatment discontinuation for 1–2 months to alleviate symptoms.76 Additionally, antibodies are generally limited to targeting proteins on the cell membrane, whereas small-molecule drugs can act via intracellular pathways. Many small-molecule drugs possess immunostimulatory properties and can act synergistically with ICIs. This discussion focuses on the exploration of several key strategies for treating patients with NSCLC, including targeting pro-tumor pathways, interfering with DNA damage response (DDR) mechanisms, and modulating innate immune pathways (Figure 2).

Mutations in the KRAS gene family were once considered undruggable; however, the KRASG12C mutation has emerged as a breakthrough target. KRASG12C inhibitors have demonstrated the ability to reverse immunosuppressive TME and sensitize tumors to immunotherapy in preclinical models.77 The phase 2 KRYSTAL-7 study evaluated the efficacy and safety of combining the KRASG12C inhibitor, adagrasib, with pembrolizumab as a first-line treatment for advanced NSCLC with KRASG12C mutations. Among patients with PD-L1 expression ≥50%, the ORR was 63%, and the safety profile was manageable.78 Building on these promising results, an ongoing phase 3 trial (NCT04613596) is anticipated. Additionally, preclinical studies have shown that KRASG12D inhibitors can induce FAS expression in tumor cells and promote CTL-mediated cell death, providing a theoretical foundation for synergistic immunotherapy.79

During active DNA replication, tumor cells inevitably accumulate replication errors. Typically, the DDR recognizes DNA damage and activates repair mechanisms to maintain genomic integrity. However, a faulty DDR can lead to the production of immunogenic DNA fragments and can be recognized by receptors such as cGAS-STING, thereby stimulating anti-tumor immunity.80 This provides a theoretical basis for combining DDR inhibitors with immunotherapy. The phase 2 umbrella trial HUDSON investigated biomarker-guided combinations of durvalumab with agents targeting different pathways in patients resistant to immunotherapy. Notably, the combination of durvalumab and the ATR protein kinase inhibitor ceralasertib demonstrated significant clinical efficacy, particularly in patients with ATM mutations or low ATM protein expression, achieving an ORR of 26.1%.81 Additionally, poly-ADP ribose polymerase (PARP) inhibitors have been successfully applied to other tumors with homologous recombination repair (HRR) deficiencies. However, findings from the HUDSON trial and another phase 2 trial (UNITO-001) revealed that NSCLC patients with HRR mutations showed limited sensitivity to a combination of PARP inhibitors and immunotherapy, with an ORR of <10%.81,82 Given that targeting DDR aims to activate downstream innate immune pathways such as cGAS-STING through immunogenic DNA fragments, directly using small-molecule drugs to activate innate immune pathways can also potentially enhance immunotherapy. Preclinical studies have demonstrated clear efficacy; however, this approach remains in the early stages of clinical investigation. Future efforts should focus on optimizing drug delivery methods or designing STING agonists with tumor specificity to minimize systemic adverse effects.76,80

Strategies to overcome acquired resistance

While various strategies with ongoing clinical evidence are exploring ways to remodel the TME and overcome primary resistance to immune checkpoint blockade, there remains a need for approaches that sustain long-term anti-tumor immune responses. Considering the potential factors that drive acquired resistance, such as T cell exhaustion and defective antigen presentation, future strategies may focus on two main tactics: prolonging T cell persistence or boosting antigen recognition of T cells. These efforts aim to establish a lasting anti-tumor response and raise the tail of the survival curve.

Dual blockade immunotherapy: Reversing T cell dysfunction

Immunosuppressive mechanisms in TME induce T cell exhaustion and anergy, leading to attenuated anti-tumor immune responses. This attenuation contributes to the development of acquired resistance to immunotherapy. Blocking these inhibitory signals or strengthening T cell function through co-stimulation with agonists is an effective approach for sustaining T cell activity (Figure 3).

Overview of acquired resistance mechanisms to immunotherapy in NSCLC and potential combination strategies to overcome acquired resistance

By releasing the brakes between antigen-presenting cells and the initial activation phase of T cells, a dual checkpoint blockade combining PD-1/PD-L1 and CTLA-4 monoclonal antibodies should elicit more robust activation of the immune system than PD-1 inhibition alone. However, randomized controlled trials have indicated that this dual immunotherapeutic approach may not achieve the anticipated synergistic effects.83,84,85 Targeting other immune checkpoints, including T cell immunoreceptors with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT), lymphocyte activation gene 3, and TIM-3, has not led to significant advancements in NSCLC, despite achieving limited success.86,87,88

Interestingly, the advent of BsAbs has overcome this impasse. Advances in molecular biology and genetic engineering have facilitated the design of more delicate and precise structural constructs. Therefore, BsAbs are not merely cocktails of two monoclonal antibodies; instead, they exhibit enhanced synergistic therapeutic effects. BsAbs targeting PD-1 × CTLA-4, including volrustomig and cadonilimab, have increased accumulation in the TME and preferable CTLA-4 blockade on PD-1+-activated T cells compared to PD-1– resting peripheral T cells.89,90 By leveraging the spatial disparity in CTLA-4 inhibition, the activation of PD-1+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) can be more effectively maintained. Additionally, their co-localized activation enables simultaneous dual targeting on the same or adjacent T cells, leading to synergistic immune activation beyond the additive effects of separate monoclonal antibodies.91 Currently, volrustomig and cadonilimab have shown promising anti-tumor activity combined with chemotherapy and manageable safety as first-line treatments for advanced NSCLC (LungCadX).92,93,94 Phase 3 randomized trials comparing PD-1 × CTLA-4 BsAb plus chemotherapy with PD-1 antibody plus chemotherapy are currently ongoing.95

In addition to the co-inhibition of immunosuppressive receptors, combinations of PD-1 blockade with agonist antibodies against costimulatory receptors can take the foot off the brake and simultaneously hit the gas of T cells. ATG-101 is a tetravalent PD-L1 × 4-1BB BsAb with high PD-L1 affinity and low 4-1BB affinity.96 ATG-101 can activate exhausted T cells upon PD-L1 binding, indicating its possible role in reversing T cell dysfunction. The development of agonistic antibodies against costimulatory molecules is essential for future trials.

Adoptive cell transfer: Replenishment of exogenous T cells

While endogenous T cell function could be maintained through immune signaling pathway agonists/antagonists, supplementation with exogenous tumor-specific T cells may offer a promising alternative strategy. Previous studies have demonstrated that effective anti-tumor immunity can be restored by replenishing the TME with fresh, non-exhausted immune cells originating from peripheral sites, suggesting the importance of active T cell trafficking.97 Adoptive cell transfer (ACT) is a form of passive immunotherapy that involves isolating immune cells from patients and genetically modifying them to enhance their specificity and efficacy against tumor cells.98 These modified cells were then expanded ex vivo to achieve sufficient numbers before being re-infused into the patient’s body. This approach circumvents the limitations posed by intrinsic immune cell exhaustion within the TME and introduces highly specialized effector cells capable of mounting a robust and sustained anti-tumor response (Figure 3).

The disruption of the PD-L1/PD-1 signaling pathways inherently complements ACT strategies, as T cells naturally undergo activation-induced upregulation of co-inhibitory pathways, which can limit anti-tumor immune responses when T cells bind to their complementary ligands.99 Numerous preclinical studies have validated the synergistic effects of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade and cellular therapies, and a combination of anti-PD-(L)1 therapy with ACT is being tested in early-stage clinical trials.100,101,102 Moreover, besides the cell-extrinsic delivery of checkpoint blockade antibodies, cell-intrinsic genetic engineering strategies, including switch receptor construction, blocking single-chain variable fragment secretion, and CRISPR-Cas9 or TALEN gene editing to interfere with PD-1/PD-L1 signaling, are attractive alternatives for enhancing cellular therapies.99 Lu et al. evaluated the safety and feasibility of CRISPR-Cas9 PD-1-edited T cells in patients with refractory NSCLC.103 The persistence of edited T cells in the peripheral and intratumoral regions supports the potential efficacy of PD-1 editing in T cells. Notably, one patient achieved a metabolic tumor response and experienced stable disease lasting 76 weeks. Nevertheless, this study used T cells from peripheral blood for gene editing, which may not have included a sufficient population of tumor-reactive T cells. Therefore, more precise tumor-targeted cellular therapies should be developed in the future, including ex vivo expansion of TILs and administration of antigen-specific engineered T cells (via transduction with chimeric antigen receptors [CARs] or T cell receptors [TCRs]).

TILs

TIL therapy has emerged early in the field of cancer immunotherapy; however, its development has been fraught with numerous challenges. Over the years, TIL therapy has undergone a tumultuous journey characterized by technical hurdles in isolating and expanding sufficient numbers of functional lymphocytes, a lack of in vivo persistence, and severe toxicities. A proof-of-concept trial compared TIL therapy with ipilimumab for treating late-stage melanoma.104 Eighty-four patients received TILs and ipilimumab each. Patients who received TIL therapy had a higher ratio of objective responses and a significantly longer PFS. In parallel, lifileucel, an autologous, unmodified TIL therapy, has also demonstrated encouraging results in patients with unresectable stage III and IV melanoma in the post-PD-1 inhibitor setting, leading to it being the first immune cell therapy to win Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval for treating solid tumors.105,106,107 This new groundbreaking approval of TIL therapy will pave the way for advances in other cancer types such as NSCLC.

The lone-completed trial utilizing TILs as therapy was conducted as a single-arm phase 1 trial of TILs administered with nivolumab in 20 patients with advanced NSCLC following initial progression to nivolumab monotherapy.108 TIL therapy is generally safe with considerable clinical efficacy in NSCLC. Lifileucel is currently under investigation in a multicentre phase 2 trial (IOV-COM-202). Lifileucel alone demonstrated an ORR of 21% in patients with refractory metastatic NSCLC resistant to ICIs (including PD-L1-negative, low tumor mutational burden, and STK11 mutation).109 Moreover, a recently released abstract updated the efficacy and safety results in an expanded cohort of patients with ICI-naive metastatic NSCLC treated with lifileucel combined with pembrolizumab.102 The ORR (95% confidence interval) in the EGFR wild-type subgroup was 64.3% (35.1%–87.2%). The robust anti-tumor activity and durable responses imply a promising future for the lifileucel/ICI combination as a frontline therapy in metastatic NSCLC.

TCR-engineered T cells

TCRs are pivotal mediators in the activation, recognition, and cytolysis of tumor cells by T lymphocytes. When TCR binds to an MHC-peptide antigen complex on the surface of a tumor cell, it triggers signal transduction pathways that activate T lymphocytes, initiating an immune response. The diversity of TCRs arises from the random rearrangement of multiple separate gene segments during V(D)J recombination in germline genes. This diversity endows individuals with an almost limitless capacity for antigen recognition and response, enabling TCRs to identify a wide array of tumor antigens and exert cytotoxic and surveillance functions against tumor cells.110

TCR-engineered T cell (TCR-T) therapy leverages genetic engineering techniques to modify a patient’s T cells, enabling them to express specific TCRs that recognize and target tumor-specific antigens. Currently, the mainstream TCR-T targets are classified into two categories. The former targets common tumor-associated antigens, including New York esophageal squamous cell carcinoma 1 (NY-ESO-1), melanoma-associated antigen A4 (MAGE-A4), human papillomavirus type 16, and GP100, allowing for broader clinical applicability and wider patient coverage.111 Notably, TCR-T therapy targeting MAGE-A4, an afamitresgene autoleucel, resulted in durable responses in heavily pre-treated patients with human leukocyte antigens (HLA)-A∗02- and MAGE-A4-expressing synovial sarcoma, becoming the first globally approved TCR-T therapy.112 Xia et al. explored the safety and efficacy of NY-ESO-1 TCR-T cells in four HLA-A2-positive patients with NSCLC. Two patients showed clinical responses to NY-ESO-1 TCR-T cell therapy, including a 44-year-old female patient who achieved a short-term partial response for 4 months113

Tumor neoantigen-specific TCRs can be identified using high-throughput sequencing technology, thereby developing patient-specific TCR-T therapies. Foy et al. initiated a pioneering neoTCR T cell therapy phase 1 trial.114 Neoantigen-specific TCR sequences were captured using a personalized library of soluble predicted neoantigen-HLA capture reagents. Moreover, they developed a clinical-grade approach based on CRISPR-Cas9 non-viral precision genome editing to simultaneously knock out the two endogenous TCRα and TCRβ genes from circulating T cells of patients. The resulting neoantigen-specific TCR was then inserted. This engineering approach allows T cells to effectively redirect immune responses to recognize mutations in a patient’s tumor cells. Upon infusion into patients, these genetically modified T cells circulate through the blood and traffic to tumor metastases. This study demonstrated the safety and feasibility of this approach. However, anti-tumor efficacy in 16 patients, particularly those with lung cancer, remained limited. This suggests that such complex and precise TCR-T therapies have certain limitations that require further investigation.

CAR-T cells

Unlike TIL and TCR-T cell therapies, chimeric antigen receptor T cell (CAR-T) therapy is the most successful form of cellular immunotherapy to date. CAR-T therapy has achieved significant efficacy and widespread clinical application in hematological malignancies, such as lymphoma and multiple myeloma.115 In contrast to TCR-T cells, which rely on TCR recognition of MHC-peptide complexes on tumor cells, CAR-T cells utilize chimeric antibody sequences to directly bind to tumor membrane protein antigens. This antibody-antigen-like recognition mechanism endows CAR-T cells with stronger tumor-activating effects and simplifies the preclinical development process. These advantages have established CAR-T cell therapy as the mainstream approach in cellular therapies and a promising option for future lung cancer therapy.

Currently, CAR-T cell therapies targeting solid tumors are being rapidly developed. In the domain of gastrointestinal tumors, CAR-T therapies targeting antigens such as glypican-3 (GPC3), claudin18.2, GCC19, and GUCY2C have demonstrated promising efficacy in early-phase clinical trials.116,117,118,119,120 Regarding NSCLC, the selection of tumor-associated antigens has emerged as a principal challenge in the development of CAR-T therapy, driven by antigen heterogeneity of NSCLC and on-target, off-tumor toxicity. For example, EGFR is expressed in approximately 15% of NSCLC cases.121 A phase 1 study showed acceptable tolerability of CXCR5-modified CAR-T cells against EGFR in advanced-stage NSCLC; however, acute pulmonary edema was monitored in another trial with a similar CAR-T design.122 Severe pulmonary toxicity has also been reported in several NSCLC-based CAR-T cells targeting PD-L1 or mesothelin.123,124 Additionally, numerous targets such as ROR1, MUC1, and GPC3 are currently under clinical investigation.122,125 However, few studies have provided comprehensive data regarding their efficacy and safety profiles. Strategies such as adding dominant-negative receptors (such as TGF-β receptor II), enhancing cytokine paracrine functions, and implementing combinational therapies are being employed in preclinical exploration to iterate and improve CAR-T therapies.126,127 These approaches represent the next research direction for CAR-T cells targeting solid tumors.

To date, the development of ACT therapies with clinical utility for NSCLC remains challenging. Several potentially challenging issues, including (1) insufficiently specific target antigens (creating the risk of on-target or off-tumor toxicity), (2) poor trafficking, (3) short persistence, (4) loss of effector function, and (5) tumor antigen heterogeneity and immune evasion, have been thoroughly summarized and discussed in several excellent reviews.115,128,129 Accordingly, rapid technological and design advancements, including epigenetic modifications of immune cells, enhancement of function-augmenting costimulatory or cytokine receptors, and the development of universal immune cell therapy products (universal CAR-T or natural killer cells), have provided ACT therapy with a promising outlook for overcoming the challenges of solid tumors.129,130

Neoantigen vaccines: Sustaining T cell activity

Even when T cells are present, the immunosuppressive TME in resistant disease often drives T cell dysfunction or exhaustion. Chronic antigen exposure without effective tumor clearance can push T cells into a terminally exhausted state, where they express multiple inhibitory receptors and lose effector function.131 Neoantigen vaccines, especially when combined with ICIs, can help reinvigorate and sustain T cell activity. By providing a bolus of neoantigens and adjuvant stimulation, vaccines promote the expansion of polyfunctional T cells, including memory and effector subsets.132 Consequently, neoantigen vaccines can help refresh the immune response and potentially overcome the exhaustion induced by persistent antigen exposure (Figure 3).

Recent advances in cancer immunology, neoantigen prediction, bioinformatic algorithms, and vaccine technologies have sparked renewed interest in these therapeutic vaccination strategies. The success of mRNA vaccines against infectious diseases has accelerated the development of personalized cancer vaccines, with several promising clinical trials demonstrating their potential for use in various cancer types, particularly melanoma.133,134 Currently, three categories of neoantigen vaccines are under clinical investigation for NSCLC: DC, mRNA, and peptide vaccines.135,136,137

Regarding clinical application strategies, vaccine therapy is unlikely to serve as a standalone treatment but rather needs to be optimally combined with existing therapeutic modalities. Sequential or concurrent immunotherapy may produce synergistic effects, prolong T cell persistence, and control disease progression. For example, a CAR-T cell-amplifying RNA vaccine (CARVac) targeting CLDN6 may increase the persistence of adoptively transferred CAR-T cells. The CLDN6 CAR-T + CARVac combination resulted in strong clinical activity in patients with CLDN6-positive solid tumors.138 Meanwhile, in the adjuvant therapy following surgery or maintenance therapy following chemotherapy, where the patients have less tumor burden, it is more suitable for the tumor neoantigen vaccine to play a key role in removing minimal residual disease.139 A DC vaccine targeting patient-individual neoantigens in resected NSCLC patients has successfully induced systemic T cell responses in five out of six vaccinated patients, with T cell responses remaining detectable up to 19 months post vaccination. Three patients were still free of disease recurrence with a maximal follow-up of 2 years140 Currently, the phase 3, randomized, double-blind INTerpath-002 study is exploring the recurrence-free survival of individualized mRNA neoantigen plus pembrolizumab compared with placebo plus pembrolizumab in resected NSCLC patients.141

Bispecific T cell engager: Enhancing T cell recognition

Bispecific T cell engagers (BiTEs) have emerged as rising stars in the era of antibody therapeutics. BiTEs effectively redirect CTLs to malignant cells by harnessing their ability to simultaneously bind to T and tumor cells. Reduced antigen epitope presentation is recognized as a mechanism of acquired resistance; however, BiTEs can bypass MHC-mediated recognition to directly target tumor cells and mediate crosslinking between T cells and tumor cells (Figure 3).142

To date, tarlatamab, a DLL3×CD3 BiTE, has been approved by the FDA for second- or later-line treatment of small cell lung cancer.143 However, similar to the development of CAR-T cells, antigen selection remains a critical issue in NSCLC. Current clinical exploration of BiTEs for NSCLC predominantly focuses on pan-cancer targets such as epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) and carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA). EpCAM×CD3 BiTE catumaxomab has undergone early-phase clinical exploration for NSCLC treatment. However, its development was discontinued due to infusion reactions and a high incidence of immunogenicity, likely attributed to its mouse/rat chimeric constant regions and retained FcγR binding.144 Two multi-institutional phase 1 trials reported the results of CEA×CD3 BiTE cibisatamab with or without atezolizumab in patients with CEA-positive solid tumors. Seven patients with NSCLC were enrolled in the cibisatamab and atezolizumab combination cohort. However, safety signals related to immunogenicity and on-target/off-tumor effects pose significant challenges to their clinical application.145

Future perspectives for immuno-combination strategies in NSCLC

Explosive advancements in tumor drug research and development have introduced numerous possibilities for treatment strategies for patients with NSCLC. We have summarized several representative ongoing randomized controlled trials comparing immunotherapy and immunotherapy-based combination strategies in NSCLC (Table S2). Currently, a substantial gap remains between preclinical development and early clinical research, owing to insufficient attention being given to synergistic immune mechanisms and adaptive trial designs. A deeper understanding of the multilevel mechanisms of resistance, including both tumor-intrinsic and tumor-extrinsic factors that contribute to primary and acquired resistance, is essential to inform the design of effective combination strategies (Figure 4A). Moreover, strategically timed and mechanistically informed combinatorial interventions hold promise for overcoming resistance and improving long-term efficacy (Figure 4B). Optimizing the sequencing of combinational strategies

Different therapeutic agents may exert their optimal effects when used in specific contexts or combination. Theoretically, combination strategies to overcome primary ICI resistance should be employed first. By enhancing tumor immunogenicity, promoting antigen release, and disrupting the physical barriers of the TME, a subpopulation of patients can transition from an initially immunologically unresponsive state to an immune-responsive state, thus becoming better prepared for T cell activation and cytotoxicity mediated by ICIs. Afterward, during the maintenance phase of immunotherapy, the introduction of novel strategies is essential to prevent the development of acquired resistance. Additional agents or interventions, such as ACT and vaccines, can sustain and potentiate the anti-tumor immune response, prolonging the durability of clinical outcomes. Rational combination strategies that enhance tumor immunogenicity and sustain immune activation can contribute to better long-term efficacy.146 The dynamic nature of immunotherapy efficacy reflects the evolving landscape of tumor resistance, underscoring the importance of temporal optimization in treatment administration to enhance clinical outcomes.147 Future clinical research should systematically explore diverse combination regimens to maximize patient benefit.

Biomarker identification

A multitude of combination strategies are currently available; however, the indiscriminate addition of therapies can lead to overtreatment and unnecessary toxicity. The future development of immunotherapy combinations should be based on a thorough understanding of the mechanisms underlying immune resistance, enabling the targeted design of strategies to overcome these barriers. The discovery of predictive biomarkers is crucial for patient stratification and personalization of therapy. For instance, in patients with low tumor mutational burden, immunogenic approaches such as local irradiation or ADCs can induce ICD and prime a de novo immune response. Biomarkers enable clinicians to identify patients who are most likely to benefit from certain combinations of treatments, allowing for a more tailored approach to optimize therapeutic outcomes. In addition, by dynamically monitoring biomarkers, we can identify the precursor stages of acquired immuno-resistance at an earlier time point, allowing for targeted adjustments to immunotherapeutic strategies.

Mitigation of adverse events in combination treatment

Combination therapies offer the potential to enhance anti-tumor efficacy by targeting multiple pathways; however, they inevitably increase the risk of adverse events due to additive or synergistic toxicities. Mitigating these heightened risks is imperative for ensuring patient safety and treatment tolerability. Therefore, centering on the core mechanisms by which immunotherapy exerts its effects to adaptively adjust both the duration of treatment and the dosage of medications is important. For instance, a lower dose and shorter course of radiotherapy may lessen the harm to the body and lymphocytes, thus acting as a better partner for immunotherapy than traditional radiotherapy protocols. Nevertheless, the involvement of a multidisciplinary team (MDT) is crucial. MDT, including oncologists, internists, immunologists, pharmacists, and nurses, facilitates the comprehensive monitoring and management of systemic multi-organ adverse reactions. By enabling early detection and coordinated management of toxicities, MDT plays a pivotal role in making adverse events associated with combination therapies more controllable. This collaborative approach improves patient outcomes and maximizes the therapeutic potential of combination treatments by balancing efficacy and safety.

Conclusion

As our understanding of tumor immune mechanisms and immunotherapeutic agents deepens, the landscape of lung cancer immunotherapy, or even the entire oncological treatment paradigm, has entered a new era. In the post–PD-(L)1 context, the critical challenge is to identify innovative and effective combination immunotherapy strategies with considerable attention to the dynamics of anti-tumor immunity. This pursuit holds significant practical importance and research value and urgently necessitates validation through further clinical trials. By leveraging a comprehensive understanding of immune resistance mechanisms, designing targeted treatments to overcome both primary and acquired resistances is possible. Multidisciplinary collaboration and rigorous scientific research are essential for advancing these novel therapies and ultimately improving the prognosis of patients with this challenging disease.
