Intravenous lidocaine reduces systemic inflammation but not myocardial injury following thoracic surgery for lung cancer: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Nan Zhang, Di Feng, Wei Wu, Meiyun Liu, Jingjing Wu, Ji Liu, Hong Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundElevated high-sensitivity troponin T levels shortly after noncardiac surgery are closely linked to myocardial injury, a key factor in 30-day postoperative mortality. Intravenous lidocaine, known for its potent anti-inflammatory and membrane-stabilizing properties, has shown cardioprotective potential in other surgical settings, but its efficacy in noncardiac thoracic surgery remains unclear.ObjectivesThis study was a double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized trial. Participants were randomly allocated to the lidocaine or placebo group with a 1:1 ratio.DesignSingle-centre, double-blind, randomized controlled trial.SettingAcademic tertiary care medical centre.PatientsPatients scheduled for noncardiac thoracic surgery, predominantly via video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), under general anesthesia from June 12, 2021 to June 12, 2022.InterventionsPatients received intravenous lidocaine (1.5 mg kg− 1 bolus pre-induction followed by 1.5 mg kg− 1 h− 1 infusion until surgery end) or volume-matched saline. Dosing was adjusted to ideal body weight for BMI ≥ 25 kg m−². Introduction

Noncardiac surgery poses significant cardiovascular risks, with over 200 million adults undergoing major procedures annually and more than 1 million succumbing to 30-day postoperative mortality [1, 2]. Myocardial injury after noncardiac surgery (MINS), characterized by ischemia-induced troponin elevation within 30 days post-surgery, contributes to 40–50% of related deaths and frequently manifests subclinically [3, 4]. Routine postoperative troponin screening is essential for reliably detecting MINS, as over 90% of patients with this condition are asymptomatic for myocardial ischemia [5].

Lung cancer surgery exemplifies high-risk noncardiac operations, where thoracic interventions trigger systemic inflammatory cascades that exacerbate myocardial vulnerability. Following noncardiac thoracic surgery, cardiovascular complications arise in up to 30% of patients, posing a threat to their recovery and long-term functional outcomes [6]. It has been documented that less than 5% of patients experience myocardial infarction following procedures such as lobectomy, pneumonectomy, or esophagectomy, with mortality rates potentially reaching alarming levels [7, 8]. Exploring strategies to reduce MINS incidence during thoracic surgery is clinically significant for enhancing short- and long-term outcomes in lung cancer patients.

Lidocaine, a sodium channel blocker with anti-inflammatory properties, has demonstrated dual cardioprotective potential, and preclinical models have shown that it attenuates ischemia-reperfusion injury by modulating mitochondrial permeability transition pores [9, 10], whereas clinical trials have reported reduced inflammatory markers in other non-cardiac surgeries [11, 12]. However, evidence remains conflicting regarding its myocardial protective efficacy, particularly in surgeries with pronounced inflammatory activation, such as thoracic procedures. No previous randomized trial has specifically evaluated the effect of lidocaine on MINS in thoracic surgery for lung cancer, where the interplay between surgical trauma, inflammation, and cardiomyocyte vulnerability may differ fundamentally from other contexts.

Our randomized trial was conducted to evaluate the hypothesis that perioperative lidocaine infusion decreases the incidence of MINS in patients undergoing thoracic surgery for lung cancer. Specifically, our investigation focused on a cohort of predominantly low-to-moderate risk patients (ASA physical status I-II) undergoing minimally invasive video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), a population where the baseline myocardial stress may differ from that encountered in open thoracotomy. The trial employed serial biomarker profiling to clarify its cardioprotective mechanisms.

Methods

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study (#L21-232) was provided by the Medical Ethics Committee of Shanghai Pulmonary Hospital. Participants gave written informed consent before enrolling.

Study design and participants

This study was an investigator-initiated, prospective, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial. This study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki principles and the CONSORT guidelines. Patients aged 18–80 years, classified as ASA I-II, weighing 45–100 kg, scheduled for noncardiac thoracic surgery under general anesthesia with an operation time of 6 h or less, were recruited. Patients were excluded if they had contraindications to lidocaine or opioids, second- or third-degree atrioventricular block, sinus conduction block without a pacemaker, were using class I antiarrhythmic drugs or amiodarone, had a history of epilepsy, intellectual or cognitive impairment, or had undergone craniotomy in the past five years. Exclusion criteria encompassed diagnoses of myasthenia gravis, liver disease (total bilirubin ≥ 2 mg dl− 1), renal insufficiency (GFR ≤ 60 ml min− 1 1.73 m− 2), preoperative use of potent opioids (e.g., morphine, transdermal fentanyl), recent myocardial infarction (within 14 days), unstable angina with elevated CK-MB or cTnT, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) < 50%, or requirement for emergency surgery. Participants gave written informed consent before enrolling.

Randomization and masking

Eligible patients who provided written informed consent were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either lidocaine or saline (control). A computer-generated random number table was used for randomization, stored in a sequentially numbered opaque envelope to maintain allocation concealment. Blinding was upheld for patients, healthcare providers, data collectors, data managers, and outcome adjudicators concerning the study drug allocation. Prior to the induction of anesthesia, study drugs (either 1.5% lidocaine or 0.9% saline) were prepared in identical 50 ml syringes by an independent nurse.

Procedures

In the lidocaine cohort, a bolus of lidocaine at a dosage of 1.5 mg kg− 1 was administered intravenously over a period of 10 min prior to the induction of anesthesia. This was followed by a continuous infusion at a rate of 1.5 mg kg− 1 h− 1 until the conclusion of the surgical procedure. For individuals with a BMI under 25 kg m− 2, lidocaine dosage was determined using actual body weight. For individuals with a BMI of 25 kg m− 2 or higher, the dosage was based on the ideal body weight (see Appendix S1 for calculation formula). The control group was administered a volume-equivalent saline solution, adhering to the same protocol.

A detailed, standardized protocol for general anesthesia, one-lung ventilation, and intraoperative management was strictly followed for all patients (see Appendix S2 for full details). When intraoperative arrhythmia or extremely low blood pressure occurs, lidocaine should be discontinued after excluding surgical intervention (such as pulling the tracheal carina), anesthetic drugs, or equipment signal interference.

Post-surgery, patients were extubated and transferred to the post-anesthesia care unit (PACU). After surgery, all patients received standardized patient-controlled intravenous analgesia (PCIA) (see Appendix S3 for regimen details). Post-surgery, patients were observed in the ward for at least 24 h.

Outcomes

The main outcome was the serum level of high-sensitivity troponin T (hs-TnT) measured 24 h after surgery using the Elecsys® Troponin T Gen 5 STAT assay (Roche Diagnostics). This assay has a 99th percentile upper reference limit of 14 ng l− 1, a detection limit of 5 ng l− 1, and a coefficient of variation of 3.2% at 13.5 ng l− 1 [13, 14]. Secondary outcomes encompassed the incidence of MINS (hs-TnT ≥ 14 ng l− 1), myocardial stress indicators (NT-proBNP, CK-MB, and myoglobin), and inflammatory biomarkers within 48 h. Blood samples for biomarker analysis were collected at three distinct timepoints: immediately before the induction of anesthesia (baseline), at 24 h postoperatively, and at 48 h postoperatively.

Safety evaluation was mainly performed to closely monitor hemodynamic changes during the operation. In addition, if the patient experienced dizziness, metallic taste, tinnitus, and perioral numbness, lidocaine was immediately discontinued. A 20% fat emulsion is available in the operating room to manage severe local anesthetic toxicity. Simultaneously, the occurrence of postoperative adverse reactions such as nausea, vomiting, chills, and the recovery time of gastrointestinal function were observed. Serious adverse events will be reported to the principal investigator overseeing the clinical trial, who will decide on notifying the ethics or clinical trial monitoring committees.

Statistical analysis

The primary efficacy analysis was performed on the per-protocol (PP) population, which comprised all randomized patients who received the complete allocated intervention and had comprehensive data available for the primary outcome assessment at 24 h post-surgery. This methodological approach was pre-specified due to the inherent challenges in reliably imputing the primary outcome, a continuous biomarker measurement, for patients who either discontinued the intervention or were lost to follow-up. For consistency, all baseline characteristics, intraoperative data, and postoperative outcomes presented in Tables 1, 2 and 3 are derived from this PP population. To evaluate the robustness of our findings and maintain adherence to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, a sensitivity analysis was conducted for the principal binary secondary outcome, specifically the incidence of MINS, using the full ITT cohort (n = 138). In this analysis, a worst-case scenario was assumed, whereby all patients with missing outcome data were considered to have experienced the event (MINS). The safety analysis was conducted on the ITT population, which included all patients who received at least one dose of the study drug.

The sample size was calculated using the online software ‘Power and Sample Size.com.’ According to the data provided by previous studies [15], the concentration of troponin subtypes in the lidocaine group vs control group on the second day after surgery: 0.74 (0.39 to 1.37) ng ml− 1 vs. 1.07 (0.66 to 2.48) ng ml− 1, setting α to 0.05 and 1-β to 0.9, the minimum number of each group was 60 cases. Accounting for a potential dropout rate of 10%, we planned to recruit a total of 132 patients, with 66 participants in each group.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 23, IBM) and R Studio 3.5.2. The Shapiro-Wilk test assessed data distribution normality, while Levene’s test evaluated variance homogeneity. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and analyzed with Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous data were presented as medians with IQR and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U-test. Furthermore, the Hodges-Lehmann estimator was used to calculate the median difference and its corresponding 95% confidence interval for the primary outcome. To account for baseline differences in troponin levels, the absolute change from baseline to 24 h post-surgery for hs-TnT (Δhs-TnT at 24 h) was also calculated and compared between groups. Categorical data were expressed as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the chi-square test. A multivariate logistic regression model was employed to examine the variables associated with myocardial injury. A two-sided P-value below 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Between June 12, 2021, and June 12, 2022, a total of 260 patients were assessed for eligibility. A total of 122 patients (46.9%) were excluded due to not meeting the inclusion criteria. A total of 138 patients consented and were randomized to receive either lidocaine (n = 71) or placebo (n = 67). This full cohort constituted the ITT and safety analysis population. Four patients discontinued the intervention post-randomization. Subsequent loss to follow-up occurred in seven patients from the lidocaine group and eight from the control group. Ultimately, PP population for the primary efficacy analysis included 60 patients in the lidocaine group and 59 in the control group (Fig. 1).

CONSORT flow diagram

The four discontinuations all occurred in the lidocaine group and were due to: one case of extreme intraoperative bradycardia (heart rate < 50 bpm unresponsive to atropine), one of refractory hypotension (mean arterial pressure < 50 mmHg for > 10 min), one for refusal to use an analgesic pump, and one for missing postoperative biomarker data. The two clinical events, bradycardia and hypotension, were considered serious adverse events. Consequently, the safety analysis population included all 71 patients allocated to the lidocaine group and all 67 patients in the control group. No other serious adverse events were reported in either group.

The study cohort’s mean age was 59.4 years (SD = 11.1), consisting of 58 males (48.7%) and 61 females (51.3%). Baseline characteristics, including age, sex, BMI, ASA classification, cardiac risk index, smoking and surgical history, aCCI, comorbidities, preoperative medications, and pulmonary function (FEV1 and FEV1/FVC ratio) were similarly distributed between the lidocaine and placebo groups, showing no significant differences (all P > 0.05; see Table 1). The primary indication for surgery for all enrolled patients was the presence of a focal pulmonary lesion (nodule or mass) requiring resection. The distribution of surgical procedures, including wedge resection, segmentectomy, and lobectomy, was also comparable between the groups. Additionally, preoperative laboratory parameters, including hematologic, hepatic, and renal function markers, as well as inflammatory factors, showed no significant intergroup differences.

Regarding intraoperative management, most parameters, including medication dosages (notably opioids), showed no significant differences between the lidocaine and control groups (refer to Table 2). However, intraoperative urine output was significantly lower in the control group (median 100.00 ml vs. 150.00 ml, P = 0.035).

HR and MAP were analyzed at five time points: T1 (baseline), T2 (intubation), T3 (skin incision), T4 (skin closure), and T5 (post-anesthesia care unit [PACU] discharge). Both HR and MAP remained comparable between the groups at all time points (Fig. 2A and B).

The primary outcome, serum hs-TnT concentration at 24 h postoperatively, was 8.00 (6.00–15.00) ng l− 1 in the lidocaine group and 8.00 (5.00–10.00) ng l− 1 in the control group (P = 0.34; Table 3), with the difference not being statistically significant. The estimated median difference between the groups was − 1.00 ng l⁻¹ (95% CI, -3.00 to 1.00), confirming the absence of a statistically significant effect and indicating substantial variability. Furthermore, the absolute change in hs-TnT from baseline to 24 h (Δhs-TnT at 24 h) was also not significantly different between the lidocaine (3.21 ± 5.93 ng l⁻¹) and control (3.00 ± 8.56 ng l⁻¹) groups (P = 0.88). Similarly, the incidence of MINS within 48 h was comparable between the two groups (23.3% [14/60] vs. 23.7% [14/59], P = 0.96). To confirm this finding, a worst-case sensitivity analysis based on the full ITT population (assuming all patients with missing data experienced MINS) also showed no significant difference in the incidence of MINS between the lidocaine and control groups (35.2% [25/71] vs. 32.8% [22/67], P = 0.78).

Secondary cardiac biomarker analyses revealed no significant differences in NT-proBNP (24 h: median 231.10 ng l− 1 vs. 217.30 ng l− 1, P = 0.91; 48 h: median 93.40 ng l− 1 vs. 175.20 ng l− 1, P = 0.16), CK-MB (24 h: 3.63 ± 1.44 ng ml− 1 vs. 3.33 ± 1.68 ng ml− 1, P = 0.33; 48 h: 1.73 ± 0.50 ng ml− 1 vs. 3.15 ± 2.24 ng ml− 1, P = 0.20), or myoglobin levels (24 h: 126.31 ± 69.62 ng ml− 1 vs. 114.36 ± 52.09 ng ml− 1, P = 0.32; 48 h: 47.27 ± 19.7 ng ml− 1 vs. 45.9 ± 15.34 ng ml− 1, P = 0.89) at 24–48 h postoperatively.

Notably, lidocaine demonstrated potent anti-inflammatory effects, with interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels at 48 h postoperatively being 84.8% lower than controls (46.51 ± 33.60 pg ml− 1 vs. 306.96 ± 312.23 pg ml− 1, P = 0.04; Table 3). No significant differences were found between groups in inflammatory markers like high sensitivity C-Reactive Protein (hs-CRP) and interleukin-10 (IL-10), or in renal function parameters such as creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), as all P-values were greater than 0.05. Three-month follow-up outcomes, including Clavien-Dindo complication scores (0.32 ± 0.65 vs. 0.23 ± 0.47, P = 0.43) and patient satisfaction (4.66 ± 0.59 vs. 4.68 ± 0.55, P = 0.86), were comparable between groups.

Univariate logistic regression analysis identified age as the sole significant predictor of postoperative hs-TnT level in this cohort (Table 4). Patients under 65 years had a 74% reduced likelihood of myocardial injury compared to those 65 years and older (OR 0.259, 95% CI, 0.107 to 0.627; P = 0.003). No significant associations were found between myocardial injury risk and variables such as sex (male OR 2.295, 95% CI 0.96 to 5.52; P = 0.063), comorbidities (diabetes OR 2.40, P = 0.08; hypertension OR 1.38, P = 0.46), surgical approach (open vs. VATS OR 1.09, P = 0.94), or extent of resection (lobectomy OR 0.97, P = 0.98).

In terms of drug-specific safety, patients were meticulously monitored for indications of local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST). None of the patients in either cohort exhibited symptoms such as metallic taste, tinnitus, or perioral numbness during or immediately after the infusion. Other common postoperative adverse events, including nausea, vomiting, and chills, were documented as part of standard clinical care. No clinically significant differences or safety concerns related to the study drug were identified between the groups.

Discussion

This randomized trial showed that while perioperative intravenous lidocaine significantly reduced systemic inflammation, as indicated by an 84.8% decrease in IL-6 levels 48 h after surgery, it did not decrease the incidence of myocardial injury or early hs-TnT release in patients undergoing thoracic surgery for lung cancer, a finding that should be interpreted within the context of our predominantly low-to-moderate risk, ASA I-II study population undergoing VATS. Notably, the observed 23.7% incidence of MINS in our control cohort is consistent with previously established reports, which range from 14% to 28% [16, 17], validating our diagnostic rigor using the Fourth Universal Definition and Roche’s high-sensitivity hs-TnT assay (99th percentile URL 14 ng l− 1) [18].

Despite this methodological strength, lidocaine failed to reduce hs-TnT release, with the median 24-h hs-TnT levels being identical between the groups (8.00 ng l− 1 vs. 8.00 ng l− 1). Furthermore, an analysis of the absolute change from baseline (Δhs-TnT) also confirmed no significant difference between the groups. This contrasts with cardiac surgery trials where lidocaine reduced troponin release by 27% to 42% [19], likely because coronary artery bypass inherently induces greater ischemia-reperfusion injury and inflammatory cascades than thoracic surgery for lung cancer. The minimal hemodynamic fluctuations (comparable HR/MAP between the groups) and reduced surgical trauma in VATS may have diminished the cardioprotective potential of lidocaine.

Lidocaine exhibits notable anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties. In our study, a significant reduction in IL-6 levels was noted in the lidocaine group on the second postoperative day, whereas levels of hs-CRP and IL-10 remained unchanged. These findings align with a placebo-controlled trial on lumbar spinal stenosis surgery patients, which showed significantly elevated plasma levels of IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) in the placebo group compared to the lidocaine group 48 h after surgery, while IL-10 expression remained similar between the groups [20]. Contradictory evidence is presented in a study of 60 patients undergoing laparoscopic Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, which found that lidocaine infusion effectively reduced early postoperative pain but did not decrease IL-6 levels [11]. Notably, our null findings regarding opioid-sparing effects align with recent VATS trials [21, 22] and contrasting abdominal surgery reports [23, 24]. This procedure-specific variation underscores the need for contextualized analgesic protocols. While preclinical studies have extensively documented lidocaine’s anti-inflammatory mechanisms, its clinical immunomodulatory efficacy remains inconsistent and seems to depend on the context. This heterogeneity suggests that the immunomodulatory actions of lidocaine are context-dependent and are influenced by the magnitude of surgical trauma and patient comorbidities.

The dissociation between IL-6 suppression and myocardial protection challenges the prevailing hypothesis that systemic inflammation is the primary driver of postoperative cardiac injury in noncardiac surgery. While preclinical evidence suggests lidocaine offers cardioprotection through multiple pathways, including direct ion channel modulation and anti-inflammatory actions [9, 10, 25], our results indicate that these effects, at least as reflected by IL-6 suppression, are insufficient to prevent MINS in this surgical context. Three key factors may explain this finding. First, previous investigations in cardiac surgery demonstrating the cardioprotective properties [19] typically involved procedures with substantially greater ischemic challenges (e.g., direct coronary manipulation), in contrast to the minimally invasive VATS approach in our study cohort. The maintained hemodynamic stability (with no intergroup differences in heart rate or mean arterial pressure) and attenuated surgical trauma inherent in VATS likely resulted in diminished baseline myocardial stress, potentially reducing the therapeutic window for additional cardioprotective effects. Second, our observation that older age was strongly associated with an increased risk of MINS (OR 3.86 for ≥ 65 years) suggests that age-related physiological vulnerabilities may play a more dominant role in MINS pathogenesis than the inflammatory pathways modulated by lidocaine. However, it is crucial to emphasize that this interpretation is exploratory. Our study was not designed or powered to test for a formal interaction between treatment and age, and no such analysis was performed. Therefore, the concept that age might modulate the host response to this anti-inflammatory therapy should be considered strictly hypothesis-generating and requires validation in future trials. While our multivariate model identified no other predictors, this likely reflects the limited power given the modest sample size, which is a common challenge in surgical RCTs [26]. Third, our perioperative protocol terminated lidocaine administration immediately after the procedure, possibly overlooking the critical therapeutic window for mitigating delayed inflammatory cascades through sustained pharmacological modulation.

This dissociation also highlights a crucial limitation inherent in many clinical studies: the reliance on systemic biomarkers, which may not accurately reflect the complex pathophysiological processes within a specific organ’s microenvironment. The challenge of distinguishing systemic background signals from organ-specific biological activity is a frontier in biomedical research. A recent, elegant study by Zhang et al. using near-infrared fluorescence/Cerenkov luminescence dual-modality imaging exemplified this principle. They successfully differentiated non-specific antibody accumulation in tumors from the specific binding to the PD-L1 target, demonstrating that non-specific signals can sometimes dominate the overall measurement [27]. This concept is highly relevant to our findings, suggesting that while lidocaine effectively dampens the systemic inflammatory cascade (the “background signal”), it may not be sufficient to alter the specific pathways leading to cardiomyocyte injury (the “organ-specific signal”). Future research incorporating organ-specific methodologies, such as molecular imaging with cardiac-specific inflammatory tracers, could provide deeper insights into the local mechanisms of cardioprotection.

As the first RCT to evaluate the cardioprotective effect of lidocaine in noncardiac thoracic surgery, our study has inherent constraints. First, a significant limitation is the potential for a Type II error, meaning we may have failed to detect a true, albeit modest, cardioprotective effect. Our a priori power calculation was based on data from cardiac surgery trials, which typically report a larger magnitude of troponin release and greater effect sizes. In contrast, the lower baseline myocardial stress in noncardiac thoracic surgery likely results in a more subtle treatment effect that would require a larger sample size to detect. Consequently, our study was likely underpowered. Second, the study’s single-center design and focus on ASA I-II classifications limit its applicability to high-risk groups. Consequently, the findings of this study should be interpreted with caution and may not be generalizable to high-risk patients (e.g., ASA III-IV) or those undergoing open thoracotomy, where the baseline inflammatory and myocardial stress are substantially higher. Additionally, the absence of intraoperative and postoperative lidocaine concentration monitoring prevented a dose-response analysis due to unmeasured levels. Third, the fixed lidocaine regimen (1.5 mg kg− 1 bolus + 1.5 mg kg− 1 h− 1) is not optimized for pharmacokinetics. Future trials should incorporate therapeutic drug monitoring and extended postoperative infusions. Our study was based on the intravenous dose of lidocaine used in previously published protocols. The optimal dose of lidocaine for a single administration and subsequent continuous infusion is unknown, which may be the main reason for the absence of cardioprotective effects of lidocaine in the present study.

Conclusions

While lidocaine fails as a standalone cardioprotective agent in our specific study cohort of low-to-moderate risk, predominantly VATS, thoracic surgery patients, its potent IL-6 suppression, combined with the exploratory observation of age as a significant risk factor for MINS, suggests a potential direction for future research. The idea of targeting anti-inflammatory therapies toward specific patient subgroups remains a compelling but unproven hypothesis that requires further investigation. Despite the negative primary outcomes, this first systematic evaluation of lidocaine in noncardiac thoracic surgery provides critical insights: anti-inflammatory efficacy does not universally translate to organ protection, underscoring the potential importance of developing precision approaches tailored to the surgical context and patient phenotypes. Therefore, our conclusions should be confined to this specific population, and future research is warranted to determine the efficacy of lidocaine in higher-risk surgical populations, such as those undergoing open thoracotomy.
