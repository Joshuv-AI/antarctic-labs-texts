Commentary on “Does light-intensity physical activity moderate the relationship between sitting time and adiposity markers in adolescents?”
Authors: Barbara E Ainsworth, Amanda M Ferland
Published: 2023

In the September 2022 issue of the Journal of Sport and Health Science, Ayala et al.1 published results from a cross-sectional study where they tested the hypothesis that light intensity physical activity (LIPA, 1.6–2.9 metabolic equivalents (METs)) moderates the relationship between sitting time and adiposity in 219 Australian adolescents aged 14 ± 1.6 years (mean ± SD). The researchers used an accelerometer and an inclinometer to measure time spent sitting, total LIPA, and moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity (MVPA, ≤3.0 METs). Adiposity was calculated as the waist circumference z scores (zWC) and body mass index z-scores (zBMI). Total LIPA MET values were divided into low-LIPA (∼1.6–2.2 METs) and high-LIPA (∼2.3–2.9 METs). Results showed no associations between sitting time (10.8 h/day) and zBMI and zWC. However, time spent in total LIPA (259.9 ± 55.2 min/day, ∼4.33 h/day) moderated the relationship between sitting and zBMI, and time spent in low-LIPA (165.9 ± 36.8 min/day, ∼2.77 h/day) moderated the relationship between sitting and zWC and zBMI. No associations were observed for high-LIPA (93.9 ± 24.6 min/day, ∼1.57 h/day) or MVPA (50.9 min/day, ∼0.85 h/day). Moderator analysis showed that sitting time was inversely related to zBMI at higher levels of total LIPA (259.9 min/day + 1 SD, or an additional 55 min/day). Also, sitting time was inversely related to zBMI and zWC at higher levels of low-LIPA (165.9 min/day +1SD, or an additional 36.8 min/day). These findings show the importance of engaging in LIPA to minimize the impact of extended sitting time on adiposity.

Other studies have investigated the effects of LIPA on cardiometabolic health in children and adolescents. Carson et al.2 analyzed data from 1731 U.S. adolescents, aged 12–19 years, from the 2003/04 and 2005/06 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, showing small but favorable effects of low- and high-LIPA on diastolic blood pressure and high-LIPA on high-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels. Most of the favorable changes in cardiometabolic biomarkers were associated with MVPA. In an isotemporal study that theoretically replaced sedentary time with LIPA and MVPA, Hansen et al.3 analyzed data from the International Children's Accelerometry Database in children and adolescents aged 4–18 years, representing 21 studies from 10 countries. Children and adolescents were stratified into 3 age groups (<10 years, ≥10.0–14.9 years, and >14.9–18.4 years). Most theoretically favorable changes in cardiometabolic risk factors were observed when replacing 10 min of sedentary time with 10 min of MVPA. Replacing sedentary time with LIPA showed only a small but favorable theoretical change in glucose in older adolescents (>14.9–18.4 years). Alternatively, unfavorable changes were observed in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol when replacing sedentary time with LIPA in younger children (<10 years) and adolescents (≥10.0–14.9 years). The theoretical changes for LIPA were small (<0.1%) and not clinically meaningful. Moura et al.4 studied 84 male Brazilian adolescents, aged 14–18 years, in a cross-sectional study using isotemporal substitution to replace 5–60 min of sedentary time with equal amounts of LIPA and MVPA to observe the theoretical effects for cardiometabolic markers. Substituting sedentary behaviors with LIPA showed theoretically favorable changes in high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, triglycerides, the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance and insulin sensitivity, and systolic blood pressure, whereas substitution with MVPA showed a lower percent body fat. Additional studies are needed to identify other potential benefits of LIPA on children's and adolescent's health and to untangle how total-, low-, and high-LIPA intensities may influence time spent in sedentary behaviors and health outcomes.

The Youth Compendium of Physical Activities (Youth Compendium)5,6 is a comprehensive list of 196 physical activities and their associated metabolic costs in children and adolescents aged 6–18 years. Low-LIPA includes quiet play, schoolwork, television watching, standing arcade and board games, and washing dishes. High-LIPA includes weightlifting, curl-ups, gymnastics, and slow walking. Classification of active video games as low- or high-LIPA differs by age. In children aged 6–12 years, active video games (e.g., Wii balance, golf, basketball, and driving simulation, among others) are classified as low-LIPA, and in teenagers aged 13–18 years, active video games are classified as high-LIPA.7 In 2008, the Pew Research Center (herein: Center) reported that 97% of teenagers aged 12–17 years played video games on a computer, web, portable device, or console.8 In 2022, the Center reported that over 90% of adolescents aged 13–17 years used social media, and 97% reported using the internet daily.9 The 2022 American Time Use Survey reported that teenagers aged 15–19 years spent ∼1 h and 38 min playing video games or using a computer for other activities and ∼1 h and 58 min per day watching television.10 Time spent using electronic media and watching television represents nearly 3.5 h of sitting. This sedentary time is in addition to time spent sitting per day in other settings, such as in school classes, doing homework, and riding in a car.

Studies in adults show that interrupting prolonged sitting with short, frequent standing bouts was more beneficial in reducing circulating glucose than taking less frequent, longer standing breaks.11 Other studies show that interrupting prolonged sitting with 1–2 min of standing11 or intermittent LIPA12,13 reduces circulating post-prandial blood glucose and insulin levels. Chastin et al.12 described mechanistic and laboratory studies reporting that short bouts of LIPA reduced post-prandial glucose by 20% (95% confidence interval (95%CI): –37.18 to –2.96) in metabolically healthy persons and by 29% (95%CI: −33.92 to –23.52) in metabolically impaired persons. Similar results were observed for laboratory studies, showing the benefits of LIPA on post-prandial insulin levels being reduced by 25% (95%CI: –31.76 to –18.33). Whereas these studies are reported in adults, it is plausible that engaging in LIPA, especially following meals, has similar benefits in sedentary children and adolescents.

Public health guidelines are based on substantial research showing statistically significant and consistent dose-response associations between lifestyle behaviors and health outcomes.14 There is substantial research showing that MVPA reduces the risks for health outcomes and that sedentary behaviors are detrimental to health outcomes.15 In 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) published its updated Guidelines on Physical Activity and Sedentary Behavior (herein: WHO guidelines), recommending that children and adolescents aged 5–17 years should do at least an average of 60 min/day of MVPA, primarily aerobic physical activity, across the week. They also should engage in physical activities that strengthen bones and muscles and limit sedentary time, especially recreational screen time.15 The WHO has yet to issue public health guidelines for LIPA, as additional research is needed to establish causality between LIPA and morbidity and mortality health outcomes. Following the release of the updated WHO guidelines, the 2020 WHO physical activity and sedentary behavior guidelines development group issued a call for more studies on the health benefits of LIPA and of breaking up sedentary time with LIPA.16

Children and adolescents can benefit from replacing sedentary time with LIPA at home and school and during other daily physical activities. As concluded above, the study by Ayala et al.1 from the September 2022 issue of the Journal of Sport and Health Science shows the importance of engaging in LIPA to minimize the impact of prolonged sitting time on adiposity in adolescents. Accordingly, parents should encourage their children to break up sitting time with LIPA and obtain MVPA and strengthening exercises sufficient to meet the WHO Physical Activity and Sedentary Behavior guidelines.15 Further research should include mechanistic, clinical, and longitudinal studies to understand the relationships between LIPA and health outcomes in children, including LIPA's role as a moderating variable in studies of sedentary behaviors and obesity in children and adolescents.
