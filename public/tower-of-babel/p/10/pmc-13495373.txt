Clinical outcomes following surgical fixation of proximal humerus fractures in skiers and snowboarders
Authors: Christopher J. Hawryluk, Benjamin B. Rothrauff, Emily A. Whicker, Caleb S. Davis, Marilee P. Horan, Michael Nocek, Lorenz Fritsch, Peter J. Millett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDownhill skiing and snowboarding are popular winter sports, yet limited data describe outcomes after surgical fixation of proximal humerus fractures in these patients. The purpose of this study was to evaluate clinical outcomes and return to sport (RTS) after surgical fixation of proximal humerus fractures sustained while skiing or snowboarding, and to determine the influence of age, sex, sport, injury severity (Neer classification), and fixation type (open reduction internal fixation vs. closed reduction percutaneous pinning) on these outcomes.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of patients who underwent surgical fixation of proximal humerus fractures sustained while skiing or snowboarding between 2005 and 2022 by a fellowship-trained surgeon (P.J.M) was performed. Inclusion criteria were age ≥18 years, acute fracture sustained during downhill snow sports, and ≥12 months of follow-up. Exclusion criteria included prior fixation, pathologic fracture, polytrauma, arthroplasty, or incomplete records. Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) included 12-Item Short-Form Physical Component Summary (SF-12 PCS)/12-Item Short-Form Mental Component Summary, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons, Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation, visual analog scale pain, satisfaction, and RTS. Sub-group analyses compared outcomes by age (<65 vs. ≥ 65 years), sex, sport (skiing vs. snowboarding), Neer classification (2- vs. 3–4-part), and fixation type (open reduction and internal fixation vs. closed reduction and percutaneous pinning).ResultsForty-eight patients (26 females, 22 males; median age 55.8 years; 38 skiing, 10 snowboarding) were analyzed at a mean 83.9-month follow-up (minimum 12 months). Neer classification included 17 (35%) 2-part and 31 (65%) 3–4-part fractures. Median PROMs were SF-12 PCS (55.5), 12-Item Short-Form Mental Component Summary (56.0), American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (94.9), Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (4.5), Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (95.0), and visual analog scale pain (0.0). Overall, 77% (37/48) returned to sport, with 55% returning at or near preinjury level; median satisfaction was 10/10, and the revision rate was 4%. Younger patients (<65 years) had higher SF-12 PCS scores (P = .004), and snowboarders were younger (P = .003) and reported higher satisfaction (P = .048) than skiers; no other significant differences in PROMs or RTS were observed by age, sex, sport, fracture severity, or fixation type.ConclusionOperative fixation of proximal humerus fractures sustained during skiing or snowboarding yields excellent long-term clinical outcomes, with 77% (37/48) of patients returning to sport at a mean follow-up of 83.9 months, high patient satisfaction (median 10/10), and a low revision rate (4%, 2/50 operative cases). Clinical outcomes were largely comparable across age, sex, sport, fracture severity, and fixation type, underscoring the reliability of surgical management for proximal humerus fractures in active snow sport populations.

Downhill skiing and snowboarding are among the most popular winter sports with approximately 10.6 million participants in the 2023/2024 season.2 Skiing accounts for 73% of resort visits nationally, and males represent 62% of the snow sports population.3 Given the popularity of downhill snow sports, it is important to understand injury patterns and characteristics in this cohort. A query of the National Electronic Injury Surveillance System database identified 74,427 skiing and 69,732 snowboarding injuries from 2009 to 2018.11 The shoulder is the most common injury site for skiers, while snowboarders most commonly injured the wrist. Studies also show that snowboarders injured their upper extremity at twice the rate of skiers.9,11,12,23

Despite the frequency of upper extremity fractures, most research on return to sport (RTS) following surgical fixation has focused on lower extremity injuries, such as proximal third tibia and tibial plateau fractures where RTS rates range from 32% to 45.6%.15,19,22

Studies on clinical outcomes following upper extremity fracture fixation in downhill snow sport athletes are scarce. One case series reported that 19 of 29 patients returned to skiing after open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) for humeral head fractures.1 Clinical outcomes following proximal humerus fractures in general demonstrate mixed results based on age and fracture severity.1,10,20

Although proximal humerus fracture outcomes have been examined in various populations, they have not specifically been studied in downhill snow sport athletes.1,6,10,13,20,21 How injury severity, as defined by the Neer classification, affects treatment strategy for proximal humerus fractures in this cohort remains unknown along with limited RTS data. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine clinical outcomes following surgical fixation of proximal humerus fractures sustained while skiing or snowboarding, with subsequent analysis of the effect of age, sex, sport (skiing vs. snowboarding), injury severity (Neer classification), and fixation type on clinical outcomes and RTS.

Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study investigated the clinical outcomes in patients who underwent surgical fixation of proximal humerus fractures sustained while skiing or snowboarding. All surgeries were performed by a single fellowship-trained orthopedic surgeon (P.J.M.) between November 2005 and January 2022. Institutional review board approval (WCG Institutional Review Board protocol number - 20244723) was obtained prior to data collection. Eligible patients were identified through review of operative case logs and the institutional electronic medical record. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥18 years, (2) diagnosis of acute proximal humerus fracture sustained during skiing or snowboarding, (3) surgical fixation performed by the senior author, and (4) minimum clinical follow-up of 12 months. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) fractures in the setting of prior fixation or arthroplasty, (2) pathologic fractures, (3) polytrauma requiring alternative prioritization of care, (4) fracture treatment with arthroplasty, or (5) incomplete medical records or outcome data.

Patient characteristics and clinical outcomes

Demographic variables (age at surgery, sex, comorbidities) and injury-related characteristics (laterality of injury, skiing vs. snowboarding mechanism, Neer fracture classification) were extracted from the medical record and imaging studies. Operative details, including time from injury to surgery, surgical approach, fixation construct, implant type, and time from surgery to final follow-up were determined. Patients were routinely evaluated according to a standard post-operative protocol either in person or virtually until radiographic evidence of complete healing was achieved. Study subjects meeting the above inclusion criteria were contacted by email and/or phone to solicit study participation. For patients that agreed to participate in the study, informed consent was obtained prior to sending an electronic survey comprised of the following patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs): 12-Item Short-Form 12 (SF-12) physical component summary (PCS) and mental component summary, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (scale 0-100 best) survey, Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (scale 100-0 best) survey, Single Assessment Numeric Evaluation (scale 0-100 best) score, visual analog scale pain (scale 0-10 worst) pain score, satisfaction with outcomes (scale 0-10 best), and RTS (yes/no) questionnaire. For patients that underwent revision surgery, the type of revision procedure was recorded but outcome scores were excluded from further analysis.

Surgical technique

All surgeries were performed within 7 days of the injury under general anesthesia and with the patient in the beach chair position. Where appropriate, percutaneous reduction and fixation was performed. Alternatively, ORIF was performed through a deltopectoral approach. Fracture reduction was achieved using a combination of direct visualization, manual manipulation, and fluoroscopic guidance. Definitive fixation was performed with a combination of percutaneous screws and a locking plate system supplemented by sutures, cerclage, or bone graft as indicated. Following fixation, stability and alignment were confirmed fluoroscopically. Post-operatively, all patients were immobilized in a shoulder sling and participated in a standardized rehabilitation protocol or as directed by their follow-up physician upon returning to their primary place of residence.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using median and interquartile range unless normally distributed, while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Description statistics for the total cohort are presented independently. Sub-group analyses based upon age (<65 years vs. ≥ 65 years), sex (male vs. female), sport (skiing vs. snowboarding), Neer fracture classification (2-part vs. 3–4-part), and fixation type (ORIF vs. closed reduction and percutaneous pinning [CRPP]) were performed and presented independently. For continuous variables, normality was assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Groups with normally distributed data were compared using a Student t-test while non-parametric data were compared using a Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using a Fisher exact test. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. All analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS (version 31, IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 85 patients met inclusion criteria, undergoing operative fixation of a proximal humerus fracture sustained while skiing or snowboarding. Eight patients underwent arthroplasty for fracture fixation and were excluded. Two patients had prior surgery, and 1 patient had incomplete records. The remaining 74 patients were eligible with 2-year follow-up and 50 patients agreed to study participation, of which 2 patients had undergone subsequent surgery: 1 patient underwent non-specific revision surgery at another institution and the other patient underwent conversion to shoulder arthroplasty at another institution 30 months post-operatively. This lead to a 4% revision rate based on 2 out of 50 study participants undergoing subsequent surgery. Outcomes of the remaining 48 patients were analyzed in this study. The median age was 55.8 years with 26 female and 22 male patients. The right shoulder was injured in 23 cases and the left shoulder was injured in 25 cases. Skiing was the cause of injury in 38 cases, while snowboarding was the cause in 10 cases. According to the Neer classification, there were 17 2-part, 21 3-part, and 10 4-part fractures. Open reduction with internal fixation was performed in 27 cases compared to 21 cases in which percutaneous reduction and internal fixation was utilized. The average time from surgery to follow-up was 83.9 months. Overall, 77% (37/48) of patients returned to skiing or snowboarding. Of those who returned, 54% (26/48) reported returning at or near their preinjury level of participation. Sub-group analyses were performed to stratify outcomes by sex, age, sport, Neer classification, and fixation type. In comparing the 26 females against the 22 males comprising the study population, there were no statistically significant differences in demographic variables, injury characteristics, or PROMs (Table II). The median age across the entire population was 55.8 years. The younger cohort also demonstrated a statistically significant higher SF-12 PCS score (55.9) than the older cohort (46.3; P = .004). However, young and old cohorts did not differ in any other characteristic or outcome (Table III). When outcomes were analyzed by sport, the snowboarding cohort again demonstrated a younger median age than skiers (29.0 vs. 59.1; P = .003). Snowboarders also reported a higher median satisfaction following surgery (10/10) than skiers (9/10; P = .048), with all snowboarding patients reporting a satisfaction of 9 or 10 on a 10-point scale. No other differences in patient-reported outcomes (PROs) were found between skiers and snowboarders (Table IV). Fracture patterns were dichotomized according to the Neer classification into simple (2-part) and complex (3- and 4-part) fracture patterns. The simple group had a median younger age (47.0 years) than the complex group (59.4 years; P = .040) and accordingly also demonstrated a higher SF-12 PCS score (56.5) than the complex group (54.2; P = .023). There were no other differences between the simple and complex groups, including the distribution of fractures treated by percutaneous reduction and fixation vs. open reduction internal fixation (Table V). Discussion

Our study demonstrates the majority of skiers and snowboarders experience successful clinical outcomes following operative fixation of proximal humerus fractures, with an RTS rate of 77% (37/48) at an average 83.9-month follow-up and a low revision rate of 4% (2/50). Patient-reported satisfaction was high, with a median of 10.0, and minimal need for revision surgery at long-term follow-up. Overall pain scores are low at final follow-up, highlighting the durability of operative fixation in this population. Further sub-group analyses by sex, age, sport, and Neer classification revealed comparable findings across groups with few key differences.

Age

When stratified by age, the cohort consisted of younger (<65 years, n = 40) and older (≥65 years, n = 8) patients. Multiple studies have examined age differences and injuries between skiers and snowboarders.8,14,26 Kelley et al14 reviewed emergency room visits between 2000-2019 finding that injured snowboarders were younger on average, with a mean age of 20 compared to 30.1 years for skiers, with patients under 18 years representing a high proportion of snowboarding injuries compared to skiing injuries, 57% compared to 43%, respectively. Bissell et al4 investigated the incidence and risk factors of humerus fractures in skiers and snowboarders finding that humerus fractures occurred at a significantly higher rate in snowboarders compared to skiers (0.062 per 1000 snowboarder days vs. 0.041 per 1000, P < .05). For proximal humerus fractures specifically, older patients also reported a lower SF-12 PCS score (55.9) compared to younger patients (46.3), a difference that was statistically significant (P = .004), while there was no significant difference detected between ages in the other outcome measures (Table III). The SF-12 PCS score represents overall physical health rather than shoulder-specific function. The observed difference may reflect expected age-related variances in general physical health and activity level rather than a difference in outcomes attributed to a proximal humerus fracture.

Sex

There were no statistically significant differences observed in our study when comparing characteristics and outcomes by sex (26 females vs. 22 males). While some epidemiologic studies have shown higher rates of upper extremity injuries in male skiers and snowboarders,17,23 Carr et al5 found in their case control study that men and women are equally likely to injure their upper extremity. More specifically, Bissell et al4 found that sex was not a significant risk factor for humerus fractures among either skiers or snowboarders. When evaluating clinical outcomes of proximal humerus fractures overall, it is important to note that the majority of patients with complex fractures are older females (age >60 years), as injury severity and age can be confounding variables.10 Female sex has been associated with an increased risk of conversion to arthroplasty following ORIF25; however, there are limited data reporting sex differences in PROs. Our study is among the few with a balanced male-to-female cohort, and no significant differences were detected in functional outcomes, pain scores, or patient satisfaction between sexes following operative fixation for proximal humerus fractures.

Sport

Comparing skiers to snowboarders, the median age at surgery of skiers (59.1 years) was significantly higher than the median age of snowboarders (29.0 years) (P = .003) (Table IV). This is consistent with the prior study by Bissell et al4 that reported skiers sustaining humerus fractures were older than controls by an average of 7.3 years, while snowboarders with humerus fractures were younger than controls by an average of 4.1 years. Skiers also were more likely to sustain a proximal humerus fracture, while snowboarders more commonly fractured the diaphyseal and distal humerus. A potential confounding factor is skill level, where studies show snowboarders that self-report as beginner/novice have a significantly increased risk of a humerus fracture overall, while this is not observed in skiers. With snowboarders being younger on average, proximal humerus fractures in this group may be related to skill level and risk taking, while older skiers may sustain proximal humerus fractures due to age-related factors such as decreased reaction time, strength, conditioning, and osteoporosis.4,7,17,24 Self-reported skill level was not assessed in this study. Snowboarders also reported a higher median satisfaction score (10/10) compared to skiers (9/10) that was statistically significant (P = .048), while all other outcome measures were comparable, but this 1-point difference is unlikely to represent a clinically meaningful difference as both groups report high satisfaction. This finding may also be influenced by the younger age in snowboarders. Studies show that PROs after a proximal humerus fracture tend to decrease with increasing age.10,21,27 In contrast, younger patients and adolescents generally have superior PROs for fractures regardless if treated non-operatively or operatively.6,16,18,20

Neer classification and fixation type

No statistically significant differences were detected in outcomes based on Neer classification other than a higher median SF-12 PCS score in patients with simple (2-part) fractures compared to complex (3-4-part) fractures (56.5 vs. 54.2; P = .023). There also was no significant difference detected in fixation strategy of ORIF vs. CRPP between the 2 Neer classification groups (ie, 2- vs. 3–4-part). Long-term outcomes by Zhang et al were reported for 3- and 4-part proximal humerus fractures treated with locking plates at a minimum 10-year follow-up. Patients achieved favorable functional outcomes (mean Constant score 88, University of California Los Angeles 31) despite 23% of patients experiencing avascular necrosis of the humeral head at final follow-up.27 Percutaneous fixation can also provide favorable clinical outcomes in the appropriately selected patient. Keener et al reported a multicenter series (35 patients, mean age ∼61 years), where patients achieved union in all cases, with good functional results (mean American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons 83.4, Constant score 73.9) and low complication rates over an average 35-month follow-up. This cohort was comprised of 2-part, 3-part, and 4-part valgus impacted fractures with no statistically significant effect of fracture classification, patient age, malunion, or degenerative changes on outcomes.13 The results of these studies emphasize that appropriate surgical technique and achieving anatomic reduction are likely more predictive of long-term outcomes than fracture complexity or fixation strategy alone. This finding is consistent with our results, in which a statistically significant difference was not detected based on Neer classification with either clinical outcomes or fixation approach (Table V). The fixation-type analyses are observational and vulnerable to confounding by indication as treatment selection was not randomized and choice of fixation is dependent upon factors such as fracture morphology, displacement, and surgeon preference.

Limitations

This study is not without limitations. Pre-operative outcome measures were not collected due to the nature of this injury presentation and therefore cannot be compared to post-operative outcomes. There was a variable range of follow-up, 83.9 months average (range 14.9-213.8), that introduces heterogeneity where patient perception and clinical outcomes assessed at earlier follow-up may differ from those obtained later in factors such as adaptation, satisfaction, activity level and recall of sports participation. Of the 74 patients eligible with 2-year follow-up, 50 were successfully contacted and agreed to participate (67.5% follow-up rate), which may introduce selection bias as patients lost to follow-up may differ from those included. The favorable outcomes and RTS rates may represent best-case estimates as patients with persistent symptoms might have been less likely to participate. Differences in follow-up care and rehabilitation protocols may have influenced outcomes as patients often returned to their primary residency following surgery at the place of injury. Additionally, long-term radiographic follow-up was not available for all patients and complications such as early collapse or avascular necrosis may not have been fully obtained. Confounders such as skill level and skiing/snowboarding-specific questions could not be accounted for due to a standard follow-up questionnaire being utilized. The retrospective nature of this study and data from a single surgeon at a single center limits the generalizability of the findings. Future studies with larger patient cohorts representing multiple ski areas nationally may offer more comparable data regarding clinical outcomes in skiers and snowboarders that underwent surgical fixation for proximal humerus fractures. Additionally, a follow-up questionnaire that includes time to RTS, participation level, frequency of participation, and reasons for failing to RTS would provide a more comprehensive understanding of RTS outcomes.

Conclusion

Operative fixation of proximal humerus fractures sustained during skiing or snowboarding yields excellent clinical outcomes, with 77% (37/48) of patients returning to sport at a mean follow-up of 83.9 months, high patient satisfaction (median 10/10), and a low revision rate (4%, 2/50 operative cases). Clinical outcomes were largely comparable across age, sex, sport, fracture severity, and fixation type, underscoring the reliability of surgical management for proximal humerus fractures in active snow sport populations.
