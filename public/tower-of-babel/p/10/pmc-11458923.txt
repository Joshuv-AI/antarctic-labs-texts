Pitfalls on image evaluation of tumor viability and anti-tumor efficacy in metastatic mucinous breast cancer: A case report
Authors: Senri Kondo, Shoji Oura
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 66-year-old woman with metastatic mucinous breast cancer was referred to our hospital. The patient had lymph node and multiple lung metastases judged as progressive disease. Positron emission tomography showed radio tracer uptake neither in the axillary lymph nodes nor in the lung metastases. Chemotherapy brought about marked regression of the lymph node and lung metastases. Pathological study of the regressed but still swollen metastatic axillary lymph nodes showed no viable cancer cells with fibrosis and abundant mucin. Multidisciplinary treatment including chemotherapy followed by endocrine therapy fortunately resulted in complete response of the lung lesions. The patient has been well on endocrine therapy for more than 3 years without any image detectable cancer foci. Diagnostic physicians should note that the presence of mucin in mucinous breast cancers can cause underestimation of tumor viability assessment with positron emission tomography and therapeutic efficacy assessment with various image modalities.

Introduction

Positron emission tomography (PET) can judge malignant potential of small doubtful tumors through the evaluation of glucose metabolism. PET, therefore, is very useful in staging acts, recurrence checks, and the evaluation of therapeutic efficacy in various solid malignancies.

Mucinous breast cancer is classified as a special subtype of breast cancer. Due to its indolent biology, mucinous breast cancer is rarely treated with multidisciplinary therapy including chemotherapy or evaluated with PET / CT for the determination of therapeutic efficacy.

We experienced an extremely rare case of metastatic mucinous breast cancer successfully treated mainly with chemotherapy [1] and herein report diagnostic problems about image evaluation of tumor viability and therapeutic efficacy in metastatic mucinous breast cancer.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old woman was referred to our hospital for the treatment of her progressive metastatic luminal mucinous breast cancer. Computed tomography (CT) showed swollen lymph nodes larger than 2 cm in the axilla and a large metastatic tumor approximately 5 cm in the right lung with bilateral multiple small lung nodules (Fig. 1A and D). PET, however, showed avid radio tracer uptake neither in the swollen axillary lymph nodes nor even in the largest metastatic lung focus (Fig. 2). Under the judgement of life-threatening metastatic disease, the patient received sequential anthracycline- and paclitaxel-containing chemotherapies, fortunately leading to marked regression of the lymph node (Fig. 1E) and lung metastases (Fig. 1B). Despite the favorable response to the chemotherapy, the patient complained of marked pain due to the direct exposure of the chest wall which had been caused by the total necrosis of her left breast after the whole breast radiotherapy, done in another hospital, to control the bleeding from the large breast cancer. The patient, therefore, underwent palliative extended latissimus dorsi musculocutaneous (LDMC) flap grafting to cover the large skin defect for symptom relief. In the operation, regressed but still swollen metastatic axillary lymph nodes were resected for the efficacy evaluation of the chemotherapy. Pathological study showed that the lymph nodes had abundant mucin, fibrosis, and no viable cancer cells (Fig. 3). Pseudomonas infection on the necrotic breast had bacteriologically continued on the exposed chest walls at the time of operation and postoperatively caused partial necrosis of the LDMC flap, unfortunately making the patient receive long term wound care [1]. The patient postoperatively began to receive fulvestrant and palbociclib therapy. CT showed gradual regression of the multiple lung metastases, finally showing quasi complete response in 14 months after the operation (Fig. 1C). Discussion

Vast majority of mucinous breast cancers have indolent biology and generally respond well to endocrine therapies [2]. Breast oncologists, therefore, have extremely rare chances to treat metastatic mucinous breast cancers with some kind of chemotherapies. Consequently, no studies have evaluated the usefulness of PET in the efficacy evaluation of chemotherapy for metastatic mucinous breast cancers.

PET generally shows radio tracer uptake in cancers 1 cm or larger in size. PET, however, did not show meaningful radio tracer uptake even neither in the metastatic lung tumors nor in the lymph nodes in this case. On PET evaluation, 3 mechanisms have been reported to cause poor fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) accumulation in the malignant foci to date.

The first mechanism is excretion of FDG once taken up into cancer cells. It is well known that cancer cells have increased glucose metabolism, causing over-expression of glucose transporters. FDG enters cancer cells through glucose transporters followed by the formation of phosphorylated FDG in cancer cells. Phosphorylated FDG does not react further and accumulate in the cancer cells, leading to the radio tracer uptake in cancer tissue. Normal hepatocytes, however, have higher phosphatase activity than other cells, leading to the dephosphorylation and ejection of FDG in and from the hepatocytes, respectively. Therefore, well-differentiated hepatocellular carcinomas with retained high phosphatase activities are often under-evaluated with PET [3].

The second mechanism is that the accumulation of FDG appears low due to the presence of some substance within cancer cells. Underestimation of PET through this mechanism is often observed in signet ring cell carcinomas with abundant intra-cellular mucin [4].

The last mechanism is due to low cancer cell density. PET cannot show meaningful radio tracer uptake in the cancer foci of various noninvasive cancers extending sparsely and widely [5]. In mucinous breast cancer, cancer cells themselves are not different from other breast cancer cells but have large amount of mucin around the cancer cells, resulting in the underestimation of FDG uptake. PET, therefore, poses a major clinical problem in the viability assessment of even large mucinous breast cancers.

Conversely, pathological results in this case clearly showed that image modalities other than PET also had possible underestimation of therapeutic efficacy for mucinous breast cancers when judged by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors [6]. In short, retained mucin, actually observed in this case, can make the lymph nodes keep large sizes even when having no viable cancer cells.

Conclusion

Diagnostic physicians should note that the presence of mucin in mucinous breast cancers can cause underestimation both of tumor viability assessment with PET and therapeutic efficacy assessment with various image modalities.
