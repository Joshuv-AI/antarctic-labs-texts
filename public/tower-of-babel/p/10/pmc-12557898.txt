Glucose metabolic dysfunction surpasses dyslipidemia in driving hepatic steatosis and fibrosis: evidence from a nationally representative population
Authors: Mingxia Gao, Chuan Lin, Yanhua Li, Ling Li, Xiaoqiao Chen, Jing Wang, Wenhua Du, Qing Pan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence remains insufficient to clarify whether hyperglycemia or hyperlipidemia exerts a greater influence on the development of liver steatosis and fibrosis. Furthermore, it remains uncertain whether distinct glucose-lipid metabolic profiles are associated with an increased risk of these hepatic conditions.MethodsWe used NHANES data to assess liver steatosis and fibrosis via Controlled Attenuation Parameter (CAP) and Liver Stiffness Measurement (LSM), then propensity-score weighted regression was used to access associations between seven glycolipid metabolic biomarkers and the two liver indices. Radar plots and network analysis were utilized to illustrate metabolic distribution patterns and delineate key metabolic signatures distinguishing four liver health phenotypes: Healthy Control, Steatotic Liver, Fibrotic Liver, and Combined Steatotic-Fibrotic Liver. Linear regression analyses were further conducted to compare the levels of steatosis and fibrosis across eight metabolic subgroups, categorized according to distinct combinations of dyslipidemia, dysglycemia, and insulin resistance. Lastly, a tree-based algorithm was employed to identify distinct glycolipid metabolic profiles associated with increased severity of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis.ResultsA total of 9698 individuals were included (mean age: 44.2620.80 years). Propensity score-weighted regression showed that TG, GLU, HbA1c, and HDL were significantly associated with both CAP and LSM, while TC and LDL were associated with CAP only. Radar plots and network analysis revealed that the steatosis-fibrosis group had the most adverse metabolic profile, with the lowest HDL, highest insulin, HbA1c, and GLU levels. TG and GLU consistently ranked as the top two central metabolic hubs across all liver groups, while HbA1c ranked third except in the steatotic-fibrotic group, where insulin resistance prevailed. Among subgroups with a single metabolic abnormality, CAP was highest in the insulin resistance (IR)-only group (B = 14.433, P < 0.001), followed by the dysglycemia-only group (B = 10.142, P < 0.001), and lowest in the dyslipidemia-only group. In IR-associated subgroups, CAP was significantly higher when IR co-occurred with dysglycemia (B = 57.393, P < 0.001) than with dyslipidemia (B = 53.635, P < 0.001). The triple abnormalities group exhibited the highest overall CAP (B = 79.811, P < 0.001) as well as the highest liver stiffness measurement (LSM) (B = 1.543, P < 0.001). Tree-based analysis further identified that CAP was highest in individuals with insulin ≥ 14.705 µU/mL and GLU ≥ 5.861 mmol/L, while LSM was highest in those with insulin between 17.1 and 28.205 µU/mL, HbA1c ≥ 6.15%, and GLU ≥ 7.861 mmol/L.ConclusionsMetabolic abnormalities related to hyperglycemia may be more closely associated with hepatic steatosis and fibrosis than those related to hyperlipidemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01945-9.Keywords: Liver fibrosis, Liver steatosis, Dyslipidemia metabolism, Dysglycemia metabolism, Regression tree.

Introduction

Liver ultrasound is a fundamental tool in routine health examinations, widely used to assess hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, which are increasingly prevalent among asymptomatic individuals undergoing medical check-ups. A recent large-scale cross-sectional study conducted in China, involving over 5.7 million adults, reported a prevalence of hepatic steatosis exceeding 40% in the health examination population [1]. Globally, a meta-analysis estimated the pooled prevalence of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) to be 29.8%, with notable sex-specific differences—38.0% in men and 26.0% in women [2]. Without timely diagnosis and intervention, hepatic steatosis and fibrosis may progress to more severe liver diseases, including non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) [3] and hepatocellular carcinoma [4]. Beyond hepatic complications, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) has also been associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease [5], type 2 diabetes mellitus [6], chronic kidney disease [7], colorectal [8] and breast cancers [9].

Abnormalities in carbohydrate and lipid metabolism are widely recognized as key contributors to the development of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis. Hepatic triglyceride accumulation results from increased uptake of free fatty acids, enhanced de novo lipogenesis [10], and impaired VLDL secretion [11], ultimately leading to intrahepatic lipid overload and progressive steatosis. Elevated LDL may increase hepatic cholesterol burden [12], induce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses [13, 14], and promote the progression from simple steatosis to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) and fibrosis. Fasting hyperglycemia can further exacerbate hepatic lipid accumulation by enhancing gluconeogenesis and stimulating compensatory insulin secretion [15]. Among these metabolic disturbances, insulin resistance is considered the central driver of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis [16, 17]. Impaired insulin sensitivity promotes lipolysis in adipose tissue, thereby increasing the release of free fatty acids into circulation, which directly contributes to hepatic fat accumulation. Although substantial mechanistic evidence supports the involvement of both glucose and lipid metabolic disorders in the pathogenesis of NAFLD, large-scale population-based studies are still lacking to determine which of these metabolic pathways plays a dominant role in disease initiation and progression. Moreover, it remains unclear which specific combinations of glucose and lipid metabolic abnormalities confer the highest risk for hepatic steatosis and fibrosis.

To address this knowledge gap, we analyzed data from the 2017–2020 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to examine associations between glucose and lipid metabolism biomarkers and liver steatosis and fibrosis using generalized propensity score-weighted regression models. Participants were categorized into four liver condition groups and eight mutually exclusive metabolic phenotypes. We compared biomarker distributions across liver status groups and evaluated liver outcomes across metabolic phenotypes to determine whether dyslipidemia or dysglycemia plays a greater role in liver injury. A regression tree analysis was further performed to identify high-risk metabolic profiles. These findings may enhance early risk stratification and inform targeted prevention strategies for liver disease.

Methods

Study populations

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) consisted of cross-sectional studies conducted in the United States, combining in-person interviews, physical examinations, and laboratory tests. Managed by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) in collaboration with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the data were made publicly available biennially. Participants were selected using a stratified, multistage probability sampling design based on U.S. Census data, ensuring the sample represented the civilian, non-institutionalized U.S. population. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Each survey cycle received approval from the NCHS Research Ethics Review Board. Data from the 2017–2020 NHANES cycles were used in this study.

Measurement of liver steatosis and fibrosis

Liver steatosis and fibrosis were assessed using vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) performed with the FibroScan® 502 V2 Touch device in the NHANES Mobile Examination Centers. All measurements were conducted by trained health technicians following standardized procedures, with quality control measures including certification, performance monitoring, and regular calibration of equipment. Examinations with fewer than 10 valid LSM readings, interquartile range/median LSM ≥ 30%, or fasting duration of less than 3 h were considered incomplete, although all recorded results were retained for analysis. In this study, liver steatosis (fatty liver) was defined as a CAP value ≥ 248 dB/m, and liver fibrosis was defined as an LSM value ≥ 7.0 kPa. These thresholds are consistent with previously established criteria for diagnosing significant liver disease. Participants with CAP values ≥ 248 were classified as having fatty liver [18, 19], while those with LSM values ≥ 7.0 kPa were classified as having fibrosis [20, 21].

Covariates

Based on literature review and prior knowledge, covariates included age, gender, race (mexican american, other hispanic, non-hispanic white, non-hispanic black, other), education (college and above, high school and below), marital status (married/living with partner, widowed/divorced/separated, never married), drinking status (current drink, never drink), smoking status (ever smoking, never smoking, current smoking), ratio of family income to poverty, hypertension, diabetes mellitus [22, 23].

Definition of ultrasonic liver condition

Based on the definitions of liver steatosis and fibrosis, participants were further classified into four groups: Healthy control, Steatotic Liver, Fibrotic Liver, and Steatotic and Fibrotic Liver. Healthy control: Participants who had neither liver steatosis nor liver fibrosis, with CAP values < 248 dB/m and LSM values < 7.0 kPa. Steatotic Liver: Participants with liver steatosis, defined as CAP values ≥ 248 dB/m, but without liver fibrosis (LSM values < 7.0 kPa). Fibrotic Liver: Participants with liver fibrosis, defined as LSM values ≥ 7.0 kPa, but without liver steatosis (CAP values < 248 dB/m). Steatotic and Fibrotic Liver: Participants with both liver steatosis and liver fibrosis, defined by CAP values ≥ 248 dB/m and LSM values ≥ 7.0 kPa.

Definition of metabolic phenotypes

Three metabolic conditions were defined based on established clinical thresholds. Glucose abnormality was defined as a fasting plasma glucose (FPG) level ≥ 100 mg/dL or a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level ≥ 5.7%. Lipid abnormality was defined as the presence of any of the following: triglycerides (TG) ≥ 150 mg/dL, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) ≥ 130 mg/dL, or reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), defined as < 40 mg/dL in males and < 50 mg/dL in females. Insulin resistance (IR) was evaluated using the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), calculated as FPG (converted to mmol/L by dividing the mg/dL value by 18) multiplied by fasting insulin (µU/mL), divided by 22.5; a HOMA-IR value ≥ 2.5 was considered indicative of insulin resistance. Based on the status (normal ✓/abnormal ×) of glucose metabolism, Lipid metabolism, and insulin sensitivity, participants were categorized into eight mutually exclusive metabolic phenotypes. Group 1 included metabolically healthy individuals (no abnormalities). Groups 2–4 represented Only abnormalities: dyslipidemia, dysglycemia, or insulin resistance. Groups 5–7 had dual abnormalities across any two domains, and Group 8 had abnormalities in all three. Subgroup sizes ranged from 180 to 1,234 participants, (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Estimation of the associations between glucose-lipid metabolic biomarkers and liver steatosis and fibrosis indexes

To evaluate the associations between glucose-lipid metabolic biomarkers and hepatic ultrasound indices, generalized propensity score (GPS)-weighted Linear regression models were applied. The GPS was defined as the conditional probability density function of the exposure given covariates. Separate GPS models were constructed for each of the six biomarkers using Linear regression models conditioned on predefined covariates. The inverse of the estimated GPS was then used as a weight to create a pseudo-population in which the covariate distribution was independent of the exposure. Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using the average absolute correlation coefficients. A threshold of less than 0.1 was used to indicate adequate balance [24].

Metabolic portraits in healthy, steatotic, fibrotic, and steatotic-fibrotic groups

Radar plots and network analysis were applied to investigate the differences in glucose and lipid metabolism patterns across the four liver condition groups. Radar plots were used to visualize the distribution of glucose and lipid metabolism biomarkers across these groups, providing a comprehensive overview of the metabolic profiles within each group. In network analysis, the strength centrality index was calculated to assess the most influential biomarkers across different liver condition groups. The strength centrality index measures the total strength of connections that each biomarker has with other biomarkers within the network. This index helps identify biomarkers that play a central role in the metabolic network, highlighting those with the strongest interconnections. By pinpointing these key biomarkers, the analysis provides valuable insights into the critical regulators of glucose and lipid metabolism in each liver condition group.

Metabolic phenotypes and susceptible sub-populations of liver steatosis and fibrosis

Linear regression models further adjusted for age, gender, race, poverty-income ratio, hypertension status, alcohol consumption, and smoking status were employed to estimate the differences in the average levels of CAP and LSM across groups.Finally, to explore the combined influence of multiple metabolic factors on liver steatosis and fibrosis, regression tree models were constructed with CAP and LSM as outcome variables. These models aimed to identify subgroups characterized by the highest mean values of the respective liver indices.

Discussion

In this study, we identified glucose metabolic abnormalities—rather than lipid abnormalities—as the primary contributors to Liver steatosis and fibrosis among individuals undergoing routine health check-ups. Based on a large, nationally representative population sample from NHANES 2017–2020, we found that individuals with Only hyperglycemia exhibited higher levels of liver steatosis (CAP) and fibrosis (LSM) compared to those with Only hyperlipidemia. Moreover, even in the absence of glucose or lipid metabolic abnormalities, Only insulin resistance was associated with even higher CAP and LSM values. When insulin resistance coexisted with either glucose or lipid metabolism abnormalities, both CAP and LSM further increased. The highest CAP and LSM levels were observed in individuals with concurrent glucose and lipid abnormalities compounded by insulin resistance. Additionally, we found that the subgroup with insulin ≥ 14.705 µU/mL and glucose ≥ 5.861 mmol/L had the highest average CAP values, while the subgroup with 17.1 ≤ insulin < 28.205 µU/mL, HbA1c ≥ 6.15%, and glucose ≥ 7.861 mmol/L showed the highest average LSM values. These findings highlight the central role of glucose metabolism in liver disease progression and emphasize the importance of early identification and management of dysglycemia and insulin resistance in preventing adverse hepatic outcomes.

Glucose over lipids: the main driver for liver steatosis and fibrosis

It remains unclear whether Only hyperlipidemia exerts a stronger influence on hepatic steatosis compared to Only hyperglycemia. But current evidence suggests that Only dysglycemia may exert a stronger association with hepatic steatosis than Only dyslipidemia. And Initiation of hepatic steatosis may be more dependent on de novo lipogenesis than on adipose lipolysis and likely mediated by hyperinsulinemia [25–27], supported by mechanistic, clinical. Mechanistically, Hyperglycemia plays a significant role in activating key transcriptional regulators involved in hepatic de novo lipogenesis (DNL), notably carbohydrate-responsive element-binding protein (ChREBP) and sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1c (SREBP-1c). ChREBP is activated by glucose and, upon activation, induces the expression of genes involved in glycolysis and lipogenesis, thereby promoting intrahepatic triglyceride synthesis. Similarly, SREBP-1c is stimulated by insulin and glucose, leading to the upregulation of lipogenic genes and contributing to hepatic lipid accumulation [28]. Prevoius studies demonstrate that when fasting glucose reaches the prediabetic range (5.3–5.6 mmol/L), hepatic DNL accounts for over 50% of intrahepatic triglycerides, exceeding the contribution from dietary Lipid sources which falls below 30% [29]. Even subclinical insulin resistance (IR) in prediabetes exacerbates steatosis by suppressing lipolysis and promoting ectopic lipid deposition, as shown in studies linking prediabetes to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [30–32]. A study involving 652 patients with NAFLD also demonstrated that the average liver stiffness measurement (LSM) in the group with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) alone was 7.77 kPa, which was higher than that observed in the group with Only dyslipidemia (LSM = 5.74 kPa) [33].

It should be noted that although our findings indicate that individuals with Only insulin resistance or Only hyperglycemia exhibit more pronounced hepatic steatosis and fibrosis compared to those with Only hyperlipidemia, this does not diminish the critical role of dyslipidemia in the development of fatty liver disease. The primary aim of this study is to underscore the often-overlooked impact of hyperglycemia and high-sugar dietary patterns on liver health. Notably, epidemiological research in this area remains limited. Therefore, future randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and systematic reviews comparing the efficacy of glucose-lowering versus lipid-lowering therapies in mitigating hepatic steatosis will be of great importance in clarifying their respective contributions to the pathogenesis of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).

Heterogeneous susceptibility of fat liver from glucolipid metabolism sub-populations

We found that individuals with insulin ≥ 14.705 µU/mL and GLU ≥ 5.861 mmol/L exhibited significantly elevated hepatic fat content, as measured by the CAP, indicating a high-risk metabolic phenotype for hepatic steatosis. In contrast, participants with fasting insulin < 6.945 µU/mL, fasting glucose < 5.805 mmol/L, and triglycerides < 102.5 mg/dL showed the lowest CAP values, reflecting a metabolically favorable profile associated with minimal hepatic fat accumulation. These findings underscore the clinical value of precisely defining glycemic and Lipid thresholds to guide personalized strategies for maintaining metabolic health and preventing fatty Liver disease. Importantly, previous studies have also identified threshold effects in glucose and Lipid metabolism beyond which the risk of hepatic steatosis and fibrosis rises sharply, further supporting our hypothesis of heterogeneous susceptibility across metabolic phenotypes. For example, a study with analyzed data from over 30,000 individuals and found that the composite index TGH-BMI—incorporating triglycerides, fasting blood glucose, HDL-C, and BMI—showed a nonlinear association with both CAP and LSM. Specifically, among men, a TGH-BMI value exceeding 177.7 was associated with a steeper increase in CAP, while LSM rose most prominently within the range of 131.7 to 253.3. Among women, a similar acceleration in CAP was observed once TGH-BMI exceeded 94.7, suggesting that these thresholds may help identify individuals at high risk of liver pathology [34]. In addition, Fiorentino et al. (2016) demonstrated that in individuals with HbA1c-defined prediabetes, a 1-hour post-load glucose (1 h-PG) level ≥ 155 mg/dL was associated with a significantly increased risk of hepatic steatosis, even higher than that in individuals with HbA1c elevation alone. This finding suggests that acute postprandial hyperglycemia, even in the absence of overt fasting glucose abnormalities, may be an early and sensitive marker of liver metabolic stress [35].

Drinking status play less impact on severity liver steatosis and fibrosis

Additionally, our results revealed that trends in liver steatosis and fibrosis severity across the eight metabolic phenotypes were consistent with those observed in the total population. And more importantly, comparisons within metabolic phenotype groups showed no significant differences in LSM and CAP between the current drinking and never-drinking sub-populations within each metabolic phenotype subgroup (Table 3; Fig. 9). This finding can be explained by mechanisms highlighted in a previous study by Tarantino & Citro (2024) [36], which identified dysregulated lipid metabolism, particularly de novo lipogenesis (DNL), as common key contributors to both alcoholic and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease. Their work demonstrated that metabolic dysfunction, rather than alcohol consumption, emerged as the primary driver of liver fat accumulation and fibrosis. Specifically, SREBP-1, a transcription factor that regulates lipid synthesis, was found to be upregulated in both alcohol-related and metabolic dysfunction-related liver diseases, promoting increased liver triglyceride accumulation. Their work provides invaluable insights into the molecular pathways involved, and our findings further support the notion that lipid metabolism, is a central factor in liver steatosis and fibrosis.

This study has several strengths. Leveraging a large, population-based dataset, it is the first to systematically characterize glucose and lipid metabolic profiles across four liver phenotypes and to assess the severity of steatosis and fibrosis across mutually exclusive metabolic subgroups. The findings offer direct epidemiological support for the key role of glycaemic control in the prevention of hepatic steatosis and highlight risk heterogeneity through tree-based modeling, enabling more tailored prevention strategies. Nonetheless, this study has certain limitations. First, findings derived from tree-based classification comparing levels of liver steatosis and fibrosis among metabolic high-risk subgroups might not be directly generalizable to predominantly Asian populations, as our analysis was primarily based on U.S. population. Additionally, the cross-sectional design precludes definitive causal inference. Although extensive adjustments for multiple confounders were performed, residual confounding cannot be completely ruled out. Thirdly, the reliance on non-invasive measures (such as CAP and LSM from FibroScan) to assess liver steatosis and fibrosis may not precisely reflect liver histology, potentially introducing misclassification. However, these methods are widely validated and recognized for large-scale population studies, providing reliable and reproducible estimates of hepatic conditions [37, 38].

Conclusion

Our findings underscore the potential role of hyperglycemia, particularly insulin resistance, as an important factor associated with hepatic steatosis and fibrosis, suggesting that glucose management might be considered as part of preventive strategies. Additionally, the identification of metabolic subgroups at higher risk through tree-based models supports the possible benefit of precision approaches in liver disease screening and intervention.
