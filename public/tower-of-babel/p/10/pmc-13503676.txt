Preoperative onapristone-XR in postmenopausal women with progesterone receptor-positive (PgR+)/HER2-negative early breast cancer: SOLTI-1802 ONAWA trial results
Authors: Meritxell Bellet, Juan M Ferrero-Cafiero, Milana Bergamino Sirvén, Claudette Falato, Xavier González, Kepa Amillano, Nuria Chic, Serafin Morales, Ariadna Gasol, Paolo Nuciforo, Guillermo Villacampa, Patricia Villagrasa, Caroline H Diep, Davis Seelig, Carol Lange, Antonio D’Assoro, Aleix Prat, Tomás Pascual, Cristina Saura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOnapristone, a type-1 antiprogestin, has been tested for advanced breast cancer (BC), but its activity in early-stage, hormone receptor-positive, HER2-negative BC has not been fully explored. The ONAWA trial (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT04142892– Registered on January 6, 2021) was a single-arm study evaluating the preoperative effects of onapristone with extended-release formulation (ONA-XR) (50 mg BID for 21 days) in postmenopausal women with Progesterone Receptor-positive (PgR+)/HER2-negative early BC. No complete cell cycle arrest (CCCA) was achieved at day 21, however ONA-XR significantly suppressed tumor proliferation with a Ki-67 geometric mean change of −17.78% (CI95 −39.76% to 12.20%) particularly in tumors with baseline PgR≥90% (−46.21%; CI −63.02% to −21.76%). Immunohistochemistry showed suppression of phospho-Ser294-PgR and p-STAT3, indicating effects on proliferation and endocrine pathways. Treatment also induced a shift to a more endocrine-sensitive phenotype. Of the 10 patients, 60% experienced adverse events, including one grade 3 increase in gamma-glutamyltransferase. In conclusion, ONA-XR showed early tumor proliferation suppression, particularly in high PgR-expressing tumors, supporting its potential for further clinical evaluation.Subject terms: Breast cancer, Cancer genomics, Tumour biomarkers

Introduction

Progesterone receptor (PgR) expression, evaluated by immunohistochemistry (IHC), is widely used as a clinical biomarker of estrogen signaling and estrogen receptor (ER) functionality, with PGR being an estrogen-responsive gene1.

Despite its well-characterized role as a mitogen and an independent activator of ER, antiprogestins have been infrequently tested for the treatment of breast cancer (BC). While limited success for antiprogestins has been reported in metastatic settings2–4 two recent window-of-opportunity (WOO) trials have shown promising anti-proliferative effects in treatment-naïve patients diagnosed with early BC5,6.

Among the antiprogestin agents developed to target PgR in hormone receptor (HR)-positive BC, onapristone, a type 1 antiprogestin, has demonstrated clinical benefits in the endocrine treatment of BC3,4. However, the use of the original formulation of onapristone was associated with unacceptable liver function test abnormalities in one-third of patients treated within clinical trials², leading to a halt in its further development.

To overcome this limitation, an extended-release formulation of onapristone (ONA-XR) has been developed, designed to maintain minimum plasma concentrations comparable to the original formulation while reducing peak plasma levels. Safety data from two Phase I-II clinical trials involving patients with breast, ovarian, endometrial, and prostate cancers provide robust support for the continued clinical advancement of this extended-release formulation in the treatment of PgR-expressing malignancies7,8.

ONA-XR effectively inhibits the phosphorylation of Ser294 on the PgR, a post-translational modification associated with PgR’s transcriptional activity and degradation. Furthermore, ONA-XR downregulates a specific subset of PgR-regulated genes in tumor cells, regardless of whether they express the activated or non-activated (WT) PgR phenotype1. Importantly, this effect occurs independently of PgR expression status as determined by IHC. These findings highlight ONA-XR’s potential as a therapeutic agent for PgR-driven tumors, supporting the need for further clinical evaluation.

The SOLTI-1802 ONAWA trial (NCT04142892) is the first study to explore ONA-XR in untreated, PgR-positive, and HER2-negative early BC. This trial was designed to extensively assess the biological activity of preoperative ONA-XR following a short treatment window of 21 days.

Results

Patient population

Between November 2020 and April 2021, 12 postmenopausal women with early-stage, HR-positive, HER2-negative untreated BC were screened for eligibility. Ten patients were ultimately enrolled in the trial, received ONA-XR, completed the study treatment, and were included in the primary endpoint analysis (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S1). Baseline patient characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis was 70 years (range 53–81). Clinically, 60% presented with cT1c tumors, 90% had invasive ductal carcinoma, and all patients (100%) had histological grade 2 tumors and were node negative. The median baseline Ki-67, assessed centrally, was 22.7% (range 16–30).

Complete cell cycle arrest and Ki-67 variation from baseline to posttreatment (day 21)

Baseline and posttreatment tumor samples from all 10 patients were available for analysis of changes in tumor proliferation. At the end of the study treatment, complete cell cycle arrest (CCCA), defined as a centralized Ki-67 ≤ 2.7% on day 21, was not achieved in any patient. The geometric mean percentage change in Ki-67 across the entire population was −17.78% (CI95 −39.76% to 12.20%). Ki-67 expression decreased in six patients, remained stable in one, and increased in three (Fig. 2a). Only 1 of 10 patients achieved a postoperative Ki-67 value ≤ 10%. A more significant reduction in Ki-67 of −46.21% (CI −63.02% to −21.76%) was observed in patients whose tumors expressed baseline PgR levels ≥90% (N = 4; Fig. 2c), while Ki-67 increased 9.1% (CI95 −16.9–43.1%) in those with PgR expression <90% (N = 6) (Fig. 2b). When PgR was analyzed as a continuous variable, a strong inverse correlation was identified between baseline PgR expression and the relative change in Ki-67 (Corr = −0.88).

Immunostaining biomarker variations from baseline to posttreatment (day 21)

We then evaluated the dynamics of various immunostaining assays at baseline and posttreatment, including those associated with ER signaling (ER, PgR, phospho-Ser294-PgR), proliferation (Signal transducer and activator of transcriton 3; STAT3) and stem cell markers (CD24, CD44, aldehyde dehydrogenase 1 (ALDH1))9,10. ER expression by IHC remained stable in 8 patients and increased in 2 patients (Fig. 3a). In contrast, PgR decreased in three patients, remained stable in four patients and increased in two patients (Fig. 3a). While Ki-67 levels decreased in all tumors with decreasing PgR, responses by Ki-67 were diverse among tumors with stable or increasing PgR levels at surgery (Fig. 3b). On the other hand, a significant suppression of phospho-Ser294-PgR expression, as well as the Phospho-Ser294-PgR H-score, was observed in all patients following treatment. The mean difference in the phospho-Ser294-PgR H-score from baseline to day 21 was 137.5 (range: 5–230) (Fig. 3a). These findings might reflect the crucial role of phospho-Ser294-PgR as a biomarker of the activation status of PgR regulatory networks.

A double staining of phosphorylated (p)-PgR and p-STAT3 was also performed using quantitative tissue analysis through digital pathology (HALO) to evaluate the therapeutic effect of ONA-XR. Post-treatment samples showed a decrease in the percentage of total, moderately, and strongly p-PgR-positive cells, along with an increase in weakly-positive cells. Additionally, the percentage of p-STAT3-positive cells also decreased across weak, moderate, and strong staining intensities at day 21, indicating an inhibition of proliferation and cell survival pathways, likely as a consequence of the therapeutic effect (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Regarding cancer stem cell markers, we also assessed the dynamics of CD24, CD44, and ALDH1. Interestingly, CD24 expression decreased in most patients, while CD44 and ALDH1 either increased or remained stable (Fig. 3a).

Finally, we evaluated the changes in tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and the CELTIL score11, observing an overall increase in both parameters. This highlights the immune enrichment induced by ONA-XR (Fig. 3c).

Gene expression changes from baseline to posttreatment (day 21)

Gene expression data were available for nine baseline tumor samples. Based on the PAM50 signatures, six tumors were classified as Luminal A, one as Luminal B, and two as HER2-Enriched (HER2-E) at baseline. At surgery, one Luminal B tumor switched to the Luminal A subtype, while one of the HER2-E tumors transitioned to the Normal-like group (Fig. 4A). The PAM50-based proliferation score decreased in six out of nine patients. A significant correlation was found between changes in the proliferation score and baseline PgR (r = −0.74) or baseline PGR mRNA (r = −0.76) (Supplementary Fig. S3). As expected, baseline PgR and PGR mRNA levels were strongly correlated (r = 0.86), while a weak negative correlation was observed between changes in Ki-67 and the proliferation score (r = −0.34) (data not shown).

Specifically, the chemo-endocrine score (CES), Luminal A signatures, and the expression of ESR1 and PGR genes increased at the posttreatment samples. In contrast, HER2-E, Luminal B, and risk-of-recurrence signatures (based on subtype [ROR-S] and subtype/proliferation [ROR-P]), as well as Ki-67, were downregulated (Fig. 5). Safety

Overall, 6 out of 10 patients (60%) experienced adverse events, with the most common being post-surgical pain (30%), dry mouth (20%), and increase in GGT levels and alterations in transaminases in 10% of cases. Only one adverse event was graded as 3, which involved the increase in GGT. No grade 4 serious adverse events occurred. There were no treatment discontinuations or dose reductions.

Discussion

The ONAWA trial explored the biological effects of ONA-XR formulation in postmenopausal women with early-stage, ER + /PgR + /HER2-negative BC. CCCA defined as Ki-67 < 2.7% at surgery, was not achieved in any patient. However, a decrease in Ki-67 in most tumors, especially in those with high PgR expression and in those with decreasing PgR expression during the treatment was observed. Interestingly, ONA-XR induced a shift toward a more endocrine-sensitive and less proliferative tumor phenotype as shown by the increase in ESR1, PGR and CES score at surgery, and by the suppression of Ki-67 and highly proliferative PAM50 signatures. Circulating post-treatment levels of progesterone increased, indicating a complete and potent antagonist effect of ONA-XR. The reduction in Ki-67 was modest compared with preoperative anti-estrogen therapy, and only one of ten tumors achieved Ki-67 ≤ 10%. This limited antiproliferative effect aligns with prior studies of antiprogestins in HR-positive disease.

An incomplete proliferation suppression induced by anti-progesterone receptor compounds has been previously demonstrated. In a preoperative trial, Lee et al.5 measured Ki-67 after treating both pre- and post-menopausal women with telapristone acetate, another antiprogestin. In agreement with our findings, the authors failed to find a statistically significant proliferation response with similar magnitude of proliferation between pre- and post-menopausal women. They argued that in a population currently exposed to progesterone (i.e., premenopausal women), the expected effect on Ki-67 could be larger, however this cannot not apply to the ONAWA trial population. This is supported by evidence from Elia et al.6, where a reduction in proliferation was observed in a mifepristone-based window-of-opportunity study, but patients were selected for higher progesterone receptor expression (Pg > 50%) and the analysis included the PRA/PRB ratio, a factor not addressed in our study. The observed suppression of Ki-67, while not statistically significant, points to ONA-XR-induced antiproliferative activity.

Previous evidence shows that ONA-XR, unlike other antiprogestins, can reduce Ser294-PgR phosphorylation1. Phospho-Ser294-PgR is an active form of the PgR, highlighting its functional role in progesterone signaling and offering valuable insight into its role in tumor biology and response to treatment7. The phosphorylation is essential for PgR’s ability to bind DNA and activate transcription of target genes in proliferating BC cells12–16. The ONAWA study confirmed a significant suppression of Ser294-PgR phosphorylation in postmenopausal women which may impair the transcriptional activity of phospho-PgR, suggesting a a potentially greater biological activity In BC compared to other antiprogestins1. These findings further support the notion that while PgR expression reflects the mere presence of the receptor, phospho-Ser294-PgR serves as a key marker of its activation status and functional role in regulating gene networks that control key cellular processes17. On the other hand, phosphorylated STAT3 (p-STAT3 Y705) is the active form of the STAT3 protein, which plays a central role in various cellular processes, including cell survival, proliferation, and differentiation18. Notably, STAT3 is a target gene of the progesterone receptor (PR), and several studies have demonstrated PR-STAT3 interactions, particularly in the regulation of cancer stem cell (CSC) associated genes such as KLF417. Previous research has provided evidence supporting PR-STAT3 crosstalk, further underscoring its relevance in the regulation of cancer stem cell pathway19,20. In our study, the observed reduction in p-STAT3 expression following treatment also suggests suppression of this signaling pathway, potentially indicating a therapeutic effect of the treatment with ONA-XR.

Additionally, a key functional role of phosphorylated progesterone receptor (p-PR) is the expansion of the CSC compartment21. This effect would not be adequately captured by a decrease in Ki-67 levels, as stem cells are typically quiescent or exhibit very low proliferative activity22. Our findings suggest that p-PR is essential for maintaining the CSC population, which may include a subset of cells capable of early tumor dissemination. These cells might evade detection in primary solid tumors due to their limited numbers and quiescent state.

Another example of how ONA-XR exhibited antiproliferative activity is reflected in additional CSC biomarkers. A decrease in CD24 expression in 7 of 9 patients was shown, while the dynamics of CD44 and ALDH1 were more heterogeneous. Although the role of CD24 does not seem to be fully defined in BC, in general terms, prior evidence suggests that CD24 promotes tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and metastasis and preclinical evidence has shown potential antitumor activity in targeting CD241,23. High expression levels of CD24 have been associated with poor prognosis, poor disease-free survival, and overall survival in several types of cancer23. The role of CD44 in BC is still controversial. In fact, some literature has linked CD44 with poor disease-free survival (DFS) and OS in breast and other cancers24,25, whereas other studies, including a meta-analysis by Wang et al.26, failed to find this association. Regarding ALDH1 expression in BC; its use alone as a biomarker does not seem to have prognostic value24. In the same line, data from endometrial cancer patients showed that simultaneous expression of CD44 and ALDH1 is associated to extremely unfavorable overall survival. These findings highlight the potential role of CSCs in treatment resistance and tumor relapse, underscoring the need for combination therapeutic strategies that target this resilient population.

In contrast, the observed increase in TILs and the CELTIL score following preoperative ONA-XR treatment in breast cancer patients suggests potential modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment under treatment11. However, an increase in total TILs does not necessarily indicate favorable immune enrichment. Specific characterization of immune subsets (e.g., Tregs, MDSCs, TAMs, and tertiary lymphoid structures) and assessment of IFN-γ pathway activity were not performed in this study, representing important areas for future investigation. Notably, progesterone, whose activity is antagonized by onapristone, has been shown to exert immunosuppressive effects by favoring a Th-2-like cytokine profile and inhibiting mast cell degranulation, which may influence the immune landscape26. These findings suggest that PgR inhibition could modulate immune responses, but detailed functional analyses will be required to fully understand this effect.

The overall changes in gene expression were consistent with the antiproliferative effect of ONA-XR. First, we observed that HER2-E, basal-like and Luminal B signature scores as well as the risk-of recurrence (ROR) decreased, while Luminal A increased. ROR can differentiate patients with BC concerning their risk for late distant recurrence beyond what can be achieved with established clinicopathologic risk factors12,27,28. Second, CES, a unique genomic signature capable of measuring chemo-endocrine sensitivity in HR-positive/HER2-negative BC beyond intrinsic subtype, also showed increased expression following treatment12. Similarly, there was an upregulation of ESR1 and PGR, which encode ER and PgR, respectively, consistent with the expected molecular response to ONA-XR.

Taken together, these findings suggest biological changes that may increase the tumor’s likelihood of benefiting from endocrine treatment, highlighting the potential clinical interest of combining ONA-XR with anti-estrogenic therapies. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that the combination of onapristone with anti-estrogen agents (such as ICI 164384) significantly enhances tumor inhibition compared to monotherapy29. These mechanistic and in vivo data also support the rationale for combining ONA-XR with other ER-targeted therapies such as SERDs or SERMs and future studies may also consider evaluating ONA-XR in combination with SERDs/SERMs or ESR1-targeted agents, particularly in ER/PR-high tumors30. Incorporating additional biomarkers—such as ESR1 mutations, apoptosis markers, or FFNP imaging—could further refine patient selection and provide a more comprehensive assessment of treatment response beyond Ki-67.

Additionally, 19 genes associated with cell adhesion, migration, and key signaling pathways—such as JAK-STAT, PI3K, and MAPK—were significantly upregulated, along with immune-related genes such as CCL4 and IL6. This suggests a potential activation of both adaptive and innate immune responses following treatment. In line with this, previous studies have reported an immunomodulatory role of progesterone and PR, demonstrating their ability to suppress the immune response in the murine mammary gland, thereby suggesting a potential role in breast cancer31. Although these findings are noteworthy, caution is needed due to previous studies highlighting the influence of tumor sampling methods on gene expression. For instance, certain studies have shown that genes most significantly altered after endocrine therapy treatment (e.g., FOS, DUSP1, RGS1, FOSB) were similarly affected in the control group (patients not undergoing endocrine therapy)32,33. In fact, this analysis also revealed significant changes in some of those genes, such as FOS and JUN, which have been previously identified as potential artifacts, warranting further investigation and consideration in future trials.

Finally, the safety results presented here suggest that ONA-XR carries a lower risk of severe hepatotoxicity, which is in line with the conclusions of Cottu et al.7.

Our study has several limitations. First, the small sample size; however, it is important to emphasize that the ONAWA study was designed as an exploratory trial. The sample size of 10 patients was chosen to ensure a minimum exposure to ONA-XR and to generate valuable translational data, rather than to achieve statistical significance. Secondly, the limitations inherent to the scoring methodologies used in IHC assays should be acknowledged, as they include intrinsic variability that may be more pronounced given the small sample size. Additionally, our findings suggest that intermediate CCCA after short-term preoperative treatment may not be an optimal surrogate marker for responses to ONA-XR in a patient population not selected for high PgR expression. Nevertheless, the study demonstrated that short-term treatment with ONA-XR can induce changes in tumor biology while maintaining an acceptable safety and tolerability profile in postmenopausal women.

Results provided and discussed here support continued pharmacologic and clinical investigations with ONA-XR, in this patient population and others, as well as studies of potential combination therapies. In particular, and in contrast to the decrease of Ki-67 observed with antiestrogen agents, other surrogate markers such as increased apoptosis should likely be investigated with ONA-XR or other anti-progestagins. Furthermore, this fact would allow the inclusion of patients with less proliferative tumors than those recruited in this trial (all with Ki-67 ≥ 15%), which represents the majority of ER-positive HER2-positive tumors in which high PgR levels coexist.

Methods

Trial design and patient population

The SOLTI-1802-ONAWA study (ClinicalTrials.gov ID: NCT04142892– Registered on January 6, 2021) was a prospective, multicenter, single-arm, window-of-opportunity study evaluating the biological effects of a short-term of pre-operative ONA-XR in treatment naïve patients with early BC. The study design is reported in Fig. 1.

The trial was planned to recruit 10 patients with previously untreated ER + /PgR + /HER2-negative tumors. Supplementary Table S1 provides a detailed overview of the comprehensive sampling protocol for translational biomarker analysis that was incorporated in the trial.

Eligible patients were required to have histologically confirmed nonmetastatic operable invasive adenocarcinoma of the breast, with primary tumors cT1c-3 ( ≥ 15 mm of largest diameter) cN0 cM0. In addition, postmenopausal women, aged ≥18 years with an ECOG performance status of 0 or 1 were included in the study. Ki-67 ≥ 15% based on local assessment was also required for inclusion purposes. Estrogen and progesterone receptor and HER2 positivity were locally assessed for inclusion purposes and defined according to the American Society of Clinical Oncology-College of American Pathologists guidelines10.

Key exclusion criteria for the study included inoperable locally advanced or inflammatory BC, synchronous invasive bilateral or multicentric BC, metastatic (stage IV) disease, and the need for immediate neoadjuvant chemotherapy or surgical intervention. Details on inclusion and exclusion criteria of the study can be found at the study protocol (Supplementary Data S1). This manuscript has been prepared in accordance with the CONSORT 2010 reporting guidelines. A completed CONSORT checklist is provided in the Supplementary Material (Supplementary Table S2).

Procedures

All patients received ONA-XR continuously at the dose of 50 mg, orally, twice daily for 21 days, with a window of ±3 days, unless any criteria for treatment discontinuation occurred earlier. No dose modifications were permitted. A baseline tumor biopsy should have been collected within 30 days before the start of treatment.

Upon completion of the 21-day treatment period, patients either underwent surgery or, if additional neoadjuvant therapy was required, a biopsy at the discretion of the treating physician, in accordance with local practice guidelines. A mandatory tumor sample—either from the surgical specimen or an ex professo core biopsy—was required for assessment of both primary and translational endpoints. This sample had to be collected within a window of 21 ± 3 days post-treatment. An end-of-study visit was conducted 30 ± 3 days following the post-treatment tissue collection.

Biomarker analysis

Immunohistochemistry assays

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was used to assess the expression of ER, PgR, HER2, Ki-67, CD24, CD44, ALDH1, and Ser294-PgR, as well as p-STAT39,34. All IHC assays were performed on the VENTANA BenchMark ULTRA automated staining platform using the Ultraview DAB IHC detection kit (Cat. No. 760-500, Ventana Medical Systems), unless otherwise specified.

ER and PgR evaluation followed the ASCO/CAP 2020 guidelines. HER2 assessment was performed according to the ASCO/CAP 2018 guidelines. ER and PgR positivity was defined as staining in ≥1% of tumor nuclei. Ki-67 expression level was assessed using immunostainings and reported as the percentage (%) and intensity of stained tumor nuclei, using Rabbit Monoclonal Primary Antibody clone 30.9 (RRID:AB_2631262 Roche, Basel, Switzerland) in an automated Ventana Benchmark Ultra stainer ULTRA IHC/ISH System using the Ultraview DAB IHC detection Kit (Cat. No. 760-500).

The following primary antibodies were used: ER (790-4324, Ventana), PgR (790-2223, Ventana), HER2 (790-2991, Ventana), Ki-67 (clone 30-9, 790-4286, Ventana), CD24 (mouse monoclonal, clone SN3, MAS-11828, Invitrogen), CD44 (rabbit monoclonal, clone SP37, 790-4537, Ventana), ALDH1 (rabbit polyclonal, HPA002123, Sigma-Aldrich), and phospho-STAT3 (Tyr705) (rabbit monoclonal, clone D3A7, #9145, Cell Signaling Technology).

Ser294-PgR was detected using a custom phospho-specific antibody targeting PR Ser294. Ser294-PgR expression was reported as the percentage of positively stained cells and also quantified using an H-score (0–300). Samples with an H-score ≥20 were considered positive.

CD24 and CD44 expression were quantified using the H-score, calculated as 1×(%1 + ) + 2×(%2 + ) + 3×(%3 + ). For ALDH1, the percentage of positive tumor cells (any intensity) was recorded.

Hormone measurements (E2, progesterone, and FSH)

Progesterone and FSH serum levels were measured using the Atellica IM PRGE and Atellica IM FSH assays (Atellica® Immunoassay Analyzer, Siemens Healthineers), which use direct competitive chemiluminescent immunoassay technology. The reportable ranges were 0.21–60.00 ng/mL for progesterone and 0.30–200.00 mIU/mL for FSH.

Estradiol levels (E2) were quantified using the DRG Estradiol Sensitive ELISA (DRG Instruments GmbH), a competitive enzyme immunoassay for the quantitative measurement of estradiol in serum or plasma.

Gene expression analysis

A hematoxylin–eosin–stained section of each FFPE tumor block was reviewed to confirm invasive tumor content and determine the minimum tumor surface area. Total RNA was extracted from at least two 10-µm FFPE sections using the High Pure FFPET RNA Isolation Kit (Roche). Macrodissection was performed for baseline samples when required to avoid normal tissue contamination.

A minimum of ~100 ng RNA was used for gene expression profiling of 752 breast cancer–related genes using the Breast 360™ Codeset on the nCounter platform (NanoString Technologies), which includes the 50 genes of the PAM50 subtype predictor. Data were normalized using five housekeeping genes and log2-transformed. Intrinsic subtypes were assigned using the research-based PAM50 predictor. For each sample, scores for PAM50 subtype signatures (Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-enriched, Basal-like, Normal-like), the proliferation score, the risk-of-recurrence (ROR) score, and the chemo-endocrine score (CES) were calculated.

Statistical analysis

Given the exploratory nature of the study, no formal sample size calculation was performed. A minimum sample size was selected to obtain preliminary biomarker data, estimate the CCCA rate, assess changes in gene expression, and gather safety information while limiting drug exposure due to the absence of prior safety data. All analyses were pre-specified and exploratory.

For RNA-seq differential gene expression analysis, a false discovery rate (FDR) of 5% was pre-selected to control for multiple comparisons. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.1) and Microsoft Excel. Toxicity was assessed using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) version 5.0 in patients who received at least one dose of ONA-XR.
