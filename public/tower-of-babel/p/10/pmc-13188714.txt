Social and digital engagement associated with reduced depressive symptoms in adults aged 50 and older: a multi-country cohort study
Authors: Yaping Wang, Liyuan Tao, Gram Lu, Hongguang Chen, Wenzhan Jing, Chuyao Jin, Jue Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial participation and digital use are associated with reduced depression risk among older adults, but most supporting evidence does not consider both time-invariant and time-varying confounders and is inconsistent. We aimed to evaluate the impact of social participation and digital use on the incidence of depressive symptoms among older adults by a multi-country cohort considering both time-invariant and time-varying counfounders.MethodsWe used data from four nationally representative observational studies across 18 countries (2008–2021): the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the Survey of Health, Aging and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), and the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS). Participants aged 50 years or older without depressive symptoms at baseline and without related behaviors pre-baseline were included. Interested exposure social participation and digital use were measured by specific questions. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) and the European Depression Scale (EURO-D). Targeted maximum likelihood estimation method was applied to estimate adjusted relative risks (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the long-term impact of exposure on depressive symptoms onset.ResultsA total of 69,186 eligible participants were included. At baseline, 77.0%, 44.0%, 34.2%, and 49.8% of participants were exposed to social participation in HRS, SHARE, CHARLS, and MHAS, respectively, and these proportions were 55.7%, 55.9%, 4.5%, and 69.9% for digital use exposure. During follow-up, a total of 18,245 (26.4%) individuals developed depressive symptoms. The RRs (95% CI) of depression risk under social participation versus no social participation were 0.80 (0.68–0.93) in HRS, 0.80 (0.74–0.87) in SHARE, 0.86 (0.77–0.96) in CHARLS, and 0.93 (0.85–1.02) in MHAS. Compared with no digital use, the RRs (95% CI) of depression risk under digital use were 0.88 (0.72–1.08) in HRS, 0.85 (0.77–0.93) in SHARE, 0.75 (0.60–0.92) in CHARLS, and 0.88 (0.79–0.98) in MHAS.ConclusionsEngagement in social participation and digital use are associated with a reduced incidence of depressive symptoms in older adults. In the digital world, besides social participation, promoting digital use for social connection may be an effective strategy for depression prevention in ageing populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04846-4.Keywords: Social participation, Digital use, Depressive symptoms, Older adults

Background

The global increase in the aging population has accelerated due to rising life expectancy and declining birth rates. According to the latest World Population Prospects 2024, the number of people aged 65 years and older worldwide is projected to reach 2.2 billion by the late 2070s, surpassing the number of children under 18 [1]. This demographic shift has raised growing concerns because older adults frequently experience adverse physical and psychological conditions. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021, the age-standardized incidence and prevalence of major depressive disorder among individuals aged 60 years and older were 6.2% and 4.3%, respectively, and both rates are expected to increase steadily through 2050 [2]. Epidemiological studies indicate that the pooled prevalence of depressive symptoms in older adults ranges from 28.4% to 35.1% [3–6]. Although depressive disorders are more prevalent among younger adults [7], the overall burden is greater among older adults due to their higher risk of comorbid cognitive impairment and cardiovascular diseases [8, 9]. Given that most older adults with depression are underdiagnosed and undertreated [10], improving mental health in later life remains a major public health challenge.

Previous studies have linked depression to a range of psychological, social, and biological factors [11–13]. Among these, behavioral and social factors are especially important because they are modifiable and cost-effective to target [14]. For example, multinational studies indicate that older adults who engage in social activities—such as hobbies, social events, club participation, and baking—are less likely to experience depression [12, 15, 16]. In today’s digital environment, digital use among older adults has gained attention as an important determinant of health. By fostering social connections and providing easy access to health information, digital use has been linked to reduced social isolation and loneliness, which in turn contribute to better physical and psychological health outcomes [17, 18].

Although evidence exists for the associations of social participation and digital use with depression in older adults, most studies use traditional epidemiologic statistical analysis and fail to account for both time-invariant and time-varying confounders [18, 19]. Time-varying confounders are crucial for estimating the effects of social participation and digital use on depression in observational studies, especially when randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are difficult and impractical to conduct [20]. Furthermore, some confounders may be influenced by past exposure and may also affect future exposure, complicating unbiased estimation of exposure–outcome associations [21]. Therefore, advanced statistical methods are needed to better address these methodological challenges. Targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE), a doubly robust maximum-likelihood-based approach, includes a secondary “targeting” step that optimizes the bias-variance tradeoff for the target parameter. Under standard study assumptions, TMLE can provide estimates that approximate causal parameters in observational settings [22].

In this study, we aimed to examine the associations of social participation and digital use with incident depressive symptoms among older adults using the TMLE framework, based on four nationally representative aging surveys from 18 countries. We hypothesized that older adults who participate in social activities and use the Internet would have a lower risk of developing depressive symptoms.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multi-country cohort study used observational data from four nationally representative longitudinal studies: the Health and Retirement Study (HRS), the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), and the Mexican Health and Aging Study (MHAS) [23–26]. Detailed descriptions of each study are provided in the Additional file 1: Method. To ensure comparability across surveys and alignment with the TMLE framework, we collected data from similar time periods: HRS (waves 9–15, 2008–2020); SHARE (waves 4–9, 2011–2021); CHARLS (waves 1–5, 2011–2020); and MHAS (waves 3–6, 2012–2021).

We designed two analysis protocols to evaluate the impact of social participation and digital use on depressive symptoms among older adults (always exposed vs. never exposed), respectively. Participants from the four cohorts who met the following eligibility criteria were included in our study: (1) aged 50 years or older at baseline; (2) had no depressive symptoms at baseline; and (3) had no missing information on depressive symptoms at baseline (Additional file 1: Figs. S1–S4). A total of 69,186 eligible participants were followed from the date of the baseline questionnaire until the first occurrence of depressive symptoms, the last available follow-up, or the end of the study period, whichever came first. Given that exposure before baseline may influence subsequent exposure, we considered the pre-baseline exposure status and defined baseline as the interview year of the second wave of collected data for each cohort: wave 11 for HRS, wave 5 for SHARE, wave 2 for CHARLS, and wave 4 for MHAS.

Ethics statement

Each survey was conducted according to the ethical policies and procedures approved by the relevant local research ethics committee, and all participants were recruited after providing written informed consent (Additional file 1: Method). Our research used de-identified and publicly accessible datasets; therefore, ethical approval was not required for the analysis of the anonymized data.

Exposure measurement

Exposure in our study included social participation and digital use. Each aging cohort asked participants whether they engaged in any social activities in the past month or year, and whether they had used the internet in the past week, month, or year (Additional file 1: Table S1). We defined participants as exposed to social participation if they reported participation in at least one social group in the past month (HRS and CHARLS) or year (SHARE and MHAS). Similarly, participants were defined as exposed to digital use if they reported regular use (HRS), use in the past week (SHARE), month (CHARLS), or year (MHAS). We denoted the binary time-varying exposures as Ats for the social participation cohort and Atd for the digital use cohort, where Ats=1 or Atd=1 indicates exposure at time t (the follow-up waves). Given the uncertain changes in exposure during the survey intervals, we imposed the assumption that [27]: (1) the past week/month/year exposure reflects the average current exposure; (2) the next time interval exposure (2-year period between two investigations) would be consistent with the average past week/month/year exposure.

Outcome measurement

The outcome of interest was the onset of depressive symptoms, assessed using different validated scales across the cohorts: the 8-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) in HRS and MHAS, the 10-item CES-D in CHARLS, and the 12-item European Depression Scale (EURO-D) in SHARE (Additional file 1: Tables S2–S3). The 8-item and 10-item CES-D scales are widely used in epidemiological studies and have been extensively validated against the 20-item version in older populations [28]. The recommended cutoff points indicating clinical depression are ≥ 4 for the EURO-D, ≥ 3 for the 8-item CES-D, and ≥ 10 for the 10-item CES-D [29–31]. Based on these cutoffs, we defined the outcome as depression (Yt=1) or no depression (Yt=0) over time.

Covariates

Baseline constant covariates in our study included sex (male, female); the highest education level (less than upper secondary, upper secondary, tertiary); and country (SHARE only). To account for pre-baseline influences on the likelihood of engaging in social participation or digital use, we adjusted for pre-baseline status of these behaviors in the respective cohorts. These pre-baseline and baseline covariates were denoted as W. We also collected the following time-varying variables: (1) demographic characteristics: age, marital status (single, married/partnered), family income (quartiles Q1–Q4), and employment status (not working, working, retired); (2) lifestyle factors: smoking (non-smoker, former smoker, current smoker), alcohol consumption (no, yes), physical activity (active, inactive), and body-mass index (underweight/healthy, overweight, obese); and (3) health status: presence of non-communicable disease (none, one or more), and loneliness (not lonely, lonely). Additionally, time-varying variables included social participation (no, yes) and digital use (no, yes) for different analysis protocol of digital use and social participation, respectively. We denoted these time-varying variables as Lts and Ltd. Details of all covariates are provided in Additional file 1:Table S4.

Statistical analysis

We used median and interquartile ranges (IQRs) for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables to describe baseline characteristics. To address the biases from time-varying variables and the impact of pre-baseline exposure, we used TMLE, a doubly robust method that combines outcome and treatment models and only requires one of them to be correctly specified [32]. TMLE allows the integration of machine learning to flexibly fit treatment and outcome regressions adjusted for baseline and time-varying confounders [33]. According to the requirements of the TMLE framework, we structured the observational data into a longitudinal sequence:

Social participation: W,C1,A1s,L1s,Y1,C2,A2s,L2s,Y2,⋯,Ct,Ats,Lts,Yt

Social participation: W,C1,A1s,L1s,Y1,C2,A2s,L2s,Y2,⋯,Ct,Ats,Lts,Yt

Digital use:W,C1,A1d,L1d,Y1,C2,A2d,L2d,Y2,⋯,Ct,Atd,Ltd,Yt

Digital use:W,C1,A1d,L1d,Y1,C2,A2d,L2d,Y2,⋯,Ct,Atd,Ltd,Yt

Once Y = 1, it remains 1 for all subsequent nodes sorely for bookkeeping in the longitudinal outcome vectors, in accordance with the longitudinal data structure required by the TMLE framework (as implemented in the R package ltmle). Participants were operationally considered to have experienced the event at the first wave with Y = 1, and they were no longer at risk in subsequent intervals. At each time t, the probability of the observed exposure was modeled as

g(Ats|A¯t-1s,L¯t-1s,W) for social participation and gAtd|A¯t-1d,L¯t-1d,W for digital use. In our study, we specified two exposure rules (“abar” in ltmle function) according to requirements of TMLE: always exposed (A¯=1) and never exposed (A¯=0). Participants who were lost to follow-up (nonreturn of a questionnaire or incomplete responses) and who did not develop depression until the end wave of aging surveys (administrative end) were censored. In TMLE approach, the probability of remaining uncensored at each time t was estimated by gCts=0|A¯t-1)s,L¯t-1s,W or gCtd=0|A¯t-1d,L¯t-1d,W. The probabilities of exposure and censoring were used to construct inverse probabilities of treatment weights (IPTW) in ltmle. The outcome regression model (Q) was modeled for each time t as Qts=E(Yts|A¯ts,L¯ts,W) for social participation exposure and Qtd=EYtd|A¯td,L¯td,W for digital use exposure. To improve the stability of TMLE estimation and to avoid undue influence of extreme inverse probability weights, we truncated the estimated exposure and censoring probabilities at the 1st and 99th percentiles.

To handle missing data of all variates except depression at baseline (including pre-baseline and baseline characteristics, time-varying exposure and covariates), we used multiple imputation with 10 datasets via random forests, using the R packages mice and randomForest. Analyses were conducted separately on each imputed dataset and combined using Rubin’s rules [34].

TMLE analyses were conducted in R using the ltmle package with ensemble super learner algorithms, including means (SL.mean), generalized linear models (SL.glm), and generalized additive models (SL.gam). The results are presented as cumulative risk ratios (RRs) over the entire available follow-up period (from baseline to the last observed wave or administrative end) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Additionally, we did five sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings:

Repeating the TMLE models without adjusting for pre-baseline social participation and digital use.

Repeating the TMLE models without adjusting for pre-baseline social participation and digital use.

Repeating the TMLE models without adjusting for loneliness in each node, considering loneliness can mediate the association between social participation and depression.

Repeating the TMLE models without adjusting for loneliness in each node, considering loneliness can mediate the association between social participation and depression.

Including random forests (SL.ranger) in the super learner algorithm.

Including random forests (SL.ranger) in the super learner algorithm.

Excluding participants with missing data on pre-baseline characteristics and the baseline intervention (other missing data were still imputed).

Excluding participants with missing data on pre-baseline characteristics and the baseline intervention (other missing data were still imputed).

Restricting the analysis to participants aged 60 years or older in each cohort.

Restricting the analysis to participants aged 60 years or older in each cohort.

All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). Two-sided p-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. The completed STROBE checklist is provided in Additional file 2.

Association between social participation and depressive symptoms

During follow-up, a total of 18,245 (26.4%) developed depressive symptoms: 1071 (15.0%) in HRS, 11,161 (23.4%) in SHARE, 3457 (49.9%) in CHARLS, and 2556 (34.6%) in MHAS. In comparison with those who did not engage in social activities, individuals who participated in social activities exhibited a reduced likelihood of developing depressive symptoms in HRS, SHARE, and CHARLS, although no significant differences were observed in MHAS. The distribution of treatment weights estimated by TMLE approach is provided in Additional file 1: Table S11. Specifically, the estimated risks of depressive symptoms under social participation exposure versus control were 20.6% (95% CI, 19.0–22.2%) vs. 25.8% (22.3–29.3%) in HRS, 38.1% (36.2–40.0%) vs. 47.5% (45.7–49.3%) in SHARE, 52.6% (47.7–57.4%) vs. 60.8% (58.5–63.1%) in CHARLS, and 42.0% (38.9–45.1%) vs. 45.1% (43.0–47.2%) in MHAS (Table 2). The RR estimates for static socially active participants compared with socially inactive participants were 0.80 (95% CI, 0.68–0.93) in HRS, 0.80 (0.74–0.87) in SHARE, 0.86 (0.77–0.96) in CHARLS, and 0.93 (0.85–1.02) in MHAS. For country level, the positive effects of social participation were observed in 4 of 17 (23.5%) countries, with RR estimates ranging from 0.65 (0.43–0.99) in Spain to 0.86 (0.77–0.96) in China (Additional file 1: Table S12). Details of time-specific cumulative average risks and RRs by follow-up wave under the two strategies (socially active vs. socially inactive) were provided in Additional file 1: Table S13.

Association between digital use and depressive symptoms

Compared with controlled groups, participants exposed to digital use had a lower risk of depressive symptoms in SHARE, CHARLS, and MHAS, but this association was not significant in HRS. Specifically, the estimated risks under digital use versus no use were 19.9% (95% CI, 16.8–22.9%) vs. 22.6% (19.6–25.5%) in HRS, 42.1% (39.7–44.4%) vs. 49.6% (46.7–52.6%) in SHARE, 45.4% (37.8–52.9%) vs. 60.8% (59.0–62.7%) in CHARLS, and 43.1% (41.3–44.9%) vs. 48.8% (44.6–53.0%) in MHAS (Table 2). The RR estimates for participants using internet compared with those not using were 0.88 (95% CI, 0.72–1.08) in HRS, 0.85 (0.77–0.93) in SHARE, 0.75 (0.60–0.92) in CHARLS, and 0.88 (0.79–0.98) in MHAS. For country level, the positive effects of digital use were observed in 4 of 17 (23.5%) countries, with RR estimates ranging from 0.61 (0.38–0.999) in Israel to 0.88 (0.79–0.98) in Mexico (Additional file 1: Table S12). Details of time-specific cumulative average risks and RRs by follow-up wave under the two strategies (digital use vs. digital no use) were provided in Additional file 1: Table S14.

Sensitivity analyses

Figure 1 presents the results of the sensitivity analyses. For the exposure of social participation, the RR estimates remained consistent across all five sensitivity analyses: (1) without adjustment for pre-baseline exposure; (2) without adjustment for loneliness; (3) including the random forest machine learning model in the super learner algorithms; (4) excluding participants with missing data on pre-baseline characteristics and baseline interventions; and (5) restricting the analysis to participants aged 60 years and older. Details of sensitivity analyses results are shown in Additional file 1: Table S15. The results for the exposure of digital use were also robust across all five sensitivity analyses in SHARE, CHARLS, and MHAS. However, in HRS, the effect of digital use on depressive symptoms changed from nonsignificant in the main analysis to significant in sensitivity analyses (1), (3), and (4).

Discussion

Although some studies have linked social participation and digital use to a reduced risk of depressive symptoms, most studies use traditional epidemiologic statistical analysis and do not consider both time-invariant and time-varying confounders. Utilizing the advanced method TMLE, our findings provide additional evidence that social participation and digital use are associated with a reduced risk of depressive symptoms in older adults.

Consistent with previous research, our study highlights the protective role of social participation in reducing depression risk [12, 15]. Although the definition of social connection varies, it is generally recognized as an important determinant of health. According to current studies, researchers have identified social connection as a multifaceted construct, encompassing structural, functional, and qualitative aspects of social relationships [35]. Social isolation—considered the inverse of social participation or integration—is a structural component defined as the objective state of having a small social network and infrequent interactions. It has been shown to be associated with multiple adverse health outcomes, including depression [35]. Specifically, social isolation has been linked to a 32% increased risk of all-cause mortality and a 46% increased risk of depression onset [36, 37]. Given the broad scope and complex influencing factors of social activities, not all forms offer protection against depression. For instance, attendance at religious services—classified as a social participation in our study and others—has been associated with reduced depression risk in some populations [12, 38], but with increased depressive symptoms among older adults in Korea [39]. Another study found that specific social activities, such as helping others or joining sports clubs, were associated with lower depression risk in urban but not rural older adults [40]. Moreover, both the frequency and the number of social activities engaged in can influence the risk of depression [40, 41]. Despite variation by country and subgroup, social participation overall appears to be associated with a lower risk of depression.

The mechanisms by which social activities influence depression are complex [35]. One study summarized over 600 potential pathways through which leisure activities, including social engagement, may affect health [42]. These mechanisms span four domains—psychological, biological, social, and behavioral—and operate at micro, meso, and macro levels [42]. For example, on the micro-level, social interactions formed during group activities may reduce loneliness, which is strongly associated with depressive episodes [13]. Social participation may also lower depression risk by enhancing psychological well-being (e.g., life satisfaction), improving physical functioning, or increasing reliance on social resources [43, 44]. Overall, as a component of the broader construct of social connection, social participation may influence depression risk through multiple and interrelated mechanisms.

Our study also found that digital use was associated with a reduced risk of depressive symptoms among older adults, consistent with prior evidence [18]. This association may be attributed to enhanced social connectivity. Numerous studies have linked digital use to reduced social isolation, mitigated loneliness, improved social support, and strengthened social networks—all of which are associated with lower depression risk [17, 45]. Digital use may also help narrow socioeconomic disparities in depression by enabling access to health education, digital interventions, and remote consultations [12]. Therefore, promoting digital inclusion among older adults could enhance social connection and support health management.

In contrast to older adults, digital use has often been studied as a risk factor for depression among children and adolescents, mainly due to addictive behaviors [46]. This double-edged effect of digital use may depend on its purpose and frequency. As of January 2024, approximately 5.35 billion people—66.2% of the global population—use the Internet [47]. However, the proportion is significantly lower among older adults in most countries [48], suggesting reduced digital engagement and a lower likelihood of internet addiction in this population. The purpose of use also matters. For example, using the internet to escape real-world social interaction may exacerbate loneliness, whereas using it to maintain or develop relationships (e.g., chatting, video browsing, online shopping) is associated with reduced depression risk [17]. Playing online games, however, appears to have no such protective effect [19]. Thus, digital use is not universally beneficial for older adults; its value depends on healthy and meaningful engagement.

In our study, the effects of social participation and digital use on depression were not statistically significant in MHAS and HRS, respectively. Several explanations may account for these differences. First, the measurement was not exactly the same across studies. In MHAS, social participation was assessed over a 12-month period, which may introduce recall bias. In HRS, the digital use item was vague, with “regularly” lacking a clearly defined frequency. Second, in the pathway by which social participation affects depression with mediation of loneliness, social participation is not always identified as a protective factor if the quality of social participation is not enough to generate perceived available social support [49]. Similarly, if older adults used the internet primarily to avoid social interaction or potential emotional discomfort, they may experience increased loneliness [17]. Third, cultural differences may influence the pathways through which social participation and digital use affect depression across countries.

Given the observed associations, promoting social participation and digital use may support mental health in late life. The World Health Organization (WHO) has identified social isolation and loneliness as a priority health problem and policy issue and as one of the themes that cuts across the four main action areas of the UN Decade of Healthy Aging (2021–2030) [50]. On 15th November 2023, WHO announced a new Commission on Social Connection to address loneliness as a pressing health threat, promote social connection as a priority and accelerate the scaling up of solutions in countries of all incomes [51]. However, few countries have developed and implemented relevant policies. Going forward, governments should collaborate across sectors to create age-friendly environments and develop policies that promote participation in social and digital activities among older adults. At the community level, public organizations should improve infrastructure for social engagement and Internet access, offering free community-based activities, such as social or digital skills training, arts or sports groups, and volunteering. At the family and individual levels, older adults should be supported and encouraged to learn how to use digital technologies and engage in activities of interest, in order to foster connections to the broader world.

Although previous studies have identified social participation and digital use as protective factors against depression in older adults, most have relied on traditional epidemiologic statistical analysis. Our study employed a robust estimation approach to highlight the effects of social participation and digital use among older adults by accounting for various time-varying confounding. However, several limitations should be noted. First, depression was measured by self-reported information, which might not be equal to clinical diagnosis though those scales have been widely validated in epidemiological studies. Second, substantial heterogeneities exist in exposure measures, especially for the recall windows. These different time intervals may capture different exposure patterns and potentially lead to heterogeneous causal contracts across cohorts, limit cross-cohort comparability, and impair the extrapolation of our results. Third, despite adjusting for demographic, socioeconomic, and health-related factors, unmeasured confounding may still be present. Fourth, our analyses only considered whether participants engaged in social participation or digital use, without exploring the type, frequency, or intensity of these behaviors, which might also be risk factors of depression. Additionally, because information on lifetime or past psychiatric history was not consistently collected across studies, we did not exclude participants who may have a depression history but have no depressive symptoms at baseline. The inclusion of individuals with prior depression may introduce residual confounding and partly attenuate associations if prior depression influences subsequent internet use or social participation.

Conclusions

Social participation and digital use were associated with a reduced risk of depressive symptoms among older adults across a range of countries with varying income levels. In the digital world, digital use—alongside social engagement—offers a valuable tool to build and maintain social connections in later life.
