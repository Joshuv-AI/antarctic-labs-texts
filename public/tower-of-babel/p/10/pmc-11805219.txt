An In vitro Comparative Analysis of the Shear Bond Value of Sixth, Seventh and Eight Generation Dentin Bonding Agents
Authors: Prashant Jalannavar, Suresh Kumar Kalgapure, Vijay Parmar, Sopan Singh, Ashtha Arya, Karishma Dudekula, Kanika S. Dhull
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjective:This study evaluates the shear bond strength (SBS) of 6th, 7th, and 8th generation dentin bonding agents.Materials and Methods:Thirty extracted premolars were divided into three groups of 10. Group A used a 6th generation bonding agent, Group B a 7th generation bonding agent, and Group C a 8th generation bonding agent. Each tooth was fixed in acrylic resin, sectioned to expose dentin, treated with the respective bonding agent, and built up with composite resin. SBS was assessed by universal testing machine.Results:Highest shear bond value was illustrated by the 8th generation bonding agent, with subsequent values progressively lower for the 7th and 6th generation bonding agents.Conclusion:The 8th generation bonding agents exhibit superior shear bond strength, suggesting enhanced dental restoration durability. Further clinical validation is recommended.

INTRODUCTION

Modern restorative dentistry heavily relies on the efficacy of bonding agents, which ensure adhesion between dental substrates and restorative materials. The evolution from the 6th to the 8th generation dentin bonding agents reflects significant advancements in technology and methodology aimed at enhancing bond strength, simplifying application steps, and improving clinical outcomes.[1] The 6th generation bonding agents, known as “self-etching primers,” combine primer and adhesive into two separate bottles, simplifying the bonding procedure and reducing technique sensitivity associated with traditional multi-step systems. However, concerns regarding their long-term bond durability persist.[2] The 7th generation bonding agents introduced a further simplification by integrating etch, prime, and bond into a single application, thereby reducing chair time and potential for user error. Despite this user-friendly approach, questions still remain about the consistency of their bond strength and their adhesion capabilities to both enamel and dentin. The 8th generation bonding agents represent the latest advancements, incorporating nanotechnology and improved chemistry to enhance adhesive properties, promising superior bond strength, longevity, and reduced microleakage.[34] Current research aims to assess shear bond strength of dentin bonding agents across various generations through controlled ex-vivo testing to determine whether the improvements in newer generations translate to quantifiable enhancements in bond performance.

MATERIAL AND METHOD

For this study, thirty recently extracted premolars were procured and designated as samples. To ensure the integrity and hygiene of the samples, each tooth underwent a thorough cleaning process before being stored in saline solution to preserve their natural condition until required for the experiment. For ease of handling during various procedures, all teeth were embedded in acrylic resin blocks. Using a diamond saw, the teeth were reduced to create flat dentinal surfaces, which are critical for achieving consistent and reliable bonding.

The experiment was organized into three groups based on the generations of bonding agents being tested:

Group A: Utilized the 6th generation bonding agent, Clearfil SE BondGroup B: Employed the 7th generation bonding agent, G-Bond (GC)Group C: Engaged the 8th generation bonding agent, Scotch bond Universal (3M).

Each bonding agent was applied to the assigned samples following the specific guidelines provided by their respective manufacturers. To fabricate the composite resin samples, a Teflon mold was employed, resulting in specimens with dimensions of 2 mm in diameter and 3 mm in height. The composite resin used for this purpose was 3M™ Filtek™ Z250 XT.

After the bonding agents were applied and the composite resin was set, the samples were submerged in sterile water and stored at a controlled temperature of 37°C for a duration of 24 hours to simulate oral conditions and ensure optimal setting.

Shear bond strength was the key parameter assessed in this study. SBS measurements was conducted using a universal testing machine. For accuracy, each specimen was positioned such that a chisel-shaped blade was precisely aligned at the interface between the composite resin and the dentin. The testing machine applied a load at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until bond failure occurred. The maximum load at failure was recorded in Newtons. To express SBS values in Megapascals (MPa), the recorded load at failure was divided by the bonding area. This allowed for a standardized comparison of bonding strength across all groups. The SBS values obtained for each group were subjected to statistical analysis to determine any significant differences in performance among the different generations of bonding agents.

DISCUSSION

The principal aim of assessing bond strength is to determine the adhesive effectiveness of bonding agents to dental hard tissues. The development of advanced dentin bonding agents is focused on improving bonding quality while reducing application time. The clinical efficiency of restorative materials relies on their ability to adhere effectively to the dentinal surface, thereby withstanding the diverse displacing forces present within the oral environment. While compressive and tensile strengths are vital standards for evaluation, the present study concentrates on assessing shear bond strength. This focus is due to the clinical relevance of shear bond strength, as primary dislodging forces at the tooth-restoration junction manifest as shearing forces. Thus, an elevated shear bond strength signifies superior attachment of the restorative material to the tooth.[56]

The objective of this research was to assess the shear bond strengths of 6th, 7th and 8th generation dentin bonding agents. The mean SBS values differed among the various generations of bonding agents. Notably, the 8th generation bonding agents demonstrated the highest mean SBS values, followed by 7th and 6th generation agents, respectively. This trend suggests significant advancements in adhesive technology over time, leading to increased bonding strength. Our findings align with those reported in studies conducted by Ganesh (2020)[6] and by Mishra et al. (2020).[7]

The superior performance of 8th generation bonding agents can be attributed to their versatility and chemical composition. Universal adhesives are designed to function in both self-etch and etch-and-rinse modes, offering strong adhesion across various clinical scenarios. Additionally, their enhanced hydrophilicity allows for better penetration into the dentin, contributing to higher bond strengths.[89]

The higher SBS values of 8th generation bonding agents suggest they may provide more reliable and durable restorations in clinical practice, potentially leading tofewer instances of restoration failure and enhanced longevity of dental treatments.However, this study was conducted In-vitro, which doesn’t entirely replicate the complex intraoral environment where factors such as moisture control, patient variability, and mechanical stresses play significant roles. Future studies should explore long-term clinical outcomes to validate these findings.

CONCLUSION

In summary, 8th generation dentin bonding agents demonstrate superior shear bond strengths compared to their 6th and 7th generation counterparts. These findings highlight the importance of continued innovation in bonding technology and the potential benefits of adopting newer adhesive systems in dental practice.
