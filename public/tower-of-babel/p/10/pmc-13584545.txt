The association between parents’ marital status, problematic internet use, and negative emotions among Chinese adolescents: a cross-lagged panel network analysis
Authors: Tingting Fang, Jialun Song, Zhangjie Hu, Li Jiang, Feng Zhuo, Ying Yang, Linghua Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrior studies have established a link between problematic internet use (PIU) and negative emotional states, highlighting how familial factors are associated with this relationship. However, most existing studies have employed cross-sectional designs, with few utilizing cross-lagged panel network analysis to explore the dynamic symptom-level relationships among parental marital status, PIU, and negative emotions. This study aimed to investigate the dynamic network interactions among them in Chinese adolescents.MethodsThe sample comprised 10,394 adolescents who completed the Internet Addiction Test (IAT), the Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI), and the Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children (MASC) twice over a six-month period. A total of four symptom networks and two cross-lagged panel networks were developed.ResultsThe findings revealed that adolescents from divorced families exhibited significantly higher rates of PIU (PIU: T1 = 21.6%, T2 = 25.6%), depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms compared to their peers from non-divorced families (PIU: T1 = 15.3%, T2 = 16.5%). “Social anxiety” emerged as the key node of the networks across time. “Negative self-esteem” emerged as the most influential node in both the married and divorced family groups (married: OEI = 0.896; divorced: OEI = 0.888). It also functioned as a bridge symptom in the divorced group, whereas “anhedonia” served as the bridge symptom in the married group.ConclusionsThis study underscores that PIU and negative emotions differ across parental marital status groups. Key and bridge symptoms may serve as priority targets for preventing and addressing PIU and negative emotions among Chinese adolescents.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-27973-2.Keywords: Cross-lagged panel network analysis, Problematic internet use (PIU), Negative emotions, Adolescence, Parental marital status

Introduction

The Internet has evolved into an essential component of contemporary society. However, prolonged engagement with online environments may contribute to the development of Problematic Internet Use (PIU), a condition particularly prevalent among adolescent populations [1, 2]. PIU is defined as a dysfunctional behavioral pattern characterized by excessive or compulsive engagement with online activities, resulting in clinically meaningful impairment in psychosocial functioning [3]. Among the broader population affected by PIU, adolescents represent a distinctly vulnerable subgroup that demands targeted intervention and support. Approximately 26.5% of Chinese adolescents are estimated to exhibit symptoms of PIU [4]. According to the compensatory internet use theory, individuals often turn to online activities as a strategy to cope with distress and anxiety [5], indicating a potential bidirectional relationship between symptoms of depression/anxiety and PIU. Of particular significance, adolescence represents a critical developmental stage marked by substantial biological, social, cognitive, and emotional transformation [6]. Adolescents exhibit heightened sensitivity to social cues and rewards — including novel content encountered through digital screen engagement [7]— alongside an increased vulnerability to emotional and behavioral difficulties [6]. Consequently, adolescence represents a critical window for investigating PIU and its linkages to negative emotional states.

Drawing on Jessor and Jessor’s (1977) [8] problem behavior theory, family structure has been identified as a predictor of externalizing behavioral issues [9]. Prior research has indicated that approximately 7% of adolescents with PIU experience impaired and/or maladaptive family relationships [10]. Divorce, as a primary marker of marital discord that significantly disrupts family organization [11], is associated not only with psychological distress in partners but also with an elevated likelihood of addictive behaviors among their offspring [12, 13]. Consistent with the family stress and resilience framework, negative emotional dynamics between parents may transmit to their parenting practices and interactions with their children [14]. Consequently, these adolescents often turn to the Internet as an emotional outlet and a source of security, happiness, and fulfillment. They frequently prefer engaging in virtual social interactions and forming intimate relationships with online peers rather than in-person connections [15]. Exposure to such experiences can elevate the likelihood of exhibiting symptoms of PIU [1]. Furthermore, parental divorce or separation is associated with substantial adverse consequences for a child’s psychological well-being. Children and adolescents who experience parental divorce face a heightened vulnerability to various psychological difficulties, such as emotional and behavioral dysregulation, depressive and anxiety symptoms, as well as diminished academic achievement. Divorce represents a major stressful experience for all family members, with profound implications for children. It often disrupts their fundamental sense of stability and security, resulting in emotional distress and impaired adjustment to major life transitions [16]. Adolescents from divorced families exhibit a greater susceptibility to depression and anxiety, which correlates with elevated frequencies of suicidal behaviors and self-harm relative to their peers from intact biological families [17]. However, most existing studies are cross-sectional in design, which limits the examination of adolescent PIU and negative emotions across different parental marital statuses. Further longitudinal research is needed to clarify the influence of parental marital status on PIU and negative emotional outcomes.

To our knowledge, only one study has investigated the longitudinal network relationships between parental marital status and internet addiction, this study have focused exclusively on these two variables without considering the dynamic symptom interactions among parental marital status, PIU, and negative emotions [18]. To bridge this gap, the present investigation utilized a cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) model with large-scale longitudinal data to analyze the temporal dynamics of symptom networks involving parental marital status, PIU, and negative emotions among Chinese adolescents. According to the network theory of psychopathology [19], mental disorders — such as depression and anxiety — are conceptualized as complex, dynamic systems comprising symptom nodes and the causal interactions that form connections between them. Unlike traditional latent variable models that conceptualize symptoms as passive indicators of a hidden underlying disorder, the network perspective emphasizes observable causal relationships among symptoms themselves. The activation of a single symptom may propagate to other directly linked symptoms, potentially resulting in a cascading activation effect throughout the network. To model the temporal dynamics among individual symptoms as conceptualized within network theory, cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) and cross-lagged panel models (CLPMs) were combined to estimate symptom-level temporal effect networks [20]. Furthermore, central symptoms were determined by computing their expected influence (EI) values, thereby identifying nodes with the greatest overall connectivity and causal impact in the network. Identifying central symptoms facilitates the recognition of pivotal nodes in the network, providing novel perspectives for developing more targeted and efficient intervention strategies.

To sum up, the current study utilizes CLPN to examine the complex interplay between parental marital status, PIU, and negative emotions over time. Specifically, we aim to [1] explore the interconnections between the parental marital status (married and divorced), three PIU dimensions, negative emotions (anxiety/depression); [2] identify the most central and bridging symptoms within the contemporaneous and temporal network.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a cluster sampling approach. Participants were recruited from all 25 middle schools in Wuyuan County, Jiangxi Province, China. All eligible students within the participating schools were invited to participate. Inclusion criteria were: (a) age between 10 and 19 years; (b) ability to understand and communicate normally; and (c) no severe mental or psychological disorders (e.g., schizophrenia, major depressive disorder) that could affect questionnaire completion. Exclusion criteria were: (a) inability to understand the questionnaire content or complete the questionnaire independently; and (b) more than 10% incomplete data.

After data verification, the age range of participants was confirmed to be 10 to 19 years, which aligns with the World Health Organization’s definition of adolescents. Data were collected at two time points: the initial assessment occurred from June to July 2024, followed by a second assessment between January and February 2025, resulting in an approximate six-month interval between measurements. At T1, 19,856 questionnaires were distributed, and 19,249 valid questionnaires were returned (valid response rate: 96.93%; 51.8% male). At T2, 10,394 participants (54.0% of the T1 valid sample; 49.7% male) were successfully followed up. The primary reasons for attrition from T1 to T2 were school transfer, graduation, or absence on the survey day. The final analytic sample comprised 10,394 adolescents who provided complete data across both survey waves.

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the School of Nursing and Rehabilitation of Shandong University (approval number: 2024-R-157). All participants (students and their parents) received a comprehensive explanation of the study’s objectives and procedures. Written informed consent was secured from all participants prior to their completion of the questionnaires. For participants under the age of 16, written informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians.

Data collection protocol

The survey was administered using paper-and-pencil questionnaires in classroom settings. Two trained research assistants were present in each classroom to administer the survey. Before the survey, the research assistants read a standardized instruction script explaining the study objectives, questionnaire instructions, and the right to withdraw. Students completed the questionnaires independently and anonymously on site, and the research assistants collected the completed questionnaires immediately. Each questionnaire took approximately 20–30 min to complete.

To ensure accurate matching of longitudinal data, we temporarily used students’ unique school ID numbers as identifiers. The school ID was used only to generate an internal study ID (e.g., “00001”) for data matching and management. After data entry, the school ID was removed from the dataset, and all research data were stored using study IDs only. The cross-reference table linking school IDs to study IDs is encrypted and stored separately, accessible only to the principal investigator, and will be destroyed after the study is completed.

Data entry was performed independently by two researchers and cross-checked to ensure accuracy. Samples with more than 10% incomplete data were excluded; missing data for the remaining samples were handled using multiple imputation (as described in the Statistical Analysis section).

Measurement

Demographic information

During the initial phase of the survey, participants provided basic demographic details, such as gender, body mass index (BMI), age, and educational stage (middle school or high school), economy of family, place of residence, whether they were an only child, whether they have left-behind experience (Left-behind experience was assessed by asking participants: “Have you ever experienced a period of more than six consecutive months during which one or both of your parents were away working outside your hometown and you were left in the care of grandparents, other relatives, or guardians?” Participants who answered “yes” were classified as having left-behind experience, while those who answered “no” were classified as not having such experience), parenting styles, academic performance, and parental marital status. PIU symptoms were measured by Internet Addiction Test. Negative emotions were measured by Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) and the Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children (MASC).

Problematic internet use

Problematic Internet Use (PIU) symptoms was measured using Young’s (1996) Internet Addiction Test (IAT) [21]. The IAT consists of 20 items distributed across three domains: withdrawal and social problems, time management and performance, and reality substitution. Each item rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (“rarely”) to 5 (“always”), yielding a total score between 20 and 100. Elevated scores on the scale reflect more severe PIU. The Chinese adaptation of the IAT has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties in prior validation studies [22]. In this study, Cronbach α was 0.911 for T1, 0.914 for T2.

Negative emotions

Negative emotions were measured using the Children’s Depression Inventory (CDI) [23] and the Multidimensional Anxiety Scale for Children (MASC) [24]. The CDI consists of 27 items distributed across five domains: negative mood, interpersonal problems, anhedonia, negative self-esteem, and ineffectiveness. A total score of 19 or higher suggests the presence of depressive symptoms, with rising scores reflecting greater severity of symptomatology [25]. The CDI has demonstrated strong reliability and well-established construct validity across various demographic groups. In this study, Cronbach α was 0.891 for T1, 0.888 for T2. The MASC consists of 39 items distributed across four subscales: physical symptoms, harm avoidance, social anxiety, and separation anxiety [26]. Items are scored on a 4-point severity scale from 0 (“none”) to 3 (“severe”), with higher total scores indicating more pronounced anxiety severity. A total score of 65 or higher is considered clinically significant for anxiety-related symptomatology. In this study, Cronbach α was 0.934 for T1, 0.944 for T2.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive and preliminary analysis

Statistical analyses of demographic characteristics between married and divorced groups were performed using SPSS 25.0. Network analyses were performed in R Studio (version 4.3.1), including network estimation, comparative network analysis, and cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) modeling.

Contemporaneous networks analysis

The EBICglasso function within the qgraph package (v1.5) was employed to estimate the network structures of PIU and negative emotions at each assessment wave. Following the estimation of network models at each time point, node centrality indices and edge weights were evaluated. Centrality was assessed using the expected influence (EI) metric, which measures the strength and directness of a symptom node’s connections with all other nodes in the network. Edge weights represented regularized partial correlation coefficients, numerically quantifying the strength and direction of conditional associations between pairs of nodes in the network.

The precision, stability, and replicability of the network structures were evaluated employing the bootnet package (v1.1.0) through a bootstrap resampling method with 1500 iterations [27]. The centrality stability (CS) coefficient was utilized as a benchmark measure. According to established criteria, CS values ≥ 0.25 were considered indicative of acceptable stability, whereas values ≥ 0.5 reflected good stability [28]. The NetworkComparisonTest (NCT) package in R was employed to evaluate differences in network structure across time points using a permutation-based approach. This included evaluations of global strength and structural invariance across subgroups.

Cross-lagged panel networks analysis

The glmnet package [29] was utilized to perform regularized regression analyses, while the qgraph package was applied to visualize all cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) graphs. Within the CLPN framework, variable influences were expressed through autoregressive effects (within-time self-prediction) and cross-lagged effects (across-time prediction of other variables), both estimated via penalized maximum likelihood regression with LASSO regularization. Out-EI and In-EI were calculated via the centralityPlot function in the qgraph package to examine the directionality of nodal interactions [30]. Out-EI quantifies the extent to which a node at T1 predicts other nodes at T2, while In-EI reflects the degree to which a node at T2 is predicted by all other nodes at T1. It is important to note that autoregressive effects are intentionally omitted from these centrality indices. Furthermore, 1-step and 2-step bridge expected influence (BEI1 and BEI2) indices were computed to detect bridge symptoms that connect PIU and negative emotion communities within the network.

The accuracy and stability of the CLPNs were further evaluated through two bootstrapping methods implemented with the bootnet package in R. First, nonparametric bootstrapping (1000 iterations) was applied to compute 95% confidence intervals for edge weights, assessing the precision of network connections [27]. Second, the rank-order stability of centrality indices and edges was evaluated by deriving the correlation stability coefficient [27].

Results

Longitudinal measurement invariance testing

A comparative analysis of IAT, CDI, and MASC measurement invariance between married and divorced groups revealed strict measurement equivalence of the scales across the two groups (see Table S1), demonstrating comparability and stability. Furthermore, a comparative analysis of those scales’ measurement invariance between the two time points (T1 and T2) also indicated strict measurement equivalence of the scales across time.

Descriptive statistics

This study included a total of 10,394 participants, comprising 5,168 males (49.7%) and 5,226 females (50.3%). Among them, 8,770 (84.4%) were from first-marriage families, while 1,624 (15.6%) were from divorced or other non-intact family structures. The participants ranged in age from 10 to 19 years, with a mean age of 15.4 ± 1.4 years. Adolescents from divorced families exhibited significantly higher rates of PIU (PIU: T1 = 21.6%, T2 = 25.6%), depressive symptoms (CDI: T1 = 35.2%, T2 = 30.1%), and anxiety symptoms (MASC: T1 = 9.5%, T2 = 6.7%) compared to their peers from non-divorced families (PIU: T1 = 15.3%, T2 = 16.5%; CDI: T1 = 23.1%, T2 = 18.3%; MASC: T1 = 6.0%, T2 = 4.6%). Moreover, significant differences were observed between married and divorced subgroups in terms of age, grade level, family economic status, residence location, only-child status, left-behind experience, parental education style, and academic performance, as detailed in Table 1.

Temporal networks

Figure 4 presents the cross-lagged panel networks for the married and divorced groups, respectively. After excluding autoregressive edges, the married group’s network comprised 109 edges, while the divorced group’s network contained 95 edges. In the network structure of the divorced group, 87.1% of the edges exhibited consistent directionality with those in the married group. Conversely, within the married group’s network, 76.5% of the edges shared directional consistency with the divorced group. The two groups showed minimal differences in overall network closeness (Married: M = 0.054; Divorced: M = 0.053). A strong positive correlation was observed in edge weights between the groups (r = 0.853, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.798, 0.894]). However, nearly one-third of the edges (29.6%) differed in directionality, suggesting potential distinctions in symptom connectivity patterns between the two groups. For instance, the edge from “ineffectiveness” (CDI5) to “separation anxiety” (MASC4) showed opposite weights (Married: 0.004; Divorced: -0.004) (Table S7).

Discussion

Employing a cross-lagged panel network (CLPN) framework, this study examined the dynamic associations between PIU and negative emotions in Chinese adolescents, along with differences in these associations across parental marital status groups. Through longitudinal analysis, distinct temporal changes and central symptoms were identified within the network structures of adolescents from diverse family backgrounds. Furthermore, systematic group differences in network structures and temporal dynamics were observed between adolescents from married versus divorced families.

Preliminary analysis

In the initial phase of analysis, the prevalence of PIU and negative emotion symptoms among adolescents was evaluated at both time points, with particular attention to differences based on parental marital status. At both T1 and T2, the prevalence of PIU, depressive symptoms, and anxiety symptoms was higher in divorced families compared to their peers from non-divorced families, consistent with previous research [14, 16, 17]. Divorce signifies the dissolution of the family structure, often accompanied by dramatic changes in daily routines, economic conditions, and the living environment. According to the compensatory internet use theory, individuals may seek to fulfill psychological needs in the online world that are unmet in reality [5]. Adolescents from divorced families might use online social interactions, gaming achievements, or other digital activities to compensate for a lack of emotional support in their offline lives [15]. Furthermore, prolonged exposure to family stress may lead to increased levels of stress hormones (such as cortisol), which can impair the development of the prefrontal cortex—responsible for self-control—while simultaneously heightening sensitivity to immediate rewards (e.g., online content) [7]. This may create a vicious cycle of stress, impulsive use, and temporary relief.

Key symptoms in contemporaneous networks

“Social anxiety” emerged as a central symptom in the networks at both T1 and T2, consistently observed in both the married and divorced groups. Social anxiety is characterized by a core fear and avoidance of real-world social situations. In contrast, the online environment offers a low-threat alternative for socialization through anonymity, controllable self-presentation (e.g., editing messages), and the absence of face-to-face pressure. This tendency can lead individuals with social anxiety to become increasingly reliant on online interactions, potentially increasing their risk of excessive internet use. The syndrome model of addiction posits that psychosocial factors—including psychopathological conditions like social anxiety—are essential for explaining the development and maintenance of addictive behaviors [31]. Social anxiety is recognized as a substantial and potential risk factor for PIU [32]. Davis’s cognitive-behavioral model proposes that psychopathological factors, such as social anxiety, serve as distal necessary conditions for the development of PIU [33]. Individuals experiencing social anxiety or deficits in social skills tend to prefer online socialization and exhibit excessive use of social media as a compensatory strategy to fulfill unmet social needs [34]. Although online social engagement may temporarily alleviate anxiety, long-term dependence could weaken real-life social skills and exacerbate difficulties in offline social settings, possibly creating a vicious cycle: failed offline social interactions → heightened anxiety → increased reliance on the internet → further deterioration of real-world social competence. Regardless of family background, the internet provides universally accessible escape routes and reward mechanisms (e.g., likes, gaming achievements). Individuals with social anxiety—whether from divorced or married families—tend to seek compensatory fulfillment through online activities.

Parental marital status differences in symptom networks

The study found that the edge from “ineffectiveness” to “separation anxiety” showed opposite directional tendencies for adolescents from married and divorced families. However, it should be noted that the edge weights for this pathway were very small in both groups (see Results section), and therefore this finding should be interpreted with caution. In married families, ineffectiveness showed a weak positive tendency with separation anxiety. One possible interpretation is that, with reference to family dynamics rather than family structure per se, married families may, on average, provide more stable emotional environments and consistent caregiver availability, which can foster a pattern of relying on family as a secure base [35]. However, given the very small effect size, this interpretation remains speculative and requires replication. Nevertheless, given the very small effect size, this interpretation remains highly speculative and requires replication. However, it is important to note that family structure (i.e., being married or divorced) is not synonymous with family dynamics (i.e., emotional warmth, conflict frequency, or parental responsiveness). Within married families, considerable variability in dynamics exists, and within divorced families, some may still maintain supportive co-parenting relationships. Thus, our interpretation focuses on the typical dynamic patterns associated with each family structure, rather than assuming that structure directly determines dynamics.

In contrast, the weak negative tendency observed in divorced families might stem from altered cognitive perceptions of family relationships post-divorce, although this interpretation also requires caution given the negligible edge weights. Adolescents’ feelings of ineffectiveness could originate from other practical stressors (e.g., economic changes or family role reconfiguration), which might reduce specific separation anxiety. Alternatively, they could develop more independent coping mechanisms that weaken this emotional link. Given the very small magnitudes involved, these interpretations should be considered preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive.

The strongest cross-lagged effect in the married group was from “withdrawal and social problems” to “reality substitution” (IAT1-IAT3), whereas in the divorced group, the strongest effect was observed from “social anxiety” to “negative self-esteem” (MASC3-CDI4). Additionally, the strongest contemporaneous association observed in the symptom networks of both groups was between IAT1-IAT3. This finding is consistent with the lens of Compensatory Internet Use Theory [5]. This theory posits that individuals tend to engage in excessive internet use to escape or alleviate distress and dissatisfaction in offline life (e.g., social difficulties, negative emotions). Moreover, a relatively stable family environment in married households may lead adolescents to develop PIU more due to what might be characterized as habitual avoidance rather than acute stress. Divorce is often accompanied by conflict, absence, or economic pressure, which might make adolescents more likely to internalize problems (e.g., “My parents divorced because I wasn’t good enough”), potentially exacerbating self-negation. These interpretations, while plausible, remain inferential and should be tested in future research.

“Negative self-esteem” played a central role in networks of both married and divorced family groups. The positive correlation between family environment and self-worth is well-documented among adolescents across diverse cultures [36]. A positive family environment fosters adolescents’ self-esteem, thereby reducing their need for online compensation and mitigating the risk of PIU. Adolescents from unhealthy family environments, particularly those from divorced families, may experience negative emotions such as anxiety and depression [37]. The compensatory internet use theory posits that adolescents may engage in online activities as a maladaptive strategy to mitigate such distressing emotions [5]. Chronic low self-esteem impairs emotional regulation, thereby increasing susceptibility to negative affective states [38–40]. In network analysis, low self-esteem is considered “core” because it efficiently connects the two clusters of PIU and negative emotions. When individuals experience emotions such as depression or anxiety, low self-esteem prompts them to adopt maladaptive coping strategies. Thus, negative emotions can easily lead to PIU—an escape behavior—via the bridge of “negative self-esteem.” PIU behavior itself reinforces low self-esteem. Excessive internet use encroaches on time for real-life activities, leading to declines in academic performance and deterioration of social skills.

“Negative self-esteem” also acted as a pivotal bridge symptom in the divorced group, while “anhedonia” was the key bridge symptom in the married group. For adolescents from divorced families, the breakdown of the family structure may be interpreted as constituting a direct attack on their self-worth. One plausible explanation is that, to alleviate the pain caused by this perceived lack of value, they turn to the internet, primarily seeking recognition, acceptance, and a sense of worth [37]. Thus, low self-esteem may serve as the bridge connecting internal distress (negative emotions) and external compensatory behavior (PIU). For adolescents from intact but emotionally strained married families, their suffering could stem more from daily, persistent emotional exhaustion and deprivation of pleasure [41]. Consequently, anhedonia might become the bridge connecting emotional numbness (negative emotions) and sensory-seeking behavior (PIU). These interpretations, while consistent with our findings, remain inferential and require replication.

Strengths, limitations, and future research

This investigation has several notable merits. Our work contributes to the theoretical foundation of the PIU model [42] and highlights the importance of identifying central and bridge symptoms for addressing addiction-related disorders. Extending this model helps clarify how PIU and negative emotions differ by parental marital status. These results provide valuable insights for developing tailored prevention and intervention strategies for PIU and negative emotions.

Regarding the interpretation of effect sizes in this study, it should be noted that edge weights in network analysis are interpreted as partial correlation effect sizes. Although the large sample size may render even very small weights statistically significant, we have focused on reporting stronger connections (e.g., weights > 0.15) in the Results and have emphasized directional consistency rather than relying solely on p-values. For edges with very small weights (e.g., < 0.01), their practical significance lies primarily in between-group differences in directionality rather than the magnitude of the effect size itself. Despite these advantages, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, this study relies on self-reported measures for both PIU and negative emotions, which are susceptible to subjective biases such as social desirability or underreporting of PIU or negative emotions. Future research could integrate objective data (e.g., screen time tracking, parent/teacher reports) to triangulate findings and mitigate this concern. Secondly, this study employed a cluster sampling design limited to adolescents from Wuyuan County, Jiangxi Province. Although the sample is large, the geographically constrained nature of the sample may limit the generalizability of our findings to the broader adolescent population in other regions of China. Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating our findings to the wider adolescent population. Future studies should employ more diverse, nationally representative cohorts to bolster the external validity and robustness of the results. Thirdly, parental marital status was dichotomized into divorced versus intact groups. Although this binary classification facilitated network estimation and group comparisons, it does not capture the heterogeneity within intact families. For example, high-conflict intact families may confer greater psychological risk than low-conflict single-parent families. Future studies should incorporate more refined indicators of family functioning (e.g., marital conflict frequency, parent-child relationship quality, family cohesion) to more comprehensively understand the impact of family factors on adolescent PIU and negative emotions. Fourth, it is important to clarify that family structure (i.e., married vs. divorced) is conceptually distinct from family dynamics (i.e., emotional warmth, conflict frequency, parental responsiveness). Our interpretations in Section "Parental marital status differences in symptom networks" focus on the typical dynamic patterns associated with each family structure, rather than assuming that structure directly determines dynamics. Future research should simultaneously measure both family structure and family dynamics to disentangle their independent and interactive effects.

Intervention implications targeting core symptoms and family structure

Our findings identify two core psychological targets: social anxiety and low self-esteem. Their pathways to problematic internet use (PIU) and negative emotions differ systematically by family structure, necessitating tailored intervention approaches.

Social anxiety

Social anxiety is a central symptom in both family groups, driving compensatory internet use. Universal interventions should include cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) targeting social fears, gradual real-world exposure, and social skills training.

However, contextual adaptations are required based on family structure:

In divorced families, family instability, parental absence, or interparental conflict may compound social anxiety and heighten perceived social threat. Therefore, interventions should additionally incorporate emotional support from non-parental figures (e.g., teachers, mentors) and trauma-informed approaches.

Low self-esteem

Low self-esteem connects the PIU and negative emotion clusters, offering a potential “lever effect” whereby targeting self-esteem may simultaneously alleviate both conditions. General strategies include CBT for negative self-beliefs, self-compassion training, and strength-based approaches.

Pathway-based adaptations are informed by distinct cross-lagged patterns:

In married families, PIU primarily stems from habitual avoidance of social difficulties (withdrawal and social problems → reality substitution), with anhedonia as the key bridge symptom. Recommended interventions include behavioral habit correction (e.g., establishing reasonable internet use rules), real-life social skills training to reduce withdrawal, and behavioral activation to counteract anhedonia by increasing rewarding offline experiences.

Recommended interventions include behavioral habit correction (e.g., establishing reasonable internet use rules), real-life social skills training to reduce withdrawal, and behavioral activation to counteract anhedonia by increasing rewarding offline experiences.

In divorced families, PIU stems from self-worth threat (social anxiety → negative self-esteem), with low self-esteem serving as the key bridge symptom connecting internal distress and external compensatory behavior. Recommended interventions include self-esteem rebuilding (e.g., cognitive restructuring to challenge negative self-beliefs, narrative therapy to reframe family breakup), emotion regulation training, exposure therapy for social anxiety (particularly in contexts that trigger rejection fears), and strengthening non-family support systems (e.g., peer support groups, school-based mentoring).

Summary

Social anxiety and low self-esteem are universal intervention targets, but their pathways to PIU and negative emotions are shaped by family structure. Tailoring interventions to these distinct psychological pathways enhances both the precision and practical value of prevention and treatment efforts.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this research provides the first preliminary examination of the network structure and dynamics of PIU and negative emotions and its dynamics among Chinese adolescents from both married and divorced households. The results indicated substantial group differences: adolescents from divorced families reported significantly higher levels of both PIU and negative emotions than those from intact families, with “social anxiety” identified as the most central symptom in the network. The most central node in both groups was “negative self-esteem,” which also served as a bridge symptom in the divorced group. This bridging function was assumed by “anhedonia” in the married group. These central and bridge symptoms offer valuable insights for designing targeted interventions and therapeutic approaches to address adolescent PIU and comorbid negative emotions during this critical developmental period.
