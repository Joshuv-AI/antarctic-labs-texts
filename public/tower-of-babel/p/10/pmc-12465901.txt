Neuregulin 4 inhibits synovial macrophage pro-inflammatory polarization via ErbB4/Stat5b/NF-κB signaling to alleviate osteoarthritis progression
Authors: Daoliang Jiang, Xiaoli Xu, Wenjing Yang, Mengjia Tang, Yuping Zhang, Lingfeng Shi, Jiongyu Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by synovial inflammation and cartilage degradation. Synovial macrophages, as a key driver of OA progression, during all stages of OA and induced to produce inflammatory cytokines that aggravate the degradation of cartilage. Here, we demonstrate that neuregulin-4 (Nrg4) deficiency exacerbates synovial pro-inflammatory macrophage transformation and articular cartilage abrasion in a mouse model of OA. Conversely, Nrg4 re-expression significantly reduces pro-inflammatory macrophage accumulation and synovial inflammation, thereby attenuating OA progression. Mechanistically, Nrg4 activates the ErbB4/Stat5b signaling pathway, which subsequently suppresses NF-κB activation, leading to reduced pro-inflammatory macrophage transformation and pro-inflammatory cytokine production. In vitro experiments further confirm that Nrg4 directly inhibits macrophage infiltration. These findings reveal a novel role for Nrg4 in modulating synovial macrophage transformation through the ErbB4/Stat5b/NF-κB axis, offering a potential therapeutic strategy for OA by targeting synovial inflammation. This study also expands our understanding of OA protection beyond chondrocyte protection, emphasizing its role in regulating the synovial microenvironment and cartilage homeostasis in OA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06259-0.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a worldwide disease with an estimated prevalence of 3.8% overall, and more than 10% in the elderly population above 60 years [1]. It is characterized by joint deterioration, causing several symptoms including joint pain, stiffness, deformity, and dysfunction, which eventually harm the quality of life and increase socioeconomic burden [2]. Historically, OA had been recognized as a “wear and tear” disease. Risk factors such as weight overload, anatomical alterations, genetic susceptibility, as well as age-related reduction of regenerative capacity, result in cartilage loss and hinder the joint’s ability to repair itself [3]. Due to the limited understanding in the past and the inevitability of these risk factors, treatments for OA have been limited in scope and efficacy, often relying on symptomatic relief rather than addressing the underlying mechanisms of disease progression.

However, research over the past decades has elucidated that OA can be recognized as a chronic inflammatory disease. Tissues, especially the synovium, which is constructed of the cellular lining of the joint, are turned into synovitis during all stages of OA and induced to produce inflammatory cytokines that aggravate the degradation of cartilage [4]. This process is highly related to joint pain and serves as a marker of OA severity [5]. Macrophages, as the predominant cells discovered in the synovium, are responsible for perpetuating the vicious cycle by inducing an inflammatory cascade through the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, which in turn recruit more immune cells and exacerbate the inflammatory response. This chronic inflammation can lead to further cartilage degradation and joint damage, contributing to the progressive nature of OA [6].

Recent evidence has identified that synovial macrophages consist of different macrophage subsets, with overlapping pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory signals (previously categorized as M1 and M2 macrophages polarization, respectively) coexisting within the same microenvironment [7]. Increased synovial pro-inflammatory macrophages subsets exacerbate OA through multiple mechanisms, especially pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, thereby driving the progressive nature of the disease [8, 9], Therefore, targeting the inhibition of pro-inflammatory macrophage transformation in the synovium could represent an important therapeutic strategy for the management of OA.

Recent research has found that the activation of ErbB4 signaling can induce pro-inflammatory macrophage apoptosis [10]. Furthermore, the knockout of ErbB4 in macrophages resulted in elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine production in vitro [11]. These findings strongly suggest that ErbB4 signaling is involved in the down-regulation of macrophage pro-inflammatory signals. Interestingly, neuregulin-4 (Nrg4), as a specific ligand for ErbB4, has been shown to act as an anti-inflammatory cytokine, repressing the inflammatory response and alleviating apoptosis in various cells, thereby ameliorating inflammatory damage in chronic inflammatory diseases such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease or inflammatory bowel disease [12, 13]. Given that inflammatory cytokines induced by synovitis are highly related to cartilage degradation and that macrophages are predominant inflammatory cells in the synovium, targeting ErbB4-Nrg4 signaling in synovial macrophages might offer a deeper understanding and a promising therapeutic strategy to alleviate OA.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male mice, aged eight weeks and weighing between 24 and 26 g, including Nrg4 knockout (KO) on a C57BL/6J background and wild-type (WT) C57BL/6J, were sourced from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. (Shanghai, China). The mice were kept in a pathogen-free setting with a 12-hour light-dark cycle, maintained at a temperature of 23 ± 1 °C, and had unlimited access to food and water. Throughout the experiment, all the mice were given a standard chow diet from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All animals were cared for following protocols sanctioned by the Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Third Military Medical University (Army Medical University) (Chongqing, China). The animal care and use protocols adhered to the National Institutes of Health’s Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Surgical Induction of Osteoarthritis

Twelve-week-old mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of 2% (w/v) pentobarbital at a dose of 40 mg/kg. To induce osteoarthritis (OA), the surgical destabilization of the medial meniscus (DMM) combined with partial medial meniscectomy was performed. Specifically, in the right knee joint of each mouse, the tibial ligament attached to the medial meniscus was exposed through an incision in the medial articular capsule and subsequently transected using a microscalpel. Additionally, a partial medial meniscectomy was performed to exacerbate joint instability and induce more severe OA and synovitis. For comparison, the left knee joints of the mice underwent arthrotomy without transection of the medial meniscus ligament, constituting the sham group. Post-surgery, all mice were allowed to move freely.

Adeno-associated virus infection

The AAV serotype 9 vectors encoding Nrg4 (GenBank accession number NM_032002.2) and Zsgreen were obtained from Genechem (Shanghai, China). To investigate the impact of Nrg4 deficiency on the progression of OA, we selected both KO and WT mice (24–28 g) and divided them into four groups: KO-sham, WT-sham, KO-OA, and WT-OA (n = 5 per group). To further explore the influence of Nrg4 restoration on OA progression, we selected additional KO mice (24–28 g) and divided them into two groups: KO(AAV-Nrg4) and KO(AAV-Zsgreen) (n = 5 per group). Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane in oxygen, A 26G needle was inserted into the knee joint cavity under sterile conditions. A single intra-articular injection of 1 × 10^12 vg of AAV-Nrg4 or AAV-Zsgreen in a volume of 50 µL was administered in 10-week-old mice. All mice were maintained on a standard diet for 8 weeks before sacrifice.

Histological analysis

Mice knee joints were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and then decalcified in a 10% EDTA solution for 4 weeks. The specimens were subsequently dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. Serial 5-µm thick frontal sections with approximately 80-µm intervals were collected from the entire joint, within the defined anterior and posterior anatomical landmarks (appearance of significant synovial tissue/flattened femur and flattened tibial plateau, respectively) [14]. Six slides per joint, each containing one systematically selected representative section spanning the central weight-bearing regions and synovial gutters of the joint, were stained with Safranin O-fast green (S–O staining) and Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE staining). The Osteoarthritis Research Society International (OARSI) score and synovitis scoring (Table S1) were used for analysis of OA progression and the inflammatory status of the synovium, as previously described [14, 15]. All scoring was performed manually by two independent, trained scorers who were blinded to the groups. They graded the scores independently and without discussion. A third scorer would reassess the score if there was a discrepancy between the two initial scorers.

Immunofluorescence staining

Mice knee tissue histological slides underwent deparaffinization in xylene and gradual rehydration through ethanol concentrations ranging from 100 to 50%. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, antigen retrieval was performed by heating slides in Tris-EDTA buffer at 98 °C for 20 min using a microwave, and cooling at room temperature. Slides were then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 1 h before being incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted 1:200 for CD206 (#18704-1-AP), CD80 (#66406-1-Ig), F4/80 (#28463-1-AP), iNOS (#18985-1-AP) and Arginase-1(#16001-1-AP). For co-immunostaining, slides were co-incubated with two primary antibodies from different species. Post-incubation, slides were washed with PBS and subsequently incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (FITC) for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were stained using 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole, and fluorescence microscopy (Olympus, Japan) was used to capture images from randomly selected fields. For cytological immunofluorescence, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 min. Slides were blocked with 5% BSA and then incubated with a primary antibody including CD206 (#18704-1-AP), CD16/32 (#65057-1-I) and DAPI (#C1002). The rest of the staining protocol followed the same steps as the histological immunofluorescence staining procedure.

Immunohistochemical staining

After paraffin dehydration and clearing, antigen retrieval was carried out via heat-induced epitope retrieval in Tris-EDTA buffer at 98 °C for 20 min, with subsequent cooling at room temperature. Primary antibodies for F4/80 (#28463-1-AP, 1:2000 dilution), CD206 (#18704-1-AP, 1:200 dilution) and CD80 (#66406-1-Ig, 1:200 dilution) were used, followed by incubation with a HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (# ab6721, 1:1000 dilution), followed by DAB chromogenic detection. Nonimmune IgG was used instead of the primary antibody as a negative control.

Cell culture

RAW264.7 cells, obtained from the Cell Bank of Type Culture Collection of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco). Cells were cultured at 37 °C under 5% CO2. For pro-inflammatory macrophage induction, cells were co-treated with 100 ng/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS, #L2630, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and 150 ng/mL recombinant neuregulin 4 protein (rNrg4, 150 ng/mL, MCE) for 24 h. For signaling analysis, cells were pretreated with LPS (100 ng/mL) for 1 h followed by rNrg4 (150 ng/mL) for 24 h. For anti-inflammatory macrophage induction, cells were co-treated with 20 ng/mL IL-4 and 150 ng/mL rNrg4 (or vehicle) for 48 h. For Transwell migration assays, RAW264.7 cells were seeded in the upper compartment of the chambers and rNrg4 for 48 h or IL-1β for 12 h was added in the lower chambers, migrated cells were fixed, stained, and counted.

Flow cytometry for macrophage polarization analysis

To study the shifts in macrophage pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory transformation after rNrg4 treatment, flow cytometry was performed on > 99% F4/80+ RAW264.7 macrophages. Single cells were first gated by FSC/SSC scatter and pulse-width to exclude debris and aggregates. CD16/32 was selected to mark the pro-inflammatory phenotype and CD206 to mark the anti-inflammatory phenotype. PE-conjugated anti-CD206 (Biolegend, #141705) and FITC-conjugated anti-CD16/32 (Biolegend, #156613) were used to assess the macrophage subsets. M1 and M2 subsets were directly defined as CD16/32+CD206− and CD16/32−CD206+ populations respectively. PE rat isotype control and FITC rat isotype control (Biolegend) were employed to set positivity thresholds. Samples were further analyzed using a FACScan flow cytometer (Beckman).

RNA isolation and RealTime PCR

RNA was isolated from mouse synovium or cultured cells using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, CA, USA) following the previously described protocol [16], and RNA quantification was performed according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. The following primers were utilized for RT-PCR analysis: TNF-α forward (F) 5′- AGCCCCCAGTCTGTATCCTT-3′, reverse (R) 5′-CTC CCT TTG CAG AAC TCA GG-3′; IL-1β F: 5′-CCG TGG ACC TTC CAG GAT GA-3′, R: 5′-GGG AAC GTC ACA CAC CAG CA-3′; IL-6 forward F: 5′-ACT CCA GAA GAC CAG AGG AAAT-3′, R: 5′-CCA GAG ATA CAA AGA AAT GATGG-3′; GAPDH F: 5′-TCA ACA GCA ACT CCC ACT CTT CCA − 3′, R: 5′-TTG TCA TTG AGA GCA ATG CCA GCC − 3′. GAPDH served as the internal control for normalization.

Western blotting and ELISAs

Western blot was performed as previous described [16]. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies against phosphorylated (p)-STAT5b (1:500, Abcam, #ab52211), STAT (1:1000, Abcam, #ab178941), P-P65 (1:1000, Abcam, #ab76302), P65 (1:1000, Abcam, #ab32536), P-IκBα (1:1,000, CST, #2859), IκBα (1:1,000, CST, #9242), Arg1(1:1000, Abcam, #ab183333), CD206(1:1,000, CST, #24595), CD86(1:1,000, ThermoFisher, #PA5-114521), iNOS(1:1000, Abcam, #ab15323), Nrg4(1:1000, Abcam, #ab239580). Following this, the membranes were incubated with a secondary antibody (1:2000, Beyotime) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using the ECL method and their overall gray intensity was quantified with Image Lab software (Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH or β-tubulin was used as an internal loading control. The secretion of IL-1β, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) in the supernatant of cultured cells was analyzed using ELISA kits (R&D Systems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ELISA kits (R&D Systems, MN, USA) were used to analyze the secretion of IL-1β, interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) both in the supernatant of cultured cells according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Statistical information

All data are expressed as mean ± SD. Comparisons between groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by least significant difference test. To assess the main effects of genotype (WT vs. KO) and treatment (with/without AAV delivery), two-way ANOVA was performed. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 8.0.1) and SPSS (version 22.0).

Results

Nrg4 deficiency increased synovial pro-inflammatory macrophages and articular cartilage abrasion in vivo

HE staining of mice knee joint sections showed that DMM induces the erosion of cartilage (OA change) in both WT and KO mice (Fig. 1A). More clearly, Safranin O-fast green staining showed that KO mice exhibited increased articular cartilage abrasion and synovial lining cell thickness according to OARSI scores (Fig. 1B-D), indicating that the deficiency of Nrg4 can aggravate OA damage. Previous studies have emphasized that an increase in pro-inflammatory macrophages in the synovial membrane leads to the progression of OA [17]. Consistent with this, we observed a significantly higher accumulation of macrophages in the synovial membrane of KO mice compared to WT mice in the OA model (Fig. 2A-B). Specifically, the expression of the pro-inflammatory macrophage marker CD80 (M1 macrophage marker) was markedly elevated in the synovial membrane of KO mice relative to their WT littermates, while no significant difference was noted for the anti-inflammatory macrophage marker CD206 (M2 macrophage marker) (Fig. 2D-G). This indicates that Nrg4 deficiency may promote macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory subtype, resulting in a pro-inflammatory environment in the synovial membrane and the development of OA.

Nrg4 re-expression decrease synovial pro-inflammatory macrophages and alleviated OA in vivo

We next investigated whether Nrg4 has protective effects on the progression of OA by re-expressing it through intra-articular injections of AAV expressing Nrg4 (AAV-Nrg4). First, we confirmed the successful re-expression of Nrg4 in the synovial membrane and blood six weeks post-transfection with AAV in KO mice (Fig. 3A-B). Subsequently, we examined the osteoarthritic knee joints in OA mice and found that treatment with AAV-Nrg4 alleviated articular cartilage degradation and synovial inflammation in KO mice (Fig. 3C-D). Consistent with these findings, the expression of the pro-inflammatory macrophage marker iNOS was markedly decreased, while the expression of the anti-inflammatory macrophage marker Arg1 tended to increase, although this increase did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.071), in the synovial membrane of these mice (Fig. 3E-H). Additionally, the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in the synovial tissue were reduced (Fig. 4A), suggesting that Nrg4 re-expression could inhibit the transformation of macrophages into pro-inflammatory subtypes in mice synovial tissue, thereby reducing the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines and consequently alleviating OA destruction.

Nrg4 directly reduced macrophage infiltration and pro-inflammatory transformation in vitro

Our in vivo experiments observed a protective effect of Nrg4 on OA by inhibiting the transformation of macrophages into the pro-inflammatory subtype, thereby suppressing the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Subsequently, we performed in vitro experiments to determine whether the effect of Nrg4 on macrophages is direct. Firstly, according to the results from our MTT assays (Fig. 4B), 150 ng/mL rNrg4 for 24 h (M1) or 48 h (M2) was used as the condition for treating macrophages with Nrg4. Next, both the flow cytometry and immunofluorescence results confirmed that under 24 h LPS co-treatment, Nrg4 decreased the ratio of pro-inflammatory/anti-inflammatory (M1/M2) macrophages marker (Fig. 4C-J). Consistent with this finding, the mRNA levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in macrophages and their concentrations in the medium were reduced in the group treated with Nrg4 (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, the infiltration of macrophages into the synovial membrane plays a vital role in synovial inflammation. Our Transwell chamber infiltration experiments demonstrated that pre-treatment with Nrg4 significantly decreased the number of infiltrating macrophages in the lower chamber (Fig. 5B-C). Together, these results suggest that Nrg4 has a direct effect on inhibiting pro-inflammatory macrophages transformation and infiltration into synovial cells.

ErbB4/Stat5b/NF-κB signaling involved in the regulation of Nrg4 on pro-inflammatory transformation of macrophage

Pro-inflammatory macrophages play a pivotal role in the progression of OA. Nrg4 has a direct effect on decreasing pro-inflammatory macrophages and further repressing the expression of inflammatory cytokines. It is worth investigating the possible mechanisms involved. A previous study isolated ErbB4 knockout macrophages from ErbB4myeko mice and performed RNA sequencing analysis with macrophages from ErbB4FF littermates (GSE168070) [11]. ErbB4 is the main receptor of Nrg4. Therefore, we explored the data and found that cytokine signaling in the immune system was the most significantly enriched pathway from GO analysis, and Stat5b was markedly downregulated in the ErbB4 knockout macrophages (Fig. 5D-E). Furthermore, as a classic inflammatory signaling pathway, NF-κB signaling has been proven to stimulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, and can be downregulated by the activation of Stat5b signaling. Therefore, we verified the Stat5b/NF-κB signaling in the macrophages. As expected, Western blot experiments showed an obviously elevated level of phosphorylated p65 (P-p65) and phosphorylated IκBα (P-IκBα) under the activation of pro-inflammatory macrophages by LPS (Fig. 5F-G). This was also demonstrated by the high expression of pro-inflammatory subtype macrophage markers iNOS and CD16/32 (Fig. 5F-G). On the other hand, the expression of phosphorylated Stat5b (P-Stat5b) only slightly changed, suggesting that P-IκBα and P-p65 signaling could not directly upstream regulate Stat5b signaling (Fig. 5F-G). Next, Nrg4 treatment showed to active the P-stat5b and restrain the P-p65 and P- IκBα under the treatment of LPS, and down-regulation the pro-inflammatory macrophage markers iNOS and CD16/32 (Fig. 5F-G). These results demonstrated that Nrg4 might reduce pro-inflammatory macrophages by activating the P-Stat5b pathway, which further decreases the expression of P-p65 and P-IκBα. To verify the upstream signaling role of Stat5b, we next used pimozide (a Stat5b phosphorylation inhibitor [18]) to inhibit its phosphorylation. As a result, the suppression effect of Nrg4 on P-p65 and P-IκBα was significantly attenuated, and the markers of pro-inflammatory macrophages (iNOS and CD16/32) as well as the expression of inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) were increased (Fig. 5F-H). Overall, we found that the ErbB4/Stat5b/NF-κB signaling pathway is involved in the regulation of macrophages by Nrg4, which inhibits NF-κB signaling by activating Stat5b, thereby reducing the pro-inflammatory macrophage subtype and decreasing inflammatory cytokine production.

Discuss

This study investigated the effect of Nrg4 on attenuating synovial inflammation, thereby alleviating the progression of OA. The synovium, a specialized connective tissue that lines the joint capsule, produces synovial fluid to lubricate and nourish the joint. Typically, it consists of two distinct layers: an outer layer rich in type I collagen, microvasculature, and nerve fibers but relatively acellular, and an inner layer composed of 1–4 cell layers primarily consisting of macrophages and fibroblasts [19]. Modern imaging studies of OA have demonstrated significant infiltration of mononuclear cells and production of inflammatory cytokines in the synovium even before visible cartilage degeneration occurs [20]. In the early stages of knee OA, debris from damaged or degraded cartilage and subchondral bone is likely to trigger synovial inflammation. This leads to the activation of immune cells, primarily macrophages, which produce pro-inflammatory mediators. These mediators attract more immune cells and induce a phenotypic shift in chondrocytes, resulting in the production of additional pro-inflammatory cytokines and proteolytic enzymes. Consequently, this vicious cycle increases cartilage degradation and exacerbates synovial inflammation [21]. Critically, synovial inflammation in OA is not merely a secondary response but a key driver of disease progression. Chronic low-grade inflammation perpetuates a vicious cycle by stimulating the production of matrix-degrading enzymes and pro-inflammatory mediators, which collectively accelerate cartilage destruction and subchondral bone remodeling [22]. Additionally, Pain is typically the most debilitating symptom for OA patients in clinical. There is strong evidence indicating that synovial inflammation is closely linked to OA-induced pain, possibly due to the heightened sensitivity of peripheral nociceptors by pro-inflammatory cytokines, leading increased pain experience [20, 23]. Therefore, alleviating synovial inflammation represents an important target for therapeutic intervention.

STAT5 proteins exist as antiparallel dimers in the cytoplasm when inactive. Upon activation, they form homodimers, heterodimers, or tetramers and translocate to the nucleus to induce the transcription of specific genes, thereby regulating the proliferation, differentiation, and production of inflammatory cytokines in inflammatory cells [24]. STAT5 is activated in response to various molecules, including the canonical intermediate signaling molecule JAK, which can be activated by the activation of Nrg4 receptor ErbB4. The STAT5 subfamily comprises two highly homologous isoforms STAT5a and STAT5b, which seem to have distinct regulatory functions in immune cells. As a recent study showed that anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages (Arg1+/CD68+ cells) display a positive correlation with STAT5b and a negative correlation with STAT5a in atherosclerotic plaques, as revealed by integrating single-nucleus ATAC-Seq data with the expression profiles of STAT5a and STAT5b from single-cell RNA-Seq datasets [25]. Our results demonstrated that Nrg4 repress synovial macrophage M1 markers and pro-inflammatory cytokines secreting, as well as at least partly promote M2 markers in vitro experiments through the activation of STAT5b signal, which is in line with the observed positive correlation between STAT5b and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages in atherosclerotic plaques, suggesting that Nrg4 may also play a significant role in modulating an anti-inflammatory phenotype of macrophage beyond its direct anti-inflammatory effects by activating the STAT5b signal .

Nrg4 is a novel adipocytokine predominantly expressed in brown adipose tissue. As a member of the EGF-like family of extracellular ligands, it exerts its biological functions by activating the ErbB4 receptor tyrosine kinase on the cell membrane of target cells. Initially identified for its role in regulating thermogenic and metabolic functions, recent studies have further revealed its protective effects against inflammation-induced damage and its role in promoting cell survival under metabolic disturbances. Previous research demonstrated that Nrg4 can attenuate inflammation-related apoptosis in chondrocytes [16]. However, Chondrocytes are not typically considered inflammatory cells. Synovium tissue, on the contrary, can recruit a large number of inflammatory cells, mainly macrophages and T lymphocytes, from the peripheral blood, and produce synovial fluid that shapes the microenvironment for chondrocytes. We found that Nrg4 reduces macrophage infiltration into synovial tissue and controls synovial inflammation. Not only does Nrg4 complement the reduction of inflammation-related apoptosis in chondrocytes, but it also directly targets synovial tissue by attenuating macrophage infiltration and controlling synovial inflammation. This dual action suggests that Nrg4 could be a potent therapeutic agent for OA, addressing both the inflammatory and degenerative aspects of the disease. By modulating the synovial environment, Nrg4 may promote a healthier joint microclimate, potentially slowing down or even reversing the progression of OA.

One major limitation of this study is its focus on investigating the changes in the synovium during OA. However, OA is a multifaceted disease involving multiple structural changes, including the synovium, cartilage, and subchondral bone. While synovial inflammation could be an important trigger and progression risk factor, it is not the sole contributor to OA pathogenesis. Therefore, studying the entire joint structure and the integrated effects of interventions on OA is complex yet worth for further exploration. Another limitation is that we did not examine pain-related outcomes. Recent evidence has shown a close association between synovial inflammation and OA-induced pain, suggesting that the pain modulation effect of Nrg4 in OA mice deserves further investigation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study investigates the anti-inflammatory effects of Nrg4 on synovial inflammation and its role in mitigating the progression of OA (Fig. 6). Through comprehensive in vivo and in vitro experiments, we have demonstrated that Nrg4 deficiency exacerbates the accumulation of the pro-inflammatory macrophage subtype and accelerates articular cartilage degradation in the synovium. Conversely, re-expression of Nrg4 significantly inhibits pro-inflammatory macrophage transformation and synovial inflammation, thereby reducing OA damage. Mechanistically, our findings reveal that Nrg4 exerts its anti-inflammatory effects by activating the Stat5b signaling pathway, which subsequently downregulates the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to reduced macrophage pro-inflammatory transformation and cytokine production. These results suggest that Nrg4 holds potential as a therapeutic agent for OA, addressing both the inflammatory and degenerative aspects of the disease by modulating the synovial microenvironment and promoting joint health. Overall, this study provides robust experimental and theoretical support for the potential therapeutic use of Nrg4 in OA, underscoring the need for further exploration of its effects and mechanisms in diverse OA models.
