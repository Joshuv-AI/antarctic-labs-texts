Determining Applications of Intermediate Wheatgrass in Conjunction With Common Wheat: Assessing Affects on Functionality and Digestibility
Authors: Mackenzie J Ferguson, Douglas Cattani, Bin Xiao Fu, Kun Wang, Maneka Malalgoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTFortifying traditional wheat‐based products can diversify bakery applications and enhance the use of complementary grains in the food industry. Unlike common wheat, intermediate wheatgrass (IWG) is a perennial grain that is reported to have higher protein, fiber, and ash content. To maximize the use of IWG in bakery applications, this research investigates the functionality and nutritional quality of a variety of wheat‐based flatbread, noodle, pasta, and cookies, with the incorporation of different degrees of wholemeal IWG. IWG at 25% incorporation for flatbread, 75% incorporation for noodle, 50% for pasta, and 50% for cookie formulations resulted in products with quality attributes comparable to the control formulations, indicating its potential to be used in specific product formulations. Incorporation of IWG resulted in decreased glucose release over time and increased protein digestibility.Practical ApplicationsThis article contributes to the growing body of research identifying additional end use applications for IWG aside from bread formulations. IWG from the Canadian prairies has not been extensively studied and this work provides a comprehensive overview of the functional properties and composition of IWG grown in the region.

Introduction

Common wheat (Triticum aestivum) is a staple crop grown around the world and accounts for approximately half of the world's caloric intake (Erenstein et al. 2022). However, the success of common wheat comes at a cost, which is largely associated with intensive agricultural practices. Intermediate wheatgrass (IWG) has been receiving increased attention as a perennial complement to common wheat. Much of this increased attention is due to the recent success of perennial rice which has seen an increase in yield similar to that of its common counterpart and an increase in consumer demand for more nutritious and functional foods (Zhang et al. 2023; Canadian Foundation for Dietetic Research 2025). Therefore, it is essential that perennial grains, such as IWG, be utilized not only to improve the nutritional value of common wheat products but also to contribute to a more regenerative agricultural system. The potential to continually improve common wheat is present; however, there is an urgency to fortify common wheat flour to diversify food systems. In the face of our changing climate, wheat is seeing decreases in yield, variation in protein content, and suboptimal plant health in years of drought and high temperatures (Hellemans et al. 2018; Jaldhani et al. 2021). Compared to common wheat, IWG contains more dietary fiber, protein, and minerals (Tyl and Ismail 2019). However, IWG has not undergone the rigorous domestication process that common wheat has. As a result, the end use of IWG flour has been limited in applications such as bread due to poor gas holding capabilities and suboptimal gluten forming proteins but has seen some success in extruded snacks (Boakye et al. 2023; Cetiner et al. 2023). IWG was reported to be deficient in high molecular weight glutenin (HMWG) which is a protein contributes to dough strength and gas trapping capability (Tyl and Ismail 2019). This has largely limited the applications of IWG in bread systems to a relatively low percentage of 15% IWG which resulted in a successfully leavened bread product (Cetiner et al. 2023).

Aside from the nutritional benefits, IWG is a perennial grain, and as a result, has an extensive root system that can be twelve times that of common wheat. Due to the extensive root system, IWG benefits from drought tolerance, increased nutrient scavenging capabilities, and increased soil organic matter and carbon sequestration (Law et al. 2022). IWG is an allohexaploid, which has proven to be beneficial as IWG has acted as a reservoir of disease and pest resistance genes for common bread wheat (Kantarski et al. 2017). Traditionally used as a feed crop, it has undergone breeding for a food crop in the past 30 years, which has resulted in increases to seed size, threshability, and grain yield (Bajgain et al. 2023).

Limited research is available on the addition of IWG to non‐bread product formulations and its impact on functionality. Although IWG has a suboptimal gluten forming protein ratio for bread applications, there is potential for formulations in other grain‐based products. Flatbread, specifically chapatti, for example, has had its quality negatively associated with gluten strength (Kundu et al. 2017). In noodle preparation, quality of the end product is not solely dependent on the gluten forming proteins, but also how they interact with the starch components of the flour. This is especially important in the development of texture of the cooked noodle (Zhang et al. 2022). The role of gluten in pasta preparation is similar to noodle where the gluten forming proteins in matrix interacts with starch and contribute to a gel network as well as desirable texture (Chaiya et al. 2018). Additionally, in some applications, a soft flour is more desirable than a hard flour, such as in cookie applications (Karaduman 2020).

The objective of this study is to determine the suitability of IWG flour alongside Canada Western Red Spring (CWRS) wheat in applications where a strong gluten network is not critical to end product quality. Products, such as flatbread, noodles, pasta, and cookies, were assessed to determine which level of incorporation with CWRS wholemeal (CWRS‐WM) wheat resulted in a high‐quality end product. After identifying the ideal levels of incorporation of IWG for each product, the starch and protein digestibility was assessed and compared to products made with CWRS wheat.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Samples of IWG were collected in two subsequent years (2021 and 2022), from 3 regions across the Canadian prairies (Brandon, Manitoba; Carman, Manitoba; Clavet, Saskatchewan). IWG used in this study was all of the same cultivar. Due to unforeseen circumstances, there was no 2022 harvest from Clavet, Saskatchewan. For comparison a bulk CWRS sample was used which was supplied by the Canadian Grain Commission (Winnipeg, Manitoba, Canada) and was representative of the 2023 CWRS harvest. IWG samples were dehulled in house using a Codema oat dehuller (Maple Grove, Minnesota, USA). IWG samples and CWRS were milled into WM flour using a laboratory mill 3100 equipped with mill feeder 3170 (Springfield, Illinois, USA) following the AACC method 26‐05.01, using a 0.8 mm sieve (AACC International 2009). CWRS refined (CWRS‐R) flour was milled using a Barbender senior mill (Anton Paar, Montreal, Quebec, Canada) following the AACC method 26‐22.01 (AACC International 2009). Tempering was carried out prior to milling CWRS into refined flour according to the AACC method 26‐10.01 (AACC International 2009). Total starch kit K‐TSTA‐100A and dietary fiber test kit K‐TDFR‐200A were all purchased from Megazyme (Bray Ireland). Lab chemicals include 2× Laemmli buffer, β‐mercaptoethanol, concentrated TRIS‐glycine‐SDS buffer, and 4%–15% 0.1 mm 18 well criterion TGX precast gels were purchased from Bio‐Rad Laboratories (Hercules, California, US). Protein stain was acquired from Thermo Scientific (Waltham, Massachusetts, US).

Flour Characteristics

Moisture content was determined following the AACC method 44‐15.02 using a Thermo Scientific HERATHERM oven (AACC International 2009). Samples were tested in triplicate.

Ash analysis was carried out using a Thermo Scientific Thermolyne muffle furnace following the AACC method 08‐01.01 (AACC International 2009). Samples were tested in triplicate.

Crude protein content was determined using the Kjeldahl analysis following the AACC method 46‐12.01 (AACC International 2009). A Velp Scientifica DK digester (Deer Park, New York) and SMS scrubber were used for digestion, and a FOSS Kjeltec KT 200 (Eden Prairie, Minnesota, USA) was used for distillation. A conversion factor of 5.70 was used for protein determination (Boakye et al. 2023). Samples were tested in triplicate.

Total starch content was determined following the AACC method 44‐15.02 using a Megazyme total starch kit K‐TSTA‐100A (AACC International 2009). Samples were tested in triplicate.

Crude fat content was determined using the Soxhlet analysis following AOAC method 920.39 C (Official Methods of Analysis of AOAC International 2023). Samples were tested in triplicate.

Dietary fiber was determined using the AACC method 32‐06.01 in conjunction with a dietary fiber test kit purchased from Megazyme (AACC International 2009). Overall, 95% ethanol was measured after heating to 60°C, and the volume was therefore adjusted to 225 mL. After the addition of 225 mL of 95% ethanol, samples were left to precipitate overnight. Samples were measured for ash content following the same procedure as described above, and protein content was analyzed as mentioned above using AACC method 46‐12.02 (AACC International 2009). Samples were tested once.

Falling number was determined following the AACC method 56‐81.03 (AACC International 2009). Samples were adjusted to 14% moisture content. Samples were tested in triplicate.

The IWG and CWRS flours as well as the finished products were analyzed for their color using a Konica Minolta CR‐410 Chroma Meter (Mississauga, Ontario, Canada). Samples were tested in triplicate.

Starch Pasting Profile

The starch pasting profile was collected for all flour samples using a Newport Rapid Visco Analyzer (Jessup, Maryland, USA) following the AACC method 76‐21.02 (AACC International 2009). The pasting temperature, peak viscosity, breakdown, final viscosity, and setback values were determined upon analysis. The run profile started at 50°C and 960 rpm for the first 10 s. Next, the samples were held at 50°C and mixed at 160 rpm until 1 min run time had elapsed. The samples were then heated to 95°C until 4 min and 42 s of run time had elapsed. Following this heating step, the samples were held at 95°C until 7 min and 12 s of run time had elapsed. Next the samples were reduced to 50°C until 11 min of run time had elapsed. Samples were maintained at 50°C until 13 min of run time, at which point the test ended.

Mixing Characteristics

For all flour samples, the mixing characteristics were measured using a Perten Micro‐doughLAB (MDL) with a 4 g bowl. Flour samples were corrected to 14% moisture content. The run profile was performed at 63 rpm for 20 min at 30°C. The goal was to determine the optimum water absorption and mixing characteristics.

Flatbread

Flatbread product development was completed separately for each IWG sample, CWRS‐WM, and CWRS‐R following a method previously described by Piga et al. (2021). Few modifications were made to the method to optimize the process. The formulation included 100% flour, 1% salt, and 1.5% yeast. Water was added based on optimal water absorption obtained from the MDL. Flatbreads were made at 25%, 50%, and 75% incorporation with WM IWG with the remaining being CWRS‐WM. The dough was prepared in a pin mixer until optimal dough development was achieved. The dough was then sheeted to a final thickness of 2 mm and formed using a circular mold. The samples were placed on a greased sheet pan and left to proof in a climate‐controlled cabinet at 72% RH and 30°C for 15 min. The oven was preheated with a pizza stone inside to a temperature of 193°C. Flatbreads were cooked for 2 min, then cooled to room temperature and placed in a Ziplock bag till they were analyzed the next day.

Flatbread was analyzed 24 h after baking. The toughness and extensibility were analyzed using a method previously described by Piga et al. (2021). The TA. XT Plus texture analyzer (Stable Microsystems, Surrey, UK) was fitted with an HDP/TB (tortilla/pastry burst rig) to measure the extension and elasticity.

Noodles

Noodle product development was completed following a method previously described by Zhang et al. (2005). Some modifications were made to the method to accommodate for lab equipment. A pin mixer with a 100 g bowl was used to mix the dough. The dough consisted of 100 g flour and 40% water. The dough was mixed for 1 min on slow, followed by 2 min on fast and then 2 min on slow for a total of 5 min of mixing time. The dough was then sheeted to 5 mm thickness and left to rest at room temperature for 30 min. The dough was then passed through an Antree pasta maker attachment on a Kitchen Aid stand mixer. The dough was gradually sheeted to a thickness of 1 mm and was then passed through the noodle cutter. Cooking time for noodles was determined following the AACC International (2009) method 66‐50.01. Noodles were analyzed for texture and color immediately after cooking. The percent incorporation of WM IWG in the noodles was 25%, 50%, and 75%.

Noodles were analyzed the same day, within 10 min after cooking. To determine cooking time, cook loss, firmness, and work associated with cutting the noodle for which the AACC International (2009) method 66‐50.01 was followed. A TA. XT Plus texture analyzer was utilized to determine the firmness and work (Stable Microsystems, Surrey, UK).

Pasta

IWG pasta was produced using a micro‐lab extruder (Randcastle Extrusion Systems INC.) with a flour‐hole Teflon coated spaghetti die (1.8 mm) (Fu et al. 2013). The flours were blended at 25%, 50%, and 75% incorporation of WM IWG into CWRS‐WM. Ice water was added based on the water absorption obtained from the MDL. Ice water was incorporated at 34% for IWG at 25% incorporation, 33% for IWG at 50% incorporation, 31% for IWG at 75%, 35% for 100% CWRS‐WM, and 30% for 100% CWRS‐R. WM was mixed with a high‐speed asymmetric centrifugal mixer (DAC 400 FVZ SpeedMixer, FlackTec, Landum, SC, USA). The resulting dough was sifted using a 1.4 mm #14 RH (round hole) sieve to remove the over hydrated fraction, and a 500 µm #35 sieve was used to remove the under hydrated fraction. The samples were extruded at 70 rpm and 45°C with extrusion pressure of about 80–100 bar. The first 75 g of each sample was discarded to account for any material from the previous samples. Fresh pasta samples were kept refrigerated for 24 h untilthey were analyzed. Optimal cooking time was determined following AACC International (2009) method 66‐50.01.

Pasta was analyzed 24 h after being extruded. Cook time was determined, samples were analyzed for firmness and work associated with cutting the pasta, using a TA. XT Plus texture analyzer (Stable Microsystems, Surrey, UK) following the AACC International (2009) method 66‐50.01. Cooking loss was determined following the AACC International (2009) method 66‐50.01.

Cookie

Cookies were prepared with WM IWG incorporations of 25%, 50%, and 75% into CWRS‐WM flour. Samples were prepared following the AACC International (2009) method 10‐52.02 ‐Micro cookie method. The preparation of the “creamed mass” was made in a Kitchen Aid stand mixer with a paddle attachment and the procedure described in the method was followed. Mixing of the cookie dough was done using a 100 g pin mixer. No other modifications were made.

Cookies were analyzed 3 h after baking. Cookie diameter was measured using a caliper. Cookie hardness was determined using a TA. XT Plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro systems, Surrey, UK) following AIBCOOKIES program with a 3‐point bend rig (HDP/3 PB). The run parameters were a test speed of 2.0 mm/s, posttest speed of 10.0 mm/s, distance of 15 mm, trigger type set to auto‐20 g, and at a rate of 200 pps.

Sample Preparation for Digestibility

After analyzing samples for their textural properties, the most promising level of IWG incorporation was selected for each product and a representative sample was made including all regions and years of that level of incorporation. The most promising levels of incorporation were flatbread with 25% WM IWG, noodles with 75% WM IWG, pasta with 50% WM IWG, and cookies with 50% WM IWG. Cooked samples were first frozen for 3 days, followed by freeze drying for 4 days in a Labconco FreezeZone tray dryer. After freeze drying, the samples were milled in a centrifugal mill (Retsch mill ZM 200) (Newtown, Pennsylvania, USA) using a 0.75 mm screen.

Starch Digestibility

Starch digestibility was carried out following the in vitro glucose release method previously described by van Kempen et al. (2010). Ground sample from the above mentioned treatments were weighed out to 250 mg and placed into a 15 mL test tube and had a mini stir bar added to it. Next, 2.5 mL of a pepsin–guar gum solution (175 mg pepsin, 175 g guar gum, and 35 mL 0.05 N HCl) was added to each tube and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. After 30 min, samples were removed and had 2.5 mL of 0.25 N sodium acetate and 1.25 mL of pancreatin–amyloglucosidse–invertase solution added, vortexed, and placed back in the water bath for 20 min, with samples being placed at 1‐min intervals. The pancreatin–amyloglucosidse–invertase solution was prepared with 3.75 g pancreatin and 25 mL reverse osmosis water that was mixed for 10 min on a stir plate. This was then centrifuged for 10 min at 1500 g. Following centrifugation, 18 mL of the supernatant was transferred to a beaker and had 180 µL of amyloglucosidse and 10.8 g of invertase added and was stirred until dissolved. At 19 min and 30 s the first sample was removed, vortexed for 5 s, and left to stand for 25 s, then had 100 µL removed and deposited into 5 mL of 100% ethanol before being returned to the water bath. This was repeated for all samples. Sampling was done at 20, 40, 60, 120, 240, and 300 min. Samples were then centrifuged for 2 min at 300 g. The supernatant (100 µL) was pipetted into test tubes with 3 mL of GOPOD reagent and left to incubate at 50°C for 20 min. The reagent blank consisted of 100 µL of 100% ethanol and 100 µL of glucose and was used in duplicate. Samples were then read using a microplate reader at 510 nm.

Protein Digestibility

Freeze dried samples were first analyzed for their protein content via the Kjeldahl method following the AACC method 46‐12.01 (AACC International 2009). Samples were weighed to contain 62.5 ± 1 mg of protein. They were then hydrated with 10 mL of reverse osmosis water, placed in a second beaker as a double boiler, and left to hydrate under constant agitation at room temperature for 30 min. After 30 min samples were placed in a water bath at 37°C for 20 min to reach optimal temperature for enzyme digestion. A multienzyme solution of digestive enzymes was prepared daily consisting of 16 mg trypsin from porcine pancreas (13,000–20,000 BAEE unity/mg protein), 31 mg α‐chymotrypsin from bovine pancreas (≥40 U/mg protein), 13 mg protease from Streptomyces griseus (≥3.5 U/mg solid, powder), and 10 mL of reverse osmosis water. Enzymes were sourced from Simga‐Aldrich. The multienzyme solution was then adjusted to 8.0 pH with 0.1 N NaOH and 0.1 N HCl and kept on ice. After 20 min in the water bath, samples were moved to a heated stirring plate and adjusted to pH 8.0 ± 0.05. The initial pH was recorded and then 1 mL of the multienzyme solution was added and allowed to digest for 10 min under constant agitation at 37°C. The final pH was recorded.

Statistical Analysis

IWG flour from each sample was formulated into the abovementioned products. This was completed in triplicate for each level of incorporation (25%, 50%, and 75%). CWRS‐R and CWRS‐WM were used at 100% incorporation for comparative purposes. Data analysis was conducted using Origin 2025. Data were analyzed using the analysis of variance (ANOVA) at the 5% level of significance (p ≤ 0.05). A Tukey means comparison was used to indicate significant differences between samples.

Results and Discussion

Proximate Composition

IWG grown in 2021 had a higher protein, ash, and fiber content compared to IWG grown in 2022, CWRS‐WM, and CWRS‐R (Figure 1). The protein content of IWG ranged between 14.15% from Brandon 2022 to 20.31% from Saskatchewan 2021. The samplefrom Brandon 2022 and Carman 2022 both had protein contents on the lower end of the expected range for IWG that is approximately between 16% and 24% (Zhang et al. 2015). This may be explained by a trend that is seen in common wheat cultivars where in years of drought, seed size is smaller thus leading to a higher protein content (Schmidt et al. 2020). This trend can be seen when we compare the protein content to the weather data in Table 1. Saskatchewan 2021 and Carman 2021 had received drastically less precipitation compared to the 30‐year average and have the highest protein contents of 20.31% and 17.65%, respectively. Brandon 2021 also displays this trend with drought; however, the difference between precipitation and the 30‐year average is smaller compared to that in Saskatchewan 2021 and Carman 2021. The opposite of this is also true, where a more adequate or high amount of precipitation can lead to greater starch deposition during grain filling, thus diluting the protein content, but not necessarily reducing the protein quality. This is likely what was experienced in Brandon 2022 and Carman 2022. Brandon 2022 received 30.5 mm, and Carman 2022 received 8.9 mm more precipitation that their 30‐year averages. Brandon 2022 also had the lowest protein content of IWG samples followed by Carman 2022. Comparing between different regions and years there was significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences in the protein and starch content. The starch and protein contents have an inverse relationship, meaning when there is a higher protein content there is a lower starch content. Ash and lipid content did not show significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences amongst IWG regions and years. However, there were significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences seen comparing between IWG and CWRS‐WM and refined. This observation is expected as a result of the smaller seed size of IWG, meaning that the endosperm to bran ratio is different, and therefore results in a greater percentage of ash in IWG and the same can be said for the endosperm to germ ratio (Bharathi et al. 2022). The falling number for IWG was significantly (p ≤ 0.05) lower compared to CWRS samples but was within the expected range previously reported (Ferguson et al. 2024; Michigan State University 2026). The lower falling number for IWG compared to CWRS may be attributed to relative starch contents, potential starch damage during milling, and enzymatic activity (Michigan State University 2026). However, starch damage and alpha‐amylase activity were not assessed in this study. IWG flour had a significantly (p ≤ 0.05) lower L* value or was darker, compared to CWRS‐R but significantly (p ≤ 0.05) lighter compared to CWRS‐WM flour (Table 2). IWG flour was significantly (p ≤ 0.05) redder compared to CWRS‐R but significantly (p ≤ 0.05) less compared to CWRS‐WM. IWG flour was significantly (p ≤ 0.05) more yellow compared to CWRS‐WM and CWRS‐R flours. Significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences between L*, a*, and b* color properties were observed between IWG samples, which is a likely a result of compositional differences, specifically in bran and fiber content (Rosentrater and Evers 2018).

Functional Properties

IWG compared to CWRS saw significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences in peak, trough, breakdown, final, and set back viscosity (Table 3). Pasting temperature saw no significant (p ≤ 0.05) difference between IWG regions and years but saw a significant (p ≤ 0.05) difference between CRWS‐WM, refined, and IWG samples. This can be attributed to potential differences in starch chemistry, such as porosity, crystallinity, and size (Balet et al. 2019). Another reason for such perceived differences may be the higher fat and protein content which can bind with the starch fractions and make the starches more resistant to hydration and breakdown (Balet et al. 2019). This can possibly provide an explanation as to why significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) in pasting properties are observed between CWRS‐WM and IWG from Brandon and Carman in 2022. Both IWG samples had starch contents that were more similar to CWRS yet had significantly (p ≤ 0.05) lower pasting viscosities than CWRS‐WM. CWRS‐R had the highest peak, trough, breakdown, final, and setback viscosity, followed by CWRS‐WM. The trend seen in the RVA data when comparing CWRS samples and IWG samples, respectively, is that the higher the starch content, and the lower protein content, resulted in higher viscosity parameters. This is best realized when analyzing Saskatchewan 2021 and Carman 2021. Both samples experienced profound drought conditions, resulting in a higher protein content which can compete for hydration and limit capacity for the starch to swell and gelatinize (Bharathi et al. 2022). In turn, the viscosity parameters for Saskatchewan 2021 and Carman 2021 were lowest and second lowest, respectively. The differences seen between regions and years of IWG are expected due to variations in precipitation and average temperatures that have substantial impacts on kernel composition, specifically the starch and protein composition (LeHeiget et al. 2023). Furthermore, the lipid content can have a significant impact on the pasting profile. Starch‐lipid complexes can form during processing and may inhibit the starch's ability to hydrate and retrograde due to the hydrophobic nature of lipids (Chao et al. 2020). The differences in pasting properties between IWG samples and CWRS samples are likely due to compositional differences in starch seen between botanical sources (Balet et al. 2019). Such differences can be related to granule size and amylose/amylopectin ratio. Crystallinity and swelling capacity are both a function of amylose/amylopectin ratio. Crystaline regions are primarily comprised of amylopectin, whereas amylose makes up the amorphous regions and can inhibit swelling. Starch crystallinity can vary between botanical sources and, therefore, has a significant impact on the pasting properties (Singh et al. 2003).

Mixing characteristics of IWG flour had significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences between regions and years, and significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences were observed between IWG and CWRS samples (Table 4). IWG exhibited a shorter dough development time, lower stability, and a mixing tolerance index (MTI) that was lower compared to CWRS‐R but more similar to CWRS‐WM likely due to the presence of the fiber rich bran fraction and differences in the glutenin‐to‐gliadin ratio (Marti et al. 2015). IWG grown in 2021 when drought was experienced, presented a longer stability, and dough development time compared to IWG grown in 2022. This can be related with the higher protein content observed in the growing years 2021 compared to 2022. Mixing characteristics in this study follow similar trends as previously reported by Ferguson et al. (2024).

Flatbread Analysis

Flatbread firmness experienced significant (p ≤ 0.05) variation between treatments (Table 5). Flatbread made with IWG flour from Saskatchewan 2021 at the level of 25% incorporation had the highest firmness and was not significantly different from the 75% incorporation at the same location. Flatbread made with CWRS‐R flour was similar to 25% and 50% incorporation from Brandon 2021 and Carman 2021 and at 25% incorporation from Carman 2022 and 50% incorporation from Saskatchewan 2021. Brandon 2022 at the 50% incorporation level exhibited the lowest firmness. Similar low firmness values were also observed for Brandon 2022 at 75% incorporation, Carman 2022 at 50% incorporation, and CWRS‐WM. There was no clear trend between incorporation percent and flatbread firmness. Extensibility also varied significantly (p ≤ 0.05) among treatments. Most IWG‐incorporated samples were not significantly different from CWRS‐WM or CWRS‐R in extensibility. However, Carman 2022 at the 75% incorporation level exhibited significantly lower extensibility than CWRS‐WM and CWRS‐R. Overall, extensibility values were relatively stable across incorporation levels, with only isolated significant differences. Although some treatments with higher firmness exhibited moderate extensibility values, a consistent inverse pattern between firmness and extensibility was not evident across treatments. It is also noted that these growing locations received adequate precipitation during the growing season which resulted in a higher starch content and lower protein content. There is no trend to be seen between growing locations in the year 2021. This year all locations experienced differing degrees of drought and heat stress. Water scarcity and heat stress can have significant impacts on protein and starch characteristics such as protein deposition, starch granular size, and amylose/amylopectin ratio (Singh et al. 2008, 2010). This interaction could be an explanation for why there is no noticeable trend in year 2021, compared to year 2022 where temperatures were moderate, and precipitation was adequate.

The color of the finished flatbreads varied significantly (p ≤ 0.05) with the incorporation of IWG (Table 5 ). Flatbread made with CWRS‐R flour exhibited the highest L* value, indicating that it was the lightest in color, followed by CWRS‐WM (Konica Minolta 2026), whereas all IWG incorporated treatments exhibited significantly lower L* values. The trend for flatbreads with IWG incorporation did not indicate a consistent decrease in lightness across all treatments. However, a significant decrease in L* values from 25% to 75% incorporation was observed for Brandon 2022 and Carman 2022. As for a*, or the redness (+) or greenness (−) of the flatbreads, CWRS‐WM had the highest value, meaning it was most red, and CWRS‐R had the lowest value meaning it was most green or least red. Flatbread with IWG incorporated was very similar to CWRS‐WM and saw very little variance in a* values. This observation is likely due to the inclusion of bran and the resulting change in color due to the pigmentation as this difference can be observed between CWRS‐R and CWRS‐WM flatbreads. The b* value, or the measure of yellowness (+) or blueness (−), exhibited significant differences (p ≤ 0.05) between IWG samples. In Brandon 2021, Brandon 2022, and Carman 2021, the 25% incorporation level exhibited significantly (p ≤ 0.05) lower b* values than the 75% incorporation level, indicating that flatbreads at 75% IWG incorporation exhibited a more pronounced yellow hue than those at 25% in these treatments. This observation is likely due to the inclusion of CWRS‐WM as the complementary flour in IWG incorporations.

Noodle Analysis

Noodles were cooked to optimal doneness following AACC International (2009) method 66‐50.01. Noodles made with lower levels of incorporations of IWG were typically firmer than, or similar, to noodles made with CWRS‐R flour (Table 6). As the level of IWG incorporation increased, noodle firmness decreased in Carman 2021. The inverse pattern observed in Saskatchewan 2021, where firmness increased with increasing IWG incorporation. In Brandon 2021, firmness at 25% and 75% incorporation was not significantly different, whereas 50% incorporation showed a significant decrease in firmness compared to 25% incorporation. For Brandon 2022, firmness at 25% and 50% incorporation levels was similar, whereas 75% incorporation resulted in significantly lower firmness. Carman 2022 exhibited similar firmness across all three IWG incorporation levels, and these values were similar to noodles made with CWRS‐R. At 75% incorporation, Saskatchewan 2021 and Brandon 2021 exhibited a firmness similar to that of noodles made of CWRS‐WM. A potential explanation for the increased firmness observed in IWG noodles from Saskatchewan 2021 and Brandon 2021 could be due to the higher protein content, specifically non‐gluten forming proteins (Lambrecht et al. 2017). The firmness may be attributed to the potentially higher albumin and globulin content in WM IWG and CWRS‐WM flour which can aid in protein polymerization (Lambrecht et al. 2017). A quantitative analysis of the protein composition is needed to confirm this speculation. The inverse of this firmness‐protein trend may also occur, where samples with lower protein content, such as with Brandon 2022 and Carman 2021, showed less firmness at the 75% incorporation level. The increase of IWG, which was lower in protein compared to CWRS‐WM, resulted in decreased firmness similar to that of CWRS‐R noodles. The optimal flour protein content of noodles is between 10% and 12%; therefore, reducing the relative protein content can positively impact the end product quality, when reduced to optimal levels (Wang 2003). The amount of work associated with compressing the noodle followed trends similar to those observed for firmness which intuitively makes sense as the firmer the noodle, the harder it is to chew. Noodles made with CWRS‐R flour were only similar in work to IWG at 25% and 50% incorporation of Carman 2022, and 25% incorporation of Saskatchewan 2021. Noodle cook loss varied significantly (p ≤ 0.05) among treatments with higher cook loss being associated with an increase in IWG incorporation in Brandon 2022 and Carman 2021 at the 75% incorporation level compared to the 25% and 50% incorporation levels within the same treatments. These two treatments, along with Saskatchewan 2021 at 25% incorporation, showed cook loss values similar to those of CWRS‐WM and CWRS‐R. A possible explanation for the increased cook loss could be due to differences in the protein composition compared to CWRS noodles. HMWG has been associated with reductions in cook loss, which is present in higher quantities in CWRS compared to IWG (Katyal et al. 2019).

Pasta Analysis

Pasta was cooked to optimal doneness following AACC International (2009) method 66‐50.01. There was some significant (p ≤ 0.05) variation in pasta firmness between IWG samples. Most notably pasta made with flour from Saskatchewan 2021 at the 75% level of incorporation had the firmest pasta and was similar to pasta made from Carman 2021 at 75% incorporation. Both Saskatchewan and Carman 2021 had the highest levels of protein content, which may relate to the firmness. IWG from most other regions and years was similar in firmness compared to pasta made with CWRS‐R with the exception of the abovementioned treatments, as well as the Brandon 2021 at 75%, Carman 2022 at 75%, and Carman and Saskatchewan 2021 at the 50% incorporation levels. The work associated with cutting into the pasta was significantly (p ≤ 0.05) higher for Saskatchewan 2021 at 75% level of incorporation. Among the 2021 samples from the three regions, the higher levels of incorporation of IWG generally resulted in more work being associated with the pasta. Pasta made with 100% CWRS‐R required the least amount of work, which was similar to CWRS‐WM and to the 25% incorporation levels for all 2022 samples and Brandon 2021 samples, as well as to the 50% incorporation level for Carman 2022.. As for cook loss, there was no significant (p ≤ 0.05) difference between CWRS‐WM and IWG. In CWRS‐R pasta, cook loss was significantly (p ≤ 0.05) lower than in the other samples. This is likely due to the inclusion of bran in the IWG and WM samples. Lightness (L*) of pasta was significantly (p ≤ 0.05) higher in CWRS‐R than in all other treatments tested (Table 7). No consistent trend of decreasing L* value with increasing IWG incorporation was observed, although a few individual comparisons showed lower L* at higher incorporation levels (e.g., Saskatchewan 2021 at 50% vs. 75% and Carman 2021 at 25% vs. 75%). Overall, most IWG treatments were not significantly (p ≤ 0.05) different from CWRS‐WM in lightness, except for the 75% incorporation levels for Carman 2021, Carman 2022, and Saskatchewan 2021. Cookie Analysis

Cookies produced with flour from Saskatchewan 2021 and Carman 2021 at 75% incorporation showed significantly (p ≤ 0.05) higher hardness than all the other IWG treatments tested, and these values were similar to cookies made with 100% CWRS‐R flour, followed by Brandon 2021 at the 25% and 50% incorporation levels (Table 8). The lowest hardness was observed for Brandon 2022 at the 75% incorporation level. All remaining treatments produced cookies with hardness similar to CWRS‐WM cookies. As for distance, cookies made with 100% CWRS‐R flour had the greatest distance and were not significantly (p ≤ 0.05) different from most other samples tested, except for Brandon 2021 at 75% incorporation and Brandon 2022 at 25% and 50% incorporation levels, which exhibited significantly lower distance values compared to CWRS‐R. In contrast, cookie spread values followed a different pattern, with CWRS‐R exhibiting the lowest spread and most IWG‐incorporated treatments showing significantly greater spread, as all the treatments produced values significantly different from CWRS‐R. The observed differences in cookie hardness, distance, and in spread may be related to variations in protein content in these flours. Although gluten development is limited in cookies with the addition of lipids and sugars, they are still able to compete for water, thus increasing the viscosity of the dough, decreasing the spread, and increasing the hardness of the cookie (Moiraghi et al. 2011). Therefore, protein content plays an important role in the quality of cookies, alongside protein quality. Furthermore, differences in starch composition, such as amylose/amylopectin content and long chain versus short chain amylopectin, can play an important role in dough rheology and impact end quality characteristics (Zhang et al. 2020).

Starch Digestibility

Starch in products produced with CWRS‐R flour had a greater release of glucose for every time interval compared to products produced with CWRS‐WM and IWG flour as seen in Figure 2. This can be a result of a higher starch content in CWRS‐R flour compared to CWRS‐WM and IWG flours. As a result of the increased protein and fiber content, they can compete with starch for water and potentially limit the hydration and therefore enzymatic susceptibility of starch (Bae et al. 2016). Cookies exhibited the highest initial glucose release but had the slowest release over time. It was anticipated that the glucose release would be higher due to the incorporation of sucrose. However, the digestive enzymes needed to release the glucose from the sucrose were not used in the digestibility assay, and therefore, the depression of glucose release over time is a function of percentage of flour used in the formulations relative to non‐flour components. Products made with IWG all exhibited the lowest release of glucose over time compared to their CWRS‐R and CWRS‐WM counterparts. This can be potentially attributed to the greater fiber, lipid, and protein found in IWG compared to CWRS‐R and CWRS‐WM products. Fiber, lipids, and protein can all compete for hydration as previously mentioned. Additionally, these constituents of IWG flour can interact with starch, creating starch that is more resistant to enzymatic digestion (Zhen et al. 2022). Previous studies have shown that products formulated with WM IWG release glucose at a slower rate compared to the same product formulated with refined common wheat. This is thought to be due to the presence of shorter starch chains, and the presence of antinutritional factors, dietary fiber, protein, and lipids that are more prevalent in WM flours, as previously mentioned (Zhong et al. 2019).

Protein Digestibility

IVPD had significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences when comparing between products, likely as a result of differences in processing methods and non‐flour ingredients (Figure 3). The highest IVPD was observed for noodles prepared with CWRS‐R flour, followed by IWG noodles at 75% incorporation, and CWRS‐WM noodles. The differences observed between different flours for noodles were not significant (p ≤ 0.05). Pasta made with CWRS‐R flour had the highest IVPD, followed by IWG at 50% level of incorporation, and CWRS‐WM pasta. The differences observed between the pasta products were not significant (p ≤ 0.05). Flatbread made with CWRS‐R flour had the greatest protein digestibility followed by flatbread made with 25% IWG and CWRS‐WM flatbread. There were no significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences between IVPD for flatbread. Lastly for cookie IVPD CWRS‐R cookies had the highest IVPD followed by cookies made with CWRS‐WM flour and IWG at 50% incorporation. There was no significant (p ≤ 0.05) difference between IVPD for cookies. Although significant (p ≤ 0.05) differences were not observed between types of flour within products, CWRS‐R flour had numerically higher IVPD compared to CWRS‐WM and IWG products. A potential reason for this observation is due to differences in composition between refined and WM flours. WM flours contain fiber and lipids that can complex with proteins, reducing accessibility for enzymatic attack, and thus reducing the IVPD.

IVPD for all samples was elevated compared to previous digestibility studies involving common wheat. This could be a result of slightly different methods being utilized, differences in wheat cultivar and therefore protein characteristics, and sample preparation (Ma and Baik 2021; Ramya et al. 2015). Few studies mentioned to what size the products were ground after freeze drying. Particle size can have a significant impact on enzyme activity as smaller particles can make proteins more accessible to enzymatic digestion (Ma and Baik 2021).

Conclusion

Overall, IWG performed well for all products at varying levels of incorporation. In the different products, varying levels of incorporation lead to a product with acceptable quality considering the end‐use quality parameters that were tested. For flatbread, this level was 25% considering extensibility and color, compared to the WM standard; however, firmness was quite high at 25%. For noodles, the 75% incorporation rate worked well, where most IWG incorporated products showed similar firmness compared to the refined standard, and in some instances, this rate resulted in products with firmness on par with the WM standard. Considering lightness, this rate led to products similar to the CWRS‐WM standard. For pasta, at the 50% incorporation rate, the IWG containing products performed in line with the performance of the WM products, in fact the firmness was at times similar to the refined flour standard. At this rate, cook loss was also similar to the WM standard. With reference to the pasta color, IWG incorporated at 50% produced similar products to the WM standard. For cookies, in most instances, at the 50% IWG incorporation level, hardness and distance were similar to the WM products. Proximate composition of IWG across growing regions and years experienced substantial variations which may pose a hurdle for predicting IWG quality and therefore end‐use applications. Future studies would benefit from having a complete set of samples from consecutive years, allowing for two‐way ANOVA analysis between location and growing year. Implementing an annual grain quality analysis program that supports producers in determine the best applications for IWG based on the harvest's quality has the potential to positively influence IWG uptake. Incorporation of IWG has the potential to produce products with slower glucose release and therefore reduced glycemic index. Therefore, IWG may have the potential to be used for production of products geared towards diabetic diets. The protein in products made with IWG was easily digestible. However, further digestibility studies utilizing methods that more closely mimic the human digestive system would be beneficial in gaining a clear understanding of how accessible IWG proteins are to enzymatic digestion and if any beneficial peptides are created during digestion. In conclusion, IWG has the potential to be used alongside CWRS‐WM flour to produce high quality end products. Further studies to assess the protein and starch chemistry will aid in identifying which functional proteins are present in IWG and the amylose and amylopectin characteristics and the potential impacts on digestibility. Additionally, sensory analysis and consumer preference tests would shed light on the acceptance of these products in the market.
