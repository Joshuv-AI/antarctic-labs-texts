Oxidative base damage to telomeres sensitizes cancer cells to ATR inhibition
Authors: Alex Garbouchian, Natalia Cestari Moreno, Aninda Dey, Christopher J. Bakkenist, Patricia L. Opresko, Ryan P. Barnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Targeted inhibition of DNA damage response proteins has received significant clinical attention owing to the success of PARP inhibitors. Due to G1/S checkpoint inactivation, cancer cells are reliant on the G2/M checkpoint to cope with elevated DNA replication stress. We demonstrated that a single induction of 8-oxo-guanine at telomeres in cancer cells was sufficient to induce replication stress but was not cytotoxic. Here, we find inhibition of ATR, CHK1, or WEE1 after induction of telomeric 8-oxo-guanine significantly reduced cell viability by inducing genome instability. This occurred at doses markedly less than those required to increase instability in non-cancerous cells. We determined the mechanism of this instability is due to insufficient levels of RPA for damaged telomeres, forcing cells to progress through S-phase with telomere damage and exit G2-phase prematurely, prolonging mitosis. This study demonstrates targeted oxidative base damage at telomeres can enhance the therapeutic efficacy of ATR inhibition in cancer.

Introduction

Telomeres are nucleoprotein structures that prevent the DNA damage response (DDR) machinery from erroneously recognizing the ends of chromosomes as double-stranded DNA breaks (DSBs). Human telomeres consist of 5′ TTAGGG 3′ repeats on the order of several kilobases, which are bound by shelterin1. Shelterin is a 6-member protein group that binds both double-stranded (ds) and single-stranded (ss) telomeric DNA and organizes the chromosome end into a looped structure (t-loop), which obfuscates the free DNA ends2. While telomeres are critical for all cells, their relevance to proliferating cells is remarkably demonstrated in cancer, as every cancer cell engages a telomere maintenance mechanism3. The most common mechanism is telomerase upregulation (TEL), but approximately 10–15% of cancers use a homologous recombination-based, alternative lengthening of telomeres (ALT) pathway instead.

Seminal work has shown that the removal of shelterin member TRF2 from mammalian cells disrupts telomere function, leading to t-loop dissolution and chromosome fusions4. Removal of TRF1 or POT1 instead increases DNA replication stress at telomeres specifically5. This is thought to be due in part to the inherent difficulty in replicating the repetitive telomere sequence, which requires the recruitment of DNA replication and repair factors like PARP1 and BLM, mediated by TRF16,7.

Replication stress is broadly defined as the slowing or stalling of DNA replication forks. Approximately 2 decades ago, work from the Bartek and Halazonetis groups postulated that replication stress in precancerous cells was a driver of tumorigenesis due to increased genomic instability8,9. We now know that stressed replication forks can collapse due to exhaustion of replication factors, nuclease digestion, and replisome disassembly10. Thus, cells require increased time following replication stress to complete DNA synthesis so that they do not enter mitosis with under-replicated DNA, which causes chromosomal instability when cells divide.

ATR is the principal regulator of the replication stress response (RSR) in mammalian cells11. Stalled replication forks generate considerable ssDNA, which is protected by RPA binding, and the accumulation of this RPA-coated ssDNA is the signal for ATRIP, TOPBP1, and others to activate ATR. Once activated, ATR phosphorylates a variety of targets, including its main effector kinase, CHK1, to inhibit cell cycle progression from S and G2 to mitosis, suppress origin firing, and recruit DNA repair and replication factors11,12. Unlike the G1/S checkpoint, the S and G2/M checkpoints are rarely mutated in cancer, as cancer cells rely on them to mitigate the elevated genomic instability and replication stress they experience13–15. Consequently, inhibitors of ATR and CHK1 have received increased clinical attention in recent years, with at least five drugs in Phase II trials, and one in Phase III16. These include VE822 and AZD6738, the first two ATRi to enter clinical trials, which are very specific ATP competitive inhibitors of ATR kinase activity and are used in the clinic in combination with DNA-damaging agents.

In addition to replication stress, cancer cells display elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to oxidative stress17. While beneficial at slight elevations, cancer cells must maintain a balance between ROS and their antioxidant defenses to maintain viability18. Exploiting this tightrope cancer cells need to walk is another therapeutic option being pursued19,20. Our group has previously shown that chronic oxidative stress at telomeres in the form of singlet oxygen mediated 8-oxo-guanine (8oxoG) formation significantly reduces cancer cell growth, and this coincided with increased replication stress and genome instability21,22. However, cancer cells were largely unaffected by a single induction of telomeric 8oxoG but did display slight increases in replication stress markers. Non-diseased cells senesced after a single treatment, and this senescence was dependent on DNA replication following 8oxoG induction23. Together, our findings showed telomeric 8oxoG induces replication stress in dividing cells, but cancer cells were largely refractory to a single burst of this damage alone.

In this study, we sought to determine if telomere oxidation would sensitize cancer cells to low-dose ATRi. Remarkably, ATR inhibition with VE822 or AZD6738 induced genome instability following acute induction of 8oxoG at telomeres in multiple cancer cell lines, resulting in reduced cell viability. Non-cancerous cells with intact p53 signaling were additionally sensitized to telomere replication stress by ATRi, but at doses 4–10 fold higher than cancer cells. We found that ATRi and telomere 8oxoG-induced genome instability was dramatically elevated when cells were damaged at the G1/S-phase boundary or mid-S-phase, and permitting cells increased time in G2 rescued genome stability. Finally, our results suggest that when ATR is inhibited, there is insufficient RPA to protect oxidized telomeres due to dormant origin firing throughout the genome. Together, our data highlight that while telomeres are a very small portion of the genome, when they experience replication stress, this must be properly managed by the RSR, or cells prematurely exit G2-phase and produce mitotic errors, leading to reduced viability.

Results

Telomeric 8oxoG-induced replication stress requires ATR and CHK1 to maintain genome stability

Our prior work demonstrated that DNA synthesis progressing through 8oxoG in telomeres disrupts DNA replication and causes telomere replication stress21–23. This led us to postulate that restraining the RSR through ATR or CHK1 inhibition would sensitize cancer cells to telomere replication stress, as cancer cells are more reliant on ATR signaling for survival (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Consistent with our previous studies, U2OS (ALT) and HeLa LT (TEL) FAP-TRF1 cells treated with MG2I photosensitizer dye and 660 nm light (DL) to produce 8oxoG at telomeres had no increase in genome instability as evaluated by counting micronuclei (MN) (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1B). However, when ATR inhibitors VE822 or AZD6738 were added after DL treatment, we observed significant and dose-dependent increases in MN for both cell lines (Fig. 1A–D). Inhibiting the main target of ATR, CHK1, using a specific inhibitor (LY2603618) after 8oxoG induction also generated significant genome instability in both cell lines (Fig. 1E, F). Importantly, for both cell lines and all three drugs, lower ATRi/CHK1i doses induced MN only after DL treatment, without increasing MN on their own. Increased levels of RPA on chromatin are the principal signal for ATR activation. We found telomeric 8oxoG induction significantly increased RPA foci at telomeres 3 h after treatment, demonstrating 8oxoG is sufficient to disrupt telomere replication (Fig. 1G, H). However, addition of ATRi did not further increase RPA levels, suggesting 8oxoG alone induces considerable replication stress. At this same time point, we found increased telomeric 53BP1 foci, indicating telomere dysfunction, but again ATRi did not increase this over 8oxoG induction alone. 24 h after treatment, however, cells with intact ATR signaling resolved the telomere DDR (53BP1 and γH2AX foci) caused by 8oxoG (Fig. 1I, J). Conversely, when ATR is inhibited, this telomere DDR persists demonstrating that telomeric 8oxoG requires ATR signaling to restore telomere stability and function.

We confirmed the genome instability phenotype in two other cancer cell lines (SAOS (ALT) and LOX (TEL)) and found both display increased MN when ATR is inhibited following telomere oxidation (Supplementary Fig. 1E, F). This indicates the induced genomic instability is not specific to cells that use telomerase or ALT for telomere maintenance. We also confirmed 0.2 µM VE822 and AZD6738 were a sufficient dose to suppress ATR signaling even in response to UVC (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Since ATM is closely related to ATR, we tested if its inhibition following telomeric 8oxoG induction affected genome stability. However, even at a dose that inhibits hydrogen peroxide-induced signaling, ATMi did not alter MN in U2OS or HeLa cells (Supplementary Fig. 1D, G, H). Finally, we tested a second CHK1 inhibitor (AZD7762); however, this drug also inhibits CHK224. We observed increases in MN in both HeLa and U2OS cells after DL treatment, but the effects were modest compared to LY2603618, which is highly specific for CHK1 (Supplementary Fig. 1I, J)25. In total, we found that replication stress generated by 8oxoG-enriched telomeres is sufficient to require the RSR for genome integrity, sensitizing cancer cells to low-dose ATR/CHK1 inhibition.

ATR maintains cell fitness following telomere oxidation

In our previous studies, we found chronic formation of 8oxoG at telomeres reduced cancer cell viability, but an acute treatment was viable even with increasing light exposure times (more 8oxoG)21,22. Following our genome instability results, we wondered if ATRi after telomere 8oxoG induction would reduce cancer cell viability. For these experiments, we treated U2OS and HeLa cells with dye and light to induce telomeric 8oxoG, and low-dose VE822 and AZD6738, which alone are not cytotoxic. Consistently, we found induction of 8oxoG at telomeres sensitized cancer cells to low-dose ATRi as measured by counting cells 4 days after treatment (Fig. 2A–D). Similar results were observed with CHK1i (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). We confirmed this result with clonogenic survival experiments and again found reduced cell viability with the combination of ATRi and telomeric 8oxoG (Fig. 2E, F). However, when we performed Annexin V and propidium iodide (PI) staining of these cells, we observed no increase in apoptosis or necrosis after 8oxoG induction and ATRi (Supplementary Fig. 2C–E). This is similar to our observations from chronically treating cancer cells with dye and light, in which we observed significant reductions in population doubling but no obvious change in cell death. To determine if the reduced cell and colony numbers were due to decreased cell divisions, we labeled cells immediately prior to treatment with CellTrace Violet, a reagent that is evenly diluted in cells when they divide. During the first 24 h after treatment, DL alone caused a slight reduction in cell division while ATRi alone increased cell division (Fig. 2G, H). However, VE822 treatment following oxidative telomere damage significantly reduced cell divisions, seemingly halting division after 48 h. Oxidative telomere damage does not sensitize non-cancerous cells to low-dose ATRi

Our previous work showed telomeric 8oxoG-induced senescence in non-diseased, otherwise normal cell lines in a manner dependent both on p53/ATM and DNA replication23. To understand how non-cancerous cells would respond to ATRi after 8oxoG formation at telomeres, we compared RPE FAP-TRF1 cells to U2OS and HeLa FAP-TRF1 cells treated with dye and light and 0.05 µM VE822 (Fig. 3A, B). At this very low dose, both cancer lines displayed genome instability following 8oxoG induction and ATRi, but not ATRi alone. RPE cells, however, while displaying the small MN increase with dye and light treatment we described previously23, showed no change with VE822. Consistently, RPE colony survival after telomeric 8oxoG induction was reduced as we previously showed, and this was unaffected by 0.05 and 0.2 µM VE822 (Fig. 3C)23. Since higher doses of VE822 sensitized RPE cells to telomere replication stress, we treated them with 0.2 and 1.0 µM VE822 and quantified MN (Fig. 3D). Despite no change in colony survival, 0.2 µM VE822-treated cells had a significant increase in MN after 8oxoG induction; however, the total number of MN was still much lower than what we observed with cancer cells. 1.0 µM VE822 dramatically raised MN after dye and light treatment, but also the background level. With 0.2 µM AZD6738, RPE cells showed no increase in MN or change in colony survival, but higher doses elevated MN and reduced colony survival (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). As with U2OS and HeLa, ATMi did not alter RPE MN after dye and light treatment, and ATRi after telomere oxidation did not induce apoptosis (Fig. 3D and Supplementary Fig. 3C).

While there are countless differences between non-diseased and cancerous cells, we wondered if fully functional p53 signaling was allowing RPE cells to tolerate ATRi better than cancer cells. We repeated clonogenic survival experiments with wild-type and p53ko RPE cells using the same increasing doses of VE822. Remarkably, we found that increasing ATRi reversed the phenotypic differences previously observed for wild-type and p53ko RPE cells. As we previously reported23 without ATRi, p53ko cells were more resistant to telomeric 8oxoG, but this was nullified with 0.05 µM VE822 (Fig. 3E). 0.2 µM VE822 significantly reduced p53ko cell survival compared to no ATRi control, and at 0.5 µM, p53ko RPE had a significant reduction in survival compared to wild-type cells. Consistent with this, when p53ko RPE cells were treated with dye and light and ATRi, they displayed a dramatic increase in MN, significantly greater than wild-type cells (Fig. 3F). Similar results were observed for BJ FAP-TRF1 wild-type and p53ko cells, which is another non-diseased cell line pair (Supplementary Fig. 3D, E). We also treated LM216J FAP-TRF1 cells with VE822 following 8oxoG induction and found an increase in MN (Supplementary Fig. 3F). This line is not derived from cancer but is SV40 E6/E7 transformed, abrogating both p53 and p16 signaling pathways, and uses ALT for telomere maintenance26. Collectively, we find p53 signaling protects non-diseased cells from sensitivity to ATRi following oxidative telomere damage.

ATRi allows cells to enter mitosis with unresolved telomere damage

After observing the importance of p53 to ATRi sensitivity, we wondered how else the cell cycle responds to telomeric 8oxoG when ATR is inhibited. By microscopy, we found telomeric 8oxoG reduces S-phase (EdU+) wild-type RPE cells, but it does not change the percent EdU+ U2OS, HeLa, or p53ko RPE (Fig. 4A). ATRi alone reduces EdU+ cells, but there is no further change with dye and light for this phenotype. This suggested that following telomeric 8oxoG formation, the ATR pathway G2/M checkpoint prevents cells from leaving G2 with unresolved telomere damage (Fig. 4B). Consistent with this, when cells are treated with a WEE1 inhibitor after 8oxoG induction at telomeres, we observed a dose-dependent increase in MN and reduced cell growth (Fig. 4C, D and Supplementary Fig. 4A). Previous studies showed ATRi/CHK1i induces the transcription of G2/M-phase related genes in a FOXM1-dependent fashion, facilitating premature mitotic entry27,28. We confirmed that knockdown of FOXM1 abrogated the genome instability observed after ATRi and oxidative telomere damage (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. 4B). This is consistent with a mechanism that, when the RSR is inhibited following telomere replication stress, cells prematurely exit G2 and enter mitosis. In agreement with this, cell cycle analysis with EdU and phosphorylated histone H3 (pH3; mitosis marker) staining by flow cytometry showed ATRi modestly reduced S-phase and G2-phase cell levels, while increasing mitotic and G1 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4C–E). However, dye and light had no additional effect discernible by this analysis.

A phenotype of cells attempting to divide with unresolved DNA damage is prolonged mitosis29. Mitosis is such a critical aspect of the cell cycle that its timing is conserved throughout different cell types in humans, and even small changes in duration can signal cell death or growth arrest29–31. We labeled chromatin in U2OS FAP-TRF1 cells with H2B-mCherry and scored mitotic events after induction of telomeric 8oxoG and ATRi. We divided the experiment into 5 windows (Imaging Periods) to ensure an even sampling of events. For each event, we calculated the length of mitosis from nuclear envelope breakdown (NEBD) to completion of cytokinesis (Supplementary Fig. 5A and “Methods”). Cells treated with DL and 0.2 µM VE822 showed a consistent and statistically significant increase in mitotic duration and were the only cells with a mitotic duration significantly different from all other conditions (Fig. 4F). This finding strongly suggests ATRi allowed cells to enter mitosis with unresolved telomere damage following oxidation.

We evaluated the number of MN produced from each mitosis scored throughout the imaging experiment (Fig. 4G and Supplementary Fig. 5B). No condition showed increased MN during the first imaging period (up to ~8.8 h post treatment). An increase in the number of MN per mitosis began in the second imaging period, and by the third period (up to 22.4 h post treatment), the average MN per division for dye and light and 0.2 µM VE822-treated cells was ~4 and reached ~5 by period 5. Except for period 4, 0.2 µM VE822 dye and light-treated cells had more MN per division than 0.2 µM VE822 UT cells and overall was significantly different. 0.05 µM VE822 dye and light-treated cells showed significantly more MN than 0.05 µM VE822 UT, averaging ~1 MN per division in periods 3–5, dramatically higher than the background rate of ~0.1 MN per division for the controls. 0.05 µM UT was not significantly different from the no-drug controls. In summary, ATRi and oxidative telomere damage synergize to disrupt cell division, resulting in a longer mitosis and more MN per division.

Extended G2, but not mitosis, rescues genome instability after oxidative telomere damage

We hypothesized that restraining entry into mitosis would allow cells time to recover from telomere 8oxoG and RSR inhibition. To test this, we used the CDK1 inhibitor RO-3306 to control mitotic entry (Fig. 5A). When asynchronous U2OS and RPE cells were treated with CDK1i alongside 0.2 µM VE822, this completely prevented MN induction as cells were unable to leave G2-phase (Fig. 5B, C). To determine if a temporary hold in G2 could rescue ATRi-induced MN after telomere oxidation, we treated cells with DL as before and incubated them with VE822 and/or CDK1i for 16 h. Then these cells were “released” from the drug and provided fresh media with no drug for 8 h. Cells treated only with VE822 for 16 h still had a significant increase in MN, albeit less than those treated for 24 h. However, cells co-treated with VE822 and CDK1i for 16 h showed a dramatic reduction in MN, significantly less than the VE822 singly treated cells and not significantly greater than the no drug control. Work by other groups and us has shown that DNA repair synthesis in mitosis (MiDAS) is activated after telomere replication stress, and this is presumed to be cytoprotective6. We wondered if a brief mitotic arrest would similarly protect ATRi-treated cells following telomere oxidative damage, as observed with G2 arrest. To accommodate the fact that prolonged mitotic arrest increases genome and specifically, telomere instability, we synchronized cells prior to treatment (Fig. 5D and Supplementary Fig. 6)32. Cells were treated with 50 ng/ml nocodazole for only 5 h to maintain a low MN background, as holding for 10–24 h caused massive genome instability in U2OS cells. U2OS and RPE FAP-TRF1 cells were synchronized with thymidine, treated with DL in mid-S phase, and then were treated with CDK1i to prevent G2-phase exit, or nocodazole to prevent M-phase exit. Five hours later, cells were released from the hold and given 8 h to complete division. With the chosen conditions, nocodazole alone did not raise MN levels compared to the UT control, and only modestly increased MN in RPE cells co-treated with VE822 (Fig. 5E, F). However, when cells in S-phase were treated with DL and ATRi, nocodazole significantly increased MN compared to cells treated with only dye and light and ATRi. Under these conditions, CDK1i did not rescue MN for U2OS cells (but also did not raise MN), perhaps due to the synchronization or presence of VE822 after release, but did rescue RPE cells. In total, our data suggest disruption of the G2/M checkpoint by ATRi allows cells with unresolved telomere damage to enter mitosis instead of repairing the damage in G2.

Replicating cells require ATR signaling following telomere oxidation

We noticed in Fig. 5 that cells treated with DL and ATRi synchronized in S-phase produced more MN than our previous experiments with unsynchronized cells. Telomeres represent a small (0.02%) portion of the genome, which likely means that when asynchronous FAP-TRF1 cells are treated with dye and light, only a fraction of telomeres are still enriched with 8oxoG when replication forks traverse them. To address this and further confirm the genome instability we’ve observed is due to replication stress, we synchronized cells at the G1/S boundary or mid S-phase prior to telomeric 8oxoG induction (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. 7). Compared to the asynchronous control, a 20-h thymidine block did not raise the background level of MN. The potential reasons for ATRi-induced MN following telomere replication stress are numerous. Previous studies have shown ATRi can induce dormant origin firing, replication fork degradation, nucleotide depletion, and premature mitotic entry33. Fork degradation is mediated by nucleases like MRE11 following reversal, but when ATR is inhibited, forks are overprocessed, leading to fork breakage34,35. However, MRE11 is required for replication fork restart, so we tested genome stability when ATR and MRE11 were co-inhibited following telomere damage. While MRE11i alone raised MN somewhat in both U2OS and HeLa FAP-TRF1 cells, we found a dramatic and significant increase in MN following telomere oxidation when ATR and MRE11 were inhibited (Fig. 6B).

We next tested the role of CDC7 following oxidative DNA damage to telomeres. CDC7 facilitates dormant origin firing when ATR is inhibited and also promotes DSBs and DSB signaling in hydroxyurea and ATRi-treated cells36,37. Given these two critical roles of CDC7 during replication stress, we treated synchronized cells with a well-characterized CDC7i (XL413) alongside VE82238. We used different doses of XL413 for HeLa and U2OS, as we found doses above 2 µM for HeLa or 0.1 µM for U2OS significantly raised background MN in our experiments. Using the selected doses, CDC7i alone had no effect on MN in either cell line (Fig. 6C). However, when XL413 was added with VE822, we saw a significant reduction in MN compared to VE822 alone.

We also tested the effect of WEE1i on synchronized U2OS cells. While WEE1 is most known for its role in regulating mitotic entry, it also has roles during S-phase39–41. As in Fig. 4, WEE1i induced a weak MN phenotype following telomere oxidation. In contrast to ATRi, there was no increased effect when cells were damaged at G1/S or mid-S phase (Supplementary Fig. 7C). However, CDC7i again rescued the MN phenotype following oxidative telomere damage, suggesting CDC7 activity is counter to genome stability when telomere RSR is dysregulated40.

Oxidative telomere damage requires RPA recruitment for genome stability

While there is a report of replication stress causing dormant origin firing in telomeres42, we speculated that the reason CDC7i rescued genome stability was due to the availability of RPA. Seminal work has shown ATRi results in insufficient levels of RPA available to handle replication stress, due in part to dormant origin firing37,43. To investigate how CDC7 impacts RPA localization to telomeres following oxidative damage, we performed IF/FISH and labeled replicating cells with EdU. As in Fig. 1, we found increased RPA at telomeres 3 h following 8oxoG induction, and this was specific to S-phase cells (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Fig. 8B). While ATRi again caused no increase in telomeric RPA compared to DL control cells after 3 h, WEE1i and CDC7i displayed no change comparing UT to DL. However, combined ATRi and CDC7i treatment resulted in significantly more telomeric RPA foci compared to ATRi alone following DL. This combination did not change the number of telomeric 53BP1 foci compared to ATRi alone (Fig. 7B) and was not observed with WEE1i plus CDC7i. Twenty-four hours after 8oxoG induction, DL control cells resolved the telomeric RPA phenotype, while ATRi DL cells still had a significant increase, and ATRi/CDC7i treated cells had significantly more telomeric RPA than ATRi alone (Fig. 7C). This effect was specific to S-phase cells, as non-S phase ATRi/CDC7i treated cells did not have a significant increase in telomeric RPA foci compared to ATRi cells (Fig. 7D). Discussion

Selective perturbation of cancer cells over non-diseased tissue is paramount for comprehensive cancer care. Tremendous advancements have been made using DNA-damaging treatments such as platinum, nucleotide analogs, and ionizing radiation; however, they lack the ability to selectively impact cancer cells44. More targeted therapies using inhibitors of the DDR, such as PARP inhibitors, have gained significant use in homologous recombination-deficient tumors because they exploit a vulnerability specific to the cancer, elevating their sensitivity to DNA damage45.

Here, we find that by inducing replication stress at telomeres through oxidative DNA damage, we can sensitize cancer cells to low-dose ATR and CHK1 inhibition. We validated our findings using two ATR and CHK1 inhibitors at different doses, in 4 cancer cell lines. Additionally, we used two lines each, that maintain their telomeres with telomerase, or the ALT pathway to ensure there was no dependence on telomere maintenance mechanism (Fig. 1). Coincident with the increase in genome instability, we found reduced cancer cell survival and cell division, and persistent telomere dysfunction after 8oxoG induction and ATRi treatment, suggesting this treatment paradigm could be effective in stunting cancer growth in patients (Fig. 2).

Importantly, while non-cancerous, otherwise normal cell lines senesce following telomeric 8oxoG induction, they were unaffected further by low doses of ATRi (Fig. 3). This presents a possible therapeutic window in which targeted replication stress induction in cancer cells, in combination with low-dose ATRi, could cause cancer cell death, while leaving normal tissue unharmed. We hypothesized that the reason normal cells were less affected by this combination treatment was due to intact p53 signaling. Indeed, knockout of p53 significantly sensitized these cells, indicating a cytoprotective function of p53 in the context of telomere replication stress and ATR inhibition. Interestingly, 3 of the cancer lines we studied have impaired (U2OS) or absent (HeLa and SAOS) p53 signaling. The only exception being the LOX melanoma line, which also had the weakest response to ATRi and dye and light treatment46. We speculate that p53 may have a similar importance in modulating resistance to telomere replication stress in cancer cells, but is just more often than not dysregulated.

While ATRi reduces S and G2-phase cells 24 h after treatment, telomeric 8oxoG does not detectably impact this specific phenotype in cancer cells. Instead, we suspected ATRi allows cells to progress into mitosis prematurely, before resolving DNA damage or under-replicated DNA arising from telomere replication stress. Supporting this, promoting mitotic entry with WEE1 inhibition increased MN after oxidative damage to telomeres, and the increase in MN after ATRi could be rescued by FOXM1 knockdown. FOXM1 promotes premature mitotic entry in cells treated with ATRi or CHK1i, so we conclude that ATRi allowed cells with unresolved telomere replication intermediates to enter mitosis, which leads to chromosomal instability27. Consistent with this, we observed an increase in the duration of mitosis in cells after 8oxoG induction and 0.2 µM VE822 treatment. Several recent studies have shown prolonged mitosis after replication stress, and this correlates with increased genome instability and or cell death and arrest30,31,47. Furthermore, earlier work has shown that a telomere-specific cohesion defect can also lead to prolonged mitosis12,48. While we have not assessed cohesion status in our system, it is tempting to speculate that the replication difficulties caused by telomeric 8oxoG may impair sister telomere separation in mitosis.

We surmised oxidative base damage to telomeres produces replication intermediates that, when transmitted to mitosis due to ATRi, activate the spindle assembly checkpoint, delaying mitotic progression. We tested this hypothesis by blocking premature G2-phase exit with a CDK1i before release into mitosis. Impressively, we found that this delay in mitotic entry in the face of ATRi rescued the genome instability phenotype. Moreover, we confirmed this was due to a G2-specific hold, and not a mitosis hold, by comparing CDK1i with nocodazole. Even though we optimized conditions so that VE822 and nocodazole alone or in combination did not increase MN, we saw a significant increase in MN in cells with telomeric 8oxoG treated with both drugs. This shows not only that prolonging mitosis after telomere damage does not allow for repair, but that it actually exacerbates the problem.

To understand mechanistically why ATRi caused genome instability after 8oxoG formation in telomeres, we turned to cell cycle synchronization. Previously, we showed that the DDR at telomeres after 8oxoG induction was blunted in serum-starved cells, which could not enter S-phase, demonstrating that the effects of telomeric 8oxoG observed were due to perturbed DNA replication23. Here, we enriched cells in G1/S and S-phase, and found telomeric 8oxoG- and ATRi-induced more MN than in asynchronous cells. This confirms that the MN observed in our study arises from replication stress, as enriching the replicating population increased the phenotype.

ATR inhibition induces dormant origin firing, prevents recruitment of repair factors to stalled forks, and disrupts cell cycle progression43,49–51. To test the relevance of these activities in our study, we used both a CDC7 and MRE11 inhibitor. Inhibiting CDC7 prevents the firing of dormant origins caused by ATR inhibition37, and MRE11i will inhibit stalled fork resection. We found that CDC7i rescued the genome instability caused by oxidative telomere damage and ATRi, but MRE11i increased instability. The MRE11i result indicates these stalled forks require some level of resection, or these unprocessed intermediates will cause genome instability upon division. Consistent with this, combined inhibition of MRE11 and CHK1 was shown to induce both premature mitotic entry and mitoses with DDR foci52. The CDC7i result suggests that when 8oxoG stalls forks at telomeres, the absence of ATR signaling induces dormant origin firing, which erroneously promotes instability. Alternatively, and not mutually exclusively, CDC7i could prevent the non-telomere-specific effects of ATRi, i.e., global dormant origin firing, thereby freeing up more replication resources, e.g., RPA, for the telomeres.

We tested the latter hypothesis by monitoring RPA localization to telomeres in S-phase cells. At both early and later timepoints, CDC7i allowed more RPA to localize to oxidized telomeres when ATR was inhibited. In contrast, non-telomeric RPA foci decreased with the combination treatment. This indicated that our genome instability findings following telomeric oxidative DNA damage and ATRi might be mediated by RPA availability. Indeed, we confirmed this by knocking down RPA1 in U2OS and HeLa FAP-TRF1 cells. Under these conditions, telomere oxidation, without any inhibitor added, caused significant MN, DDR-positive telomeres, and reduced growth compared to control cells. Our data suggest telomeres are sensitive to the availability of RPA, which has been observed in patients with RPA mutations53,54.

In conclusion, we have determined that targeted oxidative DNA damage to telomeres can sensitize cancer cells to low-dose ATR signaling inhibition. This has important relevance for cancer treatment, because the conditions used here show non-cancerous cells are resistant to low-dose ATRi, which may translate to a higher tolerance for patients. We predict that, at worst, non-cancer cells will senesce under these conditions, leaving them benign or mildly immunogenic. Conventional cancer therapies induce considerable oxidative stress (e.g., ionizing radiation, antimetabolites, photodynamic therapies, etc.). Since telomeres are hot-spots for oxidative DNA damage, ATR inhibition may synergize with these therapies until targeted telomere therapies become available. Therefore, it is critical that we continue to develop treatment paradigms that will sensitize cancer cells to potential therapeutics at doses that do not affect non-diseased cells.

Methods

Cell culture

FAP-mCer-TRF1 HeLa, U2OS (RRID:CVCL_0042), BJ hTERT (RRID:CVCL_6573), and LM216J cells were cultured in DMEM, RPE hTERT (RRID:CVCL_4388) in DMEM/F12, SAOS2 (RRID:CVCL_0548) in McCoy’s 5A, and LOX IMVI (RRID:CVCL_1381) in RPMI, all with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 500 µg/ml G418 to maintain FAP-mCer-TRF1. BJ cells received Cytiva Hyclone FBS while all others received Gibco Value FBS. All cells were cultured at 5% CO2 and O2 at 37 °C. All lines were generated in previous publications, where they were either directly purchased from ATCC or authenticated by a collaborater21–23. Cells were checked for contamination by microscopy regularly.

Cell treatments

For acute treatments with MG2I dye and light, cells were pre-treated with Opti-MEM (GIBCO) for 15 min at 37 °C, followed by treatment with 100 nM MG2I dye for 15 min. Cells were then exposed to a high-intensity 660 nm LED light at 153 mW cm−2, as previously described. For a 5-min treatment, this results in ~46 J cm−2 of energy. Lightbox specifications were as described previously, except the glass stage was replaced with a holder for a culture flask or multi-well dish, the light path was 11 cm, and another fan was added for heat dissipation55. MG2I was provided by Brigitte F. Schmidt at Carnegie Mellon University or TargetMOL.

ATR, ATM, CDC7, CHK1, CDK1, MRE11, and WEE1 inhibitors were added after dye and light treatment unless otherwise indicated. Except for CDC7i XL413, which was PBS, all drugs were dissolved in DMSO at 10 mM, and diluted in media for treatments from aliquots.

siRNA was transfected using RNAiMax (Thermo), and DL was performed 48 h after transfection. Information for siRPA1 (s12127) and siFOXM1 (s5250) can be found at the manufacturer’s website (Thermo).

Cell growth

Cells were plated in 6-well plates at 2 × 104 per well. Cells were treated with dye and light the next day, and drugs were added after. Cells recovered 4 days with drug, and then were counted with Denovix Cell Drop. Each condition had technical duplicate or triplicates.

Colony survival

Cells were plated at low density (1–2 × 102). Cells were treated with dye and light the next day, and drugs were added after. Cells recovered for 7–10 days with the drug and then were fixed with ice-cold methanol on ice and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. After washing with water and drying, colonies were manually counted. Each condition had technical duplicates or triplicates.

Micronuclei quantification

Approximately 1 × 105 cells were seeded on a 22 × 22 mm cover glass. The next day, cells were treated as indicated and typically recovered for 24 h with the drug. Cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde diluted in PBS and stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole DAPI diluted in water before mounting. Each sample was imaged at 40x at 7-25 fields of view, depending on cell density, on a Ti2 Eclipse widefield microscope (Nikon) equipped with a Spectra III Light Engine (Lumencore) and ORCA-Fusion BT Digital sCMOS camera (Hamamatsu). Cells were counted automatically with Nikon Elements Bio Analysis Cell Count Module, and micronuclei were manually counted. Each sample had 300–1000 cells counted.

Immunofluorescence microscopy and FISH

Cells were seeded and treated similarly for the MN quantification. For RPA and TRF2 staining, cells were pre-treated with CSK extraction buffer on ice (100 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 300 mM glucose, 10 mM Pipes pH 6.8, 0.5% Triton X-100), before fixing. Cells were fixed on ice in cold methanol, and blocked with 10% normal goat serum, 1% BSA and 0.1% Triton X in PBS, 1 h RT. Cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. The next day, cells were washed three times with PBS-T before incubating with secondary antibodies and washing three times again with PBS-T. For FISH, the cells were refixed with 4% formaldehyde, rinsed with 1% BSA in PBS and then dehydrated with 70%, 90% and 100% ethanol for 5 min. Telomeric PNA probe was diluted 1:100 (PNABio) prepared in 70% formamide, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 1× Maleic Acid buffer, 1× MgCl2 buffer, and boiled for 5 min before returning to ice. Coverslips were then hybridized in humid chambers at room temperature for 2 h or overnight at 4 °C. The cells were washed twice with 70% formamide and 10 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.5, three times with PBS-T, then rinsed in water before staining with DAPI and mounting. Images were acquired with a 60× oil objective and deconvolved before analysis with Nikon AR software.

Live-cell imaging

Cells were seeded in glass-bottom 6-well plates and recovered overnight. After treatment, cells were swapped to phenol red-free DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS with or without an ATR inhibitor for imaging and placed onto the preincubated microscope stage. Images were acquired every 6 min for 36 h using an RFP filter set (20 ms exposure, 5% laser power) and differential interference contrast channels (DIC, 100 ms exposure). 2 × 2 images were stitched using NIS Elements, with a 2% overlap stitching performed in the RFP channel.

Raw images were processed using a custom filter to identify mitotic cells in an unbiased manner. This filter identified rounded cells above a diameter threshold by the identification of high-contrast circular spots in the DIC channel. The first 2 h of imaging were discarded to allow cells to adjust to the stage-top incubator. The remaining 34 h were split into five equal periods of 408 min each. The first ten in-focus cells from the five time periods that were identified by the mitotic filter were selected for analysis to ensure even sampling across the entire imaging duration. Identified cells were then analyzed manually with the following events recorded: NEBD, metaphase, anaphase and G1 begin. These events were defined: NEBD – condensation of the nucleus visualized with H2B-mCherry; Metaphase- bright vertical alignment of H2B-mCherry signal; Anaphase- first frame of separation of the H2B-mCherry signal from the vertical alignment seen in metaphase in previous frames; G1 begin- completion of cytokinesis indicated by a relaxation of the plasma membrane or diffusion of the mCherry signal. The time point at which these events occurred was recorded for each cell in each experimental condition and transformed into event duration times, with reported values from the 50 analyzed cells pooled. The number of MN produced from each division was also recorded. Three biological replicates were analyzed for each indicated experimental condition.

Immunoblotting

Cells were collected by scraping on ice and were washed with PBS. Each pellet was lysed on ice with 1x RIPA buffer supplemented with 1x Halt Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitors, 1 nM PMSF, and Benzonase for 15 min and 37 °C for 10 min. After centrifugation, protein concentrations were determined with the BCA assay, and 15–50 µg was electrophoresed on Bis-Tris gels. Protein was transferred to PDVF membranes, blocked with 5% milk and incubated with primary antibody overnight. Licor fluorescent secondary antibodies were applied the next day, and the signal was detected on Licor Odyssey.

Flow cytometry

Cell cycle

Cells were trypsinized and washed with PBS before fixing with ice-cold 70% ethanol for at least 3 h. After fixation, cells were washed once with PBS and resuspended in FxCycleTM PI/RNase Staining Solution (Invitrogen) and incubated for 15-30 min according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were analyzed using an Attune NxT acoustic focusing flow cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Gating was performed using FlowJo (BD Biosciences).

EdU/pH3 cell cycle

Cells were plated in 60- or 100-mm dishes at 0.5–1 × 10⁶ cells per dish. The next day, cells were treated with dye, light, and an ATR inhibitor. After 24 h, cells were incubated with 20 µM EdU for 1 h. Cells were washed with PBS, detached with trypsin, and centrifuged. Cell pellets were fixed and permeabilized according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). Cells were incubated overnight with primary antibody against pH3, followed by Alexa Fluor Plus 647-conjugated secondary antibody (Invitrogen) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. The Click-iT™ reaction was performed using the Click-iT™ EdU Alexa Fluor™ 488 Flow Cytometry Assay Kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After labeling, cells were resuspended in FxCycle™ PI/RNase Staining Solution (Invitrogen) and incubated for 20 min at room temperature. Cells were acquired on an Attune NxT acoustic focusing cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed using FlowJo (BD Biosciences).

Cell death

2 × 105 cells were plated in 60 mm dishes. The next day, cells were treated with dye and light, followed by the addition of an ATR inhibitor. After 96 h, the supernatant was collected, and cells were washed with PBS, detached with trypsin, and centrifuged. Cell pellets were resuspended in 100 µL of 1X binding buffer (Invitrogen). 10 µL of the Annexin V, fluorescein conjugate (FITC annexin V) (Invitrogen), and 10 µL of the propidium iodide with RNAse (50 µg/mL) were added, and cells were incubated in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. After the incubation, 400 µL of binding buffer was added. Cells were acquired using an Attune NxT acoustic focusing cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and analyzed with FlowJo (BD Biosciences).

CellTrace Violet

Cell trace violet experiments were performed as per the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo). In brief, the cell trace violet reagent was diluted 1:2000 in OptiMem and added to cells 15 min before the 5-min 660 nm light exposure for a total incubation time of 20 min. Cells were washed with serum-containing media as instructed to remove unbound dye before the addition of fresh media. Cells were collected approximately an hour later for day 0 reference, with additional collections taken every 24 h for the next 4 days. Cells were acquired using a Cytek Aurora cytometer (Cytek) and analyzed with FlowJo (BD Biosciences).
