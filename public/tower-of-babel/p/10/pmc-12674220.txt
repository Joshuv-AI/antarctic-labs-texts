InDel mutations within the bovine PER2 gene are significantly associated with reproductive traits
Authors: Yuanzhe Yang, Jiajun Song, Siyuan Shen, Yongsheng Wang, Chuanying Pan, Jiyao Wu, Xianyong Lan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFunctioning as a key regulator of circadian rhythms, the PER2 gene exerts a substantial impact on the reproductive traits of animals. However, the effect of the PER2 gene on ovarian development remains unclear. In order to examine the relationship between bovine reproductive trait and the PER2 gene, a total of 901 ovarian samples were collected, categorized into different oestrus cycles (proestrus, oestrus, post-oestrus, anoestrous), and subjected to analysis for two potential insertion/deletions (InDels) in the PER2 gene. Through agarose gel electrophoresis and DNA sequencing, two polymorphic deletion mutations (P2-D5-bp, P3-D13-bp) were identified. Furthermore, a significant association between mature follicle diameter and P2-D5-bp was found (P < 0.05). Additionally, several significant correlations with ovarian length, width, height, and white body diameter were found for P3-D13-bp (P < 0.05). These findings suggested that the bovine PER2 gene plays an important role in above-mentioned reproductive traits, offering new avenues for improving cow fertility through marker-assisted selection (MAS).Keywords: Bovine, PER2, insertion/deletion (InDel), ovarian traits, corpus albicans

Introduction

In recent years, the consistent uptick in the demand for premium-quality dairy products such as milk, leading to the burgeoning population of cows and the expansion of agricultural operations. However, a persistent emphasis on maximizing milk yield in dairy cow production has resulted in an energy imbalance, adversely impacting the reproductive traits of dairy cows. Consequently, it is crucial to further investigate the intricate and multifaceted mechanisms underlying the genetic negative correlation between milk lactation and reproductive behaviour in dairy cows, in order to foster the development of economically optimal breeding herds.1 Traditional breeding methods are encumbered by inherent limitations, including extended generation intervals, sluggish progress, low efficiency and inefficient utilization of germplasm resources in dairy cattle breeding. Sole reliance on conventional breeding methods proves impractical for achieving comprehensive genetic improvement across the population.2 Conversely, molecular breeding techniques, such as marker-assisted selection (MAS), have garnered widespread application due to their inherent advantages of heightened efficiency and accelerated breeding progress. These techniques hold particular value in the screening of pivotal traits in economically significant animals.3,4

The substantial enhancement in fertility among lactating cows is primarily linked to the regulation of the oestrus environment, follicle and corpus luteum development, as well as the precise timing of ovulation and insemination.5,6 The coordination of ovarian development is intricately governed by the circadian system, and its cyclical progression entails a series of physiological stages comprising follicle growth, maturation, ovulation, and corpus luteum formation.7 Furthermore, processes such as the oestrous cycle, embryo development, and the production and maturation of sperm are all subject to the influence of circadian rhythms.8,9 These rhythms manifest across various cell types in mammals. The mammalian circadian clock system originates from the occurrence of circadian rhythms within a self-regulating negative feedback transcription network, predominantly regulated by the transcriptional activators BMAL1 and CLOCK, alongside the repressors PER1-3 and CRY1-2.10

Research indicates that PER1 and PER2 play a crucial role in regulating circadian rhythms, with the circadian expression of clock genes PER1 and PER2 observed in rat ovaries.11 Disruptions in circadian rhythms have been linked to significant abnormalities in ovulation, fertility, and other aspects in rats.12 PER2 affects follicular development and oocyte maturation through the mTOR pathway in the mouse ovary.13 Constant light stimulation before pregnancy led to circadian rhythm disruption, and down-regulation of PER2 gene expression led to impaired ovarian function in mice.14 However, limited research has been conducted on the PER2 gene in the bovine ovarian phenotype. The use of PER2-specific small interfering RNA has been shown to inhibit PER2 expression and stimulate the expression of the ovulation-related gene Star in cow granulosa cells.15 Melatonin supplementation during the summer months can increase the expression of the PER2 gene in the placenta of cows.16

As a regulator of circadian rhythm, the PER2 gene plays a pivotal role in ovarian development. Despite its acknowledged significance, research on the involvement of the PER2 gene in dairy cows remains limited. Addressing this gap, we conducted a study using a total of 901 ovary samples from Holstein cows to investigate the relationship between the PER2 gene and reproductive traits. Our findings aim to contribute valuable insights to the breeding of cows.

Materials and methods

Statement

All experimental procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines of the Faculty Animal Policy and Welfare Committee of Northwest A&F University (protocol no. NWAFU- 314020038) for the use and care of animals in research. The factors of age and pluriparity were not used in general linear model because data about age and pluriparity was not collected.

Collection of ovarian tissue samples from cows

Based on the recommendations of the Regulations for the Administration of Affairs Concerning Experimental Animals of China, the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Northwest A&F University approved all animal experiments.

In this work, a total of 901 ovary samples were collected from healthy adult Holstein cows in the slaughterhouse, and all individuals were taken under the same feeding and management conditions. Indirectly judge the estrus stage of dairy cows by the presence or absence of follicles and corpus luteum, and corpus luteum typing, and measure the phenotypic data of ovaries, mature follicles and corpus albicans, including length, width, height and weight of ovary, diameter of follicle and corpus albicans. Weigh the ovaries with a sterile electronic scale, and measure the length, width, height, and diameter of the sample with a vernier caliper or double ruler.

Extraction of genomic DNA

Total genomic DNA was extracted from the collected ovarian tissues stored at −80 °C by high-salt method. The purity of the extracted DNA samples was checked using a Thermo NanoDrop 1000 according to the A260/A280 ratio, and then the DNA samples were diluted to a concentration of 20 ng/µl and temporarily stored at −20 °C for subsequent experiments.17

Primer designing

Based on cow PER2 retrieved from the NCBI database (GenBank accession number NC_037330.1) gene reference sequence and information obtained from the Ensembl database (http://asia.ensembl.org/index.html), three primer pairs were designed using NCBI primer blast (Table 1) and synthesized by Sangon Bioengineering Co., Ltd. Identification of InDel loci, genotyping and DNA sequencing

The InDel identification and genotyping were performed using the touch-down polymerase chain reaction (TD-PCR) method.18 PCR volume (13 μL) containing 6.5 μL × Eco Taq PCR Supermix (+dye), 0.5 μL each of forward and reverse primers, 0.8 μL genomic DNA, and 4.7 μL ddH2O. The PCR product was electrophoresed using 3.5% agarose gel, and then stained with 1.0 × TBE mixed with nucleic acid stain to identify the genotype of the InDel locus. Two representative samples were randomly selected from each genotype for sequencing, and sequencing was performed by Sangon Bioengineering Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) to verify the mutations.

Statistical analysis

The Genotype and allele frequencies, Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) and performed linkage disequilibrium (LD) for PER2 InDel loci were calculated using the SHEsis platform (http://analysis.bio-x.cn). PopGene (version 1.3.1) was used to calculate heterozygosity (He), homozygosity (Ho) and polymorphism information content (PIC) (Table 2). Using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM, USA), we used variance (t-test) to analyse the relationship between bovine PER2 gene variation and ovary, mature follicle and white body traits. We found that there are two sites (P2-D5-bp and P3-D13-bp) that are polymorphic. The generalized linear model was used to determine the relationship between InDel mutation and ovarian, mature follicle and corpus albicans diameter traits in 901 dairy cows using ovarian, mature follicle and corpus albicans diameter phenotype data at the P2-D5-bp and P3-D13-bp respectively association. The least-square was used to determine the correlation between different InDel genotypes and traits of ovary, mature follicles and white body, the adjusted linear model was Yij=µ+Gi+Eij, where Yij is the measured characteristic of each animal, µ is the grand mean, Gi is the genotypic effect, and Eij is the random error. Furthermore, the results were statistically significant when the p-value < 0.05. All statistical tests were performed as two-sided tests.

Results

Identification of InDel polymorphism of the bovine PER2 gene

The experimental protocol is shown in Fig. 1. By combining testing pooled DNA samples and those in the Ensembl database, two InDel locus with polymorphism, NC_037330.1: g.117576801-117576805 del GTTTT (5-bp) and NC_037330.1: g.117578013-117578025 del TCTCCCTTCTCAG (13-bp), which were named as P2-D5-bp (rs597171453 rs378689060) and P3-D13-bp (rs480531769). The bovine PER2 gene structure is shown in Fig. 2A. The 5-bp InDel generated two genotypes: homozygote insertion (insertion/insertion, II, 191 bp) and heterozygote (insertion/deletion, ID, 191 and 186 bp). The 13-bp InDel also generated two genotypes: homozygote insertion (insertion/insertion, II, 176 bp) and heterozygote (insertion/deletion, ID, 176 and 163 bp). They were identified by 3.5% agarose gel electrophoresis and sequence diagrams of these two mutation sites (Fig. 2B and C).

Genotypic and allelic frequencies and population indices

After genotyping the amplified products of the P2-D5-bp locus from 901 ovarian DNA Samples and the P3-D13-bp locus from 866 ovarian DNA Samples, the genotype and allele frequencies were calculated. As shown in Table 2, both in the two variant locus, the frequency of the wild-type genotype (II) was higher than ID genotype. Meanwhile, The P3-D13-bp locus identified in PER2 conforms to the HWE (P > 0.05), but P2-D5-bp did not conform to the HWE (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the value of the polymorphic information content (PIC) for two locus were 0.156 and 0.112 respectively, indicating that the investigated population belonged to low-degree PIC (0.00 < PIC < 0.25).

Associations between P2-D5-bp InDel of PER2 and ovarian phenotypic traits of the bovine

We performed correlation analysis between the ­genotypes of P2-D5-bp and ovarian phenotypic traits of the bovine. Before that, all tested individuals were indirectly judge the oestrus stage of dairy cows (anoestrous, proestrus, oestrus and post-oestrus) by the presence or absence of follicles and corpus luteum, and corpus luteum typing. As shown in Table 3, these genotypes were not significantly associated with ovarian length in oestrus, ovarian height in proestrus, or number of corpus luteum in post-oestrus. However, it is worth noting that the P2-D5-bp was significantly associated with mature follicle diameter (P = 0.005) in oestrus and corpus albicans diameter (P = 0.024) in anoestrous.

The results show that there is a strong linkage between P2-D5-bp and P3-D13-bp. Discussion

Based on the laboratory research on the biological clock gene member CYR2 gene is closely related to the reproductive performance of livestock,19 the key role of the biological clock gene member PER2 gene in regulating circadian rhythm and the literature review were carried out, so the PER2 gene was selected to explore the effect of its mutation on cattle reproductive traits. Two InDel mutations, namely P2-D5-bp and P3-D13-bp, were identified in Holstein cows, with each mutation exhibiting only two genotypes. It can be inferred that there are no DD homozygous mutations in the screened individuals, or as observed in another study, DD homozygous individuals may be lethal, thus remaining undetected in the population..20 However, the P2-D5-bp InDel locus in PER2 does not conform to Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, possibly due to artificial selection, and further research is needed to determine the specific reasons. The correlation analysis of PER2 mutations with ovarian, corpus luteum, and mature follicle traits reveals significant associations. Specifically, the P2-D5-bp mutation in the PER2 gene shows a significant correlation with the diameter of mature follicles during oestrus and the diameter of the corpus luteum during non-oestrus in Holstein cows. Additionally, the P3-D13-bp mutation in the PER2 gene is significantly associated with ovarian length, post-oestrus ovarian height, ovarian width, and corpus luteum diameter in Holstein cows during oestrus.

Herein, individuals with the P2-D5-bp mutation and the ID genotype exhibited significantly longer mature follicle diameter during oestrus compared to individuals with the II genotype. Conversely, ID individuals had smaller corpus luteum diameter during oestrus compared to individuals with the II genotype. At the same time, individuals with the P3-D13-bp mutation and the ID genotype showed significantly greater ovarian width during oestrus compared to individuals with the II genotype. Additionally, ID genotype individuals had smaller corpus luteum diameter than II genotype individuals, and post-oestrus, ID genotype individuals had significantly lower ovarian length and height compared to II genotype individuals. Therefore, we speculate that mutations in the PER2 gene affect the cyclical development of the ovaries, promoting the development of mature follicles. The mutation at the P3-D13-bp locus increases the width of the ovaries during non-estrus while decreasing the length and height of the ovaries post-oestrus. There were no significant differences in ovarian length between the two genotypes during the pre-oestrus period, but the ovarian length in ID genotype individuals was also slightly shorter. This suggests that the mutation at the P3-D13-bp locus of the PER2 gene leads to changes in ovarian morphology during different oestrous cycles. This finding is consistent with some studies indicating a significant connection between clock genes and follicular development.21,22 The reproductive rhythm of mammals is a complex and delicate process, which is jointly regulated by multiple factors. Among them, the central system oscillator and clock genes expressed in reproductive organs play crucial roles. Clock genes rhythmically express in ovarian granulosa cells and theca cells, regulating the expression of genes such as LH receptors and steroidogenic enzymes, influencing follicle development and ovulation timing.23,24

According to the results, two possible pathways can be inferred. The first potential pathway is that genetic variations in the PER2 gene may significantly impact ovarian phenotypes and related traits, including corpus luteum and mature follicle characteristics. It has been observed that the expression of clock genes influences luteinizing hormone receptor (LHR) and progesterone production in granulosa cells of bovine ovaries.15 LH, as a major ovulation-inducing hormone and regulator of luteal function, exerts its effects through the LHR receptor, playing a crucial role in the cyclical activity of the ovaries.25 LHR, a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR), is part of the rhodopsin-like receptor family.26 The necessary circadian rhythm for reproduction is regulated by clock genes,27 and ovarian biological clock is tightly controlled by the surge of LH, which is essential for the regulation of the ovarian circadian timing system.28,29 The period between luteinization and the surge of LH is essential for the development of follicles and oocytes. As the estrous cycle progresses, pre-ovulatory follicles undergo an increase in LH, leading to the expression of estrus and an endogenous LH surge, thereby influencing the follicular environment and preparing oocytes. This, in turn, provides optimal oocyte competence for maturation and subsequent embryonic development.30,31 Therefore, the potential first pathway involves influencing the regulation of LH hormones, affecting ovarian development, and leading to variations in ovarian phenotypes during different oestrous cycles.

The second possible pathway is as follows: studies have shown that middle-aged female mice with mutations in the clock gene PER2 exhibit disrupted oestrous cycles, potentially accelerating aging and ultimately leading to a decline in female fertility.32,33 Mice lacking PER1 or PER2 also display shorter circadian rhythms34 and the common mutations of them can disrupt circadian rhythms during locomotor activity and the expression of key clock genes.35 Additionally, mutations in clock genes can lead to oestrous cycle defects, possibly due to the timing or coordination of gonadotropin-releasing hormone release and the surge of LH during oestrus, causing a decrease in oestrogen and progesterone levels.36 The development of ovarian follicles is also accompanied by rich and active vascular generation activity. Studies have demonstrated that PER2 mutations can impair endothelial function, and we suspect that PER2 may also affect the generation of ovarian vascular endothelial cells, thereby hindering ovarian development and accelerating aging.37,38 Therefore, the second possible pathway involves PER2 gene mutations causing oestrous cycle disruptions and accelerated aging, thereby affecting ovarian development and leading to changes in phenotype-associated traits.

Two InDel variants, P2-D5-bp and P3-D13-bp, both located in the intron of the PER2 gene, were identified in Holstein cows. InDel mutations hold biological significance as they can impact gene structure, gene expression, gene replication, and pre-mRNA splicing, among other processes.39 Introns constitute more than half of the noncoding genome and play crucial roles in regulation and evolution. Despite being non-coding regions, intronic regions contain numerous cis-acting elements that bind to trans-acting factors, thereby regulating gene transcription.40 For instance, two InDel mutations in the intronic region of the Cry2 gene were significantly associated with litter size in AuW sheep, influencing reproduction.19 Similarly, InDels located in intronic regions within the VISFATIN gene are linked to growth traits in two Chinese native cattle breeds.41 Another example includes a 19-bp mutation in the intron region of the PLAG1 gene, which was associated with the distribution of height and geographic location of Chinese cattle breeds.42

Through linkage disequilibrium analysis and haplotype analysis, we can see that there is a strong linkage between P2-D5-bp and P3-D13-bp, and haplotype IP1-IP2 has the highest frequency. Therefore, we speculated that haplotype IP1-IP2 has a very important effect on the reproductive performance of dairy cows. We used a large enough sample size (n = 901) to make the results more reliable.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study revealed a significant association between two InDel mutations within the bovine PER2 gene and ovary morphological traits, including mature follicle diameter and corpus albicans diameter. These findings warrant further research to comprehensively assess the influence of the PER2 gene on bovine reproduction and fertility. Subsequently, such investigations could offer novel insights for leveraging the PER2 gene in MAS breeding strategies to enhance reproductive fertility in cattle.
