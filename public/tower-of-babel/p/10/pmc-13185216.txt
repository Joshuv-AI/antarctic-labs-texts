Development of a Clinical Severity Score for Indian Sickle Cell Anaemia Patients
Authors: Suraj S Nongmaithem, Amitabh Biswas, Swaroop Iyer, Jandhayala Vyshnavi, Archana Wath, Giriraj R Chandak, Dipty Jain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSickle cell anaemia (SCA) is a monogenic disorder but shows variable disease severity. We evaluated the utility of paediatric severity scores (PSS) in Indian patients and developed two modified scores: Indian Severity Score 1 (ISS1) by adding four clinical parameters to PSS, and ISS2 by excluding four biochemical measures from ISS1.MethodClinical details of 171 SCA patients were collected from hospital records and categorized into mild, moderate, and severe groups by two clinical experts. Same patients were randomly assigned to three training sets—R1, R2 and R3 (N = 86 each) and severity cut‐off values for PSS, ISS1 and ISS2 were defined to assign them into mild, moderate, and severe categories. The scores were tested on the remaining three random testing set patients, R1, R2 and R3 (N = 85 each), and overall concordance with clinical expert categorization was assessed.ResultsThe concordance of PSS was 66.7%, while ISS1 and ISS2 were 82.8% and 85.1%, respectively. In all three severity scoring models, the highest concordance was observed in mild (81%–100%) followed by severe (57%–85%), and the lowest in the moderate groups (10%–48%).ConclusionOverall, we have developed a robust, population‐specific severity score for Indian SCA patients, highlighting the importance of including population‐specific clinical features. Introduction

Sickle cell anaemia (SCA) is caused by a single point mutation (p.Glu6Val) in the beta globin gene (HBB). The highest disease burden exists in India and the African countries, including the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Nigeria [1, 2]. Although SCA is caused by a single mutation, the clinical manifestations and disease severity are highly heterogeneous, ranging from a near normal life to early mortality [3, 4]. Based on recent research and clinical definitions, the disease complications can be classified into two main overlapping pathogenic categories: the haemolytic phenotype leading to leg ulcers, pulmonary hypertension, stroke, and nephropathy; and the vaso‐occlusive phenotype causing acute chest syndrome, vaso‐occlusive crises (VOCs), avascular necrosis and liver damage and so on [5, 6]. Indian SCA patients exhibit a relatively milder disease phenotype, typically associated with higher baseline foetal haemoglobin levels. Furthermore, certain clinical features such as priapism, leg ulcers, pulmonary hypertension and so on, that are commonly observed in African populations, are rare among Indian patients [7]. In addition, several non‐genetic factors, including nutritional deficiencies, a high burden of infectious diseases, and environmental stressors, may independently exacerbate anaemia and thereby contribute to differences in disease presentation and management [8]. Finally, Indian patients with SCA are known to respond to lower doses of hydroxyurea (mg/kg body weight) and rarely require the maximum tolerated doses that are commonly needed in non‐Indian populations. Considering the heterogeneity of disease manifestations, management of SCA is a big challenge for clinicians and difficult for patients to cope with. Thus, there is a need for better assessment of clinical parameters and development of a tool for the prediction of future clinical course.

There are many important factors that help in understanding the disease pathogenesis and deciding the course of disease [9, 10]. Based on these factors, patients can be categorized as mild, moderate and severe; thereby assisting the course of diagnosis and disease management [11]. Previous studies have attempted to design a method using available biochemical parameters and clinical events that can categorize SCA patients into different disease severity groups [12, 13, 14]. Based on disease prognosis, Sebastiani et al. developed the disease severity score (DSS) model that can be used to compute a personalized DSS, allowing therapeutic decisions to be made [12]. A paediatric score system (PSS) developed using 12 parameters (8 clinical and 4 biochemical parameters) has shown significant differences in the scores for mild, moderate and severely affected SCA patients (Table 1) [14]. However, these scores have not been evaluated in Indian SCA patients, and no such score exists for them. Such a severity scoring system helps in decision‐making, where the clinician can track the progress of disease and its complications in the patients over a period of time and decide on modifying hydroxyurea dose. It will also help in evaluating the response of any new drugs based on the baseline severity and on further follow‐up.

This study was initiated with the aim to develop a method that may predict the severity outcome of Indian SCA patients to assist the clinician in better disease management. We applied the PSS developed by Tweel et al. on young Indian SCA patients [14] and also modified various clinical and biochemical parameters included in the score, as suggested by expert clinicians based on their long‐term experience with the clinical phenotype, disease course and management of SCA patients. The modified scores were significantly better in identifying the severity status of the patients, confirming the need for a population‐specific severity score for better care of SCA patients.

Methods

Patient Recruitment and Data Collection

SCA patients attending the Outpatient Department of Government Medical College and Hospital, Nagpur, were recruited after obtaining written informed consent. Informed consent was taken from all the patients, and the study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of CSIR‐CCMB (No.IEC‐65‐R1/2019, dated 10 March 2021). The inclusion criteria were patients aged 0–18 years, diagnosed with SCA based on the SS pattern on high‐performance liquid chromatography analysis. A total of 171 patients (82 females and 89 males) were evaluated for the severity index. No biological sample was freshly collected for any investigations related to this study. Medical records from the hospital were utilized to collate the clinical data and laboratory investigations. Overall, the study design is shown in Figure 1. Details of clinical parameters used for the generation of severity score are given in Table 1, and clinical characteristics of the patients are shown in Table 2. The number of recurrences of SCA‐related complications over the last 2 years was documented. Based on their prior experience and data from the patients' medical records, two seasoned paediatricians (DJ and SI) with expertise in SCA independently classified the patients into three groups: mild, moderate, and severe. Any differences of opinion between the two experts were discussed and the final decision was made with mutual agreement.

Evaluation and Validation of Severity Score

We adopted the severity score system PSS, developed by Tweel et al., on Indian SCA patients [14]. The PSS score was generated using best‐weighted system including 12 clinical parameters (Table 1) in all 171 patients and tested its ability to categorize patients with respect to the clinical experts' classification. Out of the 12 clinical parameters, six were related to cumulative lifetime incidence of organ damage, two other parameters were related to the number of recurrences of SCA‐related complications over 2 years, and four were laboratory test values (Table 1). Out of three weight scoring systems—Scores A, B, and C [14], we adopted Score C weight system, where weights (range = 5–50 points) were assigned to the clinical parameters, according to the frequency and severity of clinical complications. Based on the generated score, patients were categorized into mild, moderate, and severe and compared with clinical categorization as mentioned above.

We modified the PSS by incorporating four additional clinical parameters to the existing 12 features and termed it as Indian Severity Score 1 (ISS1) (Figure 1 and Table 1). Weights were ascribed to each additional parameter based on the number of recurrences over the last 2 years. For instance, patients with no history of blood transfusion in the last 2 years were assigned the weight of ‘0’, while the weight was ‘5’ for patients with 1–2 episodes, and ‘10’ for more than 2 episodes of blood transfusions. Likewise, other parameters were also weighted as per the patients' clinical profile. Considering the fact that the biochemical parameters in SCA are sensitive to the clinical status of the patients and/or environmental factors, in the next step, we replaced all the biochemical parameters with relevant clinical parameters (8 from PSS and 4 additional parameters as mentioned in Table 1) and generated the Indian Severity Score 2 (ISS2).

We randomized the 171 SCA patients using rand() formula in Excel and divided them into two groups, training group (N = 86) and testing group (N = 85). Further, the patients in both groups were randomized into three independent sets—R1, R2, and R3 (Figure 1). We calculated descriptive statistics of three scoring systems‐ PSS, ISS1, and ISS2 in clinically classified mild, moderate, and severe category of patients in the training group. Based on the median and interquartile ranges (IQR) from three random training sets, we defined the cut‐off score values of PSS, ISS1, and ISS2 for categorization into mild, moderate, and severe groups (Table 3). The cut‐off score values of respective scoring systems were applied to the testing group (N = 85), and the patients were categorized as per the severity status. The severity score‐based groups were compared with the clinical expert‐categorized respective groups independently in each random testing set (Table 4). The overall concordance of severity score‐based and clinical expert categorization was calculated by combining the results from all three random tests for each score (Figure 1).

Results

Patient Characteristics

The clinical characteristics of the patients are given in Table 2. The average age at recruitment was 9.7 years. Instances of vaso‐occlusive or painful crises and recurrent blood transfusion were two most common complaints in the majority of the patients (Table 2). VOC was reported in 72 SCA patients, and blood transfusion in 76 SCA patients. Repeated hospitalizations due to severe anaemia (N = 38) and acute febrile illness (N = 35) were also very high among SCA patients (Table 2).

Figure 1 showed the overall study design, randomization of patients to Training and Testing sets, and overall performance of the scoring systems by comparison with clinical expert classification. Out of 171 patients, the clinical experts classified 109 (63.7%) patients as belonging to mild, 23 (13.5%) to moderate, and 39 (22.8%) to severe category. In three randomized training sets comprising 86 SCA patients each, the proportion of mild (60.5%, 62.8% and 72.1%), moderate (17.4%, 15.1%, and 11.6%), and severe (22.1%, 22.1%, and 16.3%) categories were overlapping (Table 3). Based on the median (IQR), we defined the PSS cut‐off value of 0–5 as mild, > 5–10 as moderate, and > 10 as severe category (Table 3), which were applied to the testing set of 85 patients. Distribution of SCA patients in three severity categories was checked for concordance with clinical expert classification. Overall, from three random sets of SCA patients, a 66.67% concordance was noted between PSS‐based categorization and the clinical expert classification (Table 4). The highest concordance was observed in mild (81.8%), followed by severe (56.9%), and the lowest in the moderate category (9.68%).

Based on the clinical relevance in Indian SCA patients, four additional clinical features were added to the 12 parameters included in the PSS, and a new score, ISS1 was generated (Table 1 and Section 2). For ISS1, a cut‐off value of 0–10 as mild, > 10 to 15 as moderate, and > 15 as severe (Table 3) was specified and applied to the testing set patients. The overall concordance between ISS1‐based and clinical expert‐defined categorization significantly improved to 82.75% (Table 4). While concordance in the mild category improved marginally (89.9%), a significant improvement was noted both in severe (84.6%) and the moderate group (41.9%) (Table 4).

Since biochemical parameters are sensitive to the clinical state of the patient (steady vs. crisis state) and other environmental factors, we replaced the four biochemical parameters in ISS1 with related clinical features and generated a new score, ISS2 (Table 1). Using the same cut‐off value points as for ISS1, we observed a marginal improvement for ISS2 in overall concordance with clinical expert classification (85.1%) (Table 3). Interestingly, a significant improvement was noted for mild (100%) and moderate (48.4%) categories, and a substantial drop of ∼18% was noted for the severe group of patients (66.1%) (Table 4).

Discussion

SCA is a global genetic disorder due to a single mutation in the beta globin gene; yet phenotypic heterogeneity is very common, making patient management challenging. Multiple severity scores have been proposed for SCA, combining clinical features and laboratory parameters to make a meaningful single entity that can confidently measure morbidity and/or mortality within a given time. Among several such, two scores, namely DSS [12] and paediatric severity score (PSS) [14] are commonly used, of which PSS is considered the most appropriate for SCD children of other ancestries. Differences in the clinical presentation and disease severity in patients from different ancestral populations demand population‐specific severity scores [15, 16, 17]. We tested the PSS in young Indian SCA patients and observed that it functions poorly in proper annotation of their severity status. Modified scores based on inclusion of additional clinical features prevalent in the Indian patients significantly improved the predictability. Further replacement of various haematological parameters with related clinical features made it more robust. Overall, we demonstrate that severity scores based on population‐specific clinical features allow more accurate identification of patients' severity status and thus empower clinicians to design disease management.

One of the first attempts to identify manifestations that can predict severe clinical course in SCA infants was made by Miller et al., who identified dactylitis, severe anaemia, and leucocytosis as three easily identifiable features [18]. However, it was Sebastiani et al., who developed the first severity score predicting the risk of death within 5 years [12]. PSS developed by Tweel et al. discriminates young SCA patients into mild, moderate, and severe categories that could help tailor the management and hydroxyurea therapy [14]. We observed that the PSS could correctly determine the severity status in only two/third of Indian SCA children, suggesting that certain unique clinical/biochemical parameters in Indian patients might be restricting the applicability of PSS for determining severity in Indian SCA patients. This could be related to very low occurrence of organ damage complications, which are assigned high scores in the PSS system, as identified in this study. Analysing the frequency of clinical features and lifetime cumulative incidence of long‐term complications, we observed the former to be highly variable in SCA patients in other ancestries. While recurrent painful crises were the most common symptom as in other populations, recurrent blood transfusion was another common consequence [16]. Further, severe anaemia and acute febrile sickness led to repeated hospitalizations in Indian SCA patients (Table 2). The inclusion of four frequently observed clinical features in Indian SCA patients to the PSS (referred to as ISS1) significantly improved the discrimination as per severity, confirming that these clinical features hold larger importance in Indian patients. We further argued that only a clinical features‐based score would empower the clinician to take immediate management decisions, since loss to follow‐up is a common problem. Hence, we replaced the four biochemical parameters—total haemoglobin, fetal haemoglobin, lactate dehydrogenase, and total leucocyte count included in the original PSS with clinical features representing the same status. The All Clinical parameters‐based severity score, ISS2, performed marginally better than the combined clinical and laboratory parameters‐based ISS1 and was highly concordant with the clinical expert classification. This indicates that population‐specific clinical parameters, which are routinely recorded during clinical examination, can be used to generate severity scores which can predict the future risk of clinical complications and mortality risk.

While both ISS1 and ISS2 provide an overall similar concordance with the clinical experts' categorization, it is interesting to note that ISS2 correctly predicts all the mild cases, while ISS1 performs much better in predicting severe cases. The concordance in severe cases drops by close to 20%, which has important implications as early intervention with hydroxyurea and regular follow‐up is the key to ensuring a good quality of life for SCA patients. This also confirms the established convention that biochemical and haematological parameters are better indicators of disease severity in SCA patients. However, it may be argued that all the misclassifications are between moderate and severe cases; both categories have largely similar management plans, and hence the difference between ISS1 and ISS2‐based grouping of the patients may not significantly impact the management decision. Overall, ISS2 can assist clinicians in making management decisions at the first point of contact, while ISS1, which shares overlapping clinical parameters with ISS2 and includes additional haematological parameters routinely used for monitoring hydroxyurea therapy, provides a complementary and effective combination.

The study has several strengths and few limitations. This is the first study on generation of clinical severity score based on long‐term observations on clinical symptoms and course in Indian SCA patients. Developing from an established score and involvement of SCA clinical experts, personally involved in the follow‐up of each patient, brings quality to these assessments. One of the limitations of the study is that a significant proportion of data is collected from the records, which include response or adherence to hydroxyurea therapy. Verbal inquiries regarding pill counts and missed doses were made on every follow‐up on a monthly basis, but we could not capture the adherence as per any standard validated questionnaire. However, involvement of well‐trained paediatricians ensures data integrity and robustness. Another limitation was the missing LDH levels in the majority of the patients, although all other parameters used in the paediatric severity score were available to us. It may also be noted that the severity scores have been generated on patients from one region of India, and clinical differences in patients from other regions in the country cannot be ruled out [19].

To summarize, we have developed a population‐specific severity score suited for SCA patients in Indians, which can be used by clinicians to predict disease severity. While this enhances the relevance of the score for the Indian population, the underlying approach may be generalizable to other populations, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries with similar genetic backgrounds, clinical presentations, environmental exposures, and resource constraints. However, external validation in such populations would be required to establish broader applicability. This can help clinicians in better disease management and modulate future clinical courses in the patients.
