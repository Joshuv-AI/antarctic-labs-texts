Pelvic Position at Cup Placement Compared With Intraoperative Pelvic Motion in the Lateral Decubitus Position
Authors: Hiromasa Tanino, Ryo Mitsutake, Hiroshi Ito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIt is important for surgeons to consider the pelvic position at cup placement for accurate cup placement. This position depends on both the pelvic position at set-up and the degree of intraoperative pelvic motion from set-up to cup placement. The present study aimed to characterize and compare the pelvic position at cup placement with intraoperative pelvic motion during primary total hip arthroplasty in the lateral decubitus position. This study also investigated whether the pelvic position at set-up or intraoperative pelvic motion had a greater impact on the pelvic position at cup placement.MethodsPelvic positions were measured in 86 hips using an augmented reality-based portable hip navigation system. The pelvic reference plane was registered in the supine position, and patients were then positioned in the lateral decubitus position for surgery. Pelvic positions were recorded at set-up and at the time of cup placement.ResultsMean pitch and roll angles at cup placement were significantly different from those of intraoperative pelvic motion (P < .001, .009), with greater variations in pitch and roll angles at cup placement. Directional differences in pitch and roll were also observed. Comparison of means showed that the pelvic position at set-up was close to the pelvic position at cup placement for pitch, whereas intraoperative pelvic motion was close to the pelvic position at cup placement for roll.ConclusionsThe pelvic position at cup placement differed from intraoperative pelvic motion. The pelvic position at set-up and intraoperative pelvic motion contributed differently to the pelvic position at cup placement.Keywords: Pelvic motion, Navigation, Portable, Cup position, Total hip arthroplasty

Introduction

Acetabular cup placement is one of the most difficult and technically variable aspects of total hip arthroplasty (THA). The pelvic position at the time of cup placement affects perceived cup abduction and anteversion, and may lead to unintended errors in cup placement. Surgeons attempt to place patients in a neutral position on the operating table at the initial set-up before surgery. When the patient is positioned laterally, a neutral position means that the patient’s sagittal plane is parallel to the operating table and the patient’s longitudinal axis is in line with the long dimension of the operating table. Surgeons using free-hand or non-navigated techniques rely on anatomical landmarks and mechanical guides, with the latter assuming that the pelvis remains in a neutral lateral position throughout surgery. However, wide variations in the pelvic position at set-up have been reported [[1], [2], [3]], and significant intraoperative pelvic motion may occur during THA [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Intraoperative pelvic motion occurs more commonly in the lateral decubitus position than in the supine position [8].

It is important for surgeons to consider the pelvic position at cup placement for accurate cup placement. This position depends on both the pelvic position at set-up and the degree of intraoperative pelvic motion from set-up to cup placement [2,8]. Previous studies investigated intraoperative pelvic motion during THA in the lateral decubitus position; however, they reported intraoperative pelvic motion from set-up to cup placement without accounting for variations in the pelvic position at set-up and did not compare the pelvic position at cup placement with intraoperative pelvic motion [2,4,5].

Therefore, the objectives of the present study were to characterize the pelvic position at cup placement using a portable hip navigation system during primary THA in the lateral decubitus position, and to compare it with intraoperative pelvic motion from set-up to cup placement. We hypothesized that the pelvic position at cup placement differed from intraoperative pelvic motion from set-up to cup placement. This study also investigated whether the pelvic position at set-up or intraoperative pelvic motion had a greater impact on the pelvic position at cup placement.

Material and methods

We conducted a retrospective review of patients who underwent primary THA with a commercially available augmented reality-based portable hip navigation system (AR navigation) (AR-HIP; Zimmer Biomet Japan, Japan) in the lateral decubitus position through a posterolateral approach between September 2023 and March 2024. Eighty-seven consecutive hips (87 patients) from a single institution were included. Informed consent was waived by the aforementioned review board due to the retrospective study design. Three surgeons (H.T., H.I., and R.M.) performed the surgeries using the same pelvic positioner and retractor. One patient was excluded due to missing pelvic position data. The remaining 86 hips were included in the analysis. Seventy-three patients (85%) were female. Mean age was 70 years (range, 42-91 years). Mean height, body weight, and body mass index (BMI) were 155 cm (range, 140-177 cm), 61 kg (range, 38-100 kg), and 25 kg/m2 (range, 14-42 kg/m2), respectively. Forty-five procedures (52%) were performed on the right hip. The primary diagnoses were osteoarthritis in 63 patients (73%), osteonecrosis of the femoral head in 14 (16%), femoral neck fracture in 6, and rheumatoid arthritis in 3 (Table 1).

AR navigation is a portable hip navigation system that uses a smartphone and AR technology to perform intraoperative measurements during THA. A previous study described this system [9]. Briefly, in the supine position, a Quick Response code marker is fixed to the ipsilateral iliac crest using 2 surgical pins. The functional pelvic plane, defined by the bilateral anterior superior iliac spines and the gravity vector in the supine position, is registered as the pelvic reference plane. Patients are then repositioned into the lateral decubitus position for surgery (flip technique) [10]. Patients are stabilized with a pelvic positioner (APP Lateral Positioner; FUJIFLEX, Japan), which secures the pelvis using 3 anterior pads against the bilateral anterior superior iliac spines and symphysis pubis, and a posterior pad over the sacrum.

While this system is primarily used to assist surgeons with cup placement by monitoring the cup position, it also displays the pelvic position in real-time. Previous study demonstrated that AR navigation could measure the pelvic position in the supine position [11]. When the smartphone camera in a sterile case recognizes the Quick Response code marker on the iliac crest, AR navigation shows the angle and magnitude between the functional pelvic plane and the gravity vector in the lateral decubitus position. AR navigation allows pelvic pitch and roll to be tracked at specific surgical steps. Pitch was defined as rotation in the coronal plane, with a positive value indicating cranial rotation of the operative-side hemipelvis. Roll was defined as rotation in the transverse plane, with a positive value indicating anterior rotation of the operative-side hemipelvis (Fig. 1). The pelvic positions were measured at the initial patient set-up on the operating table after sterile draping and at the time of cup placement. In this study, we defined 3 types of differences: (1) the difference between the initial pelvic position and the ideal neutral position (0° pitch and 0° roll), referred to as the pelvic position at set-up (2) the difference between the pelvic position at cup placement and the ideal neutral position, referred to as the pelvic position at cup placement (3) the difference between the initial pelvic position and the pelvic position at cup placement, referred to as the pelvic position difference. The pelvic position difference, also known as intraoperative pelvic motion from set-up to cup placement, was calculated by subtracting the pitch and roll angles detected at cup placement from those detected at set-up.

Definitions of pitch and roll. Pitch refers to rotation in the coronal plane, with a positive value indicating cranial rotation of the operative-side hemipelvis. Roll refers to rotation in the transverse plane, where a positive angle denotes anterior rotation of the hemipelvis on the operated side. The roll angle was calculated based on the relationship between the axis on the FPP connecting the bilateral anterior superior iliac spines and the gravity vector in the lateral decubitus position, and the pitch angle was calculated based on the relationship between the craniocaudal axis on the FPP, defined as the axis perpendicular to both the gravity vector in the supine position and the line connecting the bilateral anterior superior iliac spines, and the gravity vector in the lateral decubitus position. FPP, functional pelvic plane.

A standard posterolateral approach was used. At cup placement, the operated limb was positioned with 30° hip flexion, 60° hip internal rotation, and 90° knee flexion. A retractor was placed over the anterior acetabular rim to retract the femur. The target angles for cup abduction and anteversion were 40° ± 10° and 20° ± 10°. A cementless cup (Continuum; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN) with a crosslinked polyethylene liner was used in all hips. Most of the femoral stems used were cemented stems (CMK Original Concept; Zimmer Biomet), while cementless stems (S-ROM; DePuy, Warsaw, IN) were implanted in 5 hips. In all cases, 32 mm ceramic or metal heads were used.

Outcome variables included the pelvic positions at set-up and cup placement. The magnitude and direction of the pelvic position at cup placement were compared with those of the pelvic position difference. The effects of patient-related factors, including age, height, body weight, BMI, gender, the operated side, and primary diagnosis, on the pelvic position at set-up, the pelvic position at cup placement, and the pelvic position difference were assessed. To investigate whether the pelvic position at set-up or intraoperative pelvic motion had a greater impact on the pelvic position at cup placement, we compared the mean pelvic position at cup placement with the mean pelvic position at set-up and with the mean pelvic position difference.

Statistical methods are outlined below. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Statistical analyses were performed with a two-tailed paired t-test or the nonparametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired samples, and a 2-tailed independent t-test or the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test for group comparisons. Continuous factors that affected the pelvic position at set-up, the pelvic position at cup placement, and the pelvic position difference were assessed using Pearson’s correlation. A P value < .05 was considered to be significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Version 24 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Ethical approval

The institutional review board of our university (Reference No. 23128) approved the study and waived the requirement for informed consent due to its retrospective design. This study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional committee on human experiments and the ethical standards of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

The mean pelvic position at set-up (the difference between the initial pelvic position and the ideal neutral position) was a pitch of +2.1° ± 4.9° (range, −8° to +22°) and a roll of −1.5° ± 5.2° (range, −12° to +17°) (Table 2). Among the 86 hips examined, 52 (60%) were cranial, 10 (12%) were neutral, and 24 (28%) were caudal for pitch, while 29 hips (34%) were anterior, 7 (8%) were neutral, and 50 (58%) were posterior for roll (Fig. 2). Age negatively correlated with anterior roll (R = −0.260, P = .015). Body weight and BMI negatively correlated with cranial pitch (R = −0.280, −0.226, P = .009, .037) and positively correlated with anterior roll (R = 0.285, 0.220, P = .008, .042).

The mean pelvic position at cup placement (the difference between the pelvic position at cup placement and the ideal neutral position) was a pitch of +1.5° ± 5.7° (range, −14° to +21°) and a roll of +7.9° ± 6.3° (range, −8° to +25°). The mean pelvic position difference (the difference between the initial pelvic position and the pelvic position at cup placement) was −0.6° ± 3.7° (range, −17° to +15°) for pitch and +9.4° ± 4.1° (range, +1° to +17°) for roll (Table 2, Fig. 3). Significant differences were observed in both pitch and roll angles between the pelvic position at cup placement and the pelvic position difference (P < .001, .009). Variations in pitch and roll angles were greater at cup placement than with the pelvic position difference (Fig. 3). Directional differences in pitch and roll were also observed. Regarding pitch, the cranial direction was the most frequent for the pelvic position at cup placement (46 of 86 hips, 53%), while the caudal direction was the most frequent for the pelvic position difference (45 of 86 hips, 52%) (Fig. 2a). Concerning roll, the pelvic position difference was in the anterior direction in 100% (86 of 86 hips), whereas 13% (11 of 86 hips) of cases showed a posterior or neutral roll for the pelvic position at cup placement (Fig. 2b). Twenty-three hips (27%) had an absolute pitch ≥ 5° for the pelvic position at cup placement, while 8 (9%) had an absolute pitch ≥ 10°. However, fewer hips showed an absolute pitch ≥ 5° (6 of 86 hips, 7%) or ≥ 10° (1 of 86 hips, 1%) for the pelvic position difference. The frequency of an absolute roll ≥ 10° was similar for the pelvic position at cup placement and the pelvic position difference (32 hips, 35 hips), whereas an absolute roll ≥ 5° was more common for the pelvic position difference (46 hips, 71 hips). Body weight positively correlated with anterior roll at cup placement (R = 0.277, P = .010). The operated side correlated with pitch at cup placement (right −0.3° ± 4.8°, left +3.4° ± 6.0°; P = .007) and the pelvic position difference for pitch (right −1.3° ± 2.8°, left +0.2° ± 4.5°; P = .046).

When comparing the mean pelvic position at cup placement with the mean pelvic position at set-up and with the mean pelvic position difference, the differences in pitch were +0.6° (P = .065) vs −2.1° (P < .001), and the differences in roll were −9.4° (P < .001) vs +1.5° (P = .009).

Discussion

It is important for surgeons to consider the pelvic position at the time of cup placement for accurate cup placement. The results of this clinical study showed that both the magnitude and direction of the pelvic position at cup placement differed from those with the pelvic position difference, also known as intraoperative pelvic motion from set-up to cup placement, during THA in the lateral decubitus position. When comparing the mean pelvic position at cup placement with the mean pelvic position at set-up and with the mean pelvic position difference, we observed that for pitch, the mean pelvic position at set-up was similar to the mean pelvic position at cup placement (mean difference +0.6°, P > .05). For roll, the difference from the mean pelvic position at set-up (−9.4°) was considerably larger than that from the mean pelvic position difference (+1.5°). These results indicated that the pelvic position at set-up had a greater impact on the pelvic position at cup placement for pitch, while intraoperative pelvic motion had a greater impact on the pelvic position at cup placement for roll. From a clinical perspective, our findings highlight 2 distinct challenges that surgeons must address during THA in the lateral decubitus position: achieving an accurate initial patient position to manage final pelvic pitch, and controlling for dynamic intraoperative motion to manage final pelvic roll. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the pelvic position at cup placement and intraoperative pelvic motion, and to investigate whether the pelvic position at set-up or intraoperative pelvic motion had a greater impact on the pelvic position at cup placement.

Intraoperative pelvic motion may occur as a result of various unavoidable factors, including pelvic fixation on the operating table, leg positioning during surgery, and forces generated during the surgical procedure [2,12]. Previous studies of portable hip navigation systems reported intraoperative pelvic motion in the supine position [11,[13], [14], [15]]. In the lateral decubitus position, intraoperative pelvic motion has been measured using a specialized inclinometer or portable hip navigation system. Asayama et al. and Schwarzkopf et al. reported mean cranial pitch angles of 0.47° and 2.7° and mean anterior roll angles of 14.6° and 7.3°, respectively [4,5]. Although our results on the pelvic position difference are generally consistent with these findings, it is important to note that their values do not represent the pelvic position at cup placement. The pelvic position at cup placement depends on both the pelvic position at set-up and the degree of intraoperative pelvic motion from set-up to cup placement [2,8]. In previous studies of portable hip navigation systems [5,7,16], the pelvic reference plane was registered in the lateral decubitus position before incision. Therefore, the pelvic position at set-up could not be taken into account, and only intraoperative pelvic motion was reported.

The present results revealed significant differences between the pelvic position at cup placement and the pelvic position difference. Mean pitch and roll angles at cup placement were significantly different from those with the pelvic position difference, and greater variations were observed at cup placement than with the pelvic position difference. Regarding directional frequency, cranial pitch was the most frequent for the pelvic position at cup placement, and caudal pitch for the pelvic position difference. Additionally, anterior roll was observed in all hips for the pelvic position difference, which is consistent with previous studies [4,5], whereas 13% of cases showed a posterior or neutral roll for the pelvic position at cup placement. This difference in roll direction is clinically important because roll affects cup anteversion, one of the important factors decreasing postoperative complications, such as dislocation or impingement. These results suggest that using AR navigation enables quantitative evaluation of the pelvic position at specific surgical steps, highlighting its role as an objective intraoperative measurement tool.

Intraoperative radiographs have also been used to assess the pelvic position at cup placement. Kanazawa et al. and Grammatopoulos et al. reported mean pitch angles of 1.6° and 1.0° and mean roll angles of 0.3° and 1.1°, respectively, using intraoperative radiographs [3,8]. The small roll angles reported in these studies may be attributed to the removal of retractors and returning the leg to a neutral position at the time of measurements. The use of a retractor over the anterior acetabular rim and leg positioning of internal rotation and flexion have been reported to be associated with pelvic motion [7,12].

Nishikubo et al. demonstrated that the accuracy of cup placement was improved by accounting for the pelvic position at set-up using preoperative fluoroscopy [1]. While their approach provided good accuracy for cup abduction, that for cup anteversion was lower. Pitch primarily affects cup abduction, while roll affects cup anteversion [17]. The present results showing that the pelvic position at set-up had a greater impact on the pelvic position at cup placement for pitch, while intraoperative pelvic motion had a greater impact on the pelvic position at cup placement for roll provides a partial explanation for the findings reported by Nishikubo et al.

The results obtained herein also showed that surgeons were unable to consistently position the pelvis in a neutral position at set-up; none of the cases had both pitch and roll angles within 1° of neutral, which is consistent with previous studies [[1], [2], [3]]. This variation is further exacerbated by intraoperative pelvic motion. An accurate, real-time intraoperative assessment of the pelvic position may assist surgeons in achieving more accurate cup placement. Although portable hip navigation systems improve the accuracy of cup placement [9,[18], [19], [20], [21], [22]], it remains controversial whether their use results in improved clinical outcomes that sufficiently justify the additional costs and operative time [9,[23], [24], [25], [26]].

We compared our findings regarding the relationship between patient-related factors and the pelvic position at set-up, the pelvic position at cup placement, and the pelvic position difference with those reported in previous studies. Many authors agree that increasing patient BMI correlate with unintended pelvic motion [27], but many previous studies have suggested no clear association between BMI and the pelvic position [4,7,28]. In this study, body weight positively correlated with anterior roll at cup placement. Several previous studies have investigated the effect of surgeon handedness on acetabular cup placement during THA. Song et al. reported that cup abduction in the right hip was larger than cup abduction in the left hip with right-handed surgeons, and they speculated that this difference might be due to the relationship between the surgeon’s hand and the available operating space [29], but our observed side-to-side difference in the pelvic position (right-handed surgeons in this study, more caudal pitch in the right hip and more cranial pitch in the left hip) also provides a possible explanation. Body weight and BMI negatively correlated with cranial pitch at set-up in this study, which is consistent with a previous study. Okutani et al. reported that higher BMI was associated with caudal pitch at set-up [30].

This study has several limitations that need to be addressed. We did not evaluate radiographic or clinical outcomes. Our focus was on characterizing the pelvic position at cup placement, comparing it with intraoperative motion, and establishing whether the pelvic position at set-up or intraoperative pelvic motion had a greater impact on it. Future studies are needed to elucidate how these pelvic position parameters affect clinical outcomes. Additionally, the majority of patients in this study were female, which may limit the generalizability of the present results. However, previous studies on the pelvic position have included a similar high proportion of female patients [1,3], and the findings of a previous study have not suggested gender differences in the magnitude of intraoperative pelvic motion [16]. Moreover, our analysis using mean values, which was performed to investigate whether the pelvic position at set-up or intraoperative pelvic motion had a greater impact on the pelvic position at cup placement, is an indirect analysis. A more direct method, such as multiple regression analysis, could be used in future studies to model the influence of each variable more definitively. Furthermore, all patients were positioned using the same pelvic positioner and underwent surgery through a posterolateral approach, which may limit the generalizability of the present results [28]. Prospective studies need to consider not only variations in patient positioning, surgical approaches, and pelvic positioners, but also how these factors impact the final cup position.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that the pelvic position at cup placement differed in both magnitude and direction from those with the pelvic position difference, also known as intraoperative pelvic motion, during THA in the lateral decubitus position. Furthermore, the pelvic position at set-up had a greater impact on the pelvic position at cup placement for pitch, whereas intraoperative pelvic motion had a greater impact on that for roll. These results indicate the importance of assessing the pelvic position at the time of cup placement rather than relying solely on intraoperative pelvic motion from set-up to cup placement. Moreover, these results highlight that, clinically, surgeons must address 2 distinct challenges for accurate cup placement: achieving an accurate initial patient position to manage final pelvic pitch, and controlling for dynamic intraoperative motion to manage final pelvic roll.
