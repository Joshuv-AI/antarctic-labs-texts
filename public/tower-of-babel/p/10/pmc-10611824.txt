The Effect of an Interdisciplinary Dermatological-Rheumatological Consultation on the Outcome of Patients with Psoriasis with Musculoskeletal Pain: A Prospective, Single-Center Cohort Study
Authors: Jan Alwin von Ahnen, Georg Gross, Christian Lull, Mara Blauth, Bernhard Kraemer, Victor Olsavszky, Jan Leipe, Astrid Schmieder
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPsoriatic arthritis (PsA), a disease with complex inflammatory musculoskeletal manifestations, complicates psoriasis in up to 30% of patients. In this study, we aimed to determine the effect of an interdisciplinary dermatological-rheumatological consultation (IDRC) for patients with psoriasis with musculoskeletal symptoms.MethodsThis prospective study enrolled 202 patients with psoriasis. Patients with musculoskeletal pain (MSP) (n = 115) participated in an IDRC 12 weeks after enrollment. The outcome was evaluated after 24 weeks.ResultsIn 12/79 (15.2%) patients seen in the IDRC, the prior diagnosis was changed: eight with a first diagnosis of PsA, four with a diagnosis of PsA rescinded. Treatment was modified in 28% of patients. Significant improvements in Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) (from 5.3 to 2.0; p < 0.001) and Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) (from 6.7 to 4.5; p = 0.009) were observed. By comparing changes in PASI and DLQI over the study period, an improvement in PASI of 0.7 ± 1.4 points (p = 0.64) and in DLQI of 2.9 ± 1.5 points (p = 0.051) could be attributed to participation in the IDRC.ConclusionAn IDRC of patients with psoriasis with MSP leads to a valid diagnosis of PsA and improvement in quality of life. Based on these results, an IDRC is a valuable and time efficient way for psoriasis patient with MSP to receive optimal care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-023-02667-y.Keywords: Disease management, mHealth, Mental health, Psoriasis, Interdisciplinary consultation, Dermatological-rheumatological consultation, Psoriatic arthritis

Key Summary Points

Introduction

Background

Up to 30% of patients with psoriasis suffer from psoriatic arthritis (PsA) [1]. PsA is a complex inflammatory disorder with heterogeneous manifestations including peripheral arthritis, enthesitis, dactylitis and axial involvement [2–4]. According to a meta-analysis, undiagnosed PsA may occur in 15% of patients with psoriasis [5]. Since psoriasis often manifests 10–12 years before the occurrence of PsA, an awareness and recognition of musculoskeletal manifestations in patients with psoriasis are key [6, 7]. If not diagnosed and treated in a timely fashion, affected inflamed musculoskeletal structures suffer damage and potential permanent sequelae. Studies have demonstrated that even a 6-month delay in PsA treatment can have a significant impact on patient outcomes [8, 9]. To avoid a delay in diagnosis and treatment, patients with psoriasis reporting musculoskeletal pain should thus be referred to a rheumatologist as soon as possible, which can be challenging because of the lack of rheumatologists, e.g., in Europe and North America [10–12].

For the management of patients with multisystem diseases, interdisciplinary approaches have emerged as a possible solution. A 5-year retrospective investigation of the interdisciplinary Rheumatology-Dermatology clinic at Massachusetts General Hospital revealed a more accurate evaluation of the skin and joints, better patient treatment and outcomes and lower medication costs [13]. Prior to the referral to this clinic, only 1% of dermatologists documented joint examination, and only 5% of rheumatologists noted a rash in their patients [13].

The current prospective study aims to analyze whether an IDRC improves valid diagnosis of PsA in patients with psoriasis with musculoskeletal pain and their outcomes in a timely manner.

Methods

Study Population

Patients were included in the study between August 2020 and May 2021 at the University Medical Center Mannheim, Germany, from the interdisciplinary outpatient clinic, the outpatient departments of the Departments of Dermatology and Rheumatology and the inpatient department of the Department of Dermatology. Included patients were required to be ≥ 18 years of age and to have a confirmed diagnosis of psoriasis. Exclusion criteria were insufficient German language competency.

Study Design

In this single-center intervention study with a 24-week study period, the following data were collected at the baseline visit (V0) after enrollment of patients: age, sex, body mass index (BMI), current and prior therapies, prior diagnosis of PsA, comorbidity, compliance and adherence to treatment, physical activity, alcohol use and tobacco use by using validated questionnaires. At baseline, after 12 and 24 weeks, the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI; range 0–72), the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI, range 0–30) and numeric rating scales (NRS, range 0–10) for skin pain and pruritus as well as joint pain were assessed.

At week 12 (V1), patients with musculoskeletal pain were evaluated in our IDRC with collection of disease-specific dermatological and rheumatological history and a physical examination including, but not limited to, thorough skin and musculoskeletal examination. If required, musculoskeletal ultrasound and/or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and/or plain radiography was performed. The patients were classified as patients with psoriasis: without musculoskeletal pain (Pso), with musculoskeletal pain, but no diagnosis of PsA (PMP), and PsA (Fig. 1). Dermatologists and rheumatologists jointly decided on treatment changes to cover all manifestations, if necessary. The final visit (V2) took place after 24 weeks. For patients with musculoskeletal pain or prior diagnosis of PsA, the Disease Activity Index for Psoriatic Arthritis (DAPSA) was calculated [14].

Study flowchart. The study population was divided into patients with psoriasis and no musculoskeletal pain (Pso only) and patients with musculoskeletal pain without (PMP) and with diagnosed psoriatic arthritis (PsA). In total, the study population received two or three visits with the interdisciplinary dermatological-rheumatological consultation (IDRC) in week 12 only offered to the PMP and PsA group

Statistical Analysis

JASP (University of Amsterdam, Amsterdam, version 0.16.1), Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, version 16.0) and SPSS Statistics (IBM, Armonk, version 29.0) were used for statistical analysis. Chi-square, independent t-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank test, difference-in-differences analysis, and classical and repeated measure ANOVA were used to test for significance. A statistically significant difference was assumed at p ≤ 0.05.

Ethics Approval

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Medical Faculty Mannheim, Heidelberg University (2020-515N-MA). The trial is registered at Deutsches Register Klinische Studien (registration number DRKS00020963). Written informed consent was provided by each patient before participating in the study.

Results

Total Study Population

Of 257 patients screened, 202 were included, and 150 (74.3%) completed the study until week 24 (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics are found in Supplementary Table 1. Patients reported spending an average of 9 h at doctor's appointments connected to their psoriasis in the preceding 6 months, with a substantial portion (18.3%) being admitted to the hospital because of their condition. Disease activity was mild to moderate (mean PASI of 5.3 ± 7.7), and quality of life indicated a moderate effect on patient’s lives (mean DLQI of 6.7 ± 7.6). The most frequent comorbidities were hypertension, obesity and depression/anxiety. The most common anti-psoriatic treatments among patients at baseline were topical or UV therapy (33.7%), followed by biological disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (bDMARD) targeting IL-12, IL-23 (21.8%) and IL-17 (16.8%) and conventional synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) (13.4%) (Supplementary Table 2).

During the 24-week study period, significant improvements were observed in PASI (V0: 5.3 ± 7.7, V2: 2 ± 3.9; p < 0.001) and DLQI scores (V0: 6.7 ± 7.6, V2: 4.5 ± 5.8; p = 0.004) in the overall study population. The average time spent for doctor visits decreased by about 2.5 h (V0: 8.9 ± 13.5 to V2: 6.4 ± 6 h; p = 0.038), and the percentage of patients requiring hospital admission also decreased (V0: 18.3% versus V2: 4.7%; p ≤ 0.001) (Supplementary Table 1).

Subgroup Analysis of the Study Population

We identified and analyzed three subgroups of patients with psoriasis: (1) without musculoskeletal pain (Pso): 87 (43.1%), (2) patients with musculoskeletal symptoms, but no diagnosis of PsA (PMP): 59 (29.2%), and (3) patients with a prior diagnosis of PsA: 56 (27.7%). At baseline, PASI was significantly lower in patients with PsA compared to the others (Pso: 5.8 ± 8.7; PMP: 6.4 ± 7.6, PsA: 3.2 ± 5.5; p = 0.048, Table 1). Patients from the PMP group were more likely to receive a delayed diagnosis of psoriasis with an average time from symptom onset to diagnosis of 4 years (Pso: 51.5 ± 137.8 weeks, PMP: 213.2 ± 388.7; p ≤ 0.001). The time until diagnosis of PsA was 186.6 ± 393.3 weeks. Patients from the PMP group also reported the lowest quality of life (DLQI: Pso: 5.4: ± 7.3, PMP: 8.5 ± 7.8, PsA: 6.9 ± 7.7, p = 0.054).

Subgroup Analysis of the Study Population Attending the IDRC

Of 115 patients with psoriasis with musculoskeletal symptoms at their initial visit, 79 (68.7%) participated in the IDRC at week 12, who were classified as PMP (n = 41) and PsA (n = 38) (Fig. 1). The rate of patients who required imaging for further diagnostics during the IDRC was similar in both groups (p = 0.728); musculoskeletal ultrasound was the most common imaging method (15.2%). The previous diagnosis was changed in 12 patients (15.2%): 8 patients were initially diagnosed with PsA and 4 had the diagnosis of PsA withdrawn.

Of the 79 participants of the IDRC, 56 (70.9%) received any form of systemic anti-psoriatic therapy at baseline (20 from the PMP subgroup, 36 from the PsA subgroup) with the proportion of patients already receiving systemic treatment at baseline being significantly higher among the PsA subgroup compared to the PMP subgroup with 94.8% vs. 48.7% (p < 0.00001). Of the 56 patients on systemic therapy at baseline, 46 were on bDMARDs and 9 were receiving csDMARDs; in a single case, systemic steroids were being used. Topical treatment of psoriatic plaques was used by 23 (29.1%) patients.

Treatment was modified in 23 (29.1%) patients, with 12 changes in the PMP and 11 changes in the PsA subgroup (Fig. 2). Seventeen treatment changes resulted from relevant disease activity, most frequently a switch from an existing to another bDMARD therapy (n = 8), followed by initiation of new csDMARDs and/or bDMARDs (n = 5), addition of a csDMARD or bDMARD to an existing bDMARD or csDMARD regimen (n = 2), a switch from a csDMARD to a bDMARD (n = 1) and a switch from a csDMARD to a csDMARD (n = 1). In five cases, treatment was modified as a result of the initial diagnosis of psoriatic arthritis: Topical therapy was complemented by a new systemic therapy (n = 2), a combined csDMARD and bDMARD therapy was changed to a monotherapy with a bDMARD of another class (n = 1), change from one bDMARD to another bDMARD of another class (n = 1) was performed, and the complementation of an existing bDMARD regimen with a csDMARD (n = 1) took place. In one case, an existing systemic therapy with dimethyl fumarate was de-escalated to topical therapy based on a good symptom control.

Sankey diagram visualizing changes in therapy made during the interdisciplinary dermatological-rheumatological consultation (IDRC). Patients received topical or UV treatment, interleukin-17-inhibitors (IL-17i), tumor necrosis factor inhibitors (TNFi), interleukin-23-inhibitors (IL-23i), conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (csDMARDs) or other treatment

Thus, after attending the IDRC, 64 (81.0%) of 79 participants received any form of systemic anti-psoriatic therapy, of which 26 belonged to the PMP and 38 to the PsA subgroup. Accordingly, 63.4% of PMP patients and 100% of PsA patients received systemic therapy. In 51 cases, bDAMRDs were used, in 12 cases csDMARDs, and one patient remained on systemic steroids. Topical therapy was used by 19 (24.1%) patients.

From the 79 patients participating in the IDRC, 69 (32 PMP and 37 PsA) eventually showed up to the final visit at week 24 and were included in the final evaluation. After the 24-week study period, PMP and PsA patients seen in the IDRC both showed a significant improvement in mean PASI from baseline (PMP 4.6 to 1.4, p < 0.001; PsA 4.1 to 1.4, p = 0.002; Fig. 3). Mean DLQI also significantly decreased in both subgroups (PMP 6.3 to 3.6, p = 0.001; PsA 8.7 to 5.2, p = 0.019). The DAPSA score decreased in the PMP and PsA patients (PMP 19.1 to 13.9, p = 0.027; PsA 19.4 to 16.8, p = 0.131). Both the PMP and PsA subgroups reported a decreased time spent at the doctor (PMP V0 6.1 to V2 5.0 h, p = 0.410; PsA: V0 9.5 to V2 6.3 h, p = 0.490).

Nineteen patients (9 PMP and 10 PsA) who were eligible for the IDRC in week 12 did not show up for their IDRC appointment but reappeared for the final visit in week 24. For these patients, a less pronounced decrease of PASI (PMP 3.2 to 1.7, p = 0.441; PsA 1.7 to 2.5, p = 1.000), DLQI, (PMP 4.4 to 3.3, p = 0.201; PsA 3.0 to 6.1, p = 0.206), and DAPSA score (PMP 13.9 to 5.6, p = 0.129; PsA (n = 3): 21.1 to 10.7, p = 0.500) was observed.

Effect of the IDRC

For measuring the effect of the IDRC, we compared the changes in PASI and DLQI over the study period between intervention and control group. Using a difference-in-differences analysis, we calculated that an improvement in PASI of 0.7 ± 1.4 points (p = 0.64) and in DLQI of 2.9 ± 1.5 points (p = 0.051) could be attributed to participation in the IDRC.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the impact of an IDRC on the clinical outcome and quality of life of patients with psoriasis.

Baseline characteristics were consistent with previous data showing a slight male predominance, high BMI values and high rates of comorbidities [15]. Depression/anxiety was the third most common comorbidity in our study, reflecting the substantial psychological burden associated with the disease. Since only patients with musculoskeletal complaints were eligible for the IDRC, the Pso group and patients with musculoskeletal complaints who did not participate in the IDRC served as an internal control.

Over the course of our observation, PASI and DLQI decreased significantly with important differences between subgroups. While PASI scores decreased significantly in the Pso, PMP and PsA subgroups, only the PMP and PsA subgroups attending the IDRC showed a significant reduction in DLQI. This effect was also seen in PMP patients not participating in the IDRC, while PASI and DLQI increased in the PsA group not participating in the IDRC. In accordance with the study by Luchetti et al., our study suggests that an IDRC can enable a fast confirmation or exclusion of PsA leading to a rapid optimization of treatment [16]. This has the potential to significantly improve the articular disease, resulting in a consistent improvement in quality of life. The amelioration of the DAPSA score in the PMP and to a lesser extent in the PsA subgroup support this theory. Reducing the amount of time spent on doctor's appointments is a factor that can also have a positive impact on quality of life [17]. Based on our study data, we believe that the IDRC can save time for PMP and PsA patients despite their participation in a time-consuming study, presumably because appointments with other musculoskeletal specialists such as orthopedists occurred at a lower rate. This was not the case for the Pso subgroup, as they experienced a slight increase in time spent at the doctor although attending only two visits most probably because of the study setting.

This study was designed as a single-center, prospective cohort study. No randomization took place when allocating patients to intervention or control group. It is thus very likely that there are systematic differences between patients in the intervention and the control group, which limits the validity of the results of our study.

Numbers were small in some of the subgroups evaluated. This was especially true for the patients with musculoskeletal complaints who did not visit the IDRC. For a more accurate evaluation, larger randomized studies are needed. In addition, due to the design as a single center study at a university hospital center, it is likely that the trial cohort does not fully represent the overall population of patients with psoriasis or PsA. Patients in our dermatological outpatient department are more likely to have failed topical therapy, thus requiring more intensive treatment than the average psoriasis patient. Hence, the results of this study cannot be transferred to patients with psoriasis in general.

We used “Duration of psoriasis related doctor visits in the last 6 months” as a surrogate marker for the impact of the treatment of their disease on the daily life of patients. However, patients on systemic therapy tend to spend more time at the doctor by default due to regular blood controls. This bias has to be considered when comparing the time spent at the doctor of different subgroups from this study.

The study was conducted between the summer of 2020 and the fall of 2021, at a time of COVID-19 pandemic in which lockdowns and fear of contagion made it difficult for patients to attend their IRDC appointments, largely accounting for the substantial proportion of dropouts after screening.

Conclusion

In the IDRC, we were able to diagnose or exclude PsA in a considerable number of patients with psoriasis, and treatment was optimized, which can be crucial for improving long-term outcome and quality of life [8, 18]. Our results are in line with the results of earlier studies [16, 19], providing further support for the implementation of an IDRC for patients with psoriasis with MSP.
