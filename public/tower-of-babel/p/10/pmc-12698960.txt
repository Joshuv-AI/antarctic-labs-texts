Plasma Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein Correlates With Brain Metal Burden in Wilson's Disease
Authors: Sung‐Pin Fan, Ya‐Fang Chen, Cheng‐Hsuan Li, Yih‐Chih Kuo, Ni‐Chung Lee, Yin‐Hsiu Chien, Wuh‐Liang Hwu, Tai‐Chung Tseng, Tung‐Hung Su, Chien‐Ting Hsu, Huey‐Ling Chen, Chin‐Hsien Lin, Yen‐Hsuan Ni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveNeuroinflammation driven by extracellular copper contributes to neuronal damage in Wilson's disease (WD). This study investigated the relationship between brain metal burden and peripheral neuroinflammation markers in WD.MethodsWe conducted a cross‐sectional study involving 89 participants, including patients with WD (n = 63), asymptomatic ATP7B heterozygous carriers (n = 12), and age/sex‐matched controls (n = 14). Brain metal burden was assessed using quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) MRI. Plasma glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and neurofilament light chain (NfL) levels were measured. Clinical severity was evaluated using the mini–Unified Wilson's Disease Rating Scale (UWDRS) and Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). The influence of copper chelation treatment on biomarker correlations was also examined.ResultsPatients with WD had a significantly higher level of GFAP (p = 0.02) and brain metal burden (p < 0.01) than the control group. Plasma NfL levels were marginally elevated in the WD group (p = 0.07). Notably, elevated plasma GFAP levels were significantly associated with higher UWDRS scores (r = 0.35, p < 0.01) and lower MoCA scores (r = −0.36, p < 0.01). The NfL levels were also correlated with UWDRS (r = 0.58, p < 0.01) and marginally correlated with MoCA (r = −0.32, p = 0.02). The brain magnetic susceptibility value was positively correlated with increased plasma GFAP level, particularly in the putamen (p < 0.001), which was more prominent in WD patients without chelating agent treatment (r = 0.58, p < 0.001).InterpretationsPlasma GFAP levels reflect both clinical severity and brain metal accumulation in WD, with this association influenced by copper chelation therapy.

Introduction

Wilson's disease (WD) is a rare autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the ATP7B gene, leading to impaired copper metabolism. As a result, excess copper accumulates primarily in hepatic and cerebral tissues, causing progressive liver damage and neurological dysfunction [1]. The neuropsychiatric deficits, such as depression, anxiety, cognitive dysfunction, and variable movement disorders, have been reported to interfere with patients' quality of life [2]. Although biochemical markers such as 24‐h urinary copper excretion and serum non‐ceruloplasmin‐bound copper are routinely used in clinical practice, their correlation with neurological disease severity remains inconsistent [3]. Several studies have suggested a correlation between the extent of cerebral metal deposition and the severity of neurological symptoms [4, 5, 6]. However, these findings are not universally replicated, and substantial inter‐individual variability in neurological symptoms persists, even among patients receiving stable anti‐copper therapy [7, 8]. These discrepancies suggest that factors beyond copper accumulation may contribute to the heterogeneity of neurological manifestations and therapeutic responses. Identification of such non‐copper‐related factors may provide novel insights into the pathophysiology of neurological involvement in WD and facilitate the development of surrogate biomarkers for disease monitoring.

Prolonged exposure of the brain to elevated copper levels in WD has been shown to induce astrocyte activation, neuroinflammation, and disruption of the blood–brain barrier [9, 10, 11]. Postmortem analyses of patients with WD have revealed widespread gliosis, including astrogliosis and glial cell activation, along with evidence of tissue degeneration in multiple brain regions [7, 12, 13]. Abnormal astrocytes with inflammatory features have been particularly noted in the striatum, a common site of neurodegeneration in WD [14, 15]. Additionally, elevated levels of circulating inflammatory cytokines [16], as well as the astrocytic injury marker glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) [17], and the neuronal injury marker neurofilament light chain (NfL) [18, 19], have been observed in patients with WD compared with unaffected controls. These findings suggest ongoing neuroinflammation and glial pathology in WD, despite clinical management. Quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) is an advanced MRI technique that enables in vivo assessment of brain metal content [20, 21]. QSM‐derived susceptibility values have been shown to correlate with regional metal accumulation in various neurological disorders, including Alzheimer's disease [22, 23], Parkinson's disease [24, 25], neurodegeneration with brain iron accumulation [26, 27], and WD [4, 6, 28, 29]. Recent studies suggest that individual differences in clinical severity among patients with WD may correspond with the degree of brain metal deposition observed on QSM imaging and pathological studies [30]. However, it remains unclear whether brain copper burden correlates with peripheral markers of astrocytic activation and neuroinflammation. In this study, we investigated the relationship between brain metal deposition, as measured by QSM, and circulating markers of neuroinflammation in patients with WD and age‐ and sex‐matched controls. We further examined the correlation between the circulating markers of glial activation and disease severity in WD. Furthermore, we assessed the potential modulatory effect of copper‐chelating therapy on the association between brain metal burden and peripheral biomarkers.

Methods

Participant Enrollment

A total of 89 participants were recruited from multiple centers in Taiwan between January 2019 and July 2024, including 63 patients with molecularly confirmed WD, 12 asymptomatic heterozygous carriers, and 14 unaffected controls without pathogenic ATP7B variants. The diagnosis of WD was based on both clinical diagnostic criteria recommended by the European Association for the Study of Liver (EASL) Leipzig score [31] and molecular genetic confirmation using panel‐based next‐generation sequencing analysis [6]. The unaffected control participants were accompanying friends of the WD patients and had no known history of systemic disorders and relevant neurologic or hepatic symptoms. The study protocol was approved by the research ethics committee of National Taiwan University Hospital (IRB: 202406149RINA), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Clinical and Laboratory Evaluations

All participants received a detailed neurological examination and a comprehensive review of medical records for assessment of clinical features, including age at onset, initial symptoms, disease duration, and current medications. Patients with WD were also evaluated for neurological functional status with the mini–Unified Wilson's Disease Rating Score (UWDRS) and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA). All participants underwent blood copper analysis, including measurements of ceruloplasmin and serum copper.

Genetic Analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from 10 mL of venous blood from each participant using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and the exons and exon–intron boundaries of the ATP7B gene were sequenced using targeted next‐generation sequencing, as previously described [8]. The variants were identified and classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics Standards and Guidelines [32].

Plasma Marker Measurement

Plasma samples were collected at the time the brain MRI was performed. Fasting venous blood (10 mL) was sampled and centrifuged (2500 g for 15 min) within 1 h after collection. The plasma aliquots were then stored at −80°C according to standardized procedures until testing. All plasma biomarkers, including GFAP and NfL, were quantified using the ultrasensitive Simoa technology (Quanterix, Billerica, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. All measurements were performed in duplicate by blinded board‐certified technicians, and average concentrations were calculated.

Brain MRI Acquisition and QSM Analysis

All WD patients underwent brain MRI scanning using a Siemens Verio 3T system with a 32‐channel head/neck coil (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany). Three‐dimensional T1‐weighted images and axial gradient echo (GRE) sequences were acquired. Imaging parameters for the T1‐weighted sequence were as follows: TR/TE/TI (ms) = 2000/2.3/900, FOV (mm3) = 256 × 256 × 192, voxel size (mm3) = 1 × 1 × 1. The GRE protocol parameters were as follows: TR/TE (ms) = 27/20, flip angle = 15°, FOV (mm3) = 256 × 256 × 176, number of slices = 88, slice thickness = 2 mm, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 2, echo = 1 for acquisition. The original magnitude and phase images were saved and transformed into Nifti files for further processing.

All MRI images were reviewed for quality by a senior radiologist (Y.F. Chen). Individual transformation matrices to and from Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space were generated using the segmentation pipeline of the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12, version 12.8.2, https://github.com/ChristianGaser/cat12) running on MATLAB 2023a (MathWorks Inc.). QSM was reconstructed from GRE images using the STI Suite toolbox [33] with preprocessing assistance from the SEPIA platform [34], as previously described [6]. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) was used as the reference tissue for whole‐brain susceptibility mapping. Cortical and subcortical masks were generated during the segmentation of T1‐weighted images, and regional susceptibility values were extracted using the FSL toolbox [35]. All susceptibility maps were normalized to MNI space while preserving susceptibility values via Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM12, version 7771, http://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm). Voxel‐wise regression analyses were conducted, including neuroinflammatory biomarkers and age as covariates. Clusters with voxel‐wise p values < 0.001 (uncorrected) and cluster sizes > 10 voxels were considered significant.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean values and standard error of the mean, while categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. The characters were compared by applying the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, including age and treatment duration, whether normally distributed or not. The chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test was applied for categorical variables. The age effect was regressed out with a general linear regression model that took the residuals for the partial correlation. We applied Pearson's correlation on continuous variables to investigate the correlations between neuroinflammatory biomarkers and the susceptibility level of the brain. GraphPad Prism 10 (version 10.1.1, https://www.graphpad.com/scientific‐software/prism) was applied for most statistical analysis except for the partial correlation, which was performed using Python (version 3.10). Voxel‐based analysis was performed in SPM12 using MATLAB (version R2023a, MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA).

Plasma Markers of GFAP and NfL

We observed a positive correlation between age and the measured plasma markers, including GFAP and NfL, in both control participants (Figure S1A,B) and patients with WD (Figure S1C,D). After adjusting for age, plasma concentrations of GFAP were significantly higher in WD patients with neurological symptoms compared with those patients without neurological features or other comparison groups (p < 0.01, Table 1 and Figure 1A). In addition, plasma NfL concentrations were also higher in the WD group, especially in those with neurological features, compared with the unaffected controls (p < 0.05, Table 1 and Figure 1B). Notably, in patients with WD, elevated plasma GFAP concentrations were significantly associated with greater clinical severity, as evidenced by higher UWDRS scores (r = 0.35, p < 0.01) and lower MoCA scores (r = −0.36, p < 0.01) (Figure 2A,B). Similarly, plasma NfL concentrations demonstrated a substantially positive correlation with UWDRS scores (r = 0.58, p < 0.01) and a modest negative correlation with MoCA scores (r = −0.32, p = 0.02) (Figure 2C,D).

Correlations Between Plasma Markers and Brain Metal Burden

Patients with WD, particularly those with neurological symptoms, exhibited increased magnetic susceptibility on QSM sequence brain MRI in the deep nuclear regions in comparison with controls (Table S1). After adjusting for age, the magnetic susceptibility values were significantly elevated across most subcortical regions, including the caudate nucleus, putamen, globus pallidus, and substantia nigra in the WD group compared with controls or those with a heterozygous ATP7B variant (all p < 0.01, Table S1). Further voxel‐wise regression analyses showed several positive correlation clusters between plasma GFAP levels and whole brain magnetic susceptibility levels after adjustment for age. These clusters were mainly located in subcortical brain regions, including the right putamen and left caudate nucleus (Figure 3A, Table S2). Additionally, a positive correlation between plasma NfL concentrations and magnetic susceptibility remained evident, primarily in cortical regions such as the left inferior frontal gyrus (triangular part) and the left superior occipital gyrus. However, the observed effect sizes were modest (Figure S2, Table S3).

We next scrutinized the effect of different treatment strategies on the relationship between the brain copper burden and plasma markers. Using voxel‐wise regression analysis, we observed significant clusters showing positive correlations between plasma GFAP concentrations and brain magnetic susceptibility levels in bilateral putamen only among WD patients without the use of chelating agents (Figure 3B, Table S2). Furthermore, using ROI‐based analysis, we consistently observed that the WD patients who had not received chelator therapy displayed a significantly positive correlation (r = 0.58, p < 0.01) between plasma GFAP concentration and subcortical magnetic susceptibility level after adjusting for age (Figure 4A), which correlations dampened in WD patients receiving therapies with copper chelators (r = −0.16, p = 0.45) (Figure 4B). There was no obvious relationship between plasma NfL concentrations and magnetic susceptibility values in WD patients with (r = 0.21, p = 0.31) and without copper chelator use (r = 0.24, p = 0.32) (Figure 4C,D). These findings suggest that the use of chelating agents not only reduces brain metal accumulation but also attenuates the association between brain metal burden and plasma GFAP concentrations.

The Effects of Genotypes of ATP7B on the Relationship Between Plasma GFAP and Brain Metal Burden

As the p.R778L substitution is the most common pathogenic variant among Asian WD patients [8], we then investigated this genotypic effect on the plasma GFAP concentration and metal burden on brain MRI. We observed that patients carrying at least one allele of the p.R778L variant, in either the compound heterozygous or homozygous state, were older (p < 0.001) and tended to have a less severe UWDRS score (p = 0.06) compared with WD patients having a non‐p.R778L variant (Table 3). The plasma concentrations of GFAP and NfL were comparable between WD patients having either genotype. The correlation between brain magnetic susceptibility and plasma GFAP concentrations was comparable between WD patients carrying non‐p.R778L and those having at least one allele of p.R778L (p = 0.23). These observations suggest that the interaction between metal burden and glial cell activation is not affected by different ATP7B genotypes.

Discussion

In this study, we observed that patients with WD have elevated plasma concentrations of GFAP and NfL compared with controls or heterozygous ATP7B carriers. The plasma concentrations of both GFAP and NfL correlated with disease severity in WD patients. Notably, there was a positive association between plasma GFAP concentrations and the extent of brain metal deposition, particularly in the putaminal regions, in patients with WD. This association was more pronounced in individuals not receiving copper chelators and appeared to be attenuated in those treated with copper‐chelating agents. Our findings strengthen the evidence supporting a link between brain metal deposition and glial cell activation in patients with WD.

Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported elevated plasma NfL levels in patients with WD, particularly among those with neurological involvement, and demonstrated associations with clinical severity [18, 19, 36, 37, 38]. In addition, one study showed that plasma GFAP levels were significantly higher in WD patients with neurological manifestations compared to those with hepatic presentations alone and healthy controls [17]. Our results further support these observations by demonstrating a positive correlation between plasma GFAP levels and disease severity, as measured by the UWDRS score in patients with WD. Glial activation with neuroinflammation is increasingly recognized as a key contributor to neuronal injury in WD. Excessive accumulation of copper and other metals in the brain can activate glial cells, particularly astrocytes and microglia, leading to the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress [9, 10, 11]. In postmortem pathology studies of patients with WD, prominent activation of astrocytes and iron‐laden macrophages has been observed in the central gray matter, particularly in the basal ganglia [7, 12]. Among these glial cells, Alzheimer type I astrocytes exhibited both immunoreactivity to GFAP and histochemical positivity for copper deposition [13]. In the toxic milk mouse model of WD, immunoreactivity for GFAP and CD11b, which are markers of astrocyte and microglial activation respectively, was significantly increased in the striatum and corpus callosum, accompanied by elevated inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and inflammatory enzymes in these regions [10]. The astrocyte activation and ensuing neuroinflammation are attributed to the excessive copper deposited in the brain. Several mechanisms have been proposed, including oxidative stress [9], mitochondrial toxicity [39], cell membrane damage [40], damage to DNA [41], and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [11]. Consistently, we not only observed an increased plasma GFAP concentration in patients with WD but also demonstrated a correlation with increased metal burden in the brain, particularly in the caudate and putamen. GFAP is a major intermediate filament protein expressed in astrocytes and serves as a well‐established marker of astrocyte activation [42]. During activation, astrocytes undergo morphological changes such as hypertrophy. GFAP facilitates these changes by reorganizing the cytoskeleton. Reactive astrocytes may release pro‐inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, contributing to neuroinflammation [42]. Moreover, although NfL and GFAP are sensitive markers of neuronal and astrocytic injury, they do not directly capture systemic or central nervous system inflammatory responses. Recent evidence suggests that several serum biomarkers indicative of BBB dysfunction, such as intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM‐1), P‐selectin, S100B, and GFAP, are elevated in the peripheral circulation of patients with WD [43]. Notably, ICAM‐1 levels have been shown to correlate with neurological disease severity in WD, supporting its utility as a marker of BBB disruption. These findings imply that neuroinflammatory mediators may cross a compromised BBB and become detectable in systemic circulation. In this regard, future therapeutic targeting of astrocyte activation may serve as an adjunctive strategy in mitigating neuronal injury by reducing copper‐induced astrocyte activation and plasma GFAP level could be a surrogate marker to reflect both the extent of astrocyte activation and brain metal burden.

We observed that plasma GFAP concentrations were positively associated with the extent of brain metal deposition, with the strongest correlations found in the putaminal regions of patients with WD. Elevated magnetic susceptibility in deep brain regions, including the caudate nucleus, putamen, and globus pallidus, was particularly prominent in patients with neurological manifestations [6]. These findings are consistent with previous studies utilizing QSM to assess brain metal burden in WD [4, 28, 44]. Similar susceptibility patterns in the deep gray nuclei have also been reported using susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI) [45] and T2* sequences [46, 47]. Moreover, increased susceptibility in these regions has been associated with greater brain atrophy and functional decline in WD patients [4, 5, 6]. Notably, there was a positive association between plasma GFAP concentrations and the extent of brain metal deposition, particularly in the putaminal regions, in patients with WD. We also observed that the correlation between plasma GFAP concentration and magnetic susceptibility extent was attenuated in WD patients taking copper chelating agents, either D‐penicillamine or trientine, compared with those taking zinc monotherapy. Aside from chelating copper, D‐penicillamine is well known for its anti‐inflammation activity, especially for rheumatoid arthritis [48]. In vivo animal experiments have demonstrated that D‐penicillamine can interfere with macrophage function to mitigate the inflammatory response [49]. The usage of D‐penicillamine has been reported to reduce inflammation and liver fibrosis in patients with WD [50]. In addition, trientine, another copper‐chelating agent, reportedly inhibits inflammation in multiple organs, including liver [51], eye [52], and heart [53, 54], by downregulating several inflammatory signaling pathways, including those of NF‐κB and calcineurin–nuclear factor of activated T cells [55, 56]. On the other hand, although some studies and clinical observations suggest that zinc therapy may reduce neuroinflammatory signaling indirectly by lowering brain copper levels and oxidative stress [57], our results suggest that zinc may be less effective than copper chelators at reversing established neuroinflammation, especially in symptomatic patients. Further experimental studies are warranted to investigate the mechanisms of copper chelators or zinc in inhibiting astroglial activation in WD. In addition to reflecting copper deposition, alterations observed on MRI susceptibility sequences, particularly with QSM, are more closely associated with the accumulation of intracellular iron and ferritin in deep gray matter regions [46]. In WD, iron predominantly accumulates in astrocytes and macrophages due to ATP7B dysfunction, which disrupts normal iron efflux mechanisms [58, 59, 60]. Moreover, iron overload has been proposed to exacerbate neuroinflammatory responses through microglial activation [61] and to contribute to neuronal injury via ferroptosis [62]. Our findings further support the potential role of iron accumulation in neurodegeneration in WD and reinforce the growing evidence linking brain iron deposition with glial cell activation in this disorder [63, 64].

A major strength of this study lies in the incorporation of clinical, genetic, and pharmacological data, allowing us to assess the potential mitigating effects of copper‐chelating agents on the relationship between metal accumulation and astrocytic injury in WD. However, this study has several limitations. First, both plasma sampling and brain QSM imaging were performed at a single time point, which may not adequately capture longitudinal changes in symptom severity. A prospective cohort study is needed to better evaluate how neuroinflammatory biomarkers and metal depositions evolve with disease progression. Second, all enrolled patients were under stable treatment regimens, and no treatment‐naïve individuals were included. The treatment strategies were heterogeneous, as they were determined based on individual clinical presentations and the standard practices at the time of treatment initiation. This variability may have introduced bias at the initial disease stage and could potentially influence long‐term disease trajectories. Third, the analysis of genotype interactions with the astrocyte activation marker and susceptibility markers was based on a relatively small sample size after stratification. Given the rarity of WD, larger studies are necessary to further clarify the role of genotype, particularly the effects of specific variants, on the pathophysiological mechanisms observed. Finally, we did not evaluate plasma biomarkers reflective of BBB integrity or systemic inflammatory cytokines. Future studies incorporating a more comprehensive biomarker panel, including markers of neuroaxonal injury, astrocytic activation, BBB dysfunction (e.g., ICAM‐1), and proinflammatory cytokines, in larger WD cohorts are needed to more comprehensively characterize the pathophysiological mechanisms involved.

In conclusion, our findings provide evidence of a positive correlation between plasma astrocyte activation markers and brain metal burden, particularly in deep gray matter regions, suggesting a possible saturation effect. This correlation may be attenuated by the use of copper‐chelating agents. Future studies with larger, longitudinal cohorts are needed to clarify the temporal dynamics of neuroinflammation and metal deposition and to evaluate the potential of plasma biomarkers as tools for monitoring treatment response and personalizing therapeutic strategies in WD.
