Integrating interstitial lung abnormality detection into lung cancer screening: a mixed methods approach for care pathway development
Authors: Da Hye Kim, Vincent Dinculescu, Alain Tremblay, Bhavika Kaul, Kerri A Johannson, Amanda Grant-Orser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRationaleInterstitial lung abnormalities (ILAs) are incidental parenchymal findings detected on CT scans. There is an estimated 4%–17% prevalence of ILAs in lung cancer screening (LCS) cohorts, associated with increased all-cause mortality. A substantial proportion of fibrotic ILAs progresses to interstitial lung disease (ILD), offering an important opportunity for early identification and management of ILD. However, current reporting and management strategies for ILAs are heterogeneous, underscoring the need for standardised approaches.ObjectiveTo explore current practice patterns among primary care physicians (PCPs), respirologists and radiologists and to assess the need for a standardised care pathway for ILAs identified through LCS programmes.MethodsOnline surveys and one-on-one semistructured interviews were conducted with PCPs, respirologists and radiologists practising in Alberta, Canada. Interviews were conducted using the Theoretical Domains Framework and Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research. Survey results are presented descriptively. Qualitative data were transcribed, coded and analysed according to grounded theory principles.ResultsA total of 20 PCPs and 39 respirologists completed surveys while 9 PCPs, 10 respirologists and 8 radiologists were interviewed. Sixty-one per cent of PCPs had not heard of the term ILA previously and only 13% were comfortable managing ILAs. Most respirologists (64%) did not follow a standardised approach to managing ILAs. Most PCPs and respirologists supported the development of a standardised care pathway for ILAs detected through LCS. Thematic analysis explored current knowledge and perspectives on management, clinical approach and recommendations for future directions. Barriers to a standardised pathway included heterogeneous reporting, knowledge gaps in the natural history of ILAs and healthcare resource constraints.ConclusionsThis mixed-methods study provides valuable insight into the practice patterns and perceptions of PCPs, respirologists and radiologists regarding ILAs. Given the anticipated increase in ILA detection, we have conducted a needs assessment that will be foundational to building standardised care pathways for ILAs.

Introduction

Interstitial lung abnormalities (ILAs) are defined as incidental radiologic findings in individuals without known or suspected interstitial lung disease (ILD).1 2 Although often asymptomatic at the time of detection, ILAs have important clinical implications, including an accelerated rate of lung function decline and increased all-cause mortality rates.3 4 Moreover, longitudinal studies indicate that the rate of ILA progression varies, ranging from 11% to 63% depending on the underlying demographics and imaging characteristics of the source cohort.57 Furthermore, evaluation by an ILD multidisciplinary team (MDT) can result in a diagnosis of ILD in up to 31% of patients initially identified with ILAs.8 9 ILD itself remains a frequently underdiagnosed condition, associated with substantial morbidity and mortality.10 Given that clinical symptoms often manifest late in the disease course, the incidental detection of ILAs may offer a critical window for early detection and prevention of ILD.1012

The prevalence of ILAs is estimated to be 4%–17% in lung cancer screening (LCS) populations.58 With the implementation of LCS programmes, there is an expected increased rate of ILA detection and referrals to respirologists, in part due to the shared risk factors between ILA and lung cancer, including older age and smoking.6 While the Fleischner Society and American Thoracic Society (ATS) have proposed assessment suggestions based on the risk of progression, there remains heterogeneous reporting and referral practices.12 9 1114

LCS has been piloted in Alberta, which operates a publicly funded and universal healthcare system.15 16 Currently, primary care physicians (PCPs) are responsible for managing any incidental findings identified through the Alberta LCS Program.17 This mixed-method study reviews the current knowledge and practice patterns of PCPs, respirologists and radiologists regarding ILAs and explores the factors contributing to the current heterogeneous landscape of ILA detection and management.

Methods

Study design

This mixed-methods study collected quantitative data from online surveys and qualitative data from interviews. Eligible participants were PCPs, and respirologists licensed to practice medicine in Alberta, Canada. Surveys consisted of questions including demographics, knowledge and perceptions about ILAs (online supplemental figure E1). All responses were anonymous. PCPs were recruited through email newsletters to practices who participated in the LCS pilot project, the University of Calgary’s research website, and via social media. All licensed respirologists in Alberta were invited to participate via email.

Survey respondents were invited to participate in one-on-one interviews. In addition, all thoracic radiologists participating in the LCS programme were invited, as LCS scans in Alberta are exclusively interpreted by thoracic radiologists. Purposeful sampling of non-thoracic radiologists across Alberta was also performed to enhance the external validity of this work, given many LCS programmes in other jurisdictions rely on general radiologists. In addition, it is anticipated general radiologists will be included in the Alberta LCS Program as it expands. Interviews were semistructured using the Theoretical Domains Framework and Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (online supplemental figure E2).18 Individual interviews were conducted over ZOOM and moderated by one member of the study team (AG-O). All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim using NVivo software. This study took place between May and December 2024.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to report survey responses. Several answers were condensed to a 3-point Likert scale to improve sensitivity, given the low number of some responses. Fisher’s exact test was used to measure potential associations among categorical variables. A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS software (V.29.0.1.1).

Interview transcripts were analysed based on the methodological principles of grounded theory19 and reported based on the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (online supplemental figure E3).20 21 Field notes were taken. Two investigators (AG-O and DHK) analysed the transcripts independently line-by-line and descriptive codes were applied. Codes were used to develop themes. A third coder (KAJ) assisted in the case of disagreement. A priori sample sizes were not specified. Rather interviews were conducted until data saturation was achieved. Data saturation was defined as the point at which no new codes were identified and was reached after six interviews for PCPs, five interviews for respirologists and three interviews for radiologists. A second review of transcripts refined the codes and themes, and direct quotes were extracted for theme illustration.

Patient and public involvement

Patients were not involved in the study design of this project.

Results

Study population

Twenty PCPs completed the survey, from an unknown denominator. Thirty-nine respirologists completed the survey, with a survey response rate of 33% (n=39/118). Baseline characteristics of the cohort are presented in table 1. Most PCPs (84%) were associated with community-based practices. The majority of respirologists (87%) were affiliated with academic institutions; however, they were general respirologists with ILD patients comprising less than 20% of their clinical cohort. Within the past year, the majority (72%) of PCPs saw between 1 and 5 patients with ILAs, while 17% reported seeing no patients with ILAs. Over half (53%) of respirologists saw between 1 and 10 patients with ILAs, with remaining respirologists seeing >10 patients with ILAs over the prior year.

Few respirologists currently follow a standardised approach to the assessment and management of ILAs (21%) (table 2). General respirology practices were identified as the preferred setting to manage patients (figure 1). There were no significant associations between comfort level in managing ILAs and ILD patient practice volume (p=0.16), years in practice (p=0.23) or practice location (p=0.47). Most respirologists agreed there should be a standardised referral pathway (79%).

Thematic analysis

A total of 9 PCPs, 10 respirologists and 8 radiologists completed semistructured interviews.

Primary care physicians

PCPs were not familiar with the term ILA and conceptualised ILA on the same spectrum as ILD (figure 2, online supplemental table E1). They reported minimal exposure to patients with ILD during their training and practice in comparison to more common pulmonary conditions. PCPs acknowledged their important role as the first clinical contact for patients with ILA or potential ILD. They were concerned with potentially missed or late detection of ILD in their primary practice.

Thematic analysis of interviews conducted with primary care physicians, respirologists and radiologists. HRCT, high-resolution CT; ILA, interstitial lung abnormality; ILD, interstitial lung disease; PFT, pulmonary function test.

It’s clear to me that the main care gap is actually the timely recognition of ILD in our primary care population.

Once ILA or ILD was identified, PCPs reviewed relevant symptoms and risk factors while considering further investigations, but ultimately all patients with ILA and ILD were referred to respirologists. They reported positive experiences with accessing respirologists via e-referral systems, central triages or pre-existing relationships in the community.

Similar to other incidental findings, PCPs did not expect the additional volume of ILAs through LCS to be overwhelming to their practice, if clear directions are given.

… to me it’s not that much extra work to explain to the patient and to order whatever, just a thyroid ultrasound or a Dexa scan… it’s incremental work, certainly, but I don't think it’s overwhelming.

PCPs did not routinely reference pulmonary guidelines. Rather, when available, they routinely referred to radiologists’ reports for initial management and incorporated radiologists’ recommendations into their clinical decision-making including the need and frequency of follow-up investigations. PCPs referred to specialists less often as a result:

I think it’s wise because from a systems point of view, for every [radiology report] sentence… it prevents the MD to MD call for me, which saves the system money, because with the call, [the government] loses XX bucks from me and loses probably a hundred bucks from a specialist… If the radiologist puts it in that sentence, I don't need to initiate a call, and I'm happy… not having to bother a respirologist.

There was a perception of variability in reporting and recommendations by radiologists, therefore PCPs advocated for standardisation among radiologists.

PCPs valued care pathways for other incidental findings and supported its development for ILAs. The beneficial aspects included the ease of access, up-to-date information and inclusion of a concise summary outlining next steps with literature references for further review. PCPs did not reach consensus on which communication tools were most effective for knowledge dissemination. Most PCPs discussed continuing medical education, local weekly seminars that introduce new care pathways and newsletters via email or fax. Consult letters from respirologists were found to be informative and educational.

Respirologists

General respirologists and ILD specialists commonly received referrals for incidental CT findings suggestive of ILA and ILD, but less than expected for the quoted prevalence in the literature (figure 2, online supplemental table E2). Respirologists felt identifying ILA was an important opportunity to detect ILD early. They discussed the need for guidelines on standardised reporting of ILAs.

… for the reported prevalence of ILAs, we are not seeing that much, which probably suggests that there is a gap in reporting or perhaps there is a gap in paying attention to what is reported… I think that there’s a lot that’s missing.

Respirologists found radiologists to be instrumental in detecting and directing initial management of ILA by means of their reports. For example:

… radiologists are so central to reporting [ILA]. And they are the gatekeepers and the first point at which this can be detected, having easy to understand clearly laid out advice/directive from radiology at a point when it’s detected would be very helpful to the family practitioners.

However, respirologists noted variability in radiologists’ reporting practices. Examples included differences between thoracic and non-thoracic radiologists as well as the reporting of ILA within the main body of the report with omission in the final impression section. They advocated for standardised reporting to ensure all ILAs are identified and clinically assessed. Suggestions for improvement included development of evidence-informed guidelines for radiologists and designation of a group of radiologists or thoracic radiologists for LCS.

Even though our radiology colleagues might be detecting and reporting [ILAs], if it’s not clearly laid out in the final impression, sometimes these things can get missed…I think everything starts with having uniform reporting. How do you quantify it? Is there a better way to help our radiologists? Because right now it’s a visual estimation and we know even with visual estimations, especially when you're talking about narrow margins of 5% and 6% or 10%, that can get blurry.

Respirologists felt they should assess ILAs and support PCPs in their management. Both general respirologists and ILD specialists recommended that initial assessment and management should be performed by general respirologists with involvement of ILD specialists for challenging cases. The term ILA was felt to be poorly defined though, with variable interpretation by respirologists, depending on their subspecialty training. For example, one ILD specialist shared:

ILA is an ongoing research definition and terminology that requires careful evaluation. And I don’t think we have reached consensus yet… I think there’s a common opinion amongst some of the ILD specialists. But whether or not that translates to general respirologists, I’m not entirely sure.

Respirologists agreed that ILAs require monitoring but were concerned regarding the paucity of evidence on management and long-term follow-up. They would typically conduct an initial risk assessment, including review of symptoms, CTs, pulmonary function test (PFTs) if available, and overall risk of ILD. Respirologists employed a patient-specific approach on follow-up frequency in part due to lack of supportive evidence.

I would typically do some type of monitoring for those patients to try to ensure that they don't develop an issue…I would at least get two time points to show that there’s no progression in those abnormalities or features of significant or progressive ILD. But its definitely case by case…And that’s probably suboptimal from a process perspective.

Respirologists expected higher volume of referrals with the implementation of LCS. This was a concern from both general respirologists and ILD specialists who are already working at maximal capacity. They recognised geographic barriers to standardising care, with disproportionate access to some healthcare services.

Respirologists believed most patients with ILA should be assessed by a respirologist. Respirologists agreed developing a clear action plan would be beneficial for PCPs. The care pathway should include recommendations for further testing (eg. PFTs, CTs) and respirology referral. Suggestions included a central triage system to direct patients with ILA to a dedicated committee or a respirologist, who would then formulate a plan for further investigations and follow-up. They acknowledged the limited capacity of respirologists and suggested innovative care models with recruitment of allied healthcare professionals for initial triage.

I think everything starts with having uniform reporting and guidelines on when can you keep the patient and watch? Is there something that the [PCP] can do on their own? For example, if somebody has a non-subpleural ILA, no risk factors, is that something that they could watch if there’s no clinical symptoms versus refer to a respirologist. I think these are the kinds of practical things that people actually want and these are the most difficult things to find evidence on and provide clear direction on.

Respirologists identified barriers to developing a care pathway. They highlighted knowledge gaps on the natural history and progression of ILA and the need to carefully consider risk stratification strategies. There were concerns regarding system capacity, particularly the limited availability of ILD specialists, which may contribute to delays in the care of patients with pre-existing ILD.

Radiologists

The Fleischner Society and American College of Radiology were credible sources for radiologists who provide guidance on ILA (figure 2, online supplemental table E3). There was common knowledge among radiologists such as defining ILA when there is >5% of lung involvement. However, quantitative measurements were not routinely used in real-world reporting. Subpleural reticulations were identified as ILA, whereas the presence of honeycombing was more often reported as usual interstitial pneumonia (UIP).

I do find based on the [Fleischner position] paper you can call [honeycombing] ILA, but most radiologists won't call [honeycombing] ILA, they would probably call it UIP… I think the most common one people call ILA is the subpleural reticulation. And then people are not sure if there is underlying ILD then they call it ILA…some of them could be the precursor of ILD. Some of them may stay there for the rest of their life, but not really progress.

Radiologists reported reader variability based on their training, experience and referring physicians’ specialty.

[Non-chest radiologists] may not have an awareness of what it is and therefore, if it’s not known, then it is not seen.

Radiologists believed that ILAs represent an important opportunity for early detection of ILD and should be reported. In comparison to other incidental radiological findings, their clinical significance was felt to be less well-defined. Radiologists felt that most ILAs are detected on non-high-resolution CT (HRCT) imaging and not all types of ILA required further investigation with HRCT. Reading efficiency was an ongoing concern but radiologists did not expect the additional reporting of ILAs to be highly burdensome. They were concerned with limited access to respirologists. For example:

I would love if [ILA detection in LCS] is implemented, would be nice to have some feedback… and parameters so that we’re not misappropriately using resources and making comments or referrals to [ILD specialists].

Radiologists highlighted the importance of ILA detection in LCS as these programmes provide a unique opportunity to screen for ILAs. Lung cancer and ILA were recognised to share risk factors.

[ILAs are] fairly important given that the population we deal with are smokers and we already know that they have a higher risk of interstitial fibrosis to begin with. So, I think finding ILA say in that patient [through LCS], it’s important to identify, especially if it looks more like the subpleural and potentially fibrotic form, which could progress

Radiologists believed ILAs should be routinely reported in the impression section. They emphasised the need for future guidelines to provide evidence-based direction on how to report and manage ILAs. While radiologists may provide initial management suggestions, patients with ILA should be reviewed by PCPs or respirologists.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this mixed-methods study is the first to assess the perspectives of PCPs, respirologists and radiologists on ILAs and to identify gaps in current practice. ILAs were widely viewed as a clinically important finding requiring dedicated assessment. All groups noted variability in radiology reporting. While most PCPs and respirologists used initial risk assessment strategies, they reported a lack of standardised management and follow-up. Despite emerging research on ILA prognosis, there was a consistent call for evidence-based guidelines. Most respirologists and radiologists supported a standardised care pathway, and all PCPs endorsed a referral process for ILAs detected through LCS.

Incidental findings contribute, in part, to the cost-effectiveness and mortality benefits of LCS programmes, yet there is inconsistent use of pre-existing radiologic classification frameworks (eg, S-modifier in the Lung-RADS framework, British Thoracic Society guidelines for pulmonary nodules) for reporting of incidental findings.2226 ILAs, in particular, are under- and heterogeneously documented with reporting recommendations differing across LCS programmes.132729 In prior studies, ILAs were documented in the impression section of radiology reports in only 50%–57% of cases, which aligns with the experience of PCPs in our study.9 13 30 ILAs may be underassessed as less than half of PCPs report reading the full report, and many rely on radiologists’ recommendations for initial management of incidental findings.30 Consistent reporting of ILAs is critical to guide subsequent clinical decision-making and potentially patient outcomes.

Current guidelines recommend that patients with ILD be assessed by respirologists within an MDT.31 32 Ideally, individuals with ILAs should undergo similar evaluations.1 However, feasibility may be limited by constrained healthcare resources.2 33 In the Alberta LCS Program, PCPs are the first clinicians to review incidental findings.17 Across LCS programmes, a range of models exist, with either PCPs or nurse practitioners managing these findings.3437 Our results suggest that PCPs hold a neutral stance regarding the anticipated increase in ILA detection, which differs from the barriers identified by other groups to implementing LCS in primary care practice.38 PCPs expressed willingness to manage ILAs, provided they are supported with clear, evidence-based guidance. In contrast, respirologists voiced concerns about the specialised knowledge required to manage ILAs, expertise they felt may exceed the scope of PCP practice. However, even among respirologists, ILA management lacked standardisation, with variability in follow-up practicing such as PFTs.

To address these challenges, the establishment of dedicated ILA clinics has been proposed in several jurisdictions, including the USA and the United Kingdom.9 39 While such clinics could facilitate standardised care, their feasibility in regions outside of subspecialty centres remains uncertain, especially in settings with limited healthcare resources, geographically dispersed populations and existing disparities in care.40 An alternative strategy may involve the development of care pathways that incorporate streamlined respirology e-consultations or referral mechanisms accessible to PCPs. This approach could offer pragmatic and cost-effective means of standardising ILA care in diverse healthcare settings.

Many respirologists in our study expressed concern regarding the overall management of ILAs. There was consensus that all ILAs warrant monitoring, with follow-up strategies tailored to clinical and radiologic risk factors for progression.2 While the new ATS Clinical Statement provides clinicians with some clarity, current recommendations suggest a wide range of follow-up intervals.1 2 Some degree of imaging follow-up is inherently included in LCS programmes through routine annual repeat low-dose CT scans, which could be leveraged for the longitudinal monitoring of ILAs. However, the optimal duration of follow-up remains unclear. A recent study that followed individuals with ILAs up to 23.1 years found disease progression in 80.5% of cases. The median time to progression was 3.2 years (95% CI 3.0 to 3.4 years), though certain cases, particularly those with a UIP pattern, progressed over the course of a decade.14 Quantitative imaging techniques show promise for improving risk stratification and represent an area of ongoing development.2 41 Biomarkers may also offer predictive value for disease progression and clinical outcomes.42 While existing follow-up strategies aim to incorporate progression risk, no standardised approach has yet been established.1 11 14 The natural history of ILAs beyond 10 years remains poorly characterised, and there is uncertainty regarding the need for long-term monitoring and appropriate discharge criteria. These knowledge gaps have implications for both patient care and healthcare resource utilisation, therefore concurrent advancement of clinical care models and research is essential to define best practices and optimise outcomes in this emerging area of respiratory medicine.

This study has limitations. Radiologists were omitted from survey analysis given the pre-existing literature regarding their practice patterns and recommendations.2 43 While invitations to participate were disseminated broadly, study participants were self-selected volunteers, which may have introduced potential selection bias towards physicians with a particular interest or greater familiarity with ILD. The limited population size and specific healthcare context of Alberta may reduce the generalisability of our findings to countries with different healthcare systems and screening programmes. However, the identified recommendations and barriers to developing a care pathway for ILAs may still be relevant to similar jurisdictions, particularly where primary care is responsible for screening programmes. Further studies in varied settings may be required for local needs assessments. In addition, clinical and system-level outcomes from these experience-informed and opinion-informed suggestions will require formal evaluation.

With the continued expansion of LCS programmes, the detection of ILA is expected to increase. Our findings offer a multidisciplinary stakeholder perspective on the current understanding of ILAs and highlight existing gaps in their detection and management. Our needs assessment study is timely given the recent publication of ATS ILA Clinical Statement supporting the systematic evaluation of ILA in LCS programmes. This will help guide discussions regarding strategic planning for LCS programmes.1 While this study provides a preliminary exploration of strategies to standardise ILA care, further research is needed to identify specific mechanisms to support PCPs, optimise healthcare system resources and reduce misdiagnoses. As emerging evidence continues to refine best practices for ILA reporting and follow-up, parallel investment in system-level infrastructure will be essential to ensure that care is equitable, efficient and evidence-based.
