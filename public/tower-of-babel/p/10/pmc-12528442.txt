Effect of listening to preferred music at different frequencies during warmup on physical performance and psychophysiological responses in male athletes
Authors: Nidhal Jebabli, Manar Boujabli, Ibrahim Ouergui, Nejmeddine Ouerghi, Fatma Hilal Yagin, Burak Yagin, Mariem Khlifi, Anissa Bouassida, Abderraouf Ben Abderrahman, Safaa M Elkholi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe present study assessed the effects of listening to preferred music during warm-up at different frequencies (440 Hz vs. 432 Hz) on the physical performance and psychophysiological responses in male athletes. In a double-blind crossover study design, twenty-two male athletes (Mean ± SD: age: 22.4 ± 1.6 years; body mass: 71.8 ± 8.3 kg; height: 1.83 ± 0.1 m; BMI: 22.7 ± 1.9 kg. m− 2 randomly performed the 3-min all out test (3MT), repeated sprint test (RST), squat jump (SJ) and countermovement jump (CMJ) tests after listening to preferred music at 440 Hz–432 Hz frequencies or without music during warm-up. Heart rate was measured during 3MT, Also, rating of perceived exertion (RPE) and feeling scale (FS) were measured just after 3MT and RST tests. Results showed that no music condition induced lower performance compared to listening to preferred music at 440 Hz (mean speed: p < 0.001, d = 0.82; peak speed: p < 0.001, d = 0.85; total distance covered: p < 0.001, d = 0.83; FS: p < 0.001, d = 1.21) and music 432 Hz (mean speed: p < 0.001, d = 0.75; peak speed: p < 0.001, d = 0.58; total distance covered: p < 0.001, d = 0.76, FS: p = 0.004, d = 0.77) during 3MT. In addition, a significant decrease in Total time and Fast time indices of RST, FS, SJ and CMJ values was recorded with music at 440 Hz compared to music 432 Hz (total time: p < 0.001, d = 0.65; fast time: p < 0.001, d = 0.53; FS: p = 0.018, d = 0.59; SJ: p = 0.031, d = 0.3; CMJ: p = 0.007, d = 0.41) and no music condition (total time: p < 0.001, d = 0.89; fast time: p < 0.001, d = 0.57; FS: p < 0.001, d = 1.21; SJ: p < 0.001, d = 0.64; CMJ: p < 0.001, d = 0.54). However, no significant change was observed in heart rate, fatigue index of the RST, or RPE. Listening to preferred music at a fast tempo and moderate volume during warm-up sessions improved physical performance and positive mood irrespective of whether it was tuned to 440 Hz–432 Hz. Introduction

The ergogenic benefits of listening to music on physical performance and psychophysiological responses have been well explored through several studies1–5. Specifically, the potential effect of listening to music has been examined with a variety of exercises including endurance3,6, resistance2, repeated sprint4,7 and strength8,9, in different sports10,11 and different gender11,12. In fact, the beneficial effects of listening to music on physical performance was attributed to the delayed perception of afferent signals related to neurological fatigue13, improving muscular efficiency14, neural activity15 as well as positive mood4, attention16 and self-efficacy17 increase. Over hundreds of studies, it is well-documented that the beneficial influence of music on sport performance depends strongly on different auditory characteristics, such as type, tempo, volume, time and harmony18, as well as the time of listening to music (i.e., pre-task, in-task, and post-task)3,4.

Regarding music properties, previous investigations reported that listening to loud (≥ 70dB) and fast tempo (≥ 120 beats.min− 1) preferred music at improved physical performance3,4,10,11,19.

Despite the evidences supporting the efficacy of interaction between the different acoustic properties of music in various sport settings, little is known about whether the rhythm or the frequency of music, as a sound wave, can modulate the sports’ performances as well as the related psychophysiological responses.

Theoretically, waveforms are the origin of the sounds that make up music, and their frequencies, measured in Hertz (Hz)20, influences the pitch and timbre of musical sounds21. It is commonly known that 440 Hz is the current unnatural reference frequency for tuning musical instruments22. Since 1975, according to the International Organization for Standardization (ISO) most of musical songs were tuned at a standard frequency programming of 440 Hz, called A4 pitch class (https://www.iso.org/standard/3601.html). However, from a musical point of view, some musicians played music using a natural frequency of 432 Hz, with this frequency can be reached by editing music from 440 Hz to 432 Hz using specified music software. In this consideration, Music at 432 Hz is generally considered more calming and harmonious. It has also recently begun to be used during mental preparation and recovery stages, with claims that it can enhance relaxation, reduce anxiety, strengthen the mind-body connection, and improve focus23–25.

In sport science, a recent study by Jebabli, et al.26 examined the effect of preferred music with a high tempo (140 bpm) and moderate volume (80 dB) set to frequencies of 440 Hz and 432 Hz, during warm-up, on the intermittent anaerobic speed kickboxing test. They found that preferred music at 440 Hz improved mood, reduced perceived exertion, and improved performance indices more effective than preferred music at 432 Hz compared to the control condition in both male and female kickboxers. Furthermore, female kickboxers showed a higher emotional response to preferred music at 432 Hz. To our knowledge, this study presents the first evidence of the modulation of athletic performance by musical frequency, although its applicability cannot be generalized to other types of exercise or other participant qualities. Furthermore, one of the most important and overlooked aspects is the interaction between musical frequency and tempo. In fact, previous studies combining music at a frequency of 432 Hz and slow tempo music (56 beats/min) as relaxing effect24. However, Jebabli, et al.26 reported that preferred music at 432 Hz combined with a fast tempo (140 bpm) has an ergogenic effect on physical performance without a stimulating effect on attention.

Preferred Music at fast tempos and a standard frequency of 440 Hz is generally perceived as a clear and precise ergogenic aid. To date, no study has verified whether 432 Hz tuned fast-tempo music can affect aerobic performance outcomes such as endurance running capacity. The gap therefore highlights the novelty of the present study, which extends the research scope of musical frequency from anaerobic to aerobic and anaerobic exercise modes.

To clarify, to the best of our knowledge, no study has experimentally manipulated the tuning frequency of the same fast-tempo preferred music its effect on aerobic as well as anaerobic performance outcomes. The present study was therefore designed (i) to determine whether 440 Hz and 432 Hz have differential effects on 3-min all-out running, repeated-sprint and vertical jump performance, and (ii) to assess the concomitant psychophysiological (HR, RPE, mood) responses in male athletes.

According to previous research26,27, it was hypothesized that fast tempo and 440 Hz tuned music of one’s choice listened during warm-up will be more effective in enhancing aerobic performance. In contrast, as the 432 Hz tuned music has been found to be associated with higher relaxation, emotional involvement, and lower stress responses We also hypothesized that a frequency of 432 Hz would show higher effectiveness in reducing the ratings of perceived exertion and improving mood.

Materials and methods

Participants

The sample size for this study was estimated based on a study by Jebabli, et al.3 who studied the effect of listening to preferred music on physical and psychophysiological responses with some similar outcome variables (i.e., TDC; Smean; Speak) of the present study. Thus, the sample size was carried out with a statistical power of 0.80, a probability level of 0.05 and an effect size of 0.25 using the G*Power 3.1.9.7 software. (Franz Faul, University of Kiel, Kiel, Germany). The findings showed that in order to get 80% of statistical power, 22 participants would be required. Twenty-two males (Mean ± SD: age, 22.4 ± 1.6 years; body mass, 71.8 ± 8.3 kg; body height, 1.83 ± 0.1 m; BMI, 22.7 ± 1.9 kg. m-2) volunteered to participate in the present study. Participants were male sport-science student-athletes, specializing in athletics, with previous competitive experience in the sport (5 ± 1 years), and engaged in a variety of others sports, including combat sports, basketball, and football. They performed an average regular physical activity of 16 h/week as part of their weekly training as sport science students.

All participants met the inclusion criteria, including regular training and the absence of cardiovascular or musculoskeletal disorders. Exclusion criteria were the use of performance impairing medications, recent limbs musculoskeletal injuries (less than 6 months), or previous experience with 432 Hz music. Only men were included to obtain a homogeneous sample and exclude the confounding effect of sex-related hormonal and physiological differences.

Participants were instructed to abstain from using any nutritional supplements or performance aids that might affect their performance, alcohol, and strenuous physical exercise 48 h prior to each testing session.

After a detailed explanation about the aims and risks involved in the study, participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted following the last Declaration of Helsinki for human experimentation and was approved by a local research ethics committee of high institute of sport and physical education of Kef (March 09, 2022; UR22JS01 / ISSEP-015-22) before data collection.

Experimental procedures

This study is a randomized, double-blind, crossover design examining the effect of listening to preferred music during warm-up at different frequencies on the physical performance and psychophysiological responses.

After familiarization sessions with the test procedures, instruments and scales, participants were exposed to three conditions during warm up: (1) listening to preferred music at 440 Hz, (2) listening to preferred music at 432 Hz and (3) neutral self-talk with no-music during warm-up (Fig. 1).

Schematic representation of the study design. Note: SJ: squat jump; CMJ: countermovement jump; HR: heart rate; RPE: rating of perceived exertion; FS: feeling scale.

For each condition, during two separated sessions, participants performed: squat jump test28, countermovement jump test29 and repeated sprint test (RST)30, with interval of 15-min recovery between tests, (2) 3-min all-out exercise test (3MT)31.

During 3-min all-out exercise test, peak heart rate (HRpeak) was recorded using an individual heart rate monitor (Polar TF4, Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland). After 3MT and RST tests, participants were asked about their overall physical perceptions of exertion32 and their feeling state33. Each athlete completed all the tests under the three different conditions, with a 48-hour interval between conditions.

Before each testing session, participants performed a standardized 10-min warm-up, consisting of 4-min of running at an intensity ranging between 110 and 145 beats.min− 1, lateral displacements, dynamic stretching and jumping, followed by 3-min of passive recovery.

Testing sessions were performed at the same time of day (15 ± 1 h) to avoid any diurnal variation of the performance34.

In random order, each participant’s condition was determined using an online randomization tool (https://www.random.org/).

Participants were asked to refrain from any strenuous physical exercise during the testing period, maintain usual hydration, dietary habits, sleep patterns, and not ingest ergogenic supplements (e.g., caffeine, vitamins, etc.) 24 h, before each testing session. All assessments were conducted by the same experienced investigators.

Music characteristics

During familiarization sessions, Participants were asked to select their standard preferred music to be played during warm-up of each session. All participants preferred to repeat the same songs in both test sessions (see supplementary material file). Using the “Edjing Mix” application (version 6.45.00, Android, France, the average tempo of the preferred songs chosen by the participants was characterized by a fast rhythm (Mean ± SD: 130 ± 10 bpm). All preferred tracks were played 10 min for each warm-up session using the same wireless player (AirPods Pro, Apple, US) and the same volume (i.e., moderate volume, 70 dB) for all participants. Each athlete listened to their preferred music for 10 min. This was either via a single track of that duration or through repetition of the chosen song with the same procedure applied in all conditions.

Using double-blind design, all the preferred music were recorded with two different frequencies, 440 Hz and 432 Hz (WAV format), using the “Audio Processing Object (APO)” software by independent investigators who didn’t directly take part in the present study.

Participants were informed that the musical condition would be played twice, with no mention of the frequency difference. During music conditions, no participants reported any changes in auditory frequency shift.

During no music condition, the same wireless player was worn but no music was played. Additionally, during the experimental sessions, no verbal feedback or verbal encouragement was given to the participants by the investigators.

Measures

3-min all-out exercise test

The 3MT is primarily used to assess aerobic capacity through critical velocity. The 3MT was performed in a 400-meter outdoor track. Athletes were instructed to run as maximum as possible during 3-min31. Athletes were informed about the duration of the test and no Verbal encouragement was provided. Each athlete wore a GPS wrist watch (Forerunner Model 305, Garmin, Taiwan) to measure their mean speed (Smean), peak speed (Speak), and the total distance (TDC). Heart rate monitors (Polar team 2, Polar Electro Oy, Finland) were used to measure both the peak heart rate (HRpeak) and mean heart rate (HRmean) data.

The RPE scale (6–20 Borg-scale; 32) was used to assess perceived internal training load immediately after the 3MT. Furthermore, the affective dimension (pleasure and displeasure) was measured using the feeling scale, which ranged from − 5 (very poor) to + 5 (very pleasant)33. “Try to inform us, by a number, on how you’re feeling without concern yourself with feelings of physical stress, effort, and fatigue,” question of the instructions. The FS was presented shortly after the RPE scale.

The intra-class correlation coefficient35 for test–retest trial for the present study for Smean, Speak and TDC was 0.930, 0.850 and 0.930, respectively.

Repeated sprint test

Repeated-sprint capacity is a critical indicator of an athlete’s capability to generate maximal power in short efforts and to sustain resistance against fatigue during successive sprints, with the involvement of both anaerobic provision of energy and aerobic processes of recovery. Athletes performed 06 maximal 20-m sprints interspersed by 20 s of active recovery 30. Electronic photocell gates were used to assess test performance (Globus, Microgate, Italy) with an accuracy of 0.01 s. No verbal encouragement was provided during test. After test completion, fast sprint time (FST) (i.e., fastest sprint time recorded during the six sprints), total sprint time (TST) were obtained. Squat jump and countermovement jump test

Jump tests, including squat and countermovement tests, are commonly used to assess an athlete’s explosive power as well as their lower body neuromuscular function.

For squat jump test (SJ), athletes were informed to begin their jump with their feet shoulder-width apart, their hands on their waists, and their knees flexed 90 degrees. They were informed to jump as high as they could while keeping their hand on their waist29.

Counter movement jump test (CMJ) began by standing with a straight back, legs fully extended, and feet shoulder-width apart. Athletes were instructed to keep their hands on their waists the entire time they were jumping. They were instructed to quickly bend their knees to about 90 degrees and then a quick upward motion to jump as high as they can. Jump heights were calculated using the My Jump 2 application for android36. ICC values for test–retest trial for the present study for SJ and CMJ tests were 0.818 and 0.857, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as means and standard deviation (SDs). Normal data distribution was tested and confirmed using the Shapiro Wilk test. Test-retest reliability was assessed for all variables using Cronbach’s model of the intraclass correlation coefficient and the coefficient of variation (CV)35.

Repeated one-way ANOVA (condition) was performed for 3-MT indices, HRpeak, HRmean, RSA indices, RPE, FS, SJ and CMJ to discern between condition differences.

When significant differences were observed, LSD adjusted post-hoc tests were used. Partial eta squared (η2p) effect size value was taken from ANOVA output and was classified as 0.01 = small, 0.09 = medium, 0.25 = large37. Cohen’s d (d) was calculated to quantify meaningful differences in the data with demarcations of trivial (< 0.2), small (0.2–0.59), medium (0.60–1.19), large (1.2–1.99), and very large (≥ 2.0)28.

Statistical significance was accepted, a priori, at p < 0.05. All data were analyzed using the SPSS 22 package (SPSS Inc., Chicago. IL).

Discussion

This study aimed at investigating the effects of listening to preferred music during warm-up at different frequencies (i.e., 440 Hz vs. 432 Hz) on physical performances, heart rate, perceived exertion and mood state in young active males. The main results of the present study showed that fast tempo preferred music at 440 Hz resulted increase performance in peak speed of 3MT, total time and fast time indices of RST, CMJ and SJ better than preferred music at 432 Hz and compared to no music condition. Moreover, preferred music conditions (440 Hz; 432 Hz) resulted in positive mood compared to no music condition after 3MT and RST tests. However, no significant difference was observed on heart rate during 3MT, fatigue index of RST and RPE between conditions. These results partially confirm our hypotheses.

The present study is the first to cut the tuning frequency of preferred music under otherwise identical acoustic conditions (musical identity, loudness, tempo). Previous literature has virtually always used the 440 Hz standard implicitly and without mention, and the sole available comparison26 was limited to an intermittent kickboxing specific test in male and female kickboxers. By cross testing the frequency question against continuous high intensity running, repeated sprint ability, and vertical jumps in trained men, our findings address an important gap in the literature and suggest that the standard 440 Hz tuning is more ergogenic than 432 Hz tuning.

The results of the present study indicated that Smean, Speak and TDC were improved during preferred music at 440 Hz, better than 432 Hz, compared to no music. The results of the present study support those previously reported by numerous studies that highlighted the beneficial effect of listening to music, only using standard frequency (at 440 Hz) as a tuning reference, on continuous aerobic performance (> 3 min)3,38.

For example, Jebabli, et al.3 showed that preferred music at fast tempo (140 beat.min− 1) and moderate loudness (70 dB), using standard frequency as a tuning reference, improved significantly total distance covered (8%) and mean running speed (8%) during 6-min all-out running exercise.

Currently, the physiological mechanism underlying the improvement in physical performance in the present study has not yet been demonstrated. Nevertheless, even without awareness of the subtle auditory frequency shift, athletes’ habitual exposure to the 440 Hz musical frequency in most training environments appears to have diverted their attention from unpleasant exercise related sensations while increasing their endurance capacity more effectively than with music presented at 432 Hz.

Physiologically, the stronger response to the 440 Hz frequency may be related to its greater familiarity, which facilitates increased cortical processing and attentional deployment39. In addition, stimulation at 440 Hz has been correlated with more stable autonomic regulation marked by sympathetic-parasympathetic balance and possible increased activity in motivational and persevering dopaminergic pathways40,41.

The less significant improvement at 432 Hz may therefore be more a function of the interaction between novelty and reduced attentional load than of any aversive stimulus. Further investigations should delineate whether tutor-derived differences in expectancy or subtle perceptive cues at a 432 Hz center frequency exert a modulatory role on endurance performance.

The current findings indicate that preferred music condition at 440 Hz had a significant decrease in the TT and FT indices of the RSA test, compared to music 432 Hz and no music condition. However, no significant differences were observed in FI between conditions.

Those results are supported by previous studies showing that preferred music during warm-up, using standard frequency, has a beneficial effect on repeated sprint ability26,42,43. For example, Jebabli, et al.26 observed that listening to music with a high tempo (140 bpm), moderate volume (80 dB), and a frequency of 440 Hz–432 Hz improved physical performance for a specific anaerobic speed test for kickboxing when compared to no music condition. They found that the best way to improve a particular performance was to use warm-up music with a standard frequency of about 440 Hz.

Jebabli, et al.26 mentioned that 440 Hz frequency is more common among kickboxers, which is evidenced by higher improvement in body performance, without addressing neurological justifications.

However, other studies have not proven any effect of listening to music with standard frequency4,44. An examination of the literature shows that the differences between the present findings of previous research may be due to some methodological issues. Indeed, the choice of the repeated sprint test affects the power output including the specificity of the test (e.g. running, cycling, number of sprints, distance etc.). Moreover, some studies report that untrained subjects responded significantly more to music than trained subjects and the ergogenic effects of music gradually decrease with a considerable increase in physical level42,43.

The current findings indicated that listening to preferred music 440 Hz during warm-up enhanced SJ and CMJ height compared to preferred music 432 Hz and no music condition.

To our knowledge, no study has evaluated the effect of preferred music at different frequencies on jumps tests. However, our results are consistent with those reported by Ghazel, et al.12, who found that listening to preferred music with a standard frequency and fast tempo (140 bpm) during warm-up significantly improved SJ performance in elite female handball players. Similarly, Belkhir, et al.45 showed that mean and maximum height of continuous vertical jumps (30-sec) improved after listening to motivational music during warm-up in the afternoon (5 pm) compared to no music condition. In other side, listening to preferred music failed to induce significant effect on resisted vertical squat jumps’ height46. These results should be interpreted with caution since there are several moderators that should be taken into consideration when comparing the results of the present study and those of previous investigations including music characteristics (e.g., music type, tempo, duration), participants’ characteristics (e.g., sex, training status). Moreover, the frequency of the music selected, for the previous studies, is absolutely not mentioned.

During aerobic exercise, HR and RPE become more salient thereby minimizing the impact of listening to music27,47. However, in the present study, while participants performed better in both sessions of preferred music, no significant difference was recorded between sessions in term of HRmean, HRpeak and RPE values, which contradicts our hypothesis. In fact, participants were able to run faster during 3MT after listening to preferred music (at both 440 Hz and 432 Hz), with a similar cardiovascular responses and same perceived effort at the end of test. Therefore, the stability of heart rate and RPE, in the present study, did not seem to have a negative impact on physical performance as well as the affective state of the participants.

Considering affective state, the present study showed that listening to preferred music with varied frequencies improved mood after both aerobic and anaerobic exercises compared with no music condition. To the best of knowledge, this study is the among the first that investigated the effect of preferred music at different frequencies on post-exercise mood after aerobic and anaerobic exercises. However, the results of previous investigations reported that listening to music with standard frequency, during warm-up, improve positively mood during and after physical exercise11,26,48.

It is well known that psychological responses related to well-being, emotions, behavior and cognitive aspect could affect physical performance40. Indeed, cortical processing and visceral information about the subject’s behavior and physiological state when listening to music may be linked to positive emotion and motivation10,26,49. The results of the present study showed that listening to preferred music at 440 Hz and 432 Hz were beneficial to improve feeling scale during 3MT compared to no music. These results may confirm the positive impact of listening to music in improving mood, which positively affect physical performance in endurance exercise.

For RST, the positive FS was observed to be more effective with preferred music at 440 Hz compared to preferred music at 432 Hz and no music conditions. These results are partially consistent with the findings of Jebabli, et al.26, who investigated kickboxers undergoing a specific anaerobic speed test while listening to preferred music during warm-up. In fact, both 440 Hz and 432 Hz music frequencies improved the FS scores of the participants during the anaerobic speed test. However, some gender difference in positive mood, as women were affected by the frequency difference more than men.

Theoretically, Tuis35 explains that music tuned to 432 Hz may be better received by listeners at rest because it induces stronger activity in the left prefrontal cortex, indicating higher emotional engagement and preference which is not the case in the present study35.

The reasons for this discrepancy remain unclear. However, it can likely be explained by several factors, including the participants’ level of expertise (trained, untrained), participant state (resting, active), participant gender, familiar music frequency and the timing of the FS test (before, during, and after exercise).

The current study provides relevant insights about the efficacy of using pre-task preferred music with standard frequency (440 Hz) in improving performance. However, it is of importance to highlight some limitations of this study. First, the absence of indicators like neuronal activation, which offer further information on physiological mechanisms during physical exercise after listening to preferred music at various frequencies. Second, the present study was conducted only within active men that may limit the generalization of the results. Third, the use of preferred music with non-standard frequencies, such as 432 Hz, may be more effective when restricted to the warm-up phase rather than the testing or recovery periods. Future research should investigate whether the timing of exposure to preferred music at different frequencies affects performance and physiological responses during physical exercise.

Conclusions

The findings of the present study showed that listening to preferred music during warm-up at fast tempo, moderate loudness and standard frequency (i.e., 120–140 BPM; 70 dB; 440 Hz) is the most effective combination for enhancing both aerobic and anaerobic performance without the need to alter the musical frequency. As well, feeling scale was increased following preferred music at 440 Hz more than music at 432 Hz, while heart rate, fatigue index and RPE values deemed unchangeable. From a practical perspective, athletes, coaches, and physical conditioning professionals can use preferred music at a fast tempo, moderate loudness and standard frequency during warm-up to enhance physical performance and feeling scale.
