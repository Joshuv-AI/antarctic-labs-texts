Optimizing α-galactosidase A enzyme cutoff for Fabry disease screening in chronic kidney disease patients: a single-center reassessment of diagnostic yield and cost-effectiveness
Authors: Shang-Feng Tsai, Ming-Ju Wu, Hsien-Hsu Hsieh, Cheng-Hsu Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFabry disease has been increasingly recognized in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). While dried blood spot (DBS) testing for α-galactosidase A (α-Gal A) activity is a common screening method, the optimal cutoff value for enzyme activity in this population remains uncertain. We retrospectively analyzed data from a Fabry disease screening program conducted between October 1, 2018, and September 31, 2023, at Taichung Veterans General Hospital. Male patients with CKD or ESKD underwent DBS α-Gal A enzyme activity testing. Patients with enzyme activity <2.0 μmol/L/hr were referred for confirmatory genetic testing. We assessed the diagnostic yield, baseline characteristics of true positive, false positive, and true negative cases, and performed receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis to identify the optimal diagnostic threshold. Among 1,654 screened patients, 35 had enzyme activity <2.0 μmol/L/hr, of whom 6 (0.36%) were genetically confirmed as having Fabry disease. Broadening the screening threshold from 1.2 to 2.0 μmol/L/hr enabled the identification of two additional cases. ROC analysis identified an optimal cutoff value of 1.96 μmol/L/hr (AUROC 0.996, 95%CI: 0.991–1.000), yielding a sensitivity of 98.3% (95CI%: 0.9769–0.9891) and specificity of 100% (95%CI:1-1). Patients with false-positive results had significantly lower leukocyte counts than true-positive cases. The incremental cost to detect two additional Fabry patients was $8,010 USD, representing acceptable cost-effectiveness under Taiwan’s healthcare framework. Expanding α-Gal A enzyme activity cutoff value from 1.2 to 2.0 μmol/L/hr improved the diagnostic performance of Fabry disease screening in CKD patients and proved to be cost-effective.Keywords: Fabry disease, alpha galactosidase (α-Gal) activity, cutoff value, chronic kidney disease, hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis

Introduction

Taiwan has one of the highest global incidences and prevalences of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage kidney disease (ESKD). A community-based screening program conducted between 1999 and 2009 reported a CKD stage 3–5 prevalence of 9.06%, with an incidence rate of 16.89 per 1,000 person-years [1]. In 2014, the incidence and prevalence of treated ESKD in Taiwan reached 455 and 3,219.4 per million population (pmp), respectively—the highest reported worldwide [2]. Although the incidence of treated ESKD slightly declined to 525 pmp by 2020, Taiwan continued to rank among the countries with the highest burden of ESKD, as noted in the 2022 United States Renal Data System (USRDS) Annual Report [3]. These figures highlight the substantial public health burden of kidney disease in Taiwan and emphasize the need for sustained prevention, early detection, and comprehensive management strategies targeting CKD and ESKD.

Fabry disease is an X-linked inherited disorder [4] of the glycosphingolipid metabolic pathway, characterized by deficient or absent activity of the enzyme α-galactosidase A (α-Gal A). From a systematic review and meta-analysis, the overall prevalence among dialysis patients was 0.10% [5]. This enzymatic defect leads to the progressive accumulation of glycosphingolipids, particularly globotriaosylceramide (Gb3) [6], in various cell types throughout the body, resulting in the multisystemic and heterogeneous clinical manifestations of the disease [7]. Globotriaosylceramide accumulation initiates a pathological cascade that leads to progressive tissue and organ damage [8,9], including kidney disease. Early diagnosis enables timely treatment; however, many patients are not diagnosed until after the age of 30, often presenting with significant organ involvement by that time [8]. Therefore, many studies have reported the successful implementation of screening protocols for Fabry disease in patients with CKD [10–15]. A recently published expert consensus article reviewed Fabry disease screening data collected between 2010 and 2023 in Taiwan [16], reporting a prevalence of 0.6% among patients with CKD. However, the prevalence of Fabry disease (0.16%) identified through the screening protocol for CKD patients at our institution [11] was lower than previously reported. Therefore, we conducted a reanalysis of our institutional CKD screening database to reassess the diagnostic yield and to explore potential cutoff values that may improve the performance of the screening test (dried blood spot (DBS) testing). We also aimed to analyze potential factors associated with false-positive and true-positive results, as well as to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of using a lower threshold value in the Fabry disease screening test.

Materials and methods

Definition of population and study design

We initiated a generalized screening program for Fabry disease in male patients with CKD or ESKD from October 1, 2018, to September 31, 2023. The initial screening was conducted using DBS testing to measure α-Gal A enzyme activity [17]. The DBS samples were tested by a certified reference laboratory (Archimed Life Science GmbH), which included standard internal controls and used β-galactosidase as a reference enzyme. Sample integrity was verified via simultaneous colorimetric control and extraction efficiency. As previously reported [12,18,19], patients with enzyme activity levels less than 0.6 μmol/L/hr were considered positive in the screening test and subsequently underwent confirmatory genetic testing. Those with enzyme activity between 0.6 and 1.2 μmol/L/hr were classified as suspected positive cases and were also referred for genetic confirmation. In a Spanish population study, the cutoff value was raised to as high as 2.6 μmol/L/hr [20]. In our previous study [11], we also used this threshold (enzyme activity <1.2 μmol/L/hr) to screen patients for confirmatory genetic testing. In that study [11], we recruited 1,812 male patients across pre-ESKD, dialysis, and renal transplant recipient groups between April 2018 and June 2022. The final number of true positive cases by genetic test was only four. Therefore, given the low diagnostic yield within the previous range, we expanded the screening criteria in this study to include patients with enzyme activity levels between 1.2 and 2.0 μmol/L/hr for genetic testing. Patients with enzyme activity between 1.2 and 2.0 μmol/L/hour, as determined by the DBS assay, were recalled to our hospital for genetic testing. We redefined the threshold for a positive enzyme screening test as an activity level below 2.0 μmol/L/hr. Additionally, this study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of broadening the screening cutoff to 2.0 μmol/L/hr in identifying Fabry disease.

Additionally, we compared baseline characteristics among true positive (positive enzyme test and genetic test), true negative (negative for enzyme test), and false-positive groups (positive enzyme test and negative genetic test). We also compared baseline characteristics between the false-positive and true-negative groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to determine the optimal enzyme activity cutoff value for predicting a final diagnosis of Fabry disease. Informed consent was waived due to the de-identified nature of the data. Data were collected for further analysis at Taichung Veterans General Hospital, with approval from the institutional review board (Approval number: CE20177B-3, TCVGH).

DBS enzyme assay protocol

The complete screening protocol, including DBS-based enzyme testing and subsequent genetic sequencing, is presented in Figure 1. In addition, the detailed DBS protocol is described in supplementary methods-DBS enzyme assay protocol. Enzyme activity values below established thresholds (e.g. <1.2 or <2.0 μmol/L/hour) were interpreted as suggestive of possible Fabry disease. The screening protocol includes only male patients because enzyme activity in female carriers is often within the normal range due to random X-chromosome inactivation, which reduces diagnostic sensitivity in females.

Combination of α-gal a enzyme assay and DNA analysis are used to diagnose male fabry patients.

Although we did not perform direct quantification in this study, based on established protocols and prior literature, a single 3.2 mm punch from a DBS card typically yields approximately 20–50 ng of genomic DNA, with concentrations ranging from 3 to 10 ng/µL. This amount is generally sufficient for downstream genetic analyses, such as PCR amplification and Sanger sequencing. In terms of protein, each 3.2 mm punch is estimated to contain approximately 1–2 µg of total protein, depending on hematocrit and sample quality. This protein yield is adequate for conducting α-Gal A enzymatic activity assays using standard fluorometric or colorimetric methods.

Other data collection

We expanded the methods to clearly explain that clinical and laboratory data were extracted from the hospital electronic medical record system by a board-certified nephrologist, then cross-validated by a second nephrologist for coding accuracy and diagnosis confirmation. More detailed information on data collection is provided in the Supplementary Methods – Other Data Collection.

Statistical analyses

Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test to assess associations between groups. For continuous variables, descriptive statistics were presented as means with standard deviations (Mean ± SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (median (IQR1 and IQR3)), as appropriate based on data distribution. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed continuous variables, comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). When the assumption of normality was violated, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. ROC curve analysis was conducted to determine the optimal cutoff value of enzyme activity for predicting a confirmed diagnosis of Fabry disease. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics (version 22.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) and R software (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient selection and screening algorithm

The patient selection algorithm is illustrated in Figure 2. Between October 1, 2018, and September 31, 2023, α-Gal A enzyme activity was measured using DBS testing for all male patients attending the Nephrology Department at Taichung Veterans General Hospital (n = 1716). Of these, 62 patients were excluded from the analysis due to the absence of CKD or ESKD or female gender. Initially, our screening protocol targeted patients with renal dysfunction in our CKD, hemodialysis (HD), and peritoneal dialysis (PD) clinics. All enrolled patients were expected to have underlying chronic renal dysfunction. However, during data analysis of this cohort, we identified 62 patients who did not have any diagnosis associated with chronic renal dysfunction. Specifically, 32 had only diabetes mellitus without evidence of CKD, and 30 had acute kidney injury that resolved completely after hospital discharge, with no progression to chronic kidney disease. Therefore, we excluded these 62 patients from the final analysis.

Patient selection algorithm for fabry disease in male patients with chronic kidney disease.

The remaining 1654 patients were categorized into three groups based on their renal status: 1039 patients with CKD, 118 undergoing PD, and 497 receiving HD. To enhance the diagnostic yield, we broadened the cutoff value for enzyme activity screening to 2.0 μmol/L/hr. Patients with α-Gal A enzyme activity > 2.0 μmol/L/hr (n = 1619) were considered true negatives and did not undergo further genetic confirmation testing. Conversely, patients with enzyme activity < 2.0 μmol/L/hr (n = 35) were referred for genetic confirmation testing. Upon genetic analysis, 6 patients were identified as true positives (positive enzyme activity and confirmed genetic mutations for Fabry disease) (gene information and clinical manifestation shown in Supplementary Table 1), while 29 patients were categorized as false positives (positive enzyme activity but no confirmatory genetic mutations). If the cutoff value for α-Gal A enzyme activity was set at 1.2 μmol/L/hr, the number of true positive patients identified decreased to four. By broadening the cutoff value of α-Gal A enzyme activity, two additional patients were true positive to have Fabry disease.

Baseline characteristics among three groups (true negative, false positive and true positive)

The final cohort included patients with CKD stage 3–5 as well as those receiving HD or PD. The baseline characteristics of the three groups are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. Among all patients, 1,619 were classified as true negatives, 29 as false positives, and 6 as true positives. All patients were male. The true positive group was younger, with a median age of 49 years (IQR: 36–63), compared to 68 years (IQR: 60–81) in the false positive group and 62 years (IQR: 53–70) in the true negative group. Gout was common across all three groups, with a prevalence of 48.3% in the false positive group, 50.0% in the true positive group, and 74.4% in the true negative group. Similarly, heart failure was present in over half of the patients in each group: 58.6% in the false positive group, 50.0% in the true positive group, and 57.1% in the true negative group. Notably, none of the patients in the true positive group had cirrhosis. In terms of laboratory findings, the true negative group had a higher mean WBC count (7,178 ± 2,542/μL) compared to the false positive (4,322 ± 1,607/μL) and true positive groups (6,365 ± 2,211/μL). The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was also highest in the true negative group (37.91 ± 27.59 mL/min/1.73 m2), compared to the false positive (26.07 ± 23.34 mL/min/1.73 m2) and true positive groups (26.29 ± 32.77 mL/min/1.73 m2). Electrolyte levels were similar across all three groups. Regarding medication use, the true positive group had the lowest usage rates of antiplatelet agents (16.7%) and insulin (16.7%) compared to the other two groups.

Baseline characteristics between false positive and true positive

Subsequently, we analyzed baseline characteristics to identify factors associated with false positive and true positive results (Table 1). Although not statistically significant, the false positive group exhibited a trend toward higher α-Gal A enzyme activity (1.67 ± 0.29) compared to the true positive group (1.13 ± 0.60) (detail enzyme activity showed in Supplementary Figure 1 and Supplementary Figure 2). Compared to true positive patients, those with false positive results were significantly older (69 ± 14 years vs. 49 ± 20 years, p = 0.005) and exhibited a higher prevalence of leukopenia (4322 ± 1607 cells/μL vs. 6365 ± 2211 cells/μL, p = 0.015). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of comorbidities, malignancy, hemoglobin levels, platelet counts, liver and renal function, metabolic markers, electrolytes, left ventricular ejection fraction, high-sensitivity CRP, or medication use.

ROC curve analysis and determination of enzyme activity cutoff for predicting fabry disease confirmation

The ROC curve is presented in Figure 3. An optimal cutoff value of >1.96 μmol/L/hr for α-Gal A enzyme activity was identified to predict the confirmation of Fabry disease with near-perfect diagnostic accuracy. The area under the ROC curve was 0.996 (95%CI: 0.991–1.000), indicating excellent discriminative power. At this cutoff, the sensitivity was 98.3% (95%CI: 0.9769–0.9891), and the specificity was 100% (95%CI: 1-1).

The predictive power of alpha galactosidase activity (>1.96) for Fabry disease. AUROC: 0.996 (95%CI: 0.991–1.000); sensitivity = 0.983 (95%CI:0.9769–0.9891); specificity: 1, 95% (95%CI:1-1:).

Cost-effectiveness due to the broadened cutoff value of the enzyme activity screening test from 1.2 to 2.0 μmol/L/hr

The cost per measurement for enzyme activity is $27 USD, while the cost for a genetic confirmation test is $267 USD. We broadened the cutoff value for enzyme activity from 1.2 μmol/L/hr to 2.0 μmol/L/hr to determine eligibility for further genetic confirmation testing. As a result, 30 patients with enzyme activity levels between 1.2 and 2.0 μmol/L/hr underwent genetic testing. This approach identified 2 additional true positive cases, while the remaining 28 patients were false positive. The total additional cost for genetic testing in these 30 patients was $8,010 USD, yielding two more true positive diagnoses. The cost per diagnosis is considered acceptable within the context of this study.

Discussion

Based on our findings, the screening threshold for α-Gal A enzyme activity in patients with CKD may be adjusted from 1.2 to 2.0 μmol/L/hr, demonstrating improved predictive performance while maintaining acceptable cost-effectiveness. We suggest that, when screening patients with CKD, those with α-Gal A enzyme activity below 2.0 μmol/L/hr should undergo confirmatory genetic testing.

Our data found that false-positive cases, characterized by lower enzyme activity without confirmed Fabry disease, had significantly lower WBC counts compared to true-positive cases (4322 ± 1607/mm3 vs. 6365 ± 2211/mm3, p = 0.015). The enzyme activity of α-Gal A was primarily measured in leukocytes, highlighting the importance of leukocyte count in influencing enzyme activity. DBS samples are prepared from whole blood—containing red blood cells, white blood cells, plasma, and other components—which is blotted onto filter paper and then dried [21]. During α-Gal A activity testing, the sample is processed to extract enzymes. These enzymes are primarily derived from WBC and other cells containing lysosomes. However, erythrocytes contribute minimally to the measured enzyme activity because they lack lysosomes. DBS reflects a composite intracellular enzyme activity, especially WBC. Although a dedicated leukocyte assay was not performed, the DBS enzyme activity primarily reflects leukocyte-derived activity. Therefore, patients with lower WBC counts may exhibit reduced α-Gal A enzyme activity, potentially leading to false-positive results in DBS screening tests. This observation is supported by findings from a study involving 263 patients [22], which reported that enzyme activity in dried blood samples with low leukocyte counts was often lower than the established reference intervals, potentially leading to false-positive diagnoses. The authors of previous studies have suggested that adjusting enzyme activity measurements based on leukocyte counts may improve diagnostic accuracy and reduce false-positive rates. However, given the limited number of cases in our dataset, we are unable to draw definitive conclusions regarding threshold adjustments based on leukocyte count. In our analysis, such adjustments may not be necessary, as the observed false-positive rate was low (1.75%). Furthermore, the cost-effectiveness of implementing the revised cutoff value is considered acceptable within the framework of Taiwan’s healthcare system, yielding two additional true positive patients at an incremental cost of $8,010 USD. Furthermore, these two patients were able to receive timely enzyme replacement therapy, potentially preventing the development of severe complications and leading to additional long-term cost savings. Because Taiwan’s National Health Insurance provides nearly universal coverage and standardized CKD care, the cost of screening for Fabry disease is relatively low compared with that in other countries. Therefore, the cost-effectiveness observed in Taiwan may not be as favorable when extrapolated to other healthcare systems.

For those with false positives, we informed patients about the need for further confirmatory genetic testing. No treatment was performed before confirmation; therefore, there was no clinical impact. Regarding psychological effects, unnecessary anxiety was minimal because patients were informed that the final diagnosis depended on genetic testing. Consequently, under the new threshold, the cost-effectiveness benefit was not associated with additional adverse impacts.

DBS-measured enzyme activity primarily reflects intracellular α-Gal A derived from leukocytes and other nucleated cells. However, DBS-based enzyme assays typically do not adjust for leukocyte count, as the exact WBC count is not available from DBS specimens. Some studies and confirmatory diagnostic protocols have employed isolated leukocyte samples or concurrently assessed WBC counts to calculate adjusted enzyme activity, often expressed as [22,23]: Adjusted enzyme activity = Measured enzyme activity/WBC count. This method enhances diagnostic accuracy by accounting for individual variability in leukocyte concentration. At our institution, in order to maximize case identification and throughput, we chose not to adjust enzyme activity by WBC count. Instead, potentially positive cases were directly referred for GLA gene sequencing to confirm the diagnosis.

The enzyme activities were expressed in μmol/L/h. Although various studies have proposed different cutoff values to define low α-Gal A enzyme activity, the thresholds reported in the literature range from 0.6 to 2.5 μmol/L/h. Initially, a threshold of <1.2 μmol/L/h was defined based on ROC curve analysis conducted by the Archimed Life Science GmbH laboratory [24,25]. However, the enzyme activity in patients with CKD has also been examined in several previous studies. In a Turkish CKD cohort [25], a cutoff of 1.2 μmol/L/h was used to initiate confirmatory genetic analysis, resulting in a reported prevalence of 0.95%. In another Colombian screening study of patients with ESKD [26], the cutoff value for α-Gal A activity was set at 1.15 μmol/L/hr. This stringent threshold may reflect limitations due to financial constraints, potentially leading to missed diagnoses. In a French study [27], a cutoff value of 2.1 μmol/h/L was shown to be robust and effective in reducing false-positive results, making it suitable for screening at-risk populations. In a Brazilian cohort of hemodialysis patients, an initial cutoff of 2.5 μmol/L/hr was used [28]; however, due to a high false-positive rate, this threshold was subsequently adjusted to 2.2 μmol/L/hr [29] based on ROC curve analysis, while maintaining 100% sensitivity. This cutoff value was subsequently widely adopted in male hemodialysis patients in Brazil, highlighting the importance of family screening [30]. Furthermore, a low cutoff value for positive plasma α-Gal A activity, defined as 4 μmol/L/hr, was employed in the study [31]. To date, no published study has evaluated the cost-effectiveness of adopting a less stringent cutoff value for α-Gal A activity. We propose that a cutoff value of 2.0 μmol/L/hr, within our clinical context, offers an optimal balance—enhancing the diagnostic yield for Fabry disease while maintaining an acceptable screening cost and false-positive rate.

Beyond classic Fabry disease, the implications of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) and benign polymorphisms in relation to the enzyme activity threshold warrant careful consideration. First, an appropriately defined α-Gal A activity threshold is valuable not only for identifying affected individuals but also for excluding benign polymorphisms. By minimizing the risk of misclassifying nonpathogenic variants, such thresholds enhance the specificity of screening and ensure that only individuals with clinically meaningful reductions in enzyme activity are referred for confirmatory genetic testing. Second, the emergence of novel VUS presents a continuing diagnostic challenge. In some cases, α-Gal A activity may exceed 2 μmol/L/hr despite the presence of VUS, potentially resulting in misclassification. This highlights the limitations of enzyme activity as a standalone screening tool and underscores the necessity of genetic confirmation to differentiate pathogenic mutations from VUS.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size remains limited, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. The limited number of cases may reduce the robustness of statistical comparisons between groups. We acknowledge this limitation, which may also affect the stability of the ROC-derived cutoff value. Second, the results are applicable only to male patients, as the diagnostic approach differs in females due to X-linked inheritance patterns. Because Fabry disease tends to present more severely in males, we prioritize screening in male patients in our institute. However, we also have a screening protocol for females. For female patients presenting with at least two clinical features—such as left ventricular hypertrophy (>13 mm), arrhythmia, unexplained CKD, or early-onset stroke—we initiate screening. The first step is a DBS test to measure lyso-Gb3 levels. A lyso-Gb3 value above 0.8 ng/mL is considered abnormal. If elevated, we proceed with α-Gal A enzyme activity testing (<1.5 μmol/L/hr) and confirm the diagnosis with genetic analysis. Third, the study population was restricted to individuals with established CKD or those receiving dialysis, limiting its applicability to earlier stages of disease or to the general population. In the future, we plan to incorporate machine learning techniques to develop novel diagnostic tools for this complex, multisystemic disorder. Additionally, we aim to apply large language models to prescreen all patients within our institution prior to conducting enzyme activity and genetic testing, which may further improve efficiency and cost-effectiveness. Fourthly, this threshold applies to non–leukocyte-adjusted assays; future studies should evaluate leukocyte-adjusted thresholds. Fifthly, the cost-effectiveness analysis did not account for additional metrics, including QALYs and long-term treatment costs. Finally, given the limited number of true positive cases (n = 6), the estimates of diagnostic performance should be interpreted with caution, as they may be prone to overestimation.

Conclusion

Based on our study, we propose raising the cutoff value of α-Gal A enzyme activity from 1.2 to 2.0 μmol/L/hr in patients with CKD. This revised threshold demonstrated favorable predictive performance and acceptable cost-effectiveness within the context of Taiwan’s healthcare system. False positives may be related to leukopenia.
