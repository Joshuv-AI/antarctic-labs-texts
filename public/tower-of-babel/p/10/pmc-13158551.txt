Development of a Coaxial Over‐the‐Scope Bimanual Robot Enabling Lesion‐Aligned Manipulation for ESD
Authors: Chanwoo Kim, Sang Hyun Kim, JaeYoun Kim, Woocheol Shin, SeongHyeon Won, Junho Hong, Hyuk Soon Choi, Seongil Kwon, Daehie Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEndoscopic Submucosal Dissection (ESD) requires high expertise as conventional endoscopes cannot provide simultaneous traction and dissection, resulting in unstable visualisation and inconsistent cutting depth.MethodsA coaxial over‐the‐scope bimanual robotic system was developed to enable lesion‐aligned traction and dissection while maintaining compatibility with commercial endoscopes. Engineering performance was evaluated, followed by a pre‐clinical comparison between robot‐assisted ESD (R‐ESD) and conventional ESD (C‐ESD) using synthetic tissue.ResultsThe forceps arm generated sufficient traction (0.581 N), and the coaxial mechanism provided hemispherical manipulation. In pre‐clinical trials (n = 30), R‐ESD significantly reduced total procedure time (9.20 vs. 11.80 min) and dissection time (2.99 vs. 8.35 min). Depth safety improved with shallower mean depth (2.48 vs. 4.06 mm) and a substantial reduction in muscle‐invasion‐risk regions (0.15 vs. 29.38%).ConclusionsThe proposed coaxial bimanual robotic system improves procedural efficiency and depth safety through lesion‐aligned manipulation. Its compatibility with existing endoscopes suggests clinical applicability without procedural complexity.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) cancer remains a major cause of worldwide cancer‐related mortality. According to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN 2022) of the World Health Organization, gastric cancer ranks fifth in both incidence and mortality and continues to be classified as a highly fatal disease [1]. With recent advances in endoscopic technology and the increasing detection of Early Gastric Cancer (EGC) [2], the number of procedures utilising Endoscopic Submucosal Dissection (ESD) has rapidly increased [3]. ESD is a minimally invasive curative treatment that enables en bloc resection of localised lesions. Due to its high curative rates and excellent prognoses [4], it has become the standard treatment for EGC [5].

Despite its clinical advantages, ESD is difficult to perform and requires substantial operator expertise [6]. Insufficient experience can increase the risk of complications such as perforation or bleeding [7]. The major challenges of conventional ESD arise from two main limitations: (1) difficulty in maintaining a clear operative field and (2) restricted maneuverability of the instruments [8]. During submucosal dissection, the mucosal flap often obstructs the endoscopic view, making it challenging to identify the precise dissection plane; therefore, increasing the risk of deep muscle injury or perforation [6]. Moreover, for conventional ESD, the dissection knife is inserted through the working channel, making it mechanically coupled to the endoscope. Rather than directly controlling the knife, the endoscopist must adjust the cutting direction by manipulating the bending section using the endoscope knob or by rotating the entire endoscope. Such visual‐manipulative coupling limits the instrument's degree of freedom (DoFs) and causes the success of the procedures to depend heavily on the endoscopist's experience.

To address these challenges, a variety of robot‐assisted endoscopic surgical systems have been developed in recent years. These platforms can be broadly categorised into three groups:Single‐arm add‐on robots.

However, as dissection is performed using the knife inserted through the working channel, its control is dependent on the endoscope motion. Although these systems offer high clinical compatibility, the concurrent use of essential instruments such as injection needles or clamping devices is limited as they occupy both channels. Proprietary endoscopic robotic platforms.

Systems such as EndoSamurai [15], STRAS [16], K‐FLEX [17], and EndoQuest [18] integrate custom endoscope bodies with articulated robotic arms into a single device, enabling higher dexterity through optimised camera placement and arm configuration. However, these systems require the use of proprietary endoscopes, resulting in compatibility limitations and increased overall diameter, which may pose challenges for clinical adoption.

In this study, we propose a coaxial over‐the‐scope bimanual robotic platform that can be mounted on the distal tip of a commercial flexible endoscope. Conventional platforms that rely on the endoscope's built‐in dual working channels or proprietary endoscopic robotic systems inherently suffer from limited compatibility with commercial endoscopes or restricted use of auxiliary instruments. To address these limitations, the proposed system adopts an external mounting configuration at the distal endoscope tip, enabling high compatibility with commercial endoscopes while preserving the original working channel. This design allows the simultaneous use of auxiliary instruments such as those for injection, suction, and other endoscopic accessories during robotic manipulation.

The two robotic arms are arranged coaxially around the endoscope shaft and enter the operative field from the periphery of the endoscopic view, effectively reducing visual occlusion commonly observed in dual‐channel–based systems. This configuration secures a stable operative field by elevating the mucosal flap with the forceps arm while enabling the knife arm to perform dissection independently of endoscope bending. As a result, visualisation is decoupled from manipulation, leading to improved incision stability and enhanced precision control.

Moreover, the coaxially arranged arms enable circumferential rotation around the endoscope camera, allowing the knife motion to be consistently directed inward towards the centre of the endoscope shaft. Regardless of lesion plane inclination or endoscope shaft rotation, the knife arm motion can be adjusted to remain aligned with the lesion plane. This coaxial circumferential alignment architecture maintains stable control of the robotic instruments within the endoscopic image frame, thereby enabling consistent and lesion‐aligned manipulation.

To evaluate the clinical applicability of the proposed system, a preclinical comparative study was conducted using synthetic gastric tissue models, comparing robot‐assisted ESD (R‐ESD) with conventional ESD (C‐ESD). The evaluation metrics included dissection speed, total procedure time, and depth‐based safety analysis using three‐dimensional scanning.

Design Considerations for ESD

ESD is a minimally invasive procedure that demands a high level of technical proficiency. It involves four sequential steps: lesion marking, submucosal lifting via saline injection, mucosal incision, and submucosal dissection for en bloc resection. The operator must simultaneously manipulate the endoscope tip to maintain a clear visual field while performing cutting within a confined visual field and limited instrument DoFs.

To safely and efficiently accomplish mucosal incision and submucosal dissection, three functional components are essential:Traction: elevating the mucosal flap to expose the dissection plane for a clear operative field.Dissection: maintaining consistent depth and direction along the submucosal plane with minimal muscle injury.Injection: Saline injection into the submucosal layer to provide a safety cushion to prevent perforation and muscle damage.

However, conventional single‐channel endoscopes can only perform these functions sequentially through a single working channel, making simultaneous traction, dissection, and injection infeasible. As the dissection knife is mechanically coupled to the endoscope shaft, bending and rotating the endoscope directly changes the cutting direction. This mechanical coupling impairs visual stability and makes it difficult to maintain a consistent dissection plane.

The tendon–sheath mechanism (TSM) exhibits inherent friction and hysteresis related nonlinearities, which can make distal arm motion appear less intuitive under endoscopic visualisation. Rather than attempting to eliminate these nonlinearities, the pitch and yaw DoFs are fully decoupled through independent tendon routing. In addition, the coaxial arrangement of the two robotic arms preserves intuitive control by aligning the direction of arm motion in the endoscopic image with the operator's input. Intuitive control and master–slave mapping for reduced operator fatigue

To reduce operator fatigue, each DoF of the robotic arms (roll, pitch, translation, and grasp/yaw) is mapped one‐to‐one to a master controller with a hold mode. In this mode, motor torque can be selectively enabled or disabled, allowing the operator to maintain the arm in a fixed posture or perform controlled repositioning as needed. As the movement of the bimanual arm system remains synchronized with the master controller, the operator retains predictable control and a clear understanding of the surgical environment, reducing fatigue during prolonged procedures.

Based on these design considerations, this study presents a coaxially actuated bimanual endoscopic robotic platform that provides stable visualisation, precise dissection, and improved surgical efficiency for ESD. The core objective of the design is not simply to implement two arms, but to enable coaxial motion of independently controlled bimanual instruments, allowing lesion‐aligned surgical manipulation while addressing the structural and functional limitations of existing endoscopic systems.

Design of the Coaxially Actuated Bimanual Endoscopic Robot

The proposed platform consists of four main components: the endoscope body, bimanual robot, actuation unit, and master controller (Figure 1). The bimanual robot is designed as an over‐the‐scope add‐on module that can be mounted onto the distal tip of a commercial endoscope (GIF‐Q260 (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan)). Two independent robotic arms enable stable vertical and horizontal manipulation relative to the lesion plane with their movements synchronized to the master controller for intuitive operation. Coaxial Actuation Mechanism

The two robotic arms are arranged coaxially around the central axis of the endoscope, enabling circumferential movement of the arm bases around the endoscope shaft. The circumferential rotation of the arm bases is driven by a pair of antagonistic tendons fixed to the robotic base surrounding the endoscope shaft. Each tendon is routed along a circumferential path and then bent orthogonally (90°) to pass through the arm base. When tension is applied to one tendon, a tangential force is generated along the circular path, producing a rotational moment that causes the arm base to rotate around the central axis towards the pulling direction. The opposite tendon enables rotation in the opposite direction, thereby allowing bidirectional actuation. Through this tendon routing scheme, the linear pulling displacement of the tendons is directly converted into coaxial rotational motion of the arm bases without additional gears or external actuators.

Each arm's pitch motion provides radial movement towards the centre of the endoscopic field of view. As shown in Figure 2, this coaxial configuration allows the two arms to approach the lesion from the periphery of the visual field, minimising visual obstruction while maintaining vertical and horizontal alignment relative to the lesion plane. Furthermore, as illustrated in Figure 2C, the circumferential roll motion of the arm base enables alignment of the knife arm pitch motion with the tangential direction of the lesion plane. This alignment constrains the knife motion within the tangential plane, thereby reducing unintended penetration in the depth direction and improving dissection depth safety.

Conceptual illustration of lesion‐aligned motion enabled by coaxial actuation. (A) Circumferential coaxial rotation of the arm bases around the endoscope shaft. (B) Vertical and horizontal motions of the robotic arms for lesion‐aligned manipulation. (C) Vector diagram illustrating the alignment of the knife arm motion with the tangential direction of the lesion plane. The roll motion of the arm base aligns the pitch motion vector of the knife arm with the lesion plane, constraining the motion within the tangential plane and reducing unintended penetration in the depth direction.

As the robotic module is mounted and secured at the distal end of the endoscope, the robotic arms and the endoscopic camera share the same distal reference frame. Consequently, the relative orientation between the robotic workspace and the endoscopic image coordinate system remains unchanged when the endoscope shaft undergoes bending or axial rotation. From the operator's perspective, the robotic arms consistently approach the surgical field from the same direction within the endoscopic view, corresponding directly to the master controller input. As a result, the spatial alignment between the visual coordinate system and the robotic motion is maintained mechanically through the system's structural configuration, allowing consistent manipulation without requiring additional coordinate compensation algorithms.

In addition, the relative alignment between the two arms is preserved during manipulation, reducing the mechanical coupling and unstable visualisation commonly observed in conventional endoscope‐based systems. As a result, the operator can intuitively interpret the arm motion in the endoscopic image and perform planar realignment through coaxial rotation when the lesion plane shifts. Therefore, the coaxial actuation mechanism serves as a key design element that enhances visual stability and dexterity during the dissection phase of ESD.

Tendon Routing of Each Arm

In TSM, bending and rotational motion are actuated by applying tension or relaxation to tendons that are housed within a flexible sheath. This mechanism is well suited for multi‐DoF actuation in confined spaces, such as small‐diameter environments. As ESD procedures demand high dexterity within the limited workspace at the distal end of the endoscope, TSM offers a particularly advantageous actuation approach. As shown in Figure 4, the tendon routing for each robotic arm is configured according to its respective DoFs and functional role.Tendon routing of the forceps arm

The pitch and yaw tendons are routed along independent rotational axes so that yaw rotation does not induce tendon length variation in the pitch actuation path, thereby enabling mechanical decoupling between pitch and yaw motions.

The forceps arm experiences relatively high loads during traction to secure a clear view of the dissection plane. When the mucosal flap is elevated through pitch motion, sufficient stiffness and tensile capacity are required. To meet this demand, two tendons are arranged in parallel for each bending direction, resulting in a total of four tendons for pitch control (Figure 4A). This parallel‐tendon configuration increases stiffness and enables stable positioning of the distal link under elevated traction loads. In contrast to a single‐tendon setup, the dual‐tendon arrangement has both tendons pulling side‐by‐side along the same bending axis to create a more distributed and stable bending moment.Tendon routing of the knife arm

Tendon routing of the knife arm

Tendon routing of the knife arm

In most continuum arms, the joint configuration is such that consecutive links are orthogonal to each other. Therefore, a cross‐coupling effect is present between the pitch and yaw motions along with inherent limitations of orthogonal joint configuration. To resolve such issues, the design of the knife arm focuses on the joint configuration and tendon routing. The joint configuration is designed such that a single orthogonal joint link is placed anterior to the pitch joint with the yaw joint oriented at a 90° angle (Figure 4B). When the yaw tendons pass through the pitch joint, they are routed parallel to the central axis of the arm, ensuring a constant tendon path length even during pitch motion. Conversely, the pitch tendons are routed orthogonally (90° offset) relative to the yaw tendons, preventing any change in pitch tendon length during yaw rotation. This geometric arrangement guarantees mechanical decoupling between the pitch and yaw DoFs, resulting in a fully independent bending motion with no cross‐coupling, which is commonly observed in conventional tendon routing geometries of continuum robotic arms.

Through this decoupling, the yaw joint can compensate for changes in lesion height or position during dissection, allowing the system to maintain a constant dissection depth and reduce the risk of muscle layer injury. As a result, the system consistently supports safe and controlled tissue dissection along with the intended dissection plane.

Controller and Mapping Strategy

The proposed coaxial bimanual endoscopic surgical robot adopts a master–slave control system where the operator's hand movement and the robotic arms are in sync within the endoscopic field of view (Figure 8).

Master–slave mapping of the proposed bimanual robot. (A) DoFs of the slave robot: 1 – roll, 2 – pitch, 3 – translation, and 4 – grasp for the forceps arm or yaw for the knife arm. (B) Master controller inputs mapped to each DoF: 1 – roll, 2 – pitch, 3 – translation, 4 – grasp/yaw,5 – torque ON/OFF toggle.

The master controller consists of two independent modules that correspond to the forceps arm and the knife arm. Each module provides four DoFs (roll, pitch, translation and grasp/yaw) that are synchronized with the motion of the corresponding slave arm.

As the controller adopts the same coaxial architecture as the robotic arms, the master device enables intuitive control with minimal directional ambiguity. The roll and pitch motions of each slave arm were measured in real time using the encoder integrated actuators (XM430 (DYNAMIXEL, Robotis, Korea)). Translation is controlled using a linear potentiometer mounted on the master device. The output is sampled through an ADC in the range of 0–4095. The grasping motion of the forceps arm is intuitively modulated through an “open” or “close” button that generates a continuous input signal while the operator holds on the button. The yaw motion of the knife was controlled using a dedicated rotary potentiometer installed at the tip of the master controller. The rotary potentiometer was mapped to an ADC range of 0 – 4095 with a full measurable rotation of 270°. As the robot's yaw angle is mechanically limited to −40° to +40°, the master device consists of a mechanical lock to physically restrict the potentiometer with the desired angulation. To ensure symmetric control in both rotational directions, the centre value can be defined as c−2048 for the master‐side yaw command.

To maintain consistency with the tendon‐displacement–based kinematic model presented in Section 2.3, all master‐side inputs are converted into tendon‐length variation commands. Each master input Δxm,i is mapped to the corresponding tendon displacement ΔLi using a linear relationship (11) ΔLi=km,iΔxm,i where km,i denotes the mapping coefficient for the i‐th DoF.

The mapping between the master and slave devices differs for the components of the robots. The roll and pitch motions have a mapping coefficient of 1 to exhibit a one‐to‐one mapping. However, the potentiometer input for translation and yaw requires linear scaling. Translation has a mapping coefficient that ensures that the ADC ranges from 0–4095 to correspond to the full allowable distal‐end translation. The allowable distal‐end translation range is 17.2 mm, which defines the mapping between the ADC input and the linear displacement of the arm. However, as the yaw is bounded within the mechanically restricted interval, the scaled and centred potentiometer (c−2048) is converted into the corresponding tendon displacement through the yaw coefficient.

The actuation of the motor‐driven DoFs (roll and pitch) relies on the servomotors (XM430 (DYNAMIXEL, Robotis, Korea)). These motors are controlled using an ON/OFF toggle switch on the master module. When the torque is ON, the motors actively maintain the current posture of these two DoFs, thereby preserving the corresponding orientation of the robotic arm. When the torque is OFF, the operator can freely reposition the roll and pitch angles. This torque control reduces hand fatigue and allows rapid reorientation during bimanual manipulation without affecting the mechanically passive non‐motorized DoFs (translation, grasp, and yaw). This configuration ensures independent control of all DoFs in both arms without mutual interference to perform traction and dissection simultaneously. Also, the control architecture enhances the spatial correspondence between the lesion observed in the endoscopic view and the actual motion of the robotic arms, thereby minimising cognitive load and enabling more intuitive and efficient bimanual operation during the procedure.

Results

Robotic Performance Evaluation

The performance of the developed bimanual robotic system was evaluated through two experiments focussing on its traction capability (Section 3.1.1) and motion transmission characteristics (Section 3.1.2). First, the traction force capability of the forceps arm was quantitively assessed to verify its ability to elevate and maintain tissue tension required for ESD. Then, transmission characteristics of the TSM were evaluated under various bending conditions to analyse hysteresis and motion accuracy during roll, pitch, yaw, and translation motions.

Traction Force Capability

To confirm that the forceps arm of the developed robot can sufficiently elevate the lesion, two traction‐force‐related experiments were performed.

In the first experiment, as shown in Figure 11, a load cell (BS‐3520 (Bongshin, Korea)) was connected to the distal end of the forceps using a wire and pulled vertically to measure the traction force. The minimum traction force required to elevate a lesion by approximately 10 mm is 0.135 N, while for dissection, a force of 0.17–0.48 N is required to maintain stable tissue tension during mucosal incision and submucosal dissection [20]. To evaluate the consistency of the traction force, the experiment was conducted 12 times while in comparison with the reference values. As shown in Figure 12, the developed forceps arm generated an average traction force of 0.581 ± 0.057N(n = 12), exceeding both the required minimum lifting force (0.135 N) and the cutting traction force range (0.17–0.48 N).

Experimental setup for traction force measurement. The traction force generated by the forceps arm was measured by connecting the forceps tip to a load cell using a wire.

The blue shaded region represents the traction force range to maintain stable tissue tension during mucosal incision and submucosal dissection (0.17–0.48 N). The lower blue line denotes the minimum traction force required to elevate a lesion by approximately 10 mm (0.135 N).

The second experiment utilised the same experimental setup as the maximum traction force measurement to analyse the force‐displacement characteristics of the forceps arm under traction. The load cell mounted on a linear guide rail was displaced incrementally while measuring the corresponding traction force. Figure 13 shows the measured traction force as a function of displacement, demonstrating an approximately linear increase in the traction force with increasing displacement. Linear regression analysis yielded a force–displacement slope of 0.202 N/mm. These results indicate that the proposed forceps arm provides sufficient traction to reliably elevate and maintain the lesion during ESD procedures, while exhibiting a consistent increase in traction force with applied displacement.

To quantitatively evaluate the transmission characteristics of the TSM under different bending conditions, the experimental setup was set up, as shown in Figure 14. A cylindrical jig with a diameter of 30 cm was used to create a constant curvature on the TSM path. The tendon‐sheath was wrapped around the jig to test the robot movement during bending angles of 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°, and 360°. The bending angle was kept constant for each experiment, and no additional hysteresis compensation or control parameter tuning was applied.

A six‐DoF electromagnetic (EM) tracking system (Aurora system, Northern Digital Inc., Waterloo, ON, Canada) was used to simultaneously measure translational and rotational motions at the robot end‐effector. An EM sensor was attached to the end‐effector of the robot to measure roll, pitch, and yaw angles as well as translational displacement at the same base coordinate frame. The tracking system provides a positional accuracy of 0.48 mm RMS and an angular accuracy of 0.30° RMS. A reference sensor was fixed at a predefined location to define the base coordinate frame.

For each bending condition, experiments were conducted on the fundamental motions of the robotic arm, including roll, pitch, yaw, and translation, with each motion repeated 10 times under identical input conditions. Input commands consisting of angular and translational displacements were dynamically applied to the robotic arm to ensure motion within a predefined, clinically feasible workspace while avoiding kinematic limits and excessive tendon tension. Each input was applied until the target value was reached, followed by a 1‐s dwell period at the target position. The arm was then returned to the initial position along the same trajectory.

The measured time‐domain responses are shown in Figure 15, where Figure 15A–D correspond to roll, pitch, yaw, and translational motions, respectively. Across all motion modes, a dead‐band region was observed, in which no output motion occurred when the transmission direction was reversed. This dead‐band region resulted in different output paths during increasing and decreasing input commands. To compensate for the intrinsic hysteresis and frictional transmission characteristics of the TSM, additional input was required. Furthermore, the duration of the dead‐band region was observed to increase with increasing cumulative bending angle.

Discussion

In this study, a coaxially actuated bimanual endoscopic surgical robot compatible with commercially available endoscopes was designed and developed. The developed robot executes a lesion‐aligned bimanual manipulation strategy to simultaneously improve visual stability and dissection precision. By adopting such a structure with the base of the robotic arms rotating coaxially about the central axis of the endoscope, the arms can approach the lesion from the periphery of the endoscopic field of view. Through this coaxial rotation mechanism, the robotic arms can be vertically and horizontally aligned when approaching the lesion centre. Such configuration allows the arm motion to avoid obstructing the central field of view during dissection, thereby maintaining stable lesion visualisation.

To quantitatively verify these structural characteristics, a workspace analysis was conducted based on the range of motion of the robotic arms. The results showed that the robotic arms form a hemispherical workspace with respect to the endoscope axis. Such workspace allows the arms to maintain lesion‐centred alignment through coaxial base rotation even when the lesion plane is tilted. While the forceps arm was able to elevate the tissue up to 32.2 mm, the knife arm secured an effective manipulation range of 12.6 mm in a single dissection motion. This workspace sufficiently covers the lesion size used in this study (30 × 25 mm), demonstrating that the proposed system provides adequate operational space for the required traction and dissection movement during practical ESD procedures.

To effectively utilise these mechanical and operative characteristics, a master–slave controller with an identical DoF configuration to the robotic arms was designed. The controller movement is aligned with the motion of the robot arm with the endoscopic visual coordinate system, enabling intuitive lesion‐oriented manipulation without requiring the operator to consciously be aware of misalignment. Especially, as the lesion alignment is achieved through coaxial rotation, the input directions of the controller directly correspond to the robot arm movement within the endoscopic view. In addition, the torque ON/OFF function applied to the roll and pitch axes allows the operator to fix the robotic arm position, thereby suppressing fine hand tremors during dissection and reducing the operator's workload. This function provides not only positional assistance but also enables stable traction and dissection while maintaining lesion alignment. As a result, the proposed controller design effectively integrates the mechanical alignment advantages of the robotic structure into practical operation, enhancing overall procedural performance.

The bimanual robot was safely deployed under a limited endoscopic field of view as the robotic arms were compactly designed to minimise protrusion during insertion. This compact structure reduces unintended contact with surrounding mucosal tissue while the endoscope advances through the gastrointestinal tract. After reaching the target lesion site, the robotic arms are deployed through a controlled linear motion along the axis of the endoscope, allowing the operator to visually confirm the deployment through the endoscopic view. As the deployment occurs primarily along the axial direction rather than through lateral expansion, the risk of scraping or damaging the surrounding mucosa is minimised. During the traction process, the grasped tissue may occasionally move outside the endoscopic field of view; however, the outer surfaces of the robotic mechanism were designed with smooth and rounded contours to minimise mechanical irritation or damage to the surrounding mucosal tissue. These design considerations help ensure that the deployment and operation of the bimanual robot mechanism can be performed safely even under limited endoscopic visualisation.

To validate the effectiveness of the proposed system, both robotic performance experiments and pre‐clinical ESD experiments were conducted. The robotic performance experiments aimed to verify whether the proposed system satisfies the mechanical and transmission requirements of ESD environments, while the preclinical ESD experiments evaluated how these attributes translate into actual procedural outcomes.

The robotic performance evaluation consisted of traction force experiments and TSM characterisation experiments. The traction force experiment assessed whether the forceps arm could reliably elevate lesions and generate sufficient tissue tension during submucosal dissection. The TSM characterisation experiment analysed motion transmission characteristics under endoscopic bending conditions to verify consistent manipulation performance in curved environments.

The traction force experiment confirmed that the system provides sufficient capability to reliably elevate and hold the tissue along the dissection plane. The average traction force of the forceps arm was measured to be 0.581 ± 0.057 N, exceeding both the minimum force required for lesion elevation (0.135 N) and the range required to maintain stable tissue tension during submucosal dissection (0.17–0.48 N). Additional force–displacement analysis demonstrated a relatively predictable increase in traction force with applied load, indicating its applicability as a foundation for future force control development. However, the present study primarily focused on structural validation through the evaluation of the maximum traction capability and displacement under external loading. Therefore, future work must be conducted to conduct quantitative analysis of traction controllability for precise position and force control to prevent the tissue from being damaged.

In practical endoscopic environments, curved insertion paths can affect motion transmission in TSM. Therefore, multiple TSM experiments were conducted by changing the endoscopic bending angle from 0° to 360° to evaluate transmission accuracy and repeatability under bending conditions. Input commands and output displacements were measured for each bending condition to quantitatively analyse transmission characteristics and assess consistency across repeated motions.

The TSM experiments showed that motion transmission accuracy generally decreased as the bending angle increased. In particular, the MAE of roll motion increased from approximately 1.7° under straight conditions to about 8.5° at maximum bending. Similarly, pitch motion also showed increased error under large curvature. In contrast, yaw motion showed a relatively small deviation of around 2° at maximum bending, and translation motion with errors within approximately 0.5 mm. Repeatability remained relatively stable across all bending conditions, with standard deviations within approximately 0.20°–0.83° for roll, 0.046°–0.387° for pitch, and 0.036°–0.172° for yaw. The relatively larger errors observed in roll and pitch motions can be interpreted as follows. For roll motion, friction may accumulate more than in other motions, as the actuation tendon is wrapped circumferentially around the cylindrical base of the robot. In pitch motion, the tendon must pass through multiple links, resulting in a larger contact region and consequently increased transmission loss due to friction. In contrast, yaw motion shows smaller absolute errors due to its relatively limited motion range. However, when considering the relative error tendency with respect to the motion range, a pattern similar to pitch motion is observed. For translation motion, because the tendon does not pass through multiple links as in pitch or yaw motion, comparatively smaller transmission errors may occur due to reduced cumulative friction effects.

The tendon–sheath model used in this study was developed to describe the structural design and kinematic relationships under ideal transmission assumptions; therefore, nonlinear effects such as friction and hysteresis were not considered. In practical endoscopic environments, these nonlinear characteristics may accumulate along curved insertion paths, providing a plausible explanation for the observed degradation in motion transmission under increased bending conditions. Nevertheless, the relatively stable inter‐trial repeatability suggests that the proposed structure maintains consistent motion characteristics despite the presence of nonlinear transmission behaviour. With the future application of advanced control strategies, the robot is expected to achieve improved performance. However, as this study primarily focuses on structural and system‐level validation of the robot, the implementation and quantitative evaluation of advanced control strategies to compensate for such nonlinearities remain important directions for future work.

Pre‐clinical ESD experiments were conducted to evaluate how the structural and mechanical characteristics of the proposed system translate into actual procedural performance. To compare the dissection speed and stability in dissection depth, one expert operator and two novice operators performed both R‐ESD and C‐ESD.

For the expert operator, switching from C‐ESD to R‐ESD increased the dissection speed from 63.0 mm2/min to 182.1 mm2/min, corresponding to an increase of approximately 189%. Accordingly, the total procedure time decreased from 11.8 to 9.20 min and the dissection time decreased from 8.35 min to 2.99 min. The reduction in total procedure time was shorter than the reduction observed in dissection time as additional time was required for the robotic grasper to grasp and apply traction to the lesion during dissection. As the module can be mounted onto the distal end of the endoscope and secured without complex assembly or calibration, the attachment of the robotic module itself only required a short setup time. As a result, while the robotic operation introduced some additional procedural time, the substantially faster dissection speed ultimately led to an overall reduction in the total procedure time compared with conventional ESD. Also, substantial enhancement in dissection depth stability was also observed. The mean dissection depth decreased from 4.06 ± 0.42 mm to 2.48 ± 0.38 mm, corresponding to a reduction of approximately 39%. The proportion of tissue within the safe submucosal range (1.5–4.5 mm) increased from 70.42 ± 18.67% to 92.81 ± 6.73%, while the proportion of muscle layer invasion risk (> 4.5 mm) decreased from 29.38 ± 18.69% to 0.15 ± 0.48%, representing an approximately 99% reduction in risk. In C‐ESD, the cutting direction is indirectly determined by endoscope bending and rotation. In contrast, the proposed system mechanically constrains the knife arm to maintain a tangential orientation relative to the lesion plane through its coaxial circumferential alignment. As the arm base can rotate circumferentially about the endoscope shaft, the cutting direction can be directly aligned with the lesion surface even when the lesion plane appears tilted in the endoscopic view. The motion of the knife is effectively decoupled from the endoscope movement, thereby preventing unintended vertical penetration into the deeper tissue layers during dissection, which may cause perforation. In addition, the independently implemented yaw DoF at the distal end of the knife arm enables fine adjustment of the knife tip position to keep the knife aligned with the changing plane level of the dissection plane. This decoupled yaw movement maintains relative postural stability between the knife tip and the tissue surface despite geometric changes in the lesion induced by different traction directions.

The combination of these structural characteristics enables a consistent tangential dissection motion along the lesion plane, which contributes to reduced depth variability and a lower risk of muscle layer invasion. Therefore, the improvement in dissection depth safety observed in this study may be attributed not only to bimanual manipulation but also to the proposed coaxial lesion‐aligned architecture combined with the yaw DoF that supports stable dissection.

In the novice experiments, these trends were even more noticeable. Dissection speed in R‐ESD increased by approximately 251%–318%, which was greater than the improvement observed in the expert experiment. As a result, it suggests that the robotic system effectively reduces dependence on the operator's skill level and structurally enhances dissection efficiency. Significant improvements in dissection depth stability were also observed, with the mean dissection depth reduced by approximately 22%–24%. In addition, the proportion of dissection within the safe submucosal range increased, while the risk of muscle layer invasion decreased by approximately 48%–70%. These findings indicate that the proposed robotic architecture can improve both procedural efficiency and safety, particularly for novice operators.

In addition, both expert and novice operators exhibited a gradual increase in dissection speed over repeated procedures. This trend suggests that the intuitive manipulation structure and lesion‐aligned coaxial design of the robotic system reduce the initial learning burden and enable rapid improvement in procedural efficiency. However, because the number of novice participants (N = 2) in this study was limited, the present results should be interpreted as a preliminary exploratory evaluation rather than a definitive validation of the learning curve. Future studies involving a larger number of operators will be required to more systematically evaluate the learning characteristics and generalisability of the proposed robotic system.

Compared with existing ESD assistance robots, the key distinction of the proposed system lies not merely in the implementation of a bimanual structure but in its coaxial lesion‐aligned architecture. Existing bimanual ESD robotic systems can generally be categorised into dual‐channel endoscope‐based robots and proprietary endoscopic robots, as summarised in Table 12. Dual‐channel systems enable simultaneous traction and dissection but often occupy working channels, limiting the use of auxiliary tools such as injection, suction, and clipping, and may obstruct the endoscopic field of view due to frontal arm placement. Additionally, channel‐constrained structures inherently limit arm DoF and workspace. Dedicated endoscope‐based robotic systems offer relatively high DoF but suffer from limited compatibility with commercial endoscopes and increased system diameter, which may impose clinical burdens. The need for new equipment and training also restricts seamless integration into existing clinical workflows. In contrast, the proposed system adopts an over‐the‐scope add‐on configuration mounted externally on a commercial endoscope, fully preserving working channels while enabling bimanual manipulation. By arranging the robotic arms coaxially around the endoscope axis, lesion access is achieved from the periphery of the visual field, minimising visual obstruction. Coaxial rotation enables alignment of the knife arm's dissection direction with the lesion plane, allowing dissection motions independent of endoscopic bending or rotation. Without excessively increasing structural complexity, the proposed system introduces a new design paradigm that structurally enhances dissection stability through lesion‐aligned coaxial configuration, distinguishing it from existing ESD assistance robots.

This study has several limitations. All pre‐clinical experiments were conducted using synthetic tissue models; therefore, physiological factors present in real biological tissues, such as bleeding, blood flow, tissue elasticity, and mucosal adhesion, were not fully replicated. Workspace analysis and controller evaluation were limited to computational and functional assessments, and quantitative usability evaluation involving clinical operators with diverse skill levels was not conducted. In addition, the number of operators participating and the number of procedures were limited, restricting a comprehensive assessment of operator adaptation to the system. Furthermore, because the system is driven by a TSM, increased curvature of the endoscopic insertion path may lead to accumulated nonlinear transmission errors due to friction, backlash, and hysteresis. Although this study focused on quantitatively analysing these transmission characteristics, real‐time compensation control algorithms were not implemented. Finally, the pre‐clinical ESD experiments were performed by a single expert and a limited number of novice operators, which restricts generalisation across varying skill levels, lesion locations, and morphologies. Future studies will include in vivo animal experiments to validate performance in real tissue environments, integration of real‐time control algorithms to compensate for TSM nonlinearities, and usability and learning curve analyses involving clinical operators to more comprehensively assess clinical applicability.

Conclusion

This study presents the development of a coaxial bimanual robotic platform compatible with commercial endoscopes, and its effectiveness was validated through engineering performance evaluations and pre‐clinical ESD comparison experiments. To address the limitations in visualisation and manipulation associated with C‐ESD, the proposed structure improves procedural efficiency and dissection safety through lesion‐aligned and independent bimanual control. In pre‐clinical experiments, R‐ESD increased the dissection speed from 63.0 mm2/min to 182.1 mm2/min and reduced the average dissection depth from 4.06 to 2.48 mm. In addition, the proportion of tissue dissected within the safe submucosal range increased from 70.4% to 92.8%, while muscle‐layer invasion was rarely observed. Notably, in novice‐operated dissection experiments, R‐ESD exhibited consistent improvement trends in both dissection speed and depth stability compared with C‐ESD. These trends were directionally consistent with those observed in expert‐performed procedures, suggesting that the proposed system may mitigate operator‐dependent variability during the dissection phase. These quantitative results demonstrate that lesion‐aligned manipulation based on coaxial bimanual actuation effectively contributes to improved dissection efficiency and procedural stability. Overall, these findings suggest that an add‐on robotic system compatible with existing commercial endoscopes can enhance the safety, reproducibility, and precision of ESD procedures without increasing procedural complexity. Future studies will aim to expand clinical applicability through in vivo validation, the development of control algorithms to compensate for TSM nonlinearities, and comprehensive usability evaluations with clinical operators.
