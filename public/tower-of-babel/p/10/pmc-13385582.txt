Patellar Tendon Graft Harvest: The Bone Transfer Technique
Authors: Jesper Fritz, Sebastien Parratte, Charles Brown
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) autograft for anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction (ACLR) is strong and has a low failure rate. However, postoperative anterior knee pain remains a concern because of bony defects at the harvest site, which can be reduced with proper bone grafting.Indications:BPTB autograft for ACLR is indicated for professional athletes, patients returning to pivoting sports, patients undergoing revision surgery, and patients with hyperlaxity, increased recurvatum, or increased posterior tibial slope. This technique is indicated for all these patients, except for revision ACLR, as the bone transfer from the tibial tunnel is not recommended because of the previous ACL tunnel.Technique Description:A 4-cm vertical anteromedial proximal tibial incision is made medial to the patellar tendon. The skin is mobilized using full-thickness skin flaps, and the paratenon is split longitudinally to expose the patellar tendon. The mid-third of the tendon is harvested, and bone blocks are taken from both ends of the graft, with special consideration taken to harvest a deep tibial bone block to be able to use the deep portion of the tibial bone block to bone graft the patellar defect. A coring reamer is used to create the tibial ACL tunnel, resulting in a bone cylinder to be used to bone graft the tibial tuberosity defect. Finally, the superficial portion of the tendon and the paratenon are closed, and finally, the skin incision is closed in layers.Results:As previously shown, BPTB autograft for ACLR has a low failure rate and a high return to sports rate, especially in young individuals.Discussion/Conclusion:Multiple techniques for BPTB harvest have been described, but we believe this technique addresses the major concern of postoperative anterior knee pain. A bony defect from bone block harvest is a crucial cause of postoperative anterior knee pain, but anatomical bone grafting reduces the risk of harvest-site pain. Furthermore, autologous bone graft heals faster than other grafts, and our technique is designed to address all of these factors by using an autologous bone prism for the patella and a bone cylinder for the tibial tuberosity to avoid defects and restore anatomy.Patient Consent Disclosure Statement:The author(s) attests that consent has been obtained from any patient(s) appearing in this publication. Background

Bone-patellar tendon-bone (BPTB) autograft for anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction (ACLR) was previously the gold standard because of its superior strength and low failure rate. Because of postoperative donor site morbidity, especially anterior knee pain, other grafts, such as hamstring and quadriceps grafts, with lower risks of postoperative anterior knee pain, gained popularity. Systematic reviews and registry studies over the last 20 years have shown that the patellar tendon graft has a lower failure rate than the hamstring graft,3,4 and with modern harvesting techniques, the donor site morbidity is now lower. However, donor site morbidity, most importantly, anterior knee pain, is still a concern. 1

It has been shown that one of the major reasons for postoperative anterior knee pain is a bony defect at the harvest site on the patella and tibial tuberosity, 5 but also that proper bone grafting of these harvest sites decreases anterior knee pain. 6

Sparing or repairing the paratenon during BPTB graft harvest is also considered beneficial. 2

Indications

A summary of the patient in this video: a 22-year-old football player with a noncontact twisting injury to the knee 3 months ago. Initial assessment showed positive Lachman and Pivot shift tests, and radiographs were normal; magnetic resonance imaging confirmed a complete ACL tear, with no other injuries. After 3 months of physical therapy, he had full range of motion and good quadriceps activation, but continued giveaways of the knee, and the decision was made that the patient would benefit from ACLR and due to the young age, no history of anterior knee pain and the desire to return to football, we agreed that the patellar tendon autograft was the ideal graft for this patient.

Technique Description

Surgical Setup and Preoperative Examination

After anesthesia, in a supine position on the operating room table, knee examination is performed with positive Lachman and grade 2 pivot shift tests, and the decision was made to also perform a lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET).

Surgical Technique

First, mark your anatomy, and the incision should start 1 cm proximal to the joint line, 1 cm medial to the patellar tendon, and be about 4 to 5 cm long.

Local anesthetics are used in the incision to increase the space between the fascia and the subcutaneous tissue. This way is easier to find the right plane and mobilize the skin to reach the patella and the tibial tuberosity.

Then, incise the paratenon and create 2 flaps to expose the tendon itself. Take your time here; it is important to preserve the tissue for when it is time to close again. Measure the tendon width, use a template to determine the appropriate width of your graft, and mark it properly. Here, we use a marking pen and cautery to mark the middle of the tendon, and then make tendon incisions in the fiber direction down to the tibial tuberosity. Once you are at the tibial tuberosity, you switch to the cautery and mark your bone-block of 25 mm length and continue with the chosen width—here 10 mm. I drill the central hole for the passing suture, as the bone block is more stable now than later. Then, use the oscillating saw, properly stabilized with 2 hands, to cut your bone block, and be very generous with the depth to get a large block, as we are going to use that bone for grafting later.

We finish the bone block harvest with a curved osteotome and cut any small soft-tissue bands. At this stage, we cut the deep portion of the block with a saw to create the correct size and to get the bone piece for the patellar grafting later. We use a hemostatic sponge to minimize bleeding, then we move on to the patella. Here, we use an angled retractor to push the patella down and hold the skin, exposing the lower half of the patella. In a similar fashion, as on the tibia, we use the cautery to mark out the bone block, which is aimed to be 20 mm long and 10 mm wide.

We then cut the bone block with the saw. Again, it is important to stabilize the saw to avoid cutting into the tendon on the sides and finally release it with a small osteotome and release the soft tissue. And that is the graft!

To reduce morbidity at harvest sites, it is important to bone graft the defects. First, we use the deep portion of the tibial bone block that was taken out and trim it to fit the patellar defect, and press-fit it in with a bone tap. Then, we close the tendon all the way proximally and down to the tibia, only leaving it open over the tibial defect. A key step is to close only the superficial part of the tendon and to make sure not to trap the fat pad in the tendon, as this might increase the risk of anterior knee pain. We then close the curtain, so to speak, by closing the paratenon on top. It is important to do this before you drill your tibial tunnel, as you might otherwise damage the paratenon as you are drilling your tunnel, making it difficult to close later.

We can then perform our ACLR, and we are not going to show you all of that; instead, we will just show you the coring reamer we use to drill the tibial tunnel, as this is a key step for the harvest. With the coring reamer, you get a bone cylinder, which you then can use for grafting the tibial tubercle harvest site. First, you take whatever bone you have left from the tibial bone block that was excessive to the patellar bone grafting and tap it deep into the defect. Then, you use the cylinder to cut it to match the defect (sometimes it takes a little effort), then tap it in place. Finally, you close the paratenon on top to complete the BTB harvest.

Results

This is a postoperative radiograph of ACLR with BTB and also a LET. However, we focus on the BTB harvest site and can see the cylinder acquired from the tibial tunnel sitting integrated in the tibial harvest site, and you can also see the well-maintained contour of the bone on the lateral view, both on the tibia and patella, trying to leave the bone as restored as possible.

The postoperative protocol includes no restriction on range of motion or weightbearing, but due to discomfort and poor quad control, there is usually a need for a brace and crutches when ambulating initially.

We use the limb strength index, tested with the Biodex, to determine when the patient is ready to start a running progression and return to sports.

Discussion/Conclusion

Finally, I want to emphasize the surgical pearls of the BTB harvest:

First of all, take your time to mark the anatomy to get your incision where you want it, medial to the midline, to avoid kneeling pain postoperatively.

Measure the tendon and choose the mid-third, not to take too big of a portion and risk injury to the remainder of the tendon.

Harvest a deep tibial bone block so that you can transfer some of that bone for the patellar defect.

Bone graft the patella with bone from the tibial bone block to restore the anatomy.

Close the superficial part of the tendon to avoid trapping the fat pad in the tendon.

Last but not least, use a coring reamer for your tibial ACL-tunnel to produce a bone cylinder for the tibial tubercle defect bone grafting.
