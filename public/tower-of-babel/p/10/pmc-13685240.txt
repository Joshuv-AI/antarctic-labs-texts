Coordinated maintenance of deeper roots and anatomical remodeling enhances salt tolerance in spring wheat
Authors: Xiaoya Gao, Xiaoyuan Bao, Xinying Li, Chengxin Bai, Shiji Wang, Fuyang Cui, Hong Fan, Wei He, Yali Sun, Bo Jing, Cai Zhao, Congcong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSalt stress is a major abiotic factor limiting yield formation and quality improvement in spring wheat. Previous studies on wheat salt tolerance have mainly focused on shoot injury, ion homeostasis, biomass reduction, or whole-root morphological traits. However, these approaches generally treat the root system as a whole and rarely distinguish whether salt tolerance is associated with the maintenance of deeper roots or with specific anatomical remodeling within root tissues.MethodsTo address this gap, a high-throughput paper-based cultivation platform was used to assess the shoot phenotypes, root architectural traits, and root anatomical characteristics in 28 spring wheat genotypes under control and salt-stress conditions. Salt tolerance coefficients were calculated for multiple traits and integrated using principal component analysis, membership function analysis, contribution-rate-based weighting, and comprehensive D-value clustering. Genotypes with contrasting salt tolerance were then selected for further comparison of stratified root architecture and root anatomical responses.ResultsThe results showed that salt stress significantly inhibited both shoot and root growth in spring wheat. The first five principal components explained 81.37% of the total variation, and the integrated evaluation framework effectively distinguished genotypic differences in salt tolerance. Plant height, total root length, average root length, cortex/stele area ratio, and lacuna/cortex area ratio were identified as candidate phenotypic and anatomical indicators associated with variation in salt tolerance. Further correlation and stratified root analyses showed that the maintenance of root architecture and anatomical adjustment were significantly associated with shoot growth performance under salt stress. In the deeper root layer, salt-tolerant genotypes maintained relatively more stable root traits, with root surface area reduced by 18.68%–38.61%, whereas the corresponding reductions in salt-sensitive genotypes ranged from 28.57% to 90.00%. Meanwhile, salt-tolerant genotypes exhibited a more coordinated pattern of cortical contraction, stele maintenance, and lacuna adjustment, suggesting that salt tolerance in spring wheat seedlings may be associated with the combined maintenance of deeper roots and anatomical remodeling rather than with a single root structural trait.ConclusionThis study advances the evaluation of salt tolerance in wheat by linking whole-plant performance with stratified root architecture and root anatomical organization. The results suggest that salt tolerance in spring wheat seedlings is associated not simply with total root size, but with the coordinated maintenance of deeper roots and anatomical remodeling of cortex, stele, and lacuna tissues. These findings provide a more refined root trait framework for salt-tolerant germplasm screening and root-based improvement of spring wheat.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09537-z.

Background

Salinity is one of the major abiotic stresses limiting global agricultural productivity. Salt stress affects crop growth through a combination of osmotic stress, ionic toxicity, nutrient imbalance, and oxidative injury, thereby reducing biomass accumulation, yield formation, and crop quality [1, 2]. Approximately 20% of the world’s arable land is affected by salinization, resulting in estimated annual economic losses exceeding US$27 billion [3, 4]. With ongoing climate change, improper irrigation practices, and unsustainable land-management practices, more than 50% of arable land is projected to be affected by salinization by 2050 [5]. Therefore, identifying reliable traits that reflect crop salt adaptation is a key priority for salt-tolerance evaluation and breeding.

Roots play a central role in plant adaptation to saline environments because they are the first organ exposed to salt stress and are directly involved in water uptake, ion transport, and stress signal perception [6]. Root system architecture (RSA), including root length, depth, branching, and spatial distribution, determines the capacity of plants to explore soil resources and maintain shoot growth under stress [7, 8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that root traits are closely associated with stress adaptation in several crops, and recent work has increasingly emphasized the importance of root plasticity under saline conditions [9, 10]. However, these studies have not always reached a unified interpretation. In some cases, greater total root length or deeper rooting is considered beneficial for water acquisition and stress avoidance, whereas in other cases, excessive root proliferation may increase metabolic costs under resource-limited or saline conditions. Thus, root-mediated salt adaptation should not be evaluated only by the size of the whole root system, but also by how root growth is spatially allocated and anatomically supported.

Root anatomical traits provide another important dimension for understanding salt adaptation. Anatomical remodeling, such as changes in cortical area, stele proportion, cortical cell layers, and lacuna or aerenchyma formation, can influence root respiration, radial water and nutrient transport, internal aeration, and ion movement [11–13]. For example, reduced cortical investment and increased aerenchyma formation may lower the metabolic cost of maintaining living cortical tissues, which could support soil exploration under stress. However, excessive cortical disintegration may also restrict radial transport pathways and impair root function if not coordinated with stele maintenance and root elongation [13, 14]. Therefore, the adaptive value of anatomical remodeling is context-dependent and should be interpreted together with root architectural performance. Currently, root architecture and root anatomy are often investigated separately, and their coordinated variation under salt stress, particularly in relation to the maintenance of shoot growth, remains insufficiently resolved in spring wheat.

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is one of the most important staple crops worldwide, providing a major source of calories and protein for humans. Wheat generally shows higher salt tolerance than rice and maize, and therefore has considerable potential for production in saline or semi-saline regions [15]. In recent years, important progress has been made in understanding wheat salt tolerance at the molecular and physiological levels. For instance, TaHAG1 enhances salt tolerance through histone acetylation regulation [16], TaSPL6-D contributes to salt tolerance by activating TaHKT1;5-D [17], and TaGPAT6 improves salt tolerance by promoting cutin and suberin monomer synthesis to form a diffusion barrier [18]. These studies have greatly advanced our understanding of wheat salt tolerance. Nevertheless, most of them focus on molecular regulation, ion transport, or aboveground performance, whereas less attention has been given to whether genotypic differences in salt tolerance can be explained by specific combinations of root architectural and anatomical traits.

Accordingly, the novelty of the present study does not lie simply in examining root traits under salt stress, but in integrating multiple root-related dimensions into a single evaluation framework. Specifically, we combined aboveground phenotypes, whole-root architectural traits, stratified root traits, and root anatomical characteristics to evaluate salt tolerance among 28 spring wheat genotypes. This design allowed us to ask whether salt-tolerant genotypes differ from salt-sensitive genotypes only in overall root growth, or whether they show a more specific trait combination involving deeper root maintenance and coordinated anatomical remodeling. Based on a high-throughput paper-based cultivation platform, principal component analysis, membership function analysis, and cluster analysis were used to establish an integrated salt-tolerance evaluation framework. The objectives of this study were to: (1) evaluate genotypic variation in salt tolerance by integrating shoot traits, RSA traits, and root anatomical traits; (2) identify candidate phenotypic and anatomical indicators associated with salt-tolerance differences; and (3) determine whether the maintenance of deeper roots is associated with coordinated changes in cortex–stele–lacuna organization under salt stress.

Results

Representative images and trait analysis in spring wheat

Salt stress significantly affected both aboveground and root traits in spring wheat (Table 1). Regarding aboveground traits, plant height, leaf area, stomatal length, stomatal width, and stomatal aperture all showed decreasing trends under salt stress, whereas stomatal density showed an increasing trend. For root traits, total root length, average length of all roots, lateral root count, primary root count, convex hull area, and maximum depth were reduced under salt stress, indicating that root elongation and spatial expansion were inhibited. In contrast, the width/depth ratio, cortex cell layers, cortex/stele area ratio, and lacuna/cortex area ratio showed increasing trends under salt stress. The coefficients of variation of the measured traits ranged from 12.79% to 80.93%, with most traits exceeding 20% (Table 1).

Integrated evaluation of salt tolerance among spring wheat genotypes

To reduce the dimensionality of multiple traits and evaluate genotypic differences in salt tolerance, principal component analysis was performed using the salt tolerance coefficients of the measured traits. Five principal components were extracted, with contribution rates of 36.15%, 18.74%, 11.57%, 7.86%, and 7.05%, respectively. The cumulative contribution rate reached 81.37%, indicating that these five composite indices captured most of the variation among the original traits (Table 2). Traits such as stomatal length, total root length, average length of all roots, width/depth ratio, and cortex/stele area ratio had relatively high loading coefficients, suggesting that shoot morphology, root elongation, root spatial configuration, and root anatomical allocation contributed to genotypic differences in salt-stress responses.

Characteristic values and contribution rates of 28 spring wheat varieties under salt stress condition

Based on the membership function values of the five principal components and their factor weights (0.44, 0.23, 0.14, 0.10 and 0.09), comprehensive salt-tolerance evaluation values (D values) were calculated for the 28 spring wheat genotypes (Tables 2, 3 and 4). A higher D value represented relatively stronger salt tolerance within the tested population, whereas a lower D value indicated relatively weaker salt tolerance. Hierarchical clustering based on D values classified the 28 genotypes into five groups: salt-tolerant, relatively salt-tolerant, moderately salt-tolerant, relatively salt-sensitive, and salt-sensitive genotypes. Among these genotypes, Ningchun 32, Ningchun 16, Longchun 34, Ningchun 57, Ningchun 52, and Ruichun 1 were classified as salt-tolerant; Bamai 20, Ningchun 4, Linxia 35, Ningchun 11, and SM114 as relatively salt-tolerant; 1538, 2038, Longchun 41, Yongliang 15, Longchun 30, L623, 9396, L622, Linmai 33, and 1407 as moderately salt-tolerant; Ningchun 15, Yong 2563, Longchun 35, Dingxi 48, Dingxi 49, and Bamai 19 as relatively salt-sensitive; and Dingxi 40 as salt-sensitive (Fig. 1).

Potential phenotypic indicators for screening salt-tolerant genotypes

To identify traits associated with the comprehensive salt-tolerance ranking, linear regression analyses were conducted between the salt tolerance coefficient of each trait and the D value (Fig. 2). Under salt stress, the salt tolerance coefficient of plant height showed the strongest correlation with the D value (R2 = 0.44), followed by total root length, average length of all roots, cortex/stele area, and lacuna/cortex area, with R2 values of 0.40, 0.37, 0.32, and 0.33, respectively (Fig. 2A, G, H, O, and P). Except for the cortex/stele area ratio, which decreased with increasing D value, the other traits generally increased with increasing D value. These results suggest that plant height, total root length, average length of all roots, cortex/stele area ratio, and lacuna/cortex area ratio may serve as candidate traits associated with salt-tolerance variation in spring wheat seedlings. However, because the R2 values were moderate, these traits should be interpreted as useful indicators within the present experimental population rather than as independently validated diagnostic markers.

Correlations between aboveground traits and root architectural/anatomical traits in spring wheat under salt stress

Spearman’s correlation analysis further showed that shoot traits were significantly associated with root architectural and anatomical traits under salt stress (Fig. 3). Total root length was positively correlated with plant height and leaf area, suggesting that genotypes with better shoot growth tended to maintain greater root elongation. The cortex/stele area ratio was negatively correlated with plant height, leaf area, stomatal length, and stomatal density, but positively correlated with stomatal width. These correlations indicate that shoot growth performance under salt stress was associated not only with root size but also with root anatomical tissue allocation. It should be noted that these correlation results do not demonstrate direct functional causality. Instead, they provide evidence that root architectural and anatomical traits are closely associated with genotypic variation in shoot growth maintenance under salt stress.

Stratified root architecture in contrasting salt-sensitive and salt-tolerance genotypes

Based on the D-value ranking and salt-tolerance classification, two representative genotypes with salt-tolerant genotypes, Ruichun 1 and Ningchun 57, and two representative genotypes with salt-sensitive genotypes, Dingxi 40 and Dingxi 48, were selected for further stratified root architecture analysis (Fig. 4). These genotypes were chosen as contrasting representatives to examine whether differences in salt tolerance were associated with vertical root distribution. In the upper root layer, salt stress caused relatively limited divergence between salt-tolerant and salt-sensitive genotypes. Several root traits in the upper layer were maintained to some extent under salt treatment, although root surface area and total root length showed reductions in some genotypes. In contrast, the lower root layer showed clearer genotypic differences under salt stress. In salt-tolerant genotypes, root tip number, total root length, convex hull area, average root diameter, and root volume in the lower layer did not decrease significantly under salt treatment, whereas root surface area decreased by 18.68%–38.61% (P < 0.05). In salt-sensitive genotypes, root tip number, convex hull area, average root diameter, and root volume in the lower layer decreased more markedly, with reductions of 88.24%–90.00%, 80.91%–82.00%, 28.57%–44.17%, and 73.14%–79.63%, respectively (P < 0.05; Fig. 4F, H, J, L, N, and P). These results indicate that the maintenance of lower-layer root traits was more strongly associated with salt-tolerance differences than the response of upper-layer roots in the selected contrasting genotypes. Root anatomical responses in contrasting genotypes under salt stress

Root anatomical traits were further analyzed in the four contrasting genotypes to characterize their structural responses to salt stress (Fig. 5). Under salt treatment, total root cross-sectional area and cortical area generally decreased, whereas stele area remained relatively stable. Compared with the CK treatment, the total root cross-sectional area of the salt-tolerant genotypes Ruichun 1 and Ningchun 57 decreased by 21.24% and 18.63%, respectively, whereas that of the salt-sensitive genotypes Dingxi 40 and Dingxi 48 decreased by 38.12% and 21.41%, respectively (Fig. 5B). Cortical area showed a similar decreasing trend, with reductions of 24.04% and 28.62% in Ruichun 1 and Ningchun 57, and 27.10% and 45.83% in Dingxi 40 and Dingxi 48, respectively (Fig. 5D). The number of cortical cell layers also decreased under salt stress, but the magnitude differed among genotypes. Ruichun 1 and Ningchun 57 showed decreases of 16.67% and 10.14%, respectively, whereas Dingxi 40 and Dingxi 48 showed decreases of 20.77% and 22.08%, respectively (Fig. 5E). Correspondingly, cortex/stele area ratio decreased in all four genotypes, reflecting a shift in tissue allocation between cortex and stele under salt stress (Fig. 5G).

In contrast, lacuna-related anatomical traits showed the opposite pattern following salt treatment. Compared with CK treatment, lacuna area increased by 100.70%, 22.63%, 43.99%, and 130.39% in Ningchun 57, Ruichun 1, Dingxi 48, and Dingxi 40, respectively (Fig. 5F). In the salt-tolerant genotypes, such anatomical changes occurred together with relatively better maintenance of lower-layer root traits, suggesting a more coordinated architectural-anatomical response under salt stress.

Discussion

A multidimensional phenotyping framework improves salt-tolerance evaluation and key trait identification in spring wheat

This study focused on differences in salt tolerance among spring wheat genotypes at the seedling stage and established a multidimensional evaluation framework integrating aboveground phenotypes, root architectural traits, and root anatomical characteristics. The results showed that salt tolerance at the seedling stage in spring wheat should not be regarded as the outcome of responses in a single organ or a single trait; rather, it reflects the combined effects of shoot growth maintenance, adjustments in root spatial configuration, and remodeling of root anatomical structure. The first five principal components explained 81.37% of the total variation, indicating that although salt stress induced diverse phenotypic changes, genotypic differences in salt tolerance were still concentrated in a few key dimensions. This is consistent with current understanding that the salt stress response of wheat is characterized by pronounced coupling among multiple processes, involving not only shoot growth inhibition and transpiration regulation but also changes in root morphology, maintenance of ionic homeostasis, and root structural remodeling; consequently, a single indicator is often insufficient to adequately explain actual salt tolerance [19, 20].

Previous studies on wheat salt tolerance have largely screened genotypes from single perspectives, such as plant height, biomass, leaf area, ionic balance, or stomatal behavior. However, recent research has also begun to show that salt stress simultaneously suppresses multiple root traits, including total root length, root surface area, and root tip number, indicating that a single trait is often inadequate to reliably distinguish salt tolerance differences among genotypes. Tang et al. [9], in their study of wheat root phenotypes, demonstrated that salt stress significantly inhibited total root length, root surface area, and root tip number, accompanied by changes in hormone- and gene expression-related regulation. Compared with these studies, the advance of the present work lies in not only incorporating root morphological traits into the screening process, but also further integrating root anatomical ratios into the evaluation framework, so that salt tolerance classification is no longer merely a phenotypic ranking based on the severity of injury, but begins to acquire greater structural interpretability.

More importantly, the key indicators identified in this study included not only morphological traits such as plant height, total root length, and average length of all roots, but also anatomical traits such as the cortex/stele area and lacuna/cortex area. This indicates that the identification of salt tolerance in spring wheat seedlings depends not only on whether the plant can continue to grow, but also on how the root system is maintained and remodeled. Therefore, compared with conventional single-trait screening approaches, the comprehensive evaluation system established in this study is more conducive to identifying germplasm with genuine salt tolerance potential and also provides a more operational phenotypic basis for subsequent salt-tolerance breeding based on root-related traits.

Coordinated variation in root architecture and anatomical organization is associated with aboveground growth maintenance under salt stress

The second major finding of this study is that root architectural traits and anatomical tissue ratios do not vary independently under salt stress; rather, they exhibit pronounced coordinated variation and are jointly associated with the maintenance of aboveground growth. Total root length was significantly positively correlated with plant height and leaf area, whereas the cortex/stele area was significantly associated with plant height, leaf area, and several stomatal traits, indicating that differences in shoot phenotypes are at least partly linked to on the capacity of the root system to maintain spatial extension and internal structural stability. However, these relationships should be interpreted as associations rather than direct evidence of causality, because physiological processes such as root hydraulic conductivity, ion transport capacity, and carbon allocation were not directly measured in the present study. In other words, genotypes with better aboveground growth performance do not simply possess greater tolerance at the leaf level. Their root systems also tend to display stronger spatial expansion capacity and a more favorable internal tissue configuration. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies showing that root–shoot coordination, root-mediated water uptake, ion homeostasis, and resource allocation are important components of wheat responses to salt stress [8, 21] At the same time, reviews on cereal root plasticity have emphasized that salt adaptation involves not only aboveground injury symptoms but also root spatial adjustment and architectural remodeling in saline environments [10, 22]. Therefore, the present results support and extend existing knowledge by providing evidence from spring wheat seedlings that root architectural and anatomical traits can be jointly considered when evaluating salt-tolerance variation.

The contribution of this study should therefore be positioned cautiously. We do not claim that the integration of root architecture and anatomy is entirely new, as related concepts such as root plasticity, aerenchyma formation, and root–shoot coordination under stress have been reported in cereals. Rather, the novelty of the present work lies in applying a combined phenotyping framework to spring wheat seedlings, in which aboveground traits, whole-root architecture, and anatomical tissue ratios were analyzed together within the same salt-tolerance evaluation system. This approach allowed us to identify candidate root-related traits that were associated with salt-tolerance performance under controlled conditions. Total root length and average root length may reflect the capacity of the root system to maintain spatial extension under salt stress, whereas the cortex/stele area ratio and the lacuna/cortex area ratio may indicate shifts in tissue allocation and aeration-related structures within roots. Previous studies on the Poaceae root cortex have shown that cortical tissues are dynamic functional structures and that their remodeling can influence root respiration, radial water and nutrient transport, and stress adaptation [14, 23]. In the present study, the concurrent variation in root architecture and anatomical traits suggests a possible architectural–anatomical association in spring wheat roots under salt stress. Nevertheless, because this inference is based mainly on phenotypic and correlative analyses, further physiological validation is required to determine whether these anatomical changes directly contribute to improved root function and shoot growth maintenance.

The adaptive value of deep roots depends on the integration of root configuration and anatomical adjustment under salt stress

The most distinctive finding of this study is that differences in salt tolerance among spring wheat genotypes can be further linked to the maintenance capacity of the lower root system and the associated anatomical adjustments. Our results showed that, under salt stress, upper roots were generally able to maintain a certain degree of growth, and the divergence among different genotype groups was relatively limited. In contrast, lower roots were more sensitive to salt stress, and the differences between salt-tolerant and salt-sensitive genotypes became much more pronounced in root tip number, total root length, convex hull area, average root diameter, and root volume. These results suggest that the key root-related basis of salt tolerance at the seedling stage is not merely whether superficial roots can be retained, but whether deeper roots can continue to elongate and occupy vertical space under salt stress. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that root spatial configuration and root plasticity are closely related to stress adaptation [10, 22]. Moreover, Guo et al. [24] demonstrated that the maintenance of lower-root traits contributed to drought tolerance in seedlings, further supporting the adaptive significance of lower-root maintenance under adverse conditions.

The adaptive significance of deeper roots under salt stress should be interpreted together with root anatomical plasticity. Salt stress imposes both osmotic stress and ionic toxicity, which restrict root elongation, water uptake, and ion transport, ultimately disrupting root growth and whole-plant physiological homeostasis [19, 20, 25]. Therefore, roots must balance continued soil exploration with the metabolic cost of tissue construction and maintenance. In this study, all genotypes showed decreased total cross-sectional area and cortical area, relatively stable stele area, and increased lacuna area after salt stress. However, compared with salt-sensitive genotypes, salt-tolerant genotypes showed a smaller reduction in cortical tissues, a slower decline in cortical cell layers, and a more moderate and coordinated adjustment in the lacuna/cortex area ratio. These changes indicate that salt-tolerant roots may reduce excessive investment in living cortical tissues while maintaining stele-related transport capacity, thereby supporting continued root elongation under stress. Similar anatomical plasticity has been reported in cereal crops. For example, increased lacuna formation can reduce the proportion of living cortical tissues, thereby lowering root respiration and the metabolic cost of soil exploration, which may improve the efficiency of water and nutrient acquisition under stress conditions [26–29]. In addition, studies on the Poaceae root cortex have emphasized that cortical tissues are dynamic functional structures rather than passive supporting tissues, and that their remodeling can regulate root respiration, radial water and nutrient transport, and whole-plant stress adaptation [13, 14, 23].

Root cortical aerenchyma or lacuna formation may also have a dual functional role. On the one hand, increased lacuna formation can reduce the proportion of living cortical tissues, thereby lowering respiratory cost and improving the efficiency of soil exploration under stress [28–32]. On the other hand, excessive cortical disintegration may restrict radial transport pathways and impair root function if it is not coordinated with stele maintenance and root elongation, as root cortical aerenchyma and cortical senescence have been reported to reduce radial nutrient and water transport in crop roots [23, 33]. Therefore, the adaptive value of lacuna formation depends not simply on its magnitude, but on whether it occurs in a coordinated manner with overall root function. The present results support this interpretation. In salt-tolerant genotypes, increased lacuna/cortex area ratio was accompanied by relatively stable lower-root length, root volume, and stele area, suggesting an adaptive remodeling process that may help maintain root function. In contrast, in salt-sensitive genotypes, lacuna formation occurred together with severe lower-root growth inhibition and stronger cortical contraction, which may reflect passive injury rather than effective adaptation.

Accordingly, the root basis of salt tolerance in spring wheat seedlings should not be attributed to a single structural trait. Instead, it appears to depend on the coordinated action of three components: sustained elongation of the lower root system, favorable tissue allocation between cortex and stele, and moderate lacuna formation without serious impairment of overall root function. Such coordination may help salt-tolerant genotypes reduce root maintenance costs, preserve axial transport capacity, and maintain root growth under salt stress. By contrast, salt-sensitive genotypes are more likely to experience a cascading process involving lower-root growth decline, excessive cortical contraction, and functional mismatch. This integrated root architectural–anatomical strategy provides a plausible explanation for why deeper-root maintenance is closely associated with improved salt tolerance in spring wheat seedlings.

Limitations and future perspectives

Several limitations should be explicitly acknowledged. First, all phenotypic and anatomical analyses were performed under a controlled indoor paper-based cultivation system at the seedling stage. This system enables standardized and high-throughput comparison of root traits, but it cannot fully reproduce the complex conditions of field saline soils, including soil mechanical impedance, heterogeneous salt distribution, water and nutrient gradients, ion diffusion, and rhizosphere interactions. Therefore, the root responses observed here should be interpreted as evidence obtained under controlled conditions rather than direct field performance.

Second, the detailed stratified root and anatomical analyses were performed using four contrasting genotypes selected according to D-value ranking and salt-tolerance classification. These materials are useful for comparing representative salt-tolerant and salt-sensitive responses, but they cannot fully represent the diversity of all spring wheat genotypes. Future studies should include larger germplasm panels to verify whether the candidate root traits identified here are stable and broadly applicable.

Third, the present study mainly provides phenotypic, anatomical, and correlative evidence. Although the observed associations suggest that deeper-root maintenance and anatomical remodeling may be related to salt-tolerance performance, direct physiological mechanisms were not tested. Future studies should validate these traits in soil-column, pot, rhizobox, and field saline-soil experiments, and further integrate Na⁺/K⁺ homeostasis, root hydraulic conductivity, respiration, suberin or lignin deposition, and transcriptomic analyses to clarify the physiological and molecular basis of this root adaptive strategy. Finally, future studies should include multiple salinity levels and sampling time points to determine threshold responses, genotype × salinity interactions, and the spatiotemporal dynamics of root architectural and anatomical responses.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that the coordination between root system architecture and anatomical structure is associated with salt adaptation in spring wheat seedlings (Fig. 6). Our findings support the following conclusions: (1) salt tolerance in spring wheat is not determined by a single trait, but by the coordinated action of shoot growth maintenance, root architectural adjustment, and anatomical remodeling; (2) the capacity to maintain deeper roots is a key feature distinguishing salt-tolerant from salt-sensitive genotypes; and (3) anatomical remodeling characterized by coordinated cortical contraction and lacuna adjustment helps sustain root growth and development. The results demonstrated significant genotypic variation in salt tolerance among spring wheat genotypes and identified plant height, total root length, average root length, cortex/stele area ratio, and lacuna/cortex area ratio as key phenotypic indicators. Compared with salt-sensitive genotypes, salt-tolerant genotypes maintained deeper roots more stably and exhibited more coordinated adjustments in cortex–stele–lacuna organization. Salt stress promoted root anatomical remodeling, and we propose that enhanced lacuna formation does not necessarily indicate aggravated injury, but may instead have adaptive significance under stress conditions when coordinated with deeper maintenance and stele stability. Collectively, these findings strongly support the view that salt-tolerant spring wheat can reduce the maintenance cost of living cortical tissues, optimize tissue allocation, and sustain lower-root elongation, thereby supporting root growth and salt adaptation under stress.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

In this study, 28 spring wheat varieties that have been approved at the national or provincial level in recent years were used as experimental materials. These varieties originated from arid and semi-arid ecological regions of northwestern China, including Ningxia, Inner Mongolia, Gansu, Xinjiang, and Qinghai. All plant materials were provided by the Wheat Research Group of the Department of Plant Breeding and Genetics, Gansu Agricultural University. Detailed information on the varieties is presented in Table S1.

Experimental design

This experiment was conducted from December 2, 2024 to April 13, 2025 in an artificial climate chamber at the State Key Laboratory of Aridland Crop Science, Gansu Agricultural University (36°03′N, 103°44′E). Spring wheat seeds were first surface-sterilized sequentially with 75% ethanol and 5% sodium hypochlorite for the specified duration, followed by rinsing five to eight times with distilled water. The sterilized seeds were then placed in Petri dishes lined with filter paper (1.5 cm deep and 15 cm in diameter), with 60 healthy seeds of uniform size and free from mechanical damage evenly arranged per dish, and germinated for 3 d in darkness at 25/22°C (day/night). After germination, seedlings with uniform vigor and balanced root and shoot growth were selected from each dish and transferred to a paper-based phenotyping platform.

For each variety, 30 paper pouches were prepared, with one seedling planted per pouch and positioned at the center of the filter paper to ensure close root–paper contact. One-quarter-strength Hoagland nutrient solution was then added in a timely manner to support normal seedling growth. A total of 840 paper pouches were used in the experiment. Twenty-four hours after transplanting, 15 of the 30 pouches for each variety were randomly assigned to salt stress treatment, while the remaining 15 served as the control and continued to receive one-quarter-strength Hoagland nutrient solution.

When plants reached the two-leaf stage, a 7-day salt stress treatment was imposed with two treatments: normal condition (NC, 0 mM NaCl) and salt stress (SS, 200 mM NaCl), each with five replicates. The pH of the nutrient solution was adjusted to 6.0 ± 0.1, and the solution was replaced every 3 d. Environmental conditions were maintained as follows: day/night temperatures of approximately 28 ± 2/25 ± 2 °C, a photoperiod of 14/10 h (light/dark), relative humidity of 40–50%, and a light intensity of 600 µmol m− 2 s− 1. During the experimental period, aeration was supplied twice daily for 30 min each time.

Determination of the aboveground traits

Sample collection was conducted on the seventh day of salt-stress treatment. For each treatment, three representative wheat plants were randomly selected for the determination of morphological traits. Plant height was defined as the length from the coleoptile node to the apex of the main stem and was measured using a ruler, while leaf area was calculated using the length–width coefficient method proposed by Ma et al. [34].

On the seventh day of salt-stress treatment, leaf stomatal characteristics were measured between 10:30 and 12:00 following the method described by Wang et al. [35]. Briefly, stomatal traits were assessed using a combined nail polish and transparent tape method [36], with measurements conducted on the second fully expanded leaf from the top. Nail polish was evenly applied to the abaxial leaf surface and allowed to air-dry for 2–5 min. Transparent tape was then placed over the dried nail polish layer to obtain an impression, which was subsequently mounted for observation. Stomatal images were captured using an optical microscope (Leica 2500, Leica Microsystems, Germany). For each image, five fields of view were randomly selected, and within each field, five stomata were randomly chosen for analysis. ImageJ software was used to determine stomatal length (µm), stomatal width (µm), and stomatal aperture (µm) (Fig. 7). The area of each field of view was recorded, and stomatal number per image was counted and divided by the corresponding area to calculate the mean stomatal density (µm²) for each field.

Determination of the RSA

The imaging–germination platform consisted of plant germination pouches, a pouch holder, germination pouch cabinet, a small photographic lightbox, a high-resolution scanner, and a laptop computer (Fig. 7). The paper-based system employed germination pouches made of high-quality Whatman No.1 filter paper combined with standard plant germination bags (180 mm × 125 mm). The pouch holder was a rectangular structure (20 cm high, 15 cm wide, and 30 cm long) assembled from ten transparent acrylic panels, capable of accommodating nine germination pouches simultaneously, thereby facilitating uniform placement and management of seedlings.

Similarly, the germination pouch cabinet was a rectangular structure (200 cm high, 100 cm long, and 50 cm wide) equipped with four internal shelves, each measuring 100 cm × 50 cm. An adjustable 220 V LED light strip was installed above each shelf to provide supplemental illumination and ensure precise control of the photoperiod during cultivation. The small photographic lightbox measured approximately 60 cm × 60 cm × 60 cm and was internally lined with non-reflective black or dark background materials (e.g., black foam board or black fabric) to minimize reflections and shadows during image acquisition. Evenly distributed LED light strips inside the lightbox provided uniform illumination, ensuring consistent lighting conditions across the imaging area. This enclosed imaging environment provided stable and standardized background and lighting conditions, thereby improving image clarity and consistency.

Root samples were collected on the seventh day of salt-stress treatment. For each treatment group, three representative wheat plants were randomly selected. Whole root systems were imaged using a high-resolution scanner (GP1600AF, COMET, China) at a resolution of 1200 dpi, enabling rapid and clear scanning. The entire imaging system was controlled by a laptop computer (LAPTOP−5MNUD00G, HONOR, China) to ensure stable and uniform lighting conditions during scanning, thereby optimizing image quality. The acquired digital images were saved in TIFF format. Following image acquisition, preprocessing was conducted using Photoshop (Ps), including image scaling, cropping, background adjustment, and horizontal flipping, to enhance image clarity and facilitate subsequent batch analysis. The preprocessed images were then imported into RootNav and RhizoVision Explorer software for quantitative extraction of root traits (specific parameters are listed in Table S2). To ensure standardized data management, all images were systematically renamed according to predefined sample identification codes.

Determination of root anatomical structure

Samples were collected on the seventh day of salt-stress treatment. From each plant, three root segments of approximately 0.5 cm in length were excised from the 0–3 cm, 3–6 cm, and 6–9 cm regions from the root tip, respectively, with three biological replicates established for each treatment. This fixed-distance sampling strategy was adopted with reference to the approach used by Guo et al. [37] in the study of root cortical senescence in cotton, because root cortical differentiation and senescence are strongly dependent on the longitudinal developmental position from the root tip rather than on the proportional position along roots of different total lengths. The collected root segments were first preserved in 75% ethanol for subsequent anatomical analysis of root cortical tissues. Phenotypic analysis of cortical tissue development was performed using acridine orange staining following the procedure described by Henry and Deacon [38]. The stained root segments were then embedded in Tissue-Tek CRYO-OCT compound (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at−20 °C. Transverse root sections with a thickness of 8 μm were prepared using a cryostat microtome (Leica CM1950, Germany) and immediately mounted onto microscope slides for imaging. Finally, cross-sectional images of the roots were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope (ECLIPSE Ti2-U, Nikon, Japan) and a laser confocal scanning microscope (FV−1000, Olympus, Japan) (Fig. 7). Image-based phenotypic analysis was conducted using ImageJ software (http://rsbweb.nih.gov/ij). The extracted anatomical traits included cortex cell layers, stele area, cortex area, lacuna area, cortex/stele area ratio, and lacuna/cortex area ratio.

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel 2019 was used for data recording, organization, calculation of functions, and computation of D values. SPSS 27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was employed for analysis of variance, principal component analysis, correlation analysis, and data standardization. Microsoft Excel 2019 was also used to calculate membership function values and D values. Origin 2022b (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA) was applied for data visualization and hierarchical cluster analysis. Data are presented as means ± standard error (mean ± SE). One-way analysis of variance followed by the least significant difference (LSD) test was used to determine significant differences at the 95% or 99% confidence levels. Adobe Illustrator 2020 was used for figure assembly. Systematic clustering of D values was performed using the Euclidean distance method combined with hierarchical clustering. Prior to principal component analysis, variables were standardized to eliminate differences in measurement units.
