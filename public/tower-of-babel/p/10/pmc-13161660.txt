Eye movement analysis during working memory tasks correlates with cognitive, clinical, and anatomical metrics in multiple sclerosis
Authors: Cecilie Fjeldstad Pardo, Gerardo Fernandez, Bujana Allushi, Adithya A Bhaktaram, Lorna Galleguillos, Danilo Verge, Gabriel Pardo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCognitive dysfunction involving working memory is common in multiple sclerosis (MS) but is often under-detected in clinical assessments. Noninvasive digital biomarkers like oculomotor metrics may enhance the detection of neurofunctional changes.ObjectiveDetermine whether oculomotor metrics during a visuospatial N-back task reflect cognitive dysfunction and correspond with clinical measures in patients with MS.MethodsIn a cross-sectional study, 38 patients completed an N-back working memory task with eye tracking to record gaze duration and task accuracy. Clinical measures included Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT), retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL), macular volume, Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS), and Nine-Hole Peg Test (9HPT). Linear regression models examined relationships between task accuracy, gaze duration, and clinical measures.ResultsGaze duration and task accuracy were inversely correlated (r = −0.57, p < 0.001). Shorter gaze duration and higher task accuracy, indicators of better cognitive processing efficiency, were associated with better SDMT scores, greater RNFL thickness and macular volume, and better EDSS and 9HPT completion times.ConclusionsOculomotor metrics recorded during a working memory task are associated with cognitive, retinal, and functional measures in pwMS, supporting their potential as noninvasive biomarkers to complement standard clinical assessments in detecting cognitive dysfunction.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is a chronic, immune-mediated disorder of the central nervous system that often leads to long-term neurological disability. 1 The disease is characterized by a dynamic and multifactorial pathobiology involving inflammation, demyelination, gliosis, and axonal degeneration. These processes impact both white and gray matter across the brain, spinal cord, and visual and oculomotor pathways.2,3 As a result, people with MS (pwMS) may experience a wide range of neurological symptoms such as cognitive dysfunction, sensory disturbances, motor deficits, impaired coordination and fatigue that evolve unpredictably over time. Visual symptoms, often linked to optic neuritis or ocular dysmotility, are also common and may be among the earliest clinical signs of MS. 4 Several clinical assessment tools are routinely used in research and clinical practice to monitor disease progression. The Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS) provides a comprehensive estimate of neurological impairment, with higher scores indicating greater disability. 5 The Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT) serves as a sensitive measure of cognitive performance, particularly in terms of processing speed where higher scores reflect better cognitive efficiency. 6 Additionally, the Nine-Hole Peg Test (9HPT) evaluates upper-limb coordination and fine motor control, with longer completion times reflecting greater motor impairment. 7 While these tools are well-established, they tend to detect more pronounced clinical impairments, potentially missing early or subtle functional disruptions, especially in cognitive and motor domains. 8

Developing sensitive and meaningful tools that measure subtle disability changes remains an unmet clinical and research need in MS. This has increased the interest in noninvasive biomarkers that detect early cognitive changes in MS. Among these, the retina has become a widely utilized site of investigation due to its embryological and anatomical continuity with the central nervous system. 9 Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is commonly used in clinical and research settings and enables high-resolution imaging of retinal structures such as the peripapillary retinal nerve fiber layer (RNFL) thickness and macular volume. 10 In pwMS, thinning in these layers is consistently associated with axonal degeneration and has been correlated with both physical disability and brain atrophy.11,12 Conversely, greater RNFL thickness and macular volume indicate preserved anatomical retinal integrity and healthier neuroaxonal structure.

In parallel, oculomotor metrics are being investigated as a dynamic biomarker of neurological function. Eye movements such as saccades, fixations, and gaze duration depend on coordinated activity across cortical, subcortical, brainstem, and cerebellar regions. 13 Disruptions in these networks can lead to detectable oculomotor anomalies, including prolonged fixation/gaze duration, unstable fixation indicating issues with gaze, and increased error rates in tasks requiring executive control.14,15 These changes may reflect broader neurodegenerative and cognitive dysfunction and have been associated with domains affected early in MS, such as working memory, attention, and executive function.16,17 Among oculomotor metrics, gaze duration which is defined as the cumulative time spent fixating on a target during task execution, has emerged as a reliable and reproducible measure of visual-cognitive processing across different eye-tracking platforms and task paradigms. 18 Shorter gaze duration generally reflects more efficient neural processing, whereas longer gaze duration indicates increased processing demands and reduced cognitive efficiency. In other neurological diseases like Parkinson's disease (PD) it has been shown that fixation or gaze duration is longer in all patients with PD compared to healthy controls but it is longest in cognitively impaired PD groups. 19 In Alzheimer's disease (AD), a study on visual exploration reported that patients with AD showed altered gaze patterns with more time spent within regions of interest and impaired deployment of attention as cognitive decline progressed. 20

In this study we evaluated whether oculomotor metrics, specifically gaze duration, can serve as a functional indicator of neurological cognitive impairments in MS by implementing a visuospatial N-back task test with embedded eye-tracking technology which captured said gaze duration. The N-back task is used to evaluate working memory measured through task accuracy which is defined as the percentage of correct responses for the task. This combined approach of the N-back task with eye-tracking technology enabled us to test cognition through eye movement analysis and evaluate the sensitivity of oculomotor metrics in detecting neurofunctional changes. We then examined the association between these two functional measures—gaze duration and task accuracy, and standard clinical measurements, namely EDSS, SDMT, and 9HPT, as well as retinal structural changes, including RNFL thickness and macular volume.

Methods

Study design and population

A cross-sectional study was carried out at the Multiple Sclerosis Center of Excellence at Oklahoma Medical Research Foundation (OMRF) in Oklahoma, USA. The study population consisted of 38 patients with MS (81% female). A nonrandom convenience sampling was carried out with patients receiving clinical care at OMRF. Study inclusion criteria required patients to be over 18 years of age, have no visual function impairments (high-contrast visual acuity, visual field deficits or ocular dysmotility) that would prevent the completion of the test, and be able to understand the evaluator's instructions. Patients who were unable to complete device calibration were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from individuals prior to participation in the study, which was approved by the OMRF Institutional Review Board.

Study variables

Demographic and clinical data were collected, including age, gender, race, MS clinical phenotype, disease duration and disease-modifying therapies history (Table 1). Clinical measures included oral SDMT, OCT (RNFL and macular volume), EDSS, and 9HPT.

OCT imaging was performed using a spectral-domain OCT system (Heidelberg Engineering, Spectralis) following standardized clinical protocols. Peripapillary RNFL thickness and macular volume were obtained using automated segmentation. All scans were reviewed for quality, and images with poor signal strength or segmentation errors were excluded. Responses to the N-back task and gaze duration were recorded using a specific Head Mounted Device (HMD) with embedded eye tracking technology. The N-back task was carried out by a single evaluator.

N-back task

Stimuli were presented against a black background and consisted of red circles (30 mm in diameter). A central fixation cross (15 × 15 mm) was displayed, surrounded by six peripheral boxes: two positioned 10° to the left and right of fixation, and four placed diagonally at 45° angles (top right, bottom right, top left, and bottom left). Each box measured 58 × 55 mm and was outlined with a white border to distinguish it from the background. While maintaining fixation on the central cross, participants observed three red circles that appeared sequentially in any of the six peripheral boxes, each presented for 250 milliseconds (ms). Participants were required to memorize both the spatial location (box) and the order of stimulus presentation. After the sequence, the fixation cross disappeared, and participants were instructed to shift their gaze to the boxes in the reverse order of presentation: first to the location of the last circle, then to the second, and finally to the first. Working memory conditions (WMem) and WMem loads (0-back, 1-back, and 2-back) were reflected in the instructions. An illustration of the N-back task is provided in Figure 1.

Equipment

Participants were seated comfortably in a chair and fitted with the HMD (Varjo Aero, Varjo Technologies Oy, Helsinki, Finland) equipped with integrated eye tracking. The experimental tasks were executed using ViewMind software (ViewMind, Inc, USA), which ran on a computer configured to meet the hardware requirements of the device (https://varjo.com/products/aero/). All stimuli were generated through ViewMind's proprietary algorithms and presented along the central visual axis of the HMD. Eye movements were recorded binocularly using the HMD's built-in eye tracker at a sampling rate of 200 Hz. Both calibration and data acquisition were performed binocularly.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R version 3.1.1 (RDevelopment Core Team). Measures of central tendency and measures of dispersion were calculated for continuous variables, while frequencies and percentages were presented for categorical variables. Percentage of correct responses (% of times the participant responded correctly to target position) and gaze duration (defined as the cumulative time in ms spent fixating on a target during task execution) were considered as dependent variables for each model. Clinical measurements such as EDSS, SDMT, 9HPT, RNFL, and macular volume were added as predictors. This statistical approach is particularly well-suited for capturing complex, nested data structures and for identifying subtle associations between multimodal predictors (SDMT, EDSS, 9HPT, and OCT) and eye movement behavior (gaze duration). Data analysis used only right eye data, as defined per study protocol.

Results

The study cohort included 38 patients with MS (97.4% relapsing–remitting MS and 2.6% secondary–progressive MS), comprising 31 females and 7 males. The mean age was 45.0 ± 9.9 years, with a mean baseline EDSS score of 2.3 ± 1.82 and an average disease duration of 13.5 ± 7.9 years. To evaluate whether oculomotor metrics reflect cognitive performance, study participants completed a visuospatial N-back task using eye-tracking technology. During the task, gaze duration was assessed as an oculomotor metric, while task accuracy (percentage of correct responses) was used as a measure of cognitive performance. We first examined the relationship between gaze duration and task accuracy and subsequently evaluated both metrics in relation to clinical measures, including SDMT, EDSS, 9HPT, RNFL, and macular volume. A significant inverse correlation was observed between gaze duration and task accuracy (r = −0.57, p < 0.001), such that shorter gaze duration was associated with higher percentage of correct responses during the N-back task (Figure 2).

Task accuracy—percentage of correct responses

When evaluating task accuracy to clinical metrics our results showed that SDMT was the strongest correlate of task accuracy. Better SDMT scores were associated with greater percentage of correct responses (ß= 6.02 ± 0.14, t = 41.64, p < 0.0001). In other words, patients with better SDMT scores performed more accurately on the working memory task. Similarly, better retinal measures like greater RNFL thickness (ß= 1.08 ± 0.16, t = 6.71, p < 0.0001) and macular volume (ß= 1.05 ± 0.16, t = 6.48, p < 0.0001) were associated with a higher task accuracy. Patients with better retinal structural integrity had higher percentage of correct responses. In contrast, our data showed that slower 9HPT completion time (ß= −7.21 ± 0.14, t = −51.12, p < 0.0001) and worse EDSS scores (ß= −0.46 ± 0.15, t = −3, p = 0.002) were associated with lower task accuracy. Patients who took longer to complete 9HPT and scored higher in EDSS performed less accurately on the working memory task. Collectively, SDMT, RNFL thickness, and macular volume showed positive associations with task accuracy, while 9HPT completion time and EDSS scores showed negative associations (Table 2A and Figure 3).

Gaze duration—the cumulative time spent fixating on a target

Regarding the association of gaze duration with clinical metrics the analysis revealed that SDMT, RNFL thickness and macular volume were all negatively associated with gaze duration. Better SDMT scores were associated with shorter gaze duration (ß= −0.07, t = −15.81, p < 0.0001). In other words, patients with better SDMT scores spent less time fixating on the target. For retinal structural measures, greater RNFL thickness (β = −0.08, t = −16.52, p < 0.0001) and macular volume (β = −0.06, t = −13.73, p < 0.0001) were associated with shorter gaze duration such that patients with better retinal structural integrity showed shorter gaze duration. In contrast, slower 9HPT completion time (β = 0.02, t = 5.79, p < 0.0001) and higher EDSS scores (β = 0.01, t = 3.41, p < 0.0001) were associated with longer gaze duration (Table 2B and Figure 4). Patients who took longer to complete the 9HPT and had higher EDSS scores displayed longer fixation times.

Discussion

The present study examined whether oculomotor metrics recorded during a visuospatial N-back working memory task, using eye-tracking technology, can provide insight into cognitive changes in people with MS. Two primary outcomes were assessed: (a) task accuracy, defined as the percentage of correct responses and reflecting working memory performance and (b) gaze duration defined as the cumulative time spent fixating on a target during task execution, representing a functional marker of oculomotor and visual-cognitive processing during task execution. We found an inverse correlation between task accuracy and gaze duration such that patients with higher task accuracy displayed shorter fixation times. Importantly, none of the patients had ocular dysmotility on examination, supporting the interpretation that the observed differences in gaze duration reflect cognitive processing efficiency rather than primary oculomotor impairments. These results indicate that better cognitive functioning is associated with more efficient visual-cognitive processing and show that oculomotor metrics can serve as a functional marker of cognitive changes in pwMS. However, in the clinical setting the directionality of the relationship between oculomotor metrics and cognition warrants careful consideration. Rather than reflecting a unidirectional effect, both gaze duration and task accuracy share underlying neural efficiency within distributed cortico–subcortical networks affected by MS. Two interpretations are plausible: faster information processing may reduce the time required to extract and integrate visual input, thereby shortening fixation duration; alternatively, more efficient oculomotor control may itself support cognitive performance by stabilizing visual input, reducing attentional demands, and facilitating more effective encoding of task-relevant information.

In the context of MS, slowed neural transmission and impaired network integration due to demyelination and axonal loss may increase the time necessary to stabilize visual attention, update working memory representations, and select appropriate responses resulting in prolonged gaze duration. Given the cross-sectional nature of this study, the directionality of this relationship cannot be established. However, our results show that oculomotor behavior serves as a sensitive, real-time readout of network-level processing demands. Additionally, our findings show that clinical metrics used in MS such as EDSS, SDMT, 9HPT, RNFL, and macular volume were associated with both task performance accuracy and oculomotor metrics during N-back working memory task. Among all the evaluated clinical indicators, SDMT emerged as the strongest correlate of N-back task accuracy, where better scores were associated with greater percentage of correct responses. While SDMT is a well-validated test used to measure cognitive processing speed in MS, the N-back task provides a performance-based measure of working memory that also engages broader cognitive processes. 21 The strong predictive effect of SDMT on N-back task accuracy emphasized the interconnection between processing speed and working memory cognitive domains in MS. Moreover, these results indicate that the N-back task with eye-tracking technology could complement SDMT in a clinical setting by adding a visual-cognitive dimension to the evaluation beyond processing speed alone. In addition to SDMT, better retinal structural integrity as shown by greater macular volume and RNFL thickness was also associated with better task accuracy. In MS, macular volume is considered a biomarker of global neurodegenerative processes and is associated with RNFL thickness. 22 Their emergence as predictors of cognitive performance suggests that global neuroaxonal degeneration, reflected by retinal structural measures, is associated with higher-order cognitive processing. This is supported by the growing evidence that thinning of retinal layers in MS is not only an ocular phenomenon, but it correlates strongly with brain atrophy and measures of global neurodegeneration, particularly thalamic-cortical atrophy. 23 Studies have linked retinal thinning with impairments in information processing, memory, and attention.9,11,12,24 Furthermore, retinal measures including RNFL and macular volume have been shown to precede cognitive decline and predict long-term disability outcomes in MS, including over a 10-year follow-up period.25,26 Collectively these findings position ocular metrics as a noninvasive and sensitive marker for tracking neurodegeneration and cognitive decline in MS.

Clinical metrics that evaluate aspects of disability such as 9HPT and EDSS, were inversely associated with N-back task accuracy. Our results showed that greater motor impairment identified through longer 9HPT completion times, and a higher disability burden shown by EDSS were associated with poor task accuracy. Although EDSS and 9HPT primarily assess physical and motor disability, their association with N-back accuracy suggests that greater global disease burden might also affect cognitive efficiency.

Furthermore, gaze duration showed a negative association with SDMT, where patients with shorter gaze duration had better SDMT scores. This relationship suggests that oculomotor measurements may serve as a noninvasive tool for assessing cognitive function represented by a more efficient cognitive processing in gaze duration testing in those that had better information processing speed as measured by SDMT. While SDMT captures cognitive processing speed through a psychomotor response, gaze duration reflects the efficiency of visual encoding and spatial working memory during active task execution. These two metrics are related but evaluate distinct cognitive domains and together provide a more comprehensive characterization of cognitive function in pwMS. Beyond gaze duration, prior work has shown that specific oculomotor abnormalities such as saccadic dysfunction are independently associated with reduced cognitive performance in MS.27,28

In addition to SDMT, gaze duration was also negatively associated with retinal structural measures. Shorter gaze duration was observed in patients with greater RNFL thickness and higher macular volume. Given that RNFL and macular volume are established proxies of global neuroaxonal integrity in MS rather than isolated visual pathway function, these associations likely reflect broader neurodegenerative processes. Whether visual pathway-specific contributions play an additional role cannot be determined from the present data, as afferent visual measures beyond high contrast visual acuity were not assessed. Longer gaze duration was associated with worse performance on clinical disability measures, including higher EDSS scores and longer 9HPT completion times, indicating that increased fixation time may reflect a broader disease burden, where both motor and neurological impairment are accompanied by reduced efficiency in cognitive processing. Together, these findings position gaze duration as a potential composite marker that captures the interplay between retinal structural damage, physical disability, and cognitive efficiency, reinforcing the notion that oculomotor metrics can inform on both cognition and neurodegeneration in pwMS.

Our results indicate that pwMS with more pronounced neurodegeneration or functional impairment require more time to visually process and respond to task stimuli. Previous studies29,30 have suggested that effective gaze control is fundamental for maintaining information in working memory and for sustaining appropriate stimulus–response associations. The retention of task-relevant information is largely mediated by the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and its thalamo-cortico-striatal loops. These circuits connect specific regions of the PFC with corresponding areas in the basal ganglia and thalamus, supporting the coordination of goal-directed behavior.31,32 In MS, as in other neurological conditions, prolonged gaze duration may reflect inefficiencies in cortical integration processes needed to build and maintain spatial representation of target locations. 33

Together, these findings highlight the potential of a multimodal approach to monitor neurofunctional decline in MS. Our approach shows that combining clinical, structural (retinal), and functional (oculomotor) measures captures distinct but related aspects of neurodegeneration and cognitive dysfunction, providing a more comprehensive characterization of impairment than single-domain assessments. The ability of gaze-based metrics to inform about underlying structural damage and cognitive status in real-time, in a noninvasive way, makes them particularly attractive for patient monitoring and interventional studies. Moreover, the use of the tested technology in this context demonstrates the feasibility of applying advanced eye-tracking tools in real-world cognitive tasks. From a clinical standpoint, integrating oculomotor metrics with OCT and standard assessments could improve early detection of cognitive decline and help monitor disease progression and treatment response in MS.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations that should be considered. The sample size and variability in disease stage may limit generalization of the results. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences or tracking of longitudinal changes in cognitive function. OCT metrics were limited to RNFL and macular volume and did not include ganglion cell-inner plexiform layer (GCIPL) thickness, another OCT sensitive biomarker of global neurodegeneration. Additionally, other visual function metrics such as low contrast visual acuity and color vision testing were not performed. These factors limit conclusions regarding visual pathway-specific contributions to the observed associations. Future studies should validate these findings in larger, more diverse cohorts and investigate how oculomotor and retinal metrics evolve over time and in response to disease-modifying therapies. Furthermore, fatigue and depression were not formally assessed and could not be controlled for in the analysis. Both are prevalent in MS and may independently influence cognitive performance and oculomotor behavior, representing potential confounding factors that should be addressed in subsequent studies. Future work could integrate patient questionnaires to evaluate fatigue, anxiety and depression as well as neuroimaging (e.g., functional MRI) to further clarify the neural circuits linking retinal degeneration and gaze behavior to specific cognitive outcomes.
