Linezolid-Induced Optic Neuritis in Tuberculosis: A Systematic Review of Case Reports, Case Series, and Cohort Studies
Authors: Sanjay Singhal, Ranganath Ganga, Pulkit Gupta, Aneeket Rastogi, Shilpi Arya, Shikha Agarwal, Shubham Singh, Ravindra Kumar Garg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground With the increasing use of linezolid for the treatment of drug-resistant tuberculosis, concerns have emerged regarding serious adverse effects, including optic neuritis. To systematically review the available evidence on the incidence of optic neuritis reported in the cohort, its onset, linezolid doses and duration, spectrum, management approaches, and outcomes across case reports and series studies.Methods A systematic review was conducted in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines after registration in International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD420251108339). We searched PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus. Eligible studies included case reports, case series, and cohort studies of patients with tuberculosis who developed optic neuritis while receiving linezolid. Data were extracted independently by two reviewers, and methodological quality was assessed using a validated tool for evaluating case reports and case series. Results were synthesized narratively with descriptive statistics.Results Nineteen case reports and case series, 35 cohort studies, and safety data from five randomized controlled trials were included. Most reported cases originated from India (61.6%), with a mean age of 30.6±13.0 years; 65.4% were male. Linezolid was administered at 600 mg/day in nearly 90% of cases, with a median exposure of 7 months. Visual symptoms were typically painless and bilateral. After discontinuation of linezolid, more than half of the patients achieved complete visual recovery, while 34.6% experienced residual visual impairment. Across cohort studies, the incidence of optic neuritis ranged from 0.47% to 30%, with higher rates observed in smaller studies from a single country and among patients with a low body mass index.Conclusion This infrequent but clinically significant complication requires early recognition, prompt drug withdrawal, and routine ophthalmological monitoring.Keywords: Linezolid, Tuberculosis, Optic neuritis

Introduction

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global TB Report 2024, approximately 400,000 people worldwide are estimated to have developed multidrug- resistant/rifampicin-resistant tuberculosis (MDR-/RR-TB) in 2023 [1]. India accounts for about 27% of these cases, making it the country with the largest share globally [1]. India’s high population density contributes to increasing rates of drug-resistant tuberculosis (DR-TB). WHO has now classified linezolid as a Group A drug for treating MDR-TB [2]. Its use in tuberculosis management has increased significantly, with doses ranging from 300 to 600 mg once daily for 2 to 20 months, particularly as part of all-oral longer regimens, all-oral shorter regimens, and the bedaquiline, pretomanid, linezolid, and moxifloxacin (BPaLM) regimen for treating DR-TB [3]. However, the safety of long-term linezolid use remains uncertain. Extended use has been linked to various adverse effects, including optic neuritis, which can become irreversible if not detected early [4]. Given the increasing use of linezolid and the limited consolidated data on this adverse effect in tuberculosis patients, a systematic review is necessary to compile existing evidence from case reports, case series, and cohort studies. The primary objective of this review is to determine the incidence of linezolid-induced optic neuritis (LON) reported in various cohort studies, while the secondary objective is to characterize its onset, linezolid doses and duration, spectrum, management approaches, and outcomes across case reports and series.

Materials and Methods

Our review of LON in patients receiving linezolid as part of tuberculosis treatment was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines (Supplementary Table S1). Ethical approval was not required for this study. The protocol was registered prospectively with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) (ID: CRD420251108339) [5].

1. Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they involved human subjects of any age with confirmed tuberculosis—whether diagnosed clinically, radiologically, or microbiologically—caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Participants must have received linezolid as part of their anti-tubercular treatment (ATT) regimen and reported LON as diagnosed by any criteria. We included case reports, case series, and cohort studies.

We excluded studies that did not report optic neuritis, were not temporally or causally linked to linezolid, or in which the presence of other ATTs obscured the causal relationship to linezolid. Editorials, reviews, and conference abstracts were also excluded.

2. Exposure criteria

We included patients who received linezolid as part of ATT, excluding those who received it for non-ATT purposes, such as when linezolid is used as a treatment strategy for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus infection.

3. Search strategy

We conducted a comprehensive literature search across the following databases: PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus. The search utilized Boolean operators and was customized for each database, incorporating the relevant controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH for PubMed, Emtree for EMBASE). The search strings were: "linezolid" AND ("tuberculosis" OR "tb" OR "multidrug-resistant tuberculosis" OR "extensively drug-resistant tuberculosis") AND ("optic neuritis" OR "optic neuropathy" OR "visual impairment" OR "visual loss"). We also conducted a manual search of Google Scholar to find additional articles. The last search, including manual efforts, was performed on July 20, 2025, and was limited to English. Detailed search strategies for each database are provided in the Supplementary Table S2.

4. Study selection

Data collection occurred in two distinct phases. First, relevant publications were identified and imported into Rayyan, where duplicates were removed. Two investigators (Pulkit Gupta and Aneeket Rastogi) independently screened all studies. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion, and if consensus was not reached, a third investigator (Sanjay Singhal) reviewed the data to resolve the conflict. The selected articles were then independently screened for full text by two authors (Sanjay Singhal and Shubham Singh). Any disagreements regarding final inclusion were addressed after consultation with the third author (Shilpi Arya). A PRISMA diagram was created to document each step of the selection process.

5. Data extraction

A pre-designed data collection form was utilized to gather the following information: the first author’s name, country, publication year, study design, number of patients, demographics, tuberculosis type and drug resistance, linezolid dose and duration, concomitant ATT drugs, symptoms of optic neuritis, diagnostic method (clinical or ophthalmological findings), management of optic neuritis, and outcome (vision recovery). Ophthalmological outcomes were categorized as complete, partial, or no recovery. This data extraction process was conducted independently by two reviewers (Sanjay Singhal and Shubham Singh) to ensure both completeness and accuracy. Discrepancies in data extraction were resolved through consensus. The extracted data were then synthesized for narrative and tabular presentation.

6. Risk of bias assessment

We assessed the methodological quality of the included case reports and case series using the tool developed by Murad et al. [6], which evaluates the risk of bias across four domains: selection, ascertainment, causality, and reporting. Each study was independently reviewed by two evaluators (Sanjay Singhal and Shubham Singh), with any disagreements resolved through consensus. Quality assessment results were categorized as ‘good’ if all four domains were met, ‘fair’ if three were met, and ‘poor’ if only one or two were met [7]. It is important to note that quality assessment was not conducted for cohort studies in this review.

7. Data synthesis

The extracted data were organized using Microsoft Word (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, while continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges. A narrative synthesis described the characteristics of LON and its related outcomes. To avoid inappropriate pooling across different study designs while preserving detailed clinical information, we analyzed the data from case reports/series and cohort studies separately.

Results

Through an initial systematic search, 483 articles were retrieved, with 311 remaining after duplicates were removed. Following a review of the titles and abstracts, 249 irrelevant articles were excluded, and 17 additional articles were identified through a manual Google Scholar search. This left a total of 59 articles for further full-text review, with a list of studies excluded after this assessment provided in Supplementary Table S3. Ultimately, we reviewed 19 case reports and case series involving 26 patients with LON [8-26]. Supplementary Table S4 summarizes the clinical and ophthalmological findings, management strategies, and outcomes of patients with LON in tuberculosis cases. Additionally, this review includes 35 cohort studies and safety data from five randomized controlled trials (RCTs) examining similar findings in patients with DR-TB (Supplementary Table S5) [27-66]. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). All individual cases were rated as good quality (Supplementary Table S6).

1. Case report and case series

Among 26 patients with tuberculosis who developed LON, the majority were from India (n=16; 61.6%), followed by Korea (n=5; 19.2%). Single cases were reported from Singapore, the United Kingdom, Saudi Arabia, Nepal, and Pakistan (Table 1) [8-26]. The mean±standard deviation age of the patients was 30.56±12.98 years, with most having pulmonary tuberculosis (n=11/15; 73.3%) and a predominance of males (n=17; 65.4%). Nearly 90% of the cases involved a linezolid dose of 600 mg/day, with a median treatment duration of 7 months. The diagnosis of LON was primarily based on fundus evaluation, with findings typically reversing after discontinuation of the drug in most cases (Table 2). After a median of 2 months off linezolid, significant improvement was observed, with complete recovery in more than half of the cases. However, 34.6% of patients experienced impaired vision, and one patient showed no improvement.

2. Cohort studies

Of 35 cohort studies on the use of linezolid in tuberculosis patients, the incidence of LON ranges from 0.47% to 30%. One study reported an incidence of 16.3%, along with myelosuppression (Supplementary Table S5) [27-66]. There is considerable variation in the reported incidence of linezolid-induced optic neuropathy, with multinational studies showing rates of 0.6% to 1.5%. These figures are nearly similar to safety data from RCTs (0.3% to 2%), while single-country studies indicate lower rates in samples of >100 compared to smaller samples (Table 3). Three studies reported that no patients experienced linezolid-induced optic neuropathy [27,58,59]. The diagnosis of LON is based on an ophthalmologist’s evaluation, as noted in only five studies [29,31,46,47,54]. The incidence of LON often resulted in linezolid withdrawal in most studies, with most cases resolving completely or partially afterward. Studies by Oehadian et al. [28] and Udwadia et al. [60] also indicated that patients with a low body mass index (BMI <18.5 kg/m²) are more likely to develop LON.

Discussion

Linezolid was introduced in the early 2000s for the treatment of DR-TB, following supportive evidence from in vitro studies. Today, it is one of the core anti-tubercular agents used in managing DR-TB. Long-term side effects of linezolid include hepatic and pancreatic dysfunction, hypoglycemia, serotonin syndrome, and myelosuppression. Additionally, irreversible peripheral and optic neuritis have been documented [67]. There is considerable variation in the incidence of these adverse effects. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review focusing on LON. We examined the evidence from 19 case reports and series, 35 cohort studies, and safety data from five RCTs involving tuberculosis patients. The reported incidence of optic neuritis ranged from 0.47% to 30%, likely reflecting differences in study sizes [27-61]. Multinational studies indicated an incidence of 0.6% to 1.5%, which is comparable to safety data from RCTs (0.3% to 2%). However, single-country studies with samples larger than 100 reported lower rates than those with smaller samples (8–100) [27-66]. Due to the significant heterogeneity in cohort studies regarding the incidence of LON, we did not conduct a quantitative synthesis.

All these patients were being treated for DR-TB, either MDR/extreme drug-resistant (XDR) tuberculosis. The majority of affected patients were male, often with low BMI and comorbidities such as human immunodeficiency virus and diabetes mellitus. Common risk factors for developing adverse effects include malnutrition, alcoholism, and diabetes. Studies conducted in India and China reported a higher incidence of LON compared to other regions, which has been linked to prevalent malnutrition. Furthermore, the presence of mitochondrial haplogroup U is associated with increased susceptibility to linezolid toxicity due to reduced synthesis of mitochondrial DNA-encoded polypeptides, seen in 15% of Indians and 11% of native Europeans [58,68,69]. Conversely, haplogroup H, which is more common in European and Caucasian populations, is associated with a lower incidence of linezolid toxicity [58,68,69].

Patients with LON typically present with a gradual, painless decline in visual acuity and loss of color vision. The risk of developing this condition should be considered even in the absence of ocular symptoms [11,15,21,49,54]. Common signs include blue-yellow color blindness, which may not be detected by standard color vision tests such as the Ishihara chart, and an enlarged blind spot on perimetry [11,15,21]. Fundoscopic examination may reveal bilateral disc edema, temporal pallor, or an otherwise normal fundus. Therefore, the F-M100 hue test, fundus examination, perimetry, and visual acuity tests are recommended. Diagnosing LON is challenging, especially when patients are taking other ATT that can also impair vision; however, improvement in symptoms after discontinuing linezolid supports the diagnosis.

LON usually develops within 4 to 8 weeks of therapy. Although the precise mechanism is not fully understood, it is believed that linezolid disrupts mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation in the optic nerve. Since most cellular energy is produced in mitochondria through oxidative phosphorylation, its failure can lead to the formation of reactive oxygen species. The macula, which is densely populated with photoreceptors that form a critical bundle of fibers, requires substantial energy to function, making it particularly susceptible to damage. This combination of energy deficiency and oxidative stress can trigger apoptosis and nerve damage. When the impairment occurs without axonal loss, the damage may be reversible. Most patients with optic neuritis experience improvement after stopping the medication, likely because the trigger is removed before irreversible apoptosis and permanent axonal loss occur [11,21]. Vision typically returns to normal within 2 to 4 weeks after discontinuation of therapy, and most patients can resume linezolid treatment. However, permanent withdrawal has been reported by Kuhlin et al. [42] in seven out of eight patients with LON, and permanent loss of vision after discontinuation was noted by Tac et al. [33] in two patients. Additionally, Hughes et al. [36] reported a case of death due to an unknown cause in a patient who developed LON. Khadilkar et al. [22] documented a case where there was no improvement in vision even after stopping the drug.

Given these findings, when administering linezolid as part of anti-tubercular therapy, it is crucial to exercise clinical judgment in balancing risks and benefits to prevent irreversible side effects. Raising awareness of linezolid toxicity, routinely assessing visual acuity in patients undergoing long-term therapy, and emphasizing the importance of these assessments are essential. The use of systemic steroids in cases of LON remains controversial, with some reports suggesting that they could worsen outcomes [70-71]. Therefore, whenever possible, ethambutol should be avoided in linezolid-based regimens. Reducing the linezolid dose after an initial period at the standard dose may allow for prolonged treatment while maintaining efficacy and minimizing adverse events.

Our study utilizes a significant amount of data from case reports and case series. This review does not incorporate individual participant data; instead, it relies entirely on aggregated data from published studies. Among the cohort studies, we observed considerable heterogeneity due to widely varying sample sizes—some studies included as few as eight patients [39,53,61]. Additionally, the lack of uniform diagnostic criteria, inconsistent reporting of onset timing, and the documentation of patients lost to follow-up in some studies [36] hindered our ability to conduct a quantitative synthesis. Furthermore, the absence of large prospective trials limits the relevance of these findings to current clinical practice.

In conclusion, LON has been documented in patients undergoing MDR-TB treatment that includes linezolid. While the incidence varies significantly, it is less common than previously believed. This condition typically arises 4 to 8 weeks into therapy and should be promptly suspected, as delays in diagnosis can result in irreversible vision loss. Populations in India and China are more frequently affected compared to those from other regions. It is essential to consider modifiable risk factors, such as malnutrition, before starting therapy, given that linezolid is a key drug in managing MDR-TB.
