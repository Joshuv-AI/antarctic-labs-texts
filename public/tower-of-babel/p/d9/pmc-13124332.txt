Overactive bladder among medical and nonmedical students: A comparative study of prevalence and impact on quality of life
Authors: Ayesha Ahmad, Umama Muskan, Ehsanullah Alokozay, Safa Mazhar, Ayman Noor, Umama Alam, Tauseef Ur Rehman, Musawer Khan, Iftikhar Khan, Mahera Habibi, Neha Waseem
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Overactive bladder (OAB) is a urological syndrome characterized by urinary urgency, frequency, nocturia, and, in some cases, urge incontinence in the absence of infection or other identifiable pathology. It can significantly impair health-related quality of life (HRQoL) through physical discomfort, psychological distress, and social embarrassment. This study aimed to compare the prevalence of OAB and its impact on HRQoL among medical and nonmedical university students in Pakistan. A comparative cross-sectional study was conducted between February and October 2025 among 418 university students (217 medical and 201 nonmedical) recruited through convenience sampling. Data were collected using self-administered questionnaires, including the Overactive Bladder Awareness Tool, the Perception of Academic Stress Scale, and the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire–Overactive Bladder Module. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 22. Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables, independent t tests for group comparisons, ANOVA for differences across academic years, and logistic regression to identify independent factors associated with OAB. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. OAB symptoms were reported by 23.2% of students. Smoking was significantly associated with OAB (50% in smokers vs 21.4% in nonsmokers, P = .002). In logistic regression analysis, smoking (odds ratio = 0.334, P = .029) and greater HRQoL impact (odds ratio = 1.16, P = .004) were independently associated with OAB. OAB severity scores were positively correlated with HRQoL impairment (ρ = 0.234, P < .001). Despite the burden of symptoms, only 11.7% of affected students sought medical care, while medical students demonstrated greater awareness of treatment options compared with nonmedical students (P = .046). Nearly 1 in 4 university students experienced OAB symptoms, which were associated with significant impairment in HRQoL but remained largely underrecognized and undertreated. This highlights a substantial gap between symptom burden and healthcare-seeking behavior, underscoring the need for targeted awareness, lifestyle modification, and improved access to care in university settings.

1. Introduction

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a common urological condition defined by urinary urgency, usually accompanied by increased daytime frequency and nocturia, with or without urge urinary incontinence. According to the International Continence Society, OAB is diagnosed when these symptoms occur in the absence of proven infection or other obvious underlying pathology.[1] This chronic and often distressing condition can substantially reduce quality of life (QoL). It may cause embarrassment, interfere with daily activities, affect personal relationships, and lead individuals to organize their routines around symptom control. In addition to its physical burden, increasing evidence suggests that OAB can negatively affect mental well-being, contributing to psychological distress and poorer emotional health.[2] Several medical conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipidemia, together with factors including previous surgery, smoking, sex, and overweight status (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 25), have been reported as important risk factors for OAB.[3–5]

The existing literature on OAB in young adults is limited and has yielded inconsistent findings. For example, a study from the United States reported that the prevalence of OAB with urinary incontinence among individuals aged 18 to 24 years was 2.0% in women and 0.3% in men.[6] In contrast, the prevalence of OAB among Iranian women aged 15 to 29 years was reported to be 10.9%,[7] whereas a much lower prevalence of 0.8% was observed among individuals aged 18 to 29 years in China.[8] In Pakistan, OAB appears to be relatively common among young women, with a reported prevalence of 32% in females aged 20 to 30 years.[9] Medical students, in particular, may be at increased risk because of the distinctive stressors associated with their training. These include frequent exposure to human suffering and death, a highly competitive academic environment, ethical dilemmas, the pressure to master large volumes of information within limited time, unclear academic expectations, and financial strain. Collectively, these challenges may contribute to a higher prevalence of OAB in this population.[10,11]

Despite growing literature on OAB, comparative data between medical and nonmedical university students remain limited, particularly in Pakistan. Because medical students may experience distinct academic and psychological stressors, comparing these 2 groups may help clarify whether academic environment is associated with OAB symptoms. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the prevalence of OAB and its associated factors among medical and nonmedical university students in Pakistan.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and setting

This is a comparative cross-sectional study that recruited students from multiple universities, including medical and nonmedical institutions, to ensure a diverse student population. Both undergraduate and postgraduate medical and nonmedical students from selected universities.

Were enrolled in this study. Convenient sampling was employed in this study. Data collected between February 2025 and October 2025.

2.2. Study population, sampling procedure, and sample size calculation

The study population included students from different faculties, such as Medicine, Engineering, Humanities, and Nursing. The minimum required sample size was estimated using the OpenEpi calculator with a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error. A total of 418 students were included in the final analysis, including 217 medical students and 201 nonmedical students. Convenience sampling was used

2.3.2. Exclusion criteria

Students were excluded if they had a self-reported history of neurological disorders, urinary tract infection, or other urological conditions affecting bladder function; were pregnant; had prostate disease or spinal injury; were taking medications that could alter bladder function (e.g., diuretics or anticholinergics); did not provide consent; or did not complete the survey. Exclusion conditions were assessed based on self-reported medical history in the questionnaire

2.4. Data collection instruments

2.4.1. Students’ demographics and characteristics

Age, sex, Academic program (whether medical or nonmedical students), and Level of study were included as demographic information.

2.5. Data collection procedure

Students were invited to participate through university email, student groups, and classroom announcements. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained before questionnaire completion. The questionnaire was administered either in paper form or through an online Google Form to improve accessibility. No personal identifiers were collected, and participants could not be recontacted after survey submission. Responses were reviewed for completeness and consistency before analysis.

2.6. Self-administered overactive bladder validated 8-questionnaire (OAB-V8)

The validated Arabic version of the OABV8 was used to screen and assess symptoms and severity of OAB.15. OAB-V8 is a self-reported questionnaire that assesses the burden of OAB, and the degree of discomfort caused by the symptoms. The domains are frequency, urgency, and nocturia. Urgency urinary incontinence is also included in OAB-V8. Each item has a score of 0 to 5. Males have 2 additional points, as they have a lower threshold for OAB symptoms.16 A score of 8 or more is suggestive of OAB.[12]

2.6.1. Perception of academic stress scale (PASS)

The perception of academic stress scale (PASS) was used to assess academic stress among university students. PASS comprises 3 domains: academic expectations, workload and examinations, and academic self-perceptions. It was administered as an 18-item questionnaire rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Higher PASS scores indicate greater perceived academic stress.[13]

Stress-Lifestyle Risk Score. A composite Stress-Lifestyle Risk Score was created for exploratory analysis. This score was calculated by summing coded responses for perceived academic stress and selected lifestyle-related factors, including caffeine intake, smoking status, and physical activity. Higher scores indicated greater exposure to stress and lifestyle-related risk factors potentially associated with OAB symptoms. Because this was an author-constructed composite score rather than a previously validated instrument, its findings were interpreted cautiously. Associations of this composite score with OAB symptom score and QoL impact were examined in correlation and regression analyses.

2.7. International consultation on incontinence questionnaire

International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire – OAB Module involves the frequency of urine during the day, the frequency of nocturia, the frequency of urinary urgency, and urgency urinary incontinence. The total score represents the sum of all symptoms. The intensity of these symptoms was classified as 0 to 4. The intensity of symptoms is positively correlated with the overall score.[14,15]

2.7.1. Ethics approval and consent to participate

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethical Review Board (IERB), Combined Military Hospital (CMH) Quetta (Approval No. CMH QTA-IERB/119/2025, dated September 23, 2025). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection

3. Statistical analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 22. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. All questionnaires were reviewed for completeness and consistency before analysis. Participants with incomplete key outcome data were excluded from the relevant analyses. No imputation was performed. The final analytic sample comprised 418 participants with valid responses. For regression analyses, complete case analysis was used, including only participants with complete data on all model variables (n = 373). Categorical variables were coded numerically for analysis. The chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables between groups. Independent-samples t tests were used to compare continuous variables between 2 groups, and one-way ANOVA was used for comparisons across >2 groups. Smoking status was coded as a binary variable in regression analyses (0 = smoker, 1 = nonsmoker). Due to small subgroup sizes (e.g., smokers), regression estimates can be unstable, and results are to be treated with caution. Because this was an exploratory study, no formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, and findings were interpreted cautiously with attention to effect sizes and consistency across analyses. A P-value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Sex was recorded for all participants and included in group comparisons; however, sex-stratified multivariable analyses were not performed because of sample size limitations and were acknowledged as a limitation. Given the cross-sectional design, all observed relationships were interpreted as associations rather than causal effects.

4. Results

The final analysis included 418 participants with valid responses. Regression analyses were carried out on n = 373 participants with full data. Most were between the ages of 18 and 22 (73.2%, n = 306), female (63.2%, n = 264), and undergraduate students (91.4%, n = 382). Medical students made up slightly more than half (51.9%, n = 217). More than half (55.3%, n = 231) said they did not regularly exercise, 72.3% (n = 295) said they drank a lot of coffee, 5.3% (n = 22) said they smoked, and 12.2% (n = 51) said they had a medical condition that affected their ability to urinate (Table 1).

The range of symptom bother was “not at all” (45.2%) to “extremely” (3.8%) (Table 4). Only 11.7% (n = 49) had sought medical attention for urinary symptoms in spite of these problems. 5. Discussion

This cross-sectional study of 418 university students revealed that nearly one in 4 participants reported symptoms consistent with OAB (23.2%, n = 97). The majority of respondents were young adults (73.2% aged 18–22), female (63.2%), and undergraduates (91.4%), with medical students comprising just over half of the sample (51.9%). The study highlighted important associations between symptom burden, QoL, lifestyle behaviors (notably caffeine consumption and stress-related factors), and health-seeking practices. A similar study was conducted by Shrouq Qudah with a sample size of 940, and the prevalence of OAB was 27.4% among the population[16]

This study demonstrated a clear link between OAB severity and diminished QoL. Higher OAB symptom scores correlated with greater QoL impact, and vice versa, suggesting a likely bidirectional relationship. Ramzi Shawahna et al have also reported similar findings showing higher OAB scores in female medical students with chronic diseases or those students having stressful life.[17] More severe urinary symptoms reduce well-being, while poorer QoL: through disrupted sleep, mood changes, or impaired concentration, may heighten symptom perception and reporting. This interaction reinforces the notion that OAB should be viewed not only as a condition of urinary frequency and urgency but also as one with substantial psychosocial consequences that contribute significantly to disease burden.

Nearly 3-quarters of participants (72.3%) reported regular caffeine use. Although caffeine intake was not independently associated with OAB symptom prevalence, greater consumption predicted higher QoL impact. This suggests that while caffeine may not directly increase urinary frequency, it could exacerbate the subjective burden of symptoms, possibly through sleep disruption or heightened symptom awareness. Interventions targeting caffeine moderation could therefore reduce QoL impairment, even if symptom counts remain unchanged.

The stress–lifestyle risk score did not show strong bivariate associations with OAB presence, but in multivariable analysis, it predicted higher symptom scores. This indicates that stress-related behaviors and exposures may intensify symptom severity. Targeting stress management, sleep hygiene, and healthy coping mechanisms may therefore serve as practical avenues to mitigate symptom intensity in student populations.

Smoking. Findings related to smoking were contradictory. While unadjusted prevalence rates showed smokers were more likely to report OAB symptoms (50% vs 21.4%), logistic regression suggested a protective effect (OR = 0.334, P = .029). This paradox is likely explained by the small number of smokers in the sample (n = 22), producing unstable estimates, or by confounding with other variables such as stress or QoL. Given these limitations, the smoking-related results should be interpreted cautiously and warrant replication in larger, better-powered studies. This apparent contradiction likely arises from the very small number of smokers (n = 22), which may have caused unstable estimates in the regression model.

Despite notable symptom burden (mean OAB symptom score 8.43 ± 2.79) and QoL impairment (mean QoL score 5.18 ± 2.31), only 11.7% of students had sought medical help. In a similar study Karan Sharma et al has reported that out of total pariticpants suffering from UTI’s only 7% of women has consulted doctor while remaing 93% had never visisted doctor for various myths like this disease would resolve on its own, shyness to disclose their problem and financial issues.[18] Awareness of treatment options was higher among medical students than nonmedical peers, yet a majority across both groups remained unaware. This low help-seeking may stem from stigma, normalization of symptoms, low perceived need, or time/access constraints in student populations. The gap between symptom burden and health-seeking highlights an important opportunity for awareness campaigns, destigmatization efforts, and improved access pathways, particularly in university health systems.

Both logistic and linear regression models explained only a modest proportion of outcome variance (R2 = 0.07). This suggests that while caffeine, stress, and QoL are relevant, many important predictors were not captured. Likely contributors include BMI, gynecological and reproductive factors, hormonal status, medication use, prior urinary tract infections, and pelvic floor dysfunction. Incorporating these variables into future analyses could provide more robust explanatory models. Eun Sang Yoo et al in their study have concluded that out of 458, 22.7% of participants with OAB have anxiety and 39.3% has depression, indicating a strong association between OAB and stressful life.[19]
