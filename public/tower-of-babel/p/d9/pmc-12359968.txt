Academic performance and anxiety in the evaluation of health science undergraduate students using osce: a pre- and post-covid-19 cohort study
Authors: Begoña Polonio-López, José Luis Martín-Conty, Juan J Bernal-Jiménez, Juan José Criado-Álvarez, Ancor Sanz-García, Francisco Martín-Rodríguez, Laura Mordillo-Mateos, Ana Isabel Corregidor-Sánchez, Alfonso Martínez-Cano, Carmen Romo-Barrientos, Marta Rodríguez-Hernández
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe education of health science students requires the use of assessment tools that provide valid and reliable information about the learning of practical skills. The objective and structured clinical evaluation (OSCE) method has been shown to be useful, but it can cause stress and anxiety, which may increase after COVID-19 affects final academic performance. To test whether the OSCE induces an anxiety response in participants, to analyse the cause of this emotional response and to examine the relationship between OSCE scores and students’ final performance.MethodsDescriptive-analytical study comparing two cohorts of pre- and post-COVID-19 health sciences students. The study was conducted at the Faculty of Health Sciences, University of Castilla-La Mancha, Talavera de la Reina (Spain). A total of 121 students were recruited and enrolled in two groups: 56 in the pre-COVID-19 group and 65 in the post-COVID-19 group. Both groups were analysed at the end of the 2nd year of the occupational therapy program. Demographic data and academic performance data were collected using self-developed questionnaires. A three-station OSCE examination was used to assess practical skills. Anxiety was assessed using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory questionnaire. The results were evaluated with a reliability level of 95%, and p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.ResultsIn our study, we found that the prepandemic group had lower baseline anxiety than did the postpandemic group, which was also found after the OSCE. The results of the OSCE were good and not related to the stress or anxiety that might be caused by the exam in either group. We found a decline in academic performance after the pandemic, although with better access scores.ConclusionsThere was an overall decline in academic performance after the pandemic that was not due to stress or anxiety caused by the OSCE.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07780-w.Keywords: OSCE, Anxiety, Academic performance, Occupational therapist, Health sciences undergraduate students, COVID-19.

Introduction

The current training of health professionals occurs in a changing environment, in which it is necessary for educators to reconsider the traditional models of training and evaluating clinical skills to respond to rapid changes in the health services marketplace. These changes have accelerated and aggravated by the global pandemic caused by COVID-19, which we have experienced from 2019 to 2021. As a consequence, health professionals require expert mastery of a series of skills and competencies that will help them make judgments and complex clinical decisions urgently and in different professional settings. This change has also occurred in a short space of time, which makes it essential to reflect on how we ensure that students in health professions acquire this knowledge and skills in current educational models and the consequences that this entails.

For the learning of the competencies associated with professional performance to be meaningful, it is essential, among other measures, to create a transformative educational environment for health professionals, striving to determine the best practices in clinical education, which implies consequent innovative pedagogical strategies [1, 2]. These proposals include the creation of competency-based curricula as the best way to meet the needs of future patients and their families, the incorporation of technologies in such curricula and the importance of teamwork [3–5], from the understanding of the need to develop a strong professional identity, but based, in turn, on the firm belief that our contributions to the interdisciplinary team will create synergies with other members that will improve patient care.

To respond to these challenges, it is necessary to incorporate assessment methods of these practical competencies, which, although they are more often used in medicine, are not so well tried in other disciplines. One of these methods is objective and structured clinical evaluation (OSCE) [6], in which objective assessment instruments based on practice simulations are used to measure the real clinical competence of professionals, that is, their ability to use knowledge, skills, attitudes and good judgment to adequately resolve the situations that exercise in their profession supposes [7]. The OSCE is a summative assessment that is being strongly incorporated into the curricula of health professionals as a method of evaluating clinical competency [5, 7].

The OSCE has relatively high reliability, validity, and objectivity [8–13] as a method of practical evaluation for students, but it can have an important limitation: poor performance in the test due to the feelings of stress and anxiety that it can cause [14], attributing to a lack of clinical competence what is in fact the consequence of an emotional factor, anxiety [15].

However, the anxiety referred to in this paper should be understood as the acute emotion of anxiety that may occur in an examination situation and not anxiety as a mental disorder or illness. It would be state anxiety, a transient and fluctuating emotional response characterized by a composite feeling of tension, apprehension, and autonomic activation, which occurs when being tested in performance-based situations. As opposed to trait anxiety, which is a relatively stable personality attribute, characterized by an individual tendency to experience state anxiety in response to or in anticipation of threatening stimuli.

In the context of the OSCE, state anxiety is particularly relevant due to the evaluative and potentially stressful nature of this test. Students may experience an increase in their level of state anxiety just before and during the OSCE, which may manifest itself through various physical and psychological symptoms, but its duration is always temporary, disappearing when the stressful situation does; however, trait anxiety tends to remain stable over time, reflecting a general tendency to perceive various situations as potentially dangerous [16].

It is estimated that approximately one in five students suffer from test anxiety. The incidence of test anxiety in higher education students has been correlated with perceived difficulty, which may also be driven by level of preparation or low proficiency and female gender [15].

On the other hand, the satisfactory demonstration of this clinical competence is usually a condition for academic progress since it is the result of the integration and application of the knowledge and skills learned throughout the training and requires the implementation of a series of internalized attitudes and values shared by professionals. In this sense, the summative evaluation of the OSCE should be related to overall academic performance, obtaining good results in knowledge tests if they have been obtained in competency tests.

However, this result can be altered by various factors. One of them is, as other authors have previously pointed out, the stress and anxiety produced by performance tests [17] since examination situations that require results can cause fear of a bad evaluation, which induces negative physiological, emotional or behavioral responses. This anxiety response is especially notorious before demonstration tests, such as the OSCE [18–21], and may negatively influence academic performance; elevated levels of state anxiety can affect concentration, memory and practical skills, decreasing academic performance [16, 22].

Test anxiety can be more pronounced during OSCE than during more traditional assessments, as a stressful environment is created involving real-time interaction and assessment with a standardized patient. This can produce a combination of test anxiety and performance anxiety, leading to a heightened state of arousal, which may prevent the student from being able to demonstrate their true knowledge and abilities.

Therefore, checking whether the use of the OSCE to evaluate the practical competencies of students in health disciplines has a positive or negative influence on final academic performance, as well as understanding the nature of state anxiety and its potential influence on performance during practical examinations, could help us to better manage educational resources and develop effective stress management strategies and improve students’ academic performance in these types of assessments.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

To answer these research questions, we designed an observational, descriptive-analytical, cross-sectional, and prospective study in which two cohorts of undergraduate students of the Occupational Therapy Degree were compared. Each cohort consisted of all the students who had completed their degree, the duration of which was 4 years. The first cohort started their studies in 2017 and finished in 2021, and the second cohort started in 2020 and finished in 2024.

Given the characteristics of the study, a nonprobabilistic convenience sample was used, consisting of all students enrolled in the subject of physical dysfunctions taught in the 2nd year. The first cohort carried out the tests prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, while the second did so after it once a certain normality in academic performance had recovered. The “convenience” term was used to refer that the most suitable academic year was selected for the work’s aim.

Both cohorts have been studied cross-sectionally during their second year of training. The first cohort was during the 2018-19 academic year, before the pandemic, and the second cohort was during the 2021-22 academic year, after the pandemic. The academic year of 2021-22 was the first year in which students returned to the classroom without social distancing measures and during which all preventive measures were phased out.

Both cohorts received information and training prior to the OSCE. In the prepandemic group, the training consisted of carrying out different activities for a total of 15 h. A first session was held to present the characteristics of the OSCE, in which the entire group participated. Subsequently, a first training phase was carried out in small groups (4 participants) in which audiovisual materials with clinical cases were presented and they were asked to describe the pathology, identify clinical manifestations and the phase of the disease, complemented with autonomous work to review the theoretical content and read scientific papers. The second training phase then began, consisting of interacting with real clinical cases, working with them in groups in the classroom, using the clinical skills learnt in the course and receiving feedback from the teaching staff on individual performance. Finally, an individual simulation of the OSCE test that was to be carried out later as a practical exam test was carried out. All this was recorded in a student workbook. In the postpandemic cohort, the training lasted 10 h. The presentation and training phases with audiovisual materials were maintained, but the classroom work with real clinical cases was eliminated. Likewise, the simulation phase was modified, consisting of the visualization of different stations of a previously performed OSCE.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) being enrolled in occupational therapy for physical dysfunction and (2) not having previously participated in an OSCE. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) failure to take the OSCE or any of the examination papers; (2) having a diagnosis of active mental disorder; and (3) using drugs that influence mood or anxiety.

The groups were compared in terms of their previous demographic and academic characteristics to verify that they were homogeneous.

To determine the required sample size, we use the following considerations: (1) a statistical power (from 1 to 100) of 80, (2) a significance level of p = 0.05, and (3) an expected effect size of 3.00 between the prepandemic and postpandemic periods. On the basis of these criteria, the sample needed for the present study was n = 75. Additionally, a power analysis was performed with following considerations: (1) Sample size of 112 (which it is the result of multiplying by 2 the group with less students), (2) a significance level of p = 0.05, (3) a State-Trait Anxiety Inventory mean of 30 and 26 for each group and a standard deviation of 10 (based on our data). On the basis of these criteria, the power of this is 99.

Data collection procedures

Measures of theoretical and practical academic performance were taken (tests of knowledge, OSCE and complementary activities), as well as tests of emotional response to the examination situation for all participants. In both groups, the data collection procedure was identical.

The OSCE consisted of a practical test with standardized patients in which the students had to demonstrate their ability to evaluate, clinically intervene in and document the process. There is wide variability in the literature in terms of the number of stations and their duration [23]; we have used only 3 stations, as it is a subject OSCE, because the number of competences to be assessed at each station is lower than if it were a course or degree OSCE. Moreover, in this case, the competences assessed are limited to the field of physical dysfunctions, which is the main objective of the second year of the degree course. On the other hand, the duration of each station is longer than that of the OSCEs, in which there are a greater number of stations, combining different processes at the same station.

Three stations were arranged, each lasting 10 min and 3 min, to read the instructions for each case or situation to be solved. Two wheels were made in parallel in which all the students entered the stations corresponding to each one of them at the same time. The entry into the stations, as well as the changes of station and the time available, were communicated to the students via an automated time control procedure, which involved warnings at different times by means of the public address system. To carry out the test, six consultations, two classrooms and corridors and common spaces were set up in the Faculty of Health Sciences of the University of Castilla-La Mancha (Spain). Within each station, the students had the material they had to use and were able to choose between different tools, devices, and materials according to the type of problem they had to solve. In the supplementary material we can see an example of a detailed description of the cases to be solved in three different stations.

Once the test started, it was carried out uninterruptedly, with a break (given the size of the group), during which the wheel stopped and started again after 30 min. Students who had not started the test were isolated in a classroom to avoid contact with those who had taken it. Students were not allowed to carry any electronic devices (computers, tablets, mobile phones, smart watches, or other equipment), with the exception of pens, clinical uniforms and identification. Before entering the first station, they were provided with an evaluation folder that they had to use throughout the OSCE and handed it over to the teachers who supervised the entire process. The groups were previously randomized by the person in charge of the subject.

The following clinical competencies were evaluated during the test: (1) anamnesis, (2) physical examination, (3) technical skills and procedures, (4) communication skills, (5) clinical judgment, occupational and therapeutic diagnostic management, (6) documentation of the process and (7) professionalism. The evaluation of each item was carried out according to previously standardized and objective criteria (rubric). The assessment is triangulated by means of written material to be handed in by the student after completing the OSCE, observation during the process by the subject teaching staff and assessment rubrics used by the teaching staff for the final score, as well as including that of the standardized patient. In the supplementary material we can see an example of the registration/answer sheets available to students at different stations, as well as the rubrics used in the correction of the OSCE.

The OSCE was carried out immediately after the completion of the clinical stays of the students in the field of physical dysfunctions, which took place for two months full-time (8 weeks) after the completion of the theoretical content of the subject. In addition, the students had previously received practical training on the content and structure of the OSCE, clinical cases and audiovisual documentation and had been able to carry out simulations of this topic.

To assess whether the practical test situation elicited stress and anxiety responses in the students, the STAI questionnaire was used [24]. It was applied while waiting for the tests to be carried out, as well as once the tests were completed, both in the prepandemic and postpandemic periods.

After completing the OSCE, the students had to complete a knowledge examination that consisted of questions with multiple answers about the theoretical content of the subject developed throughout the course.

Likewise, some students had received additional scores, complementary to the qualification, if they had carried out some voluntary practical activities proposed during the course by the teachers of the subject and had attended the practical classes.

Measuring tools

Different measuring instruments were used throughout the course to obtain the data. In addition, a short demographic questionnaire was designed in which data such as age, gender, income profile and grade for access to studies were collected.

To obtain the competency assessment data, various skills assessment records were specifically designed and completed by the observers, standardized patients (who had previously been trained) or the teachers responsible for the assessment. Supplementary data includes the questionnaire used by the standardized patient to assess the student at one of the stations. In addition, to assess the results of the evaluation material written by the students (for example, documentation of the process, proposal of therapeutic objectives or of different therapeutic activities), an evaluation rubric was agreed upon by the teaching staff of the subject. All assessment materials are self-developed in order to adapt them to the specific characteristics of each station being assessed. In the supplementary material we can see examples of different materials used in OSCE evaluation.

For the knowledge exams, a scale ranging from 0 to 10 points was used. Two partial tests were carried out throughout the course, and a final test, in which one of the previous parts could be recovered if it had not passed. The grade that was taken as a reference was the highest obtained by the student in each of the tests, taking the average between them to obtain the final theoretical grade.

Finally, to facilitate the attainment of a passing grade in practical tests, complementary evaluation activities were performed on a voluntary basis by the students. These activities were scored between 0 and 1 point, which was added to the final practical score for the subject. In the same way, to encourage attendance at the compulsory practical classes in which the competencies are rehearsed, students who did not attend were penalized with − 0.5 points in the final practical score for each absence from the second foul (the first did not penalize).

To evaluate the emotional response to the OSCE, the self-administered Spanish version of the STAI questionnaire was used [25–27]. The test was applied to both groups, prepandemic and postpandemic, during the waiting period before the tests were carried out and after the tests were completed.

The STAI is a self-administered test used to evaluate levels of anxiety. A total of 40 questions are used to measure two different, but interrelated, types of anxiety. Half of the questions studied so-called “state anxiety” (STAI-SA), which is based on subjective and transitory feelings of tension, apprehension and fear that can change and be modified in intensity with time. The remaining 50% of the questions evaluate so-called “trait anxiety” (STAI-TA), an emotional state that shows a personal disposition to perceive daily activities as stressful. Since “trait anxiety” (TA) is a stable personal characteristic that reflects feelings that are usual and basal [28], it was only measured at the beginning of the session. “State anxiety” (SA) represents an individual’s response to stress. Both questions had the following options: “nothing,” “a bit,” “quite a lot,” and “a lot” (scoring 0, 1, 2, and 3 points, respectively). Since each questionnaire (STAI-SA or STAI-TA) contained 20 questions, the scores obtained ranged between 0 and 60 points per questionnaire. The use of the STAI has been validated in Spanish undergraduate students, and the Cronbach’s α was 0.93 for the STAI-TA and 0.92 for the STAI-SA [29].

Human ethics and consent to participate

The study was approved by the clinical research ethics committee for Clinical Research of Talavera de la Reina (Spain) (File 23/2017). The study was not registered as a Clinical Trial; therefore, it has no Clinical Trial Number. Details of the study design, statistical analysis plan and raw data are available online. All subjects signed written informed consent, in line with the Declaration of Helsinki. No one received compensation or was offered any incentive to participate in this study. Before the practice, the subjects were informed about the general aims of the study, and their anonymity was guaranteed and filled in the consent forms. The students were advised that they could withdraw from the study at any time and that there would be no academic impact because of this withdrawal.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Science 29.0 (SPSS, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and using our own codes and base functions in R (http://www.R-project.org). For descriptive and inferential statistical analysis, different tests were employed according to the scale of the variable. Distribution normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov‒Smirnov test. In instances of normally distributed data, independent samples t tests for quantitative variables were used, and chi-squared tests were conducted for categorical variables to reveal groupwise differences, when appropriate. To assess the relationships between possible confounding factors and the pre- and post-COVID-19 cohorts, logistic regression was performed. Effect sizes were reported using Cohen’s d of Phi coefficient, when appropriate.

The results were evaluated with a reliability level of 95%, and p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

To ensure homogeneity across the cohorts and facilitate a controlled comparison, a contrast of “trait anxiety” was conducted across the two cohorts. As both cohorts were equivalent in terms of “trait anxiety”, as assessed by the STAI, it was possible to conclude that any effects observed in subsequent analyses were not the result of a groupwise difference occurring between the cohorts.

Results

Samples and characteristics of students

A total of 178 students were enrolled in both courses: 71 in the 2017–21 cohort and 107 in the 2020–24 cohort. Fifty-seven participants were excluded (15 in the 2017-21 cohort and 42 in the 2020-24 cohort) because they did not undergo OSCE or any of the tests included in the subject exam (Fig. 1). None of the subjects were excluded because they met any other exclusion criteria.

A total of 121 students participated, 56 (46.3%) in the prepandemic stage and 65 (53.7%) in the postpandemic stage. The mean age of the participants was 22 ± 2.6 years (21.4 ± 2.7 prepandemic and 22.5 ± 2.4 postpandemic), with a predominance of females, with 109 women (91.1%; 51 prepandemic and 89.2%; 58 postpandemic). Regarding the access route, the majority came from university entrance exams (59.5%, 62.5% prepandemic and 56.9% postpandemic), with an average entry score of 6.91 ± 0.87 points (6.76 ± 0.82 prepandemic and 7.04 ± 0.89 postpandemic). No significant differences (p > 0.05) between the cohorts at baseline, except for age, which was one year older in the postpandemic group (p = 0.032). Academic performance was evaluated through two types of assessment, theoretical and practical. The mean theoretical score was significantly different (p < 0.001). In the prepandemic group (n = 56), the theoretical examination score was an average of 5.99 ± 1.15 points, and in the postpandemic group (n = 65), it was 4.66 ± 1.43 points.

The practical scores measured with the OSCE were 7.23 ± 1.02 and 6.84 ± 1.19 points for pre and postpandemic group (p = 0.062); 85.7% of the prepandemic students did not have any lack of attendance (48 subjects), being 89.2% (58 people) for the postpandemic. A total of 88.1% prepandemic subjects carried out some type of complementary activity to raise the grade, which was not the case for the postpandemic. The average final practical score was significantly different (p = 0.003), at 7.51 ± 1.28 points and 6.79 ± 1.23 points before and after the pandemic, respectively. Similarly, the final grade for the course was significantly different at 6.45 ± 1.15 points and 5.32 ± 1.17 points before and after the pandemic, respectively (p < 0.001).

The competencies related to the ability to obtain clinically relevant information from different sources, select evaluation measures and apply diagnostic management skills were evaluated at station 1, and an average score of 7.80 ± 0.99 points was obtained for the prepandemic cohort; the postpandemic cohort was obtained 6.79 ± 1.56 points (p < 0.001). The communication skills required to carry out a complete anamnesis, including the physical examination, the application of evaluation techniques and the professionalism in the interaction with the patient, were evaluated at station 2. The prepandemic average score was 6.90 ± 1.50 points, and the postpandemic average score was 6.53 ± 1.27 points. At station 3, the ability to deploy specific technical skills and procedures, the application of clinical judgment, therapeutic management and documentation of the therapeutic process were evaluated. The scores obtained were 6.99 ± 1.82 in the prepandemic cohort and 7.20 ± 1.84 in the postpandemic cohort.

Emotional response to the OSCE

Table 2 shows the STAI scores of the two study groups before and after the pandemic. There were no statistically significant differences in the STAI-TA or STAI-SA according to gender, although the values that indicated a higher level of anxiety were always greater in women in both cohorts.

The mean values of trait anxiety (STAI-TA) indicate a disposition toward anxiety as a behavioral characteristic above the mean in both groups, with scores of 25.64 ± 8.79 and 29.45 ± 8.91, respectively. The values of state anxiety (STAI-SA) in the prepandemic group decreased after the test (4.01 points), as did those in the postpandemic group (1.92 points), although to a lesser extent in this group.

In the t-test to compare between the two cohorts, prepandemic and postpandemic, we found that the values obtained in the STAI varied according to the moment in which the test was performed. No statistically significant differences were found for the pre-STAI-SA or post-STAI-SA between the cohorts; however, there were significant differences (p = 0.023) between the prepandemic cohort and the postpandemic cohort for the pre-STAI-TA (with a difference of 3.81 points).

When analyzing the total results of the STAI without taking into account the cohort to which they belonged, higher values were obtained (62.69 ± 16.25 points) before carrying out the OSCE, which decreased slightly after completion (59.68 ± 16.10 points) but this difference was not significant. However, when we compared the total scores in the pre-STAI-TA + pre-STAI-SA and postpandemic stages (pre-STAI-TA + post-STAI-SA), we found that in the post-stage from the OSCE, there was a statistically significant difference (p = 0.015) between the groups (7.49 points).

Discussion

In this work, we analyzed the emotional response and academic performance of two groups of undergraduate students in health sciences who were taking practical exams through the OSCE, to which we invited the students to demonstrate their clinical competencies before facing real situations. Given the global health situation that we have recently experienced, we also wanted to verify whether the COVID-19 pandemic has generated more stress or anxiety during practical tests. Finally, the relationships between the results obtained through skills and knowledge tests and final academic performance before and after the pandemic were studied.

That the OSCE subjects students to a stressful situation that can cause anxiety is a widely known issue [30–32]. However, in our study, we found differences in its characteristics before and after the pandemic. Although in both groups, the predisposition to perceive situations as threatening (trait anxiety, TA) and to elevate consequently, the state of anxiety generated by the examination situation (state anxiety, SA) were moderate, the prepandemic group had lower baseline anxiety than did the postpandemic group; this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.05). However, logistic regression analysis did not reveal a significant association between levels of trait anxiety and the “pandemic effect”.

This may be due to different factors. On the one hand, the high values of trait anxiety could have reflected more significant aspects of a depressive state than of anxiety, as has been described in other studies [14]; this state became more accentuated after the pandemic, without becoming serious in either group, like suggest the logistic regression analysis. The differences in the levels of TA between the two cohorts were weak, which we interpret as indicating that the pandemic had a small influence on the overall psychological state of the students. This finding is reinforced by the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI) scores at very specific points in time—before and immediately after the OSCE—which accurately capture the variations in anxiety induced by the examination situation. The reduction in SA scores after the exam indicates that the anxiety measured is directly related to the exam experience and not to a generalized and persistent psychological state driven by external factors such as the global impact of the pandemic [24–27].

However, although the regression analysis did not find a direct correlation between anxiety and OSCE results, the difference in trait anxiety (TA)—with a difference of 3.81 points (p = 0.023)—suggests that the emotional impact stemming from the pandemic may have indirectly interfered with students’ concentration, memory, and knowledge processing. Elevated chronic anxiety can contribute to a decline in motivation and commitment to learning, which are critical factors in high-demand academic contexts.

Among the factors that may explain this phenomenon are disruptions in online learning and the lack of hands-on training, along with other contextual elements. Although the study does not directly analyse the online teaching process, it can be inferred that the adaptations made in the OSCE training program (a reduction in preparation hours from 15 − 10 and the elimination of in-person activities with real clinical cases) indicate a shift toward virtual or hybrid modalities. This modification, likely stemming from the restrictions imposed during the pandemic, may have affected the quality and depth of learning. The decrease in direct interaction between instructors and students—a hallmark of the virtual environment—would have limited immediate feedback and clarify doubts, both of which are essential for mastering clinical competencies.

On the other hand, the lack of prior knowledge about the characteristics of the test or a deficient organization of the OSCE can be stressors, so adequate preparation of the tests, as well as prior training or even simulation, can help reduce these high baseline values of anxiety and cope with the OSCE in better conditions [32]. In our study, training was more exhaustive and prolonged in the prepandemic group than in the postpandemic group, which could explain this result.

The study revealed significant differences in practical skill performance. For example, at OSCE station 1, students in the prepandemic cohort achieved a score of 7.80 ± 0.99, whereas those in the postpandemic cohort achieved a score of 6.79 ± 1.56 (p < 0.001). This gap is directly associated with the reduction in in-person practical training and real-case simulations. The removal of small-group work and interactions with real clinical cases in postpandemic training may limit the ability to be exposed to complex clinical scenarios, adversely affecting the ability to apply theoretical knowledge in practice and reducing confidence during evaluations.

This decline may also be related to other aspects of online learning used during the pandemic, such as the lack of face-to-face interaction and decreased opportunities for hands-on learning, which has negatively affected the acquisition of clinical skills [33]. Difficulty in accessing appropriate technological resources, connectivity problems and a less structured learning environment contribute to increased anxiety and decreased academic performance postpandemic. Several studies have investigated the impact of the pandemic on the academic performance and anxiety of health science students, which reinforcing these outcomes. Among the most frequent findings was a decrease in academic performance due to lack of face-to-face learning, leading to feelings of incompetence [34, 35]. Gilavand et al. (2023) [36] found that students who relied exclusively on online learning reported higher levels of stress and lower satisfaction with their education, which negatively influences academic outcomes. Regarding stress and anxiety, students experienced a significant increase in levels during the pandemic, which correlated with a decline in academic performance. This high level of stress and anxiety was exacerbated by factors such as social isolation, uncertainty, and concern about not meeting graduation standards. Lack of academic support also negatively affected performance, which in turn increased anxiety and depressive symptoms [37–42].

Other possible causes of academic decline can be the relaxation of control mechanisms and reduced commitment, changes in assessment design and format and socioeconomic and personal factors. Despite the postpandemic cohort having higher admission scores, there was a noted decrease in participation in voluntary complementary activities that, in the prepandemic group, helped increase final grades. This might indicate lower engagement or adaptation to changes in the teaching format, thereby affecting overall performance.

The reduction in the duration and number of OSCE stations, along with the incorporation of audiovisual materials instead of full simulations, may have generated uncertainty regarding the evaluation process. This uncertainty could negatively impact students’ confidence and, consequently, their performance in both theoretical and practical examinations.

In addition to changes in educational practices and online learning, students have faced a number of external stressors. The pandemic has brought about changes in students’ personal and socioeconomic environments (e.g., increased family responsibilities, economic hardships, or altered study dynamics). The emotional and psychological stress associated with confinement, uncertainty about the future, social isolation, and concerns about personal and family health have exacerbated anxiety levels. These external stressors have a significant impact on students’ mental well-being, which in turn affects their performance [43]. Although not directly measured in this study, there are potential factors that may have contributed to the decline in academic performance.

In our study, the fact that the prepandemic group started from a lower initial level of anxiety, which, however, decreased more after the completion of the OSCE (4.11 points compared to 1. 92 points in the postpandemic group), leads us to believe that the cause of the anxiety generated by the test is more likely to be due to the training received than to the exam outcomes or other factors, such as those mentioned above, which were similar in both groups; in the prepandemic stage, in addition to the use of real clinical cases, simulations identical to the OSCE examination were used, an aspect that we subsequently modified in the postpandemic group.

Regarding academic performance, a proportion of students reported stable or persistent negative affect. However, in our study, neither stress nor anxiety were related to the OSCE results. In all the stations, as well as in the final result, the scores were good, with averages close to 6.5 points, being, in general, better in the prepandemic cohort. This result coincides with that of other authors [15, 44, 45], who found no association between anxiety and GPA. A recent study [30] confirmed these data, finding that the mood of the students influences their confidence in their abilities but not in the academic results of the OSCE.

This can be interpreted as the low probability that the negative effects of stress and anxiety have a decisive influence on academic performance. In our work, prepandemic students obtained a very good score at the first OSCE station, which reaffirms that a certain level of anxiety increases performance. Although to a lesser extent, this result also occurred in the postpandemic group, who presented greater initial anxiety and lower OSCE performance, although equally, with good results.

The final practical qualification was made up of other complementary activities that were added to the OSCE. The results were also better in the prepandemic cohort, with a significant difference of almost 0.75 points in favour of the prepandemic group. This qualification was influenced by the fact that students were offered voluntary complementary activities that allowed them to increase their final practical qualifications. Students gained 0.4 points on average, while no postpandemic students performed these activities. The difference was not due to absenteeism either since students in both groups attended class regularly.

The theoretical scores were lower than the practical scores in both cohorts, with a difference of 1.33 points in favor of the prepandemic group. The final average score of the postpandemic group is below the approved score. This result coincides with the downward trend that also occurs in practical qualifications in the postpandemic cohort, but in this case, it highlights the important difference that exists between one and the other. This result cannot be correlated with anxiety or stress before the test since it is carried out after the OSCE and has been repeated at different times throughout the academic year, with previous training and simulations. Although in the second test, the result is favorable for the postpandemic cohort, we can attribute it to a simpler test proposed by the examiner, in view of the poor results of the first test; the final results have low scores that affect the final overall rating. This difference between the results of the theoretical and practical tests in each group compared to those of the other groups (1.52 points in the prepandemic group and 2.13 points in the postpandemic group) led us to think that students have more difficulty establishing theoretical knowledge than demonstrating their abilities. practices, although both are intrinsically related and are necessary for professional practice. In the case of theoretical knowledge, students must use a series of cognitive skills and strategies different from those used for learning, such as comprehension, memory, and the ability to synthesize, to which they lend themselves less attention to current teaching-learning models. This difference between the acquisition of theoretical and practical knowledge presents a serious problem for health sciences students that must be corrected, and this problem has worsened with the pandemic, although the work of some researchers reflects better scores during the pandemic previously [46], or even the same results between face-to-face and virtual assessments of knowledge and practical skills [47, 48].

Overall academic performance shows a clear tendency to worsen after the pandemic, with lower values in almost all evaluations, both theoretical and practical. These results cannot be related to the emotional response produced by the OSCE since we have verified that they are more strongly associated with the characteristics of the test than with performance. Nor are there differences in the previous profiles of the students since both cohorts are comparable in terms of their personal characteristics and study profiles. In addition, this occurs although university access scores are better in the postpandemic group. Therefore, perhaps during the pandemic, some control mechanisms, as well as the level of demand in the different evaluation tests, were relaxed both theoretically and practically, with students having a somewhat lower level of training and a lower commitment to carrying out activities that are not compulsory.

Stress and anxiety, which influence mood, are important factors to consider in the academic performance of health sciences students. Although the OSCE can be intimidating and often stressful for students, this approach offers an excellent opportunity to assess the clinical competence of undergraduate students [49, 50]. For this reason, it is important to take care of their well-being by reducing anxiety related to the OSCE, fostering their confidence in their abilities, providing adequate training for the evaluation tests they must take and planning their development well, which are essential elements that contribute to obtaining better global academic results [51]. However, despite being stressful, we agree with other authors [52] that the OSCE can provide meaningful learning experiences and is a valid and reliable test that can be used as a predictor of academic performance in health sciences training programs [53].

Finally, we consider the OSCE to be a key tool in assessing the clinical competence of health professionals, but the COVID-19 pandemic has led to a number of significant changes in its implementation and evaluation. One of these changes appears to be a reduction in the number of stations and their duration, together with the use of audiovisual material to assess skills without face-to-face encounters [54, 55]. In our study, the number of stations was reduced, and the use of audiovisual material was common, both in training and in the OSCE examination. Another possible difference in the postpandemic context is the implementation of a virtual OSCE by videoconferencing platforms [56, 57]; however, this option is not fully accepted, and a hybrid OSCE combining online and face-to-face components is considered more useful and is a viable option for the safe and effective assessment of trainees’ clinical competencies. This approach has emerged as a potential solution for high-stakes assessment in the post-COVID-19 context [58, 59]. In our work, we did not consider the use of the virtual OSCE in the postpandemic group, given the lower reliability of this type of tool for assessing the necessary practical skills and the students’ opinions of it, but we did consider the design of hybrid scenarios in some of the stations that make up the test useful.

The OSCE remains a test valued for its standardization and ability to provide robust results [60], but the above adaptations appear to be effective and could influence future practice by integrating virtual technologies and hybrid formats to improve the assessment of clinical competencies in the postpandemic world.

Finally, on the basis of the findings of this study, the following practical recommendations can help educators and institutions better support students in high-stakes assessments such as the OSCE, especially in postpandemic contexts:

Enhanced OSCE-specific preparation: This study shows that more extensive preparation and prior simulation sessions (as observed in the prepandemic cohort) contribute to reducing OSCE-induced anxiety. Therefore, training programs that include realistic simulation sessions and intensive practical exercises are recommended, enabling students to become familiar with the OSCE format and dynamics, thereby reducing uncertainty and improving practical performance.

This may include teaching relaxation strategies, meditation techniques, and approaches based on cognitive‒behavioral therapy aimed at helping students regulate their emotional responses in high-pressure situations.

It is advisable to assess the feasibility of implementing hybrid or virtual models that, without compromising the validity and reliability of the assessment, allow students to gradually adapt to different evaluative formats. In addition to these mental health support services for students, it is important that educational institutions promote a range of services that facilitate learning, reduce anxiety and help improve academic performance, such as: flexible learning options, offering hybrid learning models that combine face-to-face and online teaching to reduce the pressure on students; peer support programmes, establishing peer mentoring and support groups can create a sense of community and provide a platform for students to share their experiences and coping strategies; clear communication, keeping students informed of any changes to the curriculum, examinations and safety protocols; personalised learning plans, to meet students’ individual needs and help them achieve their academic goals; and professional development for educators, training them in the latest teaching methods and technologies to improve the overall quality of education and support student performance.

In summary, these recommendations—enhancing preparation through intensive simulations, implementing anxiety management strategies, adapting evaluation modalities, and support services for the university community—provide a practical framework to mitigate stress in high-stakes assessment contexts. We believe that such measures can help optimize both the academic performance and emotional well-being of students in a postpandemic environment.

Post-pandemic university teaching in the health sciences presents many challenges and opportunities for both students and teachers. To achieve a high level of quality in a virtual classroom environment, it is necessary to improve methods that facilitate active student participation, enable hands-on learning and enhance face-to-face interaction. A balanced combination of face-to-face and non-face teaching could serve to develop the skills necessary for professional performance in the health sciences. To this end, teachers need to be adequately trained in the use of educational technologies, curricula need to be redesigned, teaching and learning methods need to be reviewed and assessment methods need to be redefined in order to improve the quality of teaching.

In this new context of post-pandemic education, it is important to consider the experiences and lessons learned during the pandemic in order to redefine the future of higher education. The integration of educational technologies and the adoption of hybrid teaching methods, combining face-to-face and online teaching, could become a permanent trend. This could improve the accessibility and flexibility of higher education and benefit more students.

In addition, the pandemic has highlighted the importance of continuous training and professional development for teachers, who need to be prepared to adapt to rapid changes in the educational environment. Investment in technological infrastructure and support programmes for students and teachers will also be critical to ensuring quality education in the future.

This study has several limitations. First, the generalizability of the results is limited by the single-institution setting and the small number of OSCE stations involved. In addition, we had a small sample size and could not compare the results obtained with the OSCE with other practical value assessment methods in the health sciences to confirm whether the OSCE has a greater impact on academic outcomes. Further studies with more stations, multiple schools, and larger samples are warranted. Third, the reader should consider the results of our work with care since the duration of the COVID-19 pandemic is unpredictable and their effects; therefore, the term “postpandemic” (which was used to refer to one of the groups) could be misleading since we could not rule out the COVID-19 effect completely, and hence, COVID-19 effects could also exist during the period of time in which the OSCE was performed for the post-COVID-19 group. Fourth, different confounding variables that could influence the results were not collected because the pandemic was not previously planned; however, those that were collected were included in the logistic regression. Fifth, “non-probabilistic convenience” selection is not free of limitations; it could be strongly affected by bias of that particular academic age not related to what it is intended to explore here; therefore, the interpretation of our results should keep in mind the sample size and selection criteria. Sixth, in our study, we did not consider other academic stressors, such as coursework or clinical placements, which may be additional factors that could influence anxiety beyond the OSCE. This is an interesting line of research for future studies. Finally, note that all the students that accomplish the inclusion/exclusion criteria were included, resulting in unequal cohort sizes (56 vs. 65) this may affect the validity of comparisons, so the interpretation of the results should consider this limitation.

Conclusions

In our study, we found a decrease in global academic performance after the pandemic, although the grades for access to studies were better. This may be due to a relaxation of some control mechanisms prior to taking the exams, as well as a lower level of commitment of students to their training. Although the OSCE generates some stress and anxiety that may be due to the characteristics of the test itself, in our study, neither stress nor anxiety were related to the OSCE results but rather to the degree of previous training and exposure to simulation scenarios before the OSCE. We consider the OSCE to be a valid and reliable test that provides meaningful learning experiences and can be used as a predictor of academic performance in health sciences training programs.
