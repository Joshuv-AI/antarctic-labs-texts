Inflammation and Cognitive Decline: A Population‐Based Cohort Study Among Aging Adults With Atrial Fibrillation
Authors: Danielle Marion, Zhe Li, Austyn Roseborough, Shawn N. Whitehead, Ellen E. Freeman, David H. Birnie, Fanny M. Elahi, Vladimir Hachinski, Mary Cushman, Jodi D. Edwards
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite associations between atrial fibrillation (AF) and cognitive decline independent of stroke, pathways underlying this relationship remain unclear. Inflammatory markers, such as CRP (C‐reactive protein), are associated with blood–brain barrier (BBB) permeability, potentially leading to neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration. We estimated associations of CRP with cognitive impairment and death in aging adults with AF.MethodsAdults aged ≥45 years with prevalent AF and no cognitive impairment were identified from the REGARDS (Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke) cohort (N=30 239). Plasma CRP was measured at baseline and cognitive status measured annually using the Six‐Item Screener. Competing risks Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate cause‐specific hazard for cognitive impairment (Six‐Item Screener score ≤3). Cognitive trajectories were identified using latent class growth models and adjusted binomial logistic regression used to estimate associations between CRP and cognitive trajectories, with interactions by sex.ResultsAmong 2109 participants, 285 developed cognitive impairment and 786 died over a median 9‐year follow‐up. A doubling of baseline CRP levels was associated with increased death (hazard ratio [HR], 1.13 [95% CI, 1.08–1.19]) but not incident cognitive impairment (HR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.91–1.04]). Latent class analyses identified 2 unique cognitive trajectories: 91% had a stable trajectory, while 9% showed progressive decline. Sex‐specific models showed a 9% increased odds of progressive cognitive decline in men (odds ratio, 1.09 [95% CI, 1.01–1.17]) but not women (odds ratio, 1.04 [95% CI, 0.97–1.12]).ConclusionsHigher CRP was associated with cognitive impairment in aging men with AF, highlighting inflammation‐mediated blood–brain barrier dysfunction as a potential pathway linking AF to cognitive decline.

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and dementia are growing epidemics in the aging population, with a global prevalence projected to rise above 60 million and 139 million, respectively, by 2050. 1 , 2 AF, the most common cardiac arrhythmia, is associated with an increased risk of morbidity and death, increasing stroke risk 5‐fold, 3 and dementia is a leading contributor of disability and dependence among older adults. 4 Critically, compared with those without AF, patients with AF are at a higher risk of cognitive decline and dementia independent of stroke. 5 , 6 Despite strong evidence supporting this association, the underlying pathways linking AF to cognitive decline remain poorly understood.

A potential contributing pathway of this association that has received relatively little attention in the clinical literature is the proinflammatory state common to both AF and dementia and its impact on the blood–brain barrier (BBB). Systemic inflammation in AF may increase BBB permeability, which has been strongly related to cognitive decline and dementia. 7 , 8 Increased BBB permeability subjects the brain to neurotoxins, activating central inflammatory responses, and impairs the clearance of amyloid β, leading to its accumulation and aggregation, and resulting in neurodegeneration, synaptic impairment, and neuronal damage associated with clinical dementia. 7 , 9 Although elevated levels of inflammatory markers, including CRP (C‐reactive protein), which is produced by the liver during the acute inflammatory response, have been associated with AF and BBB dysfunction, 8 , 10 , 11 , 12 few studies have investigated associations between this marker and cognitive impairment in the AF population. 13 , 14 CRP levels increase in response to infection or inflammatory conditions, such as cardiovascular disease 15 and metabolic disorders, like diabetes and obesity. 16 , 17 Since measuring BBB dysfunction is invasive and expensive in a clinical setting, the use of circulating inflammatory markers such as CRP, may provide insight into ongoing inflammatory processes that might influence BBB integrity. To examine the role of this inflammatory marker in cognitive risk, we estimated associations of plasma CRP with cognitive decline, cognitive impairment, and death among aging adults with AF.

Methods

Data Source and Study Cohort

The REGARDS (Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke) study is a prospective population‐based cohort study of 30 239 Black and White individuals aged ≥45 years in the United States. 18 Participants were randomly sampled from commercially available nationwide lists of residents using mail and telephone contact between 2003 and 2007. 19 Eligible participants underwent a baseline telephone interview and an in‐home visit to provide measurements of physical and cognitive status and were contacted by telephone at 6‐month intervals over a maximum follow‐up period of 17 years. 18 Further details on the methods are available elsewhere. 18

The present study included participants with self‐reported AF or with evidence of AF on 12‐lead ECG (n=2514). Those with baseline cognitive impairment (n=2557), defined as a Six‐Item Screener (SIS) score ≤3, 20 or with missing CRP data (n=167) were excluded from this analysis. Additionally, for the adjusted regression models, those with missing covariate data were excluded (n=377). The REGARDS study was approved by the institutional review boards of participating institutions, and all participants provided written informed consent. For the current study, ethics approval was also granted by the University of Ottawa Heart Institute Research Ethics Board. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Exposure

The exposure was plasma CRP concentration (mg/L) collected at the baseline in‐home visit. Participants fasted 10 to 12 hours before the visit and blood samples were centrifuged at room temperature within 2 hours of collection. 18 Separated serum and plasma samples were catalogued and recentrifuged at 60 000g and 4 °C, then stored at the central laboratory at −80 °C. CRP was measured using high‐sensitivity, particle‐enhanced immunonephelometric assay (N High Sensitivity CRP, Dade Behring Inc., Deerfield, IL; interassay coefficients of variation 2.1%–5.7%). 21 CRP was log2 transformed to correct for skewness and, as such, is presented in terms of relative change (doubling or halving) rather than absolute values to reflect individual increases in CRP from baseline.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was incident cognitive impairment, ascertained by trained technicians at baseline and annually over follow‐up using the SIS, administered in the years 2003 and 2019. The SIS assesses global cognitive function and includes a 3‐item recall and a 3‐item temporal orientation (day of the week, month, year) with a score ranging from 0 to 6 (see Data S1). 20 Prior studies have validated the SIS and report high sensitivity (0.86–0.93) and specificity (0.87, compared with the Mini‐Mental State Examination) for the identification of individuals with cognitive impairment across a number of populations and settings, including community‐dwelling, tertiary referral, and primary care. Cognitive impairment was defined as a score of ≤3 at any follow‐up assessment, a cutoff previously shown to have a sensitivity of 88.7% and specificity of 88.0% for the diagnosis of dementia. 20 Additionally, death before cognitive impairment was assessed as a competing risk, accounting for 37.3% of the cohort (n=786). Deaths were ascertained through semiannual telephone follow‐ups, and medical records were sought for further validation. In the event of a proxy‐reported death, an interview was conducted with the next of kin. The secondary outcome was cognitive decline, indexed as trajectories of SIS scores over time.

Covariate Definitions

Demographic covariates included self‐reported age, sex, race, education level, and urban group. Education was categorized as less than high school, high school graduate, some college, or college graduate and above. Urbanicity was categorized as rural if the census tract where the participant lived was ≤25% urban, mixed if the census tract was 25% to 75% urban, or urban if the census tract was ≥75% urban. Vascular risk factors included self‐reported smoking and body mass index. Smoking was defined as never, past, or current smoker. Body mass index (kg/m2) was derived from measured height and weight at the baseline in‐home visit. Clinical comorbidities included self‐reported stroke at baseline, history of heart disease, diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. Participants were categorized as having a history of heart disease if they had reported previous myocardial infarction, coronary artery bypass graft, bypass, angioplasty, or stenting or if they had evidence of myocardial infarction on ECG. Diabetes was defined as a fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL, a nonfasting glucose ≥200 mg/dL, or self‐reported use of diabetic medication. Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg recorded at the in‐home visit. Dyslipidemia was defined as total cholesterol ≥240 mg/dL, low‐density lipoprotein ≥160 mg/dL, high‐density lipoprotein ≤40 mg/dL, or self‐reported use of lipid‐lowering medication at baseline. Medications included self‐reported use of antihypertensives, statins, antiplatelets, and anticoagulants during the baseline telephone interview.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study cohort by cognitive status with respect to demographic and clinical variables, using χ2 tests, t tests, or Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests. Cohort characteristics are presented as frequencies and proportions for categorical variables and means or medians for continuous variables (with measures of variation, including SD and interquartile range). We followed the Treatment and Reporting of Missing Data in Observational Studies framework to select the appropriate method for missing data. 22 Based on these guidelines, we conducted complete case analyses, excluding records with missing covariate data (n=377) and confirmed that multiple imputation would likely not improve efficiency using descriptive analyses exploring associations of missingness with exposure and outcome variables. As a complementary analysis, cumulative incidence function curves were generated to visually display the incidence of cognitive impairment and death over follow‐up for the categories of low (<1.0 mg/L), moderate (1.0–3.0 mg/L), and high baseline CRP levels (>3.0 mg/L). 23

For the primary analyses, cause‐specific competing risk Cox proportional hazards regression was used to estimate crude and adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) for associations between log2 transformed CRP and incident cognitive impairment, with CRP analyzed as a continuous variable to maintain statistical robustness and precision. A competing risks framework 24 was used to account for death as a competing risk between exposure and incident cognitive impairment. Associations between CRP and death are reported within this framework. Time was parametrized as the number of days from the baseline in‐home visit to the first occurrence of cognitive impairment, to the last recorded cognitive assessment, to the date of death before cognitive impairment, or loss to follow‐up unrelated to death and optimize model fit. CRP was log2 transformed to correct for skewness. As such, the HRs presented represent the effect of a 2‐fold change in CRP on the outcome, rather than an absolute change.

For secondary analyses, to limit missingness, we restricted follow‐up to 8 cognitive assessments, the maximum number of cognitive assessments for which the proportion of missingness was <50%. Cognitive trajectories were identified using latent class growth models, a semiparametric approach that identifies distinct classes with similar outcome trajectories over time. 25 One to 3 latent class models were tested using the hlme function of the lcmm R package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), a fixed‐effects model. 25 SIS score was modeled as a linear function over time, defined as the number of years from the baseline in‐home visit to each follow‐up SIS measurement. The number of latent classes were selected on the basis of class sizes and the Bayesian information criterion fit index. To ensure reliable estimates in subsequent analyses, we required each class to represent a meaningful proportion of the sample, with a minimum threshold of 5% per class. Binomial logistic regression was used to estimate associations between log2 transformed CRP and trajectory groups.

All models were adjusted for demographics, vascular risk factors, clinical comorbidities, medication usage, and baseline SIS score. These covariates were selected on the basis of clinical rationale and evidence of their association with the exposure and outcome variables. A multiplicative interaction term was used to determine effect modification by sex in all adjusted models, with stratum‐specific estimates estimated in the event of a statistically significant interaction (P<0.05). To ensure that inclusion of those with self‐reported AF did not bias our results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricting the cohort to those with ECG‐confirmed AF. To investigate the impact of incident stroke on model estimates, we conducted subgroup analyses among those both with and without incident stroke. Incident strokes were confirmed through medical record review or proxy interviews among those with suspected stroke events, excluding those with a self‐reported baseline stroke, resulting in 488 adjudicated incident stroke events among 460 participants over a median follow‐up of 4.4 years. 16

Proportional hazards assumptions were assessed using Schoenfeld residual tests for each covariate and the global model (P<0.05) and visual inspection of plots of the scaled Schoenfeld residuals versus time. Plots of martingale residuals and logit of the outcome versus continuous covariates were visually inspected to detect nonlinearity. To check for influential observations or outliers, we inspected plots of Dfbeta, Cook’s distance, and deviance residuals. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc. Cary, NC) and R version 4.0.3.

Results

A total of 2109 adults with prevalent AF, without baseline cognitive impairment, and nonmissing CRP values were included (Figure S1). The median follow‐up time was 9 years (interquartile range, 8.7 years), 285 individuals developed cognitive impairment and 786 died before cognitive impairment incidence. Demographic and clinical characteristics (overall and by incident cognitive impairment status) are presented in Table 1. Overall, the majority were White individuals (65.6%) and women (55%). Individuals with incident cognitive impairment were significantly more likely to report using anticoagulants compared with those without cognitive impairment (29.1% versus 20.3%); they were also less likely to be women (48.4% versus 56.1%). Median baseline CRP did not differ by cognitive impairment status. Secondary Analyses: Trajectories of Cognitive Decline Among Adults With AF

Latent class growth modeling indicated that data for patients with AF were best modeled as 2 phenotypes with unique cognitive trajectories (Figure 3). The first phenotype, labeled “stable,” included 91% of the cohort and showed high SIS scores at baseline, which remained stable over 15 years of follow‐up. The second phenotype, labeled “cognitive decline,” included 9% of the cohort, and their SIS progressively declined over the 15‐year follow‐up. Before testing for a sex interaction, no significant association was found between CRP and cognitive decline (odds ratio, 0.95 [95% CI, 0.90–1.00]). As sex was a significant effect modifier of the association of CRP with trajectory in the adjusted logistic regression model (P=0.01), we report sex‐specific estimates of cognitive decline (Table 3). A 2‐fold increase in CRP was associated with a 9% increase in the odds of cognitive decline among men (odds ratio, 1.09 [95% CI, 1.01–1.17]) after covariate adjustment. However, there was no association between CRP and cognitive decline among women (odds ratio, 1.04 [95% CI, 0.97–1.12]) after adjustment for covariates.

Sensitivity Analyses

Sensitivity analyses restricting the cohort to those with ECG‐confirmed AF or stratified by incident stroke did not impact the primary findings (Tables S2 and S3). However, in the secondary analyses including only those with incident stroke, the association between CRP and cognitive trajectory only remained significant among men with incident stroke during follow‐up (Tables S4 and S5).

Discussion

In this longitudinal analysis of community‐dwelling adults with AF, higher plasma CRP, an inflammatory marker associated with cardiovascular disease and potentially with BBB dysfunction, 12 , 26 , 27 was associated with an increased rate of death but not cognitive impairment after adjusting for demographic and clinical variables. Sex was not an effect modifier in adjusted models for incident cognitive impairment and death. By contrast, higher CRP was associated with cognitive decline trajectory in aging men with AF, but not women.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the association between CRP and cognitive decline in an AF population. Previous studies have assessed the association between CRP and cognitive function in the general population. Among these, cross‐sectional studies typically reported significant associations between elevated CRP and cognitive impairment. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 However, longitudinal studies have had conflicting results. 33 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 In another REGARDS study, higher CRP was associated with worse memory and verbal fluency at baseline but not cognitive decline over follow‐up or with incident cognitive impairment among stroke‐free adults at baseline. 21 Although in the present study we were unable to investigate dementia or dementia subtypes, others have reported that elevated CRP was associated with vascular dementia, but not Alzheimer dementia (AD) or all‐cause dementia. 34 , 38 As vascular dementia tends to progress more rapidly when compared with AD, 39 a sufficient follow‐up period may be required to detect symptoms of AD on neurocognitive batteries. Future studies should also consider investigating mixed pathologies, in which patients show signs of both AD and vascular dementia, as it is increasingly recognized as the most common pathogenesis of dementia in older adults. 39 , 40 , 41 , 42

Conflicting prior findings may also reflect variable demographics, comorbidity, and apolipoprotein EꜪ4 prevalence, measures of cognition, and lengths of follow‐up across studies. A sufficient follow‐up is critical to detect cognitive impairment among younger cohorts. REGARDS participants with AF had a mean age of 67 years and median follow‐up of 9 years. However, in the present study, cognitive impairment diagnosis relied on a SIS score ≤3. As the SIS is a brief cognitive screening instrument composed of only 6 simple cognitive tasks, it may result in a ceiling effect and a higher proportion of false negatives. 43 Further, it does not involve all measures included in clinical criteria for cognitive impairment and dementia, such as comprehensive neuropsychological testing or assessment of activities of daily living. Domain‐specific cognitive measures relating to dementia, including memory and executive function, may be more sensitive when compared with tests of global cognitive function and both a longer follow‐up period and more robust cognitive testing may have been required among individuals in this age stratum to detect cognitive impairment onset. For example, in a 25‐year follow‐up study with comprehensive neuroimaging and neuropsychological testing, higher CRP was associated with an increased risk of all‐cause dementia and dementia subtypes. 36 However, this may be less important among an AF population, who may have a higher risk of rapidly progressing vascular dementia.

BBB dysfunction, measured using cerebrospinal fluid analysis and imaging techniques, has previously been associated with cognitive decline and has been found in AF. 44 , 45 , 46 , 47 , 48 Currently, it is difficult to directly measure BBB dysfunction or CNS inflammation in vivo at scale; therefore, circulating inflammatory markers such as interleukins or CRP have been widely used to measure ongoing inflammation. Systemic inflammation in AF may increase BBB permeability, 9 leaving the brain vulnerable to the influx of neurotoxic plasma proteins and infiltration of subtypes of immune cells that may contribute to neuroinflammatory and neurodegenerative processes. 44 , 49 , 50 , 51 Previous studies have provided evidence of BBB dysfunction related to cognitive impairment, 52 irrespective of amyloid β and τ deposition, 45 and in postmortem brain tissue of patients with AD. 53 Findings from the present study provide preliminary evidence consistent with this pathway as a potential contributor to cognitive decline, particularly among men with AF, although CRP is also a nonspecific biomarker reflecting systemic inflammation and is associated with number of pathologic conditions including adiposity, diabetes, and general health status. 15 While observational studies have shown that, in the general population, the treatment of inflammation with NSAIDs significantly reduces risk of Alzheimer disease compared with no NSAID use, 54 prior randomized controlled trial data show no significant effect of NSAIDs. 54 However, a recent double‐blind placebo‐controlled trial of escitalopram+memantine in depressed older adults with subjective memory complaints showed that the addition of memantine provided a protective effect on executive functioning. 55 Future trials would be required to determine the potential benefit of anti‐inflammatory therapy in adults with AF. In addition, future studies using more sensitive markers of central nervous system infiltration may provide greater insights into the role of the BBB in AF cognitive risk. For example, recent studies have highlighted the potential role of brain‐derived extracellular vesicles as noninvasive indicators of neuropathology. 56 The ability to measure brain‐derived extracellular vesicles indicative of central inflammation and BBB dysfunction would complement recent studies describing extracellular vesicles and their associated cargo, which are altered in AD, frontotemporal dementia, and small‐vessel disease. 57 , 58 , 59 , 60

Sex‐specific differences in circulating biomarkers of inflammation have been previously reported, where women had higher levels of CRP. 61 Our study findings demonstrated that elevated CRP was associated with cognitive decline among men but not women. In post hoc analyses, women in the cohort were younger than men (65.8±9.5 versus 68.6±9.6, respectively). However, women had a longer follow‐up period when compared with men (9.2 years versus 8.2 years, respectively). This finding highlights potential sex‐specific differences in inflammatory pathways contributing to cognitive decline. While men tend to score higher on visuospatial tasks, women tend to have higher scores on tests of verbal memory, 62 such as the SIS, which may explain the higher proportion of men in the cohort with cognitive impairment compared with women. However, whether these sex‐specific findings reflect discrepancies in study methodology or true biological differences remains unknown. Given evidence for widespread sex differences in both the concentrations and effects of multiple inflammatory markers on cardiovascular disease risk, 61 future work should continue to consider sex in the evaluation of inflammation‐mediated BBB dysfunction for cognitive risk.

The strengths of this study include a large population‐based cohort with multiple cognitive assessments over 9 years. The prospective design allowed us to investigate temporal relationships between baseline CRP levels and cognitive decline, minimizing reverse causality. We were also able to account for many confounding demographic and clinical variables with death as a competing risk. However, there were several limitations. First, it is important to recognize that the SIS (primary outcome) is a screening tool and, despite prior validation studies showing comparable sensitivity to the Mini‐Mental State Examination for the detection of cognitive impairment, more comprehensive neuropsychological assessment is required to diagnose mild cognitive impairment and dementia. Accordingly, the results of the present study should be interpreted as evidence that CRP is a potential biomarker of increased cognitive risk and not a marker of the confirmed diagnosis of cognitive impairment or dementia. Second, despite adjustment for many key confounders, we cannot discount the possibility of residual confounding. We lacked sufficient data on potential confounders, such as medication usage and apolipoprotein E status and were also not able to examine effects by dementia or dementia subtype. Given prior conflicting findings that apolipoprotein EꜪ4 carriers with elevated CRP levels had the greatest risk of memory impairment 63 and that elevated CRP was associated with a lower risk of cognitive decline in those without the apolipoprotein EꜪ4 allele, 37 future research should address this. Second, we did not have information available on AF duration and subtype. Consequently, we were not able to identify those with the longest history or with persistent AF, who have the highest risk of cognitive impairment. 64 , 65 , 66 , 67 , 68 Further, we were unable to confirm whether those with self‐reported AF truly had the condition, although prior REGARDS research demonstrated that they have the same stroke risk as those with ECG‐defined AF. 69 As such, subjects with self‐reported AF may have been misclassified, particularly given the low anticoagulant use at baseline. Third, we were limited to considering CRP levels at 1 fixed time point. CRP is an acute‐phase protein of which levels can vary over time and perhaps more so among those with AF. Although CRP level is relatively stable over a 1‐year period, 70 longitudinal analyses beyond this time may require repeated longitudinal measurements of CRP. Furthermore, while CRP reflects nonspecific systemic inflammation, it may not accurately reflect BBB dysfunction or inflammation in the central nervous system, and future studies more directly examining direct markers of BBB dysfunction are required. However, animal studies have more clearly delineated the pathways linking plasma CRP to BBB dysfunction. 71 The length of follow‐up in the present study was limited to a median of 9 years, which, given the long‐term progression of cognitive decline, represents a shorter period of disease progression and may have limited our ability to capture later‐onset incident cognitive decline, potentially underestimating the association with CRP. Finally, in secondary analyses, subjects with greater cognitive decline may have been more likely to be lost to follow‐up, biasing our results toward the null.

In conclusion, higher plasma CRP concentration was associated with cognitive decline in aging men with AF, but not with incident cognitive impairment in the overall cohort. This study suggests that inflammation‐mediated BBB dysfunction or other aspects of inflammation may contribute to the risk of cognitive decline in AF populations. Future work would benefit from the use of sensitive markers of BBB dysfunction and central nervous system inflammation and comprehensive cognitive assessments, while considering possible sex‐specific differences.
