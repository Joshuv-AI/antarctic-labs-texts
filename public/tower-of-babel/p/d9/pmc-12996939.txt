Development and evaluation of patient education material for splenectomy patients
Authors: Masahiko Kita, Ryo Morita, Michinori Shirano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo develop and evaluate the PEM designed to enhance splenectomy patients' understanding, self-efficacy, and adherence to infection prevention behaviors, addressing the limitations of existing resources.MethodsWe developed the PEM with reference to the successive approximation model and evaluated the PEM after 2 joint reviews by infectious disease specialists. The evaluation method was conducted using an online survey. Native Japanese-speaking adults aged ≥18 years were recruited. We measured Knowledge tests for post-splenectomy recommendations (12 points) and Infection-Prevention Practices score (18 points, Cronbach's α =. 849). In addition, Self-efficacy, Understandability, and Actionability were measured using single items. Correlation coefficients were calculated for each variable, and multivariate analysis with Knowledge and IPPS as the objective variables was conducted to verify the relationship between the factors. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Graduate School of Human Sciences, Osaka University (Approval No: 24043).ResultsThe final study participants were 300. There were 140 female (46.7%), with more than 75% aged 50 or younger. Although Knowledge for post-splenectomy recommendations were not correlated with any variables, subjective ratings of PEM Understandability and Actionability, along with Self-efficacy, showed a significant positive correlation with IPPS (r = .23, 95% CI [0.11, 0.33], p < .001; r = .25, 95% CI [0.14, 0.36], p < .001; r = .26, 95% CI [0.15, 0.36], p < .001).However, the results of multivariate analysis with IPPS as the objective variable (F [11, 288] = 4.76, p < .001, R2 = 0.154) suggested that participants' health literacy and financial status might have a stronger influence on behavior than subjective ratings of PEM. Furthermore, 34% of participants showed a change in hesitancy toward vaccination after learning about the associated vaccine costs (X2 = 97.01, p < .001).ConclusionWe evaluated educational materials for patients undergoing splenectomy using an online survey. When developing PEMs, the ability to provide behavioral support should be considered, and a psychological approach that improves patients' subjective evaluation and fosters their sense of self-efficacy is essential.

Introduction

Splenectomy has been performed as a treatment for severe splenic injury associated with the resection of pancreatic or gastric cancer, severe hematological diseases, and portal hypertension. However, post-splenectomy severe infection (overwhelming post-splenectomy infection [OPSI]) is may occur as a complication. Despite the relatively rare incidence of OPSI, the course after the onset is rapid, the mortality rate is as high as approximately 50%–70%, and the risk of OPSI persists throughout life [1]. The need for multiple vaccinations and prompt medical consultation in the early stage of the disease has been described as a preventive measure against OPSI, and patient education is significant to practice such preventive measure [2]. Although the significance and content of patient education to cope with the long-term risk of severe infection have been emphasized, the low compliance rate has been regarded as a challenge. Studies have shown that patients frequently do not recall receiving education, and the quality of patient education is deficient [3]. Given that OPSI carries a lifelong risk and high mortality, effective patient education is crucial to ensure adherence to preventive measures such as vaccination and early medical consultation.

Patient education materials (PEM) utilised within medical settings represent a pivotal component in facilitating patient comprehension of their medical conditions, thereby enhancing their capacity to cope with treatment [4]. The evolution of these materials is evident in the transition from conventional printed resources to digital tools. In particular, a significant number of recent studies have reported the effectiveness of patient education and behavioral interventions using digital technologies in improving medication adherence [5], and smartphone applications for medication reminders and symptom monitoring [6] are innovative ways to encourage patients to change their behaviors independently. While the international evolution of digital tools has the potential to dramatically improve the quality of patient education, it has also created new gaps. Specifically, the digital divide, in which individuals lacking familiarity with digital devices (such as the elderly) are marginalised due to their exclusion from information, and the inundation of healthcare information accompanied by a paucity of medical evidence, are of particular concern. To correct these gaps, systematic evaluation of PEM is essential. In addition, interactive patient education is required based on patients' own subjective evaluation of PEM. The PEMs Assessment Tool (PEMAT) [7] is used as a concrete index to realize such evaluation. The purpose of this tool is to objectively evaluate how easy it is for patients to understand health and medical information materials (Understandability) and how easy it is to lead to concrete actions (Actionability), and it is an important guideline to select and develop high-quality patient education materials.

On the other hand, for splenectomy patients, provision of an environment that facilitates access to medical information necessary for timely and effective self-management has the potential to address the issue of patient education. However, at present, there is a serious shortage of websites that function as PEMs for splenectomy patients, both in Japan and overseas. Indeed, a study evaluating English-language websites for splenectomy patients revealed that, on average, only 49% of the information recommended by several guidelines was included [8]. Furthermore, the use of technical terminology resulted in the information being challenging for patients to understand. Japan is not an exception to this problem; an evaluation study of Japanese-language websites [9] revealed that they do not provide sufficient information essential for protecting life, such as the risk of OPSI, the type and timing of recommended vaccines, and the response to animal bites. The development of reliable, comprehensive, and user-friendly PEMs is, therefore, of paramount importance in order to facilitate an autonomous healthcare model for patients, thereby reducing reliance on medical institutions. The dearth of information in this area constitutes a significant medical gap, with the potential to compromise patient safety.

Therefore, this study aimed to develop and evaluate the PEM designed to enhance splenectomy patients' understanding, self-efficacy, and adherence to infection prevention behaviors, addressing the limitations of existing resources. This study contributes to improving patient safety and continuity of care by providing evidence-based, user-friendly educational tools for individuals at lifelong risk of infection following splenectomy.

Methods

This study employed a web-based, cross-sectional survey design to evaluate the effectiveness of a newly developed Patient Education Material (PEM).

Development of the PEM

In this study, we developed PEM by referring to the process of Successive Approximation Model [10], which is one of the educational design models. This model emphasizes interactivity and enables learner-centered design. In this study, we developed PEM with partial modification of the Successive Approximation Model. For the expert validation process, we recruited two infectious disease specialists based on the following criteria: (1) extensive experience in clinical practice for splenectomy patients, and (2) active involvement in patient education regarding post-splenectomy vaccinations. These experts acted as patient advocates within the SAM-based iterative cycles. Their role involved systematically reviewing each PEM prototype for clinical accuracy and linguistic clarity, ensuring that patient-facing information was both medically sound and practically comprehensible. This iterative feedback loop allowed us to refine the PEM content and evaluation measures before final implementation [Fig. 1].

Framework for PEM development in this study.

First, we selected the items necessary for PEM for splenectomy patients based on the OPSI prevention recommendations [11] and developed PEM Prototype 1, which emphasizes understandability and actionability. The researchers themselves selected the content of Prototype 1 and judged its understandability and actionability. In the joint review with study contributors_1, we proceeded to produce Prototype 2 after discussing the large number of characters in the explanatory text, the meaning of the illustrations, the simplification of vaccination recommendations, the modification of the explanation content on the patient card, and the clarification of the vaccination schedule. In the joint review with study contributors_2, we proceeded to produce Prototype 3 after discussing the selection of illustrations, the deletion of the COVID-19 vaccination recommendation, the addition of a free description space, and the further clarification of the vaccination schedule. Until the development of Prototype 3, patients were not directly involved in the design and evaluation. To ensure learner-centered design, the two infectious disease specialists and the researcher served as patient advocates during the iterative cycles. They evaluated the material's comprehensibility and actionability by drawing on their extensive clinical experience with patient consultations, focusing on simplifying jargon and ensuring the feasibility of the recommended actions. After prototype 3 was completed, they moved on to evaluate the online monitor before using it in actual splenectomy patients.

Regarding cultural adaptation and validation, the iterative SAM-based process functioned as a continuous pre-testing phase. Rather than a standalone pre-test, multiple prototyping rounds involving Japanese healthcare experts ensured that PEM content and measures aligned with local communication norms and vaccine perceptions. This integrated validation cycle effectively addressed cultural nuances and strengthened the construct validity of the evaluation model.

The Japanese readability of the PEM was evaluated using the jReadability(https://jreadability.net/sys/). The Japanese readability of Prototype 3 was 3.51, which was rated as “Normal”. The system had a rating scale of 6 (Very easy, Easy, Normal, Somewhat difficult, Difficult, and Very difficult), and “Normal” indicates readability as “They have the ability to understand relatively simple sentences, and can grasp the contents of sentences that are organized to some extent.” In this study, the readability of the PEM was considered to be appropriate.

Study setting and participants

The participants in this study were adults aged 18 years or older who were native Japanese speakers. Participants were recruited for this study using the crowdsourcing service Crowdworks (https://crowdworks.jp/) from November 8 to 15, 2024. This study focused on the general population rather than on actual patients.

Because this is an exploratory study to evaluate a newly developed PEM for splenectomy patients, we did not design a sample size in advance to test the hypothesis. However, we examined the required sample size to ensure the stability of the model for multiple regression analysis with 10 independent variables, which is the main analysis planned in this study. As a general rule of thumb to ensure the validity of a multivariate analysis model, 10–20 samples are required for 1 independent variable [12]. In this study, the number of independent variables was assumed to be 10. Based on this assumption, we determined that a sample of at least 100–200 participants would be required for analysis. Furthermore, because this study was conducted through an online survey, we expected that a certain number of data would be excluded from analysis due to invalid responses or withdrawal. Assuming that the valid response rate of a typical online survey is 60%, we set a target sample size of approximately 334 participants to secure the 200 valid responses required for analysis.

A total of 347 individuals were recruited for the study. The responses to the survey items were checked in detail, and the following exclusion criteria were used: (1) not responding to all survey items (n = 22), (2) spending less than 3 s on a webpage with an explanation (approximately 200 Japanese characters) that assumes splenectomy (n = 3), (3) spending less than 3 s on a webpage with an explanation (approximately 150 Japanese characters) about the function of the spleen (n = 10), and (4) spending less than 3 s on a webpage with an explanation (approximately 300 Japanese characters) about OPSI preventive measures (n = 12). To ensure the quality of the data, participants who exhibited ‘speeding’ behavior—defined as spending less than 3 s on a single informational page—were excluded. Given that the informational content ranged from 150 to 300 Japanese characters, which typically requires at least 15 s to process based on average reading speeds, a duration of less than 3 s was considered indicative of non-engagement with the material. A total of 300 subjects were included in the final analysis (Fig. 2).

Participant recruitment and registration process.

Procedure for PEM evaluation using an online survey

Participants completed an online survey using the “Qualtrics,” which took approximately 15 min to complete. First, participants answered questionnaires about basic attributes and health literacy. Then, “Instructions to assume that the patient has had a splenectomy in a traffic accident and has not yet been discharged from the hospital” was posted, and the patient was assumed to have undergone splenectomy. Subsequently, the developed PEM for patients undergoing splenectomy (Supplementary material.1) was displayed on web pages, and the participants were asked to view it. The PEM consisted of nine pages, including a cover page and a table of contents. After viewing the PEM, participants answered questions about knowledge, vaccination scheduling tasks, behavior change for infection prevention, understanding and actionability of the PEM, and self-efficacy for PEM recommendations.

Variables and data collection

We collected data on participant characteristics, health literacy, and several outcome measures to evaluate the PEM. A conceptual framework for evaluating PEM is presented (Fig. 3). In accordance with the HAPA model, self-efficacy is identified as the most potent predictor of the motivational phase, acting as a critical driver that empowers individuals to bridge the “intention-behavior gap” and initiate health-related actions [13], [14]. High-quality PEM reduces the cognitive load by simplifying complex medical information, which is a prerequisite for effective knowledge acquisition and processing [7], [15]. Specifically, materials that score high in understandability and actionability facilitate the interpretation of health information [7]. Health literacy serves as a fundamental moderator of how individual access, understand, and apply health information [16]. While knowledge alone is often insufficient for habit change, it remains the essential cognitive foundation for informed decision-making [17]. In the context of vaccination, clarifying “why” (knowledge of risk) and “how” (process of vaccination) provides the necessary rationale to support the transition from contemplation to action [13], [17]. In this study, correlation analysis and multivariate analysis were performed to verify the conceptual framework shown in Fig. 3.

A conceptual framework for evaluating PEM in this study.

Participant characteristics

We collected the following data via self-report as potential confounders.

Demographics: Gender, age, marital status, and education level.

Health and Economic Status: Self-rated economic status and presence of underlying diseases.

Information Sources: Sources of health-related information.

Health Literacy As the ability to utilize medical information may affect the primary outcomes (knowledge and behavioral intentions), we assessed health literacy. Health literacy was defined according to the World Health Organization [18] and measured using Ishikawa's Health Literacy Scale [19].

Outcome Measures for PEM Evaluation The primary outcomes were knowledge and the behavioral changes for infection prevention after viewing the PEM. We also collected data on participants' evaluation of the PEM and their self-efficacy.

Knowledge

Knowledge for recommendations after splenectomy: A 12-item true/false questionnaire based on the PEM content, scored out of 12 points.

Vaccination schedule task

Vaccination Schedule Task: A 3-item task where participants identified the correct month and year for recommended vaccinations based on provided interval information. The score was the percentage of correct answers.

Behavioral changes for infection prevention

This was measured in two parts:

Infection-Prevention Practices Score: Participants rated their change in intention for six items (e.g., hand washing, wearing masks, avoiding crowds) on a 4-point Likert scale (0 = same to 3 = very concerned), for a total score of 18 points.

Vaccination Intention: Due to the complex psychological factors influencing vaccination decisions [20], intention for the multiple vaccinations recommended in the PEM was assessed separately. Participants responded on a 3-point scale (all vaccinations, some vaccinations, or no vaccinations).

Understandability and actionability

Although there are standard provider-administered tools such as the PEM assessment tool [21], these are multi-item scales on which healthcare professionals assess PEM. In this study, the study participants themselves needed to assess the understandability and actionability of PEM, respectively, and due to the nature of the online survey, it was necessary to minimize the burden on the participants. To minimize the burden on the participants, many studies have used single-item measures [22]. For clear and simple constructs such as “self-esteem” and “happiness,” single-item measures are considered to show sufficient reliability and validity [23] [24]. Since the understandability and actionability of PEM were considered to be clear and simple constructs, we used single-item measures for the study participants in this study.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy is a variable that is closely associated with behavioral change, and it hypothetically impacts the implementation of several health behaviors [25], [26]. Scales for assessing self-efficacy have been developed in various fields [13], [27], and efforts have been made to evaluate self-efficacy in specific fields. In this study, as the self-efficacy we wanted to assess was “Whether the content described in PEM is something I can work on,” and the construct was relatively simple. Moreover, the single-item self-efficacy scale has been shown to be sufficiently reliable (=. 642) and valid (=. 795) for practical use [28]. Thus, self-efficacy in this study was measured by a single item (10 point scale).

Influence of cost on vaccination intention

To assess the potential influence of financial factors on vaccination behavior [29], we collected additional data. After participants completed all primary outcome measures, they were provided with information on vaccination-related costs. We then re-collected data on their vaccination intention (all, some, or no vaccinations) to measure any change.

In the context of the 5C model of vaccine hesitancy [30], the provision of specific out-of-pocket costs addresses both constraints and calculations. Unlike internal psychological factors such as confidence and complacency, which may require long-term communication, financial constraints are often “perceived” barriers that can be addressed immediately by providing accurate and actionable information. The goal of PEM is not simply to impart knowledge, but to help patients make confident decisions by removing each of the “psychological and physical barriers” described in the 5C model. Cost transparency is an essential step in this process.

Analysis methods

All statistical analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Japan) version 1.61 [31], which is a modified version of R Commander designed to add statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics.

Exploratory factor analysis alone was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27). A two-sided p-value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Categorical variables, including participants' demographic backgrounds (gender, age, marital status, self-rated economic status, educational background, and presence of underlying disorders), were summarized using frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Continuous variables (Health Literacy, Knowledge, IPPS, Self-efficacy, Understandability and Actionability) were first assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test [32]. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were presented as median and range.

Bivariate analyses were conducted to compare outcomes based on participants' backgrounds. Depending on the data characteristics, Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA were used for normally distributed continuous variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis test were used for non-normally distributed variables. The chi-squared test or Fisher's exact test was used for comparisons of categorical variables, as appropriate.

Correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient for normally distributed data or Spearman's rank correlation coefficient for non-normally distributed data [33], 95% confidence intervals were estimated.

To examine the construct validity (internal structure) of IPPS, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was performed. The principal factor method was used for factor extraction, and varimax rotation was applied. The number of factors to retain was determined based on the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalue ≥1.0) and examination of the Scree plot. The internal consistency (reliability) of the scale was evaluated by calculating Cronbach's alpha coefficient.

To examine the criterion-related validity and construct validity of each single-item scale (Understandability, Actionability, Self-efficacy) and Infection-Prevention Practices score used in this study, correlation analyses were conducted with the Health Literacy Scale and related indicators. Considering the possibility that each variable did not follow a normal distribution, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to count correlations. In addition to the point estimate of the correlation coefficient, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated using the bootstrap method (1000 iterations) to verify its accuracy and determine its statistical significance more rigorously. The strength of the correlation coefficient was interpreted as weak if ≥0.2 and < 0.4, moderate if ≥0.4 and < 0.7, and strong if ≥0.7 [34].

We then performed two independent multiple regression analyses to identify factors associated with knowledge and IPPS for post-splenectomy recommendations, respectively. Prior to performing the analyses, we performed tests of assumptions for multiple regression analyses, including multicollinearity (confirmation of VIF), normality of residuals (e.g., Q-Q plots), and homogeneity (residual plots). Independent variables included participants' basic characteristics (gender, age, marital status, self-rated economic status, education, and underlying medical conditions), as well as Health Literacy Scale, Understandability, Actionability, and Self-efficacy. In each model, estimates of partial regression coefficients (B) and 95% CI for independent variables were calculated to evaluate associated factors. All independent variables were entered into the models simultaneously using the forced entry method.

Finally, to quantify the change in intention to vaccinate after the cost explanation, the percentage of those who changed their intention to vaccinate to “hesitant” was calculated. The 95% confidence interval for the percentage was calculated. In addition, to examine whether there was a statistically significant change in intention to vaccinate (from “willing” to “hesitant”) before and after the cost explanation intervention, the McNemar test, which is a method for analyzing paired binary data (a before-and-after comparison of the same population), was performed. The level of statistical significance was set at less than 5% (p < .05).

Ethical approval and informed consent procedures

In this study, participants were informed that they were anonymous and voluntary, that they could exit the study at any time without detriment, that no personal information would be collected and that their data would be confidential. If they did not consent, the study was automatically terminated. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Research Ethics Committee of the School of Education, Graduate School of Human Sciences, the University of Osaka (Approval No. 24043).

Results

Participant characteristics

Overall, 347 participants were recruited, 300 of whom were included in the final analysis. Regarding the gender of the study participants, 159 (53.0%) were male, 140 (46.7%) were female, and 1 participant did not specify a gender.More than 75% were aged 50 years or younger. The diseases of those who reported having underlying diseases encompassed hypertension (n = 20), obesity (n = 11), diabetes (n = 8), chronic respiratory disease (n = 7), hyperlipidemia (n = 7), cerebrovascular disease (n = 4), cancer (n = 3), chronic kidney disease (n = 1), and others (n = 17). Social networking sites and the Internet (n = 199) were the most common sources of health-related information, followed by television (n = 123).

Factor analysis of infection-prevention practices score

Exploratory factor analysis revealed that only 1 factor had an eigenvalue of 1.0 or more (Eigenvalue of Factor 1 = 3.486, Contribution =58.1%). In the Scree plot, the eigenvalues of factors 2 and later decreased markedly, indicating that a 1 factor structure was appropriate. The internal consistency of the scale (all 6 items) was examined, and Cronbach's α coefficient was 0.849. IPPS correlated significantly with HL (r = .23, 95% CI [0.12, 0.34], p <. 001), indicating some criterion-related validity.

About the validity of single-item scales(understandability, actionability, self-efficacy)

Construct validity of the single-item scale was examined, and convergent validity was confirmed by a significant positive correlation with the Health Literacy (HL), which is theoretically expected to be related (r = .24–.30, p <. 001). Although the correlation coefficients were weak, the 95% confidence intervals calculated by the bootstrap method did not cross 0, indicating that the relationships were statistically robust. Moderate to strong correlations (r = .53–.72) were found among the three items of Understandability, Actionability, and Self-efficacy. This indicates that the items are interrelated and appropriately capture the common construct of a subject's ability to receive information, supporting the construct validity of the internal dimension.

Changes in intention to take multiple vaccines following vaccination cost disclosure

After viewing the PEM on the online survey and completing the questionnaire, participants were informed about the costs of multiple vaccinations explained in the PEM and indicated if knowing the costs had altered their intention to get vaccinated. Of the 291 participants who initially intended to receive the vaccine, 99 (34.0%) changed their intention to “hesitant” after receiving the cost explanation (Fig. 5). The 95% confidence interval for this proportion was 28.6% -39.5%. When the change in intention before and after the cost explanation was analyzed using the McNemar test, the number of participants who changed their intention from “willing” to “hesitant” (99) was significantly higher than the number of participants who changed their intention from “hesitant” to “willing” (0) (X2 = 97.01, p < .001).

Discussion

Although this study did not target patients who underwent splenectomy and evaluated online monitors with assumptions, the PEM developed here holds promise in improving knowledge about post-splenectomy precautions and promoting infection-prevention practices. Conversely, in this study, the developed PEM demonstrated inadequate educational effects on some of the contents, and the points for improvement became clear. Before actual patient education, it may be useful to evaluate PEM with online monitors to test for problems.

From “knowledge transfer” to “action support”: reevaluating the role of PEM

The results of this study offer essential suggestions about the role of PEM. This is because the role of PEM should not be as a “textbook” to unilaterally transfer knowledge but as a “practical guide” to encourage patients to refer to it when necessary and take concrete actions, aligning with the responses on knowledge-related questions. The percentage of correct answers to memory-dependent knowledge questions, including the number of pneumococcal vaccinations and the need for the Hib vaccine, was <70%, whereas the percentage of correct responses to the task of constructing a vaccination schedule while viewing the PEM was extremely high at >88%. This finding indicates that accurately memorizing complex information after reading it once is challenging; however, with relevant materials readily available, individuals can take appropriate actions. The vaccination regimen following splenectomy is complex, involving multiple vaccines and timing, and expecting patients to memorize all of them is unrealistic. Rather, decision-making about subsequent steps should be made easier for patients.

Therefore, in developing PEM, considering a design that prioritizes “ease of use” and “ease of action” rather than insisting on comprehensiveness and detail of information is crucial. For example, rather than explaining a complicated schedule in a lengthy sentence, showing it visually in the form of a checklist, a flowchart in a chronological order, or a template in the form of a calendar is more effective. A design with high “referential” that facilitates patients to quickly identify necessary information and understand it by applying it to their own situation is required. PEM should be a tool that reduces the cognitive burden of patients and allows them to act with confidence, rather than placing a burden on their memory.

Significance of psychological factors promoting behavioral change

Interestingly, knowledge were not significantly correlated with Understandability, Actionability, Health Literacy, Self-Efficacy, and IPPS. This suggests that while the developed PEM was successful in facilitating information transfer, as evidenced by high comprehension scores, knowledge acquisition alone is insufficient to promote behavioral change in this population. Furthermore, it was found that subjective feelings such as “Understandability,” “ Actionability, ” and “Self-efficacy” after reading the PEM had a greater influence on patients’ behavior change than“ knowledge ”. The results reaffirm the concept of health behavior theory and HAPA theory that “knowing” does not necessarily lead to “doing” [34]. Furthermore, the results are consistent with previous research [35] that a combination of self-monitoring, problem-solving, and planning is more effective for behavior change than mere knowledge-based interventions. Methodologically, the lack of correlation may be attributed to a ceiling effect in the knowledge assessment. If the PEM was highly effective in conveying the essential information, most participants might have achieved high scores regardless of their baseline health literacy or self-efficacy. This would result in low variance for the knowledge variable, thereby masking potential correlations with other constructs.

Although there may have been problems with the accuracy of the knowledge test, the finding that knowledge was not related to behavior change was consistent with a previous study [36], suggesting that it is necessary to emphasize the medical accuracy of information and communication design that emphasizes patient psychology when creating a PEM. In order to improve patient understanding and foster self-efficacy, it is necessary to avoid technical terms, speak in plain language, not limit to abstract recommendations, provide step-by-step instructions (Exactly what to do, when to do it, and how to do it), and use illustrations and diagrams to help patients understand intuitively. After reading a PEM, patients should feel positively that “I see. I can do it myself.” rather than feeling that it is difficult. These subjective psychological factors may be important for behavior change.

Toward implementation of patient education

The results showed significant differences in health literacy (HL), infection prevention behaviors, and self-efficacy among the individual attributes of gender, age, income, and education. These results were similar to those shown in a previous study [37]. In addition, communicative HL and self-efficacy to communicate with medical providers were lower in the younger group aged 18–40 years. This suggests that they have less interaction with medical institutions than other age groups, lack communication experience, and have a relatively low sense of ownership in health issues, and are unable to take the content presented in the educational materials as their own. Furthermore, in addition to communicative HL, critical HL, and self-efficacy, the low-income group scored significantly lower than the other groups in the evaluation of the Understandability and Actionability of the educational materials. This strongly suggests that the behaviors assumed in the educational materials (e.g., costly and time-consuming preventive measures) may be different from the actual living conditions and financial constraints of low-income groups. It also suggests that financial disparities not only cause disparities in HL and self-efficacy, but that the provided PEM itself may be a barrier to access.

To maximize the effectiveness of patient education, it is essential to personalize patient education by focusing on their attributes and challenges. In particular, it is important to provide information directly and plainly to those who have difficulty accessing and understanding information (Men and those with low education), to devise ways to enhance a sense of ownership and self-efficacy, and to present feasible options that match the living conditions of low-income groups.

The rapid development of digital health since 2000 has brought new possibilities for patient education. However, disparities in digital health literacy (DHL) [38], [39] have emerged as a significant challenge, with concerns that socioeconomic and age-related gaps may exacerbate health inequalities. These risks necessitate a comprehensive and individualized approach.

To address this, we propose a “hybrid approach” that combines digital materials with physical paper media to maximize practicality and ensure emergency preparedness. The PEM evaluated in this study is suitable for paper use and can be reliably provided within the limited time before discharge. Clinical implementation follows a three-step workflow: (1) assessing digital literacy during discharge planning to tailor PEM formats; (2) providing paper ‘quick-references’ with QR codes at discharge for accessibility; and (3) using annotated PEMs as communication bridges during multidisciplinary follow-up.

Furthermore, integration with a personalized counseling system is essential. Such a system should automatically identify users with stagnant behavior change and facilitate active intervention by specialists (push-type support) or remote counseling. This adaptive model is key to closing the digital divide and realizing patient education that leaves no one behind.

The “hesitancy” after cost explanation shown in this study can be explained from the perspective of“perceived barriers” in the health belief model [40]. In HBM, preventive behavior is determined by weighing “perceived benefits ” and“perceived barriers”. It is suggested that “hesitancy” occurred because this cost barrier exceeded the perceived benefits at the time, or it was unclear whether the benefits were worth the costs. Many studies have shown that financial incentives and cost reduction are effective in improving vaccination rates [41], [42]. To increase the feasibility of educational content for splenectomy patients, it is necessary to develop a mechanism to reduce the cost of vaccines.

Innovation

The innovative nature of this study is characterized by its utilization of an iterative framework, which integrates the Successive Approximation Model (SAM) with patients' subjective psychological assessments. This approach diverges from existing educational tools and digital applications in two fundamental ways:

First, in contrast to designs that are provider-centric or purely tech-driven, we employed SAM's agile cycles to establish a connection between clinical accuracy and user-centered usability. This was achieved by optimizing the PEM to reduce cognitive barriers through repetitive feedback.

Secondly, while the majority of digital tools prioritize data tracking or information delivery, our model employs a quantitative approach to establish a link between subjective “understandability” and “actionability” and self-efficacy. By demonstrating that these subjective perceptions are more effective predictors of intention than knowledge scores, we establish an evidence-based evaluation standard that remains effective across both paper and digital formats, thereby addressing the digital literacy divide.

Limitations

This study demonstrates the potential of PEM and highlights its limitations. However, this study has several limitations. First, we collected data assuming that splenectomies were performed through an online survey rather than in actual splenectomy patients, and the population characteristics, medical information needs, and perceived risk of infection differ from actual patients. This sampling bias should be considered when generalizing the results of this study to the actual patient population, and the validity of the data should be interpreted with caution.

In addition, the explanatory power of the model was limited (R2:15.4%) because some assumptions of the multivariate model with behavioral changes to prevent infection as the dependent variable were not met. This may be due to the presence of unmeasured variables. Furthermore, although the development of PEM was carried out with infectious disease specialists as advocates for patients, referring to the SAM model, the possibility that the opinions of patients were not sufficiently reflected should be considered. As a future perspective, when evaluating the effect of PEM in actual splenectomy patients, the study design should also consider differences in the perceived susceptibility of study subjects to infection.
