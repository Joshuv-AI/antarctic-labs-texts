Orthopedic Provider Accuracy for Ultrasound Detection of Peripheral Nerve Injuries: A Cadaveric Educational Study
Authors: Colin Ackerman, Sameer Puri, Drew Wrightson, Jeffrey Greenberg, Brandon Smetana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo determine the ability of ultrasound to detect peripheral nerve injuries among orthopedic providers before and after a short educational module.MethodsFifteen orthopedic providers used handheld Butterfly iQ+ and cart-based Sonosite SII ultrasound to evaluate peripheral nerve injury patterns created in cadaveric specimens. Participants assessed complete lacerations, partial lacerations, intact nerves, end neuromas, and neuromas in continuity at three upper-extremity sites: the dorsoradial forearm (radial sensory nerve), proximal forearm (proximal median nerve), and distal forearm (distal median nerve). Each participant evaluated all sites with both devices and predicted the injury pattern. Testing was repeated three weeks later after a brief ultrasound educational module.ResultsPosteducational module, diagnostic accuracy improved for laceration patterns and intact nerves, whereas accuracy for neuroma identification remained limited. Participants with prior ultrasound experience generally performed better, and diagnostic performance was similar between the handheld and cart-based devices.ConclusionsA brief targeted educational module showed modest gains in diagnostic accuracy for laceration patterns and intact nerves proposing that ultrasound may be useful as an adjunct for identifying these injuries. Similar observed performance between handheld and cart-based devices suggests that provider interpretation may be more important than ultrasound device.Clinical relevanceOrthopedic providers should undergo focused training for clinical adoption of ultrasound to improve diagnostic accuracy in peripheral nerve injuries. Similar accuracy between handheld and cart-based systems may also reduce barriers to incorporate ultrasound into hand-surgery practice.Key words: Handheld ultrasound, Nerve laceration, Orthopedic education, Peripheral nerve injury, Ultrasound

Technology and indications of ultrasound (US) in medicine continue to expand. Specific indications and uses are still being determined. US has been shown to be a real-time, noninvasive, and cost-effective diagnostic tool, particularly in the field of hand and upper-extremity surgery.1

Nerve injuries are found in approximately 2.6% of all upper-extremity trauma patients.2 While single laceration injuries with corresponding neurologic deficit may not need additional diagnostic tools, neurologic deficit associated with blast, traction/closed fractures, or multiple potential injury sites are more difficult to diagnose. Additionally, obtunded or pediatric populations pose challenging diagnostic situations. US can provide additional information before surgery in conjunction with history and physical examination, including assistance in diagnosing peripheral nerve injuries prior to electrodiagnostic studies, which can take upwards of 4−6 weeks to reflect injury. Hollister et al3 has reported a 96.5% correlation between preoperative US findings and intraoperative findings for peripheral traumatic nerve injuries.

US has demonstrated high diagnostic performance for peripheral nerve injury among experienced musculoskeletal radiologists and neurologists, but less is known about its use by orthopedic providers with variable US experience.3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Furthermore, access to traditional cart-based US systems may be limited in some clinical settings. Handheld US devices are more portable, increasingly available, and less costly than traditional cart-based systems, potentially expanding clinical use by orthopedic providers. However, it remains unclear whether these devices provide comparable diagnostic accuracy for peripheral nerve injury assessment.

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate orthopedic providers’ diagnostic accuracy before and after an US educational module. We hypothesized that diagnostic accuracy would improve after a brief targeted US educational module. Secondary aims were to compare diagnostic accuracy by prior US training status and US device. We hypothesized that participants with prior US training would perform better than those without prior training, and that handheld and cart-based US would demonstrate similar diagnostic accuracy.

Materials and Methods

Preparation and background

Institutional review board approval was obtained for this study and all participants’ informed consent was obtained, with the study conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

We designed a cadaveric study using a total of eight fresh-frozen cadaveric arms (5 women, 3 men; average age, 80.8 years; average BMI, 23.3 kg/m2) with no previous trauma, surgery, or radiographic abnormalities. The specimens underwent one freeze-thaw cycle and were thawed for 24 hours before dissection. Participants were recruited by convenience sampling from orthopedic providers at our institution. Eligible participants included attending hand surgeons, hand-surgery fellows, orthopedic residents, and advanced practice providers involved in hand or upper-extremity care. Providers were included if they were available for both testing sessions and provided informed consent. Prior US training was not required for participation and was recorded by participant survey. A total of 15 participants completed both sessions, including six attending hand surgeons, six hand fellows, two advanced practice providers, and one orthopedic resident. During both sessions, participants evaluated various nerve injuries using the handheld Butterfly iQ+ and a cart-based Sonosite SII US device. The scenarios assessed were complete laceration (CL), partial laceration (PL), intact nerves (sham injury), neuroma in continuity (NIC), and end neuroma (EN) (Fig. 1). Nerve injuries were created at three different sites: dorsoradial forearm (radial sensory nerve), proximal forearm (proximal median nerve), and distal forearm (distal median nerve).

Nerve injury design

In the CL condition, the nerve of interest was fully transected with a No. 15 blade. In the PL condition, approximately 50% of the nerve diameter was transected. In the intact (sham) condition, a transverse skin incision was made over the skin with a No. 15 blade, while the nerve itself was left uninjured. Neuroma models were created separately by injecting liquid latex (EnvironMolds Liquid LayTex) directly into the target nerve using a 25-gauge needle. Approximately 1.5 cc was injected, producing an estimated 300% to 400% increase in nerve diameter (Fig. 2). For end-neuroma models, the distal end of the involved nerve was completely transected with a No. 15 blade after latex injection.

Dilation in nerves created with the injection of liquid latex. Approximately 1.5 cc is shown here using a 25G needle.

The overlying skin incisions were all closed with a running 4-0 Monocryl suture to maintain blinding and simulate a clinical scanning surface. The margins of each testing site were marked with 3-0 green Ethibond sutures on top of the skin, which ensured that participants evaluated the sonographic characteristics of the injured nerve at the target site (Fig. 3).

Testing

Each participant used both US machines to evaluate each testing site and predict the nerve injury. A total of four fresh-frozen cadaveric arms were used for the first testing session. Participants were asked to assess nerves with the responses of CL, PL, intact nerve, NIC, and EN, with their responses recorded and assessed for correctness. After the first testing session, participants were asked to fill out a survey regarding previous US training (yes/no), use of US in daily practice (yes/no), comfort with US, and comfort with US to assess nerve injuries. A 5-point Likert scale was used to assess all comfort scores with US. Three weeks later, the experiment was repeated with a new set of four fresh-frozen cadaveric arms. Prior to the second testing, participants were asked to watch a 3-minute Hand-e video on US basics.8 They were also shown the US pictorial photograph demonstrated in Figure 1 and video representations of each type of nerve injury (Fig. 4). Additionally, basic features and setting adjustments for both machines were demonstrated. The second testing session followed the same structure as the first, with the same number of participants giving their observations, for a total of 720 total observations across two paired sessions (Table 1). After the second testing session, a repeat survey was administered assessing comfort with US and comfort with US to assess nerve injuries.

Statistical analysis

An a priori sample size estimate was performed using paired binary outcomes to guide study planning for the preeducation and posteducation diagnostic accuracy comparison. Sample size was estimated assuming α = 0.05 and 80% power. With a target improvement of 20 percentage points, the required number of paired observations ranged from 57 to 135 across plausible baseline diagnostic accuracy values of 30% to 70%. The present study included 360 paired observations. Since multiple observations were clustered within each participant, final inference was performed using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM). Therefore, the pair-based result should be interpreted as a planning estimate for paired responses, rather than a true power calculation for the final mixed-effects model. These findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require confirmation in larger clinical studies.

Incorrect/correct responses were summarized using percentages. Scores for participants’ comfort with US and comfort with US to assess nerve injuries were reported as averages with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Scores were compared using the Wilcoxon-signed rank test and are reported with the rank-biserial correlation due to nonnormal distributions observed on Q-Q plots and Shapiro-Wilk testing. A GLMM with a binomial distribution and logit link was used to evaluate binary accuracy outcomes (correct vs incorrect). Residuals were assessed to confirm model fit. Participant was included as a random effect to account for repeated responses within individuals. First, separate models were used to assess the overall effect of timepoint (pretest vs posttest) for each injury condition. Next, injury condition-specific models included timepoint, prior US training status, and the timepoint × training interaction to determine whether overall accuracy differed by training group, and whether pretest to posttest accuracy varied by training status. Additional injury-specific models included timepoint, US device, and the timepoint × device interaction to assess whether overall accuracy differed by device and whether pretest to posttest accuracy varied by device. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs. For all analyses, α = 0.05.

Results

Of the participants, 26.7% (4/15) had prior formal US training, and only 13.3% (2/15) reported active use of US in their daily clinical practice. On average, comfort with US increased from 1.93 (95% CI: 1.49−2.38) to 2.41 (95% CI: 1.99−2.81) between sessions (rank biserial, 0.56; 95% CI: 0.06−0.84; P = .09). Comfort with US to diagnose nerve injuries also increased from 1.36 (95% CI: 0.99−1.72) to 1.93 (95% CI: 1.40−2.46) between sessions (rank biserial, 0.71; 95% CI: 0.30−0.90; P = .04).

Accuracy for identifying CL (OR, 1.89; 95% CI: 0.86−4.17), PL (OR, 2.40; 95% CI: 0.80−7.21), and intact nerves (OR, 1.61; 95% CI: 1.00−2.61) increased from pre to post. Accuracy for EN (OR, 1.31; 95% CI: 0.31−5.53) and NIC (OR, 0.54; 95% CI: 0.19−1.45) was consistently the lowest (Fig. 5).

Discussion

In our cadaveric study, orthopedic providers demonstrated modest improvement in diagnostic accuracy for intact nerves and laceration patterns after a brief targeted educational module, partially supporting our primary hypothesis. Participants with prior US training generally demonstrated higher diagnostic accuracy, particularly for complete lacerations and intact nerves. We did not detect a considerable difference in diagnostic accuracy between the handheld Butterfly iQ+ and cart-based Sonosite SII US devices, although these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the study’s exploratory design.

The baseline accuracy rates observed in this study for CLs, PLs, and intact nerves improved following the educational intervention. While these post-training accuracies remain below the pooled sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 86% reported in a recent meta-analysis of US for traumatic nerve injuries, it is important to note that the published literature mainly reflects the results of experienced musculoskeletal radiologists and neurologists, rather than orthopedic providers with limited US training.9 Nevertheless, the observed improvements following minimal training suggest that US assessment of selected nerve injury patterns may have a relatively short learning curve for orthopedic providers, consistent with prior observations.7 Emerging technologies, including artificial intelligence assisted image interpretation may further reduce the learning curve and improve diagnostic accuracy, even among more novice users.10

The consistently low accuracy for both ENs and neuromas in continuity represents a surprising finding within the current study. This finding contrasts with the moderate diagnostic accuracy reported for neuromas by experienced sonographers.11,12 Neuromas typically appear as hypoechoic, well-defined lesions on US, with size varying based on time from injury.13 However, distinguishing neuromas from other nerve injuries requires familiarity with normal US nerve pathology and the ability to recognize subtle changes in echotexture. The latex injection model used in this study may have created neuromas with different acoustic properties than in vivo neuromas, potentially contributing to diagnostic difficulty. Additionally, the brief educational module may have been insufficient to convey the ultrasonographic features of neuromas, suggesting that more extensive training or hands-on practice may be necessary for accurate neuroma identification.

Furthermore, there was no considerable difference in diagnostic accuracy between the handheld Butterfly iQ+ and Sonosite SII, and no meaningful difference in preeducation to posteducation change was observed between devices. This suggests that within the context of this cadaveric model, diagnostic performance may have been driven more by the participant’s interpretive ability than by the US platform itself. Although cart-based systems are traditionally perceived to offer superior image quality, our findings suggest that handheld US may provide comparable accuracy for basic peripheral nerve injury assessment when used by orthopedic providers. This may offer practical advantages in terms of accessibility and cost-effectiveness, with the average conventional cart-based US often costing significantly greater than handheld US, which may improve hospital savings.14

Our study is not without limitations. The cadaveric model, while allowing for controlled injury creation, does not fully replicate the in vivo characteristics of peripheral nerve injuries. This includes tissue edema, hemorrhage, and inflammatory changes that may affect ultrasonographic appearance. As a result, the US appearance may not fully replicate in vivo nerve injuries. The latex injection method for creating neuromas, while producing measurable enlargement, may not accurately represent the echotexture of in vivo neuromas, decreasing the clinical applicability of this measurement. The small observer sample size within a single institution (n = 15) giving multiple observations, and the low number of observations in the prior training group, specifically for PLs, may limit the generalizability of these findings. The GLMM was also not formally powered, which may introduce type II error. Results should be interpreted with caution. Additionally, the 3-week interval between testing sessions may have allowed for practice and study within a provider’s day-to-day work that was not controlled for in the study design. Provider comfort was also assessed using a nonvalidated Likert-type scale. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and reflective of self-reported comfort rather than a validated provider-reported outcome measure. Future studies should evaluate US performance in clinical scenarios with possible surgical correlation and assess the durability of skills over time to determine the optimal training curriculum for orthopedic providers.

This study suggests that US may serve as a useful adjunct for orthopedic providers evaluating peripheral nerve injuries, particularly for distinguishing laceration patterns and intact nerves. Although diagnostic accuracy remained limited for neuromas, the observed overall improvement for CLs, PLs, and intact nerves after a brief educational module may support the potential for focused training for orthopedic providers to enhance diagnostic accuracy for US nerve assessment. Handheld US also demonstrated similar observed accuracy to a cart-based system in our cadaveric model, suggesting that device selection may be less important than provider training for basic nerve injury screening and allow for a more plausible implementation into hand-surgery practice. However, larger clinical studies are needed before making definitive practice recommendations.
