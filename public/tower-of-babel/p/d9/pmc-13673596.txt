Biomechanical effects of powered knee orthoses and articulated ankle-foot orthoses in people with diparetic cerebral palsy: a pilot study
Authors: Samuel Finnegan, Mikaela Juco, Ranita H. K. Manocha, Emily Rogers-Bradley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiparetic cerebral palsy affects approximately 2 in 1000 people, and can result in gait impairments such as excessive knee flexion and excessive ankle dorsiflexion during the stance phase in gait. Short-term clinical trials using robotic rehabilitation devices have shown gait improvements comparable to invasive multilevel surgery for spastic cerebral palsy. This study aimed to determine if the stiffness of articulated forms of ankle-foot orthoses influences the effectiveness of a powered knee-ankle-foot orthosis on crouch gait reduction. It was hypothesized that AFO stiffness would be an important factor influencing crouch gait in individuals with diparetic cerebral palsy when walking with a powered KAFO.MethodsA pilot study with three participants with diparetic cerebral palsy causing crouch gait was conducted to evaluate the effects of varying ankle stiffness of ankle-foot orthoses on sagittal plane kinematics and kinetics while walking with a powered-knee knee-ankle-foot orthosis. Experimental ankle foot orthoses were made to enable adjustable joint stiffness during the experiment. Optical motion capture and in-ground force plates were used to capture gait biomechanics during self-selected walking for eight experimental conditions.ResultsOverall, the presence or absence of a powered knee and AFO stiffness had an impact on ankle and knee biomechanics during walking. Across conditions using the powered KAFO, the greatest improvement in crouch at both the ankle and knee was observed with the highest-stiffness experimental AFO. Ankle torque increased with AFO use, while knee torque increased with the powered KAFO but was lower when paired with the experimental AFOs. Ankle power was not consistently improved by the KAFO and AFO conditions, while knee power generally increased with KAFO use and was highest in the powered conditions.ConclusionsThis study provides preliminary results on the impact of ankle-foot orthoses in conjunction with a powered knee exoskeleton, and may inform further exploration of treatment of pathological gaits using robotic assistive devices.

Background

Cerebral palsy (CP) is the most common neurological condition affecting children, impacting 2.1 out of 1000 live births [1]. CP often results in altered gait due to lower limb weakness, spasticity, altered motor control, and/or contracture [2]. CP is characterized by the predominant type of movement pattern (spastic, dyskinetic, ataxic, hypotonic) and by the limbs involved (quadriparetic, triparetic, diparetic, monoparetic) [2, 3]. One type of pathological gait frequently seen in individuals with spastic diparetic CP is crouch gait, identified by increased knee flexion and increased ankle dorsiflexion during the stance phase of gait [4–6].

Lower limb orthoses are commonly prescribed for individuals with crouch gait from CP [7]. Orthoses are most effective when the following conditions are met: (1) forces are distributed over a larger surface area, minimizing localized pressure on soft tissue; (2) force to control the joint is reduced through the use of a large moment arm; and (3) counterforces due to the orthosis oppose the primary force applied, summating to zero [7]. Prescribed ankle-foot orthoses (AFOs) may be either rigid, articulated, or dynamic. Rigid AFOs prevent multiplanar ankle movement and articulated AFOs allow some degree of sagittal movement at the ankle [7]. Rigid AFOs are used to stabilize the ankle during stance by primarily adjusting the shank-foot position with respect to the ground reaction force, at the expense of limited ankle range of motion [7]. Rigid AFOs are typically prescribed for people with crouch gait to help promote knee extension during stance while walking [7]. Articulated AFOs can improve swing limb clearance, stride length, cadence, and self-selected walking speed of children with spastic CP, but are typically avoided in crouch gait because of their tendency to increase knee flexion and ankle dorsiflexion during stance [8, 9]. Dynamic AFOs are designed to permit more natural ankle motion while storing and returning elastic energy during gait to improve walking performance [10].

The use of robotic exoskeletons and powered knee-ankle-foot orthoses (KAFOs) in gait training and analysis began in 1994, starting with the Lokomat (Lokomat, Zurich, Switzerland) gait trainer [11, 12]. Preliminary research has shown potential for rigid exoskeletons that provide knee actuation in improving spatiotemporal gait parameters, joint kinematics, and joint kinetics of individuals with crouch gait. External torque applied at the knee is demonstrated to improve knee extension and flexion of the wearer, with initial research demonstrating that such gait improvements persist after repeated device use [13]. Longer-term gait training with a knee exoskeleton has also been shown to increase peak knee extension angle during walking [14]. Research has shown improved knee extension after participant acclimation with such a device that provides assistive torque during stance and resistive torque during swing [15]. While initial work is promising, limited research has examined robotic exoskeletal devices for gait rehabilitation in individuals with diparetic CP, and little is known about how passive AFOs should be paired with powered knee assistance.

The present study evaluated a commercially available powered-knee KAFO (Fig. 1A), the Agilik Test Drive (Bionic Power Inc., Vancouver, BC, Canada), in individuals with crouch gait from diparetic CP. In this paper, we present pilot results from an experiment investigating the effects of a powered-knee KAFO paired with articulated AFOs of varied stiffness levels in three participants with crouch gait due to CP. This paper aims to provide preliminary information on the use of robotic gait aids as an intervention for people with crouch gait, informing the clinical prescription of AFOs paired with powered KAFOs.

Methods

AFO design and characterization

The AFOs selected for this study were designed based on parameters commonly used in prescribed articulated orthoses for individuals with CP. Generic articulated thermoplastic AFOs with adjustable ankle joints were fabricated by a certified orthotist (Colman Prosthetics and Orthotics, Calgary, AB, Canada) based on participant body dimensions, allowing the devices to be readily available and easily form-fitted (Fig. 2A). The AFOs were manufactured from polypropylene and included a medially positioned uniaxial 6841 Ultraflex USS Adult AFO Joint (Ultraflex, Pottstown, PA, USA) and a laterally positioned Flexure joint (Tamarack Habilitation Technologies, Inc., Blaine, MN, USA).

The adjustable medial ankle joint enabled tuning of both ankle range of motion and rotational stiffness for each AFO pair through the adjustment of bolt position (Fig. 2C). Range of motion was limited to 5 degrees plantarflexion and 15 degrees dorsiflexion based on standard range of motion limits for AFO users with CP. The distal adjustment bolts compress an internal polymer spring, with greater bolt insertion producing greater spring compression and therefore greater rotational stiffness at the ankle joint.

Based on preliminary benchtop testing, three distal bolt positions of 33% bolt insertion, 83% bolt insertion, and 100% bolt insertion were selected for use in the pilot study, corresponding to the low-, medium-, and high-stiffness experimental AFO conditions (P-ExpAFO-L, P-ExpAFO-M, and P-ExpAFO-H, respectively). Mean dorsiflexion and plantar flexion stiffness values were then measured across the left and right AFOs for each selected stiffness condition with a custom testing fixture designed to hold the experimental AFOs in a repeatable alignment during benchtop mechanical characterization (Fig. 2B). The fixture consists of six main components: a top component that connects to the hydraulic arm; horizontal support pins that pass through the top component; an adjustable trunk component that translates along the base plate to accommodate differences in AFO size; a shank cuff that secures the AFO to the trunk; vertical support rods that reinforce the connection between the trunk and shank cuff; and a base plate that defines the trunk position and rigidly mounts the fixture to the testing platform, similar to text platforms used in existing research [16, 17]. Prior to manufacturing, material analysis was performed to confirm that the fixture would remain stable under the maximum testing load of 1471 N, determined from the allowable user body mass of 100 kg, with a safety factor of 1.5. Mechanical testing was performed using a Mechanical Testing System (MTS) (MTS Systems Corp., Eden Prairie, MN, USA). During testing, the MTS applied vertical load to the AFO footplate while the applied force and resulting displacement were recorded. These measurements were used to calculate rotational stiffness and to characterize the low-, medium-, and high-stiffness experimental AFO conditions used in the gait study. Pilot study

The pilot study was conducted at the Movement Assessment Center (MAC) at the Alberta Children’s Hospital (Calgary, AB, Canada). The study participant inclusion criteria were as follows: 5–22 years of age, diagnosed with spastic diparetic CP causing crouch gait, able to walk without a walking aid for at least 3 m without stopping, body mass less than 100 kg, and height between 112 cm–180 cm. We considered 5–22 years of age for the study population due to Lerner et al.’s work, incorporating ages between children to adolescents [18]. Unassisted walking was required for the use of force plates during the pilot study, ensuring clean kinetic data. The weight and height restrictions were determined by the user limitations of the powered-knee KAFO being tested. The study was approved by the Conjoint Health Research Ethics Board at the University of Calgary (REB23-1396), and voluntary written informed consent was obtained from legal guardians with assent from participants. After initial consent of four participants to the study, one participant with diparetic CP was excluded due to non-typical crouch gait characteristics. Three participants (Table 1) were included in the study (age: 16.3 ± 3.7 years, height: 157.5 ± 6.1 cm, body mass: 54.8 ± 5.9 kg, sex: 1 male, 2 female). All three participants had spastic diparetic CP with crouch gait, and were at level 2 on the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) [19] (Table 1). The study included seven unique experimental walking conditions and one repeated condition: barefoot walking (BF); powered KAFO without an AFO (P-NA); unpowered KAFO without an AFO (UP-NA); powered KAFO with the participant’s prescribed AFO (P-PreAFO); powered KAFO with the experimental AFO in high- (P-ExpAFO-H), moderate- (P-ExpAFO-M), and low-stiffness (P-ExpAFO-L) configurations; and a repeated powered KAFO without an AFO condition (P-NA-R) to assess repeatability (Table 3). Sagittal plane kinematics were collected at 120 Hz using a 12-camera motion capture system (EvaRT 5.0.5 with Eagle-4 cameras (Motion Analysis Corp., Rohnert Park, CA, USA)). The modified Helen Hayes 26-marker model for 3D motion analysis was used for data collection [24]. For trials requiring the use of the powered KAFO (conditions P-NA, UP-NA, P-PreAFO, P-ExpAFO-H, P-ExpAFO-M, P-ExpAFO-L, and P-NA-R), custom medial markers were used during the static calibration trials of each condition (Fig. 1B), based on standard medial knee and ankle offset (wand) markers often employed in gait analysis to define virtual joint centers when direct anatomical marker placement is not feasible [25, 26]. Vertical ground reaction force data were recorded at 1200 Hz using four synchronized 6-channel force plates (Advanced Mechanical Technology Inc., Watertown, MA, USA). At least 3 trials for each condition were recorded per participant at a self-selected walking speed. Data processing and inverse kinematics and dynamics were performed with Motion Analysis EVa Real-Time Software (Motion Analysis Corporation, Rohnert Park, CA, USA) and Orthotrak (Orthotrak, Canada).

Statistical analysis

Given the small sample size of this pilot study, the analysis focused on condition-specific trends rather than formal statistical comparisons. Peak joint angle, torque, and power were calculated for each participant and condition for the ankle and knee. Left and right limb values were averaged for each participant, and group-level trends were summarized using the mean and standard deviation across participants. Crouch was quantified using metrics commonly reported in the literature, with average knee flexion angle during stance used as the primary metric [27, 28] and average ankle dorsiflexion angle during stance used as a secondary metric [29, 30]. Lower values for both metrics represent reduced crouch.

Discussion

While the results from this three-participant pilot study should be considered preliminary and interpreted cautiously, several interesting trends emerge from the presented results. The BF, UP-NA, and P-NA conditions help distinguish the effects of wearing the KAFO from the effects of powered knee assistance. Compared with BF, UP-NA increased peak ankle dorsiflexion angle, peak ankle torque, peak knee flexion angle, knee torque, and knee power, suggesting that the KAFO itself altered lower-limb biomechanics even when it was not powered. Both the ankle dorsiflexion and knee flexion mean stance angles were higher in UP-NA than in BF, and mean knee flexion was also higher than in P-NA, indicating that wearing the KAFO alone did not produce the same effect on crouch gait as the powered KAFO condition.

Powered assistance appeared to further affect knee biomechanics. Peak knee angle and knee power were both higher in P-NA than UP-NA. In contrast, peak knee torque was similar between UP-NA and P-NA, suggesting that the increased knee loading may have been primarily attributable to wearing the KAFO rather than the applied powered assistance. Alternatively, differences in self-selected walking speed between trials may have contributed to the observed changes in knee moment.

The differences between P-NA and P-NA-R suggest that repeated exposure to the powered KAFO may have influenced ankle and knee biomechanics. P-NA-R showed slightly lower peak ankle angle and higher ankle power than P-NA, while knee angle remained similar and knee power was lower. These changes may reflect familiarization with the device, although variability was high and trends should be interpreted cautiously. This finding agrees with previous studies that have noted changes in biomechanics with increased device familiarity in short-term trials [14, 15]. The differences between P-NA and P-NA-R may also be attributed to fatigue, as P-NA-R was performed as the last experimental condition for all participants.

Although peak knee angle across the gait cycle increased in several KAFO and AFO-based conditions, this does not necessarily indicate increased crouch, as knee flexion during stance is of primary interest when assessing crouch gait. While there is no consistently used metric for crouch reduction in the field [32], mean stance knee flexion angle is commonly reported as a measure of crouch gait [27, 28]. In the present pilot study, none of the KAFO conditions reduced mean stance knee flexion below barefoot walking. However, among conditions using the powered KAFO, P-ExpAFO-H produced the lowest mean stance knee flexion angle, indicating the greatest reduction in knee crouch and an improvement compared to P-PreAFO. The moderate- and low-stiffness experimental AFO conditions produced higher mean knee flexion angles. This trend applied to all participants, but due to the small sample size of the pilot study should be considered preliminary. This differs from results reported in previous studies, where greater AFO stiffness was correlated to increased peak knee flexion during stance [33]. The different response observed in the present study may be related to the use of a powered KAFO, which directly alters knee mechanics and may change the effect of AFO stiffness on proximal joint kinematics. The added mass of the device with no torque assistance during condition UP-NA was likely the cause of increased stance knee flexion for all participants compared to the barefoot trial and powered KAFO trials. Based on our preliminary results, it seems that an articulated AFO of high stiffness leads to lowest mean stance knee flexion when paired with a powered-knee KAFO.

Decreased mean ankle dorsiflexion during stance is also associated with reduced crouch [29, 30]. In the present study, none of the KAFO conditions reduced mean stance ankle dorsiflexion below barefoot walking. However, among the KAFO conditions, all experimental AFO configurations produced lower mean stance dorsiflexion angles than P-PreAFO, with P-ExpAFO-H producing the lowest value. The moderate- and low-stiffness experimental AFO conditions showed similar mean dorsiflexion angles, and no clear relationship was observed between decreasing AFO stiffness and the ankle crouch metric. P-NA and UP-NA also produced lower mean stance dorsiflexion angles than P-PreAFO, suggesting that the participants’ prescribed AFOs are not optimal for use with the powered KAFO. Overall, these preliminary findings suggest that the highest-stiffness experimental AFO may provide the greatest reduction in ankle crouch among the AFO configurations tested with the powered KAFO, although the small sample size and participant-specific responses warrant further investigation.

These findings are generally consistent with previous studies demonstrating improved knee extension during stance with powered knee assistance [13, 15]. However, unlike prior studies, we did not observe a reduction in mean stance knee flexion during powered walking compared with barefoot walking. The modular test-drive KAFO used in this study was less customized to each participant than the individually prescribed devices used in [13, 15], which may have limited the effects of powered assistance on crouch-related measures. Nonetheless, P-NA did reduce mean stance knee flexion relative to UP-NA, suggesting that powered knee extension assistance may improve crouch-related measures compared with wearing the KAFO without assistance. Unlike prior work that has focused primarily on knee actuation and control timing [23], this study examined the role of the passive AFO paired with the powered KAFO for the first time.

While variability between the AFO pairs was observed during benchtop characterization, the repeated measurements demonstrated acceptable repeatability for characterizing the experimental AFO stiffness conditions for the purposes of the pilot study. Identical AFO joints were used in the experimental AFOs, and observed variation was likely due to variation in the Ultraflex joints as manufactured, or variation due to AFO size differences or fabrication practices. The AFO fabricated for participant 3 (P3 AFO) showed particularly large deviation from the AFOs for participants 1 and 2. Within each AFO, repeated tests produced similar stiffness values, indicating that the test stand provided consistent measurements across trials. In addition, the measured stiffness values increased systematically from the low- to medium- to high-stiffness conditions, demonstrating that the test stand was sensitive enough to distinguish the intended experimental stiffness levels. This increase in rotational stiffness with increased bolt insertion aligned with the expected properties of the Ultraflex joint as provided by the manufacturer. Although some variability was observed across individual AFOs, the repeated measurements within each device were generally clustered relative to the separation between stiffness conditions, supporting the use of these measurements to characterize the experimental AFOs used in the study. This work identifies the need for a standardized method of AFO mechanical testing to consistently track material and joint response across AFO settings.

The preliminary results of this pilot study support the need for further research, and several limitations should be acknowledged. While our results demonstrate a potential contribution of powered KAFOs and AFOs on crouch reduction, the small sample size (n=3) limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions from the present study. Additionally, the heterogeneity of the CP population makes it difficult to identify consistent trends across the three participants. Marker-based motion capture of exoskeleton-assisted gait as presented in this pilot study is additionally complicated by potential misalignment between anatomical and KAFO knee and ankle joint centers, which can produce unintended interaction forces and errors in estimated joint motion [34]. However, despite the known limitations of motion capture paired with wearable devices, it remains the gold standard in the field for estimating joint kinematics. Future studies with larger sample sizes and a greater number of trials per condition may help improve the robustness of the results and reduce the influence of potential measurement errors.

Conclusions

This study examined gait biomechanics in three individuals with diparetic cerebral palsy using a powered KAFO paired with passive AFOs of varying stiffness. This pilot study provides preliminary insight into how passive ankle stiffness may influence the effectiveness of powered knee assistance during walking. We hypothesized that the AFO stiffness that best reduced crouch gait when paired with a powered KAFO would differ from the AFO configuration typically prescribed for walking without a powered orthosis.

Overall, the findings partially support this hypothesis. None of the KAFO conditions reduced mean stance ankle dorsiflexion or knee flexion below barefoot walking. However, among conditions using the powered KAFO, the highest-stiffness experimental AFO produced the lowest mean stance dorsiflexion and knee flexion angles, indicating the greatest reduction in crouch-related measures. All experimental AFO conditions also produced lower mean stance ankle dorsiflexion than the P-PreAFO condition. These preliminary findings suggest that the AFO configuration that best complements powered knee assistance may differ from a participant’s prescribed AFO and highlight the importance of considering the passive mechanical properties of AFOs when pairing them with powered KAFOs.

Future work should include an experiment with a larger sample size across a broader range of AFO stiffnesses to increase robustness of conclusions. Testing participants at standardized walking speeds, while collecting electromyographic and metabolic data, would also provide greater insight into muscle activity, potential compensation strategies, and gait efficiency across AFO configurations. Similar approaches may also be relevant for other populations with impaired knee control, including individuals with inclusion body myositis, spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, and knee osteoarthritis. Broader population inclusion criteria are therefore worth exploring in future studies.

Despite the limitations of this pilot study, these results provide a starting point for understanding how passive AFO stiffness affects walking with powered KAFO assistance. The findings suggest that tuning ankle stiffness may be an important design factor for improving knee biomechanics and reducing crouch gait during walking with a powered KAFO in people with diparetic CP.
