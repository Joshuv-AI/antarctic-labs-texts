Effects of refined management on medical quality, patient safety, and frontline staff’s perceived competence and job satisfaction: a single-center retrospective study in a psychiatric hospital
Authors: Yao Lu, Hao Jin, Jun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo retrospectively assess the comprehensive impact of transitioning from routine to refined quality management on medical quality, patient safety, and healthcare workers’ perceived competence and satisfaction within the same medical team.MethodsA retrospective self‑controlled before‑and‑after study was conducted in a single psychiatric hospital. A stable cohort of 90 healthcare workers was followed through two periods: the control period (January-December 2024) with routine quality management, and the intervention period (January-December 2025) with refined quality management. Data were extracted from hospital information systems, quality management archives, and specialized databases. Outcome measures included medical quality scores as measured by internally developed scales, patient safety events measured as annual incidence rates using denominator data from hospital systems, and healthcare workers’ self‑reported competence and satisfaction. Paired t‑tests were used for continuous scores, McNemar tests were applied for paired staff-level binary and ordinal data, while Pearson chi-square tests were used for patient safety incidence rates.ResultsCompared with the control period, the intervention period showed significant improvements across all domains. Scores for safety culture, management evaluation, and self‑assessed competence increased significantly (all P<0.001). All patient safety event rates decreased significantly (P<0.05), with adverse drug reactions and medical complaints showing the largest reductions (P<0.001). Overall satisfaction with the management model increased from 75.56% to 97.78% (P<0.001). ConclusionRefined quality management was associated with substantial improvements in healthcare workers’ perceptions, self‑assessed competence, patient safety, and overall satisfaction in this psychiatric hospital setting. Although limited by its retrospective, single‑center design, these findings suggest that refined quality management may serve as a promising strategy for improving hospital outcomes. Further validation through multicenter prospective studies is warranted to confirm the generalizability and sustainability of these effects.Keywords: Refined quality management, Medical quality, Patient safety, Medical staff satisfaction, Psychiatric hospital, Retrospective self-controlled study

Introduction

Medical quality and patient safety are core goals of healthcare system development and fundamental to improving hospital performance [1]. Value-based payment reforms oriented toward value creation challenge hospitals in terms of efficiency, cost management, and quality performance [2]. To adapt, establishing a scientific, refined, and efficient quality management system has become pressing. Traditional empirical management models, focusing on passive terminal quality indicators and fragmented improvements, lack process control, risk warning, and continuous optimization, failing to meet the requirements of modern hospital governance [3].

China’s public healthcare system operates under a three-tiered hospital classification, where tertiary hospitals represent the highest level of care delivery. Tertiary hospitals are expected to lead quality improvement initiatives, provide specialized services, and serve as regional referral centers. Against this backdrop, refined management has gradually integrated into medical management. Originating from modern enterprise management thought, it supports comprehensive process control and ongoing improvement of management activities through standardization, quantification, and digitalization [4]. Refined quality management represents a systematic operational framework that emphasizes fine-grained process control, real-time performance feedback and targeted task allocation at each management level. Within the domestic Chinese medical context, this framework is named refined quality management, which shares equivalent connotations with “meticulous management” or “fine-grained management” adopted in international research publications. It relies on standardized indicator systems, whole-process supervision, professional quality tools and regular performance analysis to generate measurable improvements in clinical service and organizational operation.

Unlike Total Quality Management, which emphasizes a culture of organization-wide continuous improvement, and Lean management, which focuses on waste elimination, refined quality management places greater emphasis on the stepwise decomposition of macro goals into executable and measurable micro-tasks, aiming to enhance efficiency and ensure safety and quality. Existing literature on refined quality management has largely focused on theoretical frameworks or single domains such as cost control or infection management [5, 6]. Recent studies have addressed these gaps. A hospital-level analysis of risk factors for patient safety events was reported in this journal [7]. Moreover, a mixed-methods study identified organizational factors underlying variation in quality management adoption across hospitals [8]. In addition, a retrospective cohort study in intensive care units showed that better quality assessment grades were associated with significantly fewer catheter-associated infections [9].

Psychiatric hospitals face unique challenges: patient complexity, behavioral unpredictability, poor treatment adherence, and high risks of violence, self-harm, and falls [10]. These features make conventional quality measures difficult to implement effectively. This study was conducted at Jinhua Second Hospital, a tertiary psychiatric hospital in Zhejiang Province, China. It is the leading psychiatric institution in the region and functions as the Jinhua Mental Health Center, with 1,000 beds across 15 wards. The hospital provides comprehensive psychiatric, psychological, and geriatric services, with psychiatry and geriatrics designated as key disciplines of Jinhua City. A study in a Chinese psychiatric hospital showed that systematic application of multidimensional quality management tools significantly improved adverse event reporting and fostered a non-punitive culture [11] Thus, refined management, with its emphasis on granular process control and real-time feedback, may offer particular utility within psychiatric care environments, given the high-risk and unpredictable nature of psychiatric settings. Examining its implementation in this setting provides a valuable, context-specific evidence base.

Several notable research gaps remain to be further explored. First, most published studies adopt cross-sectional designs or descriptive case reports, with few rigorous within-institution pre-post comparative analyses, which weakens the robustness of available evidence [12]. Second, evaluations often focus on hard clinical or economic outcomes while neglecting frontline workers’ work efficiency, professional growth, and subjective experience. Research shows that genuine leadership and organizational culture are core factors influencing quality improvement and patient satisfaction [13]. These findings suggest that fostering medical staff’s intrinsic motivation and innovative capacity may act as a key enabler for sustained, comprehensive rollout of clinical management frameworks. Finally, integrating refined management with China’s public hospital context, especially in psychiatric settings, requires more empirical real‑world research [14].

To address these research gaps, this study adopted a retrospective self‑controlled before and after design to examine the comprehensive effects observed in a stable clinical team after fully transitioning from a conventional quality management model to a refined management model. We focused not only on improvements in traditional core indicators such as medical quality and patient safety but also on the impact of this transition on healthcare workers’ comprehensive work ability and their satisfaction with the management system. Specifically, we sought to determine whether refined quality management could significantly improve medical quality scores and patient safety levels, whether it could significantly enhance healthcare workers’ self‑assessed competence and satisfaction, and whether these improvements were consistent across different dimensions. Through a holistic assessment from system efficiency to individual perception, this study aims to clarify the practical value and feasibility of refined management in a psychiatric hospital and to discuss its implications for hospital risk management and quality policy. Ultimately, we hope to provide a scientific basis and practical reference for specialized hospitals seeking to optimize management systems and enhance competitiveness.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study adopts a single-center, retrospective self-controlled before-and-after design. All participants were clinical medical staff at Jinhua Second Hospital who experienced the full shift from routine to refined quality management. The control period spanned January 1 to December 31, 2024, when the department operated under conventional quality management protocols. The intervention period covered January 1 to December 31, 2025, following the comprehensive rollout of refined quality management. To reduce confounding bias from inconsistent annual baseline conditions, we systematically compared 2024 and 2025 operational data, including total discharges, medical orders, infusion volumes, patient case mix, and staffing ratios. The full implementation schedule of refined management was as follows: institutional revision and team training in January 2025; Plan-Do-Check-Act (PDCA) cycles and real-time medical record audits launched in February 2025; full dynamic monitoring of all patient safety indicators launched in April 2025.

Inclusion criteria: (1) The research subjects must be registered medical staff of Jinhua Second Hospital, including practicing physicians and registered nurses. (2) The research subjects were clinical staff with relevant qualifications who maintained stable employment within the department and participated in the complete study cycle. (3) The research subjects were willing to participate in the department’s routine work, quality management activities, and related training and assessment. Exclusion criteria: (1) During the two periods covered by the study, from January 1, 2024 to December 31, 2025, medical staff who were on continuous leave, engaged in further studies, or left the clinical position for more than one month due to any reason. (2) Medical staff who left the target department during the study period due to resignation, retirement, or transfer. (3) Female medical staff who were pregnant or breastfeeding, as hospital occupational protection policies adjust their workload and participation in full-cycle quality management activities, which may affect work patterns and stability, and the study required complete data across both periods to ensure comparability.

The research team first identified 102 medical staff who were continuously on duty in both periods from the hospital’s information system as potential candidates. After applying the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 12 individuals were excluded. The reasons for exclusion were extended leave, departure from the department, or being in pregnancy or lactation period. Consequently, a fixed cohort of 90 medical staff was included in the final analysis. The study protocol has been reviewed by the Medical Ethics Committee of Jinhua Second Hospital (Approval No: jheylp2026-04-002). Given that the study is a retrospective design, it does not involve additional interventions and all data have been anonymized, the committee approved the exemption of informed consent. The entire research process strictly adheres to the principles of the Helsinki Declaration.

Routine quality management model

During the routine quality management period, the department adopted standardized basic quality control workflows. The core logic relied on compliance with existing institutional documents and regular periodic inspections. Specific daily measures covered standardized ward disinfection and ventilation, full aseptic operation specifications for all clinical procedures, and patient diagnosis and treatment implemented in accordance with national clinical guidelines. Conventional quality management work mainly included scheduled quality control meetings, random retrospective medical record spot-checks, and post-hoc summary discussions of adverse events after occurrence. All process adjustments and optimization plans were formulated passively based on existing rules and periodic inspection feedback.

Refined quality management model

During the refined management phase, the department built a multi-dimensional management framework covering organizational construction, clinical practice and supporting guarantee mechanisms. Its core was a goal-driven, data-supported operational cycle that split overall institutional strategic objectives into concrete, quantifiable and executable micro-tasks.

Firstly, we completed organizational restructuring and clarified tiered personnel responsibilities to guarantee stable implementation. A dedicated quality improvement working group consisting of all manuscript authors was established, and formal written responsibility specifications were formulated for clear role division. The department director took overall charge of the whole reform project; two head nurses were responsible for ward daily quality supervision and staff training organization; three physicians with quality management experience led PDCA cyclic improvement and medical record auditing; one pharmacy staff managed standardized psychiatric medication control; an infection control nurse tracked ward disinfection and adverse infection events.

Several authors of this manuscript held managerial positions within the hospital, with authority to revise internal rules, organize systematic training, and adjust monthly performance appraisal standards.This administrative power ensured sustained long-term executionof the refined management system and facilitated stable transformation of clinicalsafety culture among frontline staff. All group members received unifiedpre-intervention training to unify implementation standards and consistent cognition.

The intervention was carried out in three clear sequential phases with fixed training frequency and regular spot checks to secure implementation fidelity, and full training agenda details are described in the text without supporting attachments. The preparation stage fell in January 2025, lasting four weeks. All 90 participating staff joined two 3-hour offline group training sessions covering refined management theories, PDCA operation procedures, patient risk assessment, critical value disposal, psychiatric medication norms and three-tier medical record audit rules. Closed-book tests and on-site operation assessments were arranged after training, and only staff passing both evaluations could formally participate in the reform. Weekly group discussions were held to sort out outdated rules and draft updated standard operating procedures. The full launch phase ran from February to September 2025. Multiple reform modules were operated simultaneously with fixed supervision intensity: weekly PDCA weak-link analysis meetings, 48-hour real-time medical record defect feedback, monthly full-staff quality assessment linked to personal performance, daily dynamic patient risk assessment, and automatic indicator early warning via the information platform. Biweekly random on-site inspections were conducted to rectify non-standard behaviors and maintain high implementation compliance. Quantitative compliance judgment criteria were predefined before intervention launch. The stable maintenance stage covered October to December 2025. Regular monthly summary meetings were held to optimize workflows based on 8 months of practical data, while all training, inspection and assessment mechanisms remained consistent to realize cyclic sustained quality improvement.

We comprehensively revised internal institutional documents and set quantifiable annual quality improvement targets as core reform supports. Seven categories of updated standardized management rules were formulated: inpatient safety risk assessment workflow, critical value notification and disposal specifications, full-process psychiatric medication management, three-tier medical record audit standards, non-punitive adverse event reporting procedures, ward infection prevention and disinfection norms, and patient-centered communication guidelines. New unified criteria for medical records and individualized treatment plans required complete recording of patient risk grading, medication adjustment basis and regular psychological evaluation; incomplete or delayed records would be marked as quality defects and fed back in real time. Clear quantitative annual targets were issued to all staff: risk assessment completion rate ≥ 98%, timely critical value notification rate 100%, medical record qualification rate ≥ 99%, 50% growth in voluntary adverse event reports, and over 60% reduction in nosocomial infection incidence. All institutional revisions and quantitative targets were supervised through routine ward inspections and monthly performance evaluation linkage.

A complete set of multi-component clinical management measures was implemented in daily ward work, covering all reform dimensions raised by reviewers including organizational adjustment, systematic training, PDCA cyclic improvement, instant medical record review, real-time defect feedback, performance appraisal linkage, infection control, standardized medication management, dynamic risk screening and data platform monitoring. We first analyzed 2024 baseline data to identify persistent weak points such as prolonged average length of stay and incomplete pre-admission risk evaluation. PDCA cycles were adopted for each weak item to optimize clinical workflows and strengthen regular inspection of treatment plan integrity and timeliness. An independent medical record quality subgroup was set up to operate the three-tier audit system and deliver instant rectification notifications for all discovered documentation defects, with all audit results directly incorporated into monthly staff performance scores.

Multiple supporting service and data monitoring mechanisms were matched to the whole reform system. A unified standardized patient communication mode was promoted in all wards. Fixed daily disinfection schedules and tiered psychiatric drug dispensing, review and monitoring workflows were implemented for infection and medication safety control. A dedicated departmental quality indicator database was built to realize continuous tracking and monthly statistical analysis of core metrics including risk assessment completion and treatment compliance rates. The hospital information system supported automatic real-time early warning of unqualified indicators, sending instant reminders to corresponding managers for timely rectification. Each management task was decomposed to specific individuals with quantifiable evaluation benchmarks, realizing full accountability and complete traceability of all implementation outcomes.

No other hospital-wide large-scale quality improvement campaigns, unified training reforms or patient safety intervention projects were launched throughout 2025. All changes in medical quality and patient safety indicators within the observation period stemmed solely from this refined management reform, which excludes confounding interference from parallel institutional adjustments. This refined management system relies on standardized full workflows, data-driven decision-making and timely targeted problem rectification. Multi-dimensional collaborative measures transform long-term hospital strategic goals into measurable, sustained clinical quality gains, and continuously promote the internalization of patient safety awareness among all clinical staff.

Data collection

The data for this study were retrospectively extracted from multiple authenticated hospital business systems and unified annual specialized structured assessment archives following standardized data extraction forms. Data sources integrated the hospital information platform, complete electronic medical record database, adverse event real-time reporting system, human resource management system, and annual medical staff standardized assessment database to ensure full data integrity. These indicators were used to evaluate whether there were obvious structural differences in patient population and clinical activity between the two periods, reducing confounding bias caused by annual changes in clinical load and case composition. Two distinct analytical units were strictly separated throughout data collection and subsequent statistical analysis to eliminate confusion between staff-level questionnaire data and patient-level safety event data, as highlighted by the reviewer.

Basic clinical and operational baseline data of the ward in 2024 and 2025 were simultaneously collected for inter-year comparison, covering total annual admissions and discharges, constituent ratio of primary psychiatric diagnoses, grading distribution of patient disease severity, average length of stay, total medication orders, total intravenous infusion times, ward staffing ratio, and annual medication exposure characteristics.

Medical quality management and safety culture assessment indicatorsA standardized anonymous annual evaluation was conducted using the hospital-developed Multidimensional Medical Quality Management and Safety Culture Assessment Scale, organized uniformly by the hospital quality management department each year to guarantee authentic feedback without response pressure. This internal structured scale is designed to measure medical staff’s subjective recognition of institutional quality management frameworks and all daily clinical supervision links, with clear maximum scores specified for all subdimensions.The scale contains two major modules. The first module evaluates safety culture and operational processes, covering five independent subdimensions each scored out of 100 points: ① Cultural construction, covering team identity and continuous learning atmosphere; ② Reward and punishment mechanism, assessing assessment fairness and incentive effectiveness; ③ Communication and coordination, evaluating cross-department collaboration and information transmission efficiency; ④ Environmental hygiene, measuring ward cleanliness and functional layout rationality; ⑤ Adverse event reporting mechanism, reflecting reporting convenience and non-punitive reporting atmosphere. Participants self-rate each item, with higher total scores representing more positive perceptions of the management environment.The second module assesses core quality management implementation, also divided into three subdimensions with a full score of 100 per section: ① Medical safety management evaluation; ② Medical training management evaluation; ③ Ward hygiene and infection control supervision evaluation. All items adopt a direct 0–100 scoring method. The scale demonstrated acceptable internal consistency with a Cronbach’s α coefficient of 0.85. Five senior hospital quality management experts completed full content validation; all individual items with a content validity index (CVI) below 0.80 were revised or removed prior to formal use, resulting in a final scale-level CVI of 0.82.

Full indicator definitions and formulas are listed below: ① Hospital-acquired infection rate: Number of new confirmed nosocomial infection cases (diagnosed per national Hospital Infection Diagnosis Standards (Trial) [15])/ total annual discharged patients × 100%; ② Adverse drug reaction (ADR) rate: Pharmacy-verified ADR cases submitted to the national adverse drug monitoring platform / total departmental medical orders × 100%; ③ Adverse infusion reaction rate: Confirmed phlebitis, extravasation and other infusion-related adverse events / total annual intravenous infusion procedures × 100%; ④ 30-day unplanned psychiatric readmission rate: Patients readmitted within 30 days due to primary mental illness relapse or complications / total discharged patients × 100%; ⑤ Formal written medical complaint rate: Officially filed patient complaints recorded by medical affairs / total discharged patients × 100%, this denominator is appropriate as complaints pertain to inpatient care experiences during hospitalization, and discharge volume represents the population at risk for filing complaints related to their inpatient stay; ⑥ Inpatient self-harm incidence rate: Confirmed intentional self-harm episodes during hospitalization / total discharged patients × 100%; ⑦ Physical restraint complication rate: Patients developing bruising, pressure injury or aggravated anxiety following compulsory restraint / total patients receiving physical restraint intervention × 100%. Lower percentage values correspond to improved patient safety performance. As the reviewer highlighted, physical restraint is permitted under standardized clinical protocols for high-risk psychiatric patients in China, and restraint-associated adverse outcomes are routinely tracked within our hospital’s safety monitoring system.

Quality control

To ensure reliability, accuracy, and privacy security, this study implemented strict quality control and data management procedures. All core research indicators followed the norms issued by the National Health Commission of China or recognized industry standards to ensure consistency of measurement. Raw data were sourced from official hospital information systems, including electronic medical records, adverse event reporting systems, and human resource management systems. Data collection was independently performed by two trained researchers, who extracted target data and entered them into a dedicated database, followed by immediate cross‑checking. Discrepancies between the two researchers were resolved by a third senior researcher reviewing the original data source. All personal identifiers were anonymized and replaced with unique research numbers to protect privacy. Logical and range checks were conducted on key variables before data analysis to identify possible outliers or logical errors. The final confirmed database was managed by the principal researcher and stored on an encrypted, controlled server within the hospital, accessible only to authorized project members with specified permissions. The research team regularly reviewed the completeness and consistency of the data throughout the study period.

Potential sources of bias

Multiple potential bias sources are acknowledged in this retrospective self-controlled design. First, the single-center research setting limits generalizability to general hospitals or other types of specialized medical institutions. Second, although fixed individual staff characteristics are controlled via paired comparison, temporal annual trends, external hospital policy adjustments and parallel quality improvement interventions cannot be completely excluded as confounding factors. Third, all staff scale outcomes rely on self-reported subjective evaluation, which may generate social desirability bias and ceiling effects after intervention. Fourth, the optimized non-punitive adverse event reporting culture under refined management may change staff’s willingness to report minor safety incidents, affecting the comparability of patient safety event rates between the two observation years. Fifth, the two internally developed assessment scales lack cross-hospital external validation, which reduces direct comparability with research adopting internationally mature measurement instruments.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Missing data were handled using complete case analysis. Only participants with complete data for all outcome measures across both periods were included. All 90 participants had complete paired data for every outcome measure, so the cohort was identical across all indicators and no per-outcome exclusion was applied. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. Quantitative data with a normal distribution were expressed as mean [95% confidence interval (CI)], and comparisons were conducted using paired sample t-tests. Non-normally distributed data are expressed as median with interquartile range. For these variables, comparisons between groups were conducted using Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical data are presented as numbers and percentages (n, %). All staff questionnaire and satisfaction indicators were measured at the individual healthcare worker level with paired data from the same 90 participants, for which McNemar test and McNemar‑Bowker test were appropriate. In contrast, patient safety incidence outcomes were aggregated annual event data calculated using patient/admission/procedure-based denominators independent of staff count; accordingly, Pearson chi-square tests were used to compare cross-year safety rates instead of McNemar test to avoid mismatched analytical units. To quantify the magnitude of the intervention effects, effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were calculated for continuous variables, with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 considered small, medium, and large effects, respectively. All statistical analyses were two-sided tests, with a P-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Disscussion

Refined quality management is a core strategy for hospitals to address quality and safety challenges. Widespread structural challenges exist in process standardization, personnel capabilities, and information systems across nursing and medical quality [16]. To address these, our hospital systematically implemented refined quality management reforms. This single-center retrospective self-controlled study demonstrated that systematic implementation of refined management was associated with comprehensive positive changes across multiple outcomes within the same medical team. These improvements are not accidental; the fundamental driving force lies in the structured closed-loop management reforms implemented. Nevertheless, these encouraging findings should be interpreted within the context of the study’s design.

The establishment of a data‑driven monitoring and feedback system based on standardized quality indicators was a distinct feature of our approach, consistent with the consensus that clear and measurable indicators are the cornerstone for improvement [17]. By decomposing macro goals into measurable specific indicators and constructing a dynamic monitoring platform, we achieved automatic data capture and early warnings [18]. This approach verified the effectiveness of refined management in enhancing organizational performance [19]. The effect sizes observed were large: Cohen’s d values for all management perception and competence dimensions ranged from 1.93 to 5.56, far exceeding the threshold for a large effect (d > 0.8). These results demonstrate that the improvements were not only statistically significant but also of substantial practical magnitude. However, several study‑specific factors suggest these effect sizes may be overestimated. First, ceiling effects were present because post‑intervention scores for safety culture approached 95 to 97 out of 100, with standard deviations shrinking by nearly half, which mechanically inflates Cohen’s d. Second, self-report bias may have been amplified by the organizational context. Staff knew the survey was part of the quality evaluation and may have reported more favorably due to social desirability bias. Third, the absence of an external control group means improvements cannot be separated from temporal trends or the Hawthorne effect, where performance improves simply because staff are aware of being observed. Therefore, while the improvements are meaningful, these effect sizes should be viewed as upper‑bound estimates.

Implementation and in‑depth execution of management measures were crucial for achieving significant results. Hospitals differ significantly in adopting quality management practices, and our success may be attributed to structured implementation strategies. By establishing a specialized quality management team, defining clear responsibilities, and providing systematic training, we ensured unified management concepts. Middle‑level clinical managers, including nursing department heads and medical affairs supervisors, played key roles in design, communication, and supervision [12]. Our observations are consistent with findings that total quality management requires leadership from middle management to drive and implement [20]. Effective leadership is also a critical driver of patient safety culture, as shown in a recent mediation analysis [21]. Such structured integration is crucial for enhancing employee engagement and sustaining practice improvements [22].

Patient safety indicators decreased across all measured domains. Reductions in adverse drug reactions and hospital‑acquired infections were directly related to specific interventions such as standardized medication storage, computerized prescription review, and strict aseptic protocols. These findings align with previous research demonstrating the effectiveness of similar measures [23]. Notably, establishing a non‑punitive reporting process with immediate feedback significantly improved scores in this dimension, which may be the cultural foundation for early disclosure of safety incidents and systematic reduction [24]. Beyond these conventional metrics, we also observed reductions in psychiatric-specific adverse events, including inpatient self-harm incidents and physical restraint-related complications, suggesting that the refined management approach addressed risks unique to the psychiatric inpatient setting. Although these patient safety outcomes are encouraging, the single-center retrospective design limits causal attribution, and concurrent temporal trends or policy changes could have contributed to the observed reductions.

While patient dignity and treatment preferences are inherently difficult to quantify in a retrospective design, we consider physical restraint rates and self-harm incidence as proxy indicators for dignity-related safety outcomes. Reduced restraint use suggests greater respect for patient autonomy and less restrictive care, while decreased self-harm events reflect improved patient safety and well-being, both of which are closely linked to patient dignity. These improvements indicate that the refined management model not only enhanced clinical safety but also contributed to more patient-centered, dignity-preserving care practices in the psychiatric inpatient setting. Future prospective studies should incorporate direct measures of patient dignity and preference, such as patient-reported experience measures and shared decision-making participation rates, to capture these dimensions more accurately.

The improvement in medical staff’s perceptions of safety culture and self-assessed competence reflects a comprehensive psychological effect. When employees observe that the management system becomes clearer and fairer due to its refinement, and see that the management investment has actually brought about the optimization of the working environment, their trust and efficacy towards the organization will increase. This interpretation is supported by the observed improvements in satisfaction scores, which rose from 75.56% to 97.78%, and by the substantial gains in perceived fairness and organizational support. The theory of total quality management suggests that effective quality management practices can enhance the organization’s image and sense of support, thereby improving employee satisfaction [25]. Our results are consistent with evidence that perceived organizational justice significantly improves employee satisfaction in healthcare settings [26]. Moreover, engaged staff are a critical driver of patient safety culture [27]. Nevertheless, because these improvements are based on self‑reported measures, the possibility of response bias cannot be ruled out when interpreting these results. The overall satisfaction rate increased from 75.56% to 97.78%, indicating that for every five staff members, one additional staff member became satisfied after the intervention.

The improvements across all dimensions collectively point to the synergistic value of the refined quality management model. This model integrates a series of multi-level measures, including institutional reconfiguration, process reengineering, data empowerment, and systematic reinforcement of accountability. This not only optimizes specific matters such as medical record quality and clinical workflow efficiency, but also empowers medical staff and ensures patient safety, achieving a transformation from strategic goals to the internalization of clinical quality and safety culture. It is worth noting that the data foundation and process standardization strengthened by this model are creating conditions for the intelligent advancement of the management system. Integrating artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies to build a clinical decision support and quality control platform with predictive and warning capabilities has become a clear frontier direction [28].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center study conducted in a specialized psychiatric hospital, the generalizability of our findings to other types of healthcare institutions or different regional contexts requires further validation. Second, although a self-controlled before-and-after design was employed to control for individual-level confounding, this study remains retrospective in nature, and it is impossible to completely eliminate the potential influence of historical events or concurrent policy changes during the study period. Third, the refined quality management intervention was multi‑component, encompassing changes in training, supervision, feedback mechanisms, and process standardization. Consequently, it is difficult to isolate which specific element or combination of elements contributed most to the observed improvements. Fourth, while we reported comprehensive psychometric properties for the internally developed scales, the subjective nature of self-reported assessments may still introduce measurement bias, and the large Cohen’s d effect sizes should be interpreted cautiously given the absence of external scale validation and potential social desirability response bias. The lack of an independent control group also weakens causal inference. Fifth, we adopted complete case analysis to maintain a unified analytical cohort across all indicators, which discarded partial valid paired data and may introduce selection bias, and we excluded pregnant and breastfeeding on-duty female staff with adjusted workloads inconsistent with full intervention exposure, slightly narrowing sample representativeness. Sixth, the downward trend of recorded adverse drug reactions cannot be simply regarded as a true reduction in actual adverse events, as changes in detection intensity and staff voluntary reporting willingness may interfere with statistical results; despite the non-punitive reporting culture promoted by the intervention, unified event screening and recording standards were implemented in both periods to reduce deviation. Besides, readmission and complaint rates are vulnerable to confounding factors including annual patient case mix, total patient volume and discharge management modes, the relatively short follow-up period (12 months) limits our ability to assess the long-term sustainability of the observed effects. Seventh, this study focused primarily on quantitative outcomes, and the underlying mechanisms through which refined management influences staff behavior and organizational culture were not directly explored.

Based on these limitations, several directions for future research are warranted. First, multi-center, prospective studies with extended follow-up periods are needed to validate the generalizability and long-term sustainability of the refined management model across diverse healthcare settings. Second, mixed-method approaches integrating qualitative interviews or focus groups should be employed to explore the behavioral and psychological mechanisms, such as changes in communication patterns, perceived organizational support, and professional identity, through which refined management translates into improved staff performance and patient outcomes. Third, future research could examine differential responses to management changes among medical staff with varying professional backgrounds, tenure, or hierarchical positions, as well as the moderating roles of leadership styles and organizational culture. Fourth, leveraging the data infrastructure established in this study, artificial intelligence and machine learning techniques could be applied to develop predictive models for early identification of safety risks and to enable real-time, personalized decision support. Such advancements would not only enhance the efficiency and intelligence of refined quality management systems but also provide actionable evidence for hospital risk management and quality policy formulation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this retrospective self-controlled study demonstrated that the implementation of refined quality management was accompanied by marked improvements in patient safety indicators, medical record quality, and psychiatric clinical staff’s safety culture awareness and professional competence. Several methodological limitations restrict causal interpretation of the results, including its single-center retrospective design without an external control group, outcome evaluation dependent on hospital-developed self-report scales with inherent social desirability bias, complete case analysis-induced selection bias, and exclusion of pregnant and breastfeeding on-duty staff that slightly reduced sample representativeness. The large observed Cohen’s d effect sizes should therefore be interpreted prudently, and definitive causal links between the management model and outcome improvements cannot be established. Overall, our findings preliminarily support the value of refined multi-dimensional quality management in psychiatric wards, yet multi-center prospective studies with extended follow-up periods and standardized externally validated assessment tools are required to replicate and verify these results. Subsequent research can further adopt mixed qualitative and quantitative designs to unpack the internal mechanisms of quality culture change and combine hospital big data to build intelligent risk early warning systems, so as to provide more robust evidence for the widespread promotion of this refined management model.
