Evidence Summary for Ultrasound‐Guided Peripheral Arterial Catheter Placement in Adult Critical Care Patients
Authors: Ting Shi, Suqin Han, Jiali Huang, Shuting Hong, Jing Su, Hong Zhang, Xueqin Huang, Jinhua Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsUltrasound guidance can improve the success and safety of peripheral arterial catheter placement in critically ill adult patients; however, existing evidence is scattered and lacks a graded, clinically actionable synthesis. This evidence summary aimed to systematically retrieve, evaluate, and synthesize evidence on ultrasound‐guided peripheral arterial catheter placement in critically ill adult patients, in order to inform evidence‐based nursing practice.MethodsThis evidence summary was conducted following the methodological framework recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for evidence synthesis. A top‐down search was performed based on the “6S” evidence pyramid model to identify relevant clinical guidelines, systematic reviews, and randomized controlled trials. Twenty databases and resources including UpToDate, Embase, and PubMed were systematically searched from inception until August 31, 2025.ResultsA total of 20 publications were included: 3 clinical guidelines, 12 systematic reviews, and 5 randomized controlled trials. From these, 29 discrete evidence recommendations were extracted and synthesized, covering six key aspects: puncture site selection, catheterization procedures and recommendations, comparison of puncture approaches, ultrasound imaging and probe techniques, ultrasound equipment and auxiliary tools, and training.ConclusionThis evidence summary provides 29 graded recommendations for ultrasound‐guided peripheral arterial catheter placement in critically ill adult patients. Implementing these recommendations can increase first‐attempt success, decrease complications, and improve patient safety. Adoption should be tailored to local equipment availability and clinical context, while adhering to core evidence‐based principles.Trial and Protocol RegistrationThis study was registered with the Center for Evidence‐Based Nursing at Fudan University (Registration No. ES20245973). URL: http://ebn.nursing.fudan.edu.cn/home.

Introduction

The peripheral arterial catheter functions as a core tool for hemodynamic monitoring in the intensive care unit (ICU), and plays a pivotal role in circulatory support for critically ill adult patients through continuous arterial pressure monitoring and convenient blood sampling [1]. For the purpose of this evidence summary, “critically ill adult patients” refers to adult patients (≥ 18 years) requiring ICU admission for monitoring and/or treatment of life‐threatening conditions. Studies indicate that invasive arterial catheter placement is one of the most frequently performed procedures in intensive care settings [2, 3, 4], underscoring its indispensable role in critical care management. With the growing severity of illness among ICU patients and the increasing demand for precise hemodynamic monitoring, the frequency of peripheral arterial catheter placement has continued to rise. However, traditional palpation‐guided techniques exhibit significant limitations in clinical practice. These methods demonstrate lower success rates in patients with obesity, weak arterial pulses, or aberrant vascular anatomy [5] and are associated with increased risks of complications, including catheter‐related bloodstream infection and local hematoma [6]. These technical limitations and safety concerns can directly undermine the timeliness and reliability of hemodynamic monitoring in critically ill adult patients.

In response, ultrasound‐guided vascular access has emerged as a transformative approach. Compared with conventional landmark techniques, ultrasound guidance significantly improves first‐attempt success rates, reduces the number of required attempts, shortens procedural time, and decreases complication rates [7, 8]. Current evidence‐based guidelines strongly advocate for the routine implementation of ultrasound guidance during vascular access procedures, emphasizing its crucial roles in pre‐cannulation vessel assessment, real‐time needle visualization, and early identification of procedural complications [9].

Despite the availability of clinical guidelines, translating these broad recommendations into actionable practice remains challenging for bedside nurses in critical care settings [10]. Existing guidelines typically address vascular access for heterogeneous patient populations across multiple clinical contexts (e.g., operating rooms, interventional suites, and general wards) [9, 11], and do not specifically focus on the unique clinical demands of critically ill adult patients in the ICU. These patients often present with hemodynamic instability, peripheral vasoconstriction, edema, or altered vascular anatomy, which may render standard procedural recommendations insufficient or inapplicable [7]. Furthermore, primary evidence and systematic reviews on this topic are dispersed across multiple sources with varying levels of recommendation grades, and clinical practitioners may find it difficult to rapidly identify graded, directly applicable practice recommendations during busy ICU workflows. A consolidated, critically appraised evidence summary that is specifically oriented to ICU nursing practice—extracting, grading, and synthesizing recommendations from existing high‐quality sources into clearly actionable items—would therefore facilitate the implementation of evidence‐based practice in this setting.

This evidence summary aimed to: (1) systematically retrieve, evaluate, and synthesize available evidence on ultrasound‐guided peripheral arterial catheter placement in critically ill adult patients; (2) provide a reference for clinical nursing practice to improve procedural safety and success; and (3) support the standardization of evidence‐based ultrasound‐guided techniques in critical care settings.

Methods

Currently, reporting specifications for evidence summary is still lacking. This evidence summary was conducted following the methodological framework recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) for best evidence summaries and the Evidence Summary Reporting Standards of the Fudan University Center for Evidence‐Based Nursing [12]. The reporting specifications include problem establishment, literature retrieval, literature screening, literature evaluation, the summary and grading of evidence and the formation of practical suggestions. The study was registered with the Fudan University Center for Evidence‐Based Nursing (Registration No. ES20245973; URL: http://ebn.nursing.fudan.edu.cn/home).

Establishment of the Problem

The evidence‐based question was structured according to the PIPOST model [13], with the following components: P (target population for evidence application): Critically ill adult patients; I (intervention): Ultrasound‐guided peripheral arterial catheter placement; P (professionals applying the evidence): Physicians and nurses; O (outcome): Peripheral arterial catheter placement success rate, incidence of catheter‐related complications, etc.; S (place of applying the evidence): ICU; T (type of evidence): Guidelines, clinical decision‐making tools, evidence summaries, expert consensus statements, systematic reviews, and randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

Evidence Sources and Retrieval Strategies

Following the “6S” evidence pyramid model [14], we systematically searched the literature from top‐tier evidence sources downward. Twenty databases and official websites were comprehensively searched, including UpToDate, BMJ Best Practice, Cochrane Library, Guidelines International Network (GIN), Registered Nurses' Association of Ontario (RNAO), Society of Critical Care Medicine (SCCM), Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network (SIGN), Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), German Medical Society (GMS), National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), Embase, PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Yimaitong Guidelines Network, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM), Wanfang Database, VIP Database, and Chinese Medical Association journals.

The search was conducted using subject terms and free‐text words, with the search terms being “Ultrasonography/Echotomography/Ultrasonic Imaging/Imaging, Ultrasonic/Sonography, Medical/Medical Sonography/Ultrasound Imaging/Imaging*, Ultrasound/Ultrasonographic Imaging*/Imaging*, Ultrasonographic/Diagnostic Ultrasound*/Ultrasound*, Diagnostic/Tomography, Ultrasonic/Ultrasonic Tomography/Diagnosis, Ultrasonic/Diagnoses, Ultrasonic/Ultrasonic Diagnoses/Ultrasonic Diagnosis/B‐mode ultrasonography/Ultrasound guidance/Ultrasound‐guided/Ultrasonic examination/Color Doppler ultrasound/Ultrasonography, Doppler” and “Catheterization, Peripheral/peripheral arterial catheterization/Peripheral Catheterization*/Catheterization*, Peripheral Arterial/Peripheral Arterial Catheterization*/Arterial Catheterization*, Peripheral/arterial cannulation/Arterial catheter*/indwelling arterial catheter*/Peripheral arterial duct/Peripheral artery/arterial cannula/Invasive arterial conduit.” The search period was from the establishment of each database to August 31, 2025. Study Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The study inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Guidelines, clinical decision tools, evidence summaries, expert consensus statements, systematic reviews and RCTs related to ultrasound‐guided peripheral arterial catheter placement in critically ill adult patients. (2) Publications limited to the Chinese and English languages.

The study exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Incomplete literature (e.g., conference abstracts, protocols without results, or retracted articles). (2) Full‐text studies lacking extractable data for evidence synthesis, even after attempting to contact the corresponding authors. (3) Non‐evidence‐based personal monographs. (4) Studies deemed to be of low quality based on methodological quality assessment (e.g., guidelines scoring < 30% in three or more AGREE II domains, or original studies meeting less than 60% of JBI checklist criteria).

Data Extraction

The retrieved literature was imported into Zotero and duplicates were removed. Two researchers trained in evidence‐based methods independently screened the literature. Initial screening was conducted by reviewing titles, abstracts, and keywords, followed by full‐text review for re‐screening. The quality of the included literature was assessed. During the screening process, if multiple articles with identical titles by the same author were identified, only the most recent publication was retained. Similarly, for studies with closely related titles by the same author, the most recent and relevant publication was selected. Any discrepancies between the researchers were resolved through discussion, with arbitration by a third reviewer when necessary. Literature Quality Evaluation

Literature Quality Evaluation Process

The quality evaluation utilizing the aforementioned instruments (AGREE II for guidelines and JBI critical appraisal checklists for individual studies) was independently conducted by three researchers trained in evidence‐based methodology. All researchers had completed systematic training in evidence‐based research methods. In case of disagreement, discrepancies were resolved through group discussion until consensus was reached.

Literature Quality Evaluation Method

Evaluation of guidelines: Guidelines were assessed using the updated AGREE II instrument [15]. The tool comprises six domains and 23 items, with each item rated on a 7‐point scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Domain scores were standardized using the formula: (obtained score − minimum possible score)/(maximum possible score − minimum possible score) × 100%. Recommendations were classified into three levels: Grade A (all six domains ≥ 60%), Grade B (at least three domains ≥ 30% and at least one domain < 60%), and Grade C (three or more domains < 30%). Literature rated as Grade C was excluded from this study.

Evaluation of systematic reviews: Systematic reviews were evaluated using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Systematic Reviews [16], which consists of 11 items rated as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable.”

Evaluation of randomized controlled trials: RCTs were appraised using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for RCTs [16], comprising 13 items with the same response options: “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable.”

Evaluation of expert consensus articles: Expert consensus articles were assessed using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Text and Opinion Papers [16], which consists of six items rated as “Yes,” “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable.”

Evaluation of evidence summaries and clinical decision support tools: Evidence summaries and clinical decision support tools were not directly evaluated due to the lack of standardized appraisal tools [17]. Instead, their original source evidence was traced and assessed according to the relevant study type. In cases of conflicting evidence, priority was given to higher‐quality, more recent, and better‐supported evidence.

Evidence Summaries, Classifications and Recommendation Levels

Literature meeting the inclusion criteria was subjected to evidence extraction, translation, back‐translation, synthesis, and classification in accordance with standardized methodological principles: (1) Identical evidence from multiple sources was consolidated; (2) In cases of conflicting evidence, priority was given to the higher‐quality evidence; (3) For contemporaneous publications, the most authoritative and earliest published source was chosen. Discrepancies were resolved through researcher discussion, with arbitration by a third independent reviewer when necessary.

According to the 2014 edition of the JBI Levels of Evidence and Grades of Recommendation from the JBI [16], the evidence was categorized into five levels, with the strength of evidence decreasing from Level 1 to Level 5. Level 1 represents the highest quality of evidence, while Level 5 indicates the lowest. Based on the JBI FAME framework (Feasibility, Appropriateness, Meaningfulness, and Effectiveness) [16], two researchers trained in evidence‐based nursing independently rated the strength of the recommendations. This process was conducted in strict accordance with JBI guidelines for grading recommendation strength, taking into account the validity, feasibility, appropriateness, and clinical significance of the evidence. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through arbitration by a third researcher. Expert consensus meetings were held to evaluate the evidence, resulting in a final classification of recommendations as either Grade A (strong recommendation) or Grade B (weak recommendation).

Discussion

Anatomical and Procedural Rationale for Technical Optimization

Based on Evidence 1–9, a thorough analysis was conducted regarding puncture site selection and standardized catheterization procedures. The area between the radial styloid process and the distal one‐third of the forearm is the preferred site for radial artery catheter placement [32], offering significant anatomical advantages: consistent arterial diameter and thin subcutaneous tissue, which effectively reduce the risk of vessel perforation. The guideline [9] recommends thoroughly assessing vascular status before catheterization, selecting the artery with the largest diameter for puncture, and employing real‐time ultrasound guidance. This aligns with the findings of Li et al. [36], who reported a positive correlation between vessel diameter and successful puncture rates. A guideline [11] emphasizes that a complete ultrasound‐guided peripheral arterial catheter placement procedure should include four key steps: pre‐procedural ultrasound assessment, real‐time guided puncture, confirmation of catheter position, and complication assessment. Strict adherence to aseptic technique and precise puncture site selection are critical prerequisites for ensuring procedural safety [11, 18].

For pre‐procedural evaluation, the guideline [11] recommends using duplex ultrasound with color Doppler to perform a modified Allen's test, assessing collateral flow through the ulnar artery. During radial artery puncture, stabilizing the hand holding the ultrasound probe on the bed surface can improve operational stability [35]. An operational sequence of “moving the probe → advancing the needle → adjusting needle direction” is suggested [27], which helps reduce the incidence of complications.

Regarding puncture approach selection, Evidence 10–15 provides important guidance. Although the long‐axis in‐plane (LAIP) technique yields superior overall performance [11], studies [19, 21, 23, 26] indicate that for patients with difficult catheterizations, a modified short‐axis out‐of‐plane approach combined with dynamic needle tip positioning or an oblique‐axis approach may be more suitable. This study also found that in dorsal pedal artery catheterization, the short‐axis out‐of‐plane technique can reduce evaluation time [37]. This discrepancy may be attributed to the thick subcutaneous tissue and anatomical variations of the dorsal pedal artery, underscoring the need to tailor the guidance strategy according to the specific anatomical site. Furthermore, a study [31] reported that a bevel‐down approach under short‐axis view guidance resulted in higher success rates. These findings offer diverse technical options for clinical practice, further highlighting the importance of individualized operational approaches.

Clinical Value of Ultrasound Technology and Equipment Selection

The core clinical value of ultrasound‐guided techniques in peripheral arterial catheter placement for critically ill adult patients lies in the simultaneous improvement of procedural precision and safety. Evidence 16–25 systematically delineate the clinical application value of ultrasound technology and further reveals the complex interactive relationship among the device, the operator, and the patient. Regarding image optimization, ultrasound guidance with a double‐line marker combined with two‐dimensional (2D) ultrasound can significantly improve puncture accuracy [20, 34]. The guideline [9] indicates that long‐axis imaging is particularly effective for identifying vascular abnormalities, while oblique‐axis imaging can provide more comprehensive structural information. Concerning probe technique, maintaining a 45° probe insertion angle is recommended [33], and the use of a high‐frequency linear array probe is advised for optimal imaging quality [24].

In terms of equipment selection, Evidence 19–21 [18, 22, 25, 30] confirm that high‐frequency probes (5–15 MHz) used with real‐time 2D ultrasound or B‐mode ultrasound can significantly increase the first‐attempt success rate. The study by Mesa et al. [38] shows that novices performing peripheral vascular access procedures under ultrasound guidance achieved higher success rates and accuracy when using real‐time ultrasound‐guided catheterization techniques. It is also recommended that novices may use needle guides to assist the procedure and should position the ultrasound screen within their direct line of sight [18].

However, the relatively slow equipment upgrade rate in primary hospitals may lead to technological disparities that hinder the widespread adoption of these techniques. Regarding resource allocation, we propose a “Four‐Tier Technical Promotion Model”: ① Basic Tier (Portable ultrasound + Out‐of‐plane technique), ② Standard Tier (High‐frequency probe + In‐plane technique), ③ Advanced Tier (Double‐line marker + Dynamic needle tracking), ④ Intelligent Tier (AI‐assisted puncture). Medical institutions should select appropriate ultrasound devices and auxiliary tools based on their specific conditions and strengthen technical training for healthcare staff to enhance the application effectiveness of ultrasound technology. The evidence synthesis provided here aligns with established guidelines [9, 11] while bridging the gap between high‐level recommendations and practical ICU execution. Our proposed “Four‐Tier Technical Promotion Model” is intended as a clinical implementation framework rather than a replacement for primary evidence.

Training System and the “Three‐Dimensional Competency Enhancement Model”

Evidence 26–28 defines the specific components of the training curriculum. Operators should possess professional qualifications, and the procedure is recommended to be performed by senior nurses or practitioners with specialized training [28]. It is emphasized that training in ultrasound‐guided peripheral arterial catheter placement must integrate both cognitive and technical competencies, and should include group lectures, laboratory sessions, and a clinical practicum phase [11, 39].

Based on this, we propose the establishment of a “Three‐Dimensional Competency Enhancement Model”: ① Cognitive Training: Implement a progressive training structure of micro‐lectures → case libraries → workshops for the systematic study of ultrasound principles and operational protocols; ② Technical Skill Practicum: Leverage high‐fidelity vascular phantoms for plane conversion training, with a focus on probe manipulation, puncture approach selection, and strict adherence to aseptic technique; ③ Behavioral Reinforcement: Optimize the operational workflow through PDCA (Plan‐Do‐Check‐Act) cycles, with a cycle duration of ≤ 8 weeks, involving practice on real patient cases and continuous quality improvement. Research [40] has shown that Virtual Reality (VR) and Augmented Reality (AR) technologies can provide healthcare professionals with a more realistic and immersive training experience, thereby improving training outcomes and opening new pathways for the widespread adoption of this technique.

Limitations

While this evidence summary provides a comprehensive synthesis of the evidence regarding ultrasound‐guided peripheral arterial catheter placement in critically ill adult patients, certain limitations should be acknowledged. Regarding the inclusion of non‐English/Chinese literature, while our search focus remained on these two languages to ensure accuracy in appraisal, we acknowledge this as a limitation that may overlook regional practices in other language‐dominant systems. Second, the high proportion of evidence derived from international studies necessitates caution when generalizing findings, due to potential differences in healthcare settings, resource availability, and patient characteristics across regions. Third, there remains a relative paucity of high‐quality evidence specifically targeting ultrasound‐guided peripheral arterial catheter placement. Future research should aim to continuously update this evidence base and prioritize the selection and implementation of recommendations based on their feasibility, appropriateness, meaningfulness, and effectiveness to facilitate translation into clinical practice.

Conclusions

This study systematically summarizes 29 evidence‐based recommendations for ultrasound‐guided peripheral arterial catheter placement in critically ill adult patients, covering six key aspects: puncture site selection, catheterization procedures and recommendations, comparison of puncture approaches, ultrasound imaging and probe techniques, ultrasound equipment and auxiliary tools, and training. These findings offer an evidence base for the standardization of clinical practice. However, notable variations exist in infrastructure and the extent of evidence implementation across different healthcare institutions. Therefore, in clinical practice, it is recommended to carefully evaluate the feasibility of evidence recommendations by considering departmental equipment levels, individualized patient needs, and the local clinical context. Corresponding targeted optimization of operational protocols should be conducted to enhance catheterization safety and nursing quality. Future research should be grounded in evidence‐translation methodologies, integrating specific clinical settings and patient population characteristics to promote the precise translation and contextual application of evidence into practice.
