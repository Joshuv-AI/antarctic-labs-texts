Outcomes of 72-hour emergency department return visits requiring hospital admission in older adults: a nationally representative analysis
Authors: Peter A D Steel, David Hancock, Jin H. Han, Eli Madden, Michael Stern, Arindam RoyChoudhury, Adam Wright, Jessica S Ancker, Yiye Zhang, Anthony Rosen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder adult Emergency department disposition decisions are uniquely challenging. Providers must balance the risk of admission (delirium, deconditioning, iatrogenic complications, cost) with the risk of discharging evolving illness leading to potentially worse outcomes. National-level data on return visit admissions among older adults (RVAs)—hospitalizations occurring within 72 hours of an emergency department (ED) treat-and-release visit— and their outcomes in this population remain limited.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey (NHAMCS), analyzing data from 1/1/2007–12/31/2022. We included ED visits for adults aged ≥65 resulting in hospital admission and excluded direct admissions to procedural or intensive care units. RVAs were defined as hospital admissions preceded by a treat-and-release ED visit at the same facility within 72 hours. Comparator group comprised index ED admissions to general inpatient floor or ward units, excluding procedural and critical care destinations. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes included hospital length of stay and nursing home placement. We performed multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for age, sex, race, Emergency Severity Index (ESI), and comorbid conditions. Adjusted odds ratio (OR) and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were reported. ResultsAmong the sampled visits representing 3,375,062 national admissions, 206,211 (6.1%) were RVAs. In-hospital mortality was 4.0% for RVAs vs 2.3% for index visit admissions (p=0.082). After adjustment, RVA was an independent predictor of in-hospital death (aOR 2.72; p=0.028). No significant differences were seen in hospital length of stay or new nursing home placement. The five most common diagnoses in RVAs were infectious: sepsis, pneumonia, COVID-19, cellulitis, and urinary tract infection.ConclusionsIn a nationally representative sample of older adult patients, 72-hour RVA were associated with higher in-hospital mortality compared to index visit admissions to the floor. Although this difference was not statistically significant in unadjusted analyses, multivariable regression revealed a significant independent association. These findings should be interpreted as a potential risk signal rather than evidence of causation, suggesting that short delays in inpatient-level care may have meaningful consequences for older adults, particularly with infectious conditions. Future work to delineate subgroups and causal pathways will be essential to improving ED risk stratification, disposition decision-making, and post-ED care transitions.

Introduction

Disposition decisions for older adults in the emergency department (ED) are uniquely challenging. While some patients clearly require inpatient care for life-threatening illness or injury and others can be safely discharged, for many older adults the optimal disposition decision is less clear. For these patients, clinicians must balance the risks of discharging a potentially unstable patient against the well documented harms and cost of unnecessary hospitalization [1, 2].

This decision is particularly complex in older adults. Age-related physiologic changes, multimorbidity, polypharmacy, and atypical disease presentations can complicate diagnosis and prognostication [3–12]. Cognitive or functional impairment may also limit an older patient’s ability to understand and adhere to post-discharge care plans, increasing the risk of adverse events after ED release [10, 13, 14]. Conversely, hospitalization itself carries meaningful risks for older patients: including delirium, functional decline, and other iatrogenic complications [15–19].

The majority of older adults presenting to the ED are initially discharged [20]. A small but clinically significant subset will returning to the ED shortly after discharge and requiring hospital admission- commonly termed “return visit admission” (RVA) [21]. Concern that deterioration after ED discharge may lead to delayed inpatient-level care and worse outcomes is a key factor influencing ED disposition decisions in older adults [22–28]. Understanding of the characteristics and outcomes of these RVA events may therefore help inform ED decision-making, discharge planning and transitions-of-care interventions.

Prior studies examining outcomes among RVA patients have produced mixed findings. Various definitions of RVA have been used, typically ranging from 72 h to 9 days after initial ED visit [29–37]. Some investigations have reported higher rates of adverse outcomes– including intensive care use or inpatient mortality- among RVA patients, potentially reflecting premature discharge [30, 32, 38–40]. In contrast, other studies, including a large U.S. cohort analysis, found similar or even lower in-hospital mortality among RVA patients compared to index (initial) ED visit admissions [36, 41].

However, many of these studies compared RVA patients with all other hospital admissions, regardless of inpatient unit type or illness severity. While this inclusive approach enables broader population-level inference, it risks conflating fundamentally different clinical populations. Many ED patients admitted to the hospital present with life-threatening illness or other clear indications for intensive inpatient care or procedures – circumstance in which ED discharge would not reasonably be considered. As a result, prior comparisons may obscure the outcome most relevant to emergency clinicians: whether older patients whose illness severity lies near the threshold for hospitalization experience worse outcomes after initial ED discharge. Notably, to our knowledge no prior work has focused exclusively on older adults or reported dedicated sub-analyses for this important population with distinct physiology, risk profiles, and disposition challenges.

To better approximate this clinical dilemma, we used the National Hospital Ambulatory Medical Care Survey (NHAMCS) to analyze older adult RVA [42–44]. NHAMCS captures a representative sample of U.S. ED visits and hospitalizations that can be extrapolated into national estimates; and includes a variable indicating whether an admission was preceded by an ED visit in the previous 3 days. We specifically compared older adults RVA to similar-aged admissions to the floor/ward without an ED visit in the preceding 72 h (index visit admissions), thereby focusing on cases in which admission versus discharge may have been plausibly considered. Our goal was to describe the characteristics and in-hospital outcomes of older adult RVA; and determine whether these events are associated with differences in hospital outcomes compared with index ED visit admissions. To our knowledge, no prior study has examined RVA outcomes specifically among older adults using a nationally representative dataset, while restricting the comparison group to admissions reflecting discretionary ED disposition decisions.

Methods

Data source and population

We analyzed data from the ED component of NHAMCS from January 1, 2007 through December 31st, 2022. NHAMCS is administered annually by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s (CDC) National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). Each year, NCHS develops the NHAMCS data collection form, defining variables for medical chart abstraction, which is then reviewed by an expert panel for additions, deletions, and modifications approximately every two years. Trained staff abstract visit data from the medical chart with NCHS oversight. Using a multistage probability sampling design, the NHAMCS captures a representative sample of visits to US EDs in non-institutional general and short-stay hospitals, excluding federal, military, and Veterans Administration hospitals that may be extrapolated into national estimates. 42–44 Importantly, NHAMCS does not contain patient identifiers; therefore, individual patients cannot be linked across encounters, and the analytic unit in this dataset is the ED visit rather than the patient. We considered for the analysis ED visits that resulted in hospital admission or transfer to another hospital for admission for patients aged ≥ 65 at presentation. We did not include ED visits in which patients were admitted/transferred to an observation unit or to a psychiatric or detox unit.

Analytic sample

The analytic sample was constructed using a stepwise cohort selection approach (see Fig. 1). First, we identified all ED visit (n = 72,749) between 2007 and 2022 for patients aged ≥ 65 years, excluding 320,012 visits where patients were aged < 65 years. Second, we restricted the sample to visits resulting in hospital admission or transfer to another hospital for admission, excluding admissions related to mental health, detoxification, observation and/or information on whether the patient was seen within the last 72 h was unknown (55,548), yielding 17,201 ED visits of adults aged ≥ 65 that resulted in hospital admission and had available data on 72-hour return visit history. Finally, we classified 72-Hour RVA when the NHAMCS variable indicating a prior ED visit within the preceding 72 h was positive (621). To create a clinically comparable comparator group, “index-visit admissions” (IVA) visits were restricted to hospital floor or ward admissions (8,864), excluding IVA to other locations (e.g., intensive care unit, stepdown unit, operating room, or cardiac catheterization laboratory) (7,716).

Comparator group definition

The primary comparison examined 72-hr RVA versus IVA to the floor/ward. 72 Hour RVA were defined as ED visits resulting in admission to any hospital unit that were preceded by a treat-and-release ED visit at the same facility ≤ 3 days previously. The NHAMCS survey included a question to ascertain this information during the period under study, though the wording changed slightly. From 2007–2015, the question was: “Was patient seen in this ED within the last 72 hours and discharged?” and from 2016–2022, it was: “Was patient seen in this ED within the last 72 hours?” Admissions were considered and analyzed as 72-hr RVA if the response to this question was “Yes.”

In contrast to prior work, we restricted the comparator group to admissions to the general hospital floor or ward, excluding higher acuity destinations (e.g., procedural suites, stepdown units, or intensive care units) [36, 41]. This design aimed to approximate real-world disposition decisions, often characterized by diagnostic or prognostic uncertainty, where either admission and discharge may plausibly have been considered [45, 46].

Survey design and weighting

All analyses accounted for the complex NHAMCS survey design using the provided visit weights, strata, and primary sampling units (PSUs) to generate multi-year nationally representative estimates. Survey design objects were constructed according to NCHS analytic guidance. Regression models incorporated survey weights and accounted for clustering and stratification producing robust variance estimates consistent with NHAMCS analytic recommendations.

Characteristics of admissions

For each admission, we examined demographic characteristics, Emergency Severity Index (ESI) triage acuity [47], and the patient’s origin of presentation (home vs. nursing home). To facilitate interpretation, several variables within the NHAMCS dataset were grouped, an approach also taken by other investigators [48–50]. Specifically, race was collapsed into three categories: white, black, and other/unknown [50]. ESI, a 5-level scale performed at initial triage in the ED, was grouped into three categories: immediate/emergent (ESI 1,2), urgent (ESI 3), and semi- and non-urgent (ESI 4, 5) [48, 49]. Number of chronic conditions was recorded using the NHAMCS chronic condition count variable, with a range of 0–12. We also examined hospital discharge diagnoses for each admission and report the most common individual diagnostic codes among RVA admissions. We extrapolated national estimates from observed counts using survey weights provided by CDC for NHAMCS.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality. Among those surviving to discharge, we also examined length of hospital admission and new transfer to nursing home, defined as a discharge to a nursing home among patients whose origin of presentation was a private residence.

Covariate selection

Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and prior literature on predictors of mortality in older emergency department populations. Prespecified covariates included age (continuous, centered at 75 years), sex, race/ethnicity, emergency severity index, and number of chronic conditions. Covariates were not selected through data-driven or stepwise procedures.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was conducted between March 2024 to September 2024. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables and compared patient characteristics and unadjusted outcomes between RVAs and index ED visit admissions to the floor/ward. Descriptive statistics were produced as survey-weighted medians with interquartile ranges (continuous variables) or weighted frequencies and percentages (categorical variables). Between-group comparisons (RVA vs. index admissions) used survey-adjusted t-tests for continuous variables and Rao–Scott chi-square tests for categorical variables. We used survey-weighted logistic regression (svyglm) using a quasibinomial distribution to estimate the association between RVA and in-hospital mortality. Results are presented as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. Independent variables included sex, race, age (centered at 75 years)), ESI triage acuity, and number of chronic conditions. Because this study evaluated a single prespecified primary outcomes with prespecified covariates, no correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Missing data were handled using complete-case analysis within the survey-weighted models, consistent with standard NHAMCS analytic practice when the proportion of missingness is limited.

Because ED and hospital care for older adults changed substantially during early phases of the COVID-19 pandemic, we conducted sensitivity analyses excluding 2020, as well as an analysis of 2020 alone [51, 52]. All analyses was conducted using R (version 2023.06.01) with the survey, dplyr, and gtsummary packages.

Ethical Approval and Compliance with Reporting Guidelines

This study was exempted from review by Weill Cornell Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB), as it uses a publicly available data set that contains no patient identifiers, and informed consent was waived. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for observational studies [53].

Results

From 2007 to 2022, the NHAMCS sample included a weighted national estimate of 3,375,062 ED visits of adults aged ≥ 65 that resulted in hospital admission and had available data on 72-hour return visit history. Among these, 206,211 (6.11%) were classified as RVAs, defined as hospital admissions preceded by an ED visit within the past 72 h. The remaining 3,168,851 (93.89%) admissions were classified as index-visit admissions (to the hospital floor or ward).

Table 1 summarizes patient characteristics and unadjusted outcomes. There were no statistically significant differences in age, sex, race/ethnicity, origin of presentation, or reported comorbidity burden between RVAs and index visit admissions. Median age was similar in both groups (78 years, IQR 71–85). Triage acuity distributions were also similar between groups. In-hospital mortality was higher among RVA admissions (4.0% vs. 2.3%), a difference that was clinically meaningful but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.082). Median hospital length of stay was similar between groups (5 vs. 4 days, p = 0.31). There was also no significant difference in nursing homes placement following hospitalization (15.6% vs. 15.3%, p > 0.9).

In survey-weighted multivariable logistic regression adjusting for age, sex, race, ESI triage acuity and number of chronic conditions, 72-hour RVA was independently associated with increased odds of in-hospital mortality (adjusted odds ratio [OR] ≈ 2.72; 95% CI: 1.12–6.62, p = 0.028) (Table 2). Survey-weighted regression models incorporating strata, weights, and clustering confirmed this signal, with consistent effect sizes. Female sex was associated with significantly lower odds of death compared to males (OR ≈ 2.2; 95% CI:1.20–4.03, p = 0.01). Patients triaged with semi- or non-urgent had substantially lower odds of in-hospital death compared with immediate or emergent patients (OR ≈ 0.09; 95% CI:0.01–0.62, p = 0.015). Increasing age (centered at 75), the number of chronic conditions and race/ethnicity were not associated with higher odds of in-hospital death in the adjusted model.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the characteristics and in-hospital outcomes of older adult 72-hour RVAs using a nationally representative dataset, and to compare them specifically to index ED visit admissions to the hospital floor or ward. In unadjusted analyses, the RVA cohort exhibited a higher in-hospital mortality rate than the comparison group (4.0% vs. 2.3%), a clinically meaningful difference that approached—but did not reach—statistical significance. In adjusted analysis using a multivariable logistic regression model, RVA was independently associated with statistically significant increased odds of in-hospital mortality (OR 2.72). Although confidence intervals were wide, reflecting the relatively small number of mortality events among RVAs in this nationally weighted sample, the point estimate suggested a clinically meaningful increase in risk. When excluding 2020 data, the direction and magnitude of the association remained similar, but statistical significance was lost and confidence intervals widened, consistent with reduced statistical precision rather than reversal of observed effect. These findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence of a potential risk signal rather than proof of causation, and residual confounding related to illness severity, frailty, goals of care, and other unmeasured factors cannot be excluded in an observational dataset such as NHAMCS. The finding is nonetheless notable given that most RVA patients were initially considered (by either provider or patient) well enough for ED discharge. This finding must be interpreted in the context of the analytic design used in this study.

Importantly, we deliberately constructed our comparison group to reflect a common clinical dilemma: the disposition of older adults for whom both discharge and admission are plausible options. By limiting the comparator group to patients admitted to general inpatient units-excluding critical care, procedural, or stepdown units- we focused on disposition decisions most likely to be characterized by clinical uncertainty. This approach diverges from prior studies that included all admitted patients, many of whom had clear, high-acuity indications for hospitalization and would not reasonably have been considered for discharge [36, 41]. While this strategy does not fully eliminate differences in illness severity between groups (indeed, not every floor/ward admission represents a discretionary disposition), it more closely approximates the subset of ED patients for whom admission versus discharge represents a realistic clinical decision. We attempted to mitigate residual differences in illness severity by adjusting for triage acuity and other covariates in regression modelling, which helps account for residual differences in illness severity, an approach not included in prior RVA analyses.

The higher mortality we observed among RVAs may partly reflect this methodological distinction. However, it may also indicate that older adults-because of frailty, multimorbidity and vulnerability to rapid clinical change-are particularly susceptible to the adverse effects of delayed inpatient care. Importantly, NHAMCS does not include direct measures of frailty, functional status, or goals of care. Although we adjusted for age, triage acuity, and number of chronic conditions as partial proxies for patient vulnerability, these measures cannot fully capture the complexity of geriatric risk. Residual confounding related to illness severity, frailty and care preferences therefore remains possible.

Infectious conditions dominated the discharge diagnoses for RVA hospitalizations, with sepsis, pneumonia, COVID-19, cellulitis, and urinary tract infections comprising the top five categories. This pattern reinforces prior work and highlights potential mechanistic contributors to RVA and poor outcomes. Infections are common causes of return visits in older adults; [29, 30, 33, 36] and are also a frequent precipitant of delirium, a condition that remains under-recognized in ED settings [54, 55]. Delays in appropriate treatment of sepsis and delirium have been associated with worse clinical outcomes [22–28]. These findings suggest that infection-related presentations may warrant heightened caution in ED discharge decision-making, particularly for older adults with equivocal clinical stability.

We also found that RVAs were demographically and clinically similar to index visit admissions, with no significant differences in age, sex, race, or comorbidity burden. Although RVAs were somewhat more likely to originate from skilled nursing facilities, this difference did not reach statistical significance. If confirmed, this pattern may reflect a greater willingness by ED providers to discharge patients with moderate illness back to supervised environments, such as skilled nursing facilities, where clinical monitoring is available.

The elevated mortality risk among RVAs carries implications for both emergency and inpatient care delivery. For hospital-based teams, recognizing RVA status at time of admission may help identify subgroups of older adults who could benefit from closer monitoring or earlier escalation of care. For ED clinicians, these findings reinforce the importance of careful discharge planning and anticipatory guidance for older adult patients presenting with conditions prone to rapid deterioration. More broadly, RVA may represent part of a high risk utilization phenotype among older adults who were initially considered stable enough for discharge but remain vulnerable to rapid clinical decline [56, 57]. This concept may help guide future interventions, such as geriatric ED assessment pathways or integration of RVA signals into risk stratification models.

Finally, our findings highlight the need for more granular characterization of RVA etiologies. These events likely reflect a heterogenous set of circumstances, including patient non-compliance, adverse social conditions, disease recurrence, end-of-life care, inadequate rapid outpatient care access, unforeseen rapid disease progression, and suboptimal risk stratification [29, 36, 58–60]. Some patients may have been advised to return if symptoms worsened; others may have been called back for abnormal diagnostic results; and some may have represented for unrelated conditions. The present analysis focused on outcomes associated with RVA rather than predictors of RVA. However, identifying patient-level predictors of RVA represents an important direction for future research, distinguishing clinically meaningful RVA subgroups and their outcome trajectories will be essential to developing targeted interventions, including risk stratification tools, safer discharge planning or tailored post-ED transitions-of-care interventions [61, 62].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the NHAMCS dataset identifies RVAs based solely on presentations to the same ED within 72 h, limiting our ability to capture return visits to other hospitals. As a result, the observed RVA rate is likely an underestimate. Additionally, misclassification may have occurred if the survey response to the return visit question was inaccurate or incomplete. Second, although NHAMCS provides nationally representative estimates, RVAs remain an uncommon subset, resulting in small absolute cell sizes even when weighted. This limited our ability to perform more granular subgroup analyses, and some clinically meaningful differences may not have reached statistical significance. Third, although we excluded admissions to procedural or intensive care units from the comparator group to better approximate discretionary disposition decisions, a substantial number of ED patients admitted to hospital floor beds have presentations where discharge is unlikely to be considered and hospitalization is clearly required. The inclusion of such cases may attenuate observed differences. While our regression adjustment for triage acuity, age and comorbidity burden helps partially address this issue, these measures are imperfect proxies for clinical complexity or geriatric vulnerability. Direct measures of frailty, functional status, and goals of care are not available in NHAMCS and may contribute to residual confounding. As such, our design represents an incremental improvement over prior work but does not fully resolve the comparator problem.

Fourth, because NHAMCS includes only in-hospital outcomes, we could not assess deaths occurring after ED discharge, but before a potential return visit. However, prior studies have found that death outside the hospital, immediately after ED discharge is incredibly rare [40, 63]. Finally, diagnostic specificity was limited: the most common ICD-10 discharge code individually accounted for < 5% of cases. Aggregated diagnostic categories or clinical syndrome-level groupings might provide more meaningful insight into common pathways of RVA among older adults. Despite these limitations, our findings highlight RVA in older adults as a clinically important signal that merits further investigation. Larger datasets or linkage across health systems will be needed to confirm these associations and better characterize underlying mechanisms.

Conclusion

In a nationally representative sample of older adult ED patients, 72-hour RVAs were associated with higher in-hospital mortality compared to index visit admissions to the hospital floor or ward. Although this difference was not statistically significant in unadjusted analyses, multivariable regression controlling for age, sex, triage acuity, race, and comorbid burden revealed a significant independent association. These findings should be interpreted as a potential risk signal rather than evidence of causation, suggesting that brief delays in hospitalization-particularly for infectious conditions may have meaningful clinical consequences for vulnerable older adults. Future studies that more precisely delineate RVA subgroups and their underlying drivers will be essential in advancing ED and in-hospital risk stratification, safe disposition decision-making, and tailoring post-ED transitions of care interventions.
