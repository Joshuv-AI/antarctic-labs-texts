Prediction and analysis of genetic effect in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis and gastroesophageal reflux disease
Authors: Peipei Chen, Lubin Xie, Leikai Ma, Xianda Zhao, Yong Chen, Zhouling Ge
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith increasing research on idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD), more and more studies have indicated that GERD is associated with IPF, but the underlying pathological mechanisms remain unclear. The aim of the present study is to identify and analyse the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between IPF and GERD and explore the relevant molecular mechanisms via bioinformatics analysis. Four GEO datasets (GSE24206, GSE53845, GSE26886, and GSE39491) were downloaded from the GEO database, and DEGs between IPF and GERD were identified with the online tool GEO2R. Subsequently, a series of bioinformatics analyses are conducted, including Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses, the PPI network, biological characteristics, TF‐gene interactions, TF‐miRNA coregulatory networks, and the prediction of drug molecules. Totally, 71 genes were identified as DEGs in IPF and GERD. Five KEGG pathways, including Amoebiasis, Protein digestion and absorption, Relaxin signalling pathway, AGE‐RAGE signalling pathway in diabetic complications, and Drug metabolism ‐ cytochrome P450, were significantly enriched. In addition, eight hub genes, including POSTN, MMP1, COL3A1, COL1A2, CXCL12, TIMP3, VCAM1, and COL1A1 were selected from the PPI network by Cytoscape software. Then, five hub genes (MMP1, POSTN, COL3A1, COL1A2, and COL1A1) with high diagnostic values for IPF and GERD were validated by GEO datasets. Finally, TF‐gene and miRNA interaction was identified with hub genes and predicted drug molecules for the IPF and GERD. And the results suggest that cetirizine, luteolin, and pempidine may have great potential therapeutic value in IPF and GERD. This study will provide novel strategies for the identification of potential biomarkers and valuable therapeutic targets for IPF and GERD.Keywords: bioinformatics, data analysis, data mining, statistical analysis

INTRODUCTION

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is the most common type of interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) of unknown aetiology, which is typified as chronic, progressive, irreversible fibrotic, and fatal with high morbidity and mortality [1, 2]. The clinical manifestation of IPF includes progressive aggravation of dyspnoea, restrictive ventilation dysfunction, gas‐exchange disorder, hypoxaemia and ultimately resulting in respiratory failure [3]. In the past decades, the prevalence of patients diagnosed with IPF has been rising globally [1]. And the high incidence of IPF is associated with increased age and male sex, with a median survival time of 2–3 years after diagnosis [4, 5, 6]. As the global population ages, the physical, psychological, and economic burden of IPF on patients is expected to increase steadily [7].

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a highly prevalent gastrointestinal disorder worldwide and is defined as a disease in which gastric contents reflux into the oesophagus, causing reflux, heartburn, regurgitation, dyspepsia, oesophageal mucosal damage and other uncomfortable symptoms or complications [8]. GERD is classified into three categories: non‐erosive reflux disease (NERD), reflux oesophagitis (RE), and Barrett's oesophagus (BE) [9]. With the high prevalence of GERD (33%), the number of patients with poor quality of life and financial expenditure is increasing steadily, which imposes a heavy economic burden on the world [8].

Globally, accumulating studies have demonstrated that there is a relationship between IPF and GERD [10, 11, 12]. Many studies have reported a high prevalence of GERD in IPF, and up to 76% of IPF patients may have GERD [13, 14]. Also, a study including over 100,000 participants revealed the prevalence of pulmonary fibrosis is significantly higher than that of healthy controls [15]. Recently, some studies have suggested that the acid inhalation caused by GERD may be associated with pulmonary fibrosis, while pulmonary diseases may promote an altered transdiaphragmatic pressure gradient and cause GERD [16, 17]. However, the underlying mechanism between IPF and GERD still remains ambiguous and needs further exploration.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Gene expression profile data collection

Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo) is a public functional genomic database that includes high‐throughput gene expression data, chips, and microarrays. All microarray gene expression profiles of IPF, GERD, and normal controls, including four test sets and two validation sets, were downloaded from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). The GSE53845 dataset (including 40 IPF samples and eight normal samples) and the GSE24206 dataset (including 17 IPF samples and six normal samples) were selected as IPF test sets. In addition, the GSE26886 dataset consisting of 20 GERD samples and 19 normal samples, and the GSE39491 consisting of 43 GERD samples and 43 normal samples were selected as GERD test sets. Finally, the IPF dataset (GSE10667) contains 31 IPF samples and 15 normal samples were selected as a validation set, while two GPL96 datasets GSE13083 and GSE1420, which included 15 GERD samples and 15 normal samples, were selected as validation sets.

Differentially expressed genes selection

The Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each GEO data set were screened using the GEO online tool GEO2R (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/geo2r/), which is an R‐based web application using limma package. p values were used to reduce the false‐positive rate using Benjamini & Hochberg method. The threshold of DEGs screening was set as p < 0.05 and | log2(fold change) | ≥1. Visualised volcano and heat maps of DEGs were constructed by the R packages “complexheatmap” and “ggplot2”, respectively. A Venn diagram was then constructed to identify the common DEGs obtained from different GSE datasets using the Venn online platform (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/Venn/). These common DEGs were retained for subsequent analysis.

Functional classification and pathway enrichment of DEGs

DEGs were further subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopaedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis using an R package “clusterProfiler”. The GO enrichment analysis provides a functional standardisation system for genes and proteins, including the biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), cellular component (CC), while the KEGG enrichment analysis provides information on discovered pathways and their involved interactions. The enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways were selected as an adjusted p < 0.05.

Establishment of protein–protein interaction and identification of hub genes

The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of DEGs was constructed using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes (STRING) (http://string‐db.org/), and a confidence score of ≥0.4 was selected as the threshold of PPI. Subsequently, Cytoscape software (https://www.cytoscape.org/) was used to visualise the PPI network. The Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE, http://apps.cytoscape.org/apps/mcode) and Cytohubba in Cytoscape were used to screen modules of hub genes from the PPI network with a score >2 in MCODE and the Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) algorithm in the cytoHubba.

Expression analysis of hub genes

To verify the sensitivity and specificity of the hub gene expression obtained from the above analysis for the diagnosis of IPF and GERD, we plotted the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and calculated the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) of hub genes based on the IPF data (GSE10667) and GERD data (GSE13083 and GSE1420) obtained from GEO database. The ROC curve was created to quantitatively calculate the sensitivity and specificity, and AUC was used to compare the ROC curves using the R package pROC.

Construction of transcription factor gene networks

The hub genes obtained above were uploaded to NetworkAnalyst (version 3.0, https://www.networkanalyst.ca/) to construct TF‐gene networks. The TF‐gene interaction network was constructed based on the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net) via the NetworkAnalyst platform.

Construction of TF‐miRNA coregulatory network

The TF‐miRNA coregulatory network information was collected from the RegNetwork repository (http://www.regnetworkweb.org/), which integrated existing regulations in multiple databases and potential regulations based on TF binding sites. The NetworkAnalyst platform was used to visualise the TF‐miRNA coregulatory network.

Identification of candidate drugs

Based on the selected hub DEGs for IPF and GERD, drug molecules were predicted using the Searchable Platform Independent Expression database (SPIED, http://SPIED.org.uk/cgi‐bin/hgnc‐spiedxxx.cgi) and CMAP (Connectivity MAP, https://clue.io/CMAP), which is a database used for exploring potential drugs for treating diseases [18, 19]. Furthermore, the molecular docking simulation studies were carried out to predict the interaction pattern between the active site of protein and ligand using Schrodinger suite Maestro (Version 11.5) software. The protein structures of these hub genes and drugs were downloaded from RCSB Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB) (https://www.rcsb.org/), SWISS MODEL server (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/), and Pubchem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), respectively. The collected data were processed and then visually presented by previously reported study [20]. Then Pymol software (version 2.3) was used to obtain the affinity parameters and spatial 3D structures based on the binding energy obtained.

RESULTS

Identification of DEGs in IPF and GERD

The IPF datasets (GSE53845 and GSE24206) and the GERD datasets (GSE26886 and GSE39491) were downloaded from the GEO database. After screening with the threshold of an adjusted p < 0.05 and ∣log2 FC | >1.0, a total of 956 (547 upregulated and 409 downregulated genes), 1034 (520 upregulated and 514 downregulated genes), 1949 (1098 upregulated and 851 downregulated genes) and 7641 (3620 upregulated and 4021 downregulated genes) DEGs were screened from GSE53845, GSE24206, GSE39491, and GSE26886 using the “limma” package in R software, respectively. As shown in Figures 1 and 2, the volcano plot and heatmap analyses were used to visualise the DEGs. Next, the comparison of common DEGs between IPF and GERD datasets was visualised through a Venn diagram in Figure 1e, and 71 common DEGs were identified.

GO and KEGG enrichment pathway analysis of common DEGs

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed to better understand the biological functions of the identified DEGs using the clusterProfiler package in R software. The top five significant GO terms for each of the subsections (biological process, molecular functions, and cellular component) and top five KEGG pathways were clustered with the threshold of adjusted p < 0.05. In the biological process, the common DEGs were significantly enriched in leucocyte migration, cell chemotaxis, leucocyte chemotaxis, autonomic nervous system development, and artery development (Figure 3a). Under the term of cellular component, DEGs were principally associated with the collagen‐containing extracellular matrix, extracellular matrix component, fibrillar collagen trimer, banded collagen fibril, and complex of collagen trimers (Figure 3b). Among the molecular functions, DEGs were mainly enriched in extracellular matrix structural constituent, platelet‐derived growth factor binding, growth factor binding, extracellular matrix structural constituent conferring tensile strength, and integrin binding (Figure 3c). Additionally, DEGs were significantly enriched in five KEGG pathways, including Amoebiasis, Protein digestion and absorption, Relaxin signalling pathway, AGE‐RAGE signalling pathway in diabetic complications, and Drug metabolism ‐ cytochrome P450 (Figure 3d).

Construction of PPI network and identification of hub genes

The PPI network of DEGs was established by the STRING database to analyse the interaction between the DEGs and visualised by Cytoscape software (Figure 4a). And the interaction number of each gene was also shown (Figure 4b). To identify the hub genes from the PPI network, the cytoHubba plug‐in of Cytoscape software was used. And according to the MCC algorithms, we selected the top 10 genes as potential hub genes (Figure 4c), including Collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1), Periostin (POSTN), Collagen type III alpha 1 chain (COL3A1), C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12), Matrix metallopeptidase 1 (MMP1), Collagen type I alpha 2 chain (COL1A2), TIMP metallopeptidase inhibitor 3 (TIMP3), Vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM1), Asporin (ASPN), and latent transforming growth factor beta binding protein 1 (LTBP1). Besides, by using the Cytoscape plug‐in MCODE, one significant module with a score >7 was screened out (Figure 4d), which contains eight genes: Matrix metallopeptidase 1 (MMP1), Periostin (POSTN), Collagen type III alpha 1 chain (COL3A1), Collagen type I alpha 2 chain (COL1A2), C‐X‐C motif chemokine ligand 12 (CXCL12), TIMP metallopeptidase inhibitor 3 (TIMP3), Vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM1), and Collagen type I alpha 1 chain (COL1A1). Next, we took the intersection of the genes obtained by the above two methods and selected eight hub genes: MMP1, POSTN, COL3A1, COL1A2, CXCL12, TIMP3, VCAM1, and COL1A1.

Verification and diagnostic value of the 8 hub genes expression

IPF data (GSE10667) and GERD data (GSE13083 and GSE1420) were selected from the GEO database to verify the eight hub genes obtained above. And the ggplot2 package of R software was used to create boxplots. Among the eight hub genes, the expression levels of six hub genes (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, CXCL12, POSTN, and TIMP3) were significantly up‐regulated in IPF samples, while seven hub genes (COL1A1, COL1A2, COL3A1, POSTN, MMP1, TIMP3, and VCAM1) were significantly up‐regulated in GERD samples compared with the control, respectively (Figure 5a,c). Besides, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were performed and the corresponding area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated to validate the diagnostic value of these eight hub genes obtained from the above analysis (Figure 5b,d). The diagnostic value of these eight genes in IPF samples and normal samples are as follow: MMP1(AUC:0.927), POSTN (AUC:0.897), COL3A1 (AUC:0.929), COL1A2 (0.877), CXCL12 (AUC:0.869), TIMP3(AUC:0.611), VCAM1(AUC:0.652), and COL1A1 (AUC:0.914). In GERD samples and normal samples, the AUC for MMP1, POSTN, COL3A1, COL1A2, CXCL12, TIMP3, VCAM1, and COL1A1 were 0.769, 0.947, 0.907, 0.907, 0.604, 0.769, 0.791, and 0.840. Therefore, we hypothesise that MMP1, POSTN, COL3A1, COL1A2, and COL1A1 may be biomarkers between IPF and GERD.

The NetworkAnalyst databases were used to anticipate and visualise the TF‐gene interaction of hub genes. The TF‐gene interaction network consisted of 27 nodes and 31 edges (Figure 6). TF‐miRNA coregulatory network

The NetworkAnalyst tool was used to construct the TF‐miRNA coregulation network. The regulation network is composed of five hub genes, 70 miRNAs, and 24 TF, including 128 edges and 99 nodes (Figure 7). The degree of MMP1, POSTN, COL3A1, COL1A1, and COL1A2 were 13, 19, 24, 32, and 40, respectively.

DISCUSSION

In the present study, methodologies related to bioinformatics analyses were used to search the datasets of IPF and GERD, and 71 common DEGs between IPF and GERD were identified based on the GEO database. The results of GO enrichment analysis indicated that the DEGs were mainly enriched in the collagen‐containing extracellular matrix, extracellular matrix structural constituent, and leucocyte migration. KEGG pathways that were enriched included Amoebiasis, Protein digestion and absorption, Relaxin signalling pathway, AGE‐RAGE signalling pathway in diabetic complications, and Drug metabolism ‐ cytochrome P450. This further indicates that the common DEGs were closely related to extracellular matrix (ECM), metabolism, and immune response.

We constructed a PPI network via the STRING online database and Cytoscape software and screened eight hub genes (MMP1, POSTN, COL3A1, COL1A2, CXCL12, TIMP3, VCAM1, and COL1A1) according to the MCODE plug‐in and cytoHubba plug‐in of Cytoscape. To further verify the association of hub genes in IPF and GERD, we compared the expression of eight hub genes across the validation datasets using the GEO platform. Subsequently, combining ROC curve analysis and their expression levels, we validated the hub genes and finally screened five hub genes, namely MMP1, POSTN, COL3A1, COL1A2, and COL1A1. These five genes were all upregulated in both IPF patients and GERD samples, suggesting these genes may play important roles in the mechanism of IPF and GERD.

MMP1 is a member of the matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) family, which participates in the degradation of extracellular matrix and the process of vascular remodelling in normal biological and pathological processes as an immunogenic protease [21, 22]. In addition, recent studies have shown that MMPs also play important roles in extracellular tissues via signal transduction networks [23]. MMP1 is highly expressed in IPF as an IPF biomarker and leads to chronic lung injury and fibrosis [24, 25, 26]. Similarly, MMP1 was shown to be involved in the pathogenesis of GERD and played a role in the stepwise progression from Barrett's metaplasia to oesophageal adenocarcinoma (EAC) [27, 28]. Periostin (POSTN) is a matricellular protein secreted mainly by stromal cells that are involved in cell adhesion, migration, and extracellular matrix remodelling [29]. Several studies have shown that POSTN might contribute to the pathogenesis of IPF and could be a potential biomarker for predicting disease progression and therapeutic effects in IPF patients [30, 31, 32]. POSTN is also overexpressed in GERD and revealed in the progression of EAC [33, 34].

COL1A1, COL1A2, and COL3A1 are extracellular matrix‐associated genes, that belong to the collagen family [35]. In IPF, these three collagen genes were increased [36] and closely associated with the development of IPF through the extracellular matrix pathway and focal adhesion process [37]. In addition, these three collagen genes could regulate the immunosuppressive microenvironment and were linked to GERD and oesophageal carcinoma [38, 39, 40].

TF‐gene interaction was gained with the five hub DEGs. The TF genes served as regulators in accordance with genetic expressions which may result in creating cancer cells. In the TF‐gene interaction network, FOXL1, STAT3, and FOXC1 have significant interaction with the higher degree value of three. STAT3, as a latent transcription factor, plays a role in regulating fibroblast function in fibrotic lung diseases [41]. In addition, STAT3 participates in the pathophysiological progress of IPF through immune response [42]. Correspondingly, STAT3 may be implicated in the immune response in the pathogenesis of GERD [43, 44].

TF‐miRNA interaction was constructed in the present study. From the network, JUN showed a higher degree value of three. Previous studies demonstrated that JUN played essential roles in the development of IPF and GERD [45, 46, 47]. The let‐7 family and miR‐29 had significant interaction in the TF‐miRNA coregulatory network, indicating that these miRNAs may play important roles in further studies of the pathogenesis of IPF and GERD. This finding is consistent with some previous studies [48, 49, 50, 51]. TF gene is regulated by combining with the target gene and miRNA, which can regulate gene expression through mRNA degradation [52].

Taken together, we identified the common DEGs and screened out five hub genes among IPF and GERD. Based on the five hub genes, we generated the TF‐miRNA coregulatory network that involved the interaction of miRNAs and TF genes and screened potential drugs. Among all the candidate drugs, we selected three drug molecules in the study. Cetirizine, as an anti‐allergic drug, had shown its effect on GERD and respiratory diseases in previous studies [53, 54]. Luteolin played an anti‐pulmonary fibrosis role in IPF via the regulation of inflammation and pro‐fibrotic signalling pathways [55], while luteolin had an impact on the prognosis of GERD mainly through inflammatory modulating effects [56]. However, there were no relevant studies on pempidine in IPF and GERD.

CONCLUSION

Through the integration of various bioinformatics analyses, we focused on the relationship between IPF and GERD for the first time. The five hub genes (including MMP1, POSTN, COL3A1, COL1A2, and COL1A1) were upregulated in IPF and GERD, which may play important roles in the pathophysiological mechanism of IPF and GERD. The drug targets were derived through the identification of the five hub genes, which provided novel ideas for future research. Our findings may contribute to the development of early diagnostic strategies, prognostic markers, and therapeutic targets in IPF and GERD.

However, there are some limitations to our study. Firstly, we focused on the top five hub genes in our current study and lacked research on detailed molecular mechanisms. Secondly, there is no relevant basic experiment or clinical trial to verify. Thirdly, our research was only based on predictions from public databases, and larger‐sample, multi‐centre research is needed.
