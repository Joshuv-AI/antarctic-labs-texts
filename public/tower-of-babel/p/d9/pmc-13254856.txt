Dyadic coping in couples experiencing mild cognitive impairment
Authors: Chase Virta-Steel, Lucille J Carriere, Dona EC Locke, Anne L Shandera-Ochsner, Andrea Cuc, Melanie J Chandler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe diagnosis of Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) can create stress for patients and their care partners (dyads). This study aimed to understand how dyadic coping (DC; how well couples feel they cope together in the face of a stressor) is related to the psychological health and overall well-being of couples coping with an MCI diagnosis. Additionally, this study attempted to characterize the impact of the HABIT Healthy Action to Benefit Independence & Thinking® program as a novel approach for improving DC.MethodsWe analyzed DC in 213 people with MCI (pwMCI) and 205 care partners using the Dyadic Coping Inventory (Bodenmann, 2005). We compared DC scores to outcome measures of psychological health and well-being. We also analyzed DC in a subset of 135 pwMCI and 138 care partners with 3-month post HABIT DC scores to look for evidence of change.ResultspwMCI and care partner DC significantly affected their own perception of emotional functioning on other measures [quality of life (QoL) and mood for pwMCI and QoL, mood, anxiety, and sense of burden for care partners]. pwMCI DC also predicted care partner anxiety. In all cases, better DC predicted better emotional functioning. Care partner DC was also found to significantly relate to how they rated the pwMCI’s cognitive and daily functioning as well as the pwMCI’s adherence with a cognitive rehabilitation intervention. pwMCI and care partners with low baseline DC showed an increase in DC scores after completing HABIT, as did pwMCI in the medium baseline DC group. Care partners with medium baseline DC did not experience change post HABIT. High copers in both the pwMCI and care partner groups experienced a decline in DC, although scores remained average to above average.ConclusionThese results suggest that DC is an important part of a couple’s psychological health and well-being in MCI. Care partner DC may be particularly important in how partners rate their loved one’s cognitive and functional status and how they respond to cognitive rehabilitation intervention. The HABIT program may also represent a novel way to improve DC for low and medium coping couples.Keywords: Dyadic coping, Mild cognitive impairment, Caregiving, Nonpharmacological interventions

Introduction

Dyadic coping (DC) refers to the dynamic process by which romantic care partners use collaborative strategies to manage stressful events or circumstances (Bodenmann, 2005). In this context, the goal of DC is to alleviate emotional distress and improve relationship quality while navigating the stressor (Landolt et al., 2023). A dyad’s ability to use effective DC depends on how they employ both positive and negative DC styles (Bodenmann, 2005). Positive DC includes such behaviors as expression of emotional validation or empathy, practical support and assistance, or shared problem-solving. Alternatively, negative DC behaviors may include use of hostile, ambivalent, or disingenuous means of support.

DC is an important concept in dyads facing serious medical conditions. The associated medical stress experienced by one person affects their care partner as well. In other words, they experience a shared dyadic challenge, often referred to as a “we-disease” mentality (Cipolletta et al., 2019; Leuchtmann & Bodenmann, 2017). Much of the work in this field to date has focused on cancer and organ transplant patients and how they use DC in response to the diagnosis and treatment of these conditions. In general, this research has demonstrated a positive association between DC and relationship satisfaction (Fife et al., 2013; Pankrath et al., 2018), sense of well-being (Rusu et al., 2015), and even physical outcomes (Weitkamp et al., 2021).

Negative perception of one’s DC has been found to be especially detrimental to a couple’s mental health when facing disease. For example, patients with cancer who feel that they and their partner are not coping well together show increased distress, depression, and lower relationship satisfaction (Badr et al., 2010; Regan et al., 2014; Rottmann et al., 2015). The stress of caregiving can cause care partners to report less supportive DC and feeling more anxious (Badr et al., 2010; Fife et al., 2013; Regan et al., 2014). Importantly, care partners’ perception that they and their loved one are not coping well together has been found to have a bigger impact than the patients’ perception of DC on how both dyad members rate their relationship satisfaction (Pankrath et al., 2018; Rottmann et al., 2015), highlighting the key role of the care partner’s mental well-being on both individuals.

Despite the known association between DC behaviors and psychological, physical and relationship outcomes in chronically ill patients and care partners, little is known about DC in the context of Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI). MCI has been defined by a workgroup from the National Institute on Aging and the Alzheimer's Association as a symptomatic predementia phase in which an individual has cognitive changes that are more than expected for age, but they remain largely intact in their day-to-day functioning (Albert et al., 2011). Even though the person with MCI (pwMCI) is largely functional in their day-to-day life, care partners, often spouses, do report increased emotional distress and feelings of burden (Paradise et al., 2015; Seeher et al., 2013). Emotional distress and burden of MCI care partners has been shown to worsen when the pwMCI is experiencing emotional difficulties, such as depression, anger/aggression, motor symptoms, sleep disturbances, or functional limitations (Carter et al., 2008; Connors et al., 2019; Ornstein & Gaugler, 2012). Likewise, distress in a care partner may increase distress in the patient themselves (Chyu et al., 2022).

The current study sought to better understand the relationship between DC and emotional well-being in MCI for both the pwMCI and their care partner. We anticipated that DC would be highly correlated between the pwMCI and their care partner. Additionally, we anticipated that the better one member’s perception of their DC, the better their emotional well-being would be. Past research also suggests that the care partner’s perception of DC will likely impact both individuals’ mental well-being.

We also sought to understand if DC could be improved with interventions aimed at improving coping in both pwMCI and their care partners. To that end, we examined any changes to DC after participation in a wellness and cognitive rehabilitation program for pwMCI and their care partners, the HABIT Healthy Actions to Benefit Independence & Thinking® Program. The HABIT Program is a 10-day outpatient program for pwMCI and a program care partner (spouse, adult child, or other loved one) that includes cognitive rehabilitation via compensatory strategies, computerized cognitive training, wellness education, patient and care partner support groups, and yoga. This program has been described in detail in prior publications (e.g., (Levy et al., 2023)). HABIT has been shown in past research to improve pwMCI self-efficacy, mood, and quality of life (Chandler et al., 2019; Greenaway et al., 2013), as well as improve care partner mood, anxiety and burden (Amofa et al., 2021; Cuc et al., 2017). We hypothesized that participation in HABIT would improve perceived DC of participants as they learned more about MCI and different strategies to cope with the diagnosis and symptoms.

Methods

Between March of 2022 and May of 2024, 213 pwMCI and their significant other care partners (n = 205) completed the Dyadic Coping Inventory (DCI) (Bodenmann, 2005) prior to their participation in the HABIT program. Non-romantic care partner dyads (i.e., adult children, siblings, friends) were excluded from this study, as were HABIT participants who did not complete the DCI. Subject recruitment and exclusion can be viewed in the CONSORT style diagram in Fig. 1. Diagnosis of MCI was based on clinical evaluation (Albert et al., 2011). All subtypes of MCI (amnestic, nonamnestic, single or multiple domain) and potential neurodegenerative etiologies of MCI were included.There was no prescribed neuropsychological battery. Rather, MCI subtype was based on clinical neuropsychological report.

Participants enrolled and followed for the current study.

The DCI includes 37 items related to different DC behaviors that couples individually rated on a 5-point scale, from “very rarely” engaging in a certain behavior to “very often” engaging in that behavior. Items include prompts such as “I let my care partner know that I appreciate his/her practical support, advice or help”, “My care partner expresses that he/she is on my side”, and “When I am stressed, my care partner tends to withdraw”. Scores range from 37–187, with scores > 145 indicating above average coping, scores < 111 representing below average coping, and scores between 111–145 representing average coping (Bodenmann, 2005; Tkachenko et al., 2019).

Relationship between DCI and emotional well-being

Participants completed additional questionnaires of psychological functioning and well-being prior to participation in HABIT. In terms of emotional well-being, both the pwMCI and the care partner completed measures of quality of life (QoL) via the Quality of Life AD (QoL-AD) (Logsdon et al., 2002), anxiety (the Reach Anxiety Scale (Kurasz et al., 2021)), and depression [Center for Epidemiologic Studies-Depression Scale (CES-D)] (Radloff, 1977). The pwMCI additionally completed a measure of self-efficacy (Self-Efficacy in MCI)(Jurica, 2001), and the care partner completed a measure of care partner burden (Caregiver Burden Scale) (Bédard et al., 2001; Zarit & Zarit, 1990). In terms of cognitive and functional ability, cognitive functioning in the pwMCI was assessed with the Dementia Rating Scale (DRS) (Jurica et al., 2001). For daily functioning, care partners completed the Measure of Everyday Cognition (ECog) (Farias et al., 2008), Care partners also simply rated the pwMCI’s memory difficulty on a scale from 1–10, with 1 representing no difficulty and 10 representing extreme difficulty. Lastly, scores indicating successful learning of the cognitive rehabilitation intervention component of HABIT, the Memory Support System (MSS) were assessed by the intervention team as previously described (De Wit et al., 2019; Greenaway et al., 2008) at 3 time points (prior to training start, training end, and 3 months post-HABIT). Correlations in DC were examined between pwMCI and their care partners. Multivariate analyses were utilized to explore the impact of pwMCI DC, care partner DC, and any discrepancy between pwMCI and care partner DC (calculated as care partner DC – pwMCI DC at baseline) for the emotional well-being variables (QoL, anxiety, mood, self-efficacy, and care partner burden), cognitive and functional status (DRS total score, ECOG memory and executive functioning, and care partner rating of memory ability), and response to the MSS cognitive rehabilitation training (training success and adherence). If both individual DC and DC discrepancy had significant impact on a variable, hierarchical regression analyses were conducted to determine whether DC discrepancy provided additional explanatory value beyond DC alone.

Impact of habit on DC

As of September 2024, a subset of 135 pwMCI and 138 care partners had completed the 3-month follow-up time point answering the same questionnaires as part of their participation in HABIT. Participants were assigned labels of “low” (DCI <111), “medium” (DCI = 111–145), and “high” (DCI >145). Changes to DC pre and post HABIT were explored using general linear models for repeated measures with Tukey HSD pairwise comparisons and Bonferroni correction. Prior to the analysis, data were examined for outliers and violations of model assumptions. Standardized residuals were assessed for normality within each baseline cognitive impairment group (Low, Medium, High Informant DCI). Histograms, Q-Q plots, and tests of normality (Shapiro-Wilk) were examined separately for each group. When tests of normality were significant, suggesting significant outliers, the GLM was run with a filter to exclude cases with outlying standardized residuals.

All statistics were examined in the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS version 29.0.x) software.

Results

The average age of pwMCI and care partners was 74.77 (6.37) and 72.25 (6.73) years, respectively. 62.0% of the patient sample was male (39.0% female). Males accounted for 39.5% of the care partner sample (60.5% female). The average years of education for pwMCI was 16.33 (2.47) years and 16.20 (2.20) years for care partners. Participants were allowed to select multiple races to capture their identity. The majority of pwMCI identified as only White (93.4% of pwMCI). One pwMCI identified as Native American, 5 as African American or Black, 8 as Hispanic or Latino, and 6 as “other.” For care partners, 94.6% identified as only White. One care partner identified as Asian or Pacific Islander, 4 as African American or Black, 4 as Hispanic or Latino, and 3 as “other.” Overall cognitive function for pwMCI was a DRS-2 of 128.06 (9.37). Diagnosis of single domain amnestic MCI accounted for 33.6% of the pwMCI sample and multiple domain amnestic MCI for 41.7%, with fewer non-amnestic single domain (8.1%) and non-amnestic multidomain pwMCI (10.0%). The remaining were not assigned a specific subtype (6.6%).

Cognitive function was not correlated with pwMCI (R = −0.07) or care partner DC (R = −0.10), p > .05. DC scores also did not differ by MCI subtype for pwMCI (F(3, 188) = 1.23, p=.30) or for care partners (F(3, 182) = 0.36, p=.78). pwMCI DC and care partner DC were significantly correlated at R = 0.51, p<.001. Twenty-five percent of pwMCI and care partners DC were within 6 points of each other, 50% within 13 points, and 75% within 22 points of each other. Within the upper quartile, or the most discrepant scores, care partners were twice as likely (n = 31) to report poorer DC than the pwMCI (n = 15 pwMCI reported discrepantly lower DC than the care partner).

Discussion

As anticipated, pwMCI and care partner perceptions of how well they were coping as a couple were significantly correlated with one another. When more discrepancy was observed between pwMCI and their significant other, this tended to be due to care partners rating their DC lower than the pwMCI. However, discrepancy in DC perceptions had limited impact on outcomes above and beyond the impact of each individual’s DC, apart from a discrepancy in scores further worsening care partner mood.

Emotional well-being

As hypothesized, better DC was associated with better psychological outcomes and overall well-being for both pwMCI and their care partners. pwMCI DC impacted their own emotional well-being, specifically mood and QoL, as well as the care partner’s anxiety. Care partner DC impacted their own outcomes in mood, anxiety, QoL, and care partner burden. In general, the more each person felt they and their care partner coped well together, the better they were likely to view their own QoL and emotional well-being, even in the context of an MCI diagnosis. This finding is consistent with results from studies of patients with hematological and breast cancers and their care partners, (Pankrath et al., 2018; Rottmann et al., 2015) that found better QoL and relationship satisfaction to be related to better DC.

Interestingly, pwMCI DC did not predict anxiety in pwMCI, but both pwMCI and care partner DC did predict anxiety in care partners. This suggests that the more each person in the dyad feels they and their loved one are coping well together, the less anxious the care partner feels. However, patient anxiety is not impacted by DC. This finding is consistent with studies of patients with prostate cancer and their care partners (Regan et al., 2014), that determined that DC does not seem to relate to patient anxiety but is important in care partner anxiety. Perhaps if the pwMCI still turns to their loved one for support and coping, this makes the partner feel less anxious about the situation.

Not surprisingly, both pwMCI and care partners who feel mutual support in coping with the diagnosis of MCI tend to report better moods. Alternatively, when a dyad feels unable to share their MCI experience with each other, their mood worsens. Further, care partners’ sense of burden appears to relate to how much they feel they can still turn to the pwMCI to cope together in the situation. When the discrepancy in DC between the pwMCI and the care partner increased, this worsened care partner mood above and beyond the impact of their own level of DC. As the discrepancy in DC was often driven by care partners rating DC lower than the pwMCI, this would suggest that when a care partner feels they and their loved one are not coping well together, but the pwMCI believes they are, it further worsens care partner mood.

MCI can cause a lack of awareness of one’s cognitive impairment (anosognosia) and its impact on others (Piras et al., 2016). While we could not examine this specifically with this data set, it is possible that lack of awareness led the pwMCI to still positively rate their DC with their partner, due to lack of awareness of how the cognitive, emotional, and behavioral changes in MCI may impact the relationship. At the same time, the care partner who notices the MCI changes, but does not yet understand the diagnosis of MCI, may perceive symptoms of short-term memory impairment as poor dyadic coping. For example, partners often express frustration that their loved one with MCI just does not listen or does not value their needs when a task discussed is not completed. Often these behaviors reflect the underlying memory impairment of MCI and not a purposeful slight by the patient to their partner, but when the care partner does not yet understand that as a symptom of MCI, they may feel their DC is low.

In general, care partners were more impacted emotionally by their DC than pwMCI. It may be that other factors not measured with this dataset play a bigger role in emotional functioning than DC for the pwMCI themselves. This may include the existential processing of their own future that causes one to look inward rather than turn to others, changes in emotional processing from the disease itself(Cárdenas et al., 2021), and/or lowered insight, as noted above. For care partners, the challenge is instigated by their loved one’s diagnosis. Thus, it makes sense that in coping with their loved one’s diagnosis their own emotional well-being is related to how well they feel the two are processing the situation together.

Cognitive and functioning ability

Though it would seem plausible that relationships with more cognitively impaired pwMCI might fare worse when it comes to DC, there was no relationship between assessed cognition on neuropsychological testing and DC. However, on measures that the care partner completed about their loved one with MCI’s cognition and functioning, the care partners’ DC significantly impacted those measures. Specifically, the higher the care partner DC, the better they rated the pwMCI’s cognitive and functional abilities. It is possible that those who perceive they cope better together also perceive their loved one’s memory to be less severely impacted. Conversely, it may be that those who see their care partner’s memory as significantly poor, may be less able or willing to turn to them to cope together with the stress of their memory decline. Still, given that objective cognitive testing was not related to DC, it remains possible that when a care partner experiences less positive DC with the pwMCI, they may see them as more impaired than they are and rate them more severely on informant-based measures. Prior studies have highlighted how informant-based ratings of functional ability in MCI and dementia may be biased (Kemp et al., 2002; Ready et al., 2004), particularly when the partner is experiencing significant caregiver burden (Long et al., 1998). PwMCI DC does not appear to have a significant relationship with these outcomes, emphasizing the unique contribution of care partner perception of DC to the perception of cognitive and functional ability in dyads managing MCI.

MSS cognitive rehabilitation training

In terms of benefit from the rehabilitation training with the MSS, DC did not have a significant relationship with how much progress patients made in mastering the use of the MSS during the 10-day HABIT session. Past research suggests that training success with the MSS is predicted by the personality style of openness to experience (Khayoun et al., 2022) and degree of cognitive impairment (which in turn impacts long term adherence) (De Wit et al., 2021). Neither the pwMCI’s DC nor the care partner’s DC was important to adherence with use of the MSS at the end of cognitive rehabilitation training sessions with a cognitive rehabilitation specialist during HABIT. However, care partner DC was predictive of pwMCI adherence at baseline (e.g., how well they were using the memory support system before the start of their training sessions) and a three-month post-HABIT follow up. This could suggest that having a care partner who feels they cope well as a team helps the pwMCI feel encouraged to begin rehabilitation training prior to engagement with training professionals and to maintain their competencies on their own post-HABIT. At the same time, a care partner who is more actively benefiting from positive dyadic coping with better mood and QoL and less anxiety and burden may be more apt to support their loved one’s continued use of the new skills that were learned.

The impact of habit on DC

We also examined how DC scores changed after participation in the HABIT program. While HABIT was not designed specifically to improve DC, portions of the program do promote and reinforce behaviors that could potentially enhance DC for both dyad members. Low and average dyadic copers at baseline in the pwMCI group and low dyadic copers at baseline in the care partner groups saw significant improvements in DC post-HABIT. This suggests that participation in the multicomponent intervention that includes cognitive rehabilitation, computerized cognitive training, support group, yoga, and wellness education helps those struggling with DC or pwMCI who have modest DC to feel like they are coping better together in the face of MCI.

One potential mechanism by which HABIT may improve dyadic coping is through the creation of a shared framework for understanding and responding to MCI. Many couples enter the program with differing interpretations of memory failures, emotional reactions, and functional changes. Through wellness education, both members of the dyad may develop a more unified understanding of MCI and its impact on daily life, which may reduce misattributions of cognitive symptoms to lack of effort or interpersonal conflict and instead foster more collaborative problem-solving. The support group experience may additionally normalize emotional reactions, reduce isolation, and encourage more open communication about fears, frustrations, and changing roles within the relationship. Similarly, the yoga and stress management components may reduce emotional distress and improve emotional regulation, thereby enhancing supportive communication during stressful situations. Participation in computerized cognitive training may also foster a sense of active engagement and shared investment in addressing cognitive changes, potentially reinforcing collaborative coping efforts within the dyad. Further, successful implementation of the Memory Support System often requires ongoing communication, encouragement, and shared routines between the pwMCI and care partner, potentially reinforcing the perception that the couple is managing the condition together rather than individually coping with the diagnosis.

We cannot determine from this study which of the components imparted this benefit, but prior research suggests that wellness education improves patient mood and quality of life (Chandler et al., 2019), the compensatory training with the MSS improves care partner mood, anxiety and sense of burden (Cuc et al., 2017; Greenaway et al., 2012), yoga improves sense of care partner burden and anxiety (Amofa et al., 2021), computerized cognitive training benefits care partner anxiety and mood (Cuc et al., 2017), and wellness education improves care partner anxiety (Amofa et al., 2021). As there is a relationship between DC and these emotional wellness variables, it is not surprising that DC improved after HABIT.

High copers at baseline for both pwMCI and care partner groups did see a trend towards decline by the 3-month follow-up. Overall coping in this group remained in the average to high average range. Still, it is important to understand why DC may go down for some pwMCI and care partners post-HABIT. For one, the natural cognitive decline for many with MCI out to the follow-up visit may relate to lowered DC for this group. Additionally, HABIT involves education and training related to MCI, and some pwMCI and care partners become aware of and start to confront the reality of the pwMCI’s cognitive difficulties for the first time during HABIT. Couples may believe that they are coping well with this diagnosis, but for some, once they are confronted with the reality and extent of the problems, they can realize that there are gaps in their coping. Finally, it is possible that some couples may (perhaps unconsciously) present a somewhat rosier picture of their DC when completing such ratings close to the beginning of interacting with clinic staff (e.g., the baseline visit) to convey a positive impression.

Future research and limitations

Future research could examine the different forms of positive DC and determine if any specific types of positive DC or any specific behaviors are more impactful than others. The same research could also be completed for negative forms of DC to better identify problem behaviors that are especially harmful for couples with MCI. Additionally, the HABIT Program could also be further tailored to focus on improving DC and positive DC behaviors.

This study does have several limitations. The sample is not random nor representative of the general population. The overwhelming majority of participants were white and well educated. Participation in the HABIT Program involves ten full days of attending the program and associated billing to insurance and cost to participants. This limits participation to those with ample economic resources. Additionally, participants were referred by their clinicians to the HABIT Program and chose to participate. Both the selection of who is referred and who opts to enroll in the HABIT Program may be biased towards those who seek to cope better than they are currently coping with the diagnosis. Insight of the patients into their cognition and functioning was not formally assessed. It is possible that poor insight may have led to an overestimation of DC, but we are unable to explore this relationship in our data.

The response rate of completed the DCI at 3 months (64.32% of pwMCI and 67.80% of care partners) may present a subsample of HABIT participants that are not representative of all of those who engaged in HABIT, which may limit our understanding of how DC impacts HABIT outcomes. Further, the lack of a randomized control group limits our ability to assert that HABIT improves DC. It is possible that other factors influenced the improvement in DC seen pre and post HABIT.

Conclusion

In sum, there are clear benefits associated with better DC. The more a couple feels they are coping well together, the better they both will feel emotionally. Willingness to participate and ongoing adherence with cognitive rehabilitation training may also be enhanced by better care partner DC. Additionally, the HABIT Program could represent a novel approach for improving the DC of those who are not coping well together or have moderate DC in facing the diagnosis of MCI. A diagnosis of MCI can represent a major stressor and burden for couples, but improving DC represents a potential avenue to improve their well-being.
