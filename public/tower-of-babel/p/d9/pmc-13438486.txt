Drivers of social and ecological resilience in tropical homegarden systems
Authors: Mary Ann George, Martin Oliver Reader, Maarten Boudewijn Eppinga, Maria João Santos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAgroforestry systems, such as homegardens, provide social-ecological benefits but face increasing pressures from climate change. In this study, we identify drivers of social and ecological resilience to perturbances in homegardens by examining the influence of livelihood assets, ecological diversity, legacy effects and system age. We collected data from around 360 homegardens in Kerala, India, using household surveys, agrobiodiversity plots and remote sensing-derived vegetation data and analysed them by fitting linear and logistic regression models. Social resilience was positively associated with access to credit, homegarden size and past shock exposure, while ecological resilience was positively associated with plant access and negatively linked to homegarden age. Our findings suggest that homegarden resilience may be facilitated by tailored interventions that enhance financial certainty and access to plant resources, as well as preparedness strategies. These insights are crucial for ensuring the resilience of homegarden systems amid increasing environmental and socioeconomic challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13280-026-02357-3.Keywords: Agroforestry, Homegardens, Livelihoods, Resilience, Social-ecological systems

Introduction

Agroforestry, the integration of trees, crops and livestock on the same agricultural land (Nair 1993), is a promising approach to sustainable agriculture that combines food security with biodiversity conservation (Kremen and Merenlender 2018). However, agroforestry systems themselves face escalating pressures from land use change and climate change (Kumar and Nair 2004). These pressures reduce biodiversity and jeopardize the ecosystem services provided by agroforestry systems, which in turn support agricultural production (Foley et al. 2011; Dannenberg et al. 2024). For instance, climate change has reduced the natural capital stock, intensified tree-crop resource competition and reduced crop yields in tropical agroforestry globally (Watts et al. 2022). In response, agroforestry systems must exhibit resilience to these shocks and disturbances so that they can maintain ecosystem service provisioning (Meuwissen et al. 2019). This resilience in complex social-ecological systems, such as agroforestry, integrates both social and ecological dimensions. There have been numerous studies, such as Jacobi et al. (2015a, b), Keprate et al. (2024) and Nyong et al. (2020) that consider building social and ecological resilience through agroforestry systems compared to conventional agricultural practices. However, limited studies assess both the social and ecological resilience of social-ecological systems themselves (Aslan et al. 2021; Schatte and Meyer 2025). Mallick et al. (2024) evaluated both social and ecological resilience in tribal homegardens in a protected area in India, while Sari et al. (2023) measures both aspects particularly in agroforestry systems in Indonesia. Nevertheless, we need more quantitative assessments of resilience in agroforestry settings, particularly with regard to including the social dimension (Schatte and Meyer 2025).

From an ecological perspective, resilience refers to the ability to cope with challenges (Folke 2016) and for ecosystem structure and function to persist after perturbance (Holling 1973). Some characteristics of agroforestry systems might enhance their ecological resilience. For example, increasing soil organic matter reduces the system’s vulnerability to water stress since humidity is retained, thus improving soil resilience (Halberg and Muller 2012). Similarly, trees provide cooling benefits and a suitable microclimate (Zeppetello et al. 2022), defend against pest calamities by increasing parasitoid diversity (Sperber et al. 2004) and control soil erosion during floods by reducing water runoff (Udawatta et al. 2021). Thus, it is of interest to quantify ecological resilience and assess the relative importance of its different drivers in agroforestry systems.

From a social perspective, resilience represents the ability to recognise, mitigate and adapt to shocks. Social resilience can be captured through the perceptions of farmers of their capacity to face challenges and adapt to potential shocks and disturbances in the future (Adger 2006; Darnhofer 2014). Individual perceptions mould decision-making and so the farming or management strategies adopted; hence, it is critical to incorporate such stakeholder perspectives in resilience assessments (Béné et al. 2016; Jezeer et al. 2019). While perspectives may not always lead to suitable adaptation strategies, people’s perception of themselves and their abilities can influence outcomes in times of disturbances (Armitage et al. 2012). For example, in Costa Rican coffee agroforestry landscapes, the subjective perception of vulnerability, risk severity, and adaptation costs were important determinants on how adaptation took place (Rodríguez-Barillas et al. 2024). Hence, there is value in determining drivers of social resilience.

Both the social and the ecological dimensions might affect the resilience of a social-ecological system, and hence, it is imperative to consider the resilience of the social component and the ecological component in tandem to get a holistic perspective of resilience (Cumming et al. 2015). The different elements of social-ecological systems that determine resilience may be connected in different ways and with varying strength (Cumming et al. 2015), potentially giving rise to synergies or trade-offs between social and ecological resilience. Therefore, it is relevant to study social and ecological resilience within the same analysis framework, to not only identify the drivers of resilience but also to understand whether they coincide or differ for social and ecological resilience of systems, such as agroforestry systems.

Homegardens are a type of agroforestry system, covering approximately 8 million ha in South and Southeast Asia (Mahmud et al. 2020). Homegardens are traditional, integrated tree–crop–livestock production systems on small units of land next to human dwellings (Kumar and Nair 2004), where the efficient nutrient cycling, conservation of bio-cultural diversity (Montagnini 2006) and product diversification improves local food security and sustainability (Pushpakumara et al. 2012). Homegarden structure and function differs, depending on location (urban, peri-urban or rural) (George et al. 2024) and the disturbances they are exposed to (Kumar and Nair 2004). In a local context, severe flood impacts, such as the catastrophic flood in 2018 in Kerala, India, have been related to threefold reductions in homegarden soil quality and microarthropod density (Lakshmi et al. 2021). As homegardens face increasing disturbances, insights on what drives their social and ecological resilience can therefore provide multiple benefits.

Several critical features potentially influence the social-ecological resilience of homegardens: livelihood capitals, ecological diversity, legacy effects and system age (Milestad and Darnhofer 2003; Speranza 2010; Seidl et al. 2014; Jacobi et al. 2015a, b; Meuwissen et al. 2019; Paas et al. 2021). Few studies have aimed to understand the relevance of these drivers to social and ecological resilience, and particularly, agroforestry landscapes (although see Viñals et al. 2023). Therefore, our objective is to examine the influence of household livelihood capitals, ecological diversity, legacies and system age on the social and ecological resilience of agroforestry systems, specifically, homegardens in Kerala, India (Fig. 1). We used the naturally occurring sequence of conditions before, during and after the 2018 flooding to calculate the ecological resilience of homegardens, using remotely sensed data to assess vegetation productivity. We operationalize social resilience as the perception of farmers as being resilient to future perturbances collected through household surveys. We hypothesize that higher livelihood capitals, ecological diversity, and homegarden age enhance social and ecological resilience, but that legacy effects might erode ecological resilience due to insufficient time to recover from previous disturbances.

Materials and methods

Analytical framework for response and predictor variables

Response variables

Ecological resilience can be measured through three metrics, namely: resistance, recovery, and resilience (Ingrisch and Bahn 2018; Sturm et al. 2022; note to avoid ambiguity, we refer to the overarching construct as 'ecological resilience' and the specific metric as 'resilience'). Resistance is the capability to withstand disturbance, recovery is the capacity to reclaim performance after the disturbance, and resilience is the capacity to regain pre-disturbance levels of ecosystem productivity (Lloret et al. 2011; Sturm et al. 2022). While we use the term, ecological resilience, to emphasize the resilience of social-ecological systems, the characteristics of such resilience coincides with that of “engineering resilience” from Holling (1973).

Social resilience, i.e. the perception of being able to overcome future shocks, was collected from household surveys. Social resilience was classified as 1, if homegardeners expected shocks in the future and perceived that they had the ability to deal with them, or 0, if they perceived themselves as being incapable of dealing with expected shocks. Those who had faced shocks but expected none in the future and, hence, had no solutions for dealing with them, were also classified as 0, given that households subjected to past disturbances likely possess fewer assets and have lower risk tolerance.

Predictor variables

This study examines the influence of four key drivers on resilience: household livelihood capitals, ecological diversity, legacy effects and system age. Livelihood capitals—comprising of assets that buffer losses and enable adaptation—are central to social resilience, as they facilitate adaptation to shocks and disturbances (Speranza et al. 2014). Greater assets or income can expand risk horizons, fostering innovation and adaptive strategies (Armitage et al. 2012; Béné et al. 2016). Social assets, such as community cohesion and leadership, further enhance perceived coping capacity (Schwarz et al. 2011). In lake social-ecological systems, social capital supports livelihood diversification by improving access to resources, skills, credit, remittances, and migration opportunities (Goulden et al. 2013). Conversely, constraints on livelihood assets, including financial limitations, resource competition, reduced labour supply and market exclusion, undermine food system resilience in agroforestry systems (Jacobi et al. 2018).

Ecological diversity refers to the variation in land use within farm as well as landscape variation that allow for a wide range of ways to face disturbances. Ecological diversity increases the alternatives available for responding to system fluctuations without leading to a collapse of the entire system (Norberg et al. 2008; Chapin et al. 2010). Biodiversity provides an "insurance effect," buffering against disturbances through redundancy species traits, community composition and function, and the resulting ecosystem processes (Gunderson and Holling 2002; Berkes 2007). Diverse species assemblages increase the capacity for ecosystem functions (Cabell and Oelofse 2012), thereby likely improving resilience against changing climatic conditions (Di Falco and Chavas 2008). Diverse homegardens are also likely to be socially resilient since heterogeneity of cultivated products increases resilience to disturbances through income stability (Mbow et al. 2014) by spreading the risk of harvest failure. In this study, we measured ecological diversity using four metrics—Shannon–Wiener diversity within homegarden, dietary diversity of homegarden, farm heterogeneity and landscape tree cover.

Legacy effects, or number of shocks experienced in the past, can enhance social resilience by improving preparedness and strategy development for future disturbances (Yu et al. 2016). Perceptions about the probability and severity of risk and an experiential understanding of the costs and benefits associated with adaptation strategies can be affected by previous experiences and thereafter build or erode social resilience (Cinner and Barnes 2019). Alternatively, homegardens subjected to numerous past perturbances may lack available resources to fend off future perturbances, and are therefore either risk-averse or possess lower risk tolerance. Meanwhile, homegardens that have faced numerous shocks in the past may have low ecological resilience due to insufficient time to recover from past disturbances.

System age often equates higher complexity, with older and more complex agroforestry systems (Mang and Brodie 2015), exhibiting greater biodiversity compared to younger or simpler systems (Udawatta et al. 2021). In the tropics, tree height, canopy cover, biomass, habitat complexity, and species richness increase with system age (Mang and Brodie 2015). Tropical plantations exhibit positive associations between plantation age and biodiversity, wherein older plantations support more species than younger plantations, particularly those shared with primary vegetation landscapes (Tudge et al. 2023). Consequently, older homegardens may demonstrate greater ecological resilience due to their inherent higher biodiversity (Gbedomon et al. 2015; Williams et al. 2018). Older homegardens, having been through prolonged engagement in collaborative efforts, the sharing of ideas, and/or exchange of plant material, may also exhibit tighter social networks through community networking, trust and reciprocity (Buchmann 2009; Osei Boateng et al. 2025) and in turn, enhanced social resilience because homegardeners are reassured of their capacity to withstand shocks due to their embeddedness within a community.

Study site

The state of Kerala (Fig. 2) is one of the smallest states in India with an area of 38 852 km2 and a population of 33.4 million (Government of India 2011). It is topographically diverse with plains, wetlands and coasts to the west and the Western Ghats to the east of the state. Despite the high population density, the vicinity of the global biodiversity hotspot, the Western Ghats, creates a large potential for agroforestry systems to contribute to biodiversity conservation. About 80% of the 6.3 million agricultural small holdings in Kerala are homegardens (Kumar et al. 2006), covering 1.2 million ha in 2000–2001 (Guillerme et al. 2011). Homegardens are the dominant agroforestry practice in Kerala, with a vertically diverse combination of trees and crops around homesteads, sometimes alongside livestock (Kumar and Nair 2004). The three districts (Fig. 2) in the uplands of Kerala, where we conducted our study, are sub-divisions of the Western Ghats, and are characterized by a higher proportion of smaller homegardens (Sankar and Chandrashekara 2002). These three districts were also selected because fewer studies on homegardens have been conducted in these areas compared to those in coastal Kerala (Kunhamu, pers. comm.), and they were some of the districts subjected to the severe flooding in 2018 in the state, during the monsoon season (June to August). In 2018, Kerala had experienced unusually high precipitation and severe flooding, which led to the evacuation of over a million people. Numerous houses in these three districts experienced significant subsidence as a result of flooding, with many households having reported temporary relocation to the residences of neighbours or relatives due to flood-related displacement.

Data collection

In this study, data for the response and predictor variables were collected using interdisciplinary tools, namely structured household surveys, agrobiodiversity plots in homegardens and remote sensing-derived data. For each of these datasets, we explain in the next sections the methodology and the indicators measured using each methodology and data.

Household surveys

We sampled six urban centres in Kerala, their selection in consultation with an expert from the Kerala Agricultural University specializing in homegardens (Kunhamu, pers. comm.). In each centre, we set up three 10 km transects along which we surveyed a total of 360 households and their homegardens between February and April 2023. Transects were set along main roads, spaced apart by at least 30°. We avoided directions with nearby urban centres (i.e. over 20 km), to ensure that there is a single distance to one focal urban centre attributable to each homegarden per transect. At each transect, we surveyed five households, randomly sampled along four sub-transects at ≈0 km, ≈3 km, ≈6 km, and ≈9 km from the urban centre. We excluded households near main roads to avoid the road network's influence on homegarden characteristics, such as biodiversity. We ensured a minimum gap of two houses between selected households to avoid sampling similar or related households, common due to land inheritance. Data were collected simultaneously across all three transects in each urban centre by three teams of two field researchers.

We collected data on household livelihood assets, i.e. natural, social, human, physical and financial capitals, following the Sustainable Livelihoods Framework (SLF) (DFID, UK 1999; Scoones 1998), and using structured household questionnaires (Table 1; Appendix S1). Homegardeners were requested to provide their consent using an ethics-and-responsible-research consent form, developed in line with the University of Zurich guidelines on fieldwork ethics and safety. Surveys were conducted in Malayalam after translation from English and reviewed for local suitability and then recorded digitally using KoboToolbox.

Using structured household surveys, we also collected data on dietary diversity by using the Food Consumption Score (FCS), an indicator developed by the World Food Programme (WFP) based on a recall survey of the different food types consumed in the previous week (WFP 2006). We collected data on the frequency of consumption of 16 different food types in the past week that were obtained from the homegarden (Appendix S2). Data on farm heterogeneity were also collected using structured household survey, which is the sum of additional land uses within a homegarden, i.e. the presence of a vegetable garden, ornamental garden and medicinal garden would indicate a farm heterogeneity of three (Appendix S3). Legacy effects as measured by number of past shocks are an aggregation of the number of environmental, economic and social shocks reported by the homegardeners during the household survey (Appendix S3), as is the case for homegarden age (Appendix S1).

Agrobiodiversity plots

The number of different planted species of trees and crops and their abundances in the homegarden within a 10 m by 10 m plot were recorded (Appendix S4) and used to calculate the Shannon–Wiener diversity index as it captures both the number of plant species (richness) and their relative abundance (evenness). This index is suitable to detect subtle changes across homegardens and has been identified as a useful metric within assessments of homegarden system resilience (Kuruppuarachchi et al. 2024). For homegardens less than 10 cents (local area metric corresponding to ca. 0.00041 km2) in size, the entire homegarden was considered for plant identification and abundance. If household members stated that they used different parcels in their homegardens for growing different trees and crops, then multiple plots were established, and averages were used to calculate Shannon–Wiener diversity.

Remote sensing-derived data

The three metrics of ecological resilience were measured using remote sensing-derived data using the methodology of Sturm et al. (2022) (Fig. 3). These metrics were calculated by extracting the Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) values, which measures the difference between reflectance in the Near-Infrared (NIR) and red. We used the NIR and red bands of Sentinel 2A sensor. To capture the period right after the floods that happened from the 15th to the 17th of August, 2018, we assessed all available images between September and October 2018, and used for the first available data for tiles with less than 10% cloud cover. We also gathered data on NDVI for the middle of the growing period (January to February) of the following year, wherein homegardens would be productive if crops were planted and trees were not damaged. Data analysis

All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.2 (R Core Team 2024). After data curation, the datasets included 68 observations for social resilience as the response variable, 88 for resistance and 290 for recovery and resilience. Given the continuous nature of ecological resilience metrics, linear regressions were applied, with non-normally distributed predictors transformed to meet model assumptions. For the resistance dataset, square-root transformations were applied to homegarden elevation, age, family labour, distance to market, and landscape tree cover, while food expenses and homegarden income proportions were log-transformed. Similarly, in the recovery and resilience dataset, homegarden elevation, age, family labour and distance to market were square-root-transformed, and food expenses were log-transformed. Thereafter, to avoid multicollinearity, Pearson’s correlation tests were performed. Three separate correlation matrices were plotted to account for varying sample sizes across datasets. Since all variables only indicated low or moderate correlations, they were all included in the next stages of analysis.

Due to the limited number of observations post-data preparation for assessing social resilience, a multi-stage modelling approach was adopted, culminating in a combined model incorporating significant predictors from previous models (Morin et al. 2020). To assess social resilience drivers, we first applied logistic regression including all livelihood assets as predictors, using the glm function with a binomial logit link. Stepwise regression (both directions) retained only significant predictors (p < 0.05). Variance inflation factors (VIF), assessed via the car package (vif function; Fox and Weisberg 2019), were below 2, indicating no collinearity (Zuur et al. 2010). A final model incorporated significant livelihood assets alongside the four metrics of ecological diversity, past shocks, and homegarden age, using glm for multiple logistic regression. Homegarden elevation was included as a confounder due to its influence on vegetation productivity and resource access. All variables were standardised using minimum and maximum values to ensure comparability, and model residuals were assessed for compliance with assumptions. Additionally, a chi-square test evaluated the significance of soil fertility by comparing groups with and without soil fertility in relation to social resilience.

To analyse the drivers of resistance, recovery, and resilience, we applied multiple linear regression models, as all response variables were continuous. A stepwise regression (both directions) was performed, incorporating each of the livelihood assets, ecological diversity metrics, homegarden age, past shocks and legacy as predictors, given sufficient sample sizes. Model assumptions were validated by assessing homoscedasticity (residuals vs. fitted values) and residual normality (Q-Q plot). VIF values were examined to confirm the absence of multicollinearity. For resistance, a Mann–Whitney U test evaluated the significance of soil fertility differences, while another Mann–Whitney U test assessed differences in recovery based on access to plant material, following model results indicating its significance.

To confirm that we have identified the most suitable predictive model for social resilience and the three metrics of ecological resilience, we additionally employed the glmulti R package, which performs automated model selection (Calcagno and Mazancourt 2010), and allows for an unbiased exploration of all possible model combinations. Since we had numerous categorical predictors, we chose to run the genetic algorithm approach (Calcagno and Mazancourt 2010). Given the small sample sizes, we chose for model selection using the Akaike Information Criterion corrected (AICc) to rank competing models (Burnham and Anderson 2004). The final set of relevant predictors was determined based on a general threshold criterion of 0.8, selecting models within ΔAICc ≤ 2 of the best-performing model.

Results

Pearson correlation tests for the different datasets with their different sample sizes indicate no strong correlations among drivers of social resilience (Fig. 4a) and ecological resilience (Fig. 4b, c). Nevertheless, we do observe some moderate correlations (± 0.4 to 0.7) as per conventional approaches to interpreting correlation coefficients (Schober et al. 2018). Homegarden size is negatively correlated with income derived from homegardens and Shannon–Wiener diversity. Similarly, for the social resilience dataset, farming experience is negatively associated with proportion of income from homegardens, while positive correlations are observed between homegarden elevation and farm heterogeneity, as well as number of past shocks and credit access. In the case of resistance, homegarden age shows a moderate negative correlation with irrigation and Shannon–Wiener diversity, while a positive correlation is observed between homegarden age and size. We also find positive moderate correlations between Shannon–Wiener diversity and irrigation, and savings and education. For recovery and resilience, positive correlations emerge between landscape tree cover and homegarden size, homegarden age and farming experience, and homegarden elevation and past shocks.

The model results indicate that livelihood assets such as access to credit, homegarden size and number of past shocks are positively associated with social resilience. Meanwhile, homegarden age is negatively associated with resistance, while plant access is positively associated with recovery. Additionally, homegarden elevation is positively associated with social resilience, resistance and recovery.

Drivers of social resilience

Significant predictors of social resilience were homegarden size, soil fertility, credit access, number of past shocks and homegarden elevation (Table 2 and Fig. 5). Increases in homegarden size, number of past shocks, and credit access are positively associated with social resilience. Homegarden elevation is weakly associated with social resilience as indicated by the low estimate indicating a slope of regression that is approximately null. While soil fertility indicated a negative association with social resilience, the chi-square test between differences between groups with and without soil fertility in the presence and absence of social resilience shows no significance (Fig. 5b). Analysis of residuals also indicated that all model assumptions were met (Figs. S1 and S2). Cross-referencing with the genetic algorithm approach also indicates the same set of predictors as most relevant, and in addition, health (Fig. S3).

Drivers of ecological resilience

We find significant associations (p < 0.05) between resistance and soil fertility, homegarden age and elevation (Table 3 and Fig. 6). The model was significant (F-statistic (5, 82) = 5.12, p = 0.0004), and explained 23.79% of the variance in resistance (R2 = 0.238, Adjusted R2 = 0.1914). While soil fertility is negatively associated with resistance, Mann–Whitney U test reveals no significance between groups with and without soil fertility in their extent of resistance. Meanwhile, younger homegardens and those at higher elevations had higher resistance. To understand the association between homegarden age and resistance by considering the species diversity in homegardens, we also find significant association wherein younger homegardens have higher Shannon–Wiener diversity, β = − 0.07, SE = 0.016, p = 0.00001 (model also indicates significance, F-statistic (1, 86) = 21.11, p = 0.00001, and explains 19.71% variance in Shannon–Wiener diversity). Analysis of residuals also indicated that all model assumptions were met (Fig. S4). Cross-referencing with the genetic algorithm approach also indicates the same set of predictors as most relevant, and in addition, Shannon–Wiener diversity and distance to markets (Fig. S5).

Discussion

In this study, we sought to find the associations between drivers of social and ecological resilience: livelihood assets, ecological diversity, legacy effects and systems age, by using a common analytical framework for both aspects of resilience, in homegardens in Kerala. We learn that social resilience is significantly positively associated with livelihood assets such as access to credit and homegarden size, as well as number of past shocks. Meanwhile, resistance is primarily associated negatively with homegarden age, while access to plants increased recovery. The confounding variable, homegarden elevation is significant for social as well as ecological resilience. None of the drivers exhibit synergies or trade-offs between social and ecological resilience, and instead, they target either of the two types, indicating the possibility of targeting different drivers to enhance homegarden resilience.

Drivers of social resilience

Larger homegardens are positively associated with social resilience. This is likely because landholding size might provide more wealth, options for diversified cropping systems and a portfolio of options that deliver wealth, and these livelihood diversification strategies may mitigate future perturbances (Habib et al. 2023). Access to credit is also significantly linked to home gardeners' ability to anticipate future shocks and their confidence in developing strategies to address these challenges. This financial resource strengthens households’ capability to manage potential disruptions by reducing uncertainty about meeting future financial needs and ensuring financial stability during times of crisis. Several homegardeners in our study reported experiencing ongoing financial stress, attributed to limited capacity to engage in income-generating activities due to health-related issues, reduced earnings resulting from crop yield losses, and additional economic burdens such as marriage expenses. Where wealth is limited, access to subsidies and credit opportunities can enhance the social resilience of homegardeners. These findings reflect previous studies on the links between climate change resilience and financial inclusion and access (Hussain et al. 2021), such as in smallholder farming systems in Ghana (Batung et al. 2023).

Homegardeners with greater past shock exposure exhibited enhanced social resilience, in contrast to findings in Ghana that prior exposure was associated with diminished resilience (Batung et al. 2023). This contrast may reflect differences in the types of shocks experienced or homegardeners’ adaptive capacities. Larger shocks may limit adaptation time and resources, while smaller shocks can enhance preparedness. Our findings are likely due to homegardeners’ experience in managing similar challenges in the past, since experience, and the tools and codes of conduct to deal with such adversities can be sources of knowledge and help withstand disturbances (Berkes 2007).

Drivers of ecological resilience

We find that older homegardens exhibit lower resistance. While the statistical effect size is small, this finding could reflect gradual ecological shifts over time, such as soil nutrient depletion, changes in species composition or reduced management intensity. The significant negative association between Shannon–Wiener diversity and homegarden age shows that higher diversity is found in younger homegardens. This highlights that older homegardens have dominant plant species, possibly due to more plantation-style maintenance of homegardens for commercial needs in Kerala (Peyre et al. 2006). As such, this homogenization of biodiversity with homegarden age could reduce the potential of biodiversity to mediate the effects of climatic extremes, such as floods, on homegardens (Isbell et al. 2015), i.e. reducing its resistance.

We also observe that access to plant material has a positive, albeit weak, association with recovery, suggesting that access to plant materials such as saplings, from plant nurseries, farming associations or neighbours and kin, is an important resource in encouraging planting activities in homegardens. Indeed, several homegardeners reported receiving saplings and seeds at no cost annually from the “Kudumbashree Mission”, which is a women-centred community-based poverty eradication and livelihood program, implemented by the Government of Kerala (S R and S 2017). These findings also resonate with those from Bolivia of local farmers’ organisations providing access to seedlings and plant material, enhancing the resilience of cocoa agroforestry systems (Jacobi et al. 2015a, b). Meanwhile, in other regions such as Indonesia (Sari et al. 2023) and Ghana (Alhassan et al. 2019), farmers were found to plant different, shock-resistant trees and crops post-disturbances. These findings highlight the importance of supporting homegardeners with shock-resistant, locally suitable plants to promote farming.

We found no significant drivers of resilience in homegardens, possibly because our metric did not fully capture resilience with the time frame we used in our analyses. The system may not have recovered to pre-2018 productivity levels due to recurring annual floods of similar intensity. Persistent floods and landslides could be causing soil erosion, hindering vegetation recovery. Additionally, these floods may be discouraging homegardeners from cultivating as they did before 2018.

Common drivers of social and ecological resilience

The significance of elevation for social and ecological resilience underscores the relevance of physical location when it relates to extreme weather events. The observed weak, yet positive associations between homegarden elevation and resistance suggest homegardens at higher elevations are, to some extent, less impacted by floods and maintain their vegetation productivity more effectively. In the case of resistance, better drainage could reduce risk of waterlogging and elevation might reduce likelihood of homegardens remaining submerged after floods, reducing crop damage (Ngumbi 2025).

The weak positive association between homegarden elevation and social resilience may stem from larger landholdings at higher elevations, offering financial certainty during shocks, including potential for land sales. Wealthier households have resources to invest in income diversification, through commodity farming, off-farm work, or even migration to safer regions (e.g. as observed in Pakistan, Abid et al. 2016). This weak association could also reflect how poorer households with little land at higher elevations in these regions in Kerala tend to have steeper slopes, which are more prone to landslides caused by torrential rainfall (Raj and Sofi 2023).

Limitations

This study examines households that survived the 2018 Kerala floods, excluding cases of total collapse, meaning that it may overestimate resilience and underrepresent fragility. Additionally, self-reported data introduces recall and response biases, as resilience perceptions may be influenced by subjective experiences or social desirability. Furthermore, while key social and ecological resilience drivers are identified, their causal mechanisms remain unclear. For instance, self-reported soil fertility could be refined by integrating objective biophysical assessments through measurements of soil compactness (Alaoui et al. 2018) or drainage (Saco et al. 2021), which affect flood and landslide impacts, respectively. There are also disadvantages to solely capturing homegarden biodiversity using the Shannon–Wiener diversity index, as this index does not include other important dimensions of biodiversity such as genetic variation or functional diversity (dos Santos et al. 2021).

Future research opportunities

Moreover, the study considers abrupt shocks, but not gradual changes like climate warming (Lee and Zhao 2021), and distinguishing between both is valuable in determining variations in impacts on resilience. Future research should also distinguish between drivers that induce fast changes in system resilience compared to those that induce slow changes (Armitage et al. 2012). This will allow targeting enhancement of drivers such that resilience can be improved in the time it takes to enhance the social and ecological resilience of the system. There is also value in incorporating longitudinal data during and after extreme events to account for the drivers of fluctuations in social and ecological resilience.

Policy recommendations

National or regional policies that prioritize the financial inclusion of citizens and preparedness strategies in the face of extreme climatic events could enhance individuals' confidence in handling uncertainties. This could potentially improve the social resilience of homegardeners (Williams et al. 2018). The relevance of elevation highlights the need for local institutions to support homegardeners in enhancing their social-ecological resilience. Such support could range from elevation-based risk assessments and aid in the construction of retaining walls, a common adaptation in Kerala (Raj and Sofi 2023), particularly for houses along steep slopes. Furthermore, the homogenization of biodiversity that can be disadvantageous to homegarden ecological resilience can be prevented by encouraging the planting and maintenance of diverse woody plants and/or trees. Such measures could be facilitated by granting landowners full ownership rights over trees on their private land. Ownership rights would eliminate bureaucratic barriers associated with tree harvesting, for example in Kerala external approvals from regulatory bodies such as the Forest Department are required to harvest trees on private land (Guillerme et al. 2011). By eliminating such institutional barriers, landowners may be more incentivised to integrate tree cultivation into their land use practices.

Conclusion

In this study, we emphasize that a holistic assessment of system resilience includes both ecological resilience as well as social resilience, and it is worth evaluating both in future research pursuits. Furthermore, we indicate the social resilience is positively reinforced by factors such as access to credit, larger homegarden sizes and experiential learning from past disturbances, while homegarden age emerges as a driver of deteriorating ecological resistance. Since our results did not identify trade-offs between these drivers concerning their influence on social-ecological resilience, these factors could be individually targeted and enabled by current management strategies and policies in the region to enhance homegarden resilience. The first factors are actionable, either by creating cooperatives to improve homegarden management as a collaborative process, assist with accessing credit and may also enhance transfer of learnings between homegarden owners. Yet, resistance decreases with homegarden age, which might indicate a need for restoration. Meanwhile, recovery in homegardens benefits from access to plant material encouraging planting activities in privately owned homegarden systems. Further, local climate adaptation strategies and governance could benefit from removing institutional barriers to biodiversity conservation and restoration, and prioritize programs for sharing preparedness tactics to reduce impacts of potential disturbances. These would gain from and encourage homegardeners in continued planting activities, as opposed to individuals questioning the possibility of maintaining their households and homegardens in times of distress. Given predictions of perturbations of higher frequency and larger magnitudes in Kerala and other regions in the world, strengthening these social and ecological resilience-enhancing factors would facilitate improving the adaptive capacity and long-term sustainability of homegardens in these regions.
