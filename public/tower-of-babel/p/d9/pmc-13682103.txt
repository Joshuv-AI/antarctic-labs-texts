Beyond medical care: public health insurance, caloric intake, and labor productivity in rural China
Authors: Yunze Han, Kei Kajisa
Published: 2026

Introduction

Poor people remain trapped in poverty because limited budgets prevent sufficient investment in human capital [1]. Health-related financial burdens constrain such investments through multiple channels. High medical costs directly restrict access to medical care, weakening the labor force’s human health capital [2, 3]. At the same time, such costs indirectly reduce nonmedical consumption [4, 5], with items such as food being crucial for human capital [6, 7]. Many governments have introduced public health insurance (PHI) programs to reduce such financial burdens.

Previous studies have documented the overall effects of PHI and shown that it reduces poverty and increases income among disadvantaged groups [8–10]. In addition to these impact assessments, researchers have explored the mechanisms of human capital. The direct pathway is widely recognized to operate through increased medical care, which boosts health status [11]. Multiple studies, which have examined diverse PHI schemes, have consistently indicated that PHI increases the utilization of outpatient, inpatient, and preventive medical services [12–14] and improves health [15, 16]. However, Lei and Lin [17] suggested that this direct pathway is limited in some contexts, leaving conclusions about the direct pathway obscure.

In addition to the direct pathway, PHI may also generate an indirect pathway for human capital. By reducing medical expenses [18, 19] and the necessity for precautionary savings [20, 21], PHI may increase the budget for nonmedical expenditures. Some categories of nonmedical consumption may function as human capital inputs. For manual laborers in many developing countries, food is an important example, as calories are a critical resource for sustaining labor productivity [6, 7, 22, 23].1 This suggests a potential indirect pathway: PHI may increase caloric intake and thereby support productivity.

However, this indirect pathway remains ambiguous. First, some studies have indicated that PHI increases overall food consumption [25, 26], but this does not necessarily imply an increase in caloric intake. Food expenditure is an important indicator of improved welfare, but it may also partly reflect factors that are not directly related to human capital, such as taste-oriented food choices, rather than increased energy intake. Caloric intake, by contrast, can be interpreted more directly as a nutritional input relevant to labor productivity for manual laborers in low-income, labor-intensive settings [6, 7, 22, 23]. Examining caloric intake therefore allows us to assess whether PHI is associated with nutrition-related human capital. Second, even if PHI is associated with higher caloric intake, the productivity implications of additional calories remain uncertain, because the role of calories in supporting work capacity may differ across levels of development and population groups. Consequently, it is necessary to examine both the calorie and productivity components to assess the empirical relevance of this potential indirect pathway.

This study examines a potential indirect pathway through China’s New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme (NRCMS), a major PHI program introduced in 2003 for rural residents. Financed jointly by individuals, rural communities, and government subsidies, it features low premiums and voluntary participation. The NRCMS aims to increase access to medical care and has been shown to boost nonmedical consumption [27]. In the 2000s, caloric intake in rural China remained insufficient [28, 29], making it plausible that NRCMS participation may be linked to higher caloric intake and subsequent productivity.

The study examines two hypotheses: whether NRCMS participation is associated with individuals’ calorie consumption in the year of participation, and whether calorie consumption is associated with productivity in the following year (productivity was measured as earnings per working day).

The China Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS), an unbalanced panel survey that collects individual data biannually and quadrennially from 1989 to 2015, enabled us to examine these two hypotheses. Among its survey waves, the 2009 and 2011 waves are particularly suitable for this purpose because they allow us to link NRCMS participation and caloric intake in 2009 to labor productivity in 2010. For the first hypothesis, we used the variables of program participation and calorie consumption from the 2009 survey wave. For the second hypothesis, we used the “total income for 2010” divided by the “number of working days in 2010” recorded in the 2011 survey wave. Leveraging the panel data structure, we used the caloric intake of a particular person in 2009 to explain the productivity of the same person in 2010, which helps mitigate concerns about reverse causality from higher productivity to more calorie consumption.2

We used two empirical methods appropriate for the nature of each data structure. For the first hypothesis (NRCMS participation and calorie intake), we applied propensity score matching (PSM) to adjust for observed differences related to self-selection in participation. For the second hypothesis (calorie intake and productivity), we used multiple regression with inverse probability weighting (IPW) to control for covariates and mitigate potential attrition bias possibly stemming from the unbalanced panel. The estimates indicate that NRCMS participation was associated with approximately 294 kcal/day higher caloric intake. A caloric difference of this magnitude was associated with approximately 2.97%–3.91% higher labor productivity, even after controlling for other possible determinants. These two associations are consistent with a potential indirect pathway involving NRCMS participation, caloric intake, and subsequent productivity.

This study contributes to the existing literature in two ways. First, it contributes to the public health and health insurance literature. Previous studies have shown that PHI can raise income or reduce poverty among vulnerable groups [8–10], but the human capital channels underlying these broader economic outcomes need further examination. Beyond the direct pathway through medical care and health capital, this study documents two related associations: NRCMS participation is associated with higher caloric intake, and higher caloric intake is associated with higher labor productivity. These patterns are consistent with a potential indirect pathway in which PHI may relax budget constraints in ways that support nutrition-related human capital in low-income rural settings.

Second, this study contributes to development and labor economics by highlighting calories as a component of productivity-related human capital, a classic hypothesis proposed by Strauss [6]. We argue that calories are crucial in a human capital framework in which physical labor dominates subsistence income.

Background

In the late 1950s, the Chinese government established the Cooperative Medical System (CMS) to improve rural access to medical care. The system operates mainly on rural community funding, with contributions from individuals and government subsidies to build health centers and provide medicines, enabling affordable care [30]. By the 1970s, the CMS covered 90% of China’s rural population and was considered a successful model for addressing healthcare in developing countries [31]. However, by the 1980s, rural economic system reforms replaced collective agricultural production with private production, causing the CMS to lose its primary funding source and collapse [32]. By 1998, rural insurance coverage fell to 9.5% [32].

Against this backdrop, the financial burden of health issues became the leading cause of poverty in rural China in the 1990s [33]. This poverty is not only a short-term financial loss due to out-of-pocket health expenditures but also a long-term poverty trap hindering human capital accumulation. First, rural populations without the CMS struggled to access adequate medical care, leaving their health unprotected [34]. Second, food intake in rural China was limited and the absence of health insurance constrained household food consumption [35]. According to a report by the United States Department of Agriculture on China, in 2003, rural households in the middle-income quintile had a per capita annual income of approximately US$275 (including the imputed value of own-produced food consumed at home), and they devoted nearly 45% of their total expenditure on food, underscoring tight food budgets [36]. With physically demanding work common among rural residents, food and calorie shortfalls help explain low productivity [29].

To mitigate this issue, the Chinese government launched the NRCMS in 2003 to increase the utilization of medical care and nonmedical consumption [27]. The NRCMS is jointly funded by individuals, rural communities, and government subsidies, offering below-market-price health insurance to rural populations through voluntary participation. By 2008, the NRCMS had expanded nationwide.

Hypotheses

Figure 1 presents a conceptual diagram illustrating potential pathways linking the NRCMS to human capital and productivity through two channels: medical care and food consumption. First, medical care is a critical investment in health capital [37]. The direct pathway of the NRCMS may increase medical care use, thereby improving health (the blue lines in Fig. 1). Second, nonmedical consumption, such as food, is also important for human capital. While food is related to health, its caloric content is emphasized in labor economics as a classic determinant of productivity for manual laborers [6, 7, 22, 23].3 Insufficient food intake remained a concern in rural China during the 2000s ("Background" section), making this mechanism particularly relevant. Therefore, a potential indirect pathway may enhance productivity by increasing caloric intake.

Methods

Data

We used data from the CHNS [38]. The CHNS was established by the Carolina Population Center at the University of North Carolina in Chapel Hill and the National Institute for Nutrition and Health at the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. The survey covered 15 provinces through multistage cluster sampling. It included the following variables used in this study: individual characteristics, NRCMS participation, caloric intake, income, and working days. The database included biannual and quadrennial surveys since 1989, with nutritional data updated to 2011. The CHNS followed up on each sample in every wave. Sample attrition across waves included individuals who were unwilling to participate and those who migrated outside the survey areas. To compensate for this attrition, the CHNS added new interviewees to maintain the sample size.

We screened the data using three criteria. First, we retained individuals with an agricultural household registration, which was the NRCMS’s target population. Second, we included individuals aged 18–60 years, the main income-generating group. In China, 18 years is the legal age of adulthood, and 60 years is the threshold for old age. Finally, we excluded abnormal samples with a daily caloric intake of over 8,000 kcal, weight under 30 kg, or height under 80 cm.

Accordingly, we used the 2009 wave data to test Hypothesis 1. Then, we used income in 2010 divided by working days in 2010, both recorded in the 2011 wave, as a proxy for productivity in 2010. Previous empirical studies have also used income per unit of time as a practical measure of labor productivity [7, 39, 40]. In this study, the income variable comes from the CHNS constructed individual income file and captures all income components that can be measured at the individual level, including wage employment and agricultural production, but excluding components such as government subsidies, gifts, and rental income.5 Therefore, our productivity measure should be interpreted as broader individual earnings capacity per working day, rather than as a pure measure of physical productivity. Because this measure may be affected by determinants other than caloric intake, the analysis for Hypothesis 2 controls for other factors in various specifications by including factors such as education, health conditions, previous year’s productivity, regional dummies, and respondents’ main occupation recorded in 2009.

Productivity in 2010 was matched with calorie data from the 2009 wave to examine Hypothesis 2. Although the sample data were collected over a decade ago, this study remains relevant for drawing insights applicable to low-income countries that have suffered from insufficient calorie intake in rural areas, at similar stages as China.

Empirical strategy

Propensity score matching

Hypothesis 1 examined whether NRCMS participation was positively associated with calorie consumption in 2009. As participation in the NRCMS is voluntary, self-selection occurs. We adopted the PSM approach by referencing the methods used by Trujillo et al. [41] and Sun and Lyu [42] to analyze health insurance policies; the latter assessed the NRCMS.

We followed the standard PSM approach [43]. To test Hypothesis 2, we used caloric intake data from 2009 and productivity data from 2010 (reported in the 2011 wave) based on an unbalanced panel dataset. Robustness checks for hypothesis 1

The PSM estimation in this study raises two concerns. First, nonparticipants might differ from participants in unobserved factors, especially when the number of nonparticipants was small. This concern is related to the ignorability assumption underlying PSM: after conditioning on observed covariates, treatment assignment should be independent of potential caloric intake outcomes. Second, because PSM relies on matched observations, many participants were not used in the matched-sample comparison, which may limit the representativeness of the estimates.

To address the first concern, we conducted sensitivity analyses using Rosenbaum bounds [52]. This method introduces a gamma parameter to simulate the possible impact of unobserved covariates on propensity scores. If the results remain significant at higher gamma values, the PSM estimates are less sensitive. A gamma value of 2 indicates absolute insensitivity to hidden bias, values between 1.5 and 2 indicate relative insensitivity, and values below 1.5 suggest a lack of robustness [53]. Table A.2 in Additional file 1 presents the results of the sensitivity analyses. When gamma was 1.8, the results remained significant at the 5% level; when gamma was 2, the results remained significant at the 10% level. Therefore, the PSM result for Hypothesis 1 remained relatively insensitive to hidden bias.

To address the second concern, we estimated IPW and doubly robust augmented IPW (AIPW) specifications using the full sample. IPW adjusts for observed confounders by weighting observations with inverse propensity scores [54]. Unlike PSM, which uses only matched observations and may bias results by ignoring unmatched ones, IPW includes the full sample and weights observations to balance covariates between participants and nonparticipants. We further implemented doubly robust AIPW [55, 56]. Details of the AIPW specification are provided in Additional file 1. Table A.3 in Additional file 1 presents the IPW and AIPW results. The IPW estimate was 286 kcal/day, and the AIPW estimate was 312 kcal/day; both were significant at the 1% level and close to the PSM estimate of 294 kcal/day. These results support the robustness of the positive association between NRCMS participation and caloric intake under alternative observable-selection estimators.

Within the constraints of the available data, PSM, Rosenbaum bounds, IPW, and AIPW provide feasible ways to address the main empirical concerns as far as possible. However, these checks do not eliminate all limitations of the comparison. Rosenbaum bounds indicate that the PSM result is relatively insensitive to possible hidden bias, but they are based on the matched sample and do not address the potential representativeness issue caused by excluding unmatched participants. IPW and AIPW use the full sample and therefore reduce dependence on the matched subset; however, they still rely on observed covariates and cannot assess sensitivity to hidden bias in the same way as Rosenbaum bounds. Thus, no single method fully addresses both concerns. Nevertheless, the estimates are consistently positive and statistically significant across PSM, Rosenbaum bounds, IPW, and AIPW. We therefore interpret the results as evidence of a positive association between NRCMS participation and caloric intake, while acknowledging that causal interpretation remains limited.

Using the same specification as Column (2), we also conducted additional heterogeneity checks for Hypothesis 2 to examine whether the association between caloric intake and the log of productivity differs across subgroups. We estimated subgroup regressions based on 2009 information on age, income, gender, and physical labor intensity classified according to respondents’ main occupation, and then compared subgroup coefficients using Wald tests (Table A.6 in Additional file 1). The clearest heterogeneity appears by age: the coefficient is larger among individuals younger than 45, and the subgroup difference is statistically significant. The coefficients are also larger in the lower-income group and in the medium/high physical-intensity group than in their counterpart groups, but the subgroup differences are not statistically significant. Taken together, these patterns are suggestive that additional calories may be more productivity-relevant for individuals with greater physical work demands or tighter nutritional constraints.

Column (3) adds health status and medical care; the caloric intake coefficient declines slightly to 0.0112, likely because conditioning on health captures only “ [2]” in Fig. 1. This coefficient is smaller than the overall association (“ [2]” + “ [2]′”); however, the difference between Columns (2) and (3) is small, suggesting that the magnitude of “ [2]′” + “ [3]” is limited (0.0127 − 0.0112 = 0.0015). Sun and Lyu [42] found that the NRCMS’s impact on adult health is less, and Lei and Lin [17] revealed that the impact is statistically nonsignificant, limiting the bias from the NRCMS—health channel (“ [3]”). In addition, the health-mediated calorie channel (“ [2]′”) was limited in our data, leading to the close values between “ [2]” and “ [2]” + “ [2]′” + “ [3]”. Columns (4) and (5) further add 2008 productivity as a lagged outcome covariate and dummy variables for respondents’ main occupation recorded in 2009, respectively; the coefficient changes little and remains significant, supporting Hypothesis 2.

Across Columns (1)–(5), each 100-kcal increase is associated with 1.01%–1.33% higher productivity. As the sample mean of calorie was approximately 2,300 kcal (Table 3), the estimated elasticity was approximately 0.23–0.30, consistent with Strauss [6] and Kumar et al. [23].10 Given that NRCMS participation was associated with 294 kcal higher caloric intake ("NRCMS participation and caloric intake" section), a caloric difference of this magnitude corresponds to approximately 2.97%–3.91% higher labor productivity.

Robustness test results for Hypothesis 2

Although Table 6 addresses several potential biases, an additional concern is the appropriateness of using a linear model to test Hypothesis 2. Therefore, we applied a nonparametric kernel regression to examine whether the calorie–productivity association was sensitive to functional form and compared it with the original linear estimates [57]. Bekpulatov et al. [58] used this method as a robustness check.

Table A.5 in Additional file 1 presents the nonparametric kernel regression results for the average marginal effect of calories on the log of productivity. A 100-kcal increase in caloric intake was associated with approximately 1.5% higher productivity, significant at the 1% level. This estimate is close to and slightly higher than the result in "Main IPW regression results" section, suggesting the robustness of the original linear specification. Nevertheless, given the available data structure, the estimated calorie–productivity relationship should be interpreted as an association after controlling for observed covariates, rather than causal evidence.

Discussion

Taken together, the results are consistent with a plausible indirect pathway in which NRCMS may support labor productivity by increasing nutrition-related nonmedical consumption in the late 2000s. In particular, NRCMS participation was associated with higher caloric intake, and higher caloric intake was associated with higher earnings per working day. This pattern suggests that, in low-income rural settings where caloric intake remains insufficient and physical labor is common, health insurance may affect human capital not only through medical care, but also by relaxing financial constraints on productivity-related consumption such as food.

This study has two important limitations. First, although we conducted several robustness checks, these analyses cannot fully eliminate endogeneity concerns, as discussed above. Therefore, causal interpretation remains limited, and the results should be interpreted as associations rather than definitive causal effects.

Second, the findings are primarily derived from a low-income rural context in which caloric intake remained insufficient and physical labor was common. The observed associations may therefore not be generalizable beyond such settings. In middle- and high-income contexts, additional calories may have limited productivity value or may even be harmful when excessive caloric intake and obesity-related risks become more relevant concerns [24, 59, 60]. This boundary is also important for interpreting rural China as development proceeds. Recent research on rural China has begun to examine concerns such as overweight and unhealthy dietary changes, rather than calorie insufficiency alone [61]. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as most relevant to settings where calorie constraints and manual labor remain important.

Conclusions

Overall, the findings suggest that public health insurance may relax financial constraints in ways that support nutrition-related human capital in low-income settings. This implication should not be directly generalized to middle- or high-income settings. Future research could proceed in two directions. First, in low-income settings where caloric intake remains insufficient and manual labor is common, such as parts of sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, it would be valuable to examine whether public health insurance supports labor productivity by increasing caloric intake. Second, recent studies have increasingly examined factors that improve dietary quality [62–66], as well as the potential links between dietary quality and labor-market outcomes [67, 68]. A possible direction for future research is therefore to examine whether a policy–diet quality–productivity pathway exists alongside the calorie pathway considered in this study.
