Health disparities; is there progress?
Authors: Clyde W Yancy, Heather M Johnson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe contemporary history of health disparities dates to 1984 with the release of the Heckler Report. The differences in outcomes as a function of race were declared striking and not in keeping with the excellence of American Medicine and with a subsequent call to action that would eliminate health disparities. Forty years later, the gaps have widened. Root causes include economic disparity, social determinants of health and elements of bias, both implicit and explicit. Reframing the challenge and noting the extent to which disparities persist across multiple cohorts is the new call to action.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, Health equity, Prevention

Health disparities are notably extant when health differences occur between any group and a referent population and exist for reasons not attributable to biology. The rationale for the difference is couched in system differences impacting access to care, social differences reflecting the intersection of health and society, and elements of bias, both subconscious and overt, impacting communication and decision-making. Within this assortment of putative explanations for differences between populations emerges the evident reality that health and health outcomes vary for reasons that remain challenging requiring interventions still difficult to implement.

Though the history of health disparities likely encompasses centuries of life and living, the more contemporary history is benchmarked by the Heckler Report. Margaret Heckler, former secretary of Health and Human Services, 1983–1985, commissioned a review of suspected differences in health outcomes after several minority health leaders prompted concerns of disparate outcomes. Secretary Heckler’s background is pertinent as she was a pioneer of change- the only woman in her class at Boston College Law School, one of only 11 women in Congress when she was elected (1967), and a Republican serving both the Reagan and Bush Administrations [1] The subsequent report- “Report of the Secretary’s Task Force on Black & Minority Health”, was consequential. The report in its thoroughness, (within then au courant data source integrity characteristics), compellingly concluded an excess of 60,000 deaths per year among self-identified Black Americans and that 80 percent of those deaths were due to six specific disease states among which cardiovascular diseases were the leading cause. The results prompted the following assertion from Secretary Heckler:

“It can – it should – mark the beginning of the end of the health disparity that has, for so long, cast a shadow on the otherwise splendid American track record of ever improving health.” [2]

Decades later, with the Heckler Report as the benchmark, what is the evidence of progress? The most contemporary understanding of health disparities now resides in a larger discussion of health equity. The operative definition of health equity originally attributable to the Centers for Disease Control (CDC) and Prevention noted- “Health Equity is achieved when every person has the opportunity to attain his or her full health potential and no one is disadvantaged from achieving this potential because of social position or other socially determined circumstances.” [3] Equity differs substantially from equality. (See Fig. 1). Applying the exact same good or service across populations likely yields dissimilar results and risks widening already established disparities. With both a benchmark and a contemporary definition- especially with distinction from equality, progress should be easily measurable. Yet, the available update demonstrates the challenges remaining in the attainment of health equity. Carabello and colleagues addressed evidence of excess mortality and defined a new metric- Years of Potential Life Lost (YPLL), in Black Americans, 1999- 2020 [4] Over this 21-year period inclusive of the pandemic the findings portrayed a reality different from Secretary Heckler’s vision. In the absence of any change, the excess deaths over 21 years should have been 1.26 million. Instead, the reported data derived from the CDC Wonder Dataset identified 1.6 million lives lost or 76,000 excess deaths per annum, i.e., 16,000 additional excess deaths than predicted by the Heckler Report. Disparities didn’t end; the disparities worsened.

Once again, cardiovascular disease was the driver. Of concomitant concern is the economic consequence of this now 40 yr burden of disproportionate mortality. LaVeist and colleagues assessed the “Economic Burden of Racial, Ethnic and Educational Health Inequities” [5] The data noted a multi-billion-dollar loss in those working-class adults with high school education or less; similar cost implications were noted as a function of race and ethnicity. Yet another economic assessment completed by the McKinsey Institute for Economic Mobility [6] At the current pace of economic gain within the community of Black Americans, it will require decades to overcome economic inequities which in turn are central to health inequities. The most substantial evidence of such is the “social gradient” as contained in the World Health Organization report on Social Determinants of Health [7] There is a direct linear association between those least deprived and those with greatest longevity. The influence of economics then becomes one of the putative root causes of health disparities.

Bias, in multiple iterations, represents another putative cause of health inequities. Contrary to prevailing opinions, bias is ubiquitous throughout society. Nota Bene (N.B.) the depiction of bias in United States media. Varied news outlets, evaluated by a balanced media panel, have notable political leanings and questionable content validity. Worst case scenarios at both ends of the political spectrum highlight highly pejorative, even false reporting, and very poor documentation [8] There are extremes in everyday media, especially social media, feeding unfounded counternarratives and subtle but forceful “influences” driving behaviors and disrupting logical thought. This seemingly subtle but powerful influence exists in health and healthcare. Social media is especially harmful as embedded algorithms contain the potential to interject even more bias, subconsciously, in decision making and to supplant truth and evidence with contrarian points of view and overt health misinformation [9]

This everyday notation of ever-present bias applies directly to the presence of bias in health and healthcare. The penetrant depths of bias are so apparent that blind spots evolve and further obscure judgment and decision-making [10] Though the self-declaration of many is to eschew the presence of biased decision-making, the reality is much more universal. It is human nature to incorporate stereotypes and quick judgments, i.e. “fast thinking”, in everyday life as a kind of thought economy [11] Thus, in the development of health policy and the exercise of healthcare, science on occasion yields to bias- a bias that is blind to the implementor but has great consequence on the individual and/or the community.

The antidote for bias is inclusion, more specifically inclusive excellence. When systems are intended a priori to represent the context of the populations served, all groups benefit as the result of poor health and adverse health outcomes impacts not just the affected group but all others as well. Varied business entities document increased growth, faster time to market, increased profit, better innovations, and better decisions - up to 87 % better when varied voices participate in the business entity [12] There is no reason to suspect health and healthcare to be any different.

Yet another important consideration and likely the path forward is contained in the ethos of Medicine. Many legacy and learned leaders in Medicine echo and amplify a singular vision- “Medicine as a Noble Profession”. Statements dating from Hippocrates to Osler and more recent contemporaries articulate this message- “Medicine is a(n) (unqualified) love of humanity”. The Oath of Maimonides implores the physician to care for all who need care. This ultimate call for universality and altruism in care brings not equity but ethics into the discussion.

The Education Projects Group, (United Kingdom), defines the four pillars of medical ethics: beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy and justice [13] Notably declaring provide best care for those in need; do no harm; respect self-awareness: and follow the spirit of justice as advocates for what is right. This aspirational goal of Medicine is archived in the oath all physicians declare. Providing best care, indeed, providing equitable care is the de minimis expectation of Medicine and is perhaps the unavoidable truth, the awareness of which is a necessity for all physicians and care providers.

In sum, the declaration of Margaret Heckler, now decades later, has not been realized and notably the gaps are greater. The likely causes include economic disparities, health policy, the Social Determinants of Health and various iterations of bias including blind spots embedded by misinformation. Solutions are challenging but begin and end with the higher calling of Medicine. Within the intersection of equity, ethos and ethics resides the path forward to provide best care and best health opportunities for all.
