Albumin-to-creatinine ratio as non-invasive marker for prediction of non- bleeding esophageal varices. a cross-sectional observational study
Authors: Nasser Mousa, Alaa Elmetwalli, Mostafa abdelsalam, Mohamed Wahba, Niveen El-wakeel, Marwa Mansour, Mohamed Selim, Muhammad Diasty, Eman Abdelkader, Adel El-Assmy, Mohammed Abdelaziz, Ali El-Assmy, Ahmed Alhawarey

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsEsophageal varices (EV) are a frequent complication of liver cirrhosis and a leading cause of gastrointestinal bleeding and mortality. While upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is the preferred method for detecting EV, it may not be feasible in all settings. Non-invasive biomarkers could assist in identifying high-risk patients who should undergo prompt endoscopic evaluation. This study assessed the accuracy of the albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) as a non-invasive predictor of EV in individuals with liver cirrhosis.MethodsThis cross-sectional observational study included 260 adult patients with liver cirrhosis who had not undergone endoscopic screening before. All patients underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy to screen for esophageal varices (EV). Demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were collected, and the albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) was calculated from serum albumin and creatinine levels. Logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent predictors of EV. The diagnostic performance of ACR was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsOut of the 260 patients, 162 (62.3%) had EV. Patients with EV had significantly lower levels of ACR, serum albumin, platelet count, and hemoglobin, and higher levels of creatinine, bilirubin, INR, and MELD score compared to those without esophageal varices (all p < 0.05). Logistic regression analysis revealed a significant inverse relationship between ACR and the presence of EV. lower ACR independently predicted the presence of EV (adjusted OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.41–0.68; p = 0.001). ROC analysis demonstrated that an ACR cutoff ≤ 3.12 predicted EV with 88% sensitivity, 55% specificity, and an area under the curve of 0.683 (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe ACR ratio is a cost-effective biomarker that can assist in identifying cirrhotic patients at higher risk of EV, aiding in the prioritization of endoscopic screening, especially in settings with limited resources.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-026-05029-4.Keywords: Esophageal varices, Albumin to creatinine ratio, Non-invasive markers, Portal hypertension

Background

Portal hypertension is a common complication of cirrhosis and the major cause of esophageal varices (EV). Bleeding varices is the most serious complication of liver cirrhosis, with a 1-year rate of first variceal hemorrhage at nearly 12% [1–3]. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy is the gold standard for diagnosing esophageal varices. However, it is important to acknowledge its limitations and lack of availability, especially in low-income countries [4]. Several studies have examined non-invasive methods such as low platelet count, spleen size, portal vein diameter, and platelet count/spleen size ratio as potential predictors of large or high-grade esophageal varices. However, the reliability of these parameters in predicting high-risk variceal bleeding without the need for hepatic venous pressure gradient measurement remains unclear [5, 6]. Advanced cirrhosis often exhibits decreased albumin production, leading to a significant reduction in serum albumin levels. Albumin levels have been consistently identified as a strong predictor of mortality in cirrhotic individuals, highlighting its importance as a prognostic factor [7, 8]. Albumin is utilized in various scoring systems to predict outcomes for cirrhosis, including the Child-Pugh-Turcotte score, Neutrophil percentage-to-albumin ratio, and albumin-bilirubin score [9, 10] Furthermore, studies have concluded that low albumin levels can predict EV and identify category of patients who require prophylactic endoscopic management [11, 12]. An increase in serum creatinine in cirrhosis can result from various factors, including altered kidney perfusion due to splanchnic vasodilation associated with portal hypertension, leading to hepatorenal syndrome, and specific kidney damage related to cirrhosis etiologies [13]. Creatinine is a key component in cirrhosis prognostic criteria, playing a significant role in liver transplantation scoring systems like the MELD score [14]. Augustin et al. proposed creatinine as a crucial marker to identify patients at a high risk of EV [15]. Furthermore, several studies found creatinine markedly correlated with the risk of variceal bleeding [16, 17]. In addition, 24%–55% of patients with renal impairment have increased bleeding complications attributed to the inhibitory effect of uremia on platelet function [18]. Albumin to creatinine ratio (ACR) is a newly created ratio not used before for predicting the presence of EV in cirrhotic patients.

The aim of this study was to evaluate the albumin to creatinine ratio as a useful non-invasive parameter for predicting non- bleeding esophageal varices in patients with liver cirrhosis.

Methods

This Cross-Sectional Observational Study included 260 diagnosed patients with liver cirrhosis, aged more than 18 years, who had not undergone previous endoscopy screening or experienced variceal bleeding. The participants were recruited from the Tropical and Internal Medicine Mansoura University Hospital and the Egyptian Liver Research Institute and Hospital (ELRIAH) in Egypt between March 2024 and July 2024. All participants provided written informed consent after being thoroughly explained the aim and procedures of the study. Cirrhosis was diagnosed based on a combination of medical history, clinical, laboratory, and radiographic findings. This cross-sectional observational study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure transparent and comprehensive reporting.

Bias control

To minimize bias, consecutive eligible patients were recruited, standardized automated methods were used for all laboratory measurements, experienced endoscopists conducted endoscopic examinations while being blinded to laboratory results, and standardized diagnostic criteria were applied for cirrhosis and esophageal varices classification across all participating centers.

Exclusion criteria

History of upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage, esophageal and gastric varices, kidney injury, current use of beta blockers or nitrates, portal-systemic shunt or transjugular intrahepatic portal systemic shunt surgery, co-morbidities that prevent endoscopy, age under 18 years, hepatocellular carcinoma and portal vein thrombosis, withdrawal of informed consent, refusal of endoscopy, inability to tolerate the procedure.

Prior to the endoscope, all patients undergo a thorough medical history review, laboratory tests, and vital sign assessments. All patients underwent endoscopic tools by equally qualified endoscopists (blinded). Varices were diagnosed according to the American Association for the Study of Liver Disease (AASLD). Treatment was directed in accordance with AASLD [19].

Laboratory and endoscopic evaluation

5 ml of venous blood was collected upon admission, with 2 ml stored in an EDTA tube for CBC analysis and the remaining 3 ml in a clotting tube for further testing. The non-hemolyzed sera obtained after centrifugation were used to assess creatinine levels, liver functions (ALT, AST, albumin, bilirubin, and INR), and AFP. The Albumin to Creatinine Ratio (ACR) was calculated by dividing the albumin concentration (numerator) by the creatinine concentration (denominator) in the same blood samples obtained upon admission. Additionally, patients were categorized based on the Child-Turcotte-Pugh class, and MELD scores were calculated using the UNOS Internet site MELD calculator (http://www.unos.org/).

Sample size calculation

It was based on the Albumin/creatinine ratio comparison between patients with and without esophageal varices. An internal pilot study with 20 cases per group was conducted, and their results were excluded from the main study. The sample size was determined using G Power program version 3.1.9.7 with an effect size of 0.39, a 2-tailed test, α error of 0.05, and a power of 85.0%. The total required sample size for the study was 240 cases. A sample of 240 cases for each cohort was found to be sufficient for a statistically powerful study. However, considering that an expected loss of cases is estimated during follow-up, a larger sample should have been recruited at baseline.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (IBM Corp). Normality was confirmed with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics included mean ± SD for parametric variables and median (minimum-maximum) for non-parametric variables. Categorical data were presented as number and percentage. The Mann-Whitney test and t-test were used for non-parametric and parametric data comparisons, respectively. The χ2 test or Monte Carlo tests were used for group comparisons. Stepwise logistic regression was used for multivariable analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis determined the optimal cut-off point for sensitivity and specificity. Linear regression was used to predict independent variables for continuous outcomes, and adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were calculated. A significance level of p < 0.05 was used.

Handling of missing data

Missing data was effectively managed in this study. All necessary variables for analysis were present for all participants. Data completeness was maintained by verifying laboratory and clinical records promptly during data collection. As a result, no imputation methods were needed, and all 260 patients were included in the final statistical analysis.

Results

The study included 260 cirrhotic patients who underwent endoscopic screening for esophageal varices (EV). Of these, 162 patients (62.30%) were found to have EV (102 patients had small varices and 60 patients had large varices), while 98 patients (37.7%) did not (Fig. 1).

Validity of the albumin-creatinine ratio (ACR) in differentiating between individuals with and without EV

Table 2 and Fig. 2 display the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, indicating that the ACR moderately discriminates between cirrhotic patients with and without EV, with an Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.683 (95% CI 0.607–0.760, p < 0.001). ACR, at an optimal cutoff value of ≤ 3.12, demonstrated a high sensitivity of 88% and a specificity of 55%, with positive and negative predictive values of 77% and 73%, respectively. Despite its moderate discriminatory power, the high sensitivity of ACR suggests its potential value as a screening or triage tool, enabling clinicians to identify patients who may benefit from prioritized endoscopic evaluation.

ACR emerged as a significant independent predictor, showing an inverse association with the presence of EV. Lower ACR values were associated with higher odds of varices (AOR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.41–0.68, p = 0.001). Hemoglobin also exhibited a significant inverse association (AOR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.73–0.98, p = 0.024), while serum bilirubin displayed a positive association with varices (AOR = 1.32, 95% CI: 1.09–1.58, p = 0.005). Patients in Child-Pugh class C had a substantially higher likelihood of esophageal varices compared to class B (AOR = 4.76, 95% CI: 2.54–9.86, p = 0.001). In contrast, AST did not emerge as a significant predictor (p = 0.640). The overall predictive accuracy of the regression model was 78%.

Table 4 displays the correlation between the ACR and the clinical and laboratory parameters under study. ACR exhibited significant negative correlations with age (r = -0.157, p = 0.015), platelet count (r = -0.515, p = 0.001), bilirubin (r = -0.363, p = 0.029), INR (r = -0.482, p = 0.029), and Child-Pugh score (r = -0.436, p = 0.001), indicating that lower ACR values were linked to more advanced liver dysfunction and portal hypertension. Discussion

Esophageal varices are prevalent in cirrhotic patients, emphasizing the necessity for a precise noninvasive diagnostic method to enhance cost-effectiveness. This strategy can prevent unnecessary treatment for some patients and ensure appropriate treatment for others. Conducting endoscopic assessments of cirrhotic patients for screening may not be readily available or affordable, particularly in resource-constrained countries. A cost-effective screening method could be established by identifying cirrhotic patients with varices using non-invasive clinical and biochemical factors [20, 21]. In our study, the ACR has been suggested as a possible non-invasive screening tool for EV due to its simplicity and cost-effectiveness. This method can be used to initiate appropriate treatment, monitor patients, and prioritize endoscopy in resource-constrained areas.

The current study revealed that the ACR has moderate diagnostic performance in identifying EV, with an AUC of 0.683. Although the observed AUC indicates only modest diagnostic performance, the high sensitivity (88%) and a good negative predictive value (73%) at the suggested cutoff indicate that ACR may have potential utility as a screening or risk-stratification marker rather than a standalone diagnostic test in patients who may benefit from early endoscopic evaluation and reduction of unnecessary endoscopy. Additionally, the narrow 95% confidence interval range (0.607–0.760) indicates a reliable predictive power of ACR for predicting EV, especially with the presence of good accuracy of ACR (76.0%). In our study, ACR showed moderate discriminatory ability (AUC = 0.683). This level of performance is consistent with the recent studies evaluated non-invasive predictors of portal hypertension complications. For instance, a study by Zhang et al. [21] found that non-invasive biomarkers like the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio and the CONUT score had moderate predictive performance for variceal bleeding, with AUROC values of 0.65 and 0.75, respectively. This indicates that serum-based predictors, while having limited discrimination, can still be clinically valuable for risk stratification in cirrhotic patients. Additionally, the Liaoning Score, a tool for predicting esophageal varices in cirrhotic patients who have not had endoscopy, has shown moderate discrimination with AUROC values ranging from 0.65 to 0.75. The Liaoning Score has been externally validated and demonstrated good sensitivity and specificity in detecting varices [22, 23]. In this study, the ACR also showed moderate predictive performance (AUC = 0.683) for identifying varices. While the ACR is a simple and cost-effective screening tool, the Liaoning Score, incorporating platelet count and other variables, offers improved prediction accuracy. The ACR may be more suitable as a triage or risk-stratification marker rather than a definitive diagnostic tool due to its moderate specificity. Compared to other non-invasive predictors of portal hypertension, the ACR stands out for its simplicity and cost-effectiveness, utilizing two commonly available laboratory parameters. Non-invasive laboratory markers are not meant to replace endoscopy but rather aid in risk stratification and prioritization of endoscopic screening, especially in resource-limited settings. These strategies can help reduce unnecessary endoscopic procedures while identifying patients at higher risk of varices.

Previous studies have identified albumin as a predictor for EV in cirrhotic patients [22, 23]. Additionally, Gao et al. suggested that high levels of albumin may act as a protective factor against EV development [24]. The link between low serum albumin and the development of EV can be attributed to portal hypertension, which is a key factor in the development of OV. In cases of liver cirrhosis, low albumin levels may be due to reduced production by damaged liver cells, increased removal by the spleen or reticuloendothelial system, and elevated albumin loss through the gastrointestinal system, all of which are associated with portal hypertension. Additionally, albumin is a crucial component of the Child-Pugh score used to assess the severity of liver disease, and the presence of varices and the risk of bleeding are directly related to the severity of liver disease [25, 26]. Previous studies have reported renal impairment in 19%- 41% of patients with cirrhosis and acute variceal bleeding. Several studies have found that serum creatinine levels were significantly higher in cirrhotic patients with EV compared to patients without EV [27–31]. The addition of serum creatinine to the creatinine-modified Child-Pugh score improves the prognostic accuracy for patients with decompensated cirrhosis compared to the original Child-Pugh score. This enhancement is particularly notable in Class B, where serum creatinine is a key factor in predicting survival and complications like encephalopathy, hematemesis, and ascites in patients with decompensated cirrhosis [32]. Several studies have identified creatinine as a key marker for identifying EV in patients with cirrhosis. These studies have shown a strong correlation between elevated creatinine levels and the risk of variceal bleeding among cirrhotic patients [15–17, 33].

In our study, we discovered a significant inverse correlation between the albumin/creatinine ratio and the presence of EV. This suggests that lower albumin levels (numerator) or higher serum creatinine levels (denominator) may be linked to the risk of EV presence. Kothari et al. also found similar results in their study. They reported that low albumin levels can predict EV, which aligns with our findings demonstrating a significant connection between low albumin levels and the presence of EV and variceal bleeding [34]. Li et al. discovered a correlation between albumin, prothrombin time and Child-Pugh score with hemorrhage in cirrhotic patients with esophageal varices. They also found that albumin levels at a cutoff of 3.2 mg/dL had a sensitivity of 71.5% and specificity of 65.6% in predicting esophageal variceal hemorrhage [35]. An increase in serum creatinine in our ratio is linked to a decrease in the albumin/creatinine ratio, indicating a higher risk of esophageal variceal bleeding. Glisic et al. demonstrated that, patients with EV had significantly higher serum creatinine compared to patients without EV [36]. Additionally, Wong et al. previously showed that elevated bilirubin and creatinine levels are correlated with bleeding in cirrhotic patients [32]. Ismail et al. found that in cirrhotic patients, both bleeding from EV and mortality are associated with higher serum creatinine and bilirubin levels [37].

Our study declared significant negative correlations of ACR with platelets, bilirubin, INR, and Child-Pugh score. Thrombocytopenia is a common complication of liver cirrhosis, often seen alongside esophageal varices. Both conditions are closely linked to portal hypertension [38]. The inverse relationship between platelet count and ACR reflects the pathophysiology of liver disease, which is the primary cause of esophageal varices. In liver disease, impaired liver function and portal hypertension can result in reduced platelet production, decreased albumin production and an increase in creatinine levels (due to factors, such as altered kidney perfusion caused by splanchnic vasodilation associated with portal hypertension [14, 39]. These result in increasing ACR. Therefore, the negative correlation between platelets and ACR is anticipated and corresponds to the underlying disease mechanism. Moreover, platelet count can serve as a predictor of the grade of esophageal varices in cirrhotic patients, showing a significant negative correlation between platelet count and varices grades [40]. The Child-Pugh Score and MELD Score are used to evaluate the severity of liver disease. Both scores included bilirubin and prothrombine time and predict the occurrence of OV [15, 32, 35]. The negative correlations of ACR with Child-Pugh score indicate that, as liver disease progresses (higher score), the ACR decreases, signifying the predictive ability of ACR for EV. ALT is a liver enzyme that can increase in response to liver damage. The positive correlation with the ACR indicates that as liver damage worsens, the ratio may increase due to reduced albumin production and elevated creatinine levels. However, Kothari et al. did not find a correlation between liver enzymes and the occurrence of OV [34].

Generalizability of the study

The results of this study could have practical implications for managing cirrhosis, potentially reducing hospital stays and medical costs, especially in low income countries where routine endoscopic screening for all cirrhotic patients is not possible. The albumin-to-creatinine ratio, based on commonly available laboratory tests, could be used as a practical tool to identify patients who need early endoscopic evaluation. However, the study’s participants were from specialized referral centers, so the findings may not be directly applicable to community-based populations. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger and more diverse groups are needed to confirm the predictive accuracy of the ACR. Given its simplicity, low cost, and universal laboratory availability, the albumin-to-creatinine ratio may represent a pragmatic screening biomarker to optimize endoscopic resource allocation in cirrhotic patients.

Limitations of the study

This study has limitations, including its cross-sectional design, which hinders the assessment of causal relationships between ACR and EV development. The sample size was adequate for statistical analysis, but lacked internally validated using resampling techniques such as bootstrapping, nor externally validated in an independent cohort. The diagnostic performance of ACR was moderate, with an AUC of 0.683. This suggests that it is not a definitive diagnostic tool but rather a potential screening or risk-stratification marker in pilot study. The lack of internal and external validation calls for future prospective studies with a larger sample size, standardized protocols and centralized laboratory measurements could further enhance the reliability of these findings to confirm ACR’s clinical utility as a non-invasive predictor of EV. The exclusion of patients with prior variceal bleeding, renal injury, portal vein thrombosis, or intolerance to endoscopy may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

The ACR demonstrated modest discriminatory ability for predicting EV in patients with liver cirrhosis. While it may serve as a simple non-invasive marker for preliminary risk stratification, its diagnostic performance cannot substitute endoscopic screening. Further large prospective studies with external validation are required to confirm its clinical utility.
