Modern Management of Isolated Polymyalgia Rheumatica
Authors: Patricia Harkins, Sharon Cowley, Eoghan Burke, Robert Harrington, Danielle Molloy, David Kane, Richard Conway
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) is a common inflammatory rheumatic disorder affecting those over 50 years of age. It is clinically heterogenous in both presentation and disease trajectory. Diagnostic complexity is heightened by the absence of a gold standard diagnostic test and the broad spectrum of disease mimics, posing challenges even for experienced rheumatologists. The primary goal of treatment is to restore health-related quality of life by achieving sustained symptom control, suppressing systemic inflammation, and minimising treatment-related toxicity. Despite advances in our understanding of PMR, glucocorticoids (GCs) remain the cornerstone of therapy. However, frequent relapses and prolonged treatment courses in many patients result in high cumulative GC exposure with associated adverse effects, which is of particular concern in this older, typically frailer and more vulnerable patient cohort. The urgent need for effective GC-sparing agents has resulted in pivotal developments over the past decade, notably the SAPHYR trial, which supported the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of sarilumab as the first biologic therapy for refractory PMR. This represents a major shift in the therapeutic landscape, with several biologic agents now under investigation. These advances, however, highlight gaps in the current management, including the need for rapid access pathways and specialist rheumatologist evaluation in all cases of suspected PMR to facilitate an early and accurate diagnosis, stratified treatment approaches and the accurate detection of a coexisting giant cell arteritis. Furthermore, standardised definitions of relapse and remission, alongside structured monitoring protocols, are lacking. This review explores current diagnostic and treatment strategies for isolated PMR. We also highlight unmet needs in PMR management, and discuss future directions aimed at improving outcomes and redefining the care pathway for PMR.Keywords: Polymyalgia rheumatica, Glucocorticoids, Biologic, Interleukin-6, Patient-reported outcomes

Introduction

Polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR) is a common inflammatory rheumatic disease primarily involving the periarticular structures of proximal joints that occurs almost exclusively in those over 50 years of age [1]. The clinical spectrum of PMR is wide; however, it classically manifests as bilateral pain and stiffness of the shoulder, neck and pelvic girdle, each with an early morning preponderance [1].

PMR was traditionally considered as a relatively benign clinical condition, characterised by a rapid response to glucocorticoid (GC) therapy and a presumed self-limiting disease course. However, there is a growing recognition of its substantial impact on patients, including significant disability and a marked reduction in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [2–4]. Adding to its complexity, PMR demonstrates considerable heterogeneity in both clinical presentation and disease progression [5–7]. The absence of a gold standard diagnostic test, coupled with a wide range of potential disease mimics, presents substantial diagnostic challenges, even with specialist expertise.

The overarching aim of treatment in PMR is to restore a patient’s HRQoL by providing sustained symptomatic relief with suppression of the underlying systemic inflammation and prevention of disease relapse, whilst minimising treatment-associated adverse effects [8]. Despite considerable progress in understanding the pathophysiology of PMR over the past decade, GCs remain the mainstay of therapy. Although GCs are extremely efficacious initially, disease relapses are common, and treatment frequently extends beyond 5 years [6]. This results in high cumulative GC burdens for many, with associated toxicity, which is of particular concern, especially in this older and more vulnerable cohort of patients.

The need for alternative, effective and safe GC-sparing agents is readily apparent. The pivotal SAPHYR study has resulted in the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval of the first biologic therapy in PMR, sarilumab, for those with refractory PMR [9]. This has ushered in a new era in PMR management, with an increasing number of biologic therapies currently under evaluation in randomised controlled trials (RCTs) [10]. The incorporation of these new agents into the therapeutic algorithm of PMR, however, underscores several shortcomings in our current management approach. Whilst PMR has until recently been diagnosed and managed in primary care, with only refractory or difficult cases referred for specialist evaluation [11], there is now growing interest in the utility and feasibility of rapid access pathways and early specialist involvement in all cases of suspected PMR as a means to facilitate timely diagnosis, identification of a concomitant giant cell arteritis (GCA), enable disease stratification and appropriate treatment initiation. Furthermore, the evidence base for disease monitoring in PMR remains limited. There is a pressing need for a standardised consensus on the definitions of relapse and remission, as well as the development of structured monitoring strategies to optimise patient care and outcomes.

In this article, we provide an overview of the evidence to date on current management strategies in isolated PMR, encompassing diagnostic approaches and both non-pharmacological and pharmacological therapeutic options. Finally, we discuss the unmet needs in the existing care paradigm and propose directions for future management strategies.

This article is therefore based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Diagnostic Approaches

Until recently, the perception of isolated PMR as a relatively benign rheumatological condition, given its failure to cause disease-related tissue damage, resulted in its importance being overlooked in basic and translational studies, pharmacotherapeutic trials and indeed in the field of rheumatology [12]. Accordingly, its care has been primarily executed in the hands of non-specialists, with referral to secondary care reserved for those with relapsing or refractory disease, or those with GC-related adverse events [13].

Making the diagnosis of PMR can be extremely challenging, with many disease mimics and often a marked heterogeneity in disease presentation [5]. This is further amplified by the lack of a gold standard definitive diagnostic test, with diagnosis based on symptoms, signs and the evaluation of the acute phase reactants C-reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR). Whilst musculoskeletal ultrasound (MSUS) is increasingly employed, there is no definitive diagnostic finding, and not all centres have the desired level of MSUS expertise necessary. Other imaging techniques, such as positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) show promise; however, they have yet to be adopted into routine clinical practice [14]. With the current diagnostic approach misdiagnosis is common, even among experts, occurring in up to 50% of cases [7, 13, 15].

Moreover, a reported 15–20% of those with PMR have a concomitant GCA, either at disease onset or throughout the course of their disease [16, 17]. Given the risk of devastating complications including visual loss, stroke, aortic dissection, aneurysm formation and rupture in those with untreated GCA, it is imperative that GCA is screened for appropriately and accurately diagnosed in those with PMR [18, 19]. This underscores the importance of specialist expertise and input in the diagnostic approach.

The British Society of Rheumatology (BSR) guidelines for the management of PMR [20] and, more recently, the international PMR/GCA study group [21] recommend specialist evaluation in each case of isolated PMR, with rapid access strategies recommended in those with severe symptoms [21]. Given the ability of GC to mask laboratory and imaging findings which may be useful in both the diagnosis and prognostication of those with isolated PMR, their commencement should ideally be deferred until after specialist review [7]. However, active, untreated PMR has a profound impact on a patient’s function and HRQoL, and therefore, delaying the instigation of GC is only possible if there are expedited referral pathways such as “fast-track clinics” in place. The use of fast-track clinics in GCA has been widely reported, with benefits including rapid and accurate diagnosis, early initiation of appropriate therapy, reduced risk of complications including permanent visual loss, in addition to overall increased cost-effectiveness vs. standard care [22–25]. To date, three studies have reported on the use of fast-track clinics in PMR, with each supporting their role in delivering timely, accessible specialist care resulting in enhanced diagnostic accuracy and overall patient outcomes [7, 26, 27]. Patients assessed through the fast-track clinics experienced a shorter time to PMR diagnosis, a reduction in total inpatient hospital days [26, 27], and a reduced starting dose of prednisolone compared to those managed by standard care [7, 26, 27]. Furthermore, the implementation of fast-track clinics appears to be more cost-effective by lowering the annual cost for patients [27]. However, the feasibility of the implementation of a fast-track PMR clinic relies on national and local resources, and with an already strained rheumatology workforce, and long waiting lists, coupled with our aging population, additional resources would need to be assigned to PMR to ensure the successful implementation of such initiatives. Further studies, in particular, RCTs, and cost-effectiveness analyses need to be undertaken to address the precise role of the rheumatologist in the care of those with isolated PMR. However, with the expanding number of GC-sparing agents on the horizon, that only rheumatologists can prescribe, there will likely be an inevitable move away from general practitioner-led management.

Treat-to-Target and Disease Monitoring

The recently published treat-to-target (T2T) recommendations for GCA and PMR developed by an international multidisciplinary task force suggest regular monitoring of those with PMR, as frequently as every 1–4 weeks until remission is achieved [28]. The taskforce recommend that disease severity, patient comorbidities and potential predictors of adverse disease outcomes should guide treatment selection [28]. Whilst the 2015 European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) and American College of Rheumatology (ACR) recommendations for PMR management identify female sex, high acute phase reactants and peripheral arthritis as associated with worse disease outcomes and increased risk of relapse [29], the overall data in the literature surrounding prognostic markers is conflicting and inconsistent. The disease course of PMR can be highly heterogenous, and at present there is no means of accurately stratifying from the outset who will experience recurrent relapses, GC dependence and overall worse disease outcomes, including severe GC complications. As the therapeutic armamentarium of PMR expands, the ability to predict disease course and risk stratify patients is an area garnering increased scientific attention. The development of a disease stratification tool would enable personalised treatment from the time of diagnosis, with the potential to transform disease outcomes, whilst limiting unnecessary toxicity.

Following the induction of remission, it is imperative to give careful consideration to the most appropriate strategies for maintaining remission and preventing disease relapse, whilst minimising the burden of treatment-related toxicity [28]. One such means of achieving this is adapting a T2T disease management strategy, which has been employed in several rheumatological and non-rheumatological diseases [30]. By defining a specific, readily measurable and feasible treatment goal or target, and adjusting treatment accordingly to achieve this, disease activity, symptomatology and HRQoL can be optimised, while both treatment burden and disease-related adverse events are minimised [30]. However, there are several limiting factors hindering the integration of such an approach into routine PMR care [31, 32]. A major obstacle in defining a treatment target is the lack of an international consensus on the definitions of disease response, remission and relapse. This has resulted in a marked heterogeneity and inconsistency in the definitions used to define disease activity and outcomes in clinical studies [33, 34], which results in significant limitations not only in the interpretation of findings but also in the ability to inform the optimal disease activity monitoring approaches in clinical practice.

Current monitoring strategies in PMR consist primarily of a clinician-based assessment and the evaluation of traditional inflammatory markers, namely ESR and CRP. This strategy is inherently problematic considering that symptoms may be non-specific during disease relapse, with multiple potential differential diagnoses and disease mimics. Additionally, normal CRP or ESR values have been observed in up to 17% and 27% of relapses, respectively [35]. Moreover, this approach has limited value in those prescribed interleukin-6 (IL-6) antagonists with their associated neutralisation of both ESR and CRP.

In an attempt to address this, the PMR activity score (PMR-AS) was first published in 2004 [36], and has undergone a number of iterations since then including the development of the PMR-AS-CRP [36]. This composite score of five parameters includes pain, assessed using a visual analogue scale (VASp); physician global assessment assessed using a visual analogue scale (VASph); morning stiffness in minutes (MS); physical function assessed by elevation of the upper limb assessed by active shoulder abduction ranging from 0 to 3 (EUL); and finally CRP (mg/dL) [36]. Thresholds have been defined for PMR disease remission (< 1.5), medium disease activity (7–17) and high disease activity (> 17). In clinical trials and studies, PMR-AS is a commonly employed instrument for disease activity assessment [36], and indeed it is the only validated composite score for monitoring disease activity in patients with PMR. However, despite its increasing use in research studies and trials, a COSMIN-based systematic review demonstrated the current paucity of information on certain measurement properties, including structural validity and internal consistency, which may have significant implications for its clinical applicability [37].

Other outcomes such as MSUS, MRI and PET-CT imaging have been explored; however, imaging findings are impacted by the commencement of treatment, and have a low responsiveness to change once established on therapy [38, 39]. These methods are also limited by accessibility, expertise and financial cost.

Whilst Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) projects are currently ongoing to define remission, relapse and refractory disease in PMR [33], there is a significant unmet need to identify novel methods of disease activity assessment and monitoring. Accurate identification of disease relapse is essential for clinicians to prevent unnecessary exposure to potentially harmful immunosuppressive agents in the absence of a disease relapse, while also ensuring timely intervention where appropriate to avoid suboptimal disease control.

The Patient Experience in PMR Management

In clinical practice, patient-reported outcomes (PROMs) offer valuable insights at an individual level into the lived patient experience, identifying unmet needs or areas for improvement from symptomatology, to the physical, psychological and functional impacts of the disease. Focusing on outcomes that truly matter to patients can enhance and guide treatment decisions, facilitating patient-centred care and empowering and supporting patients in the self-management of their disease.

Despite evidence highlighting the major impact PMR has on a patient’s life [12, 40], and patient engagement studies identifying PROMs, in particular fatigue and sleep disturbance as being key individual concerns and research priorities of those with PMR, there is a distinct lack of clinical studies and trials evaluating the prevalence, longitudinal trajectory and impact of PROMs in PMR [41, 42]. Reflective of this, the assessment, identification and management of PROMs are frequently overlooked in routine clinical care. Moreover, the instruments used to evaluate PROMS are inconsistent and heterogenous [42]. In attempt to standardise this, the PMR-impact scale (PMR-IS) was proposed as a PMR-specific PROM composite score [43]. The PMR-IS comprises four subdomains including symptoms, physical functioning, emotional and psychological wellbeing and finally GC-related side effects [43]. Upon external validation, all subdomains demonstrated good measurement properties, with the exception of the GC-related side effect subdomain which failed to demonstrate adequate internal consistency and construct validity [44]. Consequently, the PMR-IS will require further validation prior to its incorporation into routine clinical practice, and indeed future clinical trials and studies.

Regarding GC-related side effects, the novel STEROID-PRO has demonstrated validity and reliability for measuring the impact of GC therapy from the patient perspective in a large multinational cohort with multiple rheumatic diseases, including PMR [45]. Using four subscales including social impact, impact on appearance, psychological impact and treatment concerns, the 15-item STEROID PRO aims to accurately measure and capture the impact of GCs across rheumatic diseases [45]. Ongoing validation of the STEROID-PRO will be critical to ensuring its reliability, sensitivity to change and utility both in research studies and routine clinical care of those with PMR.

Recently, it was reported that one in four patients with PMR are affected by depression [46]. In that study, depression was strongly associated with poor physical function, underscoring the psychological impact of physical limitations in PMR, and highlighting the importance of identifying and managing comorbid depression in this population [46]. Additionally, another recent publication by the same Australian group demonstrated the persistence of fatigue at 18 months post diagnosis of PMR, with over one-third of patients also reporting a perceived failure to recover to a premorbid physical function despite almost 2 years of standard care [47]. This not only has a potential significant negative impact on disease activity and treatment response but also HRQOL [2]. Moreover, the persistence of fatigue and perceived failure to recover to a premorbid physical function raise concerns over the effectiveness of our current treatment paradigm to adequately treat those with PMR, and underscores the urgent need for a new holistic and comprehensive approach to PMR management, including both pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions. This was further emphasised in the PROMs data from the SAPHYR trial, which highlighted how the general health and physical functioning of those with relapsing PMR is grossly impaired across multiple domains, and fails to improve by week 52 for those on GC monotherapy [48]. To the contrary, treatment with the IL-6 inhibitor sarilumab accompanied by a 14-week GC taper achieved robust and clinically meaningful improvements in HRQOL and PROMs [48]. It is evident, therefore, that by embracing new management approaches there is potential to modify the patient experience, and subsequently overall disease outcomes.

Non-pharmacological Management

Patient education is paramount to achieving optimal outcomes in PMR, as it supports self-management and fosters shared decision-making. The T2T recommendations suggest that all patients with PMR, and their caregivers, should receive information pertaining to PMR clinical disease features, treatment-related benefits and adverse effects, comorbidities and potential complications [28]. In order to ensure adherence to therapy, all patients need to be educated on the role of GCs and the importance of compliance with their individualised prescribed GC taper. Given the overlap of PMR with GCA, all patients should be educated on GCA, its symptoms and risks, and advised to seek immediate medical attention should they develop any characteristic symptoms [28]. Whilst the primary source of education should be the primary medical physician, patient charities are also a valuable information resource [28].

The evidence on the role of non-pharmacological therapies including nutrition, physical activity and complementary therapies in PMR management is scarce. Despite this, the 2015 EULAR/ACR PMR management guidelines do recommend “considering an individualised exercise programme for PMR patients aimed at the maintenance of muscle mass and function, and reducing risk of falls” [29]. Despite this recommendation, in the PMR cohort study, only 17% of those with PMR reported using exercise to manage their PMR, and only one-quarter of patients received specific exercise advice from their treating physician [49]. In order to improve the awareness and educate patients, and indeed healthcare professionals appropriately on the role of exercise in PMR, we need to strengthen the evidence base with adequately powered, well-designed trials of specific exercise interventions and their impact on long-term outcomes in PMR. In the interim, although there is no direct evidence on the optimal type or duration of physical activity for those with PMR, the benefits of physical activity in general, including improved functional independence, maintenance of muscle mass and function, reduced falls risk, preservation of bone mineral density, promotion of weight loss, enhanced cardiovascular health, and improvements in PROMs such as fatigue and depression, are likely to positively impact overall outcomes in this population [50–52]. Therefore, physical activity should be actively advocated for and promoted by all healthcare professionals. Moreover, the role of the full multidisciplinary team, in particular physiotherapists, should be optimised to provide safe, effective and individualised exercise programs.

Patients with PMR tend to be chronologically older, and show signs of accelerated aging with a higher prevalence of geriatric syndromes including sarcopenia and frailty [3]. The ubiquitous use of GCs and their impact on muscle mass is likely contributory. This presents unique challenges that underscore the importance of a holistic approach to PMR care, with multidisciplinary care and geriatric principles at the helm [53].

Additionally, addressing the burden of polypharmacy in this older, comorbid population and implementing “deprescribing” to improve medication use where appropriate is essential to reduce adverse effects, drug–drug interactions and drug–disease interactions [54].

Finally, the use of GC and other immunosuppressive agents in this older patient cohort is associated with a higher risk of infection. Therefore, it is imperative that all patients are educated on the importance of vaccinations, in particular influenza, pneumococcal and herpes zoster vaccines, to ensure their timely administration and reduce associated morbidity [55].

Therapeutic Agents in PMR

Glucocorticoids

GCs have been the mainstay of PMR treatment for decades. Whilst there is no evidence-based, standardised protocol for steroid taper, the 2015 EULAR/ACR recommendation suggests starting with a prednisone dose equivalent (PDE) between 12.5 and 25 mg per day [29]. The specific dose chosen within this range is primarily physician-led and patient-specific, tailored to comorbidities, likelihood of GC-related adverse events and risk of relapse. Characteristically, patients experience a profound and rapid resolution in their symptomatology upon introduction of GCs. Once remission is achieved, GCs should be tapered gradually; however, the optimal tapering strategy remains controversial largely because of the paucity of studies addressing it [56]. Previously, isolated PMR was considered a relatively “mild” condition, with most patients anticipated to complete their GC course within a 12-month period. However, we now recognise that for a significant majority of patients, their disease course is marked by frequent relapses and subsequent huge GC burdens over a number of years [6, 57, 58]. Indeed, a meta-analysis of observational studies reported the pooled proportions of individuals still using GCs at 1, 2, and 5 years were 77% (95% CI 71–83%), 51% (95% CI 41–61%), and 25% (95% CI 15–36%), respectively [6]. Data from the ACR Rheumatology Informatics System for Effectiveness (RISE) registry reported 63.8% of those with PMR remained on GCs at 12–24 months, of whom only 39% were prescribed a GC-sparing agent [58].

This long-term use of GCs is suboptimal, and highlights the significant shortcomings in our current management paradigm of isolated PMR. Moreover, those with PMR are typically older, frailer [3] and so more vulnerable to the well-recognised adverse effects of GC therapy. Up to 85% of patients with PMR experience at least one GC-associated adverse effect [7, 18]. This substantial GC-related toxicity coupled with the high relapse rate underscores the urgent need for effective GC-sparing therapies. Despite this, currently no pharmacotherapeutics apart from GCs are recommended as stand-alone first-line treatments in isolated PMR.

Management of GC-Related Adverse Events

The well-recognised GC-related adverse events frame a significant proportion of the treatment experience of those with PMR [59–61]. Nearly 70 different GC-associated toxicities affecting the musculoskeletal, dermatologic, ocular, endocrine, gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, neuropsychiatric and immunologic systems have been described [59–62]. It is important to monitor for such GC-related toxicity and identify those who may require closer monitoring. The Glucocorticoid Toxicity Index (GTI) is a standardised composite instrument developed by an international group of physician-investigators from several medical subspecialties, aiming to measure change in GC-related toxicity across two time points [62]. It has undergone validation in both real-world clinical studies and clinical trials [62]. Although its ease of use has been demonstrated, it has yet to be integrated into routine clinical care to guide management decisions.

The EULAR recommendations for monitoring adverse events of low dose GC therapy were published in 2010 [63]. Despite the significant GC-associated toxicities identified, the monitoring strategies proposed are vague, in stark contrast to those of GC-sparing agents, such as methotrexate, which have well-characterised and rigid monitoring protocols [63].

Musculoskeletal effects include GC-induced myopathy and reduced bone mineral density (BMD) with osteoporosis and fragility fractures. GC-related myopathy typically affects the proximal skeletal muscles and is characterised by pain-free weakness and muscle atrophy which affects physical performance. It can be difficult to differentiate clinically from symptoms of PMR and its many disease mimics. EULAR recommends that GC-related muscle weakness should be assessed “through questioning” at the start and end of treatment in clinical trials [63]. We advocate that in addition to enquiring about typical steroid myopathy symptoms (difficulty climbing stairs, raising from a chair or lifting objects) at each clinic visit, patient education on the importance of exercise initiatives be emphasised, with proactive early referral to physiotherapy as appropriate.

BMD decreases even with low dose GCs and there is an increased risk of osteoporosis and fractures with higher GC doses and cumulative GC burden [60, 64]. There is substantial morbidity and associated healthcare costs associated with fractures, particularly in older adults. It is important to note, however, that although GCs contribute significantly to reduced BMD, the aetiopathogenesis of osteoporosis is typically multifactorial in PMR and indeed other inflammatory rheumatic diseases [65]. Systemic inflammation associated with poorly controlled disease activity, low physical activity, poor nutrition, older age and hormonal loss secondary to menopause all negatively impact bone health [65]. Therefore, optimisation of these factors and a multimodal approach to osteoporosis management is essential.

The ACR guidelines for the prevention and treatment of GC-induced osteoporosis recommend that all adults ≥ 40 years of age, which is representative of all patients with PMR, should have their fracture risk estimated using the fracture risk assessment tool (FRAX), and undergo BMD testing within 6 months of the initial GC treatment [66]. Every 12 months, fracture risk should be reassessed, and BMD retesting should be performed every 1–3 years if not on osteoporosis prophylaxis, or every 2–3 years if established on osteoporosis prophylaxis therapy, outside of calcium and vitamin D [66]. All patients should have their calcium (1–1.2 g/day) and vitamin D (target serum level ≥ 20 ng/ml) intake optimised [66]. All patients with PMR (women ≥ 40 years old, and men ≥ 40 years who are at moderate to high risk of fracture) should be treated with an oral bisphosphonate [66]. Where an oral bisphosphonate is not suitable an intravenous bisphosphonate should be considered, and failing this, dependent on local restrictions and reimbursement policies, teriparatide should be considered. If none of these are appropriate, denosumab (a monoclonal antibody inhibitor of RANK ligand) should be commenced [66].

Endocrine and metabolic adverse effects of GCs include hyperglycaemia, insulin resistance and diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, weight gain, adrenal suppression, cushingoid features and growth suppression [59–62]. EULAR recommends that all patients should undergo fasting blood glucose levels and trough oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) at the start and end of therapy [63]. The BSR guidelines on the other hand recommend 3-monthly glucose testing [20]. If a patient has pre-existing diabetes, prior to the commencement of GC, they require very careful monitoring, and early consideration of a GC-sparing agent [20].

Cardiovascular adverse effects include arterial hypertension, ischaemic heart disease and heart failure [59–62, 67]. EULAR and BSR recommend screening for hypertension and other cardiovascular risk factors prior to the commencement of GCs [20, 63].

Patients should also be regularly assessed for GC-associated neuropsychiatric effects ranging from minor mood changes, mood swings, irritability, depression and anxiety to mania, psychosis and cognitive impairment and dementia [62].

The predominant gastrointestinal effect of GC is gastric irritation and ulceration, for which EULAR recommend routine monitoring (e.g. checking haemoglobin levels) and prophylactic gastroprotective treatment (e.g. proton pump inhibitors) where necessary [63].

The most common ocular complications of GCs are cataract formation and glaucoma. EULAR recommends baseline ophthalmologic evaluation for both in all patients commencing high dose GCs [63].

Disease-Modifying Anti-rheumatic Drugs

Conventional Synthetic Disease-Modifying Anti-rheumatic Drugs

The efficacy of conventional synthetic disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (csDMARDs) in isolated PMR remains uncertain, with studies yielding inconsistent findings.

The most studied, and indeed used csDMARD, is methotrexate (MTX) [68]. Its clinical efficacy in the management of PMR has been reported in three RCTs, albeit with mixed results [69–71]. Two of the studies demonstrated the efficacy of MTX [69, 70], whereas the third failed to show any GC-sparing benefit [71]. The studies supporting the efficacy of MTXs were of higher methodological quality; however, the total number of patients investigated in the RCTs is low (n = 194). Notably, only low dosages of MTX, equivalent to or less than 10 mg, were evaluated in these trial populations, and one may therefore postulate that a higher dosage would yield alternative findings. The results of a multicentre, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, “PMR MODE” (NL8366), assessing the efficacy of MTX at a dose of 25 mg per week in addition to a 24-week prednisolone taper are highly anticipated to answer this question [72]. Moreover, the “STERLING-PMR” (ISRCTN17828080) trial examining the role of MTX and leflunomide (LEF) in patients with PMR who have experienced at least one disease relapse is currently ongoing. It is a phase 3 open-labelled RCT comparing standard care plus MTX (starting dose of 15 mg, progressively increased to 25 mg, or reduced to 10 mg if not tolerated) or LEF if MTX is not effective or not tolerated.

Despite the conflicting results and lack of clear evidence showing any true reduction in GC-related adverse events, the 2015 EULAR/ACR recommendations include a conditional recommendation to consider the early incorporation of MTX with a GC regimen in those at increased risk of GC-related adverse events or prolonged disease course [29]. Moreover, these recommendations also recommend MTX in those experiencing relapse with an inadequate response to GC or GC-related adverse events on follow-up [29]. The recent French Society of Rheumatology guidelines for the management of PMR also recommend MTX as an alternative to IL-6 inhibitors in GC-dependent PMR [73].

Until recently, the role of LEF as a GC-sparing agent in refractory PMR was supported only by two case series [74, 75]. However, a multicentre observational study by Vinicki et al. compared the use of MTX (n = 143; median dose 15 mg/week) and LEF (n = 43; 20 mg/day fixed dose) as GC-sparing agents, and reported the superiority of LEF for achieving remission and GC discontinuation (p = 0.001) [76]. Furthermore, the time until GC discontinuation was shorter in the LEF group (median 4.7 months) versus the MTX group (median 31.8 months) (p < 0.001) [76]. Although this fosters promise for the use of LEF in refractory PMR, several important limitations must be considered. These include its non-randomised design, the lack of a specified GC starting dose or tapering regimen (which was left to the treating physicians discretion), and the higher CRP and ESR levels in the MTX group at baseline and at the time of enrolment suggesting potentially greater disease activity. Additionally, a large number of patients in the MTX group received doses of 15 mg or less, which may represent underdosing and could have contributed to its apparent inferiority [76]. Therefore, the results of the ongoing randomised, double blind, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial “PMRLEFRCT” (NCT03576794), which is assessing daily leflunomide 20 mg in combination with a 26-week GC tapering regimen, are highly anticipated to clarify the role of LEF in the management algorithm of PMR. Additionally, the results of STERLING-PMR (ISRCTN17828080), as described above, will provide valuable insight into the role of LEF in the management of PMR.

Azathioprine is another csDMARD whose role in PMR has been evaluated in an RCT [77]. Although the investigators reported a significantly lower mean prednisolone dose at 52 weeks in the azathioprine group (1.9 mg) compared to placebo (4.2 mg), its use was associated with a higher incidence of adverse events [77]. Moreover, the study was limited by a small sample size and a high dropout rate [77].

Biologic Disease-Modifying Anti-rheumatic Drugs

Interleukin-6 Antagonists

Given the pivotal role of interleukin-6 (IL-6) in the pathogenesis of PMR, and the consistently elevated levels of serum IL-6 levels in those with active disease [78], the exploration of the efficacy of IL-6 antagonists in PMR has ushered in a new era in the management of PMR.

Following the positive results of the pivotal SAPHYR study (NCT03600818), sarilumab, a fully human anti-IL-6 receptor alpha monoclonal antibody, was approved for the management of refractory PMR by the FDA in 2023 [9]. This phase 3, double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT evaluated the efficacy and safety of subcutaneously administered sarilumab in 118 patients with active GC-refractory PMR on 7.5 mg/day prednisolone [9]. A significantly higher number of patients in the sarilumab group (200 mg every 2 weeks, with 14-week GC taper) compared with the comparator group (placebo every 2 weeks with 52-week GC taper) achieved sustained remission from weeks 12 to 52 (28.3% vs. 10.3%; p = 0.0193) [9]. Additionally, at week 52, the cumulative GC burden was significantly lower in the sarilumab (777 mg) vs. placebo group (2044 mg) (p < 0.001), with the GTI also favouring the sarilumab group [97 (76–121.5)] compared with the placebo group [112.5 (85–141)] [9]. The most commonly reported adverse event during sarilumab treatment was neutropenia, which occurred in 15% of patients and resulted in treatment discontinuation in 12% [9]. Notably, no infections were associated with the neutropenia. However, white blood cell counts should be carefully monitored in those treated with sarilumab, with dose adjustment or cessation of therapy as appropriate, should neutropenia occur.

Tocilizumab, an IL-6 receptor antagonist, has also had positive outcomes in two phase 3 trials [79, 80]. The SEMAPHORE study (NCT02908217), a phase 3, double-blind, parallel-group, placebo-controlled RCT, examined the efficacy of intravenously administered tocilizumab in those with GC-dependent PMR, requiring doses of prednisolone ≥ 10 mg per day [79]. A total of 101 patients were randomly assigned to receive tocilizumab at a dose of 8 mg/kg every 4 weeks or placebo every 4 weeks, with both groups undergoing a predefined standardised tapering of orally administered prednisolone [79]. The primary outcome of a CRP-PMR-AS < 10, in addition to attainment of a prednisolone dose of 5 mg per day or a reduction in prednisolone dose by 10 mg from baseline at week 24, was achieved in 67.3% of those in the tocilizumab group compared to 31.4% in the placebo group (p < 0.001) [79]; 49% of patients in the tocilizumab arm were no longer receiving GCs at 24 weeks compared to 19.6% in the placebo arm (p < 0.001) [79].

The PMR SPARE (NCT03263715) double-blind, placebo-controlled, multicentre phase 2/3 RCT evaluated the use of tocilizumab in 36 newly diagnosed, treatment-naïve patients with PMR [80]. Those in the tocilizumab group received 162 mg subcutaneously every week along with an 11-week GC taper, whilst the placebo group received a weekly placebo, in addition to the same 11-week GC taper [80]. At 16 weeks, GC-free remission was achieved in 12 out of 19 patients (63.2%) receiving tocilizumab compared with 2 out of 17 patients (11.8%) receiving placebo (p = 0.002) [80]. The median cumulative GC burden was also less in the tocilizumab arm (727 mg) compared to the placebo arm (935 mg) (p = 0.003) [80].

Despite the positive results from both these studies, with each one meeting their primary endpoint (Table 1), and despite the successful indication of tocilizumab in patients with GCA, it has to date failed to attain regulatory approval for use in patients with isolated PMR. Moreover, whilst the regulatory approval of sarilumab represents a pivotal step in the advancement of PMR care, several unanswered questions remain, including the role of sarilumab in newly diagnosed patients, and the optimal therapeutic duration and method of treatment withdrawal. Additionally, given the effects of IL-6 inhibitors on the acute phase reactants CRP and ESR, there is an urgent need for a reliable serum biomarker to assess disease activity.

Investigational Targeted Therapies

Anti-Tumour Necrosis Factor Alpha Agents

Early observational studies suggested potential efficacy of tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNFα) antagonists in PMR [81, 82]. However, two subsequent phase 3 RCTs failed to confirm a meaningful therapeutic effect (Table 1) [83, 84].

The largest of these RCTs investigated infliximab, a chimeric monoclonal anti-TNFα antibody, in 51 newly diagnosed, GC-naïve patients with PMR [83]. Participants were randomised to receive either intravenously administered infliximab (3 mg/kg) or placebo, in combination with a 16-week GC taper [83]. At 52 weeks, there was no significant difference in the primary endpoint (proportion of patients relapse free) between groups [83]. Secondary outcomes, including the number and duration of relapses, in addition to cumulative GC exposure were also comparable [83].

A smaller phase 3 RCT evaluated etanercept, a recombinant human TNF dimeric receptor fusion protein, at a dose of 25 mg subcutaneously biweekly or placebo, in 20 newly diagnosed patients with PMR [84]. Patients did not receive any GCs in either group, and after 14 days, the PMR-AS score decreased by 24% from baseline (p = 0.01) in the etanercept group; however, the score remained unchanged in the placebo group (p > 0.05) [84].

Given the modest improvement only with etanercept, and the failure of infliximab to meet its primary endpoint, no further studies are currently planned to examine TNFα antagonists in PMR. Nonetheless, important limitations should be acknowledged. Both RCTs were small in scale and the etanercept trial had an exceptionally short follow-up of only 14 days [84]. Moreover, both studies evaluated the use of anti-TNFα agents in new-onset PMR, and as such, their use in established refractory PMR remains unknown, and may warrant further investigation.

B Cell Inhibition

Growing evidence suggests a potential role for B cells in the pathophysiology of PMR, given the presence of an altered peripheral B cell distribution in active disease [85, 86]. This is also supported by the findings of the BRIDGE-PMR RCT and its 1-year extension study, which demonstrated that B cell depletion with rituximab, an anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, may be effective in treating PMR (Table 1) [87]. This proof-of-concept 21-week RCT demonstrated the benefit of a single 1-g dose of rituximab, in addition to a 17-week prednisolone taper, in achieving GC-free remission at 21 weeks (48% in rituximab group versus 21% in the placebo group; p = 0.049) in both newly diagnosed patients and those with refractory PMR [87]. In the 1-year extension study, 47% of those in the rituximab group versus 23% in the placebo group (p = 0.12) remained in GC-free remission at 52 weeks following the initial single 1-g dose of rituximab [88]. There are currently two further RCTs with longer follow-up periods underway: the REDUCE-PMR-1 (NCT05533125) evaluating rituximab in new-onset PMR, and REDUCE-PMR-2 (NCT05533164) evaluating rituximab in relapsing PMR.

CTLA4 Fusion Protein

Abatacept is a fusion protein of the extracellular domain of the human cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated antigen 4 (CTLA-4) which is linked to a modified Fc of human immunoglobulin 1 (IgG1) that modulates the T cell co-stimulatory signal. Given the involvement of T cells in the pathogenesis of PMR [2, 19], abatacept underwent evaluation in those with early-onset PMR in a phase 3, multicentre, double-blinded placebo-controlled RCT, the ALORS study (Table 1) [89]. Patients were randomised to receive either abatacept (18 patients) or placebo subcutaneously in combination with a 12-week GC taper (16 patients). Fifty percent in the abatacept group versus 22% in the placebo group reached the primary endpoint at week 12 which was the obtainment of a CRP-PMR-AS ≤ 10, without GC or rescue treatment; however, the difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.07) [89]. Upon further analysis, no significant differences were observed in cumulative GC dose between the two groups at week 12 and 36 [89]. Moreover, the effect of abatacept on CRP-PMR-AS was predominantly driven by reduction in CRP, with minimal impact on pain, physician global assessment or other PROMS [89]. Consequently, no other trials evaluating abatacept are underway or planned.

Janus Kinase Inhibitors

The Janus kinase (JAK) family of intracellular tyrosine kinases, which comprises JAK1, JAK2, JAK3 and tyrosine kinase (TYK) 2, in association with signal transducers and activators of transcription (STAT) have a pivotal role in mediating the downstream signalling of a significant number of proinflammatory cytokines. Subsequently, the role of the JAK/STAT pathway and its targeted inhibition using orally administered small molecule JAK inhibitors (JAKi) has garnered much interest in the management of immune-mediated rheumatic diseases, and recently PMR (Table 1).

Baricitinib, which is a JAK1 and JAK2 inhibitor, exerts a rapid dose-dependent inhibition of IL-6-induced STAT3 phosphorylation, therefore halting the entire subsequent inflammation cascade. Baricitinib was evaluated in the treatment of recent-onset PMR (< 6 months) in the BACHELOR trial, a phase 2, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled study [90]. The primary outcome of the study was the attainment of low disease activity, defined as PMR-AS ≤ 10 at week 12, notably without the use of oral GCs. Fourteen (78%) of 18 participants in the baricitinib group achieved this primary outcome compared with 2 (13%) of 15 participants in the placebo group (relative risk 5.8, 95% CI 3.2–10.6; adjusted p < 0.0001) [90]. The difference in response between the two groups was observed as early as week 2. After week 12, baricitinib dose was reduced from 4 to 2 mg or placebo, for a further 12 weeks, and treatment response was maintained in 16 (89%) of 18 participants. Baricitinib was discontinued at week 24, and during the observational period up to 36 weeks, there were no disease flares. Although promising, these findings require validation in a phase 3 trial, which is currently ongoing (“JAK-SPARE1” EudraCT2020-005081-34). This is particularly important considering that, although patients were required to be GC free in the 2 weeks prior to enrolment, there was no restriction on GC use before this period, and data on cumulative GC exposure prior to enrolment were not reported. Additionally, it should be noted that the trial design permitted subdeltoid GC injections at week 1 and 4, which could have influenced outcomes.

Similarly, in the “EAST PMR”, a single-centre, open-label RCT, tofacitinib, a JAK1–3 inhibitor, at 5 mg twice daily, was evaluated as a monotherapy versus oral GCs in treatment-naïve patients with PMR [91]. At 24 weeks, there was no statistically significant difference in primary outcome between each group, with all patients in both groups achieving a PMR-AS ≤ 10 at 12 and 24 weeks [91]. Another phase 2 trial (NCT04799262) evaluated tofacitinib at 10 mg per day alongside a GC taper, in both new onset and refractory patients with highly active disease which was defined as a PMR-AS ≥ 17 [92]. By week 20, 12 of the 14 patients (85.7%) achieved remission (p = 0.014), defined as a PMR-AS < 7 and a GC dose ≤ 2.5 mg/day (prednisone equivalent) [92]. The mean GC dose at baseline was 15 mg, which reduced to 1.3 ± 1.2 mg (p < 0.001) by week 48, and a total of six patients (42.9%) discontinued GC therapy altogether [92].

A phase 3 RCT, the ITTGPMR study (NCT06172361), is currently ongoing assessing tofacitinib in combination with 4 weeks tapering GCs versus GCs only, in those with high disease activity, defined as a PMR-AS > 10.

Whilst these phase 2 trials are promising, JAKi will need to demonstrate a favourable safety profile, especially in this older, vulnerable and comorbid cohort of patients. Although no new safety signals were identified in the above trials, these studies were inadequately powered to investigate the occurrence of major cardiovascular events, venous thromboembolism, malignancy or serious infection, which, following the post hoc analysis of the ORAL Surveillance trial [93] and the subsequent warning about JAKi use in elderly patients at higher cardiovascular risk in rheumatoid arthritis, are of concern. Future trials will need to be sufficiently powered, with appropriate follow-up periods, to address such safety concerns, prior to the incorporation of JAKi into the management algorithm for PMR.

Interleukin-17 Inhibitors

A phase 3 double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT, “REPLENISH” (NCT05767034), is currently ongoing, assessing the efficacy and safety of the IL-17-A antagonist secukinumab in those with relapsing, GC-dependent PMR. Its objective is to evaluate secukinumab at doses of 150 mg or 300 mg every 4 weeks, in combination with a tapering GC regimen. Given the involvement of T helper 17 (TH17) cells in the pathogenesis of PMR [94] and a promising phase 2 RCT, the TITAIN study (NCT03765788), in GCA [95], there was much anticipation for the results of REPLENISH. However, Novartis recently announced the failure of secukinumab to show any benefit in either newly diagnosed or patients with relapsing GCA in the phase 3 GCAptAIN trial (NCT04930094). While these results underscore uncertainties regarding its potential role in PMR, further evidence from the REPLENISH trial are eagerly awaited.

Antibody Drug Conjugates

A recent phase 2, randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled RCT (AIM-PMR study; NCT04972968) evaluated a novel antibody–drug conjugate ABBV-154, comprised of adalimumab (a human monoclonal anti-TNFα antagonist) conjugated to a GC receptor modulator (GRM) [96]. This was developed to mitigate systemic GC-related adverse effects by selectively delivering the GRM to cells that express transmembrane TNF, including activated immune cells [97].

In total, 181 patients with GC-dependent, refractory PMR were randomised 1:1:1:1 to placebo (n = 50) or ABBV-154 at 40 mg (n = 42), 150 mg (n = 45) or 340 mg (n = 44) alongside a standardised GC taper [96]. The primary efficacy endpoint was time to disease flare. The trial was voluntarily terminated early by the sponsor at week 24, as interim analysis determined that the benefit-risk profile did not sufficiently differentiate ABBV-154 from other currently available therapies for PMR. Nonetheless, time to flare at week 24 favoured the ABBV-154 cohort compared to placebo with dose-responsive hazard ratios (HRs): 40 mg, HR 0.49 (95% CI 0.27–0.88; p = 0.017); 150 mg, HR 0.44 (95% CI 0.25–0.79; p = 0.006); 340 mg, HR 0.20 (95% CI 0.09–0.42; p < 0.001) [96]. Moreover, incidences of treatment emergent adverse events were similar between groups [96].

Although these findings suggest potential GC-sparing efficacy, interpretation is limited by early termination, reduced exposure time and sample size. Overall, ABBV-154 provides a proof of concept for antibody–drug conjugates in PMR, but confirmatory, adequately powered trials would be required to define clinical utility.

Future Perspectives in the Management of PMR

While significant strides have been made over the past decade in the management of isolated PMR, in particular the FDA approval of the first biologic therapy, sarilumab for use in refractory disease, substantial uncertainties remain. Relative to PMRs prevalence, associated disability and significant impact on HRQoL, the progress in management remains inadequate. Indeed, the research agenda and knowledge gaps continue to expand, highlighting persistent knowledge gaps across diagnosis, disease monitoring and optimal treatment strategies.

The precise role of the rheumatologist in the care of those with PMR is an ongoing matter of debate. Whilst the importance of specialist evaluation and the benefit of rapid access referral pathways are clear, the practicalities of increased resources for PMR in an already strained rheumatology workforce remain uncertain. However, with an increasing number of biologic agents under evaluation, and the anticipated evolving therapeutic landscape, the need for specialist input will become even more relevant. More studies evaluating the utility, feasibility and cost-effectiveness of early specialist input with rapid access services in ensuring enhanced short- and long-term outcomes including an accurate diagnosis, instigation of appropriate early therapy, stratification for GC sparing agents as appropriate and minimisation of GC-related adverse events are required. However, in order to optimise the role of the rheumatologist, there is an urgent need for the development of a disease stratification tool. In addition to serological or imaging biomarkers, future research should explore the potential of a multimodal prognostication tool including clinical and demographic features, serological and imaging markers, and PROMs. Indeed, the role of PROMs in PMR management needs greater attention in future studies and RCTs.

There is a need for clear therapeutic algorithms to guide the management of both early and refractory PMR, along with consensus recommendations for optimal management at each stage of the disease. The potential of a GC-free management in early PMR may be feasible, as demonstrated in the promising results of the BACHELOR RCT [90]. However, these findings need to be validated in a phase 3 trial, and the roles of other biological therapies in this context also warrant further investigation. Although both biologic agents and JAKi are currently more expensive than oral GCs, the emergence of biosimilars and low cost generics may reduce cost barriers. When considered alongside improved efficacy and safety profiles, particularly in terms of reducing cumulative GC exposure and associated adverse effects, the early, upfront use of these therapies may become a justified and practical approach in future PMR management.

Additionally, we know that many GC-dependent patients with PMR are often not considered for management escalation or a GC-sparing agent [58]. There is perhaps an inertia when the maintenance GC dose is deemed to be relatively low; however, we need to adapt the same standards to GCs in PMR as we do in our other inflammatory rheumatic diseases. The current guidance on monitoring and managing GC-induced adverse events is vague, resulting in much ambiguity and resultant failure to implement appropriate strategies.

Finally, there is a need to intensify translational research. Our current understanding of the pathophysiology of PMR is limited, and in order to guide the development and evaluation of targeted therapeutics, greater clarity on the pathogenetic mechanisms is urgently required.

Conclusion

Despite a recent resurgence of interest in PMR within the rheumatology community and literature, isolated PMR management remains underrepresented in the research landscape compared to other immune-mediated rheumatic disease of a similar prevalence. Critical knowledge gaps persist, particularly in the areas of diagnosis, disease monitoring and long-term management. In this context, we have underscored the importance of early rheumatology input, the development of evidence-based diagnostic tools and prognostic markers, improved monitoring strategies, and approaches to minimise GC exposure and associated adverse effects. With the expanding therapeutic horizon, including the emergence of effective GC-sparing agents, these priorities are increasingly relevant to improve overall outcomes in those with PMR.
