Diffuse Esophageal Leiomyomatosis and the Surgical Challenges Encountered
Authors: Tanvi Luthra, Sachit Anand, Anjan Kumar Dhua, Devendra Yadav, Vishesh Jain, Prabudh Goel, Devasenathipathy Kandasamy, Sandeep Agarwala, Rohan Malik
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDiffuse esophageal leiomyomatosis (DEL) is a rare condition characterized by the growth of multiple benign smooth muscle tumors (leiomyomas) in the esophagus. These tumors can cause obstruction and functional impairment of the esophagus, leading to symptoms such as difficulty swallowing, chest pain, and weight loss. The disease can present with leiomyomas or glomerular nephropathy in other body parts, hearing defects, astigmatism, or myopia (Alport’s syndrome). However, isolated cases are also reported in the literature. Surgical management of DEL presents several challenges due to the location and extent of the tumors. Herein, we describe a girl afflicted with DEL and required multiple surgeries for management, highlighting the unique challenges faced by the surgical team in restoring esophageal function and improving the patient’s quality of life.

INTRODUCTION

Diffuse esophageal leiomyomatosis (DEL) is an uncommon disorder that is infrequently reported in pediatric patients. While nonmalignant, it frequently manifests with severe dysphagia and accompanying symptoms such as vomiting and chest pain, significantly impacting the individual’s quality of life. In this case report, we aim to discuss a unique clinical presentation and the surgical challenges faced in the management of a case of DEL.

CASE REPORT

A 7-year-old girl presented with a history of recurrent episodes of dysphagia that commenced at the age of 1.5 years. These episodes were typically initiated with upper abdominal pain accompanied by discomfort and difficulty swallowing, particularly with solid food as opposed to liquids. In addition, she experienced excessive salivation during these episodes, which typically persisted for 7–10 days and resolved spontaneously with symptomatic treatment.

She underwent evaluation by a pediatric gastroenterologist and was subsequently referred to the pediatric surgery department to exclude the possibility of achalasia. Upper gastrointestinal (GI) endoscopy was unremarkable except for erosions in the lower esophageal mucosa. Contrast study and computed tomography (CT) imaging studies demonstrated thickening of the esophageal muscular wall throughout [Figures 1 and 2]. This raised suspicion of extraluminal compression by the thickened muscular wall, but the pathology was still inscrutable. She was admitted for a thoracoscopic biopsy to clinch the diagnosis.

(a and b) Contrast-enhanced computed tomography chest - Circumferential thickening of esophageal wall with compromised lumen

On examination, her overall condition was stable, with no fever and normal vital signs. No signs of pallor or jaundice were observed. Pulmonary examination revealed normal breath sounds, and her abdominal examination was unremarkable. During thoracoscopy, the esophagus was found to be dilated and bulky along its entire length [Figure 3]. A biopsy of the esophageal wall confirmed the presence of leiomyomatosis.

(a and b) Thoracoscopy and biopsy of esophagus - Grossly dilated esophagus. Inset - Esophageal biopsy

Three months later, she underwent esophagectomy (right posterolateral thoracotomy approach) and esophageal replacement with a gastric pull-up. It was noted that the cervical esophagus, the gastroesophageal junction, and the stomach were not involved in the process of leiomyomatosis. The transhiatal posterior mediastinal route was chosen for the gastric pull-up.

In the postoperative period, she had a leak from the cervical anastomosis that gradually worsened and led to the development of a cervico-pleural fistula and also recurrent right-sided loculated empyema. This required re-exploration and a thoracotomy (thrice) to achieve a pleural toilet and assessment of the gastric pull-up.

An attempt was made to re-establish the integrity of the esophagogastric anastomosis in the cervical region. However, the leak recurred, and as there was no improvement in her clinical status, it was finally decided to dismantle the cervical anastomosis and offer a cervical esophagostomy as a salvage procedure. The pulled-up stomach was reposited back into the abdomen, and a feeding gastrostomy was created at the site of fundal anastomosis. There was another surgical intervention required after this for retraction of the cervical esophagostomy. During this intervention, a major intraoperative bleed was encountered because of injury to the right common carotid artery puncture (repaired primarily).

She was lost to follow-up for 6 years during the COVID-19 pandemic, after which she presented in a preserved condition with a documented weight gain but a hoarse voice suggestive of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury. There was crowding of ribs on the right hemithorax and restricted chest movement as a sequel to previous thoracotomies. In this admission, the patient underwent evaluation for potential treatment options to provide her with a replacement for her esophagus. A colon interposition was considered a possible option for esophageal replacement in this patient. Looking at the contrast-enhanced CT bone windows (done for evaluation of the carotid trunk status following injury to it in the last operation performed), there was an evident gross asymmetry between the thoracic cavities, with the right being contracted compared to the normal left side. The thoracic inlet was also affected in the process, and the thoracic inlet was also narrow [Figures 4 and 5].

Three-dimensional reconstruction image of right thoracic cage demonstrating rib crowding

Computed tomography chest demonstrating reduced anteroposterior diameter of right hemithorax and thoracic inlet

A left colic pedicle-based is isoperistaltic segment of the colon from hepatic flexure to the descending colon was used to interpose in a retrosternal plane. Although the length was not an issue, the anteroposterior diameter of the thoracic inlet posed a challenge as the colon interposition had to be negotiated through the narrow thoracic inlet (as was expected when reviewing the CT images), leading to immediate congestion and violaceous discoloration of the transposed colon at the cervical end. In addition to the color changes, a well-lubricated nasogastric tube (NGT) was not negotiable further down beyond the thoracic inlet when passed beyond the level of anastomosis. This was confirmed by digital examination through the lumen of the colon conduit. To circumvent this, partial manubriectomy and excision of the left sternoclavicular joint were performed, which led to the immediate return of circulation and normalization of the color of the colon and easy passage of the NGT. Cervical anastomosis, cologastric anastomosis, and a feeding jejunostomy completed the procedure.

A contrast study was performed on the 7th postoperative day, which confirmed the patency and functionality of the colon interposition [Figure 6]. On a follow-up visit 3 months after the definitive surgery, the child is consuming a normal diet and gaining weight. No deformity, reduced range of motion, or joint instability in the left upper limb was found on follow-up. She has returned to her school curriculum and is satisfied with the surgical outcome.

DISCUSSION

The esophagus is a muscular tube connecting the pharynx to the stomach and transporting food and liquids through peristaltic contractions. The intricate network of muscles in the esophagus enables coordinated movement for the passage of ingested material. Diffuse Esophageal Leiyomyomatosis (DEL) is a rare condition characterized by the growth of multiple benign smooth muscle tumors (leiomyomas) in the esophagus. These tumors can cause obstruction and functional impairment of the esophagus, leading to symptoms such as difficulty swallowing, chest pain, and weight loss. The disease can present with leiomyomas or glomerular nephropathy in other body parts, hearing defects, astigmatism, or myopia (Alport’s syndrome).[1] However, isolated cases are also reported in the literature.[2] When leiomyomatous tumors develop within the esophageal wall, they disrupt this coordinated movement, leading to symptoms of dysphagia and chest pain. Esophageal Leiomyomatosis is a rare and challenging condition characterized by the growth of benign smooth muscle tumors in the esophagus, leading to significant functional impairment and symptoms such as difficulty swallowing and chest pain.[3] The first case of esophageal leiomyomas was reported by Hall in 1916 in a 17-year-old girl presenting with dysphagia.[1] Esophageal leiomyomatosis usually afflicts the young population in the first two decades of life, but no age group is spared. It is characterized by an increase in smooth muscle cells within the esophageal wall, resulting in thickening around its circumference. In a systematic review conducted by Ziogas et al. on DEL among pediatric patients, they found only 34 articles highlighting the scarcity of literature on pediatric patients.[4] It needs to be differentiated from leiomyoma, a localized, encapsulated growth, and a genuine tumor. Furthermore, it ought to be distinguished from the diffuse thickening of the esophageal muscles around its circumference, which can happen in neuromotor functional disorder.

Presentation is usually with slowly progressive dysphagia over a long period; this is due to the gradual thickening of the walls of the esophagus caused by the increased growth of both circular and longitudinal smooth muscle layers. This leads to the narrowing of the esophageal lumen and ultimately impairs the ability of the esophagus to propel food toward the stomach. Other signs and symptoms include vomiting, indigestion, a persistent cough, difficulty breathing, and weight loss. Diagnosing this condition before surgery is typically challenging due to its rarity. In the systematic review by Ziogas et al., it was found that the most common manifestations included dysphagia and GI symptoms, observed in 90% of cases. Other notable presentations included failure to thrive and pulmonary symptoms.[4] Various diagnostic techniques, such as chest X-rays, barium swallow tests, endoscopy, CT scans, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and endoscopic ultrasound, are commonly used. Esophageal leiomyomatosis is usually suspected based on distinct radiological findings, including a tubular mass in the mediastinum and significant thickening of the esophageal wall observed in chest X-rays, barium swallow tests, and CT scans. CT and MRI are particularly effective in differentiating between pseudoachalasia and achalasia. These imaging methods reveal the expansion of soft-tissue masses, resulting from the thickening of the esophageal wall, into the cardia of the stomach. This characteristic feature is absent in achalasia and is considered indicative of esophageal leiomyomatosis. In addition, they provide valuable information about the extent of involvement in leiomyomatosis.

Typically, the illness primarily affects the lower part of the esophagus, although around 35% of patients have experienced involvement of the entire esophagus. In addition, about 80% of cases show that the disease extends into the cardia and the fundus of the stomach. In our case, the patient presented with progressive dysphagia over several years, indicating a long-standing and chronic course of esophageal leiomyomatosis, but the disease was limited only to the esophagus.

Surgical management of esophageal leiomyomatosis is complex and requires careful consideration of the tumors’ location, size, and extent. In addition, the involvement of the deep layer of muscularis propria can make both endoscopic resection and surgical treatment difficult.

In cases where endoscopic resection is not feasible or carries a high risk of perforation, surgical options such as enucleation and myectomy of the involved portion of the esophagus may be considered.[35] These surgical techniques aim to remove the leiomyomatous tumors while preserving the functionality of the esophagus. In the case of esophageal leiomyomatosis, surgical intervention is often necessary to alleviate the obstructive symptoms and improve the patient’s quality of life. However, the location and extent of the tumors present significant challenges for the surgical team. The intricate nature of the esophageal musculature and the proximity of vital structures such as the trachea and major blood vessels necessitate precise surgical planning and execution to avoid potential complications. In the review by Ziogas et al., out of 58 pediatric patients, most patients with DEL underwent esophagectomy, reported in 85.2% of cases. Reconstruction methods followed with gastric transposition being the most common at 37.8%. Other surgical interventions included thoracotomy (60%), laparotomy (47.1%), and neck incision (33.3%). Notably, 14.8% of patients did not undergo esophagectomy, indicating a tailored approach to treatment based on individual patient conditions and disease extent.[4] In cases where recurrent complications arise after gastric pull-up, an alternative option, such as colon interposition, can be considered, which was needed in our case.

The retrosternal route is a valuable option for oesophageal substitution, especially when the posterior mediastinum is unavailable. This route utilizes the native anatomy; the sternum provides a protective barrier for the esophageal substitute and preserves its vascular supply by reducing the chances of extrinsic compression.[6] Mobilization of the esophageal substitute can be easier with the retrosternal route as it utilizes existing anatomical planes; however, the thoracic inlet may pose a challenge due to its narrow anteroposterior diameter. Widening of the thoracic inlet is achieved by prophylactic resection of the sternoclavicular joint, including one-half of the manubrium and clavicular head.[78]

The multiple surgeries required for management described in this case report highlight the innovative approaches employed to address the unique challenges posed by esophageal leiomyomatosis. From thoracoscopic biopsy to esophagectomy and subsequent esophageal replacement with a gastric pull-up, the multidisciplinary surgical team navigated complex procedures to restore esophageal function. The postoperative complications experienced by the patient underscore the complexity of managing esophageal leiomyomatosis. From cervical anastomotic leaks to loculated empyema, the patient faced a challenging postoperative course.

In conclusion, the case report of esophageal leiomyomatosis and its surgical management not only sheds light on the challenges posed by this rare condition but also adds valuable insights to the existing literature on esophageal leiomyomatosis and surgical management of esophageal diseases. The report aims to emphasize on the high index of suspicion needed for the diagnosis of DEL cases and a tailored, multidisciplinary approach to their management.
