Barriers and facilitators for the adoption of peritoneal dialysis: protocol for a systematic review of qualitative studies
Authors: Ravi Shankar, Anjali Bundele, Joshua Low, Wei Zhen Hong, Amartya Mukhopadhyay
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPeritoneal dialysis (PD) is an effective home-based treatment for end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) that offers several advantages over in-centre haemodialysis, including better quality of life, increased autonomy and lower costs. Despite these benefits, PD remains underused globally, with wide variations in adoption rates across countries. Qualitative studies have explored the experiences, perceptions and decision-making processes of patients, caregivers and healthcare providers regarding PD, but their findings have not been systematically synthesised. This protocol outlines a systematic review of qualitative studies to identify and synthesise the multilevel factors that influence PD adoption.Methods and analysisWe will conduct a comprehensive search of electronic databases (PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, MEDLINE, The Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, Scopus) and grey literature sources for qualitative studies published in English from each database inception to June 2024 that explore barriers and facilitators to PD adoption. Two reviewers will independently screen titles, abstracts and full texts for eligibility based on predefined criteria. Eligible studies will include those that use qualitative methods (eg, interviews, focus groups, observations) to explore the perspectives of adult ESKD patients, their caregivers and/or healthcare providers on factors influencing PD adoption, initiation or maintenance. Data will be extracted using a standardised form and synthesised using thematic analysis. The methodological quality of included studies will be appraised using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme Qualitative Checklist. Confidence in the review findings will be assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation-Confidence in the Evidence from Reviews of Qualitative research approach.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required as this study will merely synthesise data from published studies. The results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publications as well as conference presentations.Study registrationPROSPERO, CRD42024570521.

Introduction

End-stage kidney disease (ESKD) is a growing global public health problem, with over 2 million people worldwide requiring kidney replacement therapy (KRT) in the form of dialysis or transplantation.1 The incidence and prevalence of ESKD are projected to rise steadily due to ageing population, increased and the growing burden of diabetes and hypertension.2 KRT places a significant burden on patients, families and healthcare systems, with high costs, reduced quality of life and increased morbidity and mortality.3

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is a home-based dialysis modality. In PD, the patient’s peritoneal membrane is used as a natural filter to remove excess fluid and waste products from the blood. Dialysis fluid is instilled into the peritoneal cavity through a surgically placed catheter, and solute and fluid removal occurs through diffusion and osmosis.4 PD can be performed manually, known as continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis, which involves multiple fluid exchanges throughout the day. Alternatively, PD can be done using an automated cycler, known as automated peritoneal dialysis, which typically occurs overnight while the patient sleeps.5

PD offers several potential benefits compared with conventional in-centre haemodialysis (HD). PD allows patients to dialyse at home on their own schedule, providing greater flexibility, autonomy and the ability to maintain employment and family roles.6 PD is associated with better preservation of residual kidney function,7 lower risk of haemodynamic instability8 and improved quality of life in some domains.9 PD is also more cost-effective than HD in most healthcare settings, with the potential for significant cost savings for patients and health systems.10

Despite these advantages, PD remains underused in most countries. Globally, only 11% of dialysis patients use PD, with wide variation in utilisation rates across countries, ranging from 0% to 71%.11 Even in countries with relatively high PD utilisation, such as Hong Kong, Colombia and Mexico, PD use has plateaued or declined in recent years.5 Numerous factors contribute to the low uptake of PD, operating at multiple levels, including individual patient factors, family and social influences, healthcare provider knowledge and attitudes, institutional and organisational barriers, and health policy and reimbursement issues.4

Qualitative research is particularly well-suited to explore the complex, multilevel factors that influence PD adoption. Qualitative studies can provide rich, in-depth insights into the lived experiences, perceptions and decision-making processes of patients, caregivers and healthcare providers regarding dialysis modality choice and use, while shedding light on how individual, interpersonal, organisational, community and policy factors interact to shape PD practices and outcomes across diverse healthcare contexts and populations.12 13 Several qualitative studies have been conducted to examine patient, caregiver and provider perspectives on PD, but their findings have not been systematically synthesised. The previous systematic reviews have explored patient and health system barriers to PD adoption,14 but these reviews focused on quantitative studies and did not include qualitative evidence. To our knowledge, no qualitative evidence synthesis has been conducted on this topic to date.

Synthesising qualitative research is important for several reasons. First, it can provide a more comprehensive and nuanced understanding of the factors that influence PD adoption, which may not be fully captured by quantitative studies.15 Second, it can identify common themes and patterns across studies, as well as areas of divergence or contradiction, which can inform the development of more robust and context-specific interventions.16 Third, it can highlight gaps in the existing qualitative literature and generate hypotheses for future research.17

Methods and analysis

This systematic review protocol has been developed in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols guidelines.19 The final review will be reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines20 and the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research statement.21 The protocol has also been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews.

Eligibility criteria

This systematic review will employ the SPIDER (Sample, Phenomenon of Interest, Design, Evaluation, Research type) framework to guide study selection, ensuring a comprehensive and focused approach to identifying relevant qualitative studies addressing barriers and facilitators to PD adoption.

Sample

Studies will include adult patients (≥18 years) with end-stage kidney disease eligible for PD, their informal caregivers (family members, partners, friends) and healthcare providers involved in PD care. This encompasses patients considering, choosing or declining PD, as well as nephrologists, nurses, social workers, dietitians, surgeons and other professionals directly involved in PD patient care and education.

Phenomenon of Interest

The review will explore barriers and facilitators influencing PD adoption, initiation or maintenance. This includes patient-level factors (knowledge, attitudes, fears, preferences, lived experiences), caregiver-level factors (support structures, perceived burden, understanding of PD requirements), provider-level factors (knowledge, skills, attitudes, experiences in recommending or managing PD), system-level factors (healthcare infrastructure, resource availability, training programmes, policies) and sociocultural factors (cultural beliefs, social norms, community perceptions).

Design

Primary qualitative studies using any recognised qualitative methodology (eg, phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, qualitative description, content analysis) and mixed-methods studies with a substantial qualitative component addressing our research question will be included.

Evaluation

Studies evaluating experiences, perceptions, attitudes, beliefs and decision-making processes related to PD adoption will be considered. This includes reasons for choosing or declining PD, challenges in initiating or maintaining PD, facilitators supporting successful PD use and perceptions of PD compared with other renal replacement therapies. We seek in-depth insights into patients’ lived experiences, caregivers’ perspectives and healthcare providers’ professional judgments.

Research type

Qualitative research and qualitative components of mixed-methods studies will be included. Pure quantitative studies will be excluded.

Studies published in English from database inception to June 2024 will be considered, allowing for a historical perspective and capturing recent evidence. Eligible studies may be conducted in any healthcare setting where PD is offered or discussed, including hospitals, dialysis units, outpatient clinics, home settings and community health centres.

Exclusion criteria encompass studies focusing solely on paediatric populations, those examining only clinical outcomes or technique survival without exploring adoption factors, quantitative studies without qualitative components, reviews, editorials, commentaries, opinion pieces, conference abstracts, and unpublished theses or dissertations.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy will be developed in consultation with a medical librarian to identify eligible studies. We will search the following electronic databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, CINAHL, MEDLINE, The Cochrane Library, PsycINFO and Scopus. The search strategy will combine terms related to peritoneal dialysis, qualitative research, and barriers and facilitators, using both controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH terms) and free-text keywords. The search terms will be adapted for each database. The final search strategies will be peer-reviewed by a second librarian using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies checklist.20 A draft search strategy is provided in online supplemental appendix I.

We will also hand-search the reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews to identify additional eligible studies. Grey literature sources, such as conference abstracts, theses and dissertations, will be searched using ProQuest Dissertations & Theses Global, Open Access Theses and Dissertations, and Open Grey. We will contact authors of relevant conference abstracts to inquire about the availability of full-text publications or reports.

Study selection

The search results will be imported into Covidence systematic review management software (Veritas Health Innovation Ltd., Melbourne, Australia) for screening. Covidence is a web-based platform that streamlines the study selection process by allowing multiple reviewers to independently screen studies, resolve conflicts and maintain an audit trail.21

After removing duplicates, two reviewers will independently screen the titles and abstracts of all records against the eligibility criteria. Studies that clearly do not meet the inclusion criteria will be excluded at this stage. The full-text articles of the remaining potentially eligible studies will be retrieved and independently assessed by the two reviewers. Any disagreements on study inclusion will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer, if needed. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be recorded and reported. Data extraction

A standardised data extraction form will be developed and piloted on a small sample of included studies. The form will be designed to capture key information about study characteristics, participant characteristics, data collection and analysis methods, barriers and facilitators to PD adoption, illustrative participant quotes, and authors’ conclusions and recommendations (online supplemental appendix II). The form will be modified based on feedback from the pilot testing to ensure clarity and comprehensiveness.

Two reviewers will independently extract data from each included study using the finalised form in Covidence. Any discrepancies in data extraction will be resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer if needed. If any information is missing or unclear from the published reports, we will contact the study authors for clarification or additional data.

Quality appraisal and risk-of-bias assessment

The methodological quality of the included studies will be critically appraised using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) Qualitative Checklist.22 The CASP checklist is a widely used tool for assessing the trustworthiness, relevance and results of qualitative studies. This tool, specifically designed for qualitative research, comprises 10 questions that assess various aspects of study quality and potential sources of bias. These aspects include clarity of aims, appropriateness of methodology, research design, recruitment strategy, data collection methods, researcher-participant relationship, ethical considerations, rigour of data analysis, clarity of findings and overall value of the research.

Two reviewers will independently apply the CASP checklist to each included study. Any disagreements will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer. While we will not use the CASP checklist to generate a numerical score or to exclude studies based on quality, it will provide a comprehensive understanding of each study’s strengths and limitations, including potential biases.

The results of this quality and risk-of-bias assessment will be reported transparently and will inform our data synthesis and interpretation of findings. We will critically consider how the methodological limitations and potential biases of individual studies might impact the credibility and transferability of our review findings.

Data synthesis

The data will be synthesised using a thematic synthesis approach, as described by Thomas and Harden.23 This approach involves three stages: (1) line-by-line coding of the findings from primary studies, (2) development of descriptive themes by grouping codes into related categories and (3) generation of analytical themes that go beyond the primary studies to address the review questions.

In the first stage, two reviewers will independently read and re-read the full text of each included study and apply initial codes to the relevant data extracts using Covidence. The reviewers will then meet to compare and discuss their coding, identify areas of agreement and disagreement, and develop a preliminary coding framework. The framework will be refined iteratively as the reviewers code additional studies, with new codes added as needed to capture emerging concepts.

In the second stage, the reviewers will work collaboratively to group the codes into descriptive themes based on their similarities and differences. The descriptive themes will be further refined through constant comparison within and across studies to ensure coherence and consistency. The reviewers will also examine the relationships between themes to develop a thematic map that illustrates the key concepts and their connections.

In the third stage, the reviewers will generate analytical themes that go beyond the primary studies to address the review objectives. This will involve interpreting the descriptive themes in light of the research questions, theoretical frameworks and wider literature. The analytical themes will be developed through an iterative process of discussion and consensus among the review team, with consideration of the relevance, coherence and robustness of the themes.

Throughout the synthesis process, the reviewers will maintain a reflexive stance by regularly discussing their own backgrounds, assumptions and biases, and how these may influence their interpretations of the data. The synthesis findings will be presented narratively, with illustrative participant quotes and visual displays (eg, thematic maps, tables) used to support the interpretation. The Socio-Ecological Model24 will be used as an organising framework to map the identified barriers and facilitators to different levels of influence (individual, interpersonal, organisational, community, policy). Sociocultural factors will be considered at multiple levels within the Socio-Ecological Model, including individual beliefs and norms, family and community influences, and broader cultural contexts that may impact policy implementation. The review will aim to elucidate how these factors operate across different levels and their interactions with other barriers and facilitators.

Confidence in the evidence

The confidence in the review findings will be assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation-Confidence in the Evidence from Reviews of Qualitative research (GRADE-CERQual) approach25 (online supplemental appendix III). The GRADE-CERQual approach provides a systematic and transparent method for assessing the confidence in individual review findings, based on four components: (1) methodological limitations of the primary studies contributing to the finding, (2) coherence of the finding with the underlying data, (3) adequacy of data supporting the finding, and (4) relevance of the studies to the review question.

For each review finding, two reviewers will independently assess the four components using the GRADE-CERQual guidelines and assign a confidence level (high, moderate, low or very low) based on their judgments. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The confidence levels and the reasons for the judgments will be presented in a Summary of Qualitative Findings table, along with the review findings and supporting data.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Ethics and dissemination

As this systematic review involves analysing data from already published studies, it does not require ethics approval. Findings will be disseminated through open-access peer-reviewed journal publication, conference presentations and targeted webinars for clinicians, policymakers and healthcare organisations. A lay summary will be distributed to patients and caregivers via advocacy groups and social media. We will engage healthcare organisations and policymakers to translate findings into strategies for increasing PD adoption.

The review aims to provide insights into multilevel factors influencing PD adoption, guiding intervention development, policy changes and improvement of patient education tools. By synthesising qualitative evidence, we hope to contribute to increased PD uptake, potentially improving patient outcomes and healthcare resource utilisation. Identified gaps in literature will suggest areas for future research, including specific barriers or facilitators, new interventions, and understudied populations or contexts.

Discussion

This systematic review will provide a comprehensive synthesis of qualitative evidence on the barriers and facilitators to PD adoption from the perspectives of patients, caregivers and healthcare providers, addressing a key gap in the current literature. The findings can inform strategies to increase PD utilisation at multiple levels. At the individual patient level, the review may identify knowledge gaps, misconceptions, fears and concerns that hinder PD adoption, informing patient education and decision aid interventions.26 It may also highlight the importance of patients’ values and preferences, emphasising the need for individualised counselling and shared decision-making.27 At the interpersonal level, the review may reveal the critical roles of family members and caregivers, uncovering their knowledge gaps and concerns, and suggesting opportunities for peer mentoring and group education interventions.28 29 For healthcare providers, the review may identify gaps in knowledge, skills and attitudes, as well as organisational barriers, informing provider education and quality improvement initiatives.30 It may also emphasise the importance of multidisciplinary team-based care.18 At the community level, cultural beliefs, social norms and public misconceptions may be identified, guiding the design of culturally sensitive education and outreach programmes.31 The review may also highlight opportunities for community engagement and partnerships.32 At the policy level, health system barriers such as inadequate reimbursement and fragmented care delivery may be revealed, informing advocacy efforts and policy reforms to create enabling environments for PD.33 34

The strengths of this review include its comprehensive search strategy, rigorous methods for study selection, appraisal and synthesis. The use of Covidence software for systematic review management will enhance the efficiency, transparency and reproducibility of the review process. The application of the Socio-Ecological Model as an organising framework will provide a holistic understanding of the complex, interacting factors that influence PD adoption across diverse contexts. The use of the GRADE-CERQual approach will provide a structured and transparent assessment of the confidence in the review findings.

Potential limitations of the review include the restriction to English-language publications. The focus on qualitative evidence may also limit the generalisability of the findings to other settings and populations, as qualitative studies are often context-specific. The synthesis of qualitative findings is inherently interpretive and may be influenced by the reviewers’ own backgrounds and biases, although efforts will be made to maintain reflexivity and transparency throughout the review process.35

Another limitation is the potential for publication bias, as qualitative studies with null or negative findings may be less likely to be published than those with positive results.36 We will attempt to mitigate this bias by searching for grey literature and contacting experts in the field to identify unpublished studies, but some relevant studies may still be missed. Finally, the review will not directly compare the effectiveness or cost-effectiveness of different strategies for increasing PD uptake, as this would require a separate quantitative synthesis of intervention studies.37

This systematic review protocol outlines a rigorous approach to synthesise qualitative evidence on barriers and facilitators to PD adoption. By providing a comprehensive understanding of multilevel factors influencing PD utilisation, the review findings will inform targeted interventions and policies to increase PD uptake. The inclusion of perspectives from patients, caregivers and healthcare providers will ensure a holistic view of the challenges and opportunities in PD adoption.

The review’s findings have the potential to enhance patient choice, improve clinical outcomes and contribute to more sustainable kidney care delivery models. By identifying key barriers at individual, interpersonal, organisational and policy levels, stakeholders can develop tailored strategies to overcome these obstacles. Similarly, recognising facilitators can help in leveraging existing strengths within healthcare systems and communities to promote PD.
