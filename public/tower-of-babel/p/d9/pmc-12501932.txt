Human Coronavirus-229E Hijacks Key Host-Cell RNA-Processing Complexes for Replication
Authors: Snigdha Sarkar, Song Feng, Hugh D. Mitchell, Madelyn R. Berger, Tong Zhang, Isaac K. Attah, Chelsea M. Hutchinson-Bunch, Victoria N. Prozapas, Kristin Engbrecht, Stephanie King, Amy C. Sims, John T. Melchior
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The recent rise in zoonotic coronavirus outbreaks underscores the urgency to understand virus–host interactions to develop potent antiviral therapeutics. Systems biology approaches, particularly proteomics, have been invaluable in providing a global overview of such interactions. However, these conventional approaches rely on measuring protein abundance changes that do not capture all molecular changes associated with altered regulatory pathways. In this study, we employed a high-throughput structural proteomics approach called limited proteolysis-based mass spectrometry (LiP-MS) to capture protein conformational changes, which we demonstrate are better proxies for functional alterations. We applied this tool to profile the molecular landscape of different human lung cells following human coronavirus-229E (HCoV-229E) infection. We found that HCoV-229E uses a multipronged approach to hijack key RNA-processing pathways and assemblies as part of a host-shutoff strategy to achieve effective replication. We confirm our results with structural data derived from changes in the assemblies after infection. We go on to show that modulation of two of these assemblies, the Nop56-associated pre-rRNA complex and the spliceosome C-complex, can attenuate HCoV-229E replication, indicating that we have identified viable host-cell therapeutic targets with potential to provide broad efficacy against coronavirus infection.

Introduction

Global urbanization and climate change have triggered outbreaks of zoonotic coronaviruses that have severely disrupted the global economy. Human coronaviruses (HCoV) are a large family of enveloped positive-sense, single-stranded RNA viruses that can cause mild-to-severe respiratory distress in humans. The danger posed by novel strains of coronaviruses was exemplified by the recent pandemic caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), which claimed millions of lives and overwhelmed healthcare systems worldwide. The rapid evolution of viral progeny enhanced transmission and evasion of existing countermeasures, which exposed flaws in traditional strategies aimed at directly targeting the virus, as new drugs and interventions struggle to keep pace with the continuously evolving subvariants. , Remarkably, a recent analysis revealed that virus-host protein interactions appear conserved across different pathogenic coronaviruses suggesting that fortifying host-cell machinery required for viral replication has significant therapeutic potential to protect against a broad range of different coronavirus strains.

Most coronavirus studies overlook the nuanced host–virus interactions taking place within specialized organelles, as positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus replication takes place exclusively in the cytoplasm. , However, evidence across multiple virus families supports that efficient replication requires either exporting essential nuclear factors into the cytoplasm or blocking the transport of proteins from the cytoplasm to the nucleus to evade host innate immune defenses. Picornaviruses and flaviviruses disrupt nuclear pore complex proteins, causing nuclear proteins to accumulate in the cytoplasm. − Flaviviruses, alphaviruses, and coronaviruses also inhibit importin α/β-mediated nuclear import. − Moreover, viral proteins from rhinoviruses, coronaviruses, alphaviruses, and flaviviruses localize to host nuclei, and blocking their nuclear translocation reduces viral replication or pathogenicity. − To explore how human coronaviruses alter the nuclear-protein landscape during infection, we focused our initial efforts on nuclear-protein-enriched lysates from mock-infected and infected human lung cells. The goal of the current study was to use a high-throughput structural biology approach to gain a systems-level understanding of how the coronavirus exploits host nuclear machinery for cellular takeover and replication.

Traditional mass spectrometry technologies enable the quantification of changes in protein expression but fail to capture changes in protein structure, which modulate the formation of macromolecular assemblies that serve as key cellular “control points” that drive molecular function. , Commonly referred to as “protein dark matter”, this extensive network of proteins and their molecular interactions within different functional pathways is the true regulator of cellular phenotype. However, these interactions remain largely unexplored and uncharacterized as traditional structural biology approaches to quantify changes in protein structure are laborious, expensive, and low-throughput, and they fail to identify changes at the global level. As a result, critical regulatory mechanisms and host-cell assemblies that are susceptible to attack by pathogens can be completely overlooked.

As part of the Predictive Phenomics Initiative at the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL), our team has adopted a suite of structural proteomics tools that leverage the high-throughput capabilities of mass spectrometers to capture global changes in cellular protein structure. In this study, we showcase limited proteolysis-mass spectrometry (LiP-MS), , which is a bottom-up proteomics approach that captures changes in protein expression, identifies proteins undergoing conformational changes, and provides the location on the protein that is altered. In recent years, LiP-MS has been applied across a range of biological applications, including exploring how drugs bind to targets in a complex cellular environment, , and improving our understanding of disease pathologies. , It has also been successfully executed in diverse biological systems, such as bacterial, yeast, and plant extracts, highlighting the versatility of this technology. − Using LiP-MS, we probed human lung epithelial cells and fibroblasts infected by HCoV-229E, an α-coronavirus that causes the common cold (Figure ). The LiP-MS workflow drastically enhanced the identification of pathways hijacked by HCoV-229E in lung cells and revealed that like their β-coronavirus counterparts, HCoV-229E is deploying a multipronged “host-shutoff” strategy to inhibit host protein synthesis in favor of viral proteins. Further, we provide a comprehensive list of key RNA-processing factors hijacked by HCoV-229E and show that modulation of two of these targets, the Nop56-associated pre-rRNA complex and the spliceosome C-complex, can inhibit HCoV-229E replication. This study highlights the power of LiP-MS to provide a more comprehensive landscape of changes within host cells, while providing a list of potential host-cell therapeutic targets and associated structural changes of their protein subunits.

General experimental strategy in this study. Immortalized lung cells were infected with HCoV-229E, and nuclear proteins were enriched 24 h postinfection. Parallel samples treated with an equivalent amount of the buffer carrier serve as the mock-infected (noninfected) control. Nuclear proteins in their nondenatured state were divided into two equal parts for the limited proteolysis-based mass spectroscopy (LiP-MS) workflow. These two halves were used to identify changes in protein abundance and conformation via liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). An in-house computational pipeline was used to analyze, visualize, and rank the infection-induced alterations. A sophisticated enrichment analysis combining the abundance and conformational changes identified the Spliceosome C-complex and the Nop56p-associated complex as major sites of biological perturbation caused by HCoV-229E infection. These hits were cross-verified in an orthogonal infection model comprising human airway epithelial (HAE) cells. Lastly, the findings were validated by showing that inhibiting the targets reduced viral replication.

Methods

Viruses and Viral Titration

Wild-type HCoV-229E was obtained from BEI resources for research purposes (NIAID, NIH: Human Coronavirus, 229E, NR-52726). MRC5 (human lung fibroblast) cells were used to generate HCoV-229E passage 1 (p1) viral stocks. HuH7 (liver epithelial) cells were used to generate HCoV-229E p2 viral working stocks and quantify viral titers following infection. Viral titers were quantified using standard plaque assay approaches. Briefly, cells (4.5 × 105 cells per well) were plated in 6-well dishes from Corning (Cat# 08–757–214). The cells grew to be 90–100% confluent overnight before performing the plaque assays. Viral samples were serially diluted, plated, and grown under 0.8% agarose and media. A 1× Neutral Red working dye was added to the plates to visualize the plaques for counting.

Immortalized Cell Culture

A549 (human lung epithelial cells); BEI resources: NIAID, NIH: Human Lung Carcinoma Cells (A549) Expressing Human Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme 2 (HA-FLAG), NR-53522 cells were cultured in high glucose Dulbecco’s modified essential medium (DMEM; Gibco 11–995–073), containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Cytiva; SH3007003HI) and 1% Antibiotic-Antimycotic 100× (Anti-Anti; Gibco). MRC5 (human lung fibroblasts, ATCC CCL-171), were cultured in α minimum essential medium (α-MEM; Gibco 12–561–056) containing 10% FBS and 1% Anti-Anti. HuH7 (human liver epithelial cells, Japanese Cancer Resource Bank JCRB0403) were cultured in low glucose DMEM (Gibco 11–885–092) containing 10% FBS and 1% Anti-Anti. Media serum concentrations were reduced to 4% prior to infection.

Primary Cell Culture

Primary human airway epithelial (HAE) lung cells were isolated from distal lung tissue and processed as described previously. HAE cultures were plated under BEGM growth media and allowed to reach confluence. Within 7 days of plating, cultures were grown under an air–liquid interface (ALI) for 6 weeks to allow full maturation and the development of cilia. Cultures were then shipped to PNNL and allowed to acclimate for at least 7 days prior to infection.

Sample Processing for Proteomics

A549 and MRC5 cells were mock-infected or infected with HCoV-229E (MOI 3) and harvested at 24 h postinfection (hpi). Media from infected wells was collected at 4, 8, 16, and 24 h postinfection to determine HCoV-229E growth kinetics in both cell types. Nuclear extracts were generated using Abcam’s Nuclear Extraction Kit (AB113474) according to the manufacturer’s instructions except for DTT and protease inhibitor were not added to the pre-extraction or extraction buffer. The protein content of the nuclear extracts was quantified using Pierce BCA protein assay kits (Cat#: 23227) and the extracts were subjected to LiP-MS.

Prior to infection, HAE cultures were washed twice for 30 min with 1× sterile phosphate-buffered saline to remove mucous from the apical surface. HAE were mock-infected or infected (MOI 3) with HCoV-229E and harvested 24 hpi. HAEC were removed from their trans-wells using 0.5% trypsin, and soybean trypsin inhibitor was used to neutralize the trypsin. Cells were washed three times in sterile 1× phosphate buffered saline to remove trypsin and soybean trypsin inhibitor. Whole cell pellets were snap frozen and stored at −80 °C until further use. The whole cell pellets were lysed using a hand-operated pellet-pestle homogenizer inside a biological safety cabinet. After protein concentration was measured through BCA, the lysates were subjected to LiP-MS.

Limited Proteolysis-Based Mass Spectrometry (LiP-MS)

The nuclear extracts from mock-infected and HCoV-229E-infected A549 and MRC5 immortalized lung cells were split into two equivalent parts: control and LiP sample (∼100 μg each). The control samples were treated with trypsin alone, while the LiP samples were treated with Proteinase K (nonspecific protease) prior to trypsin digestion. First, Proteinase K was added to the LiP samples at an enzyme: substrate ratio of 1:200. An equivalent volume of water was added to the control samples at the same time. All of the samples were incubated at 25 °C for 1 min, followed by immediate incubation at 98 °C for 5 min. Solid urea was added to the samples to achieve an 8 M final concentration of urea in the solution. Following this, samples were reduced with DTT (5 mM) and alkylated with IAA (40 mM) at 25 °C and constant shaking at 1000 rpm. After diluting samples to achieve a urea concentration of 2 M with 50 mM Tris HCl, pH 8.0, samples were digested first with LysC (enzyme/substrate ratio of 1:100) for 2 h at 25 °C shaking at 800 rpm followed by an overnight digestion with trypsin (enzyme/substrate ratio of 1:100) at 25 °C with constant, shaking at 800 rpm. Samples were acidified to 1% FA and then were desalted with C18 solid phase extraction, peptide concentrations were determined with a BCA assay, and samples were diluted to 0.05 μg/μL for liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS).

Mass Spectrometry Data Acquisition

Mass spectrometry analysis was done using a Q-Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) by acquiring data sets for liquid chromatographic separation of a 0.05 μg/μL peptide solution loaded on the separation column. The temperature of the ion transfer tube and nanoelectrospray voltage were set at 300 °C and 2.2 kV, respectively. Each data set was collected for 60 min following a 14.5 min delay after completion of sample trapping and start of gradient. FT-MS spectra were acquired from 380 to 1800 m/z at a resolution of 60 k (AGC target 3e6) and the top 12 FT-HCD-MS/MS spectra were acquired in data-dependent mode using an isolation window of 0.7 m/z at a resolution of 45 k (AGC target 1e5). For HCD fragmentation, a normalized collision energy of 30 was used with a 45 s exclusion time, analyzing only charge states 2 to 6. Liquid chromatography separations were done using a Thermo Dionex Ultimate, configured with 2 pumps for sample trapping and reverse-flow elution of the sample onto the analytical column, respectively.

A Thermo Dionex Ultimate 3000 configured with 2 pumps was used for liquid chromatography separations. One pump was used for sample trapping, and the second pump was used for reverse-flow elution onto the analytical column. An in-house prepared 4 cm × 100 μm i.d. packed with 5 μm Jupiter C2 was used as the trap column. Peptides elution was done using a reverse phase C18 column (30 cm × 75 μm i.d., 1.7 μm particle size of Waters Acquity BEH particles, Waters) at 200 nL/min with buffer A (0.1% formic acid in H2O) and buffer B (0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile). The gradient profile used for separation was (min, %B): 0.0, 1.0; 2.6, 1.0; 6.3, 8.0; 53.5, 25.0; 58.5, 35.0; 61.3, 75.0; 62.8, 95.0; 65.5, 95.0; 66.0, 50.0; 67.0, 50.0; 67.6, 95.0; 71.0, 95.0; 71.3, 1.0; 92.0, 1.0.

Database Searching

Raw files (.raw) generated by the Thermo mass spectrophotometer were searched against a Homo sapiens Uniprot/SwissProt database (Proteome ID #UP000005640, 20383 entries) using MaxQuant (Ver. 1.6.17.0) for label-free quantification (LFQ). The double-digested and the control (trypsin alone) samples were processed separately in MaxQuant, with digestion mode selected as “semispecific”. The following settings were common to both the samples: (1) variable modificationsN-terminal peptide acetylation, oxidation at methionine, (2) fixed modification–carbamidomethylation at cysteine, (3) FDR at protein level0.01, (4) FDR at peptide level0.01, (5) match between run-match time window0.7 min (6) minimum number of unique peptides that a protein group must have to be in the final quantification table was set to 2. All other parameters were set to the default MaxQuant settings. To identify viral proteins, the RAW files from the only trypsin-treated samples were searched against a manually curated FASTA file containing the structural and nonstructural protein sequences from GenBank: AF304460.1.

Proteomics Data Processing

The output data tables from MaxQuant were analyzed using Python scripts available at (https://github.com/pnnl-predictive-phenomics/LiP-processing-script?tab=readme-ov-file). The data set contained both protein-level and peptide-level identification and quantification results. The peptide-level quantification included misidentified peptides such as contamination and reverse peptides. We first filtered out all the misidentified peptides, followed by normalization through median centering across samples and replicates for both the protein-level and peptide-levels of quantification. Then we calculated the log 2 fold changes between mock samples and infected samples with quantifications of trypsin-digested proteins, student t test is also performed to show the statistical significance of protein abundance changes. Using the log 2 fold changes of proteins that have significant abundance changes (p < 0.05) from trypsin-digested samples, we normalized the peptide-level quantification of double-digested samples. This was to account for the protein abundance changes in corresponding samples. After the normalization, we performed student t test and calculated log 2 fold changes. We also labeled each peptide based on the digested patterns with three categories (tryptic, semitryptic, nontryptic). The significantly changed (p < 0.05) peptides in the double-digested sample were filtered to assign proteins to the different structural alteration levels, and also to generate the structural barcodes.

Ranking Proteins by Structural Change

The following parameters were used to score the structurally altered proteins solventaccessibilityscore=number of semi tryptic peptides in the double digested samplesnumber of fully tryptic peptides in the trypsin only samples perturbationscore=(sum of absolutefold change of significant semi tryptic peptides)×(solventaccessibilityscore)

Two thresholds were chosen for the two parameters to classify proteins in three levels. Briefly, proteins with at least one statistically significant semitryptic peptide were considered as level 1, proteins with solvent accessibility score >0.5 as level 2, and proteins with solvent accessibility >0.5, and perturbation score >4 as level 3. The subset of proteins found in level 2 are expected to have overall higher solvent accessibility which would generally reflect in a higher number of semitryptic peptides compared to fully tryptic peptides in the protein. By taking into account the overall coverage of the protein, we reasoned that proteins in this level have a more robust readout of structural change. Lastly, the proteins in level 3 have the highest perturbation scores, reflecting significantly higher fold changes at the semitryptic peptide level, and thus, are considered as undergoing the most prominent infection-induced conformational change.

Structural Barcodes

The 2D-structural barcode was used to visualize the change in solvent accessible peptide regions resulting from HCoV-229E infection. The significantly altered (p < 0.05) semitryptic peptides from the double-digested samples were filtered and aligned with the protein sequence available on Uniprot database (Proteome ID #UP000005640). The peptide-level intensities were rolled-up to quantify residue-level structural changes. Specifically, the intensity corresponding to each residue was calculated by summing up the normalized intensities of significantly altered peptides that contain that residue. After rolling up the intensities, we calculated the log 2 fold changes and performed student t test at the single site level. Then we selected all the residues that are significantly changed (p < 0.05) and visualized them in the barcode, where red represented positive fold changes, and blue represented negative fold changes.

Enrichment of Functional Protein Complexes

For initial functional enrichment analysis, the gene ontology (GO) terms associated with significantly upregulated, significantly downregulated, and proteins with significant structural change at the three levels were identified using DAVID 2021. , To decrease the redundancy of biological information, the GO terms for biological processes, molecular function, and cellular compartments were grouped based on similarity using default agglomeration settings in DAVID. The enrichment score of each group is calculated as the geometric mean of the EASE scores (modified Fisher Exact) associated with the underlying gene ontology terms in the group. The functional clusters with enrichment scores below 1.3 were filtered out. Representative terms summarizing the GO terms in each functional cluster was used to represent them in figures.

Detected proteins were sorted by differential abundance significance for the abundance study or an average percentile rank score combining the solvent accessibility and perturbation scores for the LiP-MS study. To determine the combined effects of the abundance and structural approaches in an equitable manner, equal numbers of proteins from the top sections of the ranked proteins were used for enrichment. For each cell type, the top proteins from each list were combined into one nonredundant set, and the entire list of proteins detected from each approach was combined to form one background pool. For gene sets, we used the CORUM data set from the Harmonizome resource, , and the Gene Ontology. , Gene sets were filtered such that all sets with an overlap of less than 4 with the background were removed. Significant enrichment with each gene set was determined using a one-sided Fisher Exact test with the EASE adjustment. For noncombined analysis, abundance changes with corrected p-value < .05 were used as differentially abundant, while three levels of differential structure, based on solvent accessibility and perturbation score thresholds were used to identify structurally changed proteins.

The top 450 significant proteins from each approach were found to provide the most informative combined enrichment results. P-values from each query of the gene set database were corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg correction for multiple testing. Results with adjusted p-values above 0.01 (−log10(p-value) = 2) were filtered out.

NOP56 Loss of Function Studies with siRNA

For siRNA-mediated knockdown, two Silencer Select Pre-Designed siRNA species were purchased from ThermoFisher (catalog no. s20642, s20643). Immortalized human lung cells (A549 and MRC5) were plated and allowed to adhere for two h prior to transfection via Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Catalog no. 13788–150; Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines. For each experiment, controls included transfecting cells with a nonsense siRNA to ensure no loss of cell viability occurred following transfection alone, and in parallel siRNA species known to induce apoptosis were used to ensure siRNA transfection could elicit desired outcomes (AllStars HS cell death (Qiagen 1027298) siRNA AllStars negative control siRNA; Qiagen 1027280). The Silencer Select Pre-Designed siRNA (ThermoFisher) species (at a concentration of 30 pmol per well) directed against nop56 were individually transfected, in triplicate, into A594 and MRC5 cells 24 h prior to infection with HCoV-229E at an MOI 0.01. Media from infected cells were collected at 4, 20, and 30 h postinfection and assayed by plaque assay to determine levels of replication-competent virus released following treatment. Viral titers from the two siRNA treatments were averaged together and compared with the viral titers in the control samples.

Identification of Alternate Splicing Events

A549 and MRC5 cells were mock-infected or infected with HCoV-229E (MOI 3) in a 6-well plate. At 24 hpi, media from infected wells were collected to determine HCoV-229E titers in both cell types, the remaining media removed, and 300 μL of Trizol (Invitrogen/ThermoFisher) added per well. Total RNA samples were sent to Arraystar for downstream RNA sequencing (RNaseq; enriched for polyadenylated mRNA species) analysis using the Illumina platform. BAM files from RNaseq data were used as input to SplAdder (https://github.com/ratschlab/spladder, version 3.0.4), which identifies and quantifies splicing events in RNaseq alignment files. For our analysis, the instructions provided by the authors for “use on large cohorts” were used (https://spladder.readthedocs.io/en/latest/spladder_cohort.html). The software package Bisbee was used for differential expression analysis of splicing event counts, using the hdf5 output files from SplAdder as input. Bisbee was used to evaluate each of the following 5 splicing events separately: alternative 5′ splice sites, alternative 3′ splice sites, exon skipping, intron retention, and mutually exclusive exons. The number of statistically significant events as evaluated by the Bisbee package, are presented in the form of a bar graph.

Spliceosome Inhibition Studies

Pladienolide B doses were tested at 72 h postinfection of immortalized human lung cells infected with wild-type HCoV-229E. A 2 mM stock of pladienolide B was diluted into 2, 4, 8, 16, 31, 62.5, 125, 250, 500, and 1000 nM for the experiment. Uninfected cells were assayed for cytotoxicity following drug treatment alone (cell viability assay), and HCoV-229E-infected cells were also assayed for cell viability following infection and treatment with and without the dilution series of drugs (multiplicity of infection (MOI) was 0.125). All wells were assayed using Promega’s CellTiter-Glo luminescent cell viability assay according to manufacturer’s instructions at the times postinfection/treatment listed above. Numbers of relative light units were determined by using a BioTek Microplate Spectrophotometer using Gen5 software. Results are graphed as percent inhibition of cytopathic effect and cytotoxicity calculated by normalization of the data to 0% inhibition (wells treated with only DMSO and infected, least cell viability) and 100% inhibition (wells treated with DMSO and UV inactivated virus, highest cell viability) 100 × (1 – (100% inhibition – experimental)/(100% inhibition – 0% inhibition)) as determined by GraphPad Prism.

Results

Fibroblasts are More Susceptible to HCoV-229E Infection

HCoV-229E primarily affects the upper respiratory tract but can also cause severe lower respiratory tract infections in immunocompromised individuals. , Thus, we conducted molecular profiling studies on human lung fibroblast cells (MRC5), which provide structural support throughout the entire respiratory tract, and alveolar type II epithelial cells (A549), which are present only in the lower respiratory tract. We inoculated each cell line with HCoV-229E and observed increases in viral titers over a 48 h period (Figure A). Viral replication was significantly increased in the MRC5 cell, which had a 100-fold increase compared to the A549 cells 24 h postinfection, consistent with previous studies that indicate a higher rate of infection and replication in the fibroblasts.

(B) The log 2 fold changes of LFQ intensities of viral proteins present in infected cells vs mock-infected cells from untargeted proteomics are presented in the form of a bar graph. One asterisk (*) represents p-value <0.05 and two asterisks (**) represents p < 0.01 for Student’s t test comparing virus-infected and mock-infected samples.

Given these observations, we chose to conduct LiP-MS profiling studies at 24 h postinfection to gain insights into potential cell-specific processes altered by HCoV-229E. The central dogma holds that canonical coronavirus replication occurs exclusively in the cytoplasm. However, the surge of research prompted by the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic confirmed compelling evidence that the highly pathogenic human CoV (SARS-CoV, MERS-CoV, and SARS-CoV-2) can directly affect the host-cell nucleusdisrupting chromatin architecture, altering histone modifications, and interfering with epigenetic regulationultimately impacting nuclear function and the cell’s control over its own machinery. − Thus, we focused our efforts on profiling molecular changes in nuclear extracts derived from infected cells to determine whether HCoV-229E operates in a similar manner. We initially searched our proteomics data sets for HCoV-229E structural and nonstructural proteins (NSPs) (Figure B). We identified the spike glycoprotein, nucleocapsid, and membrane protein in both the infected A549 and MRC5 nuclear extracts, which are structural proteins that play key roles in virus entry, assembly, and replication. Despite major differences in viral titers, relative abundance of these proteins appeared similar between the two cell types, apart from the spike glycoprotein, which was higher in the MRC5 extracts. However, we observed several NSPs exclusively in the MRC5 nuclear extracts including papain-like proteinase (NSP3), replicase polyprotein 1a and the terminal adenyltransferase (NSP8). The replicase polyprotein 1a is proteolytically processed to generate ∼10 NSPs, which are necessary for establishing the viral replication complexes and host-cell manipulations. This further supports the higher susceptibility of MRC5 cells to HCoV-229E infection compared with the A549 cells.

HCoV-229E Infection Differentially Impacts Protein Expression Profile of A549 and MRC5 Cells

A major advantage of LiP-MS is the ability to quantify changes in both protein expression and structure in the same sample (Figure S1). In total, we identified 3419 proteins and 3051 proteins from A549 and MRC5 samples, respectively. Given robust identification of the viral proteins in our samples and that coronavirus replication occurs in the cytoplasm, we evaluated the quality of the nuclear extraction process by performing an enrichment analysis on all identified proteins to determine their canonical locations in the cell. As expected, a majority of the identified proteins have previously been associated with the nucleoplasm (Figure S2A). A closer examination of these proteins revealed that 65% of the identified proteins have been documented to be exclusively of nuclear origin or found in either the nucleus or the cytosol (Figure S2B). We found that 35% of the proteins were non-nuclear in origin and found mostly in the cytoplasm. While we clearly have cytosolic contamination of the nuclear-enriched samples, we noted it was consistent across all of the experimental groups, indicating it is primarily a function of the nuclear extraction kit and not a remodeling of the intracellular environment specific to infected cells, which could bias the proteomics analysis.

A principal component analysis of all A549 and MRC5 proteins after HCoV-229E infection revealed a stark divergence between cell types and state of infection (Figure A), indicating that each experimental group has a distinct proteomic signature. Indeed, HCoV-229E infection resulted in differential expression of over 30% of identified proteins within each cell type (p-value <0.05 and |log2(FC)| > 0.5), when compared to respective mock-infected controls (Figure S3). HCoV-229E infection led to roughly equivalent increases (>500 proteins) and decreases (>650 proteins) in both the A549 (Figure B) and MRC5 cells (Figure C). However, a comparative analysis revealed surprisingly little overlap in the proteins that were commonly upregulated across cells, with only ∼5% of those identified exhibiting increases in both cell types (Figure D). There was a larger overlap of ∼26% common proteins downregulated in both cell types (Figure E). These protein expression changes suggest HCoV-229E infection is inducing unique responses from A549 and MRC5 cells 24 h postexposure.

Changes in Host-Cell Protein Structure in Response to HCoV-229E Infection

We used the second LiP-MS aliquot to quantify relative changes in protein conformation by using a fast-acting, nonspecific protease to probe conformational changes in the solvent accessible region of proteins following HCoV-229E infection (Figure S1). We optimized our workflow using proteinase K to cleave solvent accessible peptides on the protein surface before being quenched by denaturation. The remaining sample is digested with trypsin to produce a mixture of semitryptic and tryptic peptides. By quantifying changes in the semitryptic peptide pattern between experimental samples, we can determine which proteins undergo conformational changes and the regions on those proteins that are structurally altered. Approximately 35% of the peptides identified from double-digested nuclear lysates were semitryptic (Figure S4A) indicating that proteinase K action was likely only limited to surface-exposed peptides as intended. We normalized all of the semitryptic peptide abundance by changes in protein abundance to ensure observed differences are not due to changes in protein expression (see Methods section for details). After normalization, we identified around 1900 statistically significant semitryptic peptides altered across the samples (Figure S4B). This translated to 817 proteins in A549 cells and 787 proteins in MRC5 cells that showed statistically significant structural alterations after infection by HCoV-229E.

Cellular processes and proteins are naturally dynamic, which could result in identifications of structurally altered proteins that are unrelated to HCoV-229E infection. To help filter out fleeting structural changes that might be attributed to these background protein dynamics, we established a structural scoring system to quantify the degree of structural change within the proteins. We developed two quantifiable metricsthe protein solvent accessibility score and the perturbation score (see Methods section for details). The solvent accessibility score measures relative changes in the protein surface area that result from conformational changes, while the perturbation score acts as a multiplier by factoring in fold-change in abundance of those semitryptic peptides. We classified structurally altered proteins into three levels, with level 1 including any protein with a structural alteration, level 2 including proteins that have a solvent accessibility score greater than 0.5, and level 3 having a solvent accessibility score greater than 0.5 and a perturbation score greater than 4. To ensure these two metrics are not biased by protein size, we analyzed the distribution of proteins of various sizes across the different scores (Figure S5). We found the distribution was uniform, indicating the scoring metrics are independent of protein length or size.

The scoring analysis of structurally altered proteins in the infected A549 and MRC5 cells is displayed in Figure . Remarkably, we observed nearly identical binning of structurally altered proteins within each scoring tier across both cell types. Binning at level 2 dropped ∼50% of the structurally altered proteins in each cell type, while binning at level 3 resulted in only 75 proteins within each of the cell types (Figure A,B). Indeed, ranking by perturbation score showed that only 10% of the altered proteins qualified for level 3 for either cell line (Figure C,D) and that approximately half of the proteins scored above the inflection point within the figure. We also observed an overlap of ∼32% of proteins that were structurally altered in both HCoV-229E-infected A549 and MRC5 cells at level 1, which remained consistent at level 2 and dropped to 21% at level 3 (Figure S6). Approximately half of the level 1 structurally altered proteins overlapped with proteins with differential expression after HCoV-229E infection (Figure S7). Collectively, these data show that structural readouts are identifying a substantial number of proteins affected by HCoV-229E that are absent from the conventional proteomics expression data and that there may be more overlap in affected proteins between infected cells than is evident from expression analysis alone. It should be noted that these thresholds are arbitrary and akin to setting fold-change thresholds for proteins that undergo abundance changes, which is routinely performed in proteomics data sets. The purpose of this ranking and classification system is to hone-in on the hotspots of biological perturbations induced by HCoV-229E infection and will often be data-specific.

Changes in Host-Cell Biological Processes in Response to HCoV-229E Infection

Next, we set out to identify the cellular pathways impacted by the HCoV-229E infection. First, we performed a traditional functional enrichment analysis using DAVID that groups proteins within a pathway based on their Gene Ontology (GO) categorization within biological processes, molecular function, and cellular compartment. We analyzed proteins that exhibited changes in expression and/or changes in structure and provided a complete list of the enriched functional clusters in Tables S1 and S2, respectively. Initially, we kept expression and structural data separate to contrast pathways identified from the different techniques. The top functional clusters altered in the A549 and MRC5 cells for proteins with differential expression are shown in Figure S8A. We observed minimal overlap in the upregulated pathways between cell types after infection, suggesting differences in the response of A549 and MRC5 cells to HCoV-229E infection. We observed upregulation of several mitochondrial processes in the infected A549 cells, which was completely absent in the MRC5 cells. Importantly, we observed no major differences in the mitochondrial proteins detected across the different experimental groups, indicating these observations were unlikely due to differences in sample enrichment. In fact, this could result from the vast remodeling of the host-cell endomembrane system by coronaviruses , or alternatively, a stress response in the A549 cells to HCoV-229E, leading to the translocation of several mitochondrial proteins to the nucleus. In the infected MRC5 cells, we observed upregulated G-coupled protein receptor signaling, mRNA processing, dynein dynamics, and ion transport. These functional differences in the upregulated pathways of MRC5 cells compared to A549 cells could be due to different stages of viral replication, which appear to be at earlier stages. We observed much greater overlap in downregulated pathways in both cell types after HCoV-229E infection, with a distinct impact on host protein translation processes and N-linked glycosylation which is important for proper protein folding and signaling.

We performed the same GO functional enrichment analysis using only the list of structurally altered proteins within each scoring tier, (Figure S8B). Consistent with downregulated proteins, we observed a major impact on protein translation processes at all three levels of structural scoring in both cell types following HCoV-229E infection. The structural data further revealed new patterns of functional enrichment independent of the expression data, amplifying pathways important for mRNA splicing and granule lumen in both cell types. Proper splicing and removal of introns from pre-mRNA is critical for generating mature mRNA for translation and is a reported target of RNA viruses. Similarly, SARS-CoV-2 and HCoV-OC43 proteins have both been reported to disrupt stress granule formation to subvert host defenses. , Though the upregulated protein expression data hinted that mRNA splicing might be altered in HCoV-229E-infected MRC5 cells, the structural data revealed that this pathway is more impacted in both cell types than originally observed. Likewise, granule lumen-related processes were completely absent in protein expression data but more robust from structural enrichment, demonstrating complementary information provided from the LiP-MS workflow.

Given the complementary nature of the expression and structural data, we developed an integrated pathway enrichment analysis that accounts for all proteins undergoing changes in expression or structure (Figure S9). For this analysis, we ignored the directionality of expression data to cast a large net to include any proteins with statistically significant expression changes or structural changes at the lowest level. We generated a heatmap of cellular pathways impacted by HCoV-229E infection for each cell type, distinguishing those due to changes in protein expression, structure, and then a combination of both. We found that structurally altered proteins identified several pathways not captured by enrichment with proteins that had altered expression. This is most apparent in the A549 cells where protein expression changes indicate that HCoV-229E is highly impacting mitochondria-related functions, while structural changes indicate HCoV-229E is impacting host protein translation pathways. In infected MRC5 cells, there was more overlap in functionally altered pathways identified from expression and structural data, which both strongly indicate HCoV-229E is primarily affecting host protein translation. In addition to protein translation, our analysis also identified a few additional common pathways altered by HCoV-229E in both cell types, such as cytoplasmic stress granules and the melanosome. Stress granules are well-documented to be produced by the host-cell as a protective mechanism in response to viral infection. Interestingly, the melanosome, which provides color and protects tissues from the sun is important for production of vitamin D which has been speculated to play a key role in the severity of response to COVID-19. One surprising observation is the increased number of altered pathways in the infected A549 cells compared to MRC5 cells (55 vs 39) despite them having drastically lower viral titers.

HCoV-229E Targets Common RNA Machinery in Both Fibroblasts and Epithelial Cells

Our next goal was to identify the critical macromolecular assemblies that act as control points within the functional pathways that are hijacked by HCoV-229E for replication. For this analysis, we turned to the Comprehensive Resource of Mammalian Protein Complexes (CORUM) database , which provides annotated protein complexes based on the protein subunit building blocks of the complexes. Again, we performed independent analysis with proteins undergoing expression changes or level 1 structural changes and a weighted analysis that included both data types to identify 19 different macromolecular assemblies altered by HCoV-229E in each cell type (Figure A). Consistent with the functional pathway analysis, A549 cells had a striking increase in the number of altered complexes, with modest overlap in those identified by proteins with altered abundance or structure (∼26%). The overlap between abundance and structural data was more apparent in the MRC5 cells (∼60%). We identified nine common assemblies impacted by HCoV-229E in both cell types including the ribosome and associated subunits, Parvulin, the SNW1 complex, the Spliceosome C-complex, transactivation response RNA-binding protein (TRBP) containing complex, the large Drosha complex, and the Nop56p-associated pre-rRNA complex. Most of the complexes contained proteins structurally altered at level 2 or above, indicating that these complexes are undergoing significant structural changes (Figure S10). In line with the functional enrichment data, these assemblies are critical regulators of protein translation in the host cell.

Protein translation is a highly orchestrated process that relies on multiple assemblies to work in concert to process mRNA into mature forms for protein synthesis by the ribosomes. To gain insight into the interconnectivity between the common assemblies identified in both HCoV-229E-infected cell types, we built an unbiased network association plot where nodes represent the protein subunits that make up each complex, and edges depict their association with either one or both cell types (Figure B). The map visualizes the relationship between the assemblies based on their proximity to each other, i.e., the more proteins shared between two assemblies, the closer they appear in the map. Thus, as would be expected, the full ribosome and both 40S and 60S subunits are closely related and form a dense network of shared protein subunits. On the other hand, the spliceosome C-complex and the large drosha complexes are only distantly related to the ribosomal proteins, with more unique proteins (nodes) associated with them. This is understandable as these play larger roles in pre-mRNA processing. The Nop56p-associated complex is more closely related as it shares many proteins with the ribosomal complexes consistent with its critical role in assembly of 60S ribosomal subunit. , A complete list of proteins associated with each complex and heatmaps of their expression or structural changes is provided in Table S3.

HCoV-229E Targets RNA Machinery in Primary Lung Cells from Human Donors

To verify our findings, we applied our LiP-MS workflow to whole cell lysates extracted from well-differentiated primary human airway epithelial (HAE) cell cultures extracted from the bronchi of the healthy donor lung tissue. , The airway epithelium forms the first line of defense against respiratory virus infection ,, and thus, we reasoned that infection of HAEs cultured at air–liquid interface would best recapitulate the host cellular response. We observed that viral titers in the HAEs were comparable to those in the immortalized MRC5 lung cells (∼107) 24 h postexposure (Figure A). Surprisingly, we only observed a mere 96 proteins with significant changes in abundance after infection by HCoV-229E (Figure B); a drastic decrease compared to the immortalized cell lines, where we observed over 1000 proteins with altered abundance in each cell type. Only a small fraction (<1%) of these 96 proteins were also differentially regulated postinfection in the immortalized lung cells (Figure S11A). On the other hand, we observed 624 structurally altered proteins in infected HAE cells (Figure C,D), which had a larger overlap (10%) with structurally altered proteins in immortalized cells (Figure S11B).

Enrichment analyses on the altered proteome in the HAEs revealed nine functionally altered pathways that were previously identified in the immortalized cells, including several RNA-processing steps such as RNA binding and mRNA splicing via the spliceosome (Figure E). Enrichment analysis using the CORUM database identified 12 complexes that were altered due to HCoV-229E infection in HAE cells (Figure F) with five of the identified complexes overlapping those previously identified in the A549 and MRC5 cells. These included the C-complex spliceosome, 40S ribosomal subunit, Large Drosha complex, Nop56p-associated pre-rRNA complex, and the ribosome. Out of these, the large drosha, Nop56p, and spliceosome C complexes were also significantly enriched at level 2 structurally altered proteins (Figure S12). Given the meager changes in protein abundance after infection, most of the enrichment data was powered by structural changes, suggesting the structural data is providing an extra layer of sensitivity in the identification of altered cellular regulatory pathways and assemblies. Notably, the structural alterations in the ribosome, spliceosome C-complex, and Nop56p complexes were observed in all infection models, regardless of whether the data sets originated from nuclear-enriched samples or whole cell extracts. These consistent identifications underscore the sensitivity and robustness of the LiP-MS technology in samples of increased complexity.

Manipulation of Host-Cell Control Points Attenuates HCoV-229E Replication

We next sought to determine whether we could manipulate the key assemblies targeted by HCoV-229E to disrupt replication. For these verification studies, we down-selected targets to the Nop56p-associated pre-rRNA complex and the spliceosome C-complex, as they were structurally altered in all three cell types at level 2 or above and had readily available inhibitors or siRNA for testing. We started with the Nop56p-associated pre-rRNA complex which is a part of the small nucleolar ribonucleoprotein complexes (snoRNPs), specifically the “C/D-box” snoRNPs, that guide the 2′-O-methylation of RNA. Previous proteomic characterization of immunoprecipitated human Nop56p-associated ribonucleoprotein complexes led to the identification of 62 ribosomal proteins and 45 nonribosomal accessory proteins. Our analysis found HCoV-229E infection structurally alters a majority of associated proteins and results in decreased expression of several of the associated ribosomal subunits in both A549 and MRC5 cells (Figure S13A). Despite the diversity in associated proteins, the core proteins of snoRNPs include the NOP56-NOP58 heterodimer, NHP2L1, and fibrillarin (FBL). Thus, we targeted the NOP56 protein with siRNA-mediated knockdown, reasoning that the removal of this core scaffold component of the RNP would have the most disruptive impact. Both A549 and MRC5 cells were treated with two distinct siRNAs targeting NOP56 for 24 h, prior to infection with HCoV-229E, and viral titers were measured periodically postinfection. The average viral titers from the two siRNA treatments are presented in Figures A,B. We observed Nop56 knockdown had no impact on the viral titers in the A549 cells (Figure A), which, as with previous infection studies, showed meager increases in viral titers in both control and treated cells. However, Nop56 knockdown in MRC5 cells resulted in a reduction in HCoV-229E titers across all time points (Figure B), suggesting that HCoV-229E requires formation of functional snoRNP assemblies for efficient viral replication.

Next, we investigated the role of the spliceosome C-complex, which is the final iteration of a dynamic spliceosome assembly that orchestrates the removal of introns in pre-mRNA to form mature mRNA. As SARS-CoV-2 infection has been reported to cause alternative mRNA splicing, , we first investigated if we observe a similar phenotype in the HCoV-229E-infected cells. To do so, we performed RNA sequencing on infected A549 and MRC5 cells and quantified the five most common types of observed alternative splicing events: alternative 3′ splicing, alternative 5′ splicing, exon skipping, intron retention, and mutually exclusive exons. Again, we observed minimal impact on infected A549 cells, whereas infected MRC5 cells had a drastic increase in all five types of splicing events (Figure C). Thus, while our proteomics data strongly indicate that HCoV-229E is targeting the spliceosome in all three cell types (Figure S13B), a distinct functional impact is only observed in the MRC5 cells. Considering the observations from NOP56 knockdown studies in A549 cells and the lack of alternatively spliced transcripts, our data suggests that though assemblies are showing structural perturbations due to HCoV-229E exposures, the virus has not yet succeeded in compromising housekeeping function in A549 host cells.

To verify the importance of the spliceosome C-complex for HCoV-229E replication, we used pladienolide B to inhibit splicing factor 3b subunit 1 (SF3B1) and block the formation of the spliceosome assembly. In MRC5 cells, HCoV-229E infection causes pronounced cytopathic effect (CPE) i.e., loss of cell viability, typically evident after 72 h of infection. The disruption of viral replication reduces infection-induced cell death. We treated MRC5 cells with a range of pladienolide B concentrations (see Methods section for details), simultaneously exposed the cells to HCoV-229E, and evaluated whether disruption of the complex could inhibit infection-induced CPE. A549 cells were not tested as our previous studies indicated that assembly function was not compromised within the infection timeline under investigation, and they are resistant to CPE. As expected, HCoV-229E induced significant CPE in untreated MRC5 cells 72 h postinfection (Figure D). However, we observed a dose-dependent decrease of CPE in cells treated with pladienolide at the time of infection with HCoV-229E starting at 125 nM, suggesting the inhibition of splicing reduces the virus-induced cell death. The inhibitor pladienolide B induced substantial cytotoxicity in the mock-infected MRC5 cells (Figure S14), supporting previous studies. Taken together, this indicates the spliceosome C-complex is crucial for viral replication, and potentially a target for protecting cells against HCoV-229E infection, but other small molecules or drugs should be explored that have less cytotoxic effects on the host cell.

Discussion

In the current study, we applied a structural proteomics workflow to deeply characterize the molecular phenotypes of human lung cells exposed to HCoV-229E. By integrating global changes in protein structure and expression in different cell types, we uncovered novel insights into cell-specific vulnerabilities and resilience to HCoV-229E. Our analysis revealed that HCoV-229E engages in a multifaceted “host shutoff” strategy, repurposing various intracellular RNA-processing assemblies, such as the Nop56p-associated pre-RNA complex, and spliceosome C-complex to successfully colonize and replicate within host cells. Further, we demonstrated that manipulation of these assemblies attenuates HCoV-229E replication, verifying these cellular control points as host-based therapeutic targets for countering viral infection and spread.

A consistent phenotypic difference that we observed between cell types was a resistance to HCoV-229E infection in the A549 epithelial cells compared to the MRC fibroblasts. This is unlikely to be caused by differential access to the host cell, as both cell types express similar levels of aminopeptidase N, the cell surface receptor used by HCoV-229E to enter the cells. Indeed, despite the lower viral titers in the A549 cells, our enrichment analyses show substantially more altered molecular pathways than the MRC5 cells, with a unique response specific to mitochondrial processes. A critical mediator of the innate immune response is the mitochondrial antiviral signaling (MAVS) protein that is located on the outer mitochondrial membrane. Upon recognition of viral RNA by retinoic acid-inducible gene-I receptors (RIG-I), MAVS is activated which triggers the production of type I interferons (IFN) and a phenotypic switch to an antiviral state. , The RIG-I-MAVS-dependent IFN signaling pathway is effectively counteracted by SARS-CoV-2 and MERS-CoV to continue proliferation. Our data showed an upregulation of MAVS in the A549 cells that was notably absent in the MRC5 cells postinfection supporting the notion of a differential MAVS-RIG-I response (Figure S15). It could be that in the MRC5 cells which are clearly more susceptible to replication, MAVS expression is directly manipulated by HCoV-229E to evade the host-cell response, as has been shown with SARS-CoV-2. More focused mechanistic studies are required to test this, but we highlight this as one example of how contrasting detailed cellular responses to infection across different cell types can highlight potential cell-specific vulnerabilities.

Despite the phenotypic differences, our findings indicate that HCoV-229E universally impacts protein synthesis pathways, indicating it is using a host shutoff strategy akin to that employed by SARS-CoV and MERS-CoV. , This strategy shuts down the translation of antiviral proteins in the cells to facilitate the takeover of the cellular machinery, which is reallocated to favor viral protein translation within the host. Our data indicate that HCoV-229E specifically inhibits cap-dependent translation initiation, which requires formation of the 40S ribosomal subunit, the 43S preinitiation complex, and the cap-binding complex eIF4F that binds to the 5′ cap of the host mRNA (Figure S9). Our structural analysis revealed significant disruption in the 40S ribosomal subunit, and our expression data show downregulation of several of the eukaryotic initiation factors in the 43S complex (EIF1AX, EIF2A, EIF3A, and EIF5) and eIF4F complex (EIF4E and EIF4G) in both cell types (Figure S15).

Another mechanism for prohibiting protein translation is through direct inhibition of the entry of host mRNA into the ribosomal assembly. For instance, the SARS-CoV-2 nonstructural protein 1 (NSP1) binds to the 40S ribosomal subunit, where it uses its C-terminus to block the entry channel and impede entry of host mRNA. , Indeed, direct cellular expression of NSP1 from HCoV-229E has been shown to interact with the 40S ribosomal subunit and suppress protein synthesis, supporting the notion that HCoV-229E uses a similar mechanism. The 2D-structural barcodes generated using our LiP-MS data are in agreement, showing that HCoV-229E impacts the 40S ribosome and specifically induces conformational changes in ribosomal subunits 2 and 3 that make up the entry channel (Figure A,B). Moreover, by projecting the structural changes onto a 3D structure of the NSP1-bound 40S ribosome, we infer that the structural alterations in the HCoV-229E-infected A549 cells show a decrease in the detection of semitryptic peptides within these regions, indicating that the channel is masked from our nonspecific protease, possibly by NSP1 from HCoV-229E (Figure A,C). Conversely, we observe increased exposure of the entry channel in the infected MRC5 cells (Figure B,D). This could be due to the MRC5 cells being at a later stage of infection, where the virus has completely taken over the host machinery, repurposing it for viral protein synthesis. Consequently, the channel may need structural reconfiguration to facilitate the translation of viral mRNA. Interestingly, the HCoV-229E genome contains highly structured elements such as stem loop 1 (SL1) in the 5′ untranslated region (UTR), which interacts with NSP1, and allows viral mRNA to evade translation blockage. , This could enable the virus to circumvent the requirement for initiation complexes, facilitating direct recruitment of the ribosome for viral protein synthesis. This might also provide insight into why initiation complexes appear downregulated in MRC5 cells while the translation entry tunnel remains accessible to viral mRNA.

Our data suggests that HCoV-229E infection had a profound impact on RNA-processing assemblies such as the Nop56p-associated pre-rRNA complexes, which play a critical role in ribosome assembly, stability, and translation efficiency. Ribosome biogenesis begins in the nucleolus, where pre-rRNA undergoes a series of highly coordinated steps to be processed into mature rRNAs and assembled into ribosomal particles. The Nop56p pre-rRNA complex, a component of the C/D-box snoRNPs ensures proper cleavage and modification of the pre-rRNA during processing. The NOP56 complex uses snoRNA as a scaffold and the core proteins (NOP56, NOP58, NHP2L1, and FBL) to catalyze the site-specific 2′-O-methylation of ribose sugars in pre-rRNA. This methylation guides proper folding and structural stability to the mature rRNA, which is critical for proper assembly of functional ribosomal subunits. Moreover, 2′-O-methylation of the 5′ cap region of host RNA protects the RNA from host innate immune sensors such as RIG-I. Remarkably, coronaviruses are reported to use NSP16 in concert with NSP10, to catalyze the addition of 2′ O-methyl group to the ribose sugars in viral mRNA to mimic the 5′ cap in eukaryotic mRNA to protect it from recognition by the host’s innate immune sensors and evade the type I interferon response.

Our study suggests that HCoV-229E infection disrupts protein translation through two distinct pathways: by impairing the function of mature ribosomes and by interfering with ribosome biogenesis via its effects on snoRNP complexes, particularly the NOP56 complex. For instance, we observed a downregulation of several of the ribosomal subunits associated with snoRNPs, indicating a disruption in ribosome biogenesis (Figure S13A). This agrees with a recent study that demonstrated NSP1 of SARS-CoV-2 can localize to the nucleolus, where it binds to pre-rRNA to inhibit pre-rRNA processing and ribosome formation. It is unclear whether NSP1 exerts its actions by outcompeting the snoRNPs for the host pre-rRNA or, alternatively, whether it binds directly to the snoRNP assembly to modulate its function. Examining the four primary members of the NOP56 complex, we observed a major structural change in the core NOP56 protein in both A549 and MRC5 cells following HCoV-229E infection (Figure ). Visualizing the LiP-MS data using the NOP56 2D-structural barcode (Figure A,B), and projecting the same changes on the 3D structure of the NOP56 complex (Figure C,D), we find that the HCoV-229E infection resulted in higher exposure of two NOP56 key regions in MRC5 cells, which were not altered in A549 cells. The first regions spanned residues 59–77 of NOP56, which is critical for binding the methyltransferase fibrillarin, suggesting infection leads to the dissociation of the critical methylating enzyme on the host snoRNPS. The second region spans residues 280–396, which encompasses the “nop” domain, the RNA-binding domain that anchors NOP56 to the NHP2L1:snoRNA complex. This suggests that in the MRC5 cells, HCoV-229E obstructs snoRNP formation and, in turn, methylation of host RNA. It could also be that it is repurposing specific components of the snoRNPs and NOP56 complex to methylate its own RNA at the expense of the host RNA. This protects the viral RNA from host immune sensors while simultaneously accelerating degradation of host RNA as part of the host shutoff strategy.

Lastly, we revealed that HCoV-229E drastically induces mRNA alternative splicing events (Figure C). Interestingly, a recent study in post-mortem lung samples of patients with COVID-19 found a strong correlation between alternatively spliced transcripts and disease severity, suggesting this is a key antagonistic process to suppress the host immune response to infection. Indeed, SARS-CoV-2 can selectively induce expression of unproductive splicing isoforms of key antiviral (interferon and class I MHC) genes as a means for evading host-cell immune sensors. Our study indicates that HCoV-229E alters normal splicing events by directly acting on the spliceosome complex. This agrees with a recent study showing that the SARS-CoV-2 protein NSP16 can bind to splice sites U1 and U2 on the small nuclear RNAs (snRNAs) that make up the spliceosome to increase intron retention and disrupt proper maturation of host mRNA, thereby suppressing global mRNA splicing. Our study identified the C-complex spliceosome, which coordinates the ligation of the exons and release of the intron lariat. Consistent with the SARS-CoV-2 study, we observe alterations in U2 snRNP that forms part of the catalytically active site of the C-complex (Figure S13B). Our data support the notion that HCoV-229E is interacting with the spliceosome and likely disrupting function. As the majority of CoV mRNA species do not rely on the host-cell spliceosome for translation, we suspect HCoV-229E hijacks the spliceosome to trigger nonsense-mediated decay of improperly spliced mRNAs, including NOP56 above, as another strategy to impair host protein synthesis and even ribosome biogenesis. More sophisticated structural studies are warranted to investigate how HCoV-229E modulates the spliceosome at different stages of assembly.

Limitations of This Study

In this study, a commercial kit was leveraged to obtain samples enriched with nuclear proteins. Though 35% of the identified proteins were associated only with cytosolic organelles, we note the distribution of nuclear and non-nuclear proteins is consistent across the four sample groups and, hence, unlikely to confound the identification of cell-specific biological signatures. Furthermore, the same complexes were identified in whole cell extracts of human primary cells. Pure isolations of different cellular compartments in infection models will be more informative in future studies. Another limitation of this work is the lack of orthogonal methodologies to independently validate structural alterations from LiP-MS. However, the remarkable overlay of structurally altered regions with previously reported binding areas of NSP1 in the ribosome entry channel obtained by cryo-EM strongly supports the accuracy of the methodology. Finally, it is important to note that while spliceosome inhibition decreases the cytopathic effect induced by HCoV-229E, cytotoxicity is associated with the spliceosome inhibitor used in this study. As such, considerable drug discovery efforts are necessary to translate these findings into viable therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

In summary, our study offers detailed insights into human lung epithelial and fibroblast cell proteomes in response to HCoV-229E. The structural proteomics workflow enhanced our insights into the molecular mechanisms in both cells and ultimately revealed that HCoV-229E uses strategies to take over host cells remarkably similar to those reported for SARS-CoV-2. We identified multiple host-cell assemblies as key battlegrounds for infection and demonstrated that strategic manipulation of these host-cell “control points” can inhibit viral replication. These control points should be considered as viable therapeutic targets that could offer protection against a broad range of coronaviruses.
