Dynamic attraction of leached metal species enables durable glucose electrooxidation in a strong acid
Authors: Xiang Liu, Tiancong Zhou, Bo-Jun Yuan, Qiujin Shi, Peiyun Zhou, Ye Wang, Jing Li, Chunyu Zhang, Mingfei Shao, Haohong Duan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrooxidation of organic compounds to produce organic acid in an acidic electrolyte can circumvent product acidification step with high cost in alkaline electrolyte. However, catalysts often suffer from low stability in acid owing to severe leaching. Herein, we report a strategy to dynamically attract leached metal species to stabilize catalyst in a strong acid. In electrooxidation of glucose to formic acid over a lead dioxide (PbO2) catalyst, switching the electrolyte from HClO4 to H2SO4 reduces Pb2+ leaching by up to 30 times. As a result, a 275-hour stable performance at 1 A cm‒2 is achieved in H2SO4 (pH 0.3) using a membrane electrode assembly. Experimental evidence demonstrates that the SO42− ion attracts the leached Pb2+—generated from chemical reduction of PbO2 by glucose—by in-situ forming PbSO4 precipitate on the electrode, which is then oxidized to the active PbO2 phase. Benefiting from acidic electrolysis, we achieve raw cellulose conversion to formic acid without intermediate separation through an acid hydrolysis−electrolysis tandem process. This work demonstrates an efficient strategy to stabilize electrocatalysts that suffer from leaching issues in acidic conditions.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Renewable energy, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

Depletion of non-renewable fossil fuels has intensified the demand for sustainable alternatives1. Formic acid (FA) is an essential raw material for pesticides, dyes, leather, and rubber, and is also a promising liquid organic hydrogen carrier2. Current industrial FA production relies on a multi-step coal-based process, involving methanol synthesis from carbon monoxide, carbonylation to methyl formate, and eventually hydrolysis to FA (Fig. 1a). This route is energy-intensive and highly relies on diminishing fossil resources. Sustainable FA production from renewable biomass-derived glucose offers an environmentally benign alternative. When driven by renewable electricity (e.g., solar or wind), glucose electrooxidation reaction (GOR) to produce FA can be performed under mild conditions (room temperature and ambient pressure), with the additional benefit of co-producing fuels and chemicals at the cathode, such as hydrogen3–5.

It is worth noting that most previous reports for GOR were conducted in alkaline conditions (e.g., 1 M KOH), thus FA is present in its conjugate base form, formate, in the alkaline electrolyte. This inevitably costs an additional acidification step and chemicals (such as H2SO4) to generate FA (Fig. 1b). This situation also holds true for electrooxidation of other biomass-derived molecules (such as glycerol, 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF))6–8, wherein carboxylates were actually present in the alkaline electrolyte as the product that necessitates an additional acidification step to generate corresponding carboxylic acids.

In contrast, acidic electrolysis allows to directly obtain FA without acidification. Unfortunately, the development of electrooxidation of organic compounds (not limited to glucose) in acidic electrolytes is lagging far behind that in alkaline ones. A fundamental challenge lies in the inherent instability of most metals and metal oxides in acid, especially at anodic potential, owing to the metal species leaching issue9,10. There are only a few examples showing long-term stability in acidic medium11–15. Shi and co-colleagues reported a manganese dioxide (MnO2) catalyst that achieved 865 h of stability for glycerol electrooxidation in a 0.005 M H2SO4 electrolyte at 10 mA cm‒2 (ref.14). They attributed the high stability to glycerol scavenging surface-active OH species, preventing catalyst over-oxidation to soluble MnO4‒ and subsequent leaching. Nonetheless, both increasing acidity or current density significantly undermined MnO2 stability. Even if the current density increased to 60 mA cm‒2, the stable operation time was greatly reduced to 70 h. Very recently, Shao and co-colleagues demonstrated the applicability of MnO2 catalysts for GOR with 960-h stability, but it was also attained at very low current density (10 mA cm‒2)15. In addition, their catalysts’ leaching became severe at low glucose concentration, which hinders the high glucose conversion desired for practical applications. Except for the oxide electrocatalyst, Nakata and colleagues employed a chemically inert boron-doped diamond (BDD) electrode for GOR, but it delivered low activity and selectivity16. Consequently, it remains challenging to develop a GOR catalyst that is capable of achieving long-term stability in an acidic electrolyte under ampere-level current density.

Herein, we reported a strategy to dynamically attract the leached lead (Pb) species from lead dioxide (PbO2) catalyst during GOR, giving durable performance at ampere-level current densities in a strong acidic electrolyte (H2SO4, pH 0.3). We initially showed that a PbO2 electrode suffers from significant potential increase and metal dissolution during GOR in 0.5 M HClO4 electrolyte (pH 0.3). Then, we recognized that adding sulfate ions at a mere 2% mole ratio (SO42−/HClO4) into the above solution promoted the stability with 4-fold suppression of Pb2+ leaching with enhanced stability. Further fully switching the electrolyte to 0.5 M H2SO4 at the same pH led to a 30-fold reduction in Pb2+ leaching with high stability. The catalyst displays a high current density of approximately 800 mA cm‒2 for eight cycles of electrolysis, and high Faradaic efficiency (FE) of FA with a slight decrease (from 90.8% to 79.4%). We demonstrated that the PbO2 catalyst can be supplied from the anode plate in lead-acid batteries, mitigating the environmental hazard of lead-acid waste. To understand the promoted catalyst stability in H2SO4 electrolyte, electrochemical evaluation, elemental analysis, and operando Raman spectroscopy were conducted, collectively revealing that SO42− ions play a crucial role in attracting the leached Pb2+ by forming PbSO4 over the electrode surface. By employing a PEM-based MEA as the electrolyzer, FA concentration can be accumulated to a record-high 6.0 M, and a 275-h stable operation was demonstrated at a current density of 1 A cm‒2 with FA FE of 64.3%.

Benefiting from applying acidic electrolyte for GOR, we coupled it with upstream raw cellulose hydrolysis without intermediate separation, achieving a thermo−electro tandem process for biomass-to-chemical valorization. Process modeling (with Aspen Plus V12) indicated that this acidic FA production route offers economic viability, primarily due to reduced separation costs. Eventually, we demonstrated that the strategy can be extended, by showing that introducing iodate ion (IO3−) into the electrolyte can stabilize PbO2, which is because Pb2+ is attracted in the form of lead iodate (Pb(IO3)2). This work demonstrates an efficient strategy for durable electrooxidation under acidic conditions by attracting leached metal species, which differs from previous ones that focus on stabilizing the catalyst’s structure to suppress metal leaching, such as stabilizing the ruthenium (Ru) catalyst by alloying with nickel (Ni) for oxygen evolution reaction (OER) in acidic electrolyte17.

Results

Metal leaching in HClO4 electrolyte

PbO2/Ti catalyst is a dimensionally stable anode, exhibiting activity under acidic conditions and finding applications in organic pollutant degradation and ozone production18–20. Therefore, we selected this component for study and initially synthesized it via an electrodeposition method (see details in “Method” section)21. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis revealed a β-PbO2 phase (Supplementary Fig. 1a), and scanning electron microscope (SEM) images showed micron-sized PbO2 islands firmly adhered to the Ti substrate (Supplementary Figs. 1b, c). The surface of these islands is covered with an extensive, irregular network of cracks (0.3–3.5 µm in width), likely originating from internal stress generated during film growth and drying. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis revealed binding energy peaks for Pb 4f7/2 and Pb 4f5/2 at 137.9 eV and 142.8 eV, respectively, confirming PbO2 composition over the catalyst surface (Supplementary Fig. 1d).

We then conducted GOR over the as-prepared catalyst in 0.5 M HClO4 electrolyte (pH 0.3) in an H-cell separated by a membrane (Nafion-117) by chronopotentiometry. As shown in Fig. 2a (top), the PbO2 catalyst initially required a low anodic potential, approximately 2.2 V vs reversible hydrogen electrode (abbreviated as VRHE), to achieve an ampere-level current density (1 A cm‒2), implying its promising catalytic activity. However, the catalyst showed an obvious potential increase (60 mV h−1) during a 6-h continuous electrolysis. Inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) analysis showed Pb concentration of 33.5 mg L‒1 in the post-reaction electrolyte (Fig. 2b), accounting for 19.2% of the initial Pb loading (Supplementary Table 2). This significant leaching of Pb results in performance degradation and also raises serious environmental issues, greatly hindering its practical application in acidic GOR.

We then investigated FA-producing performance over the PbO2 electrode. Since the preceding continuous electrolysis may give rise to FA accumulation and its further mineralization (to produce CO2), we evaluated cycle stability by periodically refreshing the electrolyte (0.5 M glucose in 0.5 M HClO4). The conversion of glucose (a six-carbon compound) to FA (a one-carbon compound) may involve various possible intermediates along the reaction pathway. This implies that the number of electrons required to generate one molecule of FA is not fixed. Therefore, we calculated an apparent FE for FA in the following studies, unless otherwise specified, based on the assumption of a two-electron transfer process for complete glucose conversion to each FA molecule (see details in “Product analysis” section). As shown in Fig. 2c, the FA FE reached 90.9% in the 1st cycle and slightly decreased from 90.9% to 70.6% after the 8th cycle, showing the high selectivity for FA production.

Unfortunately, in accordance with the foregoing electrolysis under chronopotentiometry (Fig. 2a, top), the catalyst suffered from significantly reduced current density (1st cycle: 911 mA cm‒2, 2nd cycle: 318 mA cm‒2, 8th cycle: 172 mA cm‒2). We assumed that the current density decrease is related to Pb leaching. To demonstrate this point, the electrolyte was immediately sampled after the 1st cycle, which indeed gave a high Pb concentration (71 mg L‒1, Supplementary Fig. 2), in accordance with aforementioned Pb leaching results in continuous electrolysis (Fig. 2b). Notably, if open-circuit potential (OCP) was applied after the 1st cycle for 5 min (that is, without immediate electrolysis for the 2nd cycle), the Pb concentration in the electrolyte rapidly increased to 726 mg L‒1, which is 10-fold higher than that sampled immediately after the 1st cycle (Supplementary Fig. 2). To understand this phenomenon, a 3-h electrolysis was performed at 1 A cm‒2 in 0.5 M HClO4 electrolyte in the absence of glucose (Supplementary Fig. 3). As a result, the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) potential was stable, and Pb concentration in the post-reaction electrolyte was only 4.9 mg L‒1 (corresponding to a total Pb mass of 1.7 mg). Therefore, as depicted in Fig. 2d, we proposed that the prominent Pb leaching in the presence of glucose (at OCP and during electrolysis) is owing to PbO2 reduction to soluble Pb2+ in the electrolyte via chemical reduction by glucose, with specific discussion shown later.

High stability in H2SO4 electrolyte

Intriguingly, we found that adding only a low concentration of Na2SO4 (10 mM) to the above 0.5 M HClO4 electrolyte (corresponding to a SO42‒/HClO4 mole ratio of 2%) significantly enhanced the stability of the PbO2 electrode at otherwise identical reaction conditions (Fig. 2a, middle). The Pb2+ concentration in the post-reaction electrolyte was significantly reduced to 8.6 mg L‒1, representing a 4-fold decrease compared to the value observed in the absence of SO42‒ addition (Fig. 2b). Since the pH change was negligible after introducing 10 mM Na2SO4, we postulate that SO42‒ presence suppressed PbO2 electrode leaching. Simultaneously, adding 10 mM Na2SO4 also significantly enhanced the cycling stability of the catalyst in eight consecutive cycles, although the current density decreased moderately from 809 mA cm‒2 to 688 mA cm‒2 (Supplementary Fig. 4). Notably, upon the removal of SO42‒ in the subsequent 9th and 10th cycles, the current density dropped markedly to 413 mA cm‒2, confirming the positive effect of SO42‒ in enhancing catalyst stability. A comparison of the Pb concentration in the post-reaction electrolytes showed that Pb leaching was suppressed to some extent in the presence of 10 mM Na2SO4, with the leached Pb concentrations mostly falling in the range of 10–40 mg L‒1. The elevated Pb concentration observed in this set of experiments, compared to that after 6-h electrolysis (Fig. 2b), is likely attributable to the different solution volumes used in the cyclic electrolysis tests vs the long-term electrolysis experiment.

To simplify the mechanism study of SO42‒’s role, suppress Pb leaching and reduce HClO4 usage with relatively high cost, the electrolyte was fully replaced with 0.5 M H2SO4, with the same pH 0.3 with that in 0.5 M HClO4. As depicted in Fig. 2a (bottom), the anodic potential was stable during 6-h constant-current electrolysis at a high current density of 1 A cm‒2, exhibiting similar high stability to that observed in HClO4 electrolyte containing Na2SO4. ICP-OES analysis (Fig. 2b) showed that Pb concentration in the post-reaction electrolyte was merely 1.1 mg L‒1 (total lead mass of 0.4 mg). This concentration is significantly lower than the lead levels found in wastewater from the paint, battery manufacturing, and electroplating industries, making it viable to treat to meet standards by current wastewater treatment techniques22. The low Pb concentration represents an 8-fold reduction compared to the 0.5 M HClO4 electrolyte with 10 mM Na2SO4 and a 30-fold reduction relative to the pure 0.5 M HClO4 electrolyte, which suggests a great suppression of PbO2 leaching in the H2SO4 electrolyte. Note that the stability was attained at ampere-level current density (that is, 1 A cm‒2), more practical than previous studies at low current density (at 10 mA cm‒2)14,15. A longer time stability evaluation at 1 A cm‒2 in a two-electrode MEA reactor was shown later. Cycling stability tests further corroborated the promoted stability of PbO2 in H2SO4 electrolyte (Fig. 2e). In the 1st cycle, the PbO2 electrode in H2SO4 electrolyte showed comparable activity (current density: 821 mA cm‒2; FA FE: 90.8%) to that in HClO4 electrolyte. After the 8th cycle, the current density was largely maintained, in evident contrast with the rapid decay in HClO4 electrolyte and moderate decay in 10 mM NaSO4-containing HClO4 electrolyte (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 4). Importantly, the post-reaction electrolysis electrolyte was continuously sampled in the cycling test, constantly showing low Pb concentration (<5 mg L‒1) in H2SO4 electrolyte (Fig. 2e)—presenting more than 14-fold reduction in Pb leaching compared to that in HClO4 electrolyte. In addition, the similar initial anodic potentials (approximately 2.3 VRHE) to reach the same current density (1 A cm‒2) under chronopotentiometry (Fig. 2a) and the similar current density in the 1st cycle (Fig. 2c, e) suggest a similar initial activity in HClO4 and H2SO4 electrolytes without an anionic effect on the performance. Moreover, the FE of FA was largely maintained after 8 cycles (FE: 79.4%), and was stable within a wide potential window due to suppression of OER (Supplementary Fig. 5a), indicating the high selectivity of FA over PbO2 in H2SO4 electrolyte. The slight decrease of the FE for FA (98.9% at 1.6 VRHE, and 86.0% at 2.4 VRHE) was due to weak FA mineralization, as demonstrated by online gas chromatography (GC) analysis (Supplementary Figs. 5b, c) and differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 5d–f and Supplementary Note 1).

Leaching of toxic Pb2+ from PbO2 electrode may result in environmental hazard23, and introducing H2SO4 electrolyte significantly suppresses it. More importantly, we demonstrate that the PbO2 anode plate in lead-acid batteries can be recycled and repurposed as the catalyst (Fig. 2f, left and middle). The PbO2 catalyst supplied from the lead-acid batteries (denoted as Battery PbO2) exhibits excellent cycling stability in H2SO4 electrolyte. After eight cycles, the current density decreased moderately from 511 to 454 mA cm‒2, while the FE of FA declined from 66.7% to 56.2% (Fig. 2g). The Battery PbO2 remained intact after reaction (Fig. 2f, right). In contrast, it fractured in HClO4 electrolyte due to glucose-induced reduction, rendering it entirely unsuitable for GOR (Supplementary Fig. 6). The inferior performance of Battery PbO2 compared to the electrodeposited PbO2 is presumably due to the presence of crystalline PbSO4 in Battery PbO2 that mitigates FA production (as discussed later in the section “Reversible reconstruction between PbSO4 and PbO2” and Supplementary Note 2).

Reversible reconstruction between PbSO4 and PbO2

We then delved into understanding the origin of the high GOR stability of PbO2 in H2SO4 electrolyte. We rationalize that if PbO2 was leached to soluble Pb2+ ion by glucose as proposed above, it is more likely to react with SO42− to form PbSO4 precipitate, considering its low Ksp (1.8 × 10‒8) and insolubility in acid. However, no precipitate was observed at the bottom of the reactor after electrolysis. We then compared the XRD patterns of the catalyst before (sample was denoted as PbO2-prisitine) and after 0.5-h electrolysis at 2.2 VRHE (sample was denoted as PbO2−0.5 h electrolysis), to investigate if any PbSO4 phase was formed. The XRD results reveal the maintenance of a β-PbO2 crystalline phase, with weak diffraction peaks of the PbSO4 phase (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 7). We postulate the following possibilities for the low intensity of the PbSO4 phase. PbSO4 may possess a low crystallinity, or was scarcely generated during electrolysis in a H2SO4 electrolyte, or was indeed generated during electrolysis but was oxidized to PbO2 before off-line XRD measurement.

Preceding observation shows that PbO2 was leached to soluble Pb2+ in the HClO4 electrolyte containing glucose at OCP condition (Supplementary Fig. 2). Therefore, we anticipate that PbSO4 might be formed if a fresh PbO2 electrode was immersed in a H2SO4 electrolyte containing glucose at OCP condition (sample was denoted as PbO2-OCP). The XRD results indeed show the appearance of crystalline PbSO4 phases (Fig. 3a). This result demonstrates our hypothesis that PbO2 was chemically reduced to Pb2+ by glucose, which was then precipitated by SO42− to yield PbSO4. Figure 3b displays SEM images of the samples under three conditions: prior to electrolysis (left; sample: PbO2-pristine), after 0.5-h electrolysis in a 0.5 M H2SO4 with 0.5 M glucose electrolyte (middle; sample: PbO2-0.5 h electrolysis), and after 0.5-h immersion in the same solution at OCP condition (right; sample: PbO2-OCP). Compared to PbO2-pristine, the PbO2-0.5 h electrolysis sample exhibited significant morphology variation. The surface cracks became less distinct, accompanied by increased roughness, in addition to the appearance of more nano-sized particles. This observation is presumably attributed to dissolution and then reformation of PbO2. PbO2-OCP sample displayed even more pronounced changes compared to PbO2-prinstine, with its surface being completely covered by crystalline PbSO4 with 0.5−7 μm in size and smooth surface texture (Supplementary Fig. 7g). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) showed PbO2 phase in PbO2-prinstine and PbO2-0.5 h electrolysis samples, and PbSO4 phase in PbO2-OCP sample (Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9). In particular, the PbO2-0.5 h electrolysis sample shows low-crystalline PbSO4 with very low abundance during HAADF-STEM measurement (Supplementary Fig. 8d), consistent with the foregoing XRD results. XPS measurement further verified the appearance of PbSO4 over PbO2-0.5 h electrolysis surface with a low amount (4.8 at.%; Supplementary Figs. 10a, b) and the maintenance of PbO2 phase in the bulk of the catalyst by etching the catalyst surface with sequential Ar+ ion beam (Supplementary Figs. 10c, d).

Based on these experimental evidences, we rationalize that PbSO4 might be formed via Pb2+ precipitation with SO42− during GOR, and it was immediately oxidized to PbO2 under anodic potential without allowing PbSO4 crystallization to a high degree. To corroborate this possibility, operando Raman spectroscopy measurements were conducted. It should be noted that the measurements were conducted at room temperature because the spectrometer and the homemade operando Raman cell could not be equipped with a thermostatic water bath, while other foregoing and following electrolysis tests were conducted at 50 oC unless noted specifically. Prior to do this, we first identified the characteristic signals from PbOₓ (at 77 cm‒1) over PbO2 in HClO4 electrolyte and those from PbSO4 (at 441, 451, 607, 643, and 977 cm‒1) over pure PbSO4 powder, and also ruled out the interference from SO42‒ and HSO4‒ ions in the H2SO4 electrolyte (Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Fig. 11). Then, operando Raman spectroscopy was performed on the PbO2 electrode in the H2SO4 electrolyte with glucose. As shown in Fig. 3c, under OCP conditions, the weak PbSO4 signals are consistent with our rationale that PbO2 plausibly undergoes chemical reduction by glucose to form Pb2+, with the following precipitation to give PbSO4. As the applied anodic potential increased to 1.7 VRHE, the PbSO4 signals became more pronounced. When the potential was further elevated, the PbSO4 signals gradually diminished, implying oxidation of PbSO4 to PbO2. Compared to PbSO4, the signal from PbO2 was constantly weak, with difficulty in identifying its variation at elevated potential.

Furthermore, the PbO2-0.5 h electrolysis sample (electrolysis over PbO2 in H2SO4 electrolyte containing glucose at 2.2 VRHE for 0.5 h; with a weak XRD pattern of PbSO4 phase depicted in Fig. 3a) was immediately transferred into a HClO4 electrolyte containing glucose for Raman measurements under OCP conditions. As depicted in Fig. 3d, PbSO4 signals were observed (the bottom curve), unambiguously demonstrating the formation of PbSO4 phase over the electrode surface during GOR in H2SO4 electrolyte. When an anodic potential of 2.2 VRHE was applied, the PbSO4 vibration peaks rapidly decreased within 60 s and completely disappeared after 180 s. These observations again demonstrate that the as-formed PbSO4 was electrochemically oxidized to PbO2, which is then chemically reduced to Pb2+ by glucose. Owing to the absence of SO42− in the electrolyte, Pb2+ readily dissolved and leached into the solution. Taken together, the above evidences demonstrate the crucial role of SO42− in attracting the leached Pb2+ back to the electrode in the form of PbSO4.

In this case, the redox of the Pb(IV)/Pb(II) pair might be preserved in a H2SO4 electrolyte by establishing a robust PbO2/PbSO4 redox, rather than an unstable PbO2/Pb2+ redox. To demonstrate this point, cyclic voltammetry (CV) tests were conducted in HClO4 and H2SO4 electrolytes, respectively, without adding glucose to investigate Pb(IV)/Pb(II) redox difference. In a HClO4 electrolyte (Fig. 3e, top), the onset potential of the reduction current for PbO2 appeared at 1.1 VRHE. The polarization curve decreased with increasing scanning cycles, which is because PbO2 was electrochemically reduced to Pb2+ at negative potentials, and the release of Pb2+ into the electrolyte hinders its further oxidation and reduction over the electrode, as evidenced by the significantly decreased polarization current during anodic and cathodic scans, respectively. Thus, the PbO2/Pb2+ redox during CV measurement is irreversible in HClO4 electrolyte, with the reaction shown as: PbO2 + 4H+ + 2e‒ → Pb2+ + 2H2O.

By evident contrast, in the H2SO4 electrolyte, the presence of SO42‒ shifted the onset potential of PbO2 reduction towards a more positive position (1.58 VRHE), and stabilized the reduction peak at 1.35 VRHE during negative scan (Fig. 3e, bottom). These variations are attributed to the transformation of redox from PbO2/Pb2+ to PbO2/PbSO4. On one hand, the low equilibrium concentration of Pb2+ in the solution owing to PbSO4 precipitate formation shifts the onset oxidation potential of PbO2/PbSO4 towards a positive position. On the other hand, the generation of PbSO4 effectively inhibits Pb2+ release into the electrolyte, enabling stable polarization current during anodic and cathodic scans. Thereby, PbO2/PbSO4 redox during CV measurement is reversible in H2SO4 electrolyte, with the reaction shown as: PbO2 + 4H+ + 2e‒ + SO42‒ ⇌ PbSO4 + 2H2O. Furthermore, our study demonstrates that over-sulfation of PbO2 to generate highly-crystalline PbSO4 occurs by such CV measurement, which showed decreased FA FE compared to the preceding low-crystalline PbSO4 generated during GOR (Supplementary Fig. 12 and Supplementary Note 2).

Based on the above experimental evidence, a plausible mechanism for the promoted stability of PbO2 in H2SO4 is proposed (Fig. 3f). During GOR, PbO2 serves as the active phase to catalyze glucose oxidation to FA (see detailed mechanism later), and simultaneously PbO2 is reduced to Pb2+. In the H2SO4 electrolyte, SO42− dynamically attracted the leached Pb2+ in the form of PbSO4 precipitate over the electrode surface, greatly inhibiting Pb leaching. Under high potentials, the formed PbSO4 is oxidized to PbO2 to continuously catalyze glucose oxidation. As a consequence, a dynamic attraction of Pb species was established via reversible reconstruction between PbO2 and PbSO4, greatly preventing Pb2+ leaching and thus enhancing catalyst stability in such a strong acidic electrolyte.

Membrane electrode assembly performance

Subsequently, continuous GOR for FA electrosynthesis at ampere-level current densities was validated in an MEA electrolyzer. After screening Ti substrates with different pore sizes (Supplementary Fig. 13a), 40-mesh Ti mesh was ultimately selected as the substrate for PbO2 electrodeposition to balance mass transport of glucose to the three-dimensional framework and the loading amount of the PbO2 catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 13b). Concurrently, a peristaltic pump was set to its maximum operational limit (89 mL min‒1) to further augment convective mass transfer in the anodic chamber (Supplementary Fig. 13c). On the cathode side, a commercial Pt/C catalyst was directly sprayed onto a PEM (Nafion 117). As illustrated in Fig. 4a, GOR occurs at the anode, and the generated protons migrate through the PEM to the cathode for the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). In addition, the electrolyte was recirculated. The chronopotentiometry test results at different current densities are shown (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 14a). Within a wide range of current density up to ampere level (250–1000 mA cm‒2), the FE of FA was maintained at above 75%. At even higher current density of 1500 mA cm‒2, the FA FE shows a slight decrease to 64%, primarily due to FA mineralization to CO2, as evidenced by significantly increased bubbles in the anolyte effluent.

To increase FA concentration in the electrolyte to reduce energy consumption during product separation (the detailed analysis is shown in the “Techno-economic analysis” section), catalytic performance at higher glucose concentrations was investigated. To achieve near-complete conversion, prolonged electrolysis was carried out in 0.5 M H2SO4 electrolyte with different glucose concentrations at a constant current density of 1 A cm‒2 and with electrolyte recirculation (Supplementary Fig. 14b–f). To our delight, FA concentration increased from 1.3 M (after 3-h electrolysis of 0.5 M glucose, with glucose conversion of 85.9%) to a high concentration of 6.0 M (after 15-h electrolysis of 2.5 M glucose, with glucose conversion of 88.7%) compared to previously reported FA concentrations (below 3 M in Fig. 4c and Supplementary Table 1). In addition, linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements of PbO2 were conducted (Supplementary Fig. 15). The results show that GOR is more preferential than FA electrooxidation, explaining the accumulation of FA to a high concentration at high glucose conversion without severe FA mineralization.

We then evaluated catalyst stability in the MEA electrolyzer at an ampere-level current density (1 A cm‒2). Regarding the electrolyte flow mode, if the electrolyte were recirculated, the accumulated FA would be easily mineralized at high glucose conversion, which decreases the FE for FA. To suppress FA mineralization, the electrolyte was flowed in a single-pass mode. A low flow rate (2.6 mL min‒1) was selected to mitigate high electrolyte consumption. As a result, the reaction can be stably operated at ampere-level current density (1 A cm‒2) for 275 h (Fig. 4d), showing a slight increase in cell voltage (by approximately 0.4 V) and stable FE of FA (from 69.3% to 64.3%). The slightly lower FE compared with that in Fig. 3e is probably owing to the lower flow rate that mitigated glucose mass transfer. It should be noted that the stability was attained at 1 A cm‒2, at a harsher and more practical condition compared with previous results (at 10 mA cm‒2)14,15. Moreover, the pH variation was neglected during electrolysis, without the necessity to continuously replenish alkali (e.g., KOH) if GOR was conducted in alkaline electrolyte (see detailed discussion in Supplementary Note 4).

Reaction mechanism studies

After illustrating the promoted stability, we managed to elucidate the reaction mechanism of GOR over PbO2, considering the limited mechanistic studies in acidic electrolytes compared to those in alkaline ones. We first demonstrated that the reaction follows an electrochemical oxidation pathway via the Langmuir−Hinshelwood (L−H) mechanism (Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17), where glucose and reactive oxygen species co-adsorb on the catalyst surface for reaction. We then identified the nature of such reactive oxygen species, which are proposed to be adsorbed hydroxyl species (OH*) generated by water activation, as evidenced by spin-trapping electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurement employing 5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline-N-oxide (DMPO) and 2-tert-butoxycarbonyl-2-methyl-3,4-dihydro-2H-pyrrole-1-oxide‌ (BMPO) as the probes (Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. 17b, c). Finally, we proposed a sequence C−C cleavage in glucose from C-1 to C-6, where C-1 to C-5 contributed to FA formation while the terminal C-6 carbon was converted to formaldehyde, as evidenced by time-dependent product analysis, 13C-isotopic tracer experiments, and product distribution by using of expansion of polyol (Supplementary Note 6 and Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19).

Direct conversion of cellulose

Benefiting from the high GOR performance in acidic electrolyte, we managed to couple upstream acid hydrolysis of raw biomass and downstream electrolysis in a thermo−electro tandem process. Since both of the processes are conducted under acidic conditions (that is, in H2SO4 solution in the following studies), intermediate separation and purification steps can be removed, making it possible to realize direct raw cellulose conversion to FA.

Microcrystalline cellulose (40−60 μm, Aladdin Reagent Co. Ltd) was first subjected to acid hydrolysis in a hydrothermal autoclave using 0.05 M dilute H2SO4 solution as the catalyst (Fig. 5a). The resulting acidic hydrolysate was concentrated 10-fold via rotary evaporation under 50 °C, followed by decolorization treatment by activated carbon. Without the need for glucose separation, the obtained hydrolysate containing approximately 1 M glucose in 0.5 M H2SO4 solution was compatible with the following electrolysis process, providing optimal glucose concentration, electrolyte pH, and SO42− for acidic GOR.

a Scheme of the raw cellulose-to-FA conversion process under acidic conditions. b Cell voltage, c FE of FA, and d concentration changes of glucose and FA for electrolysis of cellulose hydrolysate over PbO2/Ti mesh at 1 A cm‒2 and 50 °C. Potentials in the associated figures were not iR-compensated (i current, R resistance). The error bars in c, d represent the standard deviation of two independent measurements by using the same kind of catalyst. Source data for (b–d) are provided as a Source Data file.

As shown in Fig. 5b, the electrolysis of the hydrolysate shows a high current density of 1 A cm‒2 at a cell voltage of 3.3 V, suggesting a comparable performance with the aforementioned one using pure glucose as the reactant. The FA FE in the first hour was 69.2% (Fig. 5c), which is slightly lower than that using pure glucose solution, which might be attributed to the electrooxidation of other co-existing components in the electrolyte, such as HMF via glucose dehydration, as evidenced by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 20). After 6 h of electrolysis, glucose conversion reached 64.4% with a FA concentration of 2.5 M, demonstrating the feasibility of FA electrosynthesis readily from raw cellulose feedstock via a thermo−electro tandem process (Fig. 5d).

We admitted that the raw cellulose-to-FA processing requires substantial optimization in future work. First, the co-existing impurities in the electrolyte can be removed by using an energy-efficient method, a way to increase downstream GOR performance. Secondly, the glucose yield via acid hydrolysis of cellulose was relatively low. This is presumably due to the highly ordered crystalline structure of cellulose that comprises glucose units inter-linked by extensive hydrogen bonds, which prevents chemical reagents (such as H2SO4 in our case) from penetration and reaction. Recently developed microwave-assisted and steam explosion methods might be leveraged to enhance cellulose hydrolysis efficiency24,25.

Techno-economic analysis (TEA)

To investigate the economic benefit of GOR under acidic electrolysis conditions, a preliminary TEA was conducted (Supplementary Note 7 and Supplementary Figs. 21 and 22). Specifically, the raw material and process costs of FA production for both alkaline and acidic processes at an industrial scale were compared (Supplementary Tables 3–6). Figure 6a illustrates the costs (in orange color) and revenues (in blue color) associated with raw materials and products per kilogram of FA for alkaline (left) and acidic (right) processes. Owing to the non-recyclable potassium hydroxide (KOH), the alkaline process exhibits a higher raw material cost (3.73 USD kgFA‒1) and a lower revenue (0.02 USD kgFA‒1). By contrast, under acidic conditions, H2SO4 can be recycled through a simple flash evaporation process with a recovery rate of 90%, delivering a much lower raw material cost (0.24 USD kgFA‒1) and higher total revenue (0.90 USD kgFA‒1).

TEA analysis of FA production for acidic and alkaline processes.

a Costs (in orange) and revenue (in blue) per kilogram of FA produced for alkaline (left) and acidic (right) processes. b Process flow diagram for alkaline (upper right) and acidic (bottom right) processes, sharing the same electrolysis section (left). c Total annualized costs (TAC), total capital costs (TCC), and total operation costs (TOC) for alkaline and acidic processes. Detailed composition of d TCC and e TOC. Source data for (a, c) are provided as a Source Data file.

Notably, the primary difference between the acidic and alkaline processes lies in the product separation units (Fig. 6b, right), while the electrolysis units (Fig. 6b, left) were not considered to simplify the comparison. The alkaline separation sequence includes six continuous unit operations (Fig. 6b, upper right), including neutralization, concentration, crystallization, drying, and two-stage distillation of the concentrated liquor. While under acidic conditions (Fig. 6b, bottom right), neutralization of sulfuric acid is unnecessary. Two-stage extractive distillation is performed using sulfolane as the extraction solvent, producing high-purity FA in distillation column 3. The solvent can be subsequently recovered by flash evaporation. Aspen Plus V12 was employed for process simulations, the ELECNTRL model for electrolyte components, and the UNIQUAC model for non-electrolyte components. The FA production rate was set at 160,000 metric tons per year (additional parameters are provided in Supplementary Table 3).

Based on these processing units, the total capital cost (TCC), total operating cost (TOC), and total annualized cost (TAC) for acidic and alkaline processes are shown in Fig. 6c, where TAC equals the sum of TOC and one-third of TCC. Compared to the alkaline process, the acidic one shows a significant reduction in TCC, which is directly attributed to a smaller number of unit operations. Specifically, in the alkaline process, large stream flow is involved in the neutralization, concentration, crystallization, and first distillation steps. In contrast, in the acidic process, over 92% of the water is removed in distillation column 1, allowing for miniaturization of equipment in the acidic process. Figure 6d supports these findings, showing that the capital cost of distillation column 1 dominates the total TCC. Regarding TOC, the acidic process leads to 25.3% saving compared with the alkaline process, primarily because sulfolane is introduced as an extractant under acidic conditions, which enhances the volatility ratio of water/FA and thus facilitates water removal by distillation. It should be noted that even with extractive distillation, the water separation step (corresponding to distillation column 1) still accounts for the majority (82.5%) of TOC (Fig. 6e). Therefore, it is essential to develop an electrolysis unit to yield a high concentration of FA for a reduced TOC. The afore-attained high concentration of glucose (6.0 M) in our study is anticipated to help reduce TOC. Collectively, the acidic process for FA separation offers lower TCC and TOC, leading to higher revenue with strong competition for industrial applications. Besides the rigorous model of the separation processes, we admitted that a full process simulation from raw cellulose to FA is necessary, and its limitations in the present work are provided (Supplementary Note 7), calling for a more comprehensive study in future work.

Generality of the dynamic attraction strategy

The dynamic attraction strategy can be described in a general fashion (Fig. 7a). For metal oxide (MOn; PbO2 in our case) using as the electrocatalyst at anode, it is suffering from reductive leaching to metal ions (Mx+) by organic molecules (glucose in our case) under acidic conditions. Introducing suitable anions (Ay‒) is capable of in-situ attracting Mx+ by forming MyAx precipitates (PbSO4 in our case) on the electrode surface, followed by oxidation to MOn under anodic potential.

Generality of the dynamic attraction strategy.

a Schematic diagram of a dynamic attraction strategy. b Stability of PbO2 electrode at current density of 1 A cm‒2 in glucose-containing 0.5 M HClO4 and 0.1 M NaIO3 at 50 °C. c XRD analysis of the PbO2 before reaction (PbO2-pristine), after 0.5-h electrolysis (PbO2−0.5 h IO3) and after 0.5-h OCP immersion (PbO2-OCP IO3) in glucose-containing 0.5 M HClO4 and 0.1 M NaIO3 electrolyte. d Cycle stability of PbO2 electrode in HClO4. Reaction conditions: 0.5 M HClO4, 0.1 M NaIO3, and 0.5 M glucose at 2.2 VRHE and 50 °C for 0.5 h. Potentials in the associated figures were not iR-compensated (i current, R resistance). Source data for (b–d) are provided as a Source Data file.

Based on this understanding, we managed to demonstrate the generality of the strategy by introducing other anions with the formation of Pb precipitate, which are also stable under acidic conditions. Pb(IO3)2 was considered as the candidate, due to its low Ksp (Ksp = 3.7 × 10‒13) and it is stable under acidic conditions. We conducted potentiostatic electrolysis over a PbO2 electrode in a 0.5 M HClO4 electrolyte containing glucose and 0.1 M NaIO3. The electrolysis was conducted in an H-cell at 1 A cm‒2 and 50 °C. As shown in Fig. 7b, after an initial decrease, the anodic potential was stable during 6-h electrolysis, and the Pb concentration in the post-reaction electrolyte was only 0.98 mg L‒1, preliminarily indicating the efficacy of stabilizing PbO2 catalyst under acidic conditions by adding IO3−. The leached Pb was even lower than that in the H2SO4 electrolyte (Fig. 2b), which is probably because Pb(IO3)2 has a lower Ksp than PbSO4.

After electrolysis, no distinct crystalline phase of Pb(IO3)2 was detected in the catalyst by XRD analysis, whereas highly crystalline Pb(IO3)2 was formed under OCP condition (Fig. 7c), in accordance with the results when H2SO4 electrolyte was applied (Fig. 3a). SEM images show that the catalyst morphology underwent reconstruction after 0.5-h electrolysis, with the surface featuring restructured PbO2 nanoparticles (Supplementary Figs. 23a–c). In contrast, the catalyst under OCP conditions was covered with highly crystalline Pb(IO3)2 sheets (Supplementary Fig. 23d, e). These results imply a similar Pb2+ attraction strategy by forming Pb(IO3)2 precipitate.

Cyclic stability tests were then conducted at a constant potential of 2.2 VRHE in an acidic electrolyte containing 0.5 M HClO4, 0.1 M NaIO3, and 0.5 M glucose. As shown in Fig. 7d, after six cycles, both the FA FE and current density were largely maintained. The concentration of Pb in the electrolyte during cycling tests consistently remained below 0.3 mg L‒1. It should be noted that the suitability of using IO3‒ to stabilize PbO2 during GOR is slightly compromised by the competitive oxidation of periodate ions (IO3‒) to periodic acid (H5IO6, see details in Supplementary Note 8 and Supplementary Fig. 24).

Discussion

Durable electrosynthesis of organic compounds under acidic conditions at high current density presents a critical challenge. A significant contributor to catalyst deactivation is the reductive leaching of active species, a process initiated by the chemical reduction of the catalyst by organic molecules. By attracting leached metal ions with specific anions in the electrolyte, we successfully improved the stability of a PbO2 catalyst in GOR, showing ampere-level current density under acidic conditions with pH 0.3. To demonstrate its environmental friendliness, we confirmed that a PbO2 anode plate from lead-acid batteries can be directly employed as the catalyst for GOR, showing stable performance and minimal Pb2+ ions leaching. The mechanism for promoted stability was elucidated, by showing that the leached Pb2+ is attracted by SO42‒ with the formation of PbSO4 over the electrode, followed by oxidation to PbO2 under anodic potential.

The high stability was validated in an MEA electrolyzer, showing stable voltage and FA FE for 275-h continuous operation at a current density of 1 A cm‒2. Owing to the stable performance and high FE for FA at high glucose conversion, a record-high concentration of FA at 6.0 M was achieved, potentially reducing energy consumption associated with product separation. We then demonstrated raw cellulose conversion to FA by coupling upstream acid hydrolysis of raw biomass and downstream electrolysis. Since both of the processes are conducted in acidic conditions, intermediate separation was avoided. By demonstrating markedly lower material, capital, and separation costs, TEA confirms the enhanced industrial viability and commercial prospects of GOR under acidic conditions compared to alkaline ones. The dynamic attraction strategy was extended by forming Pb(IO3)2 precipitate to stabilize the PbO2 catalyst.

We admit that further studies are needed. Regarding the catalyst stabilization mechanism, it is worth understanding why PbSO4 favors precipitation over the electrode rather than into the bottom of the reactor. Regarding real biomass conversion, it is necessary to optimize the whole process from acid hydrolysis of raw biomass to downstream electrolysis.

This strategy is designed based on the mechanism of metal leaching via the formation of soluble ions during electrolysis. The instability of many catalysts originates from this reason, as exemplified by the transformation of NiOOH to soluble Ni2+(ref. 26), RuO2 to soluble RuO42‒(ref. 17), MnO2 to MnO4‒ (ref. 15), and Co3O4 to Co2+ (ref. 27). Therefore, exploring more specific methods based on this dynamic attraction strategy might be possible, beyond conventional one to inhibit metal leaching by engineering a strong catalyst surface, as in the case of alloying Ru with Ni in acidic OER (ref. 17).

Methods

Chemicals and materials

Except noted, all chemicals were purchased and used without further purification. Deionized water was used throughout the experiments. lead nitrate (Pb(NO3)2, 99%), arabinose (C5H10O5, 99%), erythrose (C4H8O4, 99%), glycolaldehyde (C2H4O2, 99%), FA (HCOOH, 99.5%), formaldehyde (HCHO, 99.5%), sodium fluoride (NaF, 99%), and sodium sulfate (Na2SO4) were purchased from Aladdin Reagent Co. Ltd (Shanghai, China). Glyceraldehyde (C3H6O3, 96%) was purchased from TargetMol, USA. Avicel cellulose (100–200 mesh, 99%), D-Glucose (C6H12O6, 99%), D-Glucose-6-13C (12C513CH12O6, 99%), and D-Glucose-13C6 (13C6H12O6, 99%) were obtained from Macklin Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Sulfuric acid (H2SO4, 98 wt%), nitric acid (HNO3, GR), and perchloric acid (HClO4, 70 wt%) were obtained from Beijing TongGuang Fine Chemicals Company. Platinum/carbon catalyst (SPT40N, Pt 40%, C 60%), proton exchange membrane (Nafion 117: thickness 183 μm) were purchased from Shengernuo Co. Ltd. (Suzhou, China). Sandblasted titanium plate, sandblasted titanium mesh (40-mesh), BDD electrode, and IrTaTi electrode were purchased from Yileka Electrochemical Space Station Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China).

Synthesis of PbO2 electrode

The PbO2 electrode was prepared via an electrochemical deposition method. Commercial sandblasted titanium plates (thickness 0.3 mm) were cut into 12.5 × 10 mm pieces and washed in ethanol by ultrasonics for 5 min. Then, it was immersed in a 20 mL aqueous solution containing 0.5 M Pb(NO3)2, 0.1 M HNO3, and 10 mM NaF with an immersed area of 1 cm2. PbO2 electrode was synthesized by constant-current anodic electrodeposition at 20 mA cm‒2 for 1 h in a polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE) vessel maintained at 65 °C. The PbO2 electrode was allowed to stand in the electrolyte for 15 min following electrodeposition, prior to being dried in a forced-air oven at 60 °C. The electrodeposited PbO2 electrode had an effective geometric area of 1 cm2.

Synthesis of PbO2/Ti mesh electrode

PbO2/Ti mesh was synthesized using similar procedures, except that the titanium plate was changed to a sandblasted titanium mesh (40-mesh).

Characterizations

XRD patterns were measured using a D8 ADVANCE (Bruker, USA) equipped with Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.5406 Å) at 40 kV and 40 mA, with a scan angle of 5°–80° and a scan rate of 10° min-1. HAADF-STEM imaging was carried out in a probe-corrected Thermo Scientific Themis Z microscope operating at 300 kV. XPS was measured on Thermo ESCALAB 250XI with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source. All XPS spectra were calibrated using the C 1 s line at 284.8 eV. EPR measurement was conducted in an EPR spectrometer (FA-200 (JEOL)). The SEM analysis was conducted using a HITACHI SU-8010 microscope. ICP-OES was conducted on ThermolCPOES7200 (ThermoFisher, USA) with axial detector mode. 13C NMR spectra were recorded on a BRUKER AVANCE NEO 500 NMR instrument using the C13CPD pulse sequence.

H2SO4 hydrolysis of raw cellulose

A total of 400 mL of acidic cellulose hydrolysate was prepared by hydrolyzing 4.0 g of microcrystalline cellulose with 40 mL of 0.025 M H2SO4 solution in a 100 mL PTFE-lined autoclave (150 °C, 8 h) over ten repeated batches. The combined hydrolysate was concentrated to ca. 25 mL via rotary evaporation at 60 °C, followed by decolorization with 1 g of activated carbon (stirring at 60 °C for 10 min) and filtration, yielding approximately 20 mL of concentrated hydrolysate.

Online DEMS measurement

In online DEMS measurements with QAS100/PrismaPro DEMS system (PFEIFFER VACCUM and LingLu Instruments (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.), the Ag/AgCl electrode and Pt wire were used as reference electrode and counter electrode, respectively. Due to the inability to stir the DEMS electrochemical cell, a PbO2 electrocatalyst supported on a Ti mesh substrate was employed as the working electrode to enhance glucose mass transport during testing. The online DEMS cell has been shown in our previous work28.

Operando Raman measurement

Operando Raman spectroscopy was conducted on a confocal Raman microscope (LabRAM HR-800, Horiba Jobin Yvon, France) at a 532 nm laser wavelength. Prior to the measurements, the Raman shift was calibrated using the Raman band of Si at 520.7 cm−1. The Operando Raman cell has been shown in our previous work29.

Electrochemical methods

The electrochemical measurements of the samples in an H-cell separated by Nafion 117 membrane were performed with a CS310X electrochemical analyzer (Wuhan Corrtest Instruments Corp., Ltd.) except noted. The three-electrode system consists of Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) as the reference electrode, Pt foil (1 cm2) as the counter electrode, and the as-prepared PbO2/Ti (1 cm2) as the working electrode. All potentials measured against Ag/AgCl (EAg/AgCl) were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (ERHE) scale in this work using ERHE = EAg/AgCl + 0.197 V + 0.059×pH. During the electrochemical tests, the cathode and anode chambers were each filled with 40 mL of electrolyte. In addition, all potential measurements in this work were recorded without the application of iR compensation. The solution resistance was determined by the electrochemical impedance spectroscopy technique at open circuit potential in a frequency range from 105 to 1 Hz with a perturbation of 5 mV. The resistance of the 0.5 M H2SO4 electrolyte in the H-cell reactor was measured as 1.1 ohm (±0.1) on average. All cell parts, including the counter electrode, were washed with ethanol and deionized water after every experiment to remove any contaminants. The 0.5 M H2SO4 electrolyte was prepared by slowly adding concentrated sulfuric acid into deionized water, stored at room temperature (298.15 K) in a dark place, and remained usable for one month. The electrolysis was performed by applying a constant potential ranging from 1.6 to 2.4 V vs. RHE for 0.5 h.

The electrochemical measurements of the samples in an MEA were performed with a CS1350 electrochemical analyzer (Wuhan Corrtest Instruments Corp., Ltd.). The ink for the counter electrode consisted of 4 mg Pt/C commercial catalyst and 20 μL of 5 wt% Nafion solution in 2 mL ethanol, by ultrasonication for 30 min. The ink was sprayed on a Nafion 117 membrane directly. A piece of carbon paper (YSL-30-T) served as the cathode current collector. In the case of electrolyte recirculation, the volume was 20 mL. The resistance of the 0.5 M H2SO4 electrolyte in the MEA reactor was measured as 0.6 ohm (±0.1) on average.

Product analysis

To analyze liquid products, 400 μL of electrolyte solution was collected during the chronoamperometry test. For the reaction products obtained from long-term electrolysis tests, appropriate dilution by distilled water is applied to ensure that the concentration of the products falls within the linear range of the standard curves. The samples were then analyzed by an HPLC (Agilent 1200 Infinity Series) equipped with a variable wavelength detector and a differential refraction detector. The glucose and oxidation products (FA, formaldehyde, arabinose, erythrose, glyceraldehyde, and glycolaldehyde) were detected using an organic acid column (Coregel 87H3) and aqueous H2SO4 (5 mM) as the mobile phase (0.6 mL min–1). Prior to analysis, the electrolyte was suitably diluted to bring analyte concentrations within the calibrated range of the calibration curve, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 25. CO2 and O2 were detected using Online GS (Shimadzu) equipped with a TCD detector. In the detection of polyol oxidation reaction products, due to the overlap of chromatographic peaks between formaldehyde and glycerol in HPLC analysis, the concentration of formaldehyde during electrolysis was quantified via ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption measurement with a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV7600Pro, Shanghai Lengguang Technology) following the Hantzsch reaction30. Ammonium acetate (15.4 g) in water (50 mL), glacial acetic acid (0.3 mL), and acetylacetone (0.2 mL) were mixed to form a solution under stirring, which was further diluted with water (49.5 mL). Subsequently, 20.0 µL of anolyte, 2.0 mL of distilled water, was mixed with the acetylacetone solution (2.0 mL), which was further heated to 60 °C for 10 min. After cooling for 10 min, the absorbance of the sample solution at 413 nm was measured. The quantification of HCHO was obtained from the calibration curves by applying standard solutions with known concentrations of commercially purchased pure HCHO.
