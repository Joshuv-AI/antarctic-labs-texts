Complications and management of bronchobiliary fistula following microwave ablation for hepatic malignancy: a retrospective analysis
Authors: Yinxiang Wu, Lu Li, Chuncao Shi, Xiaoyuan Bu, Liping Gao, Zhiwei Xiao, Kui Wang, Jiquan Chen
Published: 2025

Introduction

Hepatic malignancy is the fifth most common malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, and its incidence and mortality rates are increasing. Treatments for hepatic malignancies mainly include surgical resection and locoregional therapy. Microwave ablation (MWA) is one of the most common thermal ablation techniques and is widely used to treat hepatic malignancies. Thermal ablation has advantages of high safety, low cost, and low invasiveness [1–3]. MWA works by heating local tumor tissue, which leads to coagulation necrosis and ultimately kills tumor cells. However, MWA may also lead to injury to adjacent tissues. Severe complications such as bile duct stenosis, hepatic abscess, biloma, bleeding, colon perforation and gastric perforation have been reported [4, 5]. Bronchobiliary fistula (BBF) is an infrequent but severe post-microwave ablation complication caused by pathological communication between the biliary system and bronchial tree. BBF often presents with biliptysis (bile in the sputum), fever, cough, dyspnea and hemoptysis [6–9].

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the clinical manifestations, diagnoses and treatment of BBF following MWA for hepatic malignancies in our hospital. Our aim was to improve the understanding of BBF by analyzing its clinical characteristics, possible pathogenesis and treatment methods.

Methods

Patients who underwent ultrasound-guided percutaneous MWA for hepatic malignancy at the Shanghai Eastern Hepatobiliary Surgery Hospital between November 2015And September 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. Patients diagnosed with BBF following MWA were included in this study.

All microwave ablations were performed percutaneously under ultrasound guidance using the MTC-3 C microwave ablation system (Weijing Jiuzhou Medical Devices Nanjing China). The microwave ablation system is capable of producing 5 to 120 W of power at 2450 MHz which can drive up to two antennae simultaneously. After standard skin preparation and sterile drapingLocal Anesthesia was administered, and the ablation electrode was inserted into the liver tumor under real-time ultrasound guidance. A single- or dual-needle ablation strategy with single- or multi-point energy delivery was employed based on tumor size and Location, with power And duration adjusted dynamically according to tumor dimensions And anatomical position. Continuous ultrasound monitoring enabled real-time adjustments using fusion imaging, and the procedure was terminated upon complete coverage of the target tumor by a transient hyperechoic zone, indicating successful coagulation necrosis. All microwave ablation procedures were performed by one of four interventional physician, each with over 10 years of experience in ultrasound-guided percutaneous ablation. No hydrodissection or artificial ascites was utilized in this cohort, as these techniques were not routinely adopted during the study period in our hospital.

Data on the location and size of the ablated lesion, ablation duration and power, clinical presentation, diagnostic methods, treatment approaches, and prognosis were collected and analyzed.

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Third Affiliated Hospital of Naval Medical University (EHBHKY2024-K001-P001). Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients.

Characteristics of BBF

Biliptysis is a specific symptom observed in all patients, with fever being the second most common symptom (7 patients, 77.8%). Additional manifestations of biliary-bronchial fistula (BBF) included shortness of breath, chest discomfort, and dyspnea (Table 1). Only 2 patients (22.2%) underwent bilirubin testing for pleural fluidAnd 1 patient(11.1%) for sputum all of which returned positive results. Imaging (CT or MRI) revealed pneumoniaAnd different amounts of pleural effusion in all patients. Other imaging features also revealed hepatic abscesses in 3 patients(33.3%) bilomas in 2 patients(22.2%)And subphrenic fluid collections in 2 patients(22.2%). However, a fistula connecting the biliary lesion to the lung was demonstrated on MRI in only one patient (Fig. 1). A fistulous tract linking the biliary system and bronchial tree was detected by percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography (PTC) in 2 out of 4 patients (50%) and by endoscopic retrograde cholangiography (ERC) in 1 out of 5 patients (20%) (Fig. 2).

Therapeutic options and outcome

Less-invasive procedures were performed in all nine patients. Percutaneous drainage was performed in all the patients including pleural drainage (5/9 55.6%) subphrenic drainage (2/922.2%) biloma drainage (2/9 22.2%) and hepatic abscess drainage (3/9 33.3%). For the treatment of bronchobiliary fistula (BBF) 5 patients (55.6%) underwent percutaneous transhepatic cholangial drainage (PTCD) as the primary approach. Additionally 5 patients (55.6%) underwent endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP). Among them 5 patients received endoscopic nasobiliary drainage (ENBD)And 2 patients underwent endoscopic retrograde biliary drainage (ERBD). One patient received both ENBDAnd PTCD. Moreover, 2 patients (22.2%) were treated with bronchoscopic embolization. Among patients treated with PTCD (5/9) 3 achieved symptom resolution while 2/5 patients receiving ENBD/ERBD had complete fistula closure. Bronchoscopic embolization (2/2) failed in both cases. Surgery was performed in only 1 patient (11.1%) who underwentLocal hepatectomy, fistula closure, diaphragm repair And T-tube drainage after failing multiple minimally invasive treatments, including bronchoscopic embolization, percutaneous drainage And ENBD. He eventually recovered after the surgery. Two other patients achieved a complete cure, as evidenced by the complete disappearance of symptoms including cough and biliptysis, and they remain alive to date. Three patients witnessed an improvement in their symptoms, with a notable reduction in manifestations such as cough and biliptysis. Regrettably, they later succumbed to liver cancer. Unfortunately, one patient underwent a series of interventions including pleural drainage, biloma drainage, ENBD, ERBD, hepatic abscess drainage, and anti-infective therapy. Despite these efforts, the patient died two months after the onset of biliptysis due to uncontrolled infection and gastrointestinal bleeding. Two additional patients still endured recurrent bouts of biliptysis post-treatment. One received treatments including pleural drainage, ENBD, and ERBD but died 11 months after the onset of biliptysis. The other underwent PTCDAnd biloma drainage, succumbing 8 months after the initial manifestation of biliptysis. (Table 1).

Discussion

Thermal ablation has been widely used for hepatic malignancy because of its advantages of being effective, minimally invasive, easy repetition and preservation of the liver parenchyma. Thermal ablation is a first-line option for liver tumors, especially in patients who are unsuitable for surgery [4, 6, 10]. Radiofrequency ablation (RFA) and microwave ablation (MWA) are the most commonly used thermal ablation techniques. MWA works by heating local tumor tissues, causing coagulation necrosis that ultimately kills tumor cells. Compared to RFA, MWA provides numerous benefits, such as an expanded cellular necrosis area, reduced procedure length, and elevated temperatures reaching the targeted lesion. Nevertheless, a broader necrotic region also increases the likelihood of harm to nearby non-target organs, potentially resulting in complications. Bile duct injuries, biliary stricture, hepatic abscess, biloma, bleeding, colon perforation, gastric perforation and pleural effusions are the major complications of MWA [4–6, 10–15].

Bronchobiliary fistula is an infrequent but serious complication of MWA with a high mortality rate and significant impact on the patient’s quality of life. It is characterized by abnormal communication between the biliary tract and bronchial tree through the diaphragm [4, 6, 16]. Biliptysis (the presence of bile in sputum) is a cardinal and specific symptom of BBF with a bitter taste. Other symptoms include cough, fever, dyspnea, hemoptysis, chest pain, and abdominal pain [6, 17–19]. In this study all the patients presented with biliptysisAnd cough, And 77.8% experienced fever.

The pathophysiological mechanisms underlying BBF formation following MWA remain unclear. It involves multifactorial mechanisms. Potential etiologies for BBF may include thermal injury to the diaphragm, local infection with subsequent abscess formation following ablation, biliary stricture, or biloma formation. First, direct thermal injury to the diaphragm during ablation of dome lesions (segments VII/VIII) is a primary trigger [7, 10, 19, 20]. In this study, tumor lesions in all patients were located near the diaphragm, at either VII or VIII. Another common hypothesis is that increased pressure in the biliary system leads to BBF formation. MWA may cause bile duct injury, which can result from direct mechanical trauma or thermal injury during needle placement. Such injury can precipitate a range of complications, including, but not limited to, stricture in the bile ducts, biloma, bile leakage, or biliary fistula. Increased pressure in the biliary system or within the biloma and abscess can trigger an inflammatory response in the subphrenic space, potentially leading to a breach into the chest and bronchial system after local invasive processes that penetrate the diaphragm [5, 10, 16, 21–24]. Furthermore, inflammatory adhesions between the bile system, diaphragm, and lungs can contribute to the development of BBF [22, 23, 25]. In this study, 66.7% of patients had hepatic abscesses, bilomas, or subphrenic fluid collection.

The development of BBF following MWA of hepatic dome lesions highlights the urgent need for prophylactic strategies to prevent thermal injury to adjacent structures. Hepatic tumors near the diaphragm pose unique challenges during ablation. Three key techniques—hydrodissection, artificial ascites, and laparoscopic-assisted ablation—have demonstrated efficacy in addressing these issues and minimizing complications at such high-risk locations. Hydrodissection and artificial ascites involve injecting sterile fluids (e.g., 5% dextrose or saline) to create thermal buffers between the liver capsule and diaphragm, dilate the extrahepatic space, establish protective barriers between the ablation site and adjacent vital structures, enhance imaging clarity for subdiaphragmatic tumors, and mitigate microwave-induced thermal damage, thereby lowering inadvertent injury to surrounding organs and postoperative complications [26–30]. Recent research supports their efficacy in treating dome lesions.Song et al. (2023) demonstrated comparable complete ablation and complication rates between tumors in difficult locations (e.g., adjacent to the gastrointestinal tract, diaphragm, or subcapsular regions) and those in conventional sites, validating hydrodissection as a reliable method to protect the diaphragm and preserve its integrity [28]. Kitchin et al. (2014) further confirmed artificial ascites’ safety and effectiveness: intraperitoneal instillation of 5% dextrose reduced diaphragmatic injuries during percutaneous hepatic ablation, improved visualization of dome lesions, and alleviated post-procedural body wall pain. Their data showed fewer complications in the intervention group (4.0% minor, 0% major) versus controls (8.1% minor, 2.7% major), underscoring its clinical utility [29]. Laparoscopic microwave ablation technology have offered an excellent alternative treatment for HCC patients with well-compensated cirrhosis. This technique is safe and effective, featuring low rates of complication and locoregional recurrence. While percutaneous ablation is effective and widely used, Laparoscopic microwave ablation should be readily available for many HCC cases that are not suitable for percutaneous ablation due to their location near the liver dome, capsule, or gastrointestinal tract. Laparoscopic microwave ablation boasts several advantages: it allows direct visualization of the peritoneal cavity, enables enhanced staging via intraoperative ultrasound, facilitates liver mobilization, and permits hemostasis when necessary. Compared with conventional B-ultrasound-guided microwave ablation, it provides a clear surgical field, allowing safe operations under direct laparoscopic visualization. Moreover, peri-hepatic tissues and organs can be moved intraoperatively to reduce collateral damage to surrounding tissues. The exploratory field of vision is clear and extensive, unrestricted by the size of abdominal incisions or current imaging technologies. For liver cancers located at the hepatic dome near the diaphragm, conventional B-ultrasound-guided procedures risk diaphragmatic injury. In contrast, laparoscopic surgery allows for convenient observation and positioning. Meanwhile, when necessary, artificial ascites can be established using saline to create a water barrier that isolates the liver from the diaphragm, while also absorbing some of the heat, thereby avoiding diaphragmatic injury [31–36]. In this study, no patient underwent hydrodissection or received artificially ascites.

The diagnosis of BBF relies primarily on biliptysis and imaging. Biliptysis refers to the expectoration of sputum containing bile. Biochemical examination of sputum or pleural effusion may show positive bilirubin levels, indicating communication between the bronchial tree and biliary system. This examination is simple, convenient, non-invasive and has a high rate of positive results in BBF. However, the submission rate for bilirubin testing in the sputum or pleural fluid is low. In this study, only two patients underwent bilirubin testing in the pleural fluid and one patient underwent sputum bilirubin testing, all of which were positive. Bronchoscopy can also reveal bile-like secretions in the airways that test positive for bilirubin [6, 23, 27]. The fistula tract was observed less frequently on CT or MRI scans. In this study, MRI revealed a fistula in only one patient. Imaging can also reveal indirect signs, such as hepatic abscess, biloma, pleural effusion, subphrenic fluid collection, pneumonia, atelectasis, and intrahepatic bile duct dilation—these features are consistent with the typical post-ablation complication imaging findings summarized in previous studies [6, 21, 25, 37]. Invasive inspections such as PTC or ERC can directly visualize contrast agents entering the pleural cavity or bronchial tree from the biliary system [6, 21, 25]. In this study only 50% of PTC (2/4)And 16.7% of ERC (1/6) showed the fistula tract.

BBF seriously impairs respiratory function and degrades the quality of life, necessitating prompt and effective treatment. Currently, there are no established guidelines for the treatment of BBF. Most patients receive systemic supportive care and antibiotics therapies. Minimally invasive treatments and surgical interventions are the primary therapeutic approaches for managing BBF. Minimally invasive procedures are increasingly used to treat BBF. These treatments, such as percutaneous drainage or endoscopic procedures, can relieve biliary trace pressure and suppress bile reflux from the biliary system into the bronchial tree. They can effectively alleviate BBF symptoms and promote fistula healing in most patients. Percutaneous drainage includes percutaneous transhepatic cholangiography drainage (PTCD), hepatic abscess drainage, biloma drainage, subphrenic effusion drainage, and pleural effusion drainage. Endoscopic interventions, such as endoscopic nasogastric bile drainage (ENBD) and endoscopic retrograde biliary drainage (ERBD) are effective treatments for BBF by allowing bile flow preferentially into the duodenum [6, 19, 23, 24, 27, 38]. In this study all patients received biliary drainage measures such as PTCD or ENBD. 5 of them received PTCDAnd 5 patients underwent ENBD or ERBD. Notably, while endobronchial embolization has been reported as a successful treatment for BBF [39], the two patients in this study showed poor therapeutic response to this intervention. Most patients can be successfully treated with drainage and endoscopic interventions. However, surgical management should be considered after minimally invasive therapies like biliary drainage have failed [8, 24]. Surgical approaches often include local hepatectomy, resection and fistula repair. Some patients may require pulmonary lobectomy [6, 27]. In this study, 8 patients were treated with minimally invasive approaches, and only one required surgical intervention following the failure of therapies such as percutaneous drainage, ENBD, and bronchial embolization.

For tumors adjacent to the diaphragm future treatment protocols should incorporate the following procedures to minimize the risk of thermal injuryAnd associated complications. First, Anatomical isolation via hydrodissection or artificial ascites should be implemented before ablation to create a thermal buffer, physically separating the tumor from the diaphragm And minimizing direct thermal injury. Second, routine imaging surveillance with MRI or CT within 1 week post-ablation is essential to detect early signs of subphrenic fluid collections, bilomas, hepatic abscesses, or intrahepatic bile duct dilation, which may indicate evolving complications. Third, timely drainage such as PTCD or ENBD should be initiated for identified effusions to relieve biliary pressure and prevent progression to BBF. This systematic approach integrating physical barrier formation, imaging surveillance, and drainage effectively disrupts the pathomechanism of thermal injury-induced fistula formation. By establishing a standardized workflow from prophylactic isolation to early complication detection and prompt intervention, it ensures safety in minimally invasive treatment of dome-region liver tumors, potentially reducing the incidence of severe complications like BBF and improving long-term patient outcomes.

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective, single-center design. The small sample size of only 9 BBF cases limits the statistical power and generalizability of our findings. As data were collected from medical records, potential information bias could not be eliminated. A portion of surgical records documented without precise segmental identification (e.g., segment VII/VIII for dome lesions) precluded the accurate extraction of BBF incidence exclusively for dome lesions. Additionally, variations in ablation techniques (e.g., power and time settings) among operators may have influenced outcomes. The relatively short follow-up period for some patients and confounding by underlying liver cancer progression further complicate the interpretation of treatment efficacy. Future multicenter prospective studies with standardized protocols are needed to validate these results and optimize BBF management strategies.

Conclusions

BBF is a rare but serious complication of MWA for hepatic malignancy, marked by biliptysis as a key symptom. Sputum bilirubin testing offers a simple diagnostic tool. Minimally invasive treatments like PTCD and ENBD are effective, while surgery may be needed for refractory cases. Early intervention improves outcomes.
