A double-blind, controlled trial of circadian effective light therapy in patients with Parkinson's disease
Authors: Bradley T. Wyman, Dan Adams, Suzanne Hendrix, Jeffrey Groves, Wayne Croft, Noel Ellison, C. Warren Olanow, Karl Kieburtz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite current medical therapy for Parkinson's Disease (PD), many experience persistent motor symptoms and significant unmanaged non-motor issues. Previous studies suggest that light therapy (LT) provides benefits for motor and non-motor features of PD. This study evaluated Circadian Effective LT (CELT) with a spectral band between 460 and 545 ​nm for the motor and non-motor features of PD. We conducted a multi-center, randomized, double-blind, controlled clinical trial of CELT in people with Parkinson's disease on standard-of-care therapy. Ninety-two participants (45 active, 47 control) were randomized 1:1 to active or control CELT for 1 ​h each evening for 6 months. Patients were evaluated in their ‘ON state’. The mean (SE) change on the primary endpoint of the MDS-UPDRS parts 1–3 was −17.7(2.8) active vs −9.7(3.5) control, for a LSM difference of −8.0 (4.4) (p ​= ​0.074, 95% Confidence Interval: −16.7, 0.8), reflecting a trend toward improvement over control. Key secondary endpoints included PDQ-39 and CGI which favored active treatment (−5.7 ​± ​2.7; p ​= ​0.038, and −0.4 ​± ​0.2; p ​= ​0.066 respectively), while PDSS-2 Disturbed Sleep showed no difference between groups. Other secondary endpoints that supported the overall treatment effect of CELT included ESS (−1.52 ​± ​0.78; p ​= ​0.054) and CGI-E (−0.3 ​± ​0.2; p ​= ​0.088). Daily LT, with a circadian effective targeted spectrum of light, was well tolerated by PD patients, had no serious adverse effects and showed improvement in both motor and non-motor MDS-UPDRS and other scores. Larger double-blind studies are warranted to further assess the effectiveness of CELT in PD.Clinicaltrials.gov registrationNCT02175472.

Introduction

The human circadian rhythm regulates numerous physiological functions, including the diurnal cycle of melatonin and steroid hormone release that is important in the control of sleep and wakefulness. Dysregulation of circadian rhythm is ubiquitous in Parkinson disease (PD) [1], even in early stages [2], and is associated with impairments in sleep and daytime wakefulness. Bright light exposure is the most powerful regulator of circadian rhythm [3,4].

Intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) express melanopsin, a photopigment sensitive to short and medium wavelengths that controls melatonin secretion [5,6]. The ipRGCs are not directly involved in conscious vision, however, they receive input from the rods and cones and are the principal conduits for circadian regulation [[7], [8], [9]]. ipRGC function is impaired in PD [6,10,11]. This impairment may be driven by PD-associated retinal pathology [12,13] and perhaps by general aging factors [14]. Augmented routine light exposure with light therapy (LT) has been studied as a PD treatment and reported to be beneficial, particularly for non-motor symptoms such as sleep, fatigue, and depression that are troublesome and lack definitive treatment in PD [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. This study was designed to assess whether exposure to light therapy tuned to target a circadian response, could benefit both motor and non-motor features in PD patients receiving standard anti-parkinsonian therapies. Based on these considerations, we evaluated an LT regimen of 950 lux, narrow-band short and medium wavelength light in a 6-month, prospective, randomized, double-blind, multi-center, controlled study. We measured both motor and non-motor symptoms during the ‘ON state’ to determine if light therapy could demonstrate clinical improvements on top of the current best therapy at its peak effectiveness.

Materials and methods

Design

The study was a 6-month, prospective, multicenter, double-blind, randomized, controlled trial designed to test the safety and efficacy of CELT in patients with PD. Clinical sites were located in Massachusetts, Utah, and the Netherlands.

Randomizing, masking, and study procedures

Light was delivered with a table-top device that consisted of a light panel with a simple on/off switch. Subjects were trained in the use of the devices prior to beginning treatment.

Eligible patients were randomized 1:1 to receive treatment at home for 60 ​min daily at approximately 7 p.m. with either the ‘active’ LT (950 lux, λ ​= ​460–545 ​nm) or a ‘control’ white light with an irradiance and bandwidth not thought to provide clinical benefit (100 lux, λ ​= ​415–780 ​nm). The ‘active’ light emission was generated by LED arrays that emitted two narrow bandwidths with peaks at λ ​= ​470 ​nm (blue or short wavelength) and λ ​= ​530 ​nm (green or medium wavelength). These blended to produce a field of light of specific irradiance corresponding to peak spectral sensitivity for circadian regulation via melanopsin-mediated photoreception [8]. The ‘control’ device was identical in appearance except the blue and green LEDs in the light panel were replaced with polychromatic (white) LEDs. Both lights appeared white and were visible against the background of the ambient indoor light. The devices produce about 1/10th the illuminance of traditional 10,000 lux bright light used therapeutically, though it should be noted that some controlled bright light studies used an illuminance as low as 7500 lux [15] and uncontrolled studies had illuminance as low as 1500 lux [23]. The site principal investigators, clinical evaluators and study participants were masked to the treatment allocation. Light qualities were not identical in appearance, so care was taken to ensure that participants, study raters and central staff were not exposed to descriptions of device characteristics or both devices side by side to maintain blinding to group allocation. Participants were asked to refrain from discussing their intervention with the study staff or each other to help maintain blinding.

Based on the results from a large observational study [24], CELT was delivered each evening in an effort to putatively slow the circadian rhythm cycle that is thought to be advanced by dopaminergic medications [2,25,26]. A post-hoc analysis was performed to assess the impact of seasonal changes in sunlight due to the various site locations (see below). Compliance with CELT was tracked by a built-in electronic data logger that recorded the dates and times the device was used.

Patients were exposed to CELT or control light therapy for six months and evaluated at Baseline, 1, 3, and 6 months, and again after a 2-month washout. The assessments and endpoints are listed in Table 1 and results for baseline and 6 months are reported. Motor assessments, including Part 3 of the MDS-UPDRS were completed in the morning in the ‘ON state’, within 1 ​h of receiving a dose of PD medications. Testing in the ‘ON state’ provided the best evaluation of CELT as an adjunctive therapy on top of best standard of therapeutic treatment at peak effectiveness. The time since the last dose was recorded for each exam. Safety eye examinations were performed at baseline and at the conclusion of the study. Participants

People with a diagnosis of PD who were stage II-III on the Hoehn-Yahr (HY) scale when in the ‘ON state’ and who signed an IRB-approved informed consent were eligible to be enrolled into the study. Participants were required to be on stable doses of anti-parkinsonian medications for at least 6 weeks prior to entry, with no anticipated changes during the course of the study. Participants were also expected to maintain their baseline level of concomitant medication, supplements, exercise, caffeine intake and sleep habits, to the extent possible, during the study. Participants were excluded if they had major motor complications such as severe dyskinesias and unpredictable OFF periods sufficient to interfere with study procedures. Exclusion criteria also included clinically significant medical or psychiatric disturbances in the judgment of the investigator, a history of alcohol or drug abuse, and cognitive impairment (Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA) score <24). Subjects with complicated or unstable PD, previous experience with light therapy, night shift work schedules, anticipated multi-time zone travel, use of photosensitizing drugs, and physical eye trauma or disease were also excluded.

Statistical analyses

All primary and secondary endpoints were prespecified in the Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) prior to analysis and unblinding of the study data. The safety population included all randomized patients who received at least one treatment. The modified intention-to-treat population (mITT) was the primary efficacy population and included all patients who were randomized, received treatment, and had any post-baseline efficacy data. Participants were followed in the study and encouraged to participate for the complete duration whether or not they had protocol violations or medication changes (which were closely monitored and recorded).

The efficacy endpoints, shown in Table 1, were calculated as change between baseline and six-months, and the active treatment group was compared to the control group. While all data were analyzed at all timepoints, all of the SAP specified endpoints were at 6 months. The primary and secondary endpoints were analyzed using a mixed model repeated measures (MMRM) analysis. Site, treatment group, visit, and the visit-by-treatment interaction were included in the model, with the baseline value for each variable as a covariate. The CGI-I and CGI-E outcomes were analyzed using the baseline CGI-S score as a baseline covariate in these models. An ANCOVA analysis using observed cases was conducted on each analysis set as a sensitivity analysis for the primary endpoint. A post-hoc statistical analysis was done to correct for potential seasonal influences.

To control the overall type 1 error rate, the primary and secondary endpoints were specified hierarchically. The significance of each endpoint was evaluated in succession as long as they continued to show significance. P-values were provided for descriptive purposes whether or not they met significance based on the hierarchy.

Safety evaluations were analyzed descriptively and included adverse events and the results of the final eye examination.

Funding was provided by PhotoPharmics, Inc, and the study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT02175472).

Results

A total of 92 participants were recruited between 2014 and 2016 and enrolled into the study (active n ​= ​45, control n ​= ​47) at three sites. Demographics and baseline summary statistics for the primary and key secondary endpoints are presented in Table 2 and baseline values for other secondary endpoints are presented in Table 3. In the active group there was a higher baseline age (70.2 years vs. 65.9 years, p ​= ​0.009) and a somewhat higher percentage of men (73.3% vs. 63.8%, N.S.). There were no other notable baseline differences between the 2 groups. All participants but one were receiving levodopa. A CONSORT flow diagram of patient disposition is provided in Fig. 2. One participant in the active group did not have post-randomization data, and 83 (90%) of patients completed the trial. There were 7 discontinuations in the active treatment group: 1 due to adverse events (self-reported impaired vision in the left eye but with a normal eye examination, and lightheadedness) and 6 due to withdrawal of consent (all were due to concerns about the daily study time commitment). There were 2 discontinuations in the control group: 1 due to an adverse event (vertigo) and 1 due to lack of efficacy. All study discontinuations were at the discretion of the participant. Eight device deficiencies were reported (7 active and 1 control). Discussion

The primary endpoint of this study, the sum of MDS-UPDRS parts 1–3 (Table 5), showed a large, clinically meaningful difference [27,28] that trended in favor of the active device but was not statistically significant (LSM ​= ​−8.0, p ​= ​0.074). The contribution to the MDS-UPDRS results of the individual part scores and component scores are shown in Supplement Table S7 and Supplement Table S8. Part of this improvement was driven by Part 1 (Non-Motor Experiences of Daily Living) with an effect size of −2.5 points, where six of the thirteen questions favored active over control with a p ​< ​0.1 and no questions favored control. In Part 2 (Motor Experiences of Daily Living), three of the thirteen questions favored active over control, while no questions favored control, also suggesting a broad impact on motor features. The MDS-UPDRS was assessed in the ‘ON state’ to better demonstrate the effect of STL for improving Parkinson's symptoms beyond current best medical care, at peak effectiveness, which, from a study design perspective, increased the difficulty of showing significance.

In the analysis we used age as a covariate to test whether the differences in age between active and control (70.2 vs. 65.9 years, p ​= ​0.009) was a confounder but the treatment interaction was not significant.

The primary endpoint for this study was originally planned as the change from baseline to the 6 month visit in the sum of MDS-UPDRS parts 2 ​+ ​3, with a resultant focus only on motor features. However, prior to locking the database and unblinding the study, and without any knowledge of the results, the SAP was modified, and the primary endpoint changed to be the sum of MDS-UPDRS parts 1 through 3. This change in primary endpoint was based on the observation that the inclusion of the newly revised MDS-UPDRS part 1 provided a more representative assessment of non-motor features of PD allowing CELT's effectiveness to be evaluated across all elements of PD. Results of the MDS-UPDRS parts 2 ​+ ​3 can be found in Supplement Table S7. All data was audited by the FDA which awarded Breakthrough Device designation for this specific bandwidth light therapy.

In addition to the primary endpoint, four of the twelve key secondary and secondary endpoints, CGI-I, PDQ-39 Total, ESS, and CGI-E trended toward a positive treatment effect (p ​< ​0.1). This suggests that the impact of CELT on non-motor features is broader than just improvement in sleep. The PDQ-39 showed a difference of −5.7 points, consistent with a clinically meaningful effect, as Peto et al. [29] found the meaningful clinically important difference (MCID) to be −1.6 points. Part 2 of the MDS-UPDRS and the PDQ-39 are both patient-reported outcomes, and the favorable results suggest that the benefits of CELT were meaningful to participants.

An ad-hoc meta-analysis estimate and its 95% confidence interval integrated the totality of evidence regarding a possible treatment effect using a linear mixed effects meta-analytic approach combining outcomes. This was calculated by combining the primary, key secondary and secondary endpoints (Table 5). A second meta-analysis was also calculated that included these endpoints plus the exploratory efficacy endpoints (Supplement Table S7). Both meta-analysis estimates achieved statistical significance (p ​= ​0.0218, and p ​= ​0.0041 respectively) favoring a treatment effect. This preponderance of evidence for a treatment effect is visually evident in each of the graphical summaries of each of the endpoints (Table 5, Supplement Table S7, Supplement Table S8).

While these observed improvements are consistent with those seen in other LT studies in PD [[15], [16], [17], [18],20,23,24,30,31], this is the first long-term controlled study to signal broad improvement across many Parkinson's symptoms. These other studies all used different types of LT which had different spectra than the CELT used in this study. Of particular note, Paus's [15] two week study with 36 subjects demonstrated improvement across multiple endpoints but no significance was reported compared to the control group. In a 3-month study with 83 participants, Rutten [16] found subjective improvements in sleep. In a two-week, double blind trial with 31 subjects, Videnovic et al. [17] found improvement with daytime sleepiness (ESS) and subjective sleep. Although our study did not specifically recruit patients with daytime sleepiness and fatigue, we also showed a trend toward improvement on the ESS (−1.5, p ​= ​0.054). The development and use of CELT drew on prior work to select the timing of light administration and specific wavelengths of light designed to more efficiently target the circadian signaling system including the melanopsin containing retinal ganglion cells responsible for non-visual and circadian signaling [23,24].

The mechanism whereby phototherapy might provide benefit for PD patients is not known. Circadian rhythms are clearly disrupted in PD, likely from an early stage [1,2,32,33], and disruption is associated with sleep disturbance and impaired motor function. Measurable abnormalities in serum melatonin are a hallmark of this circadian dysregulation [1,26]. The impairment of melanopsin-mediated signaling associated with alpha-synuclein aggregation in the inner retina [6,[34], [35], [36]], the presence of Lewy pathology in the suprachiasmatic nucleus, or both may render PD individuals less sensitive to the normal light signaling that is important in the regulation of circadian rhythms [6,37]. LT of the appropriate wavelength and intensity may be able to ameliorate these changes in PD (disease specific, or age-related) and help to normalize the circadian rhythm. Thus, starting with the hypothesis that light can be used to modulate the circadian rhythm we designed a therapy light that would likely be circadian effective. Improved regulation of circadian rhythm associated with light therapy could account for improvement in sleep and mood and may also have a direct or indirect impact on both non-motor and motor PD symptoms. Further study would be required to determine whether our observed effect on motor and non-motor features is associated with plasma melatonin concentration [10], some characteristic of its regulation across the diurnal cycle, or another mechanism altogether.

Our decision to use evening light was supported by a large retrospective analysis of 129 PD subjects which showed symptomatic improvement using evening light for up to 8 years [24]. Endo et al. [18] used 1 ​h of bright evening light in 16 subjects for approximately 3 months and reported improved subjective measures of sleep. We did not see any consistent evidence of difficulty falling asleep, despite the evening application of CELT.

As seen with other circadian related studies such as seasonal affective disorder studies, light therapy typically improves circadian related symptoms within a few weeks with little further improvement [38]. However, as seen in Fig. 2, an increasing effect was seen in this study over 6 months, which may be evidence that CELT involves additional mechanisms of action [3,39].

It is noteworthy that there was also a sizable benefit in MDS-UPDRS 1–3 scores in both the active and the control group as shown in Fig. 3 through Month 3 and then continued improvement only in the active group through Month 6. While this initial improvement in the control group likely represents a placebo effect, it is possible that the intensity of the control white light, which was brighter than ambient indoor light, provided some anti-parkinsonian benefit and that the magnitude of benefit of CELT as used in the present study was masked due to a potentially active control. Rutten et al. [16] used a similar white light as a control in their study for depression in PD, showing approximately 50% improvement from baseline in both active and control groups on the Beck Depression Scale (primary endpoint) with no significant between groups difference. This suggests a potentially confounding treatment effect from their control device.

This study has important limitations. The design of the light source was built with the constraints of using off the shelf-LED components with fixed wavelengths and bandwidths. While determining the optimal stimulus spectrum would be prohibitively challenging, the design was intended to impact the circadian system. Though the mechanism of action may involve circadian regulation, there was no direct measure of circadian function, nor were participants selected for having evidence of circadian dysregulation. The study design, comprising the selection of the stimulus spectra, spectra intensity, time of day, duration and frequency of CELT administration was informed based on the available, albeit limited and sometimes contradictory, information available at the time. However, testing alternative combinations was beyond the scope of this study.

The strength of this study was that it was a randomized controlled, double-blind study with prespecified endpoints using an active control. It adds to the growing evidence of the value of LT in PD and adds further support to the previously published studies. With 92 randomized subjects, followed for 6 months, it had a relatively large number of subjects compared to other PD LT studies which generally used fewer subjects and shorter durations [15,23,33,40]. Two of the larger, studies by Artemenko et al. and Willis et al. had 40 and 129 subjects [24,30] and followed the subjects for 6 months and 8 years respectively but the studies were non-randomized and non-controlled. Endo and Rutten's studies [16,18] treated the subjects for 3-months, whereas all other studies were conducted for six weeks or less. One additional strength was the collection of a large and robust set of many of the standard PD endpoints including the full MDS-UPDRS parts 1–4 including all subscores, PDQ subscores, PDSS subscores, BDI-II, BAI, CGI, ESS, and WOQ. This rich dataset will guide the design and execution of future studies.

In summary, the CELT used in this study was well tolerated and had a good safety profile with no major device-related adverse events. The study did not meet statistical significance for the primary endpoint, but showed a strong trend toward efficacy, particularly for non-motor features, which warrants further investigation. The Food and Drug Administration, based on reviewed of these data, has granted Breakthrough Device designation for this CELT. Additional efficacy studies are currently underway (NCT04453033).
