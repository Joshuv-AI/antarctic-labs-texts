Iatrogenic thoracic loose body
Authors: Lei Gao, Dan Su, Xian-Ning Wu
Published: 2025

A 59-year-old woman with a 5-year history of autologous fat grafting for breast augmentation visited our outpatient department in December 2023 for the evaluation of incidentally detected breast masses during routine health screening (figure 1A). The patient denied any respiratory or systemic symptoms. Chest CT revealed bilateral round-shaped breast masses with fat density and peripheral calcification, which was further verified by the breast ultrasound examination (figure 1B,C). In addition, the location and imaging features of the breast masses were consistent during the follow-up period (figure 1B,C). Another mass (2.5 cm×2.0 cm) with identical radiographic features was also identified in the anterior mediastinum region (figure 1D). Given the absence of symptoms and the painless nature of the breast masses, annual follow-up was recommended. However, during the most recent evaluation, the mediastinal mass had completely disappeared (figure 2A), while a morphologically comparable round mass emerged in the thoracic cavity (figure 2B). Three-dimensional reconstruction analysis further demonstrated that the thoracic mass had shifted from the anterior mediastinum region (figure 2C) to the oblique fissure (figure 2D), supporting passive translocation rather than de novo growth. Based on clinical history and imaging characteristics, the final diagnosis was iatrogenic thoracic loose body.

The clinical features of peritoneal and intrapleural loose bodies have been previously described.1 2 The imaging characteristics of thoracic loose bodies are comparable with mediastinal cysts or tumours originating from pericardium, bronchus and thymus. However, while the size of these primary mediastinal masses may increase during the follow-up period, their location is immovable and iatrogenic aetiology is absent.2 The mechanisms of loose body formation remain poorly characterised. To our knowledge, this is the first report describing an iatrogenic thoracic loose body resulting from breast augmentation with autologous fat grafting, a widely accepted surgical technique for postmastectomy and aesthetic purposes.3 Accidental intrathoracic adipose injection during the grafting procedure could potentially explain the formation of thoracic loose body. Subsequent biomedical forces, including respiratory motion and negative intrathoracic pressure, may further facilitate their movement. Although fat graft resorption remains one of the most predominant risks, the incidence of severe complications associated with this procedure is low. Thus, selective rather than mandatory follow-up is recommended for patients undergoing this procedure.3

In summary, the loose body formed after iatrogenic procedures can passively move within the thoracic cavity due to respiratory motion. While the mechanical compression of loose body on mediastinal structures (such as superior vena cava) may lead to potential symptoms, intrathoracic infection due to the rupture could be another severe complication. Therefore, patients with these symptoms should be prioritised for diagnostic evaluation, and regular imaging surveillance is required to verify the location of thoracic loose body. Surgical intervention can provide an ultimate option for the patients with potentially life-threatening conditions. Due to the sustained asymptomatic status throughout past years, this patient opted for imaging-based follow-up at annual intervals. Given the diagnostic value in differentiating benign from malignant mediastinal masses, MRI examination would serve as a critical imaging modality for the follow-up of this patient.
