A blockchain-assisted secure federated learning architecture for intrusion detection in internet of things networks
Authors: Muhammad Kamran, Salwa Muhammad Akhtar, Anusha Gilani, Asma A. Alhashmi, Sawera Kanwal, Abdulbasit A. Darem, Adel A. Alofairi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The fast growth of Internet of Things (IoT) systems has made them very susceptible to advanced cyber-attacks, and an intelligent and privacy-sustainable intrusion detection system is required. Conventional centralized intrusion detection frameworks have the drawbacks of data privacy threats, scale constraints, and a single point of failure, and conventional federated learning still faces the threat of malicious client membership and a lack of trust during model aggregation. To overcome these obstacles, this paper suggests a federated learning (B-FL) system based on a Blockchain to ensure safe and reliable intrusion detection in the distributed Internet of Things. The framework proposed is a combination of federated and blockchain-based trust management to guarantee decentralized collaborative model training and maintain data confidentiality. The use of smart contract-based verification tools and trust-weighted aggregation counteracts the adversarial threats, such as model poisoning, data manipulation, free-rider behavior, and Sybil attacks. Testing is performed on the CICIoT2023 dataset, which consists of traffic produced by 105 IoT devices in 33 different types of attacks, and it allows testing all the aspects of its work in terms of a real and heterogeneous network. Findings reveal that the proposed B-FL model has high detection rates, high convergence stability, and enhanced robustness as compared to traditional methods of centralized and federated intrusion detection. Another study, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis, supports the presence of excellent discriminative ability with respect to several classes of intrusion. Though the integration of the blockchain has a marginal increase in computing overhead, it benefits the system in terms of transparency, reliability, and aggregation security significantly. In general, the suggested framework offers a scalable, privacy-aware, and trust-conscious IoT intrusion detection system in the next generation to enable secure collaborative intelligence in dynamic and adversarial IoT environments such as mining and mineral-processing environments.

Introduction

The blistering development of the Internet of Things (IoT) has radically changed contemporary cyber-physical infrastructure, providing the ability to provide mass connectivity to various fields, including smarter cities, industrial automation, healthcare, transportation, and critical infrastructure. These cybersecurity challenges are particularly significant in smart mining environments, where distributed IoT and IIoT devices support extraction systems, mineral-processing plants, environmental monitoring, and autonomous industrial assets. It is projected that by the year 2025, there will be billions of heterogeneous IoT devices deployed over the globe that will constantly generate and exchange sensitive data across distributed and frequently untrusted networks1,2. Although such connectivity is incredibly efficient and automated, it is also coming with substantial cybersecurity issues. The IoT devices are often resource-constrained, geographically distributed, and deployed, with low security provisions, being a prime victim of cyberattacks like botnets, data exfiltration, denial-of-service, and advanced persistent threats3,4. As a result, the issue of having a solid and scalable intrusion detection system in IoT settings has become a significant research issue.

Conventional intrusion detection systems (IDS) were mainly created to work on centralized and resource-based networks. These solutions have various inherent weaknesses when applied to IoT ecosystems, such as bottlenecks in scalability, privacy, and single points of failure5. In centralized IDS architectures, raw traffic data is collected in a central server, which is infeasible with large-scale IoT deployments under bandwidth and restrictions on data sharing requirements by regulation6. Besides, centralizing sensitive IoT data exposes sensitive information to privacy and trust threats, especially when used in healthcare, smart homes, and industrial systems7. These constraints have led to the investigation of paradigms of distributed intrusion detection and privacy-preserving intrusion detection.

Federated learning (FL) is a relatively new, promising solution to the drawbacks of centralized learning, which allows collaborative training of models on distributed equipment without exchanging raw data8. Under an FL-based IDS, it is the local training of detection models by the IoT devices or edge nodes that are not involved in exchanging the model with another device, a coordinating server. This paradigm has greatly minimized the exposure of data and incurred communication overhead, and enables the system to learn various and geographically dispersed attack patterns9. Consequently, FL has been recently frequently used in the study of IoT intrusion detection, and it has shown greater privacy and scalability than conventional centralized methods10,11.

Federated learning presents both a novel and serious attack surface despite the benefits it provides. In contrast to centralized systems, FL assumes that the participating nodes act honestly in the process of training. This is not a common assumption in the real world of IoT. Hacked or malicious devices may deliberately change local model updates to worsen the performance of global models in a process known as model poisoning12. Options include attackers to introduce backdoors, to skew decision boundaries or to apply random or adversarial updates to the learning process13. Also, another weakness of the method is free-riding behavior, whereby members post low-quality or falsified updates in order to gain the advantages of the global model without actually providing substantial information14. Such weaknesses are very critical to federated intrusion detection deployment in adversarial IoT environments.

In an effort to reduce the problem of trust and security within a distributed environment, blockchain technology has been a growing point of research as a decentralized trust management and audit mechanism15. Blockchain offers immutability, transparency, and resistance to tampering, which is why it can be used in the context where parties do not completely trust each other. Blockchain has been used in IoT security studies, specifically in authentication of devices, secure data exchange, access control, and security event loggers16. In more recent works, a range of studies have examined the application of combining blockchain and federated learning to improve accountability and coordination in distributed learners17. These attempts signify how blockchain can be used complementary way to FL to offer decentralized verification and trust implementation.

However, current federated learning systems based on blockchain technology to detect IoT intrusions have significant shortcomings. In the majority of previous works, blockchain is utilized as a passive registry to store the updates of the model or gain access to or training history18. Although these practices make the process of federated learning more transparent, they do not essentially guarantee the process. Particularly, the consensus of local models is often trust-agnostic, i.e., even in the case of the use of blockchain, the malicious or poisoned updates may affect the global IDS. Trust in itself is mostly explicit, heuristic, or not integrated in the learning processes, which restricts its ability to deal with adaptive adversaries19. It results in an essential and unaddressed research gap: the existing FL-based IoT intrusion detection systems do not have a mechanism that both quantitatively measures the trust of the participants and directly involves them in the process of aggregating the model in adversarial settings. When model poisoning attacks are present, when the federated learning does not adopt trust-aware aggregation, devices that are compromised can ruin the performance of detection and system reliability, which are the very goals of federated learning20. More so, it is hard to assign responsibility, audit security decisions, or conduct forensic analysis after mass attacks without a verifiable and tamper-proof record of training behavior.

As recent research shows, federated learning-based intrusion detection systems show degradation of up to 2035% in the attacks of data poisoning and model manipulation, and this had a major effect on the reliability of the models globally21. Moreover, the IDS centralized architectures disclose up to 70 percent of sensitive network data during aggregation of the information, and this is a very serious privacy concern in distributed IoT systems. Even though blockchain implementation enhances auditability, current blockchain-based FL systems tend to offer passive logging and no real-time validation of trust, and thus, collaborative learning systems are susceptible to malicious participants.

It is scientifically and practically significant to overcome this gap. Scientifically speaking, it is necessary to ensure the very learning process in order to improve the federated intelligence within adversarial settings. Practically, IoT implementations in practice, i.e., intelligent grids, smart transportation, and industrial control networks, cannot be based on intrusion detection systems that can be compromised by insiders or by unreliable participants22. With the increasing growth of IoT ecosystems across organizational and administrative borders, there arises an urgent need to have reliable, attack-resilient, and auditable intrusion detection frameworks.

The suggested framework goes beyond the current solutions as the active trust enforcement comes into action, where blockchain-based validation will dynamically assess the reliability of participants and provide collateral model aggregation23. This mechanism guarantees preservation of privacy, decentralized trust management, and strong intrusion detection performance, which are important constraints of previous IDS architectures.

This paper is inspired by these issues and suggests a secure federated learning system based on blockchain in detecting intrusions in IoT networks, with particular emphasis on overcoming model poisoning attacks. In contrast to the current methods, the suggested framework incorporates a permissioned blockchain as an active enforcement system instead of a passive storage system. Smart contracts are used to evaluate the behavior of the participants continuously and give dynamic trust ratings based on the quality and consistency of the submitted model updates. Such trust scores are then directly integrated into a trust-weighted process of model aggregation so that bad or low credibility contributions are either punished or discarded before affecting the global intrusion detection model. Current federated IDS models that use blockchain do not incorporate mathematically enforced trust enforcement in the aggregation process, and thus, the poisoned updates can affect global learning.

Literature review

There have been significant research efforts towards secure and scalable intrusion detection of the IoT as the devices continue to be devised in domains with rigid privacy and latency requirements. In this literature review, related works are segmented into four streams which directly inform our proposed approach: (1) federated learning to identify IoT intrusion, (2) blockchain applications to secure the internet of things and act as a trust layer in federated learning, (3) defenses and attacks in federated learning most notably, model poisoning, and (4) trust- and weight-based aggregation. To summarize each of the streams, I will highlight the most important results, point out the technical constraints, and outline clear gaps that will inspire our contribution.

Federated learning for IoT intrusion detection

Federated learning (FL) has been put forward more generally as a privacy-preserving model alternative to centralized model learning, where raw traffic or telemetry is unable to be disseminated. Empirical literature in the recent past shows that FL holds a practical potential for distributed IDS on devices and edge nodes with resource constraints. introduced a federated learning pipeline that was specific to detecting DDoS attacks in IoT, which focused on the preprocessing and aggregation of information on the device to minimize communication overheads and maintain the utility of detection24. Conducted large-scale empirical research that determined the trade-offs of local model capacity, data volume per node, and global performance in resource-limited IoT environments; they discovered that naive FL configurations are poor in cases where local datasets are small and highly non-IID, as is typical of IoT applications25. All these works validate FL and also raise practical limitations: device heterogeneity, compute, communication cost, and distributional skew- all of which impact the performance and resilience of IDS.

Although several FL-based IDS designs have been reported to achieve good accuracy when the benchmark datasets are clean, it is generally assumed that the participants are honest and are not on local training in clean datasets; these assumptions are not always true in a hostile internet setting. In addition, several of these implementations are concerned with model selection and communication efficiency as opposed to explicitly assessing adversarial robustness. There is thus a gap in the literature between proof-of-concept FL IDS research and deployment-ready, adversary-aware systems that do not compromise detection performance under realistic threat assumptions22.

IoT security on blockchain and its implementation as an FL trust layer

The concept of blockchain has gained extensive research as a decentralized system to offer integrity, provenance, and auditability in IoT systems. Some MDPI and other related articles discuss the use of blockchain in device authentication, secure logging, and access control in IoT networks, stating that immutability and distributed consensus can be used when the participants span administrative domains26. As an illustration, a hierarchical blockchain-enabled FL architecture for the IoT with a particular emphasis on confidentiality and distributed coordination among edge clusters27. introduced a consortium/blockchain-integrated FL system, which can register the computational capacities and combine the devices to maximize cross-chain aggregation to deal with heterogeneity and coordination overheads6.

To be more precise, with regard to IDS, recent research involves integrating blockchain and FL to provide security to update delivery, generate impeccable logs of model changes, and implement reward or recognition systems. BFLIDS, a blockchain-based federated learning IDS on Internet of Medical Things (IoMT), records transactions on blockchain, and model artifacts are stored in a decentralized fashion7. The researchers showed blockchain-enhanced FL in smart homes, with role-based access and knowledge-distillation methodologies to deal with sparse intrusion data. These publications demonstrate the obvious usefulness of blockchain as an accountability and coordination layer.

However, in the majority of available integrations, the role of blockchain is passive: updates are stored there, access controls are made, or audit trails are provided. Importantly, such schemes do not usually commit blockchain-based measures of trust or reputation to the mathematical combination generating the global model; therefore, a malicious but recorded update may still get aggregated in the absence of further protection15,16. This restrains the defensive efficacy of blockchain against insidious evasion assaults that attack the procedure of learning itself.

Federated learning model-poisoning attacks and defenses

Model-poisoning attacks are attacks where attackers develop local updates to poison or subvert the global model, and are well-documented and becoming more advanced. More recent high-impact research demonstrates that many existing defenses can be overcome by multi-round, consistency-sensitive poisoning. A model-poisoning protocol, called PoisonedFL, was introduced, which demonstrates that numerous state-of-the-art defenses fail in longer-term, more covert attack strategies, alongside the fact that such system defenses are more vulnerable than thought previously, and that defense strategies need to take this fact into account28. The finding of alternating and stealthy patterns of poisoning, which are observed in Complementary analysis in Nature Scientific Reports (2024) and other sources, avoids the process of static anomaly detectors and simple aggregation filters10. The parameterized studies also reveal that the timing, magnitude, and number of participants under adversarial control vary drastically to alter poisoning efficacy.

Defensive work has suggested strong aggregation policies (median, trimmed mean), update anomaly detection, and client validation; most of these solutions are either utility or computationally costly in the case of IoT edge devices. Other defenses are based on powerful assumptions (fraction of Byzantine clients, clean validation data), which are unrealistic in heterogeneous IoT applications. Some more recent works suggest a contract-based or hierarchical secure aggregation with the help of smart contracts to organize the steps of aggregation and punish misbehaving parties8. These solutions are promising, though frequently fall short of a strict coupling on measured trust that forms a direct factor in aggregation weights, which are enforced in an auditable and on-chain fashion. Therefore, although the defense literatures offer useful primitives, there is an open question of the challenge of designing a lightweight and scalable mechanism (a) to detect malicious updates reliably in the IoT context, and (b) to impose penalties or exclusion by some decentralized and tamper-proof authority.

Trust- weighted aggregation methods

Various streams of literature suggest weighted aggregation, the use of varying contribution weights on clients according to a metric of reliability, as one means of enhancing FL resilience. Recent works suggest the use of device-attribute-based weighting, cross-validation performance weighting, as well as reputation systems based on the past quality of updates29. Indicatively, in a 2024 study on trust management of IoT devices, device attributes were integrated to produce weights that were used to aggregate, and with benign clients being more influential, it was found to be more accurate. Intelligent contract research in the area of decentralized FL also indicates that reputation updates and rewards of honest participation may be automated by the use of on-chain mechanisms30. However, the majority of the suggested weighting schemes are ad hoc, and they are computed centrally or stored in off-chain reputation databanks; they lack an enforceable quality or a real-time incorporation of aggregation. Further, little is known that quantifies the dynamics of trust in active adversarial strategies- how the trust scores are supposed to change when the attackers deliberately change behavior (i.e., act honestly during a large number of rounds, then poison) to avoid detection. Some recent literature is starting to concern dynamic trust and enforcement using smart contracts, but these are still early and typically lack IoT-scale testing with limited resources.

Poisoned FL and other state-of-the-art poisoning attacks have been proven to break down the multi-round, adaptive procedures on static defenses. However, not all of the suggested FL-IDS systems incorporate strict attack-aware experiments that represent such strategies31.

Absence of dynamic, enforced trust-integration: There are mechanisms of weighted, reputation-based aggregation, but they are commonly heuristic, centralized, or off-chain; they do not offer an auditable, smart-contract-enforced pipeline where (a) trust is learned as a result of observed update behaviour, (b) trust is dynamically updated in response to adaptive adversaries, and (c) the pipeline directly relates to aggregation to prevent poisoning in real time32. These holes express an opportunity to provide a specialized contribution: an authorized blockchain actively assessing, storing, and enforcing trust scores, together with a mathematically-grounded rule of trust-weighted aggregation formulated to IoT constraints, and tested against advanced poisoning attacks.

As opposed to passive on-chain logging techniques, smart contracts are used to execute verifiable trust-scoring functions, as well as to apply trust-weighted aggregation rules at aggregation time. We consider strategies of multi-round poisoning (based on poisoned FL) and assess degradation in realistic IoT settings (poisoning with limited local compute, non-IID data, and intermittent connection). Lastly, our architecture focuses on lightweight cryptographic and consensus decisions that are applicable to permissioned/consortium applications that are prevalent in industrial and smart-city IoT applications- following recent suggestions of hierarchical blockchain + FL architecture29. Although there has been a significant advancement in federated learning-based intrusion detection and the development of blockchain as a security and audit layer in an IoT setting, the literature is still incomplete with regard to the consideration of trust, security, and adversarial robustness in the integrated approach. Even earlier literature usually views blockchain as an active logging or coordination system, trust and reputation, where they are discussed, are either heuristic, centrally governed, or not related to the real model aggregation mechanism. In addition, most FL-based IDS systems are tested based on benign or weaker adversarial conditions, without considering complex multi- round and adaptive model-poisoning attacks that have demonstrated a threat to the state-of-the-art defenses. Thus, there is a severe research gap in a lightweight, IoT-compatible framework, which (i) dynamically measures participant trust based on established learning behavior, (ii) implements this trust in a decentralized and auditable way using smart contracts, and (iii) directly incorporates trust into the aggregation to prevent poisoning attacks in real time. Sealing this gap compels the proposed trust-aware federated intrusion detection framework that is supported by blockchain, described in the next section.

Materials and methodology

System overview

The Internet of Things (IoT) environments are growing rapidly, with several challenges to cybersecurity brought about by distributed deployment, the ability to heterogeneously enable devices, and inadequate computational capabilities. The limitations of traditional centralized intrusion detection systems include privacy leaks, limitations to scale, and susceptibility to single-point failure. To address these constraints, the current paper suggests a B-FL framework, which is a Blockchain-Enhanced Federated Learning system that aims to detect intrusion in the IoT network privately and attack-resiliently.

Within the suggested architecture, intrusion detection models are locally trained on local edge node traffic data. Rather than sharing raw datasets, the federation network shares only encrypted model parameters with the federation network. An update layer verifies updates received by a blockchain layer using smart contracts that dynamically calculate trust scores using update behavior. These trust values are built into global aggregation, thus making sure that untrustworthy or adversarial players do not have much control of the final model. This design approach provides security in distributed learning and maintains the privacy of data, audit, and resistance to model-poisoning attacks.

System architecture description

The suggested system architecture is meant to provide a secure, decentralized, and trustworthy intrusion detection system of the IoT environment through the combination of federated learning with a permissioned blockchain system. The architecture consists of four closely interrelated parts: IoT client and federated learning coordinator, permissioned blockchain network, and a trust management layer with smart contracts. All these elements work together to provide a privacy-safe learning experience and make it resistant to malicious or unreliable members.

The IoT client nodes are scattered edge objects that work on gathering real-time traffic information on the network produced by heterogeneous devices, including sensors, gateways, and smart appliances. Rather than sending raw and sensitive data to a centralized server, the clients locally train an intrusion detection model on their local data. This decentralized learning system has a huge mitigating effect on privacy loss and communication cost. After the local training, the model parameters or gradients are not sent to the permissioned blockchain network but are sent instead to the network to be aggregated. The blockchain serves as a secure validation and logging infrastructure that ensures immutability, transparency, and traceability of updates made by the clients. In such an environment, smart contracts can automatically determine the validity of updates, identify suspicious contributions, and calculate dynamic trust scores based on the consistency of behavior and past performance of their clients.

The federated learning coordinator just accesses the validated updates by the blockchain ledger and carries out trust-weighted aggregation, so that trusted clients play a bigger role in the global model. The updated model of worldwide intrusion detection is then retransmitted to the IoT nodes, and a sequence of successive enhancements of the model on the rounds of federated learning is allowed, without compromising the integrity of the system (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Blockchain-enabled federated intrusion detection framework.

Design of intrusion detection model

The proposed intrusion detection model is applied with a deep neural network architecture, which is implemented to classify the traffic of an IoT network in multi-class. The model is comprised of an input layer and three fully connected hidden layers of neurons, 128, 64, and 32, respectively. Every concealed layer employs the ReLU activation function to bring about non-linearity as well as enhance feature learning potential.

The second hidden layer is followed by a dropout layer (rate = 0.3) to reduce overfitting and increase the chances of generalizing the model. The output layer uses a softmax activation function to predict the traffic as belonging to different categories, such as benign and different types of attacks.

The Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001, a batch size of 64, and a categorical cross-entropy loss function are used in training the model. It is conducted by training on several local epochs per federated round to be able to learn effectively with distributed data from the IoT.

Figure 5 depicts how the proposed deep learning-based intrusion detection architecture is built to identify the types of traffic on the IoT network as benign and various types of attacks. The input layer is first fed with normalized network flow features generated after preprocessing, which are some characteristics of communication, like the packet statistics and protocol behavior. These characteristics are passed on to several hidden collections of densities, in which nonlinear activation functions facilitate the model to acquire complex and multidimensional traffic patterns that comprise cyberattacks. As features are successively abstracted, discriminative representations are learned by the network, which distinguishes normal device communication and malicious intent, e.g., DoS, reconnaissance, spoofing and botnet attacks.Fig. 5Deep learning-based intrusion detection model architecture.

Discussion

The validation of the proposed framework in real conditions of a network is made possible by the experimental assessment of the CICIoT2023 dataset, which is one of the most extensive real-to-life IoT intrusion detection data sets, with its traffic generated by 105 heterogeneous devices and 33 different types of attack. The results obtained can be used to validate the idea that the suggested Blockchain-supported Federated Learning (B-FL) model proves to be more accurate and resilient in detection than the traditional centralized and standalone federated models. The enhancements in performance show the efficiency of distributed collaborative learning in managing heterogeneous distribution of IoT data and preserving the privacy of the data at the device level. As opposed to centralized IDS architecture, which experiences risks of data exposure and lacks scalability, the federated paradigm enables decentralized model training without exposing raw network traffic, hence maintaining sensitive operation details.

Moreover, the incorporation of blockchain technology creates one more layer of trust and accountability into the federated process of aggregation. The use of smart contract-based validation techniques makes participation safe, as the updates by the clients are verified before aggregation, which greatly limits the vulnerabilities that come with model poisoning and malicious client behavior. This trust-centric aggregation scheme promotes the global model upholding, especially in adversarial IoT scenarios where the compromised nodes are typical.

The findings also show that, in the case of non-IID and distributed data settings, which are common features of large-scale IoT systems, secure federated aggregation enhances learning stability. The proposed B-FL framework allows stateful privacy, decentralized intelligence, and inseparable validation, which offers a scalable and robust solution to the next-generation IoT intrusion-detection systems used in security-sensitive applications. Overall, the results indicate that the integration of blockchain-based trust management as part of federated learning can strengthen system security as well as lead to stable intrusion detection performance in various and dynamically changing IoT networks.

In order to measure the computational cost imposed by the implementation of blockchain, we performed a quantitative analysis of the main elements, such as smart contract execution, PBFT consensus latency, and on-chain storage operations. It was found that the average latency of the smart contract execution was around 25–40 ms per update, and PBFT consensus also added another delay of around 50–90 ms, depending on the number of nodes involved. On-chain storage and retrieval operations added a small overhead of 1020 ms per transaction. The suggested B-FL system will impose a moderate level of computational overhead of about 8–15 percent relative to conventional federated learning. Nevertheless, this overhead is compensated by major enhancements in security, trust management, and adversarial attack resistance, which makes the framework appropriate in secure IoT settings.

Security and implications in practice

The suggested Federated Learning B-FL framework utilizing Blockchain has high security and operational benefits to real-world IoT intrusion detection implementations. Contemporary IoT systems are decentralized and resource-constrained and prone to coordinated cyber threats; thus, typical centralized security systems are frequently inadequate. The federated learning using blockchain technology is capable of providing collaborative intelligence, which is secure, and the centralized data collection is not required. Security-wise, the framework enhances the resilience of the systems, introducing trust-sensitive model aggregation and immutable records of participation. The identity management approach is based on blockchain, which blocks participation of unauthorized nodes and helps to prevent Sybil attacks, and smart contract validation can ensure that global learning is contributed only by verified and behaviorally consistent model updates. This mechanism minimizes the risks related to model poisoning, unreliable clients, and adversarial manipulation considerably, which are also of primary concern in distributed IDS systems.

In practice, the decentralized design enhances scalability and flexibility to heterogeneous IoT systems that include smart cities, health monitoring systems, industrial internet of things, and smart transportation systems. The framework meets the developing data protection demands because the raw data is not exchanged, and it is locally stored, which reduces the communication overhead and privacy concerns. Even though computer overhead is moderate due to the integration of blockchain, the experimental findings show that the security gains are more than the extra latency, especially in security-sensitive applications. The suggested framework also promotes learning continuously in dynamic settings where patterns of attack change with time. Safe federated cooperation enables Internet of IoT nodes distributed geographically to collaborate in increasing detection capacity without breaching confidentiality. The system therefore provides an effective avenue to reliable, privacy-aware, and scalable intrusion detection that can be used in intelligent next-generation IoT ecosystems.

Limitations and future work

Although the goal of the proposed study has been fulfilled through successful performance improvements, there are a number of limitations that offer guidance to future research and practical improvements of the proposed Blockchain-enabled Federated Learning (B-FL) framework. First, blockchain incorporation creates extra computational and communicational overhead that can have an impact on the IoT environments that demand latency and have highly resource-constrained edge devices. Though the overhead seen is reasonable in a laboratory environment, more needs to be done to optimize consensus algorithms and blockchain-based lightweight protocols that can be deployed at scale in real-time.

Second, the existing framework presupposes the stability of client involvement in federated training. The reality of IoT ecosystems in the real-world is that devices are not always available, as there are connectivity issues, energy constraints, and mobility issues, which can affect the aggregation stability. Adaptive client selection and asynchronous federated learning could be considered in future tasks to enhance the robustness of the work in the case of dynamic conditions of participation.

The other shortcoming is associated with the generalization of datasets. Although the CICIoT2023 dataset is highly diverse in terms of attacks, actual operation environments can have attack patterns that have never been observed or have zero-day attacks. Adding mechanisms of continual learning, online adaptation, and the use of transfer learning would also benefit the generalization of the models to changing cyber threats. Moreover, the mechanism of trust evaluation at the moment is based on behavioral consistency and updated validation measures. Future studies can consider enhanced reputation models, explainable AI-based trust estimation, and game-theoretic protection measures to enhance adversarial resilience. Combination with lightweight edge intelligence and energy-sensitive optimization methods is also a significant trend in the sustainable IoT security solution.

In its further evolution, the offered framework can be expanded to fully autonomous and self-adaptive intrusion detection systems, which could share the threat intelligence on the distributed IoT domain in real-time. With these developments, scalable, privacy-guaranteed, and trustworthy cybersecurity infrastructures compatible with next-generation smart environments and Industry 5.0 applications would be encouraged.

Conclusion

This paper introduced a federated learning (FedL) framework based on a Blockchain, which is used to detect intrusions in heterogeneous IoT systems in a safe and privacy-preserving manner. The suggested method incorporates decentralized federated learning and blockchain-based trusted management to address the most significant issues related to data privacy, the presence of malicious clients, and the safety of model aggregation. The framework enables collaborative learning without transmitting raw network traffic, thereby preserving data confidentiality and achieving high detection rates.

The experimental assessment of the CICIoT2023 data proved that the given model can be characterized by a high level of classification accuracy, high convergence rates, and high resistance to adversarial threats such as model poisoning and untrustworthy client contributions. The use of smart contract-based validation and trust-weighted aggregation schemes is important to guarantee a reliable involvement and enhance the integrity of the federated learning process. Also, finding supports the claim that secure aggregation with the help of blockchain accountability enhances intrusion detection functionality when the conditions of non-IID and distributed IoT data are met. Despite the blockchain integration presenting moderate computational overhead, the obtained security, transparency, and trustworthiness of the system are highly rewarded in comparison with the shortcomings. Beyond general IoT security applications, the proposed blockchain-assisted federated intrusion detection framework is also applicable to smart mining and mineral-processing IIoT systems, where trust-aware collaborative learning can strengthen the protection of distributed industrial infrastructure.

Overall, the suggested B-FL framework offers a resilient, scalable, and reliable solution to next-generation IoT intrusion detection networks. Decentralized intelligence combined with verifying trusts that cannot be altered is a plausible future in achieving safe, privacy-sensitive, and responsive cybersecurity systems in upcoming intelligent and connected systems.
