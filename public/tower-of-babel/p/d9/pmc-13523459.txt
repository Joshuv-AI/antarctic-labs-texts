Lyme Myopericarditis With Effusive-Constrictive Physiology
Authors: Nazih Alhirtani, Grace Bigham, Carly Fabrizio, Craig A. Laing, Rebecca Kumar, Syed Waqas Haider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLyme cardiac involvement typically presents with atrioventricular (AV) conduction disease. Pericardial involvement with effusive-constrictive physiology is uncommon. Distinguishing inflammatory from fixed constriction has important management implications.Case SummaryA 45-year-old man developed progressive pleuritic chest pain and dyspnea 4 months after a tick bite. An electrocardiogram showed diffuse ST-segment elevation with PR-segment depression without atrioventricular block, and he was treated for acute pericarditis. Persistent symptoms prompted echocardiography demonstrating moderate pericardial effusion and new left ventricular systolic dysfunction (left ventricular ejection fraction 35%-40%). Lyme serology showed IgG Western blot reactivity (10/10 bands), consistent with late disseminated Lyme disease. Cardiac magnetic resonance demonstrated diffuse pericardial thickening/enhancement with pericardial T2 edema, loculated effusion, right ventricular tethering, and ventricular interdependence. Right-heart catheterization confirmed effusive-constrictive physiology. He was treated medically, with recovery of left ventricular function without pericardiectomy.DiscussionSerial cardiac magnetic resonance and invasive hemodynamics identified a reversible inflammatory phenotype supporting conservative therapy.Take-Home MessageMultimodality imaging can identify a reversible inflammatory phenotype and guide therapy in effusive-constrictive pericarditis.

History of Presentation

A 45-year-old man with no prior medical history was in his usual state of health until October 2023, when he sustained a tick bite while working outdoors. The tick was removed manually; attachment duration was unknown. He subsequently developed 2 episodes of a viral-like illness with fatigue in October and November 2023. In January 2024, he developed progressive substernal pleuritic chest pain worsened with deep inspiration, with a 20-pound unintentional weight loss over several weeks. An electrocardiogram demonstrated diffuse ST-segment elevation without reciprocal depression, with PR-segment depression in the inferior leads and PR-segment elevation in aVR, consistent with acute pericarditis. High-sensitivity troponin was within reference range, and N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) was normal. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) in early February 2024 demonstrated a preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 60% to 65% with a small pericardial effusion (<1 cm). Despite therapy, he developed worsening chest pain, progressive fatigue, and dyspnea on exertion. In early March 2024, he was evaluated by his primary care physician and referred to the emergency department. Laboratory testing demonstrated NT-proBNP 599 pg/mL, erythrocyte sedimentation rate 58 mm/h, and C-reactive protein 62.5 mg/L; high-sensitivity troponin (4 ng/L) remained within the reference range. NT-proBNP rose to 1,646 pg/mL days later. Repeat TTE in early March 2024 demonstrated a newly reduced LVEF of 35% to 40% with a moderate pericardial effusion (1-2 cm) without tamponade physiology. An index cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) study performed at the outside hospital in early March 2024 demonstrated improvement in LVEF to 56% and patchy subendocardial intramyocardial enhancement involving the basal-to-mid inferior septum and inferior walls without associated myocardial edema.

Past Medical History

The patient had no prior medical history.

Differential Diagnosis

Initial diagnostic considerations included acute pericarditis, myopericarditis, viral myocarditis, ischemic myocardial injury, and Lyme carditis.

Investigations

Given his complex presentation, the patient was transferred in early March 2024 for evaluation of suspected myopericarditis and consideration of right-heart catheterization.

At the receiving center, Lyme serology was positive by enzyme-linked immunoadsorbent assay (IgM and IgG), with confirmatory Western blot demonstrating IgG reactivity (10/10 bands) and negative IgM bands. No atrioventricular block or other conduction abnormalities were observed on electrocardiography or telemetry. TTE in mid-March 2024 demonstrated an LVEF of 50% to 55% with abnormal septal motion (noted in the formal report as consistent with left bundle branch block), which was interpreted as septal bounce, raising concern for ventricular interdependence. CMR in mid-March 2024 demonstrated diffuse pericardial thickening and enhancement with a loculated effusion abutting the right ventricular (RV) free wall and right atrium (RA), restricted diastolic RV filling, and impaired RV systolic function. These findings supported inflammatory pericardial involvement in the setting of prior myocardial enhancement.

Right-heart catheterization in mid-March 2024 confirmed effusive-constrictive physiology (Table 1). Predrainage hemodynamics demonstrated RA pressure 16 mm Hg (with blunted Y descent), pulmonary artery pressure 30/13 mm Hg (mean 20), pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP) 13 mm Hg, right ventricular end-diastolic pressure 14 mm Hg, Fick cardiac output/cardiac index 4.26/2.24, pulmonary vascular resistance 1.65, systemic vascular resistance 1,446, and intrapericardial pressure 13 mm Hg. After pericardiocentesis (approximately 50 mL removed), intrapericardial pressure fell to 3 mm Hg, but RA pressure remained elevated at 17 mm Hg without meaningful improvement in the Y descent, with pulmonary artery pressure 31/13 mm Hg (mean 21), PCWP 17 mm Hg, right ventricular end-diastolic pressure 16 mm Hg, and cardiac output/cardiac index 4.5/2.4. Persistence of elevated RA pressure despite normalization of intrapericardial pressure definitively established constrictive physiology beyond effusion-related tamponade. Invasive Hemodynamics Before and After Pericardiocentesis

Serial CMR was obtained for longitudinal phenotyping after invasive confirmation of effusive-constrictive physiology. The April 2024 study demonstrated a loculated pericardial effusion with diffuse pericardial edema and delayed enhancement consistent with active inflammatory pericarditis (Figure 1). Follow-up imaging in September 2024 showed resolution of effusion and edema with persistent pericardial thickening and delayed enhancement, along with improving ventricular interdependence (Figure 2). By March 2025, there was no residual effusion or edema, with further reduction in pericardial enhancement and no myocardial enhancement (Figure 3). Collectively, these findings demonstrate progression from active inflammation to residual remodeling with gradual resolution of constrictive physiology. Serial T2-weighted imaging demonstrated progressive resolution of pericardial edema across all three timepoints (Figure 4). Management

Antimicrobial therapy was initiated with ceftriaxone and transitioned to doxycycline 100 mg twice daily due to suspected drug hypersensitivity, completing an approximately 21-day guideline-concordant course of Lyme-directed therapy. Anti-inflammatory therapy included colchicine 0.6 mg twice daily for 3 months, followed by 0.6 mg daily for an additional 3 months, along with a structured nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug taper.

Guideline-directed medical therapy was deferred during hemodynamic instability and tachycardia. Once stabilized, metoprolol succinate was initiated in late April 2024 (12.5 mg daily, increased to 25 mg daily), and losartan 25 mg daily was added.

Outcome and Follow-Up

TTE as of September 2025 demonstrated recovery of LVEF to 55% to 60% without pericardial effusion or echocardiographic evidence of constrictive physiology. Follow-up imaging demonstrated improvement in pericardial inflammation and RV tethering. Pericardiectomy was not required. Inflammatory markers normalized during follow-up, paralleling clinical and imaging recovery.

Discussion

This case demonstrates Lyme-associated myopericarditis presenting with hemodynamically confirmed effusive-constrictive physiology in the absence of conduction abnormalities. Invasive hemodynamics established persistent constrictive physiology despite normalization of intrapericardial pressure, and serial CMR captured evolution from edematous inflammatory activity to residual enhancement with improving ventricular interdependence. Persistent pericardial enhancement likely reflects low-grade inflammatory remodeling, supporting sustained anti-inflammatory therapy. This imaging-hemodynamic integration informed conservative management and avoided unnecessary pericardiectomy.

Lyme carditis commonly presents with atrioventricular conduction disease, whereas clinically apparent myocarditis is less frequent (approximately 10% in a historic centers for disease control and prevention cohort).1 Cardiac involvement may also include pericarditis, myopericarditis, and transient left ventricular systolic dysfunction.2 CMR studies generally demonstrate nonischemic patterns of myocardial involvement in Lyme carditis, although enhancement patterns may vary and should be interpreted in clinical context.3 In this case, Lyme-associated myopericardial disease occurred without conduction abnormalities and progressed to effusive-constrictive pericarditis (ECP). The patient's serologic profile, with IgG positivity and negative IgM on Western blot in the context of untreated tick exposure approximately 4 months earlier, is most consistent with late disseminated Lyme disease with cardiac involvement rather than early acute infection. Infectious disease consultation supported guideline-concordant Lyme-directed therapy without prolonged antimicrobial treatment.

The variability in LVEF across imaging modalities likely reflects dynamic loading conditions and pericardial constraint rather than primary myocardial dysfunction. Although the initial enhancement pattern was subendocardial, the absence of troponin elevation, lack of a coronary distribution, and complete resolution of myocardial enhancement on follow-up imaging argue strongly against infarction and support a reversible inflammatory process. We report this case to emphasize that the absence of atrioventricular block does not exclude Lyme cardiac involvement and to demonstrate how integration of invasive hemodynamics with serial CMR may identify a reversible inflammatory ECP phenotype, guide therapy, and avoid unnecessary surgical intervention.

ECP is defined by persistent elevation of RA pressure after pericardiocentesis despite normalization of intrapericardial pressure.4 In this case, right-sided pressures remained elevated after pericardial drainage, confirming constrictive physiology beyond effusion-related tamponade. The concurrent rise in PCWP (13-17 mm Hg) after pericardiocentesis reflects ventricular interdependence: relief of pericardial constraint increased right-sided filling, with transmission of pressure to a relatively noncompliant left ventricle within the inflamed pericardial space. Contemporary frameworks recognize that this phenotype may represent inflammation-driven, potentially reversible constriction rather than fixed fibrotic disease.5

CMR enables tissue-level characterization distinguishing active inflammation (late gadolinium enhancement with T2-weighted edema) from residual scar (persistent enhancement without edema). Pericardial late gadolinium enhancement and inflammatory biomarkers have been associated with reversibility of constrictive physiology after anti-inflammatory therapy.6 In this case, serial CMR captured the transition from edematous inflammatory activity to residual enhancement, clarifying that imaging normalization lags clinical recovery. Distinguishing this residual remodeling phase from ongoing active inflammation is essential to avoid premature escalation of anti-inflammatory therapy or unnecessary surgical referral. This differentiation directly informs management by identifying patients in whom surgical intervention can be safely deferred.

As inflammatory effusive-constrictive physiology may resolve with targeted antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory therapy, conservative management is often appropriate. Observational data demonstrate that most patients with ECP after pericardiocentesis experience resolution of constrictive features with medical therapy, with pericardiectomy reserved for refractory cases.7 Integration of invasive hemodynamics with serial CMR in this patient established a reversible inflammatory phenotype and supported avoidance of surgery. To our knowledge, serial CMR demonstrating this inflammation-to-remodeling trajectory in Lyme-associated effusive-constrictive physiology is rarely described. This case underscores that negative or atypical myocardial findings should not dissuade clinicians from recognizing inflammatory pericardial disease, particularly when multimodality imaging and hemodynamics demonstrate a reversible phenotype.

Conclusions

In patients with inflammatory pericardial disease, persistence of elevated right-sided pressures after pericardiocentesis should prompt recognition of inflammatory effusive-constrictive physiology rather than premature labeling of fixed constriction. Serial CMR allows differentiation of active inflammatory constriction from residual pericardial remodeling, guiding therapy, monitoring recovery, and avoiding unnecessary surgical intervention.
