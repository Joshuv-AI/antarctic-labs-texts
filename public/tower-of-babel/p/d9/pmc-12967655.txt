Brain Folding Trajectories in Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis
Authors: Mahan Shafie, Carlo Tamba, Harpreet Bhinder, Canadian ALS Neuroimaging Consortium (CALSNIC), Sanjay Kalra, Fabrizio Pizzagalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a clinically heterogeneous neurodegenerative disease, and neuroimaging markers offer a promising avenue to capture this variability. Cortical folding alterations in ALS remain largely unexplored despite growing interest in neuroimaging markers of the disease. This study is the first whole‐brain investigation of sulcal morphometry in ALS. A total of 222 individuals diagnosed with ALS and 194 healthy controls (HC) were recruited through the Canadian ALS Neuroimaging Consortium (CALSNIC) for a longitudinal investigation. Using T1‐weighted MRI processed with FreeSurfer and BrainVISA, we extracted cortical thickness and four sulcal features: width, mean depth, surface area, and length, across 123 sulci. We identified widespread alterations in ALS, with 19 sulci showing increased width and length alongside reduced depth and surface area, primarily in frontal and parietal regions surrounding the motor strip. The central sulcus (CS) emerged as the most consistently affected region, displaying bilateral widening and reduced depth, changes that closely tracked motor decline. Longitudinal analyses revealed progressive widening and reduced depth and surface area of the CS. These alterations closely tracked the progression of motor symptoms over the course of the disease and aligned with regional cortical thickness alterations. Our findings demonstrate that brain folding patterns, and in particular CS, are altered in ALS and correlate with clinical progression, resembling the neurodegenerative pattern of the disease. By revealing complementary and sensitive changes, sulcal‐based metrics may offer promising neuroimaging biomarkers for early detection, prognosis, and patient stratification in ALS.Keywords: amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, brain folding, sulcal morphometry

Introduction

Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disease caused by a complex interaction of genetic and environmental factors (Feldman et al. 2022). Yet, our understanding of the underlying pathophysiology remains limited. The recognized phenotypic heterogeneity in ALS often contributes to a significant delay in diagnosis. Recently, there has been growing interest in predicting and identifying factors that influence the risk and progression of ALS to improve patient prognosis. Imaging‐based metrics hold promise as biomarkers to address these needs and provide insight into disease biology, including heterogeneity (Goutman et al. 2022).

Advanced imaging protocols have recently been utilized to study and quantify structural, functional, and metabolic abnormalities in ALS (Kassubek and Pagani 2019; Juengling et al. 2022). Notably, voxel‐based morphometry (VBM), surface‐based morphometry (Shen et al. 2016; Chen, Zhou, et al. 2018; Chen, Liu, and Ma 2018), and deformation‐based morphometry (DBM) (Dadar et al. 2020; Lajoie et al. 2025) have proven sensitive in detecting motor and extra‐motor cortical and subcortical atrophy by assessing global or regional gray matter loss and cortical thinning in ALS. However, interpreting these results can be challenging, as they do not isolate specific anatomical features and differences may arise from various factors or a combination of the elements.

Cortical folding, or gyrification, reflects a tightly regulated interplay between genetic programs, neural progenitor behavior, and mechanical forces such as differential tangential expansion of cortical layers and axonal traction, which may offer new insights into the investigation of neurodegenerative disorders (Llinares‐Benadero and Borrell 2019). Cortical folding patterns, which are believed to be genetically and structurally independent of cortical thickness and surface area (Fabrizio Pizzagalli et al. 2020), remain largely unexplored in ALS patients. Changes in cortical folding are anticipated in ALS, given the neurodegenerative nature of ALS and the influence of underlying white matter structures, alterations frequently reported in ALS patients (Gorges et al. 2018; Chavoshnejad et al. 2023). Studies have revealed abnormal gyrification patterns in both neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders (Sasabayashi et al. 2021). While increased gyrification has been observed in neurodevelopmental disorders (Y. Chen et al. 2023; Shokouhi et al. 2012; Im et al. 2016), neurodegenerative disorders are associated with abnormally low gyrification, characterized by sulcal widening and reduced cortical thickness (Sighinolfi et al. 2024; Wang et al. 2021).

The objective of this study was to investigate alterations in brain cortical folding in patients with ALS. Specifically, we applied a sulcal‐based morphometry pipeline in order to map differences between ALS patients and healthy controls (HC). We further examined the relationship between alterations in sulcal measures and disease progression, including motor function and cognitive abilities. We hypothesized that patients with ALS would show disrupted patterns of cortical folding, especially in motor‐related brain regions. These disruptions could serve as biomarkers of neurodegeneration and may be detectable over the course of disease progression. We also expected that altered folding patterns would be linked to disease severity, particularly motor deterioration.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

Data was obtained for participants across the nine sites of the Canadian ALS Neuroimaging Consortium (CALSNIC) (Table S11), a platform aimed to establish innovative MRI‐based biomarkers for ALS (Kalra et al. 2020). Study participants in CALSNIC were recruited prospectively and underwent standardized clinical assessments and imaging on scanners operating at 3 T.

Enrollment in CALSNIC required patients to have a diagnosis of either sporadic or familial ALS, and in accordance with the revised El Escorial research criteria, be classified as possible, probable, probable laboratory‐supported, or definite ALS (Brooks et al. 2000). Healthy controls (HC) were excluded if they had any history of neurological disorders, such as stroke or traumatic brain injury, or if they had a significant psychiatric condition (Kalra et al. 2020). Patients with primary lateral sclerosis, progressive muscular atrophy, frontotemporal dementia (FTD), and ALS‐FTD were also enrolled in CALSNIC; however, these patients were excluded from this study.

A total of 222 individuals with ALS and 194 HC were included in the study at baseline. Participants were selected for longitudinal analysis if they had undergone visits at all three time points in the CALSNIC protocol: baseline, 4 months, and 8 months. This subset comprised 83 ALS patients and 100 HC. Comprehensive demographic and clinical information is summarized in Table 1. The study received ethics approval from the Health Research Ethics Board at each participating site, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants (Kalra et al. 2020).

Clinical Characteristics

Clinical evaluations comprised both motor and cognitive assessments. Motor function was assessed through standardized examinations, including the ALS Functional Rating Scale‐Revised (ALSFRS‐R) and its subdomains, which served as indicators of disease severity (Appendix 1999; Cedarbaum et al. 1999). Disease progression rate (DPR) was estimated as (48‐ALSFRS‐R)/(symptom duration) (Kimura et al. 2006). UMN involvement in bulbar and limb regions was quantified using a 12‐point UMN burden score, derived from signs such as spasticity, hyperreflexia, Babinski reflex, and ankle clonus, each side of the body. This UMN burden score has previously been shown to correlate with various imaging metrics in ALS (Ta et al. 2021; Müller et al. 2024; Ishaque et al. 2022; Dadar et al. 2020). DPR and UMN scores were categorized based on their median values. Additional motor performance was evaluated using finger and foot tapping tasks for both the left and right sides (finger and foot tapping scores) (Kalra et al. 2020). Cognitive and behavioral functioning were measured using the Edinburgh Cognitive and Behavioral ALS Screen (ECAS), and its subdomains including ALS‐Specific and ALS Non‐specific subdomains (Abrahams et al. 2014). We categorized ECAS total and subscores to define cognitive impairment using the Quantile Regression North American (QR‐NAC) statistical approach described in a previous study (McMillan et al. 2022). This method established normative ECAS thresholds for each score, based on specific age groups and education levels. HC participants also completed the ECAS and tapping assessments for comparative analysis.

MRI Data Acquisition

Participants underwent a standardized multimodal MRI protocol using research‐dedicated 3‐T scanners. A high‐resolution 3D T1‐weighted scan (1 × 1 × 1 mm3) was utilized for this study. Further details on MRI data acquisition are provided elsewhere (Kalra et al. 2020).

MRI Data Analysis for Sulcal Morphometry

Sulcal‐based morphometry was assessed following the ENIGMA‐Sulci protocol, a standardized approach outlined in previous studies (Fabrizio Pizzagalli et al. 2020; Dojat et al. 2018; Sun et al. 2022). This method utilizes the BrainVISA 5.1.2 framework (https://brainvisa.info/web/index.html), which incorporates various tools including the Morphologist software (Mangin, Rivière, Cachia, et al. 2004; Perrot et al. 2011; Rivière et al. 2002). Structural image preprocessing involved intensity inhomogeneity correction and brain tissue segmentation, a process performed using FreeSurfer version 7.4.1 (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/). We additionally extracted cortical thickness measures for all 68 Desikan‐Killiany cortical regions using FreeSurfer.

The Morphologist software was applied to preprocessed images, incorporating FreeSurfer segmentation to facilitate sulcal extraction, identification, and morphometry. This process automatically segmented and labeled 123 sulci (62 in the left hemisphere and 61 in the right hemisphere) of the human cerebrum, as defined by the BrainVISA atlas (Video S1). All the results are reported using BrainVISA nomenclature (Figure 1A).

Annotated lateral (A) and medial (B) views of the left hemisphere showing the anatomical locations of 62 labeled sulci, providing a comprehensive reference for sulcal identification in morphometric analyses. Each sulcus is color‐coded and mapped along its typical anatomical trajectory between adjacent gyri. (C) Illustration of four key sulcal shape metrics derived from the central sulcus of the left hemisphere: Width (distance across the sulcus), mean depth (average depth relative to the cortical surface), surface area (exposed surface of the sulcal wall), and length (extent along the sulcal path).

For each identified sulcus, we extracted a set of morphological descriptors in individual‐subject space, including width, mean depth, surface area, and length (Figure 1). These descriptors served as input data for statistical analysis. Sulcal length was determined by counting the voxels at the junction between the sulcus and the brain hull. Mean depth was calculated as the average geodesic distance from the bottom of the sulcus to the brain hull. Surface area was defined as the total sulcal surface area. Mean width was calculated as the ratio of enclosed cerebrospinal fluid volume to sulcal surface area (Mangin, Rivière, Cachia, et al. 2004; Fabrizio Pizzagalli et al. 2016, 2020). Global measures for each of the four sulcal measurements, including global width, global depth, global surface area, and global length, were calculated for each subject by summing the values across all individual sulci.

Quality Control

Prior to extracting the morphometric properties of sulci, a thorough quality control process was conducted, following BrainVISA recommendations and the ENIGMA‐Sulci protocol. This involved a visual inspection of the labeling for all 123 sulci in each subject by two independent reviewers using the Anatomist toolbox (https://brainvisa.info/web/anatomist.html).

In cases where a sulcus was not accurately identified by the automated algorithm, its classification was manually adjusted based on the BrainVISA atlas. The Morphologist segmentation framework is designed to detect up to five types of elementary structures within a cortical fold, with each sulcus typically comprising a collection of nodes. These nodes are assigned to specific sulci using a probability map. To ensure precise identification, adjustments were made at the level of individual nodes.

Finally, due to the significant inter‐subject variability in sulcal morphology, some minor sulci could not be consistently identified across all subjects. As a result, these sulci were underrepresented in the dataset but were retained for subsequent analyses. Notably, these sulci, along with those that frequently required manual re‐labeling, largely aligned with those deemed unreliable in a prior study. The previous study utilized four independent test–retest datasets and reported high reliability across the brain, with intraclass correlation (ICC) interquartile ranges between 0.65 and 0.85 (Fabrizio Pizzagalli et al. 2020). ICC was used as a measure of reliability, evaluating the consistency of cortical fold measurements, with values typically categorized as follows: below 0.4 as “poor” reproducibility, 0.4–0.75 as “fair to good”, and above 0.75 as “excellent”. For the current study, we included a total of 205 sulcal features (out of a total of 492), including 50 width, 70 mean depth, 59 surface area, and 26 length, with ICC values greater than 0.75.

Statistical Analyses

We examined differences in sulcal measurements between the ALS and control groups using linear mixed‐effects models, including sex, age, handedness, and intracranial volume, as measured by FreeSurfer, as fixed effects, with recruiting site as a random effect. To explore the association between sulcal measurements and ALS clinical characteristics, we used linear mixed‐effects models, adding education as an additional fixed effect. Additionally, to assess differences in the trends of associations between the two groups, we introduced diagnosis as an interaction term in our models. For subgroup analyses, similar models were utilized.

For longitudinal analyses, mixed‐effects models were employed to assess changes in sulcal measures over time, incorporating the same covariates as in baseline models and including subject as a random effect. Diagnosis was again introduced as an interaction term to evaluate group‐specific differences in longitudinal trajectories. For subgroup analyses, similar modeling strategies were applied to assess differences in sulcal changes across subgroups over time. To investigate associations between sulcal measures and clinical characteristics across timepoints while accounting for the repeated‐measures structure, we used repeated measures correlation via the “rmcorr” R package. This approach estimates within‐subject correlations between two continuous variables across multiple observations, effectively controlling for inter‐individual variability.

We implemented the sulcal morphometry visualization tool to map and display group‐level and clinical associations across the cortical sulci (Shafie and Pizzagalli 2025). To account for multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini‐Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR), and FDR < 0.05 was set for statistical significance. All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.3) and RStudio (version 2024.12.1) software.

Results

Baseline

Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

The study included 222 ALS patients and 194 HC. Clinical characteristics and motor and cognitive assessments of ALS, including ALSFRS‐R, UMN burden, and ECAS scores, are presented in Table 1.

Comparison: ALS vs. HC

Analyses of cortical morphometry revealed significant differences in sulcal measures between ALS patients and HC, with notable findings, after correction for multiple comparisons, in 19 sulcal measures across width, depth, length, and surface area characteristics (Figure 2, Table S1). These sulcal measures were selected for further subgroup and correlation analysis at baseline.

(A) Sulcal phenotypes compared between ALS and HC groups at baseline. P‐values are mapped along typical sulcal trajectories between gyri, with darker red indicating stronger statistical significance. (B) Sulcal phenotypes showing altered measures in ALS patients compared to HC at baseline, including width, mean depth, surface average, and length. Mean values and standard deviations are displayed. Fifteen sulci showed greater width in ALS, two showed reduced mean depth, one showed reduced surface average, and one showed increased length (all FDR < 0.05).

Among these, the central sulcus (CS) stood out with the most prominent differences. Specifically, its width measures in both the right and left CS were higher in ALS patients (FDR < 0.001, d = 0.761 and 0.760, respectively), while the left CS mean depth was lower (FDR = 0.015, d = −0.315). Additionally, the left CS surface area was reduced in ALS compared to HC (FDR = 0.007, d = −0.235).

Precentral and frontal regions demonstrated a substantial number of findings, with wider width in ALS patients. These included the left and right superior precentral sulcus width (FDR = 0.010, d = 0.558 and FDR = 0.023, d = 0.533, respectively) and right intermediate precentral sulcus width (FDR = 0.023, d = 0.467). Additionally, the left intermediate frontal sulcus (FDR = 0.007, d = 0.520), left median frontal sulcus (FDR = 0.024, d = 0.366), and left superior frontal sulcus (FDR = 0.027, d = 0.328) showed higher width in the ALS group. In the mesial frontal area, the right calloso‐marginal posterior fissure (FDR = 0.007, d = 0.520) exhibited a higher width measure in the ALS group.

The lateral fissures also showed notable differences, with wider width in ALS patients, including the right and left posterior lateral fissure (FDR = 0.010, d = 0.561 and FDR = 0.024, d = 0.527, respectively), right central Sylvian sulcus (FDR = 0.012, d = 0.609), and right retro‐central transverse ramus of the lateral fissure (FDR = 0.015, d = 0.523).

Parietal regions displayed variability, with wider width and reduced depth in ALS. Differences were observed in the width of the right superior postcentral intraparietal superior sulcus (FDR = 0.017, d = 0.534) and the mean depth of the left superior parietal sulcus (FDR = 0.038, d = −0.245).

Posterior regions also exhibited alterations, with wider width and increased sulcal length in ALS patients. These included the width of the right parieto‐occipital fissure (FDR = 0.015, d = 0.515) and the length of the left anterior occipito‐temporal lateral sulcus (FDR = 0.028, d = 0.470).

Comparison: Subgroups

Analyses of selected sulcal measures across disease severity groups identified four significant findings before correction for multiple comparisons (Figure S1). Specifically, right CS width was greater in the high UMN burden group (p = 0.021, Cohens's d = 0.223), right parieto‐occipital fissure width was greater in the fast‐progressing group (p = 0.025, Cohens's d = 0.251), and right superior precentral sulcus width was greater in the low ECAS Total Score group (p = 0.042, Cohens's d = −0.350). However, none of these findings remained significant after applying FDR correction.

Correlations With Clinical Scores in ALS Group

After applying FDR correction, only one negative association was identified between right width of parieto‐occipital fissure and ALSFRS‐R Total Score (FDR = 0.029, β = −0.289). We also examined the correlations between sulcal measures and specific subdomains of clinical scores (Table S3). Longitudinal

The longitudinal analysis included 83 ALS patients and 100 HC, each of whom had assessments at all three time points: baseline, 4 months, and 8 months.

Comparison: ALS vs. HC

Analyses of alterations in sulcal measures from baseline to follow‐ups in the ALS group revealed four significant findings after FDR correction. Specifically, the width of the right and left CS increased over time (FDR < 0.001, f = 0.47 and 0.42, respectively), while the mean depth of the left CS decreased (FDR = 0.017, f = 0.34) and the surface area of the right CS decreased (FDR = 0.047, f = 0.30). In contrast, no changes were observed in the HC group. When introducing diagnosis as an interaction term to examine group differences in longitudinal trends, differences were observed in these sulcal measures (p < 0.05, f = 0.24, 0.19, 0.19, 0.17, respectively, Figure 4).

To examine whether the trajectory of changes in selected sulcal measures differed based on disease severity over time, we introduced an interaction term for DPR, UMN, and ECAS groups in the longitudinal analysis. Global Measures

At baseline, a comparison of global sulcal measures between ALS patients and HC revealed a difference in global width (FDR < 0.001, d = 0.750), with ALS patients exhibiting greater sulcal width compared to HC. No differences were observed for other global measures (all FDR > 0.05). At baseline, correlation analyses between global sulcal measures and clinical characteristics in the ALS group revealed no associations after correction for multiple analyses (Tables S7 and S8).

Longitudinal analyses revealed changes in all four global sulcal measures in the ALS group over time. The most pronounced alterations were observed in global width, which showed an increase (FDR < 0.001, f = 0.37), and global surface area, which exhibited a decrease (FDR < 0.001, f = 0.39) through follow‐ups. Additionally, decreases were observed in global length (FDR = 0.017, f = 0.24) and global mean depth (FDR = 0.042, f = 0.21). In contrast, no longitudinal changes were observed in the HC group (all FDR > 0.05). Analyses of longitudinal correlations between changes in global measurements and clinical scores in the ALS group revealed multiple associations after FDR correction for multiple comparisons (Tables S9 and S10).

Cortical Thickness

At baseline, ALS patients showed significant cortical thinning (FDR < 0.001) across motor and frontal regions (Figure S2A–C). The strongest effects were observed in the bilateral precentral gyrus (right: d = −0.74; left: d = −0.66) and the bilateral superior frontal cortex (d = −0.54 and −0.53). Across all regions, cortical thickness was consistently reduced in ALS patients compared with HC. All sulcal and cortical measures with medium to high effect sizes (d > 0.5) are shown in Figure S2D.

Longitudinally, no cortical region showed significant thinning within the ALS group after FDR correction (FDR > 0.05). Similarly, no longitudinal changes reached significance in the HC group. The diagnosis × time interaction was also non‐significant across all regions, indicating that longitudinal cortical thinning did not differ between ALS and HC (Figure S2B).

Discussion

In this study, we provide the first whole‐brain mapping of sulcal morphometry in ALS, characterizing alterations, their progression, and clinical associations. ALS patients exhibited widespread sulcal changes, with the CS emerging as the most prominently affected structure, showing bilateral widening and reduced depth that closely tracked ALSFRS‐R decline. Longitudinally, the CS was also the most sensitive sulcus to progressive alterations. These findings are consistent with the known vulnerability of UMNs in the precentral gyrus adjacent to the CS and align with prior imaging studies reporting cortical thinning, reduced complexity (Chen et al. 2020; Agosta et al. 2012), and volumetric atrophy (Shen et al. 2016; Chen, Zhou, et al. 2018) in the motor strip, supporting the hypothesis that degeneration begins in the motor cortex before extending to extra motor regions (Brettschneider et al. 2013).

The cortical morphometry findings closely align with the sulcal alterations observed in our study, reinforcing a coherent pattern of structural degeneration in ALS. At baseline, both modalities point toward prominent involvement of the motor and frontal areas, where we observed increasing sulcal width alongside decreasing cortical thickness, two changes that anatomically and mechanistically complement each other. Notably, sulcal width differences, particularly in the CS, appeared slightly clearer at baseline when compared directly with cortical thickness alterations in the adjacent precentral gyrus, suggesting that sulcal morphology may capture certain aspects of structural degeneration with higher contrast. This pattern was consistent with previous studies reporting, in particular, thinning of the precentral gyrus among ALS patients (Walhout et al. 2015; Schuster et al. 2014). In addition, the sulcal map also revealed a more spatially extensive pattern of baseline alterations, with some frontal and temporal regions already showing detectable changes, whereas prior cortical thickness studies generally report these non‐motor areas as being affected later in the disease course (Tahedl et al. 2021).

Consistent with previous studies, we observed no significant further thinning of the precentral gyrus over time, supporting the notion that motor‐cortex cortical thinning is an early or plateaued phenomenon in ALS (Schuster et al. 2014; Walhout et al. 2015). By contrast, sulcal measures, particularly within the CS, showed more pronounced longitudinal progression. This divergence indicates that sulcal morphology may be more sensitive to short‐term structural changes, likely because sulcal features capture a broader set of influences beyond cortical thinning alone. Sulcal remodeling in ALS might be the visible product of converging neurobiological and biomechanical cascades that act on the mature folding architecture of the human cortex (Llinares‐Benadero and Borrell 2019), which could lead to widened sulci (Richman et al. 1975; Tallinen et al. 2016). An additional contributing factor may be progressive white‐matter atrophy, which would not be detected through cortical thickness measures but could nonetheless alter sulcal geometry through subcortical degeneration (Mangin, Rivière, Cachia, et al. 2004). The early degeneration of long‐range fibers in ALS, especially the corticospinal tract and fronto‐temporal projections, may still influence sulcal pattern (Xu et al. 2010). This could also explain that regions such as the CS, where multiple fiber systems converge, are particularly vulnerable to these subtle shifts, displaying the earliest morphometric changes. Pathological proteins propagate along structural and functional networks, sequentially engaging premotor, prefrontal and parietal hubs (Thome et al. 2022). This ordered spread mirrors our longitudinal MRI findings in which sulcal widening extends from peri‐rolandic fissures to the superior frontal and intraparietal sulci, suggesting that sulcal metrics capture the spatiotemporal footprint of network‐level degeneration rather than isolated regional atrophy. Although the motor cortex often shows the earliest and most severe involvement, extra motor cortical areas can exhibit subtle but progressive morphological changes over time, reflecting the multiregional and multisystem nature of ALS (Trojsi et al. 2012).

Given that sulcal and cortical measures differ in their genetic and developmental underpinnings, the enhanced longitudinal sensitivity of sulcal features may offer an advantage for detecting early or evolving pathology (Fabrizio Pizzagalli et al. 2020; Sun et al. 2022). Based on current evidence, measures of sulcal morphometry could serve as sensitive markers of neuropsychiatric disorders, and their variations appear partly independent of those observed in cortical thickness or surface area (Panizzon et al. 2009; Sun et al. 2022). These characteristics highlight sulcal morphometry as a promising tool for detection of subtle neurodegenerative changes and underscore its potential utility in preclinical or prodromal stages of ALS.

A key contribution of this study is distinguishing between sulcal measures, width, depth, surface area, and length, each capturing different aspects of cortical change. Sulcal width emerged as particularly sensitive in ALS, with several alterations linked exclusively to this measure, potentially reflecting cortical atrophy or white matter reorganization. Depth, in contrast, reflects the extent of sulcal invagination and may be influenced differently by cortical thinning or remodeling (Cykowski et al. 2007; Reese McKay et al. 2013). Assessing these measures together offers a more comprehensive view of neurodegenerative processes and how structural degeneration manifests in ALS.

The CS, which separates the primary motor from the primary somatosensory cortex, emerged as the most consistently affected sulcus in ALS, reflecting the hallmark involvement of UMN degeneration, which usually serves as a reliable landmark for identifying the precentral gyrus under both normal and pathological conditions (Pizzagalli et al. 2013). Alterations in surrounding regions, frequently reported in the literature, may further contribute to bilateral CS morphological changes (Chen et al. 2020; Agosta et al. 2012). Our findings not only highlighted distinct CS morphometric alterations in ALS but also revealed robust associations with clinical measures, particularly ALSFRS‐R. This strong involvement of the CS is consistent with prior MRI studies showing pronounced structural changes around the motor strip in ALS, including reduced gray matter volume in the precentral gyrus and CS operculum, reported in meta‐analyses of VBM studies (Shen et al. 2016; Chen, Zhou, et al. 2018) as well as cortical thinning and reduced gyrification in the precentral and paracentral cortices among asymptomatic C9orf72 carriers. The accelerated thinning observed longitudinally in these regions suggests that structural alterations may precede symptom onset, reinforcing the CS as a key target for investigating neurodegenerative processes and potential imaging biomarkers in ALS (Veenhuijzen et al. 2023).

Morphological changes in the CS also showed distinct clinical associations. At baseline, widening of the right CS correlated with ALSFRS‐R bulbar subscores, suggesting peri‐central alterations in orofacial regions linked to speech and swallowing impairments, while the left CS demonstrated broader associations across bulbar, breathing, and motor measures, including foot‐tapping and UMN scores. Depth and surface area of the left CS were further related to bulbar and UMN dysfunction, indicating that multiple sulcal dimensions (vertical [depth] and expansive [surface area]) capture different aspects of motor deterioration. Longitudinally, right CS width and surface area tracked ALSFRS‐R decline and foot‐tapping deficits. This continuity of right‐sided effects hints at a prominent role of the right hemisphere's motor regions in motor tasks and fine‐motor function. On the left, width and depth again emerged as sensitive markers of clinical decline, consistent with the dominant hemisphere's involvement in motor and integrative functions. Together, these findings emphasize that sulcal morphology, across width, depth, and surface area, captures complementary facets of motor dysfunction and progression in ALS, with clear laterality patterns.

Multiple frontal sulci, including the bilateral superior and right intermediate precentral sulci anterior to the CS, exhibited increased width in ALS, implicating premotor regions involved in motor planning and coordination. These alterations are consistent with evidence that ALS extends beyond the primary motor cortex to premotor and supplementary areas, related to higher‐order motor dysfunction (Trojsi et al. 2012; Zejlon et al. 2024). Preliminary correlations of the superior precentral sulcus with ECAS scores suggest links to extra‐motor cognitive involvement via frontostriatal networks (Castelnovo et al. 2020). This is also consistent with previous studies that reported decreased cortical complexity and reduced cortical thickness in the bilateral precentral gyrus, which was also correlated with disease progression (Chen et al. 2020; Agosta et al. 2012). Additional widening of the superior, median, and intermediate frontal sulci further implicates executive control circuits, with exploratory associations to tapping performance pointing to deficits in fine‐motor and executive‐motor integration.

Multiple sulcal alterations in the parietal lobe highlight broader involvement of sensorimotor and visuospatial networks in ALS. Posterior to the CS, widening of the right superior postcentral intraparietal sulcus, correlated to tapping performance, suggests reorganization of somatosensory regions, consistent with prior reports of reduced cortical complexity and gyrification in the postcentral gyrus (Chen et al. 2020; van Veenhuijzen et al. 2023). Depth alterations in the left superior parietal sulcus further support the role of parietal–frontal circuits in motor planning and sensory integration (Bharti et al. 2022; Castelnovo et al. 2020). Notably, widening of the left parieto‐occipital fissure correlated with ALSFRS‐R and tapping scores and differentiated certain DPR groups, pointing to heterogeneity in cortical changes and disease trajectories; however, it remains unclear whether these changes are primary or secondary to other pathological processes in ALS. Taken together, these findings illustrate multifaceted parietal involvement, extending beyond the classical focus on motor pathways to include sensorimotor and spatial‐processing circuits in ALS. These findings align with previous evidence of parietal and occipital thinning, reduced gyrification, and decreased functional connectivity in ALS (Agosta et al. 2012; Chen et al. 2020; Ai et al. 2023), as well as early parieto‐occipital alterations in asymptomatic C9orf72 carriers, underscoring involvement beyond the motor strip (Veenhuijzen et al. 2023).

The right lateral (Sylvian) fissure and its posterior and retro‐central segments showed significant widening in ALS with clinical correlations. The posterior fissure, situated between the parietal and temporal lobes, was associated with ECAS total and sub scores, implicating cognitive and language networks, while the retro‐central transverse ramus and central Sylvian sulcus correlated with ALSFRS‐R measures, suggesting motor and bulbar involvement. Given the fissure's role in speech and auditory processing, these changes likely reflect cortical thinning in peri‐sylvian regions underlying language, bulbar, and respiratory control, consistent with prior reports of reduced cortical thickness and altered connectivity in superior temporal regions (Loewe et al. 2017; Veenhuijzen et al. 2023). Additionally, decreased cortical complexity of the cingulate cortex has been demonstrated in a previous study, which may align with the observed alterations in sulcal morphometry within the internal frontal regions (Chen et al. 2020). In addition, the broader spatial extent of baseline sulcal alterations, extending into non‐motor regions that typically show cortical thinning only later in the disease, suggests that sulcal features may capture signs of disease progression earlier than cortical thickness measures (Walhout et al. 2015; Schuster et al. 2014).

A number of limitations should be acknowledged. Despite a relatively large ALS cohort, the overall sample size may have limited statistical power for detecting subtle effects or subgroup distinctions. The short follow‐up interval, while sufficient to reveal sulcal changes, may have restricted the full characterization of longitudinal trajectories, and floor effects or limited sensitivity cannot be excluded. We did not stratify by onset type or genetics, which may reveal mutation‐specific neuroimaging patterns (Veenhuijzen et al. 2023; Balendra and Isaacs 2018); this will require datasets with genetic data, as CALSNIC does not include such information. Finally, our longitudinal sample likely reflects a bias toward slower‐progressing patients, as only individuals able to tolerate three MRI sessions were included. Patients with severe bulbar symptoms, respiratory compromise, or behavioral changes are less likely to complete longitudinal scans and may therefore be underrepresented. Although this limits generalizability to the full ALS population, it is noteworthy that sulcal measures still detected change even within this milder subgroup. Future work should aim to include patients with a broader range of clinical severity.

Sulcal morphometry should be viewed as one component of a broader neuroimaging framework and interpreted in conjunction with complementary metrics, including functional MRI and other structural imaging approaches to more fully capture the multisystem nature of ALS‐related neurodegeneration. Beyond the cerebral cortex, recent studies have highlighted substantial cerebellar involvement in ALS (Bede et al. 2021; Tahedl et al. 2024), often accompanied by widening of major cerebellar fissures (Chipika et al. 2022), as well as marked subcortical (Chipika et al. 2020) and brainstem pathology (Tahedl et al. 2023). Such deep gray matter and brainstem changes, while clinically highly relevant, are less expected to manifest as alterations in cortical sulcal or surface‐based measures. Accordingly, although these non‐cortical structures were outside the scope of the present analysis, their involvement warrants further investigation in future multimodal studies.

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that sulcal morphometry, particularly the CS, is altered in ALS, which closely tracks motor function deterioration and may serve as quantifiable biomarkers of disease progression. These findings establish sulcal‐based metrics as promising neuroimaging biomarkers for early detection, prognosis, and patient stratification in ALS, thereby enabling more personalized treatment approaches.
