Management of Erythrodermic Psoriasis with Systemic Therapies: A Systematic Review
Authors: Luca Mastorino, Francesco Leo, Giada Frigatti, Nicole Macagno, Paolo Dapavo, Pietro Quaglino, Simone Ribero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundErythrodermic psoriasis (EP) is a severe and rare variant of psoriasis. Clinical features include scaling and erythema affecting more than 75% of body surface area, associated with systemic symptoms such as lymphadenopathy, arthralgia, fever, fatigue, dehydration, serum electrolyte disturbances, and tachycardia, making this condition a potentially life-threatening disease. Differential diagnosis can be challenging, encompasses atopic dermatitis, cutaneous adverse drug reaction, and advanced cutaneous lymphoma. Following a correct diagnostic framing, appropriate systemic treatment must be initiated. Unfortunately, there are no recent up-to-date guidelines and standardized treatment options for EP are still lacking.ObjectiveTo review the current reported systemic treatment options for EP.MethodsThis systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines, and based on a search in MEDLINE, PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library for articles in English from first available publication to 9 November 2024.ResultsIn all, 145 studies were included in the review. Case reports and case series are the main available work, reporting heterogeneous outcomes and effectiveness with nonbiologic and biologic systemic agents. Among non-biologic systemic treatments, methotrexate and cyclosporin are the most widely reported as treatment for EP, showing clinical response in over 60% of cases, with cyclosporine offering a faster onset of action and being suitable for acute management. Available randomized controlled trials include patients with EP treated with etretinate, infliximab, certolizumab-pegol (CZP), Ixekizumab, guselkumab, risankizumab, and deucravacitinib. However, these trials were not specifically designed for erythrodermic psoriasis, and the sample size of EP patients included is limited, resulting in reduced statistical power and limiting the reliability of the findings. Among TNF-α inhibitors, infliximab is the most reported agent, with data on 103 patients. Certolizumab pegol (CZP) also showed promising results, with PASI 75 achieved in over 80% of patients at 52 weeks. A retrospective analysis comparing infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept, ustekinumab, and efalizumab found TNF-α inhibitors to be superior to other biologic classes. Regarding IL-17 inhibitors, secukinumab is the second most frequently studied biologic, with 93 patients reported. It demonstrated rapid efficacy, achieving PASI 75 in more than 80% of patients by week 8. A head-to-head comparison with ixekizumab showed comparable outcomes. Among IL-23 inhibitors, risankizumab led to PASI 90 in over 75% of patients at week 16, suggesting high efficacy despite more limited data.ConclusionsNon-biologic systemic drugs appear to be a rational first-line therapy, with cyclosporine showing good results in managing the acute phase and methotrexate being effective in maintaining remission. In the case of contraindications or treatment failure of traditional systemic therapies, among biologic drugs, the rapidity of action, safety, and limited evidence of efficacy are in favor of IL-17 inhibitors and risankizumab. However, the findings we report are limited by the evidence available in current literature, which is characterized by low statistical power.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40257-025-00977-1.

Key Points

Introduction

Psoriasis is a prevalent chronic inflammatory disease that is primarily driven by tumor necrosis factor (TNF) signaling, interleukin (IL)-23 and the Th17 axis [1]. While plaque psoriasis represents the most common phenotype, approximately 1–2.25% of patients progress to EP, a severe dermatologic emergency characterized by ≥ 75% body surface area involvement with confluent erythema, exfoliative scaling, and systemic manifestations [2]. EP typically affects males more than females (3:1 ratio) at a median age of 54 years, often progressing rapidly from pre-existing psoriasis plaques [3]. In some cases, the spontaneous appearance of EP has also been reported [2]. The erythroderma usually manifests clinically as widespread erythema, followed by exfoliative scaling over the subsequent 2–6 days, and is also associated hair loss and nail dystrophy [2, 4]. The diagnostic complexity of EP stems from its clinical overlap with other erythrodermic conditions. Critical differential diagnoses include pityriasis rubra pilaris, severe cases of atopic dermatitis, systematized or generalized drug eruptions, and the erythrodermic stage of cutaneous lymphoma such as stage IV mycosis fungoides or Sezary' syndrome, all sharing features of systemic inflammation yet requiring divergent therapeutic approaches [2, 5, 6]. Overall, 25% of cases of erythroderma are caused by psoriasis [2].

Differentiating between EP and erythrodermic atopic dermatitis (EAD) based solely on histopathology during erythrodermic exacerbations can pose significant challenges owing to their similar manifestations and overlapping histologic changes [1]. Moy et al. reported the presence of similar immune cellular infiltrates (Th1, Th2, Th17, and Th22 cells) in lesions from patients with EP or EAD [7]. Significant Th1/Th17 skewing is observed in patients with EP, indicating an inflammation polarization like that seen in plaque psoriasis. Increases in several Th2 markers (CCL11/MCP3/MCP4) in patients with EP compared with health controls has also been also described. However, this histological overlap underscores the critical role of clinical context, EP often presents with a history of abrupt drug withdrawal or a history of psoriasis vulgaris, while EAD is preceded by childhood eczema and persistent pruritus [1].

EP can be challenging to treat owing to the extensive skin barrier damage and immune dysregulation, and patients with EP may face a spectrum of potentially fatal systemic complications, bearing a profound burden on their quality of life [1, 2]. EP usually develops in patients with scarce control of psoriatic disease (almost 80% of cases) [2, 3]. However, numerous factors such as drugs (e.g., lithium, ACE inhibitors and interferon), systemic infection, and abrupt withdrawal of systemic medications (e.g., corticosteroids) may trigger EP [8]. A triggering factor is described in more than 50% of cases [10].

Thermoregulatory disturbances, reactive lymphadenopathy and superimposed bacterial infections are common sequelae. Hypovolemia, high-output cardiac failure, anemia, high incidence rates of serum electrolyte disturbances (hypokalemia, hypocalcemia, hyposodemia, and hypophosphatemia), and acute respiratory distress syndrome are rare and severe complications [3]. Patients often complain of fever, chills, fatigue, and malaise [4, 5, 8, 9].

There is a wide range of severity in patients with EP, which is characterized by a rapid course of the disease requiring aggressive systemic therapy to achieve disease control, alternated with more prolonged course of chronic erythroderma affected by frequent relapses [2].

There is no high-quality scientific evidence and no shared guidelines and consensus on which to base treatment recommendations for EP. Randomized controlled trials (RCT) dedicated to EP are not available, and the disease is usually investigated in pair with vulgar psoriasis or generalized pustular psoriasis mainly in Japanese populations [11–14]. Moreover, head-to-head studies are scarce and limited to real-life experience [9]. This lack of literature is attributed to the low incidence of this form of psoriasis [2].

Treatment should be dictated by the severity of disease at time of presentation and the patient’s comorbidities [2]. According to the National Psoriasis Foundation guidelines (NPF GLs), dating to 2010, and the more recent European guidelines [158], cyclosporine and infliximab appear to be the most rapidly acting agents for the treatment of EP; vitamin-derivates, such as acitretin and methotrexate may be appropriate first-line choices, although they usually work more slowly [2]. However, the large clinical variability in patients presenting with this form of psoriasis historically often led to other treatment modalities such as combination therapy [2].

In the last 14 years many new systemic treatments, such as biologics, have been demonstrated to be efficacious and safe in the treatment of psoriasis, also in a severe form such as EP, becoming a promising first line option [9].

The aim of our study was to review current literature on the use of systemic and biologic drugs for the treatment of EP to offer a wide and comprehensive perspective of possible EP management.

Methods

A comprehensive systematic review was performed, according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. A search on MEDLINE, PubMed, Scopus, and Cochrane Library was conducted using the combination of the following keywords and medical subject heading (MeSH) terms: “erythrodermic psoriasis,” “erythroderma” AND “methotrexate,” “acitretin,” “etretinate,” “cyclosporin,” “corticosteroids,” “systemic treatments,” “phototherapy” “adalimumab,” “etanercept,” “certolizumab,” “certolizumab pegol,” “bimekizumab,” “infliximab,” “golimumab,” “efalizumab,” “secukinumab,” “ixekizumab,” “brodalumab,” “ustekinumab,” “risankizumab,” “tildrakizumab,” “guselkumab,” “dupilumab,” “tralokinumab,” “lebrikizumab,” “deucravacitinib,” “zasocitinib,” “upadacitinib,” “abrocitinib,” “baricitinib,” “tofacitinib,” “dimethyl fumarate,” and “apremilast,” up to 9 November 2024. Only manuscripts in English were considered, and owing to the comprehensive and quantitative nature of the present SR no restriction related to article type was applied; case reports, letters to the editor, case series, cross‐sectional, RCTs, abstracts, and registry‐based studies were all included. Study selection was based on the use of at least one systemic drug to treat EP, independently of whether efficacy was reported; we included all systemic therapies listed in all articles to reduce publication bias. Two authors (NM and FL) independently assessed the risk of bias of each included study, in accordance with methods recommended by the National Institutes of Health Quality Assessment Tool for Case Series Studies. The authors resolved disagreements by consensus‐based discussion, and a third and fourth author (LM, GF) were consulted to resolve disagreements when required. In cases where the same sample was used for more than one study, all studies were reviewed to determine the extent to which there may be duplication in the reported outcomes between studies. The full text of selected studies was reviewed, and data on demographics, article type, number of reported cases, response to treatments were extracted from articles texts, tables, figures, and summarized in a descriptive table.

Results

In all, 2373 articles were identified based on search strings, and following removal of duplicates, publications in languages other than English, and literature not related to humans, a total of 672 records were selected. These were screened according to title and abstract and 483 were excluded because out of scope; 189 papers were assessed for eligibility by the authors, and finally, 145 articles were selected for inclusion in this review (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram

Given the extreme heterogeneity of the data, in particular the nonuniform use of efficacy outcomes, especially for older records, the authors decided to report efficacy by mean Psoriasis Area Severity Index (PASI), or achievement of PASI 50, PASI 75, PASI 90, or PASI 100 if reported or derivable from the texts. In the case of the absence of a clear and shared clinical reported outcome, the terms “Complete Response” (CR), “Good Response” (GR), “Partial Response” (PR), “Stable Response” (SR), and “Non-response” (NR) were used according to the descriptions in the publications. It needs to be noted that some studies are cited in multiple results sections, as patients may have received more than one sequential or concomitant treatment.

The study characteristics, clinical and safety outcomes of individual reports are detailed in Supplementary Table 1 (nonbiologic systemic treatments, including phototherapy and targeted small molecules), Supplementary Table 2 (biologic systemic treatments) and Supplementary Table 3 (anecdotal reports and combination therapies).

Methotrexate

Methotrexate is described as monotherapy in 17 studies, 11 case reports [15, 17, 20, 22, 24–27, 29–31], 4 case series [16, 19, 21, 28], and 2 cohort studies [18, 23], involving 66 patients of whom 76.5% were male, with a mean age of 41.2 years. The average dose in the adult population was variable where reported between 7.5 and 25 mg, and in the pediatric population from 0.2 to 0.4 mg/kg [16–19, 21, 22, 24, 30]. A total of 35 patients achieved a complete response after a follow-up ranging from 5 to 38 months [18, 21, 23]. Overall, seven patients had a GR and six patients had a PR with an follow up of 32.8 months [16–18, 23, 27]. In total, three patients achieved a SR [19, 26, 29] and nine patients had NR within a treatment time from 2 weeks to 5 years [18, 20–22, 24, 25, 30]. The cohort study by Haustein et al. is the one with the highest reported CR in 78% of treated patients, GR in 17%, and PR in 2% of 36 patients, and all patients showed at least a minimal response with a clinically significant therapeutic benefit; in 48 (31%) cases skin lesions were cleared within 3–5 months and remained stable [23]. The study by Kumar et al. in a case series on pediatric patients observed an SR of disease in all three patients analyzed in a time-varying between 7 and 9 months, with subsequent achievement of CR in 14.8 months in two of the three patients; treatment was continued up to a maximum follow up of 46 months [19]. Overall, considering CR and GR, treatment was effective in 70% of patients. The only study reporting PASI is that by Korstanje et al., with PASI 75 being achieved [17]. The fastest CR was obtained in the study by Kujpers et al. in which one of the two patients observed showed recovery after 5 months of treatment at a dose of 7.5 mg/m2 [21]. Considering safety, there was one case of pulmonary embolism with subsequent patient death [21], one case of liver fibrosis [23], and one case of breast cancer [25]. In the study by Collins et al. 1992 two patients failed to respond to treatment for immediate cessation following the occurrence of two adverse events (AEs) [18].

Etretinate

Etretinate is reported in the treatment of EP in eight publications, five case reports [25, 35–38], one case series [34], one cohort study [32], and one RCT [33], with a total of 25 patients described, 80% male, with a mean age of 57.6 years. The reported dose ranged from 0.8 mg/kg/day to 100 mg/day. CR was described in 12 patients with a total follow up ranging from 8 months to 2.5 years, while 3 patients had a GR in 8 months [32–34]. Four patients did not achieve a response within a variable time of 10–20 days [25, 35, 36, 38]. Two severe AEs were reported: one fulminant hepatitis [36] and one bone bridge [37].

Acitretin

Acitretin was used as a treatment for EP in 11 articles: 10 case reports [24, 25, 29, 31, 39–43, 45] and 1 cohort study [44], involving a total of 13 patients, 58.3% male, with a mean age of 43.6 years. The reported dose ranged from 0.2 mg/kg/day to 0.75/mg/kg/day. CR was reported by a pediatric EP patient from birth in the study by Selleras et al. after about 3 months of treatment, with a maximum follow up of 7 years [39]. GR was reported by the study of Valenzuela et al. in one patient after 8 weeks of treatment [45]. PR was observed in one patient in the study of Jaffer et al. after 6 months of treatment [41]. NR was reported by 8 studies with a total of 10 patients (76.9%), after a reported maximum treatment time of 1 month [24, 25, 31, 38–40, 42–44]. Only one AE leading to discontinuation of the drug was reported in the study of Quinn et al., namely hypertriglyceridemia [24].

Cyclosporin

Cyclosporin was used as a treatment for EP in 21 articles: 14 case reports [20, 24, 25, 29, 40, 42, 43, 47, 48, 50, 54–57] 5 case series [35, 46, 49, 52, 63] and 2 cohort studies [44, 51] for a total of 64 patients, 66.7% male, with a mean age of 38 years. The doses used ranged from 2 to 8.5 mg/kg/day, with a maximum follow up of 1 year and a tendency to relapse after dose-reduction and discontinuation. CR was reported by 31 patients in a period ranging from 20 days to 1 year [40, 42, 43, 46–48, 50, 51, 56] GR by 10 (9 PASI 70) [29, 44, 53–55, 57] and NR by 10 in a period ranging from 7 days to 8 weeks [20, 24, 25, 35, 44, 56]. In the first cases reported in the literature, CR was usually reported in the first 3 weeks of treatment [46, 47, 50], while in some cases the treatment took longer to achieve full efficacy, from 6 weeks to 1 year [43, 48]. The only cohort study that analyzed a substantial number of patients is the SIMPSO study with 33 patients and an overall efficacy, with complete response (CR) achieved in 67% of cases and PASI 70 in 27%, within a median time of 2–4 months [51]. Redigheri et al. reported a rapid relapse after discontinuation of treatment, and Fraga et al. an improvement in a pediatric patient with systemic symptoms but not involving the skin [29, 42]. Regarding AEs, there was one case of hypertension and nephrotoxicity [55], one case of fever with worsening skin picture [35], one case of tremor associated with creatinine elevation [20]. In the SIMPSO multicenter cohort study, out of 33 patients, 15 reported adverse events. Treatment was discontinued in six patients: one due to hypertension, one due to cerebrovascular disorders and four due to poor compliance [51]. The treatment was also shown to be safe and did not affect the oncologic status during the study period in a patient with breast cancer, as reported in the study by Sutthipisal et al. [47]. In the pediatric study by Dogra et al. the lack of response led to the addition of acitretin in two of four patients and methotrexate in one of four patients, in the absence of further information [53].

Phototherapy

In total, five studies reported phototherapy as treatment for EP, one heliotherapy [60], two nbUVB [29, 42], and two PUVA [58, 59], in three cohort studies and two case reports for a total of 22 patients [29, 42, 58–60]. Demographic data are available in only two studies on two patients, both females and aged 83 and 7 years [29, 42]. Four studies reported NR even after 30 treatments [29, 42, 58, 59], while Snellman et al. reported one PR in 15 patients treated with heliotherapy [60]. Two cases of clinical worsening were reported during treatment [29, 60].

Targeted Small Molecule Drugs

Apremilast

Apremilast at the approved dose for plaque psoriasis was used in five patients in five articles: four case reports and one patient in a case series [61–64]. The mean age was 62 years with 75% males. Overall, all cases had considerable treatment efficacy with four of five patients showing at least one GR [62–64]. Arcilla et al. reported one PR [61], Megna et al. a CR [64], and another study by the same author reported a progressive response from PASI 75 to PASI 90 within 16–22 weeks [62]. Papadavid et al. reported reaching PASI 90 and Krishnamoorthy et al. PASI 100 after 20 days and 10 weeks of treatment, respectively [62, 63]. No AEs were reported during treatment [61–64].

Deucravacitinib

Deucravacitnib was recently tested in a RCT and a cohort study, both from Japan, involving a total of ten patients [11, 65]. Data are available for the RCT of Imafuku et al. and in this study the mean age was 46.5 years and all were males. Of these, three (37.5%) achieved PASI 75, one (12.5%) achieved PASI 100, and four (50%) achieved an sPGA score of 0 or 1 after 52 weeks of treatment, with no adverse events reported [11].

Infliximab

Infliximab was evaluated as treatment for EP in 16 studies: 8 case reports [24, 25, 30, 38, 66, 68, 70, 71, 75, 77], 4 cohort studies [72–74, 78], 3 case series [67, 70, 71], and 1 RCT [76], involving a total of 103 patients, 67.5% male, with a mean age of 50.2 years. The reported dose ranged from 2.7 to 10 mg/kg per administration with follow up varying from 12 weeks to 11 years. The five patients from the randomized controlled trial [76] reported a mean reduction in PASI scores of 61.2% after 4 weeks, with continued improvement and a sustained response through the final follow-up at 40 weeks (mean PASI reduction of 87.5%). Three patients showed CR, of which in one case after the first infusion [66], in another after 40 weeks [75], and a generic GR in 18 patients after 4 to 52 weeks [24, 30, 67, 68, 72, 76]. Pulhalon et al. reported PASI 90 achievement in 40% of patients, and PASI 75 in 60% (temporal data are not reported) [69]; both patients reported by Yip et al. achieved PASI 75 [70]. Viguier et al. reported a progressive response from 33.3 to 48% among patients achieving PASI 75 at 4 and 14 weeks, respectively [73]. Torii et al. similarly reported attainment of PASI 75 in 64.3% of patients and PASI 90 in 45.2% within 12 weeks of treatment [74]. Rongioletti et al. documented PR after 4 weeks of treatment [25], and only Santos-Juanes et al. reported NR after 14 weeks [71].

Considering the studies with the largest number of patients, the treatment showed efficacy in 48–65% of patients [67, 72, 74, 76]. In the cohort study by Torii et al., 87.5% of patients had AEs [72], while in another study the same author reported that 9 of 45 patients had AEs, 5 specifically with infections [74]. Vacas et al. reported reactivation of a zoonosis (Chagas-Mazza disease) in one patient [77]. Torii et al. in the only RCT reported an infectious event in three of five patients [76].

Etanercept

Etanercept was reported in five studies including two case reports [25, 79], one case series [43] and two cohort studies [73, 78] on a total of 20 patients with a mean age 32.2 years, 64.3% male [25, 43, 73, 78, 79]. The dose used was the standard 50 mg/twice a week for 12 weeks, then 50 mg/week [73, 79]. In the cohort study by Esposito et al. a progressive response to treatment was seen with 80% achieving PASI 50 at 12 and 24 weeks, 50% PASI 75 at 12 weeks and 60% at 24 weeks, with mean PASI decreased from 39.1 to 5.1 [78]. In the cohort study of Viguier et al. half of patients achieved PASI 50 at 12 weeks and maintained it at 24 weeks [73]. Agarwal et al. reported that PASI 90 was achieved in 6 weeks in a patient with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infections [79]. Campione et al. observed no response after 10 weeks of treatment in 2 pediatric patients [43], and Rongioletti et al. reported a PR [25]. Regarding AEs, there was one case of urinary tract infection [78], one case of pruritus [78], and one case of upper airway infection [43].

Adalimumab

Overall, five studies have been published in which patients with EP were treated with adalimumab, comprising three case reports [80–82], one case series [71], and one cohort study [73], involving a total of 12 patients, 58% male, with a mean age of 37.6 years. Two patients achieved CR [80, 81], one within 3 weeks [80]; on the other hand, two patients showed no response after 6 and 8 weeks of treatment. [71, 82] Viguier et al. reported PASI 50 attainment in half of patients at 12 and 24 weeks, with a drug survival of 100% at 12 weeks and 57% at 24 weeks [73]. No major AEs were reported [71].

Certolizumab Pegol (CZP)

CZP was studied in the RCT dose finding study by Okubo et al. in 2022: seven patients took CZP 200 mg every 2 weeks, and 8 took CZP 400 mg every 2 weeks [13]. In the first group the mean age was 56 years and all patients were male, while in the second group the mean age was 47.8 years, with 75% males [13]. The efficacy was progressive with 26.7% of the entire study cohort achieving PASI 75 and/or PASI 90 in 4 weeks, 64.3% PASI 75 and/or PASI 90 in 16 weeks, 83.3% PASI 75, and 75% PASI 90 in 52 weeks. No AEs were reported [13].

Golimumab

Golimumab was reported as the sole treatment for EP in the case report by Lee et al. on a 32-year-old male who achieved PASI 75 after 12 weeks [83].

Ustekinumab

A total of 14 articles described the use of ustekinumab in 51 patients with EP: 6 case reports [31, 85, 86, 89, 90, 93], 4 cohort studies [73, 88, 91, 94], and 4 case series [71, 84, 87, 92], with 72.9% males and a mean age of 49.3 years. The most frequently reported dose was 45 mg, while in a few cases 90 mg was used and 80 mg in only one case, with a reported maximum follow up of 114 weeks. A generic CR was reported in one patient [93], and one patients had GR in 2–4 weeks [31, 87, 89, 90]. PASI 75 was achieved by almost all patients [71, 84, 86–88, 91, 92]; in the study by Pescitelli et al. 66.6% achieved PASI 75 at 16 weeks and 86.3% at 28 weeks [91]. Santos-Juanes et al. reported a patient achieving PASI 75 within 4 weeks [71], and other studies reported a PASI 75 response between weeks 8 and 16 [84, 87, 88, 91, 92]. Castineiras et al. reported NR in a patient after 4 weeks, reaching PASI 75 at 8 weeks [86]. PASI 90 was reached within a timeframe ranging from 4 to 114 weeks [71, 85, 92], and in particular Pescitelli et al. reported a 68.2% achievement of PASI 90 at 28 weeks [91]. PASI 100 was reported within 12 weeks by Saraceno et al. in one of the two patients treated [87], and in 24 weeks in the study by Damiani et al. [94] The treatment appeared safe with only two cases of latent tuberculosis infection reported [92], and in one case treatment had to be stopped [90]. Pangilian et al. reported no safety issues in an patient with HIV who was previously treated with methotrexate and acitretin [31].

Secukinumab

Overall, 20 studies reported on secukinumab as a treatment for EP, comprising 11 case reports [31, 53, 97–99, 101, 102, 104, 105, 107, 109], four cohort studies [94, 106, 108, 110], and five case series [55, 95, 96, 100, 103] involving a total of 92 patients, with a mean age of 45.1 years and 84% male. The dose used was that for psoriasis with a maximum follow up of 3 years. CR was reported in two patients, in one within 12 weeks [102], and PASI 100 was observed in 8–12 weeks in two patients by Mugheddu et al. [55] Similarly to the patient followed in four other studies [99, 101, 105, 109], 80% achieved PASI 100 at 16 weeks in the study by Mateu-Puchades et al. [96], while in the study by Damiani et al. 5 of 13 patients showed complete resolution of disease after a median of 3 weeks of treatment [94]. Zhao et al. and Avallone et al. (including 15 patients with EP) reported a slower attainment of PASI 100, at 18 and 48 weeks respectively [106, 107]. PASI 90 and PASI 75 were attained in the majority of patients, from 38.5 to 82%, within a period of 4–8 weeks to 48 weeks [53, 55, 94, 96, 98, 103, 104, 106]. Reymundo et al. reported the attainment of absolute values ≤ 2 in more than half of patients observed within 1 year [110]. Apart from one case of oral candidiasis and acute urticaria, no major side effects were reported during treatment [94].

Ixekizumab

In total, 51 patients with EP treated with ixekizumab are reported in 11 publications: 3 case reports [113, 115, 117], 2 case series [100, 114], 3 cohort studies [106, 108, 116] and 1 RCT, discussed in two papers [14, 111]. The mean age was 53.6 years, 71.8% male, and treated for a reported maximum follow up of 10 months at standard doses. In the RCT by Saeki et al. in 2014, 100% of patients (n = 8) achieved PASI 75 by 12 weeks, 62.5% PASI 90, and 25% PASI 100. At 24 weeks, a minimal decrease in response was observed with 87.5% stable at PASI 75, but it should be considered that all patients had nail and scalp involvement, and the study also reported an improvement in the specific scores for difficult areas: NAPSI and PSSI [111]. In a follow up study by the same authors on eight patients, an improvement in PASI was observed, from 42.8 to 2.8 [112]. Lo et al. reported PASI 90 at 12 weeks in 11.1% of patients and PASI 75 in 55.6% [100], similar to that reported by Morita et al. (25% PASI 90 at 12 weeks, 25% at 24 weeks, 50% and 75% of PASI 75 at 12 and 20 weeks) [116]. In 4 patients PASI 100 was achieved in 8–12 weeks [111, 113, 115, 117]. Vinaixa Aranzazu et al. reported a stable CR at 9 months after discontinuation due to concomitant tumor [115]. Saeki et al. reported an infection in six of eight patients, and a hypersensitivity reaction in two [112]; Lo et al. reported one case of injection site reaction [100] and Morita et al. one case of seizure [116].

Brodalumab

Brodalumab was used for the treatment of EP in five publications: one case report [119], two case series [120, 121], one cohort study [108], and one open-label study [118], involving a total of 25 patients, all male and with a mean age of 51.3 years. The treatment was used at standard dose with a reported maximum follow up of 116 weeks [121]. In the open-label study by Yamasaki et al., a GR was observed in the 12 patients treated and mean PASI decreased from 36 to 2.02 at 52 weeks [118]. Megna et al. reported a very good response in two patients within 12 weeks, with PASI 90 and PASI 100 achieved [120]. Mota et al. in three patients reported that PASI 100 was achieved between 8 and 14 weeks of treatment, with a follow up of treatment and maintenance of response between 52 and 116 weeks [121]. Only one patient had a suboptimal response (PR) in the study by Bernardini et al. [119] Regarding AEs, 50% of the patients described by Yamasaki et al. had nasopharyngitis [118].

Bimekizumab

Three patients with EP were effectively treated with bimekizumab [122–124]. Valenti et al. showed in a 44-year-old man an achievement of PASI 90 in 4 weeks [122], Megna et al. an achievement of PASI 100 in 4 weeks [123], and Licata et al. reported a CR [124]. No AEs were reported.

Guselkumab

Guselkumab was used for EP in 34 patients in six studies: three cohort studies [108, 127, 129], one case report [126], one case series [128], and one RCT [125]. The mean age of patients was 51.8 years and 80% were male. In the RCT by Sano et al., which involved 11 patients, 10 of whom completed the final follow-up at 52 weeks, all patients (n = 10) achieved PASI 90 at week 52. Among them, six patients reported nasopharyngitis as AEs [125].

Megna et al. and Welsh et al. reported a CR in three patients in 16–20 weeks [126, 128]. Chiang et al. reported a progressive response with a PASI 75 achieved by 15.4% of 13 patients at 4 weeks and increasing to 53.9% at 20 weeks, with a slight decrease at 28 weeks (46.2%) [127]. In the RCT by Sano et al., 6 of 11 patients reported nasopharyngitis as AEs [125].

Risankizumab

Risankizumab has been described in five studies including two case reports [131, 132], one cohort study [108], one phase 1 clinical trial [130], and 1 RCT [12], involving a total of 23 patients (90.9% male), with a mean age of 60 years. Yamanaka et al. in their RCT reported PASI 90 attainment in 77.8% of patients treated at this time point, in the absence of AEs [12]. Overall, a clinical response was observed in all patients within 16 weeks [12, 131, 132].

Tildrakizumab

Megna et al. and Trevisan et al. reported the achievement of PASI 100 in two patients, both within 16 weeks, in the absence of AEs [133, 134].

Other Drugs

There are numerous anecdotal descriptions in literature of the use of nonconventional and nonbiological treatments for psoriasis such as corticosteroids [20], mycophenolate mofetil [135], hydroxyurea [24], 6-thioguanine [24], azathioprine [24], tacrolimus [25], bexarotene [25], thioguanine [25], carbamazepine [25], cyclophosphamide [25], alefacept [136], efalizumab [73], tripterygium glycoside [137], naltrexone [138], and Goeckerman therapy [139]. Most of these treatments are reported to be ineffective in the treatment of psoriasis [20, 24, 25, 66, 73, 135–139], and in particular both corticosteroids and azathioprine used for EP in the two reported experiences obtained NR [20, 25]. In the study by Geilen et al., mycophenolate mofetil was effective [135], as was combined UVB+coal tar therapy known as Goeckerman therapy [139], and low doses of naltrexone [138]. Wang et al. reported an improvement of skin lesions within 2 weeks with tripterygium glycoside [137].

Combined Therapies

There are numerous experiences with combined therapies in literature [16, 17, 20–22, 24, 29, 34, 35, 42, 55, 70, 90, 93, 119, 140–155]. The most commonly used treatments involve combining traditional systemic drugs, such as methotrexate and cyclosporine, with other systemic, topical, or phototherapy treatments [16, 17, 21, 34, 35, 70, 90, 93, 140–142, 146–148, 151, 153, 154]. These strategies are effective overall, with only 13 patients with NR [21, 22, 34, 90, 111, 119], SR [55], or PR [16, 21, 35, 93, 147, 152]. In the experience of McDonald et al. a CR was obtained in 3 months by combining corticosteroids and methotrexate [141], while in the study of Tuyp et al. complete efficacy in 3 years with methotrexate+etretinate [16] and the combination with a glycyrrhetin derivative also led to CR in 6 months [151]. Cyclosporin and corticosteroids were effective within 3 months in the study by Bruzzese et al. [148] nbUVB plus calcipotriene [20], acitretin combined with azathioprine with or without corticosteroids are other possible combinations that led to CR [22, 145]. Redigheri et al. observed CR with isoniazide+sertraline within 3 months in a patient with EP + Latent Tuberculosis Infection (LTBI) [42]. Adalimumab together with topical products such as salicylic acid and calcipotriene achieved CR within 12 months [150]. The combination of several systemic treatments resulted in the occurrence of several AEs such as the elevation of transaminases by combining methotrexate and etretinate [16], the occurrence of non-melanoma skin cancer with the combination of methotrexate and nbUVB, and dryness, hypertension, and increased creatinine when combining cyclosporine and acitretin [21].

Discussion

The treatment of EP is limited by the low quality of the evidence available to date, although RCTs (all from the Japanese region) have been carried out over the past few years that include patients with EP treated with etretinate, infliximab, CZP, Ixekizumab, guselkumab, risankizumab, and deucravacitinib; these are not dedicated studies, and the population considered is limited to a few patients, less than 10 usually, with statistical power limitations on this sub-population [11–14, 76, 111, 125]. The studies comprising the largest number of patients to date are that of the SIMPSO group on 33 patients treated with cyclosporine [51], that of Torii et al. on 44 patients [74], and Viguier et al. on 24 patients on infliximab [73], and that of Pescitelli et al. on 22 patients treated with ustekinumab [91].

The main experiences in the literature are case reports and case series, and the reporting of efficacy is often plagued by a lack of reporting of scores or outcome measures to assess the severity of EP, frequently reporting generic therapeutic results, such as “good response,” “response,” and “good efficacy,” especially in older publications, and the use of PASI has been progressively introduced since the late 1990s. The studies focus mainly on a population aged between 40 and 60 years, predominantly male (about 70%), and the therapeutic follow up is very variable and not always reported, while in some cases even 11 years of continuous therapy was reported [30].

When selecting the first line of treatment, clinicians should consider the severity of the disease, which is quite variable, the patient's comorbidities, and previous treatment history [2]. Regardless of the systemic therapies that will be used, the National Psoriasis Foundation Guidelines (NPF GLs) recommend the use of topical steroids, emollients, wet dressing, and continuous therapeutic support together with proper systemic treatment [2]. Admission should be considered in patients who are unstable and have electrolyte disturbances [2]. During admission it is essential to correctly frame the pathology, including with biopsies and histological examinations, and to differentiate it from severe pathologies such as cutaneous lymphomas [6]. Antibiotic therapy may be suggested during the course of treatment, considering the risk of staphylococcus over-infection, and possibly repeated swabs in case of suspected infection [2]. As erythrodermic psoriasis (EP) can often be worsened by concomitant therapies, identifying and potentially discontinuing the treatment contributing to the exacerbation is important. In our systematic review, though this was not the primary objective, we observed several drugs and clinical events that were possibly associated with the onset or worsening of EP. These included enalapril [27], bupropion/naltrexone [54], imiquimod [75], hemodialysis [99], eye surgery [87], the CoronaVac vaccine [107], durvalumab/tremelimumab [115], hydroxychloroquine [137], and infliximab [148]. Airway infections were also reported as potential triggering factors [154].

The first nonbiological systemic treatment used in a patient with EP was methotrexate. Overall, the treatment was effective with about 70% of patients achieving a clinical response in a range of time varying from 5 to 30 months. Some limitations to its first-line use are related to the slow therapeutic response (the fastest CR obtained after 5 months of treatment) and the possible occurrence of severe AEs [21, 23, 25]. However, the treatment is a good option in maintenance therapy.

With regards to vitamin A derivatives, etretinate showed promising results during the 1980s and 1990s, with progressive responses lasting months [32–34]. Its use is limited following its withdrawal from the market in Western countries due to adverse effects [156]. Acitretin has not shown acceptable overall efficacy, with more than 70% failures reported. Cyclosporine is the first-line non-biologic treatment recommended by the NPF GLs [2]. Treatment is rapid in EP, with initial responses in the first week of treatment and CR in 14 days [50]. The success rate reported in literature is 80% overall, and in the SIMPSO cohort study overall responses were > 90% in 33 patients [51]. In the inpatient setting, given the possibility of daily monitoring, and posological manageability, cyclosporine appears to be the first-line non-biologic drug of choice [2, 157]. However, once an adequate response has been achieved, treatment should be promptly transitioned to a different maintenance therapy, such as a biologic agent or methotrexate, owing to the potential for associated side effects. These include serious adverse events, progressive renal impairment, rebound upon discontinuation, and the risk of disease progression in cases of misdiagnosed cutaneous T cell lymphoma [2, 20, 23, 51, 55, 157].

Experiences in literature on phototherapy, apremilast, and deucravacitinib are scarce. In general, phototherapy is reported to be poorly effective. Data on TyK 2 inhibitors do not suggest strong efficacy, with only 1 patient (12.5%) achieving PASI 100 at 52 weeks in the RCT by Imafuku et al. of eight patients treated [11]. Apremilast is reported to be effective in all the studies analyzed, and the limited experience and lack of rapid response warrant further investigation [61–65]. Anecdotal use of azathioprine [22] and mycophenolate mofetil [135], in monotherapy or combination therapy, has shown adequate results, but the off-label nature of these therapies, and possible side effects, limit their use and experience [22, 135]. The heterogeneity of published studies makes it difficult to evaluate combination therapies between systemic drugs, and combinations between systemic and topical drugs, also limiting the attribution of possible efficacy. As previously noted, the combination of topical and systemic traditional and biologic therapy appears to be common sense overall, considering both topical steroids, vitamin D derivatives, and emollients with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs [2, 21, 22, 151, 153]. No studies were found that described the use of dimethyl fumarate in EP, or the use of JAK inhibitors other than deucravacitinib, as well as IL4 and IL13 inhibitors, although as previously noted the role of Th2 response has been described in EP, and also the initial differential diagnosis from EAD is not always easy [1].

Infliximab is the most widely described treatment in the treatment of EP; moreover, it is recommended as the first-line biologic in the NPF GLs. A total of 103 patients treated with infliximab have been described, showing in some cases a near CR a few days after infusion, with CRs after 4 weeks [24, 25]. However, studies with larger sample sizes have shown PASI 75 attainment in 48-64.3% of patients in 12–14 weeks [73], and a Japanese study report PASI 90 as a target, achieved by less than half of the study population [74]. When considering these results, alongside the risk of immediate and delayed adverse effects, as well as paradoxical reactions, Infliximab may become a less favored option over time, particularly given the growing availability of other biologic agents [72, 76, 148, 158]. Limited experience is available for other anti-TNFalpha with etanercept showing a PASI 75 in 60% of patients, and adalimumab reporting poor efficacy [78]. CZP on the contrary, in a RCT showed progressive response up to a PASI 90 achievement in 72% of patients at 52 weeks, placing it as the therapy with the most robust evidence of efficacy in this drug class [13]. A retrospective analysis of infliximab, adalimumab, etanercept, ustekinumab and efalizumab shows the superiority of anti-TNFalpha over the other two treatments [73].

Ustekinumab, in the 51 patients described in literature, performs well overall, with numerous documentations of PASI 90 [31, 71, 84–94]. Notably, in the study by Pescitelli et al. almost 70% of patients reached PASI 90 within 28 weeks. This result is even more striking when one considers that a large proportion of the patients treated in the studies described did not benefit from the 90 mg dosage, but from the 45 mg dose [91].

Among IL17 inhibitors, secukinumab is the drug studied on the largest number of patients, i.e. 91. Treatment was effective and rapid in most of the cases described, with PASI 50 achieved in 7 days, and PASI 100 in 8 weeks [101]. Considering the studies with the largest number of patients observed, more than 50% of patients achieved PASI 100 or PASI ≤ 2 after 48 weeks [106, 110]. Ixekizumab showed efficacy and rapid action in several case reports, with PASI 100 achieved in 8 weeks [117]. However, the only RCT with available data reported PASI 100 attainment in only one of four patients after 12 weeks of treatment, which is consistent with another cohort study reporting PASI 90 in 25% of patients at 20 weeks [111, 116]. More limited experience is available for brodalumab with 25 patients treated. The drug demonstrated a rapid onset of action; Megna et al. reported two patients who achieved PASI 75 within 3 weeks and PASI 100 by week 12 [120]. In the open-label study by Yamasaki et al. in 12 patients, a near CR was observed [118]. Bimekizumab was effective in all three patients described in the case reports currently available in the literature, with PASI 100 [123], PASI 90 [122], and PASI 75 [124] achieved after 4 weeks, respectively. The rapidity of action, especially of brodalumab and bimekizumab, might recommend first-line therapy with an IL17 inhibitor also given the efficacy found in patients with latent tuberculosis infection, cancer, and increased cardiovascular risk [159–162]. Comparisons between different molecules only include a comparison of secukinumab and ixekizumab, which showed similar results in a cohort study [106].

Data on IL-23 inhibitors is scarce. Guselkumab showed attainment of PASI 75 in 50% of patients at 12–24 weeks [127]. This was less than that achieved than risankizumab, in which the RCT by Yamanaka et al. reported attainment of PASI 90 in nearly 80% of patients at 16 weeks, placing it as the drug of choice in this therapeutic class, equaling the results of IL-17, with similar rapidity [132, 163].

The limitations of this manuscript lie in its comprehensive and quantitative nature. Wanting to describe all treatments reported in literature for erythrodermic psoriasis, no specific study selection was conducted, nor was the quality of the studies analyzed. In general, in literature the evidence is of low quality and often derived from case reports or small case series. The rarity of EP has led to studies involving small sample sizes, resulting in findings with low statistical power and limited external validity. No specific sensitivity analyses were conducted about the risk of selection bias.

Conclusions

EP still emerges as a condition of difficult therapeutic framing and management. To date, there are no dedicated RCTs for this severe manifestation of psoriasis. The latest available guidelines date to 2010, and the therapeutic scenario for psoriasis has completely changed with the availability of interleukin inhibitors. Among nonbiologic systemic drugs, a rational approach would include an initial approach with cyclosporine and maintenance therapy with methotrexate. In the case of contraindications to traditional systemic therapies, among biologic drugs, the rapidity of action, safety, and limited evidence of efficacy would seem to suggest IL-17 inhibitors and risankizumab. However, the most robust studies with the largest number of patients also allow infliximab, CZP, and ustekizumab to be identified as viable treatment options.

Future research should prioritize the design of EP-specific randomized controlled trials to establish evidence-based treatment algorithms. Additionally, the identification and validation of predictive biomarkers could play a crucial role in guiding therapeutic decisions and enabling personalized medicine in this rare and severe form of psoriasis.
