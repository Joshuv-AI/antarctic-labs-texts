Camera-assisted and navigated single-position prone transpsoas lateral lumbar interbody fusion: illustrative case
Authors: Rouzbeh Motiei-Langroudi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDSingle-position prone transpsoas lateral lumbar interbody fusion (PTP-LLIF) is an evolving minimally invasive surgery technique that merges the biomechanical and anatomical advantages of prone positioning with the LLIF approach. While PTP-LLIF enhances lumbar lordosis restoration and operative efficiency by eliminating patient repositioning, it presents unique ergonomic and visualization challenges for surgeons. This technical report describes a novel modification of the technique using the Teligen camera to improve intraoperative visualization and reduce surgeon fatigue.OBSERVATIONSThe approach follows standard PTP-LLIF workflow with modifications allowing the use of retractor-mounted optical cameras and integrated navigation. The system enables real-time visualization of disc preparation and cage placement while minimizing reliance on repeated fluoroscopic imaging.LESSONSIntegration of the Teligen system improves ergonomic posture for the surgeon, reduces the frequency of radiation exposure, and provides continuous visual access for both the lead surgeon and the operative team. Enhanced intraoperative efficiency and teaching opportunities are also noted. The Teligen camera–assisted technique offers a promising enhancement to PTP-LLIF by improving surgeon ergonomics and visualization without compromising procedural effectiveness. Early clinical experience suggests potential reductions in radiation exposure and operative time. Minimally invasive surgery (MIS) techniques have undergone vast development over the past few decades. One such technique is the single-position prone transpsoas lateral lumbar interbody fusion (PTP-LLIF), which combines the advantages of an LLIF with the anatomical benefits of positioning the patient prone.1 Among many benefits, this approach preserves and maximizes the natural lumbar lordosis (due to the positioning itself as well as use of an LLIF cage),2–6 takes advantage of position (and gravity) to create a better working corridor (i.e., retracting the psoas muscle more posteriorly and retroperitoneal, peritoneal, and vascular elements more anteriorly),7–9 eliminates the need to reposition the patient,5 and enables concomitant posterior access for decompression and instrumentation,1 altogether improving patients’ pain and function outcome.6,10

Despite its potential benefits, the PTP-LLIF technique is not devoid of challenges. First, lateral access exposure and visualization can be more challenging in the prone position compared with the lateral decubitus position. Second, the requirement for surgeons to adopt awkward postures to maintain exposure through a lateral incision can lead to fatigue, decreased precision, and potential for increased complications. Also, the need for frequent confirmation with lateral radiographic views (through fluoroscopy) while working through a lateral incision can be cumbersome and add to the difficulty. Third, in most practices the approach still needs specialized equipment (that can be used only for PTP-LLIF), adding to the medical costs and capital.

To tackle the ergonomic limitations of PTP-LLIF, emerging and advanced technologies have been deployed. These include use of a surgical robot,11–13 navigation,13 both conventional and virtual live fluoroscopy instrument tracking systems,14 an exoscope,7 and retractor-mounted cameras.15

In line with these efforts, we explored the utility of a camera-assisted navigated technique utilizing the Teligen endoscope port.16 The Teligen enables enhanced visualization of the surgical field while providing surgeons ergonomic benefits through the use of a heads-up screen. By applying this technology to the PTP-LLIF approach, we aimed to improve intraoperative ergonomics and visualization without sacrificing the effectiveness of the procedure. This report outlines our innovative application of the Teligen endoscope port within the context of single-position PTP-LLIF, emphasizing the advantages gained in terms of surgical ergonomics and visualization.

Methods

The procedure is performed in general alignment with a PTP-LLIF approach, with some modifications. A schematic of a regular room setup is shown in Fig. 1. The patient is placed under general endotracheal anesthesia and neuromonitoring electrodes are attached. We typically use triggered electromyography (tEMG) and somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEPs), including saphenous nerve SSEPs, for our LLIFs (whether performed in the lateral decubitus or prone position). The patient is then flipped prone onto the operating room table (Jackson or ProAxis), with arms outstretched and hips extended. In our practice, we use regular chest and hip pads but carefully tape the patient to the bed at the hips and lower chest level to minimize motion during the interbody insertion portion. Using tapes or a separate hip bolster, we also try to open the crest away from the L4–5 disc space if needed. The disc level, along with the posterior and anterior margins of the vertebral bodies of interest, is marked over the skin using fluoroscopy. Following this, the patient’s back and flanks are prepped and draped in the usual sterile fashion. Shanz pins are placed in the iliac crest contralateral to the approach side (i.e., if planning to perform the LLIF from the left, Shanz pins will be secured to the right iliac crest, and vice versa). A spin is performed for neuronavigation using an OEC 3D (GE HealthCare) coupled with the Brainlab platform. Depending on the case, either the pedicle screws (DePuy Synthes Viper Prime for percutaneous surgery and DePuy Synthes Expedium 5.5 for open surgery used in our institution; Johnson & Johnson MedTech) or the lateral interbody cage (DePuy Synthes Conduit lateral cage) can be inserted first. Our preference has been to place the screws first in patients without a history of prior fusion and the interbody cage first in those who require more extensive posterior lumbar work.

Schematic view of the room setup.

The lateral side is exposed as in a regular LLIF in the lateral decubitus position (LD-LLIF), with the surgeon preferably seated for this portion. Briefly, the skin, subcutaneous tissue, external and internal oblique, and transverse abdominis muscles are opened in a muscle-sparing manner down to the transversalis fascia, which is then opened. After gaining access to the retroperitoneal space, blunt dissection continues until the psoas muscle and the transverse processes are palpated. The level is then confirmed using navigation and lateral and anteroposterior (AP) radiographs. A quiet zone devoid of tEMG activity is identified through neuromonitoring, and the stimulation probe is anchored into the disc space, followed by sequential dilator tubes over the anchor. The periphery of the dilator tubes is again checked for EMG activity by rotating them 360°. On confirming a quiet zone, the self-retaining three-blade retraction system (Tedan XL Lateral Access System, Tedan Surgical Innovations) is introduced and attached to the bed-mounted arm, with the handles pointing upward (Fig. 2). To avoid interference with the navigation camera and pedicle screw placement, the arm is preferentially positioned on the bed ipsilateral to the approach side, closer to the patient’s arm (i.e., cephalad to the incision). The surgical field is checked again with tEMG, and the blades are retracted cephalocaudally and anteroposteriorly as necessary. A Phantom Spike disc shim is advanced over the posterior blade and secured inside the disc to provide more stability to the entire retractor system and mitigate the risk of anterior retractor migration.

Surgical field view after securing the self-retaining retractor system to the arm. The camera (red arrow) is slid over a fourth blade (yellow arrows), which itself is mounted to the self-retaining retractor (green ovals).

A fourth blade is placed anteriorly, secured to the self-retaining retractor main frame, and a Teligen optic camera (DePuy Synthes) is advanced over the blade for enhanced visualization (Fig. 2). The camera simply slides over the blade using a clip and can be advanced further or retracted to change the magnification and adjust the view. The camera’s own light source and two additional blade-mounted light sources illuminate the field (Fig. 3). The discectomy and endplate preparation are performed similarly to a regular LLIF using a scalpel blade, navigated Cobb elevator, and navigated discectomy dilators of different sizes (6, 8, 10, etc.), along with a shaver, pituitaries, and curettes (both straight and angled) (Fig. 4). The best-fitting cage is determined by preoperative images as well as intraoperative trials, and finally the cage (DePuy Synthes Conduit lateral cage used in our institution) is inserted under navigation and fluoroscopy guidance. A redacted version of the view through camera is shown in Video 1. At the end, hemostasis is performed, the retractors are removed, and the surgical sites are closed in a standard fashion.

Illustrative Case

In this report, we present a noteworthy case of a 44-year-old female who experienced persistent severe mechanical low back pain along with bilateral radicular leg pain, affecting the L3 and L4 dermatomes. Her medical history included rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, papillary thyroid carcinoma, and renal cell carcinoma, all of which were in remission. Over the years, she sought relief through various conservative and nonoperative methods, such as medications, physical therapy, multiple sacroiliac joint injections, epidural steroid injections, medial branch blocks, and radiofrequency nerve ablations, but none provided lasting relief.

On examination, she exhibited mild weakness (4/5) in left hip flexion, knee extension, and ankle dorsiflexion, along with a diminished left patellar reflex (1+) and a positive straight leg raise test on the left side. Standing scoliosis assessments and lumbar radiographs revealed grade 1 L3–4 spondylolisthesis with dynamic instability and local lateral bending at L3–4, while lumbar, lumbopelvic, and global parameters were otherwise normal. MRI results indicated bilateral lateral recess stenosis and T2 hyperintensity at the L3–4 facet joints (Fig. 5). A stable grade 1 L4–5 spondylolisthesis was also observed without impressive MRI features (facet hyperintensity or central, lateral recess, or foraminal stenoses).

B and C: Flexion (B) and extension (C) standing lateral radiographs showing grade 1 L3–4 spondylolisthesis with dynamic changes. D and E: Postoperative AP (D) and lateral (E) radiographs obtained in the illustrative case.

Because of the severe, persistent, and progressively worsening nature of her symptoms, the patient chose to proceed with neurosurgical intervention, specifically an L3–4 PTP-LLIF with MIS L3–4 pedicle screw fixation. The patient gave her written consent for the surgery.

The PTP-LLIF procedure was performed as outlined, with Video 2 illustrating the critical surgical steps via fluoroscopy. Postoperative radiographs are displayed in Fig. 5. There were no complications, and blood loss was less than 50 ml. The patient was discharged with notable immediate improvement in her radicular leg pain and maintained stable neurological status. On her 3-month follow-up, she had significant improvement in her back pain (3/10 on a visual analog scale) and full resolution of leg pain.

Discussion

Anterolateral approaches to the spine, including LLIF, have been a strong tool in the MIS armamentarium to obtain better lumbar lordosis restoration, deformity correction, and indirect decompression. Performing LLIF in the prone position, first described in 2020, provides additional lordosis and avoids the need for patient repositioning (and the consequent additional prepping and draping).1,5 Studies show that PTP-LLIF has a better potential in restoring segmental and lumbar lordosis, compared with LD-LLIF, proving the significance of the position itself in providing better lordosis per lumbar level. Per reports, the additional lumbar restoration averages approximately 2° per level.2–6 Of note, the difference is more pronounced in proximal lumbar (L1–4) segments.9 Moreover, the prone position provides some additional anatomical advantages. In a cadaveric study in which pins were percutaneously inserted at the middle of the L2–3, L3–4, and L4–5 discs bilaterally, it was shown that peritoneal and kidney violation were less in the prone position compared with the lateral position (0/18 vs 5/9 for peritoneum and 3/18 vs 3/9 for kidney, respectively). Combining the violations, the study found that violation in general was significantly greater in the lateral decubitus versus prone position (8/9 vs 3/18, p = 0.0006). Moreover, the distance from the quadratus lumborum to the posterior-most reflection of the peritoneum was longer in the prone position compared with the lateral position (8.7 cm vs 2.9 cm, p = 0.01).8 Moreover, the PTP approach provides a better working corridor by retracting the psoas muscle posteriorly. This is mostly due to extension of the hip joints and the consequent retraction of the muscle.9 Whether or not psoas retraction leads to lumbar plexus retraction is still debatable, but a cadaveric study has shown up to 1 cm of femoral nerve retraction with hip extension, adding to the safety of the PTP approach by creating a safer zone for cage entry.17

The PTP-LLIF technique is not devoid of challenges, though. First and foremost, lateral access exposure and visualization can be more challenging in the prone position compared with the lateral decubitus position due to the position and ergonomics of performing a lateral access procedure in this position. Second, while the PTP approach is beneficial in retracting the psoas muscle and opening the retroperitoneal corridor compared with LD-LLIF, it has been associated with an elongated surgical corridor from skin to the lateral surface of the disc (average 13.5 cm vs 11.9 cm, respectively) and shortened distance between the colon and the disc, psoas, and quadratus muscles, according to a study in 34 subjects.18

Observations

Many have used emerging technology to overcome the ergonomic limitations of PTP-LLIF. These include use of surgical robots,11–13 navigation systems,13,14 and exoscopes.7 To our knowledge, there is only one other report of a retractor-mounted camera in PTP-LLIF.15

Along with those efforts, we have recently explored the utility of the Teligen camera in this setting. Teligen is an endoscope port that received FDA clearance in October 2022 and has been in the market since. It was originally designed to be used in MIS posterior lumbar procedures, including microdiscectomies, laminotomies, laminectomies, foraminotomies, and TLIFs. The Teligen enables enhanced visualization of the surgical field, while providing surgeons ergonomic benefits through looking at a heads-up 4K screen. Given its benefits, surgeons have explored its utility beyond posterior lumbar indications, such as posterior cervical foraminotomies.19 By applying this technology to the PTP-LLIF approach, we aimed to improve intraoperative ergonomics and visualization and surgical time without sacrificing the effectiveness of the procedure. In our experience, the incorporation of the Teligen camera–assisted technique and navigation into the single-position PTP-LLIF provides multiple benefits. First, it decreases the awkward surgeon posture. Throughout the lateral portion of the case, the surgeon can be seated comfortably with a more neutral spine position, mostly looking at an overhead monitor, substantially reducing fatigue. This is particularly pertinent given the increasing demands placed on spine surgeons who perform complex operations regularly. Second, direct, enhanced, and magnified visualization is provided by the endoscope port for most of the steps (from cutting the disc and starting discectomy through cage insertion), adding to the information already provided by radiographic imaging and navigation. Surgeons are often challenged by limited operative views when working through a lateral incision in the prone position. Using a camera, all steps are basically visualized. The introduction of the high-definition camera enables the surgeon to visualize the instruments and subsequently the cage with acceptable magnification on an overhead 4K screen monitor, without the need to change position or look through a small surgical field. The camera also allows for adjustments in the angle of view, image contrast, and hue; features image capture and video-recording capabilities; and is equipped with an irrigation system that helps clean the surgical field and camera lens without needing to retract it from the surgical field.

Moreover, use of the Teligen camera has decreased the number of x-ray (fluoroscopy) shots in our practice. For instance, while inserting a discectomy dilator to break the disc and reach to the contralateral disc surface, the surgeon can directly see the tool and relevant numbering and markers (like 40, 45, 50 mm, etc.) over the screen. Directly seeing the instrument on screen and having in mind to keep the handle of the instrument parallel to the floor, surgeons can ask for an x-ray shot only when necessary. We have also been able to decrease the number of times the fluoroscope needs to switch between AP and lateral views, which has been a major nuisance in PTP-LLIF. It is worth noting that in all lateral approaches, optimal and excellent cage placement (balance between proximity to the anterior border to gain more lordosis versus the posterior border to gain more indirect decompression, reaching from ipsilateral to the contralateral cortex, parallelism to the coronal axis of vertebra rotation, etc.) is key. As such, despite using the camera and navigation, we still use fluoroscopy (more in AP, though, with a few confirmatory lateral shots) during cage placement to avoid compromising this step. Third, surgical trainees, observers, and other members of the surgery team (including the scrub nurse) can clearly see the surgical field of view simultaneously, enhancing the trainee’s learning and helping nurses engage more with the performed surgery.

The focus of this report has been more on the camera-assisted technology. However, using simultaneous navigation has also helped us streamline the process and achieve the goal of better ergonomics and less radiation exposure for the staff. While playing a major role in the posterior portion of the procedure, it also helps with the lateral part in several steps, including locating the entry point over the lateral contour of the disc, preparing the endplates, estimating the size of the cage (length, AP width, and height) and finally cage placement itself. However, it should be noted that the spine anatomy (and as such accuracy of the navigation platform) changes after insertion of the cage given the cage-to-disc height ratio and availability of lordotic cages. In multilevel surgeries, we recommend relying on fluoroscopy for the second cage placement (and so on) or spinning (or calibrating) the navigation system for each cage, with the cost of additional time.

While the initial results of adopting this technique are promising, we acknowledge that comprehensive studies involving larger cohorts and long-term follow-ups are required to establish definitive evidence regarding the efficacy and outcomes associated with this modified approach. PTP-LLIF per se is relatively new in many institutions (including ours), and we still do not have a large pool to statistically analyze standard versus camera-assisted PTP-LLIF techniques. Enlarging our case volumes of standard and camera-assisted PTP-LLIF and already having an established case volume of dual-position LLIF (i.e., LD-LLIF followed by flipping to the prone position for the posterior instrumentation), we can better compare surgical variables including operative time, blood loss, radiation dosimetry, surgical cost, and intraoperative and postoperative complications between the 3 groups (LD-LLIF, standard PTP-LLIF, and modified PTP-LLIF). This will be the focus of future research.

Lessons

The application of the Teligen camera–assisted technique in single-position PTP-LLIF presents a novel approach that addresses both visualization and ergonomic challenges in this minimally invasive spine surgery. It may also help in decreasing the radiation exposure and operative time, points that need to be proven in large-scale future studies.
