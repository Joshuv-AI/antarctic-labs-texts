Tolerance of outbreak-associated Candida parapsilosis isolates to antiseptics in a dry surface biofilm model
Authors: Gabrielle Lameado Pereira, Jenyffie Araujo Belizario, Sérgio Ricardo Ambrósio, Gil Benard, Regina Helena Pires
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the susceptibility of Candida parapsilosis clinical isolates, originally recovered from healthcare workers’ hands during a hospital outbreak, to commonly used hospital antiseptics under a dry surface biofilm (DSB) model. Unlike traditional hydrated biofilm systems, DSBs replicate low-moisture, nutrient-limited environments frequently encountered on hospital surfaces and medical devices. Over 12 days, monospecies DSBs were formed on glass coverslips and exposed for 3 min to seven antiseptics preparations, namely ethanol 70% (EtOH 70%), 0.5% alcoholic chlorhexidine (CHG 0.5%), 1% aqueous chlorhexidine (CHG 1%), 0.12% aqueous chlorhexidine (CHG 0.12%), 10% povidone-iodine (PVPI), 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and 70% isopropanol (IPA). Viability was assessed by log10 CFU/mL reduction, with ≥ 4 log10 considered microbiologically effective. EtOH 70% and CHG 0.5% consistently achieved > 5 log10 reductions, maintaining high activity despite desiccation. In contrast, PVP-I, IPA and CHG 0.12% showed poor performance (< 1 log10 median reduction), while 1% CHG 1% and H2O2 displayed intermediate efficacy. Hierarchical clustering revealed four distinct susceptibility phenotypes, suggesting intraspecific variability in tolerance to desiccation and biocides. Comparisons with previous hydrated biofilm data indicated reduced antiseptic efficacy under dry conditions, particularly for PVP-I. These results underscore the resilience of C. parapsilosis DSBs and highlight the need to revise disinfection protocols to address phenotypic diversity and persistence in clinical settings, especially on high-touch surfaces and invasive devices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40814-x.Keywords: Healthcare-associated infections, Antiseptic efficacy, Biocide tolerance, Hospital disinfection protocols, Phenotypic variabilitySubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

In recent years, a significant epidemiological shift has been observed in invasive fungal infections, marked by a decline in the prevalence of Candida albicans and a proportional rise in non-albicans species such as Nakaseomyces glabratus (formerly Candida glabrata), Candida tropicalis, and, notably, Candida parapsilosis1. The latter has emerged as a prominent etiological agent of invasive candidiasis, ranking as the second or third most frequently isolated species in various hospital settings2,3.

Furthermore, genotyping studies conducted over the past decades have revealed patterns of sustained horizontal transmission and, in some cases, endemic establishment of C. parapsilosis in healthcare units. In contrast to C. albicans, whose clonal clusters are typically transient and limited in scope, C. parapsilosis clusters tend to persist over extended periods, affecting multiple patients within the same ward4–7. This often silent dynamic may go unnoticed without the implementation of active molecular surveillance programs.

Another clinically relevant factor is the remarkable ability of C. parapsilosis to form dense, well-organized biofilms on inert surfaces, which enhances its resistance not only to antifungal agents but also to biocidal compounds widely used in healthcare settings1. While most studies on environmental biofilms focus on moist biofilms, there is growing recognition of so-called “dry surface biofilms” (DSBs), which are characterized by the presence of viable microorganisms under conditions of low humidity and limited nutrient availability8. These biofilms, although often invisible to the naked eye, are commonly found on hospital surfaces and may persist even after terminal cleaning procedures considered effective. Investigations in intensive care units have shown that even after disinfection with a neutral detergent followed by a 500 ppm chlorine solution, multidrug-resistant strains remained viable, indicating the resilience of DSBs to conventional cleaning protocols9.

In this context, preventive measures targeting the disinfection of inert surfaces and invasive devices, such as central venous catheters, are essential for the control of nosocomial candidemia. Although C. parapsilosis is well known for its ability to form biofilms on inert surfaces1,2, its potential persistence in the form of DSBs has yet to be properly characterized in the scientific literature.

Considering that many hospital antiseptics and disinfectants show limited efficacy against Candida planktonic cells or hydrated biofilms, it is hypothesized that dehydrated biofilms may serve as overlooked clinical reservoirs, with potential implications for the cross-transmission of pathogens. In this context, the present study provides, for the first time, a novel assessment of the ability of clinical C. parapsilosis isolates recovered from the hands of healthcare workers to form dry surface biofilms in vitro, as well as the effectiveness of widely used hospital antiseptic formulations against these structures.

The evidence generated may support a reassessment of current hospital disinfection practices and highlight the need for targeted strategies aimed at eliminating dehydrated biofilms in clinical environments, particularly in Brazilian healthcare settings where these isolates were originally obtained.

Results

The efficacy of antiseptics was evaluated based on the log10 reduction (CFU/mL) of viable C. parapsilosis cells recovered from dry biofilms after 3-min exposure to the tested compounds. The data are shown in Fig. 1 as boxplots, illustrating the distribution, median, and variability of reduction values for each formulation. Among the agents tested, EtOH 70% and CHG 0.5% yielded the highest reductions, both exceeding 5 log10 CFU/mL, thus meeting the microbiological efficacy criterion. Intermediate activity was observed for CHG 1%, H2O2, and CHG 0.12%, while the lowest reductions were recorded for IPA and PVP-I, with median values below 1 log10 CFU/mL.

Log reduction of CFU in dry surface biofilms of Candida parapsilosis after exposure to different antiseptics. Biofilms were developed on glass coverslips over a 12-day cycle of alternating hydration and desiccation phases and subsequently exposed to antiseptics for 3 min. Data represent pooled results across 20 clinical isolates of C. parapsilosis sensu stricto, with values expressed as median and interquartile range from three independent experiments. A reduction of ≥ 4 log10 CFU/mL was considered the threshold for microbiological effectiveness.

Two-way ANOVA revealed a statistically significant effect for both the “yeast isolate” factor (P = 0.00377) and the “antiseptic” factor (P < 0.0001), indicating that both variables contributed to differences in antifungal response. Post hoc analysis using Tukey’s test identified four statistically distinct efficacy groups among the antiseptics. The highest-performing group included EtOH 70% and CHG 0.5%, which exhibited significantly superior activity. CHG 1% and H2O2 formed an intermediate group, whereas PVPI, IPA, and CHG 0.12% clustered among the least effective agents. To better visualize individual susceptibility profiles, a heatmap was constructed to display log10 reduction values for each isolate-antiseptic combination (Fig. 2).

Heatmap of C. parapsilosis isolates susceptibility to antiseptics. Biofilms were developed on glass coverslips over a 12-day cycle of alternating hydrated and dry phases and then exposed to antiseptics for 3 min. The heatmap displays the average log10 reduction values for each clinical isolate (rows) across all antiseptics tested (columns). Colour intensity reflects the magnitude of reduction, with darker yellow indicating higher efficacy.

Hierarchical clustering was performed using the Ward method and Euclidean distance based on the mean log10 reduction values of each isolate across all antiseptics tested (see Supplementary Table S1). The resulting dendrogram (Fig. 3) revealed four major clusters, each reflecting a distinct susceptibility profile. The orange cluster grouped isolates with low overall susceptibility, showing minimal reductions regardless of the antiseptic used, suggesting a more tolerant phenotype. The green cluster included isolates with intermediate responses and variable susceptibility, indicating moderate resistance. The red cluster was composed of highly susceptible strains, which exhibited strong reductions against most agents. Finally, the purple cluster encompassed the most sensitive isolates, which were markedly inactivated, particularly after exposure to the most potent compounds, such as EtOH 70% and CHG 0.5%.

Hierarchical clustering of Candida parapsilosis clinical isolates based on susceptibility to antiseptics. Dissimilarity was assessed using Euclidean distance and Ward’s linkage method, with log10 reduction values calculated relative to untreated biofilms (negative control). For each isolate, the mean log10 reduction across all antiseptics was used as input. Four major clusters are highlighted, representing distinct phenotypic profiles of tolerance.

To investigate whether the differences in antiseptic susceptibility observed among the phenotypic groups could be attributed to variations in biofilm biomass, the baseline biomass of C. parapsilosis biofilms was quantified for each isolate using the crystal violet staining method. These individual biomass values were then grouped according to the four phenotypic clusters identified in the dendrogram. As shown in Fig. 4, the boxplots display comparable distributions of biofilm biomass across the four clusters. Statistical analysis using the Kruskal–Wallis test revealed no significant differences among the groups (H = 0.20, p = 0.978), indicating that the variations in antiseptic efficacy identified in this study were not attributable to differences in initial biofilm biomass.

Based on their average log10 CFU reduction values after antiseptic exposure, isolates were classified into four phenotypic groups reflecting different susceptibility patterns. Boxplots show the distribution of biomass within each group, including the median, interquartile range, and outliers. Statistical analysis using the Kruskal–Wallis test showed no significant differences in biomass among the groups (H = 0.20, p = 0.978).

Discussion

The C. parapsilosis isolates examined in this study were originally recovered from the hands of healthcare workers during a documented hospital outbreak an oncological intensive care unit (ICU) in Brazil and later provided by a fungal strain repository. Their epidemiological origin underscores their clinical relevance, particularly in the context of healthcare-associated infections (HAIs), where C. parapsilosis has emerged as a significant opportunistic pathogen1,6,7. The species’ strong affinity for inert surfaces and human skin, especially the hands, facilitates its persistence in hospital environments, its involvement in cross-transmission, and its role in endemicity and outbreak events4,10.

While the classical definition of a biofilm does not mandate the presence of moisture, most experimental models and hospital infection-control strategies still focus on biofilms formed under hydrated conditions. In this regard, evaluating the ability of C. parapsilosis to develop biofilms under low-humidity conditions, known as DSBs, offers an innovative and biologically relevant perspective, given its proven capacity to colonize surfaces and withstand antiseptic action8,9,11. Testing antiseptic efficacy against dry biofilms formed by these outbreak-associated hand isolates simulates critical, yet often overlooked, scenarios in disinfection protocols, such as contamination on high-touch surfaces, medical equipment, and the apparently clean hands of healthcare personnel.

Analysis of antiseptic performance against dry monospecies biofilms formed by outbreak-associated C. parapsilosis isolates recovered from the hands of healthcare workers in a Brazilian oncology ICU revealed marked differences in efficacy. Although several of the tested compounds are routinely recommended in national and international hospital disinfection guidelines12–14, only EtOH 70% and CHG 0.5% consistently achieved > 5 log10 CFU/mL reductions, confirming their microbicidal potential even under low-moisture conditions15. Ethanol is thought to mediate microbial killing primarily via rapid protein denaturation and membrane disruption. In contrast, alcoholic formulations of chlorhexidine might combine the swift bactericidal effect of alcohol with the substantivity of CHG, improving persistence and penetration in structured biofilms (as evidenced by CHG substantivity in plaque-like biofilms)16.

When compared with our previous findings17 on hydrated (wet) monospecies biofilms of C. parapsilosis, a clear decrease in susceptibility was observed: CHG 0.5%, while remaining highly effective, dropped from 9.1 to just above 5 log10 CFU/mL, and PVP-I 10% fell sharply from 8.18 to < 1 log10 CFU/mL in the dry biofilm model. This reduction in activity is likely related to the structural and biochemical changes that occur during desiccation, including increased matrix density, reduced diffusion of aqueous-based agents, and possible induction of stress-adapted phenotypes, all of which can hinder biocide penetration and efficacy18–22. In addition, the limited efficacy of PVP-I, aqueous CHG, H2O2, and IPA under dry biofilm conditions highlights their reduced penetration and stability when confronted with the dense extracellular matrix of DSBs, as reported in previous studies8,15. These findings highlight the challenge of eradicating dry surface reservoirs in healthcare environments, even with agents considered highly potent under hydrated conditions.

In addition to the differences observed among antiseptics, the data revealed marked heterogeneity in the response of clinical C. parapsilosis isolates to the treatments applied. This phenotypic variation was evidenced by hierarchical clustering analysis, which classified the isolates into four distinct groups based on their mean log10 reduction profiles. The orange cluster comprised isolates with the lowest overall susceptibility, showing modest log reductions regardless of the antiseptic tested, suggesting a more tolerant phenotype. The green cluster exhibited an intermediate response, with variable efficacy across compounds. The red and purple clusters encompassed the most susceptible isolates, with the latter displaying pronounced reductions, particularly following exposure to EtOH 70% and CHG 0.5%, indicating high sensitivity.

These distinct phenotypic patterns suggest the existence of intraspecific subpopulations with differing abilities to adapt to desiccation and resist biocidal agents. Previous studies have already reported variability among C. parapsilosis strains in biofilm formation and extracellular matrix composition18,19,22, factors that can directly influence antiseptic penetration and efficacy. Importantly, when we compared the baseline biofilm biomass (quantified by crystal violet staining) across the four clusters, no significant differences were found, indicating that the reduced susceptibility observed in certain groups was not attributable to higher biofilm mass alone but likely reflects intrinsic phenotypic differences in tolerance. The identification of phenotypic clusters with reduced response is clinically concerning, as it indicates that certain lineages may persist in the hospital environment even after cleaning procedures deemed effective.

This heterogeneity observed in dry biofilms is consistent with epidemiological evidence reported by Escribano et al.4, Guinea et al.6, and Fekkar et al.7, which documented the prolonged clonal persistence of C. parapsilosis in hospital units, often associated with contamination of inert surfaces and the hands of healthcare workers. Such findings support the hypothesis that the phenotypic variability observed in vitro, particularly in dry biofilm models, may have direct implications for the ability of these strains to sustain chains of intra-hospital transmission and contribute to endemicity.

Furthermore, Govrins and Lass-Flörl1 highlight that the biofilm matrix acts as a physical barrier to the action of biocides, while Daneshnia et al.2 demonstrated that genotypic variability among isolates is directly related to their survival capacity following disinfectant exposure. In addition to the physicochemical barriers imposed by the extracellular biofilm matrix, specific molecular mechanisms may also contribute to the tolerance observed among the isolates analysed. As discussed by Maillard and Pascoe12, resistance to disinfectants and antiseptics is not limited to the direct inactivation of active compounds but involves multiple factors, including modulation of cell permeability, activation of oxidative stress response systems, metabolic shifts associated with stationary-phase physiology, and the formation of persistent subpopulations. The latter was corroborated in our previous study22, in which persistent cells were detected in C. parapsilosis monospecies biofilms even after exposure to 0.5% alcoholic CHG. These mechanisms are amplified within biofilm communities, where microenvironmental heterogeneity favours protective niches and limits the effective penetration of chemical agents. In the specific case of dry biofilms, such protection may be even more pronounced due to matrix compaction and the reduced diffusion of liquids.

In this context, the findings of the present study provide, for the first time, evidence that disinfection protocols based on standardised tests using reference strains or planktonic forms may not adequately reflect the complexity of clinical biofilms, particularly under desiccation conditions, although this study has some limitations that should be acknowledged. Firstly, it was conducted in a single healthcare institution and focused exclusively on C. parapsilosis sensu stricto isolates obtained from the hands of healthcare workers of the oncology intensive care unit during a local outbreak. Although the isolates had previously been genotyped and their antifungal susceptibility profiles characterised23, we did not investigate potential cross-resistance to antiseptics or antifungals in this specific dataset. Secondly, although our DSBs model is well established and aligned with European recommendations24,25, it was performed on sterile glass coverslips, which may not fully represent the physicochemical complexity of real hospital surfaces. Further studies involving additional surface types, multispecies biofilms, and broader isolate collections from different geographic regions would help confirm the generalisability of our findings.

According to European standard24, a ≥ 4 log10 reduction is required to demonstrate fungicidal activity, yet our data show that only EtOH 70% and CHG 0.5% consistently achieved this benchmark against C. parapsilosis dry surface biofilms. This reinforces the need to account for intraspecific diversity and the real-world behaviour of clinical isolates when validating biocidal formulations intended for use in healthcare environments, especially in critical care areas where the silent persistence of C. parapsilosis may sustain intra-hospital transmission chains even after cleaning procedures deemed effective.

Methods

Antiseptics

The seven antiseptics evaluated in this study comprised commercially available hospital formulations routinely used in Brazil, with pure active ingredients employed when necessary to avoid interference from excipients. The products included 70% ethanol (EtOH 70%, Rialcool 70®, Rioquímica S.A., São José do Rio Preto, Brazil), widely used for hand and skin antisepsis; chlorhexidine digluconate (CHG) in three formulations—0.12% oral rinse (Riohex Gard®, Rioquímica), commonly used in mechanically ventilated patients; 0.5% alcoholic solution (Riohex®, Rioquímica), applied for skin and hand antisepsis; and 1% aqueous solution (Needs®, Raia Drogasil S/A, São Paulo), used as a topical antiseptic; hydrogen peroxide 3% (H2O2, Rioquímica) and isopropyl alcohol 70% (IPA, Cinord Indústria Farmacêutica Ltda., Serrana, Brazil), both indicated as general-purpose hospital antiseptics; and povidone–iodine 10% (PVP-I, Riodeine®, Rioquímica), a classic iodophor used for skin antisepsis and wound care. All antiseptics were tested in their undiluted form, following manufacturers’ instructions and in line with national and international guidelines for hospital disinfection and antisepsis12–14. These formulations therefore represent products routinely employed in healthcare facilities and correspond exclusively to direct-acting biocidal agents, with no film-forming barrier properties.

C. parapsilosis isolates

Twenty cryopreserved clinical isolates of C. parapsilosis sensu stricto, obtained from the hands of hospital healthcare workers during an outbreak in an oncological intensive care unit in Brazil, were used23. These isolates were donated by the Carlos da Silva Lacaz Culture Collection (São Paulo, Brazil). As detailed by Thomaz et al.23, these isolates were originally recovered in June 2019 using a standard broth plastic bag technique, followed by selective culture in Brain Heart Infusion broth with antibiotics, subsequent confirmation on chromogenic medium and MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. Microsatellite typing revealed no evidence of close clonal relatedness among these isolates, indicating that they represent genetically distinct strains rather than duplicates from a single healthcare worker. The antifungal susceptibility profiles (azoles, echinocandins, and amphotericin B) of these isolates have been published previously23, and the same set of isolates was employed in the present study. Reference strains Candida parapsilosis ATCC 90018 and Candida albicans ATCC 10231 were used as technical controls.

Dry biofilm formation and viability assessment

Dry biofilms were developed following the model described by Ledwoch and Maillard26 and by Ledwoch et al.27, which alternates hydration and desiccation phases in the presence of organic load. Sterile circular glass coverslips (13 mm diameter) were used as adhesion surfaces, placed in flat-bottom 12-well cell culture plates (Corning Costar™, Fisher Scientific™, Loughborough, UK). Each well received 1 mL of yeast extract peptone dextrose (YPD, Difco Laboratories Inc., NJ, USA) supplemented with 3% foetal bovine serum (Sigma™ Life Science, Dorset, UK) and a standardized fungal suspension (0.5–1.5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL). Plates were incubated at 25 °C for 48 h to allow microbial adhesion and initial biofilm development. The supernatant was then removed, and plates were kept at 25 °C for another 48 h, initiating the dry phase. After this period, 1 mL of the same nutrient solution was added again to each well for a 48-hour hydrated phase. This cycle was repeated every 48 h for 12 days, resulting in mature biofilms. All antiseptic efficacy assays were performed at this final time point (day 12), when biofilms were in their dry phase26,27. To test antiseptic efficacy, 1 mL of the selected antiseptic was applied to each well and left in contact with the biofilm for 3 min, in accordance with the contact time recommended by the European standard EN 1500 for hygienic hand rub testing28. Control wells received 1 mL of sterile PBS. Afterwards, the coverslips were transferred to 15 mL Falcon tubes containing 9 mL of a neutralizing solution29, composed of: 10 g Tween 80, 1 g lecithin, 0.5 g L-histidine, 2.5 g sodium thiosulfate (Na2S2O3), and 3.5 g sodium lactate (C3H3NaO3) in 1000 mL of sterile water. Samples were subjected to sonication followed by vortexing. Then, 100 µL aliquots were plated on YPD agar using a Drigalski loop and incubated at 37 °C for 48 to 72 h. Colony counts were used to calculate the log reduction factor (RF = log10 [CFU_control/CFU_treated]), where CFU_control represents the mean number of viable cells recovered from untreated biofilms (negative control) and CFU_treated corresponds to the mean count after exposure to the antiseptic26,27,30. In line with European standards for fungicidal activity24,25, antiseptics were considered effective if they achieved a reduction of ≥ 4 log10 CFU/mL compared to the untreated control24,31. All analyses were performed in triplicate for each antiseptic tested32.

Quantification of biofilm biomass by crystal violet staining

The total biomass was quantified by staining with 0.1% crystal violet, followed by solubilisation in 33% acetic acid and measurement of optical density at 590 nm, as described by George et al.33. Wells containing only medium were used as controls. Experiments were performed in biological triplicate.

Statistical analysis

A two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the interaction between the biological factors “yeast isolate” and “antiseptic treatment”, adopting a significance level of 5% (p < 0.05). Post hoc comparisons among groups were conducted using Tukey’s test to identify statistically significant differences in antiseptic susceptibility. To explore phenotypic patterns of response across the C. parapsilosis isolates, a hierarchical clustering analysis was also carried out using Ward’s method and Euclidean distance, based on the mean log10 reduction values per isolate. Additionally, baseline biofilm biomass was quantified for each isolate using the crystal violet staining method (absorbance at 590 nm) and grouped according to the four phenotypic clusters identified in the dendrogram. An exploratory Kruskal–Wallis test was then applied to assess whether biofilm biomass differed significantly among the clusters. All statistical analyses were carried out in Python (version 3.12; Python Software Foundation, USA) using custom-developed scripts. Data manipulation and computation were conducted with the NumPy (v2.3.2), Pandas (v2.3.1), and SciPy (v1.16.1) libraries. Statistical modeling was performed using Statsmodels (v0.14.5), and visualisation of results was generated with Matplotlib (v3.10.3) and Seaborn (v0.13.2).
