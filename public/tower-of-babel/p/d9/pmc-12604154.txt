Prevalence and risk factors for tungiasis in Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Abayeneh Girma, Indiris Abdu, Amere Genet, Kasaye Teshome
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTungiasis, caused by Tunga penetrans, is a neglected tropical disease of major public health concern in sub-Saharan Africa. It is associated with significant health, psychological, and economic consequences. This review aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of tungiasis in Africa and identify its associated risk factors.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and African Journal Online. Studies published between June 2007 and December 2023 were included. Data were analyzed using a random-effects model. Heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, and sources of heterogeneity were explored via subgroup analyses and Galbraith plot. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger’s test. Risk factors were summarized as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsThirty-seven studies comprising 29,760 participants from ten African countries were included. The pooled prevalence of tungiasis was 36.23% (95% CI: 30.4–42.1). Male sex (aOR 1.53, 95% CI: 1.05–2.01), living in houses with earthen floors (aOR 4.92, 95% CI: 1.91–7.93), and poor caregiver or family knowledge of tungiasis (aOR 2.33, 95% CI: 1.08–3.58) were significant risk factors.ConclusionThe findings indicate a high-pooled prevalence of tungiasis, highlighting its ongoing status as a common neglected tropical disease in Africa. The identification of male sex, earthen floors, and poor knowledge as significant risk factors provides a clear etiological profile. This evidence points to the necessity of targeted interventions focused on household-level sanitation and public awareness campaigns to effectively prevent and control tungiasis in endemic regions.Protocol registrationThis systematic review and meta-analysis protocol is registered in PROSPERO under the registration number CRD42024597922.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11922-2.

Introduction

Tungiasis is a neglected skin disease that affects both humans and animals. A female sand flea known as Tunga penetrans causes it. It is commonly called “pulga de areia, niguá, pique, bicho do pé, bichodo porco or jatecuba, jigger, sand flea or chigoe” [1]. Tungiasis is prevalent in certain regions of the world, particularly in tropical and subtropical areas where resources are limited [1]. It primarily affects children aged 5 to 14, older adults over 60, and individuals with disabilities [1, 2].

Globally, over 1 billion people live in areas where tungiasis is prevalent, with developing countries being the most affected [1]. The disease is widespread in approximately 88 countries worldwide, the majority of which are in Africa [3]. It is commonly found in tropical regions of Africa—including Cameroon, Ethiopia, Kenya, Madagascar, Nigeria, and Uganda—as well as in Central and South America and the Caribbean, particularly during the hot and dry seasons [4, 5]. More than 75% of the East African population resides in rural areas, which are highly susceptible to the disease [6]. The World Health Organization has included tungiasis in its 2021–2030 plan to control neglected tropical diseases [7].

Tungiasis is associated with several factors, including social and economic conditions, healthcare-seeking behaviors, waste disposal practices, and community knowledge of hygiene and sanitation. The presence of reservoir animals—such as dogs, cats, pigs, cattle, sheep, horses, mules, rats, and mice—near residential areas is also considered a contributing factor to tungiasis. In addition to poor housing conditions (such as cracked, mud, dusty, or earthen floors), certain behavioral factors contribute to the high prevalence of tungiasis. Prolonged dry seasons are also recognized as a climatic risk factor for tungiasis [4, 5].

Tungiasis is characterized by symptoms such as erythema, intense redness, swollen lymph nodes, abscesses, ulceration, abnormal skin growth leading to peeling, painful cracks, muscle weakness, loss of toe and toenail tissue, toe and toenail deformities, and skin thickening [8, 9]. Severe cases can result in fissures, disfigurement, amputation, immobilization, chronic lymphedema, and secondary infections, including septicemia, tetanus, and toxic shock syndrome [6]. The use of sharp objects such as needles, pins, and thorns to remove fleas can lead to the transmission of blood-borne infections, including HIV and hepatitis B and C viruses [6]. The skin lesions caused by the disease can facilitate the proliferation of various bacterial species, such as Staphylococcus aureus, Clostridium spp., and Enterobacteriaceae, potentially causing further complications in immunocompromised individuals [5, 6]. It may also increase the risk of open wounds, which can lead to anemia and tetanus, especially in areas with low vaccination coverage [6]. The impact extends to impairing the quality of life for families and communities through stigma, reduced school attendance, discomfort, and poverty. Additionally, the economic burden is further exacerbated by medical costs [5, 10].

Preventing and controlling tungiasis requires a comprehensive strategy involving multiple approaches. Therefore, various environmental and behavioral measures, such as improving personal and environmental hygiene, washing feet before wearing closed shoes, proper waste management, and maintaining safe sanitary living conditions to mitigate the impact of the disease, are essential. Several primary studies conducted in different regions of Africa have yielded inconsistent results. Although Africa is particularly vulnerable to tungiasis, the disease has received relatively little attention from academics, healthcare professionals, policymakers, and charitable organizations, even in areas where it is common [4, 5]. Despite its global impact, tungiasis remains underrecognized. Three meta-analyses on tungiasis reported varying pooled prevalence rates. The first, conducted in two selected regions of Ethiopia in 2025, found a pooled prevalence of 39% [11]. The other two, covering sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) in 2020 and 2025, reported pooled prevalence rates of 33.4% [4] among the general population and 37.86% [5] among school-age groups, respectively. However, no study has yet examined the pooled prevalence and associated factors of tungiasis across the entire African continent, encompassing all age groups. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and associated factors of tungiasis in Africa.

Methods

Study area

Africa is the second most populous continent in the world after Asia, with over 1.5 billion people as of 2025 [12]. It features diverse geographical landscapes, ranging from highland plateaus to lowland deserts, and is home to a wide variety of ethnic groups, languages, and cultural practices. Design and protocol registration

International databases (ScienceDirect, Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and African Journal Online), websites, and International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) were searched to confirm that no previously published meta-analysis on this topic exists. This review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [13] (see Supplemental File 1). The protocol for this systematic review and meta-analysis has been registered with the PROSPERO under registration number CRD42024597922.

Search strategy

The search strategy for this systematic review and meta-analysis employed a structured approach to ensure comprehensive coverage of the relevant literature. A systematic search was conducted across multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, ScienceDirect, and African Journal Online (see Supplemental File 2), to identify studies on the prevalence and determinants of tungiasis published up to December 2023. Studies from June 2007 onwards were included to focus on the most recent two decades of epidemiological evidence and reflect current public health contexts. These studies were accessed and incorporated into the review. To identify relevant studies, the following search terms were used individually or in combination with Boolean operators: “tungiasis” OR “Tunga penetrans” OR “jigger flea” OR “sand flea disease” AND (“Africa” OR “Sub-Saharan Africa” OR “West Africa” OR “East Africa” OR “Central Africa” OR “Southern Africa” OR “all African countries”).

Study selection and quality appraisal

Version X8 of EndNote software (Thomson Reuters, USA) was used to remove duplicate articles. Then, IA, AmG and KT independently screened the titles and/or abstracts of each article according to predefined inclusion criteria. Articles that met these criteria underwent a thorough full-text evaluation, as illustrated in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 2). Any disagreements between the authors were resolved through discussion. The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) checklist for prevalence [14] and case‒control [15] studies quality appraisal were subsequently applied, as detailed in Supplemental File 3 and Figs. 4A and B, which includes nine and ten criteria, respectively. The final analysis included studies with low or moderate risk of bias, categorized by scores of 6–8 (low), 3–5 (moderate), and 0–2 (high) [16].

Data extraction

Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft®, Redmond, Washington, USA) was used as a template for data extraction. Three reviewers, IA, AmG and KT, independently collected information from each study. They recorded details such as the author’s name, year of publication, country where the study was conducted, study design, setting, sampling method, sample size, and number of cases. Data on the prevalence of tungiasis, predictors of tungiasis, and duration of follow-up for each study were also documented. Any discrepancies identified between the reviewers during data extraction were resolved by consensus or fourth-party adjudication.

Data analysis

We employed a random-effects model for the analysis and used the I² statistic to quantify heterogeneity among the studies. In addition, Galbraith plots, and subgroup analyses based on sample size, study country, study setting, sampling method, study design, and publication year were conducted in order to explore possible sources of heterogeneity. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic, and was interpreted as follows: low (0% to 25%), moderate (25% to 50%), substantial (50% to 75%), and considerable (75% to 100%) heterogeneity. For studies reporting on risk factors, we extracted adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and their 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We performed separate meta-analyses for each risk factor that was reported by at least three studies. Pooling was conducted using a generic inverse-variance random-effects model to account for anticipated heterogeneity. The results are presented as pooled Odds Ratios with 95% confidence intervals. To assess publication bias, we utilized funnel plots, and regression-based Egger’s tests. Sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the influence of individual studies on the overall effect size. All analyses were conducted via Stata version 14 with “metan” command (StataCorp LLC 4905 Lakeway Drive College Station, Texas 778454512, USA).

Results

Selection of studies

We identified 967 studies through searches of the specified databases. After 102 duplicate records were removed, 865 articles remained for title and abstract screening. Among these, 181 studies were excluded because they did not meet the inclusion criteria during this screening phase. The full texts of 684 articles were subsequently assessed in detail, resulting in the inclusion of 37 studies in the final systematic review and meta-analysis. The complete selection process, along with the reasons for exclusion, is presented in Fig. 2.

Characteristics of the included studies

The 37 included studies were conducted between 2007 and 2023 and involved a total of 29,760 participants across Africa [3, 17–52]. Twenty-eight studies were conducted in community settings, and nine were conducted in schools. Twenty studies used simple random sampling to select participants, nine used systematic random sampling, and seven employed purposive sampling methods. The sample sizes ranged from 78 to 4,035 participants. The reported prevalence in individual studies varied from 5.83% in Ethiopia to 65.0% in Kenya. Thirty-five of the included studies were cross-sectional and the remaining two were case-control studies. These studies were conducted in ten African countries: Kenya (12), Ethiopia (7), Uganda (7), Nigeria (4), Tanzania (2), Cameroon (1), Zambia (1), Rwanda (1), Madagascar (1), and Gabon (1). Pooled prevalence of tungiasis

The pooled prevalence of tungiasis in Africa was 36.23% (95% CI: 30.37–42.09) on the basis of a random effects model. Significant heterogeneity was observed among the included studies (I² = 99.2%, p < 0.001) (see Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis

We conducted a subgroup analysis to identify factors contributing to the variation among the studies. We examined variables such as the number of participants, country, study setting, sampling method, study design, and year of publication (Table 2). Studies with more than 500 participants reported a higher prevalence rate of 39.73% (95% CI: 31.69–47.78) than the 32.46% (95% CI: 24.77–40.15) reported in studies with fewer than 500 participants. The highest prevalence of tungiasis was observed in Cameroon at 53.00% (95% CI: 50.12–55.88), whereas the lowest was in Zambia at 13.50% (95% CI: 10.10–16.90).

Subgroup analysis

With respect to the study settings (Table 2), the pooled prevalence of tungiasis in community-based studies was 38.23% (95% CI: 31.64–45.42), whereas school-based studies reported a prevalence of 29.09% (95% CI: 17.82–40.36). Studies that selected participants purposively reported a prevalence of 37.58% (95% CI: 25.38–48.77); those using simple random sampling had a prevalence of 36.35% (95% CI: 27.40–45.30); and studies employing systematic random sampling reported a prevalence of 34.89% (95% CI: 26.77–43.01). In terms of study design, cross-sectional studies reported a prevalence of 36.50% (95% CI: 30.39–42.60), whereas case‒control studies reported a prevalence of 31.53% (95% CI: 0.81–63.87). The prevalence of tungiasis in studies conducted before 2020 was 34.50% (95% CI: 28.95–40.04), whereas studies conducted after 2020 reported a prevalence of 38.99% (95% CI: 27.73–50.26).

Quality assessment, heterogeneity and publication bias

All included articles were of high quality, as demonstrated in Supplemental File 3 and Figs. 4A and B. Egger’s regression intercept test for small-study effects yielded a p value of 0.654 (Table 3), indicating no evidence of publication bias. However, the funnel plot suggested some positive publication bias (Fig. 5A). Eleven studies with extreme prevalence estimates were excluded in the sensitivity analysis, which reduced the pooled prevalence from 36.69% (95% CI: 36.16–37.22) to 29.88% (95% CI: 29.13–30.64), indicating these studies had disproportionate influence on the overall estimate, as shown in Fig. 5B. Finally, the Galbraith plot revealed some heterogeneity among the included studies. The dispersion of studies away from the “no-effect” line indicates considerable between-study variation in effect estimates (Fig. 6).

Factors associated with tungiasis infestation in Africa

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, Table 4 presents the factors associated with the spread of tungiasis in Africa. Seven studies reported that men were 1.53 times more likely to have tungiasis than women were (aOR: 1.53, 95% CI: 1.05–2.01). Nine studies reported that people living in homes with earthen floors were 4.92 times more likely to have tungiasis than those with cemented floors were (aOR: 4.92, 95% CI: 1.91–7.93). Finally, four articles indicated that families or caregivers with poor knowledge of tungiasis were 2.33 times more likely to be affected than those with good knowledge were (aOR: 2.33, 95% CI: 1.08–3.58). These factors were significantly associated with tungiasis in Africa.

Discussion

Human tungiasis is a major global public health concern that disproportionately affects communities in underdeveloped and developing countries. Poverty remains a key determinant of various skin conditions, including tungiasis [53]. The findings of this study provide critical insights for responsible bodies and policymakers, highlighting the urgent need to prioritize and implement effective intervention mechanisms for tungiasis across Africa. This study aimed to measure the pooled prevalence of tungiasis and its associated risk factors in Africa. In this review, 37 studies with a total of 29,760 participants were included.

In this meta-analysis, the pooled prevalence of tungiasis was 36.23%. The current result was comparable with those of community-based studies reported in the Trinidad, West Indies (31.4%) [54], Fortaleza, Northeast Brazil (34%) [55], Northeast Brazil (39%) [56], and meta-analyses conducted among the general population (33.4%) [4] and school-age children (37.86%) [5] in SSA. However, our finding was greater than those of studies conducted in South Brazil (1.6%) [57] and 1.35% and 0.89% in the first and second national tungiasis surveys of Kenya [58], respectively. In addition, it was lower than that reported in some previous surveillance studies conducted in Nigeria (41.5%) [59], Brazil (51.3–62.2%) [60–62], Uganda (62.8%) [50] and Kenya (65%) [46]. Sociodemographic characteristics, seasons of data collection, study populations, sample sizes, methodological approaches, and economic conditions may all contribute to these variations.

Addressing the high combined burden of tungiasis in Africa necessitates targeted healthcare policies and strategic interventions. These efforts should prioritize improving access to sanitation and hygiene, increasing community income, strengthening screening and early detection, and integrating prevention-focused approaches. Additionally, providing comprehensive training for healthcare professionals, increasing community awareness regarding tungiasis, and enhancing laboratory services and facilities are essential. These initiatives aim to improve treatment outcomes, reduce tungiasis risk, and ensure holistic care for affected individuals.

With respect to the subgroup analysis, studies conducted in school settings reported a lower prevalence of tungiasis, at 29.09%, than did community-based studies, with a prevalence of 38.23%. This finding was in line with previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses conducted among the general population [4] and school-aged children in SSA [5]. Inflammation, pain, and itching have been reported to impact individuals’ ability to sleep, walk, and work in focus, ultimately lowering their income, academic performance (e.g., for students) and the social stigma associated with the infestation [5, 10]. This systematic review and meta-analysis also revealed that studies conducted before 2020 had a tungiasis prevalence of 34.50%, whereas some more recent studies (after 2020) reported higher prevalence (38.99%), but substantial heterogeneity (I²=99.2%) prevents firm conclusions about temporal trends. This finding is consistent with a previous systematic review and meta-analysis conducted among general population in selected regions of Ethiopia [11] and school-age children in SSA [5]. In contrast, a previous systematic review and meta-analysis conducted among the general population [4] revealed that 33.8% of the studies published from 1980 to 1999 were higher than 33.4% of the articles published from 2000 to 2020. This might be because the disease currently receives limited attention from the scientific community, as outlined in the World Health Organization’s (WHO) DNDi Roadmap 2021–2030 [7]. Emphasis should be placed on monitoring the prevalence and implementing comprehensive prevention strategies to mitigate the spread of the disease worldwide. Among the countries with the highest prevalence rates, Cameroon led with 53.00%, followed by Kenya at 39.17%, and then Ethiopia at 38.43%, indicating that nearly half of the individuals in these nations were infected with tungiasis. This matches with previous meta-analysis conducted on school-age children in sub-Saharan Africa [5]. In contrast, the highest pooled prevalence of tungiasis was recorded in Ethiopia (46.5%), according to a previous systematic review and meta-analysis conducted among the general population in SSA [4]. This suggests that there are insufficient epidemiological data on the disease, with the social impacts of its morbidity being neglected as a result. Key stakeholders, including the academic community, healthcare providers, decision-makers, funding agencies, and pharmaceutical companies, therefore desire further epidemiological research to identify high-transmission areas of tungiasis for additional prevention and new treatments.

Regarding the risk factors for tungiasis, males were 1.53 times more likely to be infected with tungiasis than females. Supportive results were reported from Nigeria [59], Brazil [55, 60, 62] and the WHO [1]. This difference may be explained by the observation that boys more frequently play in unpaved streets, leading to higher exposure rates. Conversely, women have a lower prevalence rate, primarily because their outdoor exposure is less frequent. The disparity is not attributed to different health-oriented behaviors after infection (e.g., women more rigorously removing penetrated fleas than males) [63]. Moreover, the disparity in prevalence between the two genders may be largely due to differences in adherence to proper hygienic practices, with females generally exhibiting greater concern for their personal body hygiene than males do. However, a survey study conducted in southern Brazil among schoolchildren [57] revealed that the prevalence of tungiasis was twice as high in females as in males. Moreover, a survey study conducted in Trinidad, West Indies [54] and a previous systematic review and meta-analysis conducted among the general population in SSA [4] reported that there were no significant sex differences. Accordingly, the disparities in observations are probably due to exposure and environmental influences rather than variations in susceptibility [4].

Houses with an earthen floor had 4.92 times higher odds for tungiasis infestation than houses with a cemented floor. This outcome was consistent with previous systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in selected regions of Ethiopia [11]. Earthen floors with dust provide an ideal environment for jigger fleas to live and reproduce, offering shelter for flea eggs and larvae and allowing the parasite’s life cycle to continue within the household. This is because fleas typically inhabit areas 2–5 cm below the sand surface in oxygen-poor environments, which explains their high prevalence on earthen floors [64]. These floors facilitate direct and frequent skin contact with infested soil, increasing the risk of flea penetration [65, 66]. Consequently, sleeping or walking barefoot on an earthen floor for extended periods increases the likelihood of contracting the disease. Environmental modifications, such as installing cement flooring, can effectively prevent human transmission. This intervention contrasts with the earthen floors commonly found in huts and many rural villages in endemic areas [4].

Families or caregivers with poor knowledge of tungiasis had 2.33 times higher odds of infestation. This outcome was comparable with that of the Northeast Brazil study [67]. The majority of African communities are illiterate, and illiteracy may contribute to a lack of awareness about the advantages of various risk factors and their connection with tungiasis. Furthermore, this is due to the low economic value of uneducated parents, which limits their ability to provide essential hygiene protection items such as shoes, soap, and other necessities [68].

Strengths and limitations

This review highlights the combined prevalence of tungiasis on the African continent. Additionally, it identifies the aggregated risk factors linked to tungiasis infestation. However, certain limitations exist in this review. First, high heterogeneity (I² = 99.2%) indicates pooled prevalence should be interpreted with caution. Second, variation in adjustment factors across included studies may introduce residual confounding in pooled risk estimates. Third, the predominance of cross-sectional studies limiting causal inference. Fourth, limitation to English-language studies may introduce language bias, potentially excluding relevant French and Portuguese-language studies from Francophone and Lusophone Africa. Fifth, geographic underrepresentation. Finally, the small sample sizes of some of the included articles and limited number of studies for many risk factors could influence the meta-analysis results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review and meta-analysis revealed a substantial prevalence of tungiasis across Africa, with substantial variation across countries, study setting, sample size, study design, sampling method, and publication year. Being male, living in a house with an earthen floor, and having poor knowledge about tungiasis among caregivers or family members were significantly associated with higher prevalence. Public health strategies should therefore prioritize community education campaigns and programs to improve household flooring in endemic areas. Furthermore, additional well-designed studies across diverse African settings are essential to better understand the patterns, contributing factors, and overall dynamics of tungiasis on the continent.
