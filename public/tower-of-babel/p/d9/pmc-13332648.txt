Redo Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement for Degenerated Valve With High-Risk Coronary Anatomy
Authors: Chloe Kharsa, Gal Sella, Mangesh Kritya, Karl Abou Zeid, Joe Aoun, Stephen H. Little, Nadeen N. Faza, Michael J. Reardon, Fernando Ramirez Del Val, Neal Kleiman, Sachin S. Goel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesHere we describe a step-by-step, imaging-guided technical approach to redo transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) in a degenerated ACURATE Neo2 valve (Boston Scientific) with high-risk right coronary artery (RCA) anatomy and highlight strategies to prevent coronary obstruction during valve-in-valve implantation.Key Procedural StepsA 76-year-old man who previously underwent TAVR using a 27-mm ACURATE Neo2 valve presented with heart failure due to severe prosthetic aortic regurgitation. Preprocedural computed tomography demonstrated a critically high-risk anatomy for RCA obstruction, with the coronary ostium originating between the upper and lower crowns of the Neo2 frame and limited sinus of Valsalva accommodation. Because of his prohibitive surgical risk, redo TAVR was performed using a 26-mm SAPIEN 3 valve (Edwards Lifesciences). The procedure incorporated meticulous computer tomography–based planning, prophylactic RCA wiring for coronary protection, and stepwise, high and controlled valve deployment with real-time positional adjustments to achieve precise alignment at the Neo2 upper crown.Potential PitfallsValve-specific ACURATE Neo2 geometry increases coronary obstruction risk, making careful imaging, valve selection, and coronary protection essential.

Iatrogenic coronary artery occlusion is a rare but potentially catastrophic complication of transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR), most frequently involving the left coronary artery. Right coronary artery (RCA) obstruction is less commonly reported, yet it carries similar hemodynamic consequences. The risk of coronary compromise is influenced by multiple anatomical and procedural factors, including low coronary ostial height, bulky leaflets or annular calcification, valve oversizing, and aggressive deployment strategies.1 In this case report, detailed preprocedural computed tomography (CT) for a valve-in-valve (ViV) revealed that the RCA originated between the lower and upper portions of the ACURATE Neo2 (Boston Scientific) upper crown, a configuration that placed it at exceptionally high risk.

Case Summary

A 76-year-old man presented with progressive dyspnea (NYHA class III). He had undergone TAVR 6 years earlier with a 27-mm ACURATE Neo2 valve. His medical history was significant for diabetes mellitus, coronary artery disease, hyperlipidemia, obesity, obstructive sleep apnea, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and a dual-chamber pacemaker implanted for sick sinus syndrome. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated concentric left ventricular hypertrophy, severe aortic regurgitation, grade III restrictive diastolic dysfunction, and preserved left ventricular ejection fraction of 65% to 70% (Video 1). Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed flail prosthetic leaflets with severe transvalvular aortic regurgitation without paravalvular leak (Video 2). Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed severe aortic regurgitation (regurgitant volume: 67 mL; regurgitant fraction: 52%), mild left ventricular dilation, and holodiastolic flow reversal in the descending aorta (Video 3).

The patient was diagnosed with acute decompensated heart failure secondary to prosthetic valve degeneration. Given a Society of Thoracic Surgeons risk score predicted mortality of 5.91% and prohibitive surgical risk, he was referred for redo TAVR. Preprocedural CT (Figure 1, Video 4) revealed complex anatomy. The left coronary artery originated above the Neo2 upper crown below the commissural posts, whereas the RCA originated between the lower and upper portions of the Neo2 upper crown, placing it directly within the anticipated path of the displaced prosthetic leaflets. Procedural Steps

Redo TAVR was performed with the patient under general anesthesia with fluoroscopic and with TEE guidance. Percutaneous femoral access was obtained, and systemic anticoagulation was achieved with an activated clotting time maintained above 250 seconds. Given the extreme risk of RCA obstruction, a guiding catheter was advanced into the RCA, and a coronary guidewire was positioned for prophylactic protection with readiness for immediate bailout chimney stenting if flow compromise occurred. The degenerated ACURATE Neo2 valve was crossed, and a Safari XS (Boston Scientific) wire was advanced into the left ventricular apex to provide stable support. Based on preprocedural CT demonstrating constrained sinus anatomy and high-risk coronary relationships, a balloon-expandable 26-mm SAPIEN 3 valve (Edwards Lifesciences) was selected to allow predictable radial expansion and precise positioning. To achieve optimal positioning, the valve was advanced slowly and under continuous fluoroscopic and TEE guidance, with careful attention to coaxial alignment within the prior prosthesis. During deployment under rapid ventricular pacing, stepwise expansion was performed to maintain stability and allow real-time positional adjustments. Interaction with the prior Neo2 frame resulted in mild upward valve movement, which was corrected using controlled push-pull maneuvers and wire tension modulation, ultimately allowing precise alignment of the outflow frame with the Neo2 upper crown (Video 6).

During deployment, the coronary guiding catheter was disengaged while maintaining the coronary wire in situ as a safety measure. Aortic root angiography and TEE further confirmed optimal valve position and absence of paravalvular leak. Hemostasis was achieved, and the patient was transferred to recovery in stable condition.

The postoperative course was uneventful. Follow-up transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a well-expanded and well-seated SAPIEN 3 valve with preserved left ventricular ejection function (55%-59%), grade II diastolic dysfunction, and a mean transvalvular gradient of 12 mm Hg (Video 7). The patient reported substantial improvement in symptoms and was discharged home with outpatient follow-up arranged. Postprocedural CT was deferred because of underlying renal dysfunction (serum creatinine: 2.4 mg/dL; estimated glomerular filtration rate: 27 mL/min/1.73 m2) and reassuring intraprocedural angiographic and echocardiographic findings.

Potential Pitfalls

The ACURATE Neo2 valve's unique geometry compounds a risk for coronary obstruction during ViV procedures. Its tall supra-annular frame, wide open upper crown, and high sealing skirt provide initial hemodynamic advantages but can limit sinus space and promote upward displacement of leaflets. Beyond anatomical considerations, long-term outcomes after Neo2 implantation have demonstrated a relatively high incidence of moderate-to-severe paravalvular leak (PVL) and structural valve degeneration compared with other contemporary TAVR systems, as documented in randomized trials.2 Although the valve was subsequently withdrawn from the market, it was used in over 50 countries,3 and the earliest recipients are now presenting with late degeneration and PVL, often necessitating redo TAVR interventions.

Redo TAVR with a Neo2 prosthesis presents unique technical challenges. The combination of tall frame height, narrow sinus geometry, supra-annular leaflet position, and high skirt can markedly increase the risk of coronary obstruction, particularly when coronary ostia lie between or beneath the Neo2 crowns.4 In this case, early leaflet degeneration resulted in severe regurgitation and a precarious RCA anatomy, creating a scenario where ViV expansion without careful planning could have led to catastrophic obstruction.

Meticulous preprocedural CT assessment was critical in guiding the intervention. Detailed measurements of coronary height, sinus dimensions, valve-to-coronary distances, and leaflet thickness, along with careful evaluation of the Neo2 crown's spatial relationships, informed both valve selection and procedural strategy. The SAPIEN 3 balloon-expandable valve was chosen for its predictable expansion and precise positioning, allowing the outflow frame to align with the Neo2 upper crown. Prophylactic wiring of the RCA provided an immediate bailout option in the event of obstruction, highlighting the importance of proactive coronary protection. In this case, although coronary obstruction was a major concern, the anatomical assessment suggested an intermediate rather than prohibitive risk. The design characteristics of the ACURATE Neo2 valve, particularly the presence of a retention gap within the upper crown, were anticipated to reduce the likelihood of complete leaflet apposition against the coronary ostium. Therefore, a strategy of prophylactic coronary wiring with readiness for bailout chimney stenting was selected. In cases with higher predicted risk, alternative approaches such as BASILICA (bioprosthetic aortic scallop intentional laceration to prevent coronary artery obstruction) or balloon-assisted BASILICA may be considered,5,6 although these were not deemed necessary in the present case. This highlights the importance of individualized procedural planning based on detailed CT assessment and valve-specific considerations.

This case underscores the indispensable value of a multidisciplinary heart team. Optimal outcomes in high-risk redo TAVR procedures rely on integrated expertise in interventional cardiology, advanced imaging, and cardiac surgery, ensuring individualized procedural planning and rapid response to complications. As the global population of Neo2 recipients ages, clinicians will increasingly encounter structural degeneration and complex ViV scenarios, making careful preprocedural assessment, procedural precision, and coronary protection essential for patient safety and long-term success.

From a practical standpoint, achieving precise valve positioning during redo TAVR can be approached in a structured, stepwise manner. This includes 1) meticulous preprocedural CT-based planning to define the target landing zone and its relationship to the prior prosthesis; 2) slow, controlled valve advancement to maintain coaxial alignment within the existing frame; 3) continuous multimodality imaging guidance with fluoroscopy and TEE to confirm real-time positioning; 4) stepwise deployment under rapid-ventricular pacing to ensure procedural stability and allow incremental adjustments; and 5) anticipation of device-frame interaction, particularly upward valve displacement in supra-annular platforms, requiring fine positional correction using push-pull maneuvers and guidewire tension modulation. In high-risk anatomies, integration of coronary protection strategies further allows operators to prioritize optimal valve positioning while maintaining procedural safety.

Operators should be prepared to perform fine positional adjustments using push-pull maneuvers and guidewire tension modulation, particularly in the setting of tall supra-annular frames such as ACURATE Neo 2. These principles are critical to ensure accurate deployment and to minimize the risk of coronary obstruction or malposition.

Conclusions

Redo TAVR in degenerated ACURATE Neo2 valves can be safely performed despite extremely high-risk coronary anatomy. Through detailed preprocedural CT assessment, careful prosthesis selection, high and controlled deployment technique, and proactive coronary protection, a complex ViV procedure was successfully completed without coronary compromise. As increasing numbers of Neo2 recipients present with late valve failure, these technical principles will be essential for optimizing procedural safety and outcomes.
