Systemic immune profiling of heterologous versus homologous boosting of COVID-19 vaccination
Authors: Xu Han, Hudachuan Jiang, Hui Zheng, Pengfei Jin, Lingjie Xu, Fengze Wang, Simin Li, Chengwei Shao, Siyue Jia, Jialu Feng, Ming Xu, Jingxin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCompared with homologous boosting, heterologous boosting with a different COVID-19 vaccine following priming generates stronger antibody responses against severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) as well as variants, particularly for inactivated COVID-19 vaccine(CoronaVac). However, it is still unclear about the potential immune enhancement mechanism underlying heterologous boosting.MethodsIn this study, we isolated spike protein binding-specific monoclonal antibodies at day 180 post a homologous booster with CoronaVac or a heterologous booster with Ad5-nCoV based on two-dose of CoronaVac using the single B cell sorting platform. Subsequently, we verified their neutralization activity to SARS-CoV-2 variants, germline gene sequences and affinity kinetics targeting SARS-CoV-2 NTD/RBD/S1. Additionally, we conducted an in-depth analysis of the immunological response characteristics, by integrating single-cell RNA/V(D)J sequencing(scRNA/ V(D)J-seq).ResultsOur study demonstrated that heterologous boosting with Ad5-nCoV elicited more mature B cells with higher affinity and activated more abundant immune-related pathways compared to the homologous boosting with CoronaVac. In addition, Ad5-nCoV boosting expanded unique clonal types of B and T cells, whereas CoronaVac boosting led to a small-sized clonal expansion. Furthermore, the utilization of germlines associated with neutralizing antibody were preferentially enriched in recipients with Ad5-nCoV boosting.ConclusionsAbove all, our study gives insights for elaborating the systemic immune landscape of heterologous-boosting COVID-19 immunization by the novel single B cell sorting platform and scRNA/V(D)J-seq technology. Background

The immune protection induced by the primary series of SARS-CoV-2 inactivated vaccines has significantly waned over time, particularly due to the emergence of circulating Variants of Concern references [1–3]. Therefore, World Health Organization has recommended that individuals vaccinated with inactivated vaccines for primary immunization are suggested to consider vector-based or mRNA COVID-19 vaccines as a heterologous booster [4, 5]. We have previously conducted several trials to evaluate the heterologous prime-boost immunization regimens [6], and the recombinant adenovirus type-5 COVID-19 vaccine (Ad5-nCoV) has been approved an emergency-use listing by the WHO [7]. The results demonstrated that individuals who received the two doses of inactivated vaccine CoronaVac followed by a boost with Ad5-nCoV exhibited significantly higher neutralizing antibody levels against both the wild-type(WT) and Delta(B.1.617.2) variant compared to those who underwent homologous boosting with CoronaVac. Cytokine profiling further indicated a Th1-biased immune response induced by heterologous booster immunization. The highly efficient boosting effects on the immune responses supported the approval of heterologous prime-boost immunization regimen of Ad5-nCoV following two doses of inactivated COVID-19 vaccines in China in February 2022 [8]. The underlying immunological mechanisms that contribute to the enhanced immunogenicity performance associated with the heterologous boosting with Ad5-nCoV remain largely unexplained.

In previous study, we have conducted bulk RNA sequencing in participants who primed with two-dose of CoronaVac before, vaccinated with CoronaVac and Ad5-nCoV as a third dose, which supposed that up-regulated innate immune pathways stimulated by the CoronaVac plus Ad5-nCoV schedule were significantly correlated with vaccine type and neutralizing antibodies level [9]. However, current studies, have yet to identify and characterize the antibody repertoire, and deep immune profiling of single cell and BCR/TCR sequencing of the homologous boosting of CoronaVac and heterologous boosting of CoronaVac plus Ad5-nCoV.

In this study, we aimed to identify the antigen-specific B cells and generated specific monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) from participants who initially vaccinated with two-dose of CoronaVac followed by homologous boosting or heterologous boosting with Ad5-nCoV, based on the single B cell sorting platform, and to illustrate exact cellular responses and related molecular mechanisms by single-cell RNA sequencing(scRNA-seq).

Results

Humoral immunity

The study design is depicted in Fig. 1A. Baseline characteristics, including age and the interval between primary series and booster dose, were comparable between the Ad5-nCoV and CoronaVac (ICV) groups (Table S2). Prior to boosting, both groups exhibited similar levels of anti-spike (S) IgG and anti-nucleocapsid (N) IgG against the wild-type (WT) and variants of concern (VOCs) (Fig. 1B–H).

At day 14 post-boost, the Ad5-nCoV group elicited significantly higher anti-S IgG geometric mean concentrations (GMCs) compared to the ICV group. Specifically, GMCs against WT were 30,619 ng/mL (95% CI: 23,944– 39,111) versus 10,864 ng/mL (95% CI: 7,643– 15,467) in the ICV group (p < 0.001). Similarly, the Ad5-nCoV group induced superior cross-neutralization against Alpha, Beta, Delta, and Omicron, with GMCs of 34,434 ng/mL, 17,219 ng/mL, 35,481 ng/mL, and 25,763 ng/mL, respectively, all significantly higher than those in the ICV group (p = 0.025, p < 0.001, p < 0.001, and p = 0.005, respectively; Fig. 1B, D, E, G, H; Table S3). Both regimens induced significant increases in anti-S IgG titers against all tested strains at day 14 compared to baseline (all p < 0.0001), followed by a gradual decline through day 180.

Although anti-S IgA levels were numerically higher in the Ad5-nCoV group, the differences did not reach statistical significance (Figure S1). Notably, anti-N IgG levels were significantly lower in the Ad5-nCoV group (378.4 ng/mL at day 14) compared to the ICV group, reflecting the induction of anti-N antibodies by the inactivated vaccine priming regimen rather than the adenoviral vector booster (Fig. 1C).

Advantaged mAbs selection

Five participants from each group were assessed for the proportion of WT SARS-CoV-2 S antigen-specific memory B cells at day 180. The percentages of S antigen-specific memory B cells were 2.8%±1.2% in the Ad5-nCoV group and 1.9%±0.9% in the CoronaVac group (Figure S2).

In addition, S-specific memory B cells targeting the WT of SARS-CoV-2 were sorted from other 12 PBMCs at day 180 post-vaccination by FACS (Fig. 2 and Figure S3). A total of 6 × 10^7 cells in each group were preliminarily screened, and a total of 2,433(1,245 in the Ad5-nCoV group and 1,188 in the ICV group) pairs of H and L variable genes of the mAbs were obtained and transiently expressed in Expi293F cells/ExpiCHO cells. Among the 2,433 mAbs, 483 (19.9%) positive mAbs bound to recombinant S trimer of WT, with 325 (26.1%) in the Ad5-nCoV group and 168(14.1%) in the ICV group (p = 0.063)(Fig. 2A). Additionally, 380 (15.6%) positive mAbs bound to RBD(205(16.5%) in the Ad5-nCoV group and 175(14.7%) in the ICV group, p = 0.262), and 126 (5.2%) mAbs bound to NTD (68(5.5%) in the Ad5-nCoV group and 57(4.8%) in the ICV group, p = 0.516) (Fig. 2A).

To prioritize high-affinity clones, we pre-selected 106 mAbs (77 from Ad5-nCoV, 29 from ICV) with an OD450 ≥ 1.0 for large-scale expression and purification. The geometric mean EC50 for S-binding was 0.4 nM (95% CI: 0.2–0.6) in the Ad5-nCoV group versus 0.7 nM (95% CI: 0.2–2.0) in the ICV group (p = 0.279; Fig. 2B). The mAbs were classified into high, medium, low, and non-binding categories according to the binding activity. Notably, the Ad5-nCoV group exhibited a higher proportion of medium binding activity antibodies than the ICV group (Fig. 2C).

Except 26 non-binding antibodies, we assessed subtype specificity of 80 mAbs (61 from the Ad5-nCoV group and 19 from the ICV group), followed by screening for neutralizing activity against wild-type SARS-CoV-2 (Fig. 2D). In the Ad5-nCoV group, 18 (29.5%) were RBD-specific, 20 (32.8%) NTD-specific, 14 (23.0%) S1-specific, and 9 (14.8%) potentially bound to a quaternary structure in S trimer or S2. The positive rate of neutralizing activity among these mAbs was 73.8%(45/61) in the Ad5-nCoV group, with all S1-binding mAbs consistently exhibiting neutralizing activity, and 66.7%(12/18) RBD-binding and 70.0%(14/20) NTD-binding mAbs. While in the ICV group, of the 19 mAbs, 8 (42.1%) were RBD-specific, 7 (36.8%) NTD-specific and 3 (15.8%) S1-specific. 89.5% (17/19) of mAbs in the ICV group demonstrated positive neutralizing activity.

Cross-neutralization assays identified broad-spectrum mAbs. For instance, mAb No. 265 from an Ad5-nCoV recipient neutralized WT, Alpha, Gamma, Delta, and Omicron sublineages (BA.1, BF.7, and XBB) despite moderate binding affinity. Conversely, mAbs from ICV recipients (e.g., No. 6 and No. 11) exhibited potent neutralization against early variants but limited activity against XBB (Fig. 2E). Interestingly, NTD-specific mAbs primarily neutralized WT virus, whereas broad-spectrum activity was predominantly associated with S1-targeting mAbs, which were more prevalent in the Ad5-nCoV group.

Genetic analysis revealed significantly higher somatic hypermutation (SHM) rates in the heavy chains of Ad5-nCoV-derived mAbs (range: 1.3–9.8%) compared to ICV-derived mAbs (range: 1.1–6.2%; p = 0.012; Fig. 2F). CDR3 length distributions were similar between groups (Fig. 2G). Preferential usage of IGHV3-30 and IGKV1-39 was observed in the Ad5-nCoV group, whereas ICV recipients favored IGHV1-58 and IGKV3-20 (Fig. 2H, I).

The affinity kinetics analysis of 55 nAbs with explicit epitopes targeting SARS-CoV-2 NTD/RBD/S1 were shown in Figure S4. The affinity values (KD, nM) ranged from 0 to 446.8 nM, including 49 high-affinity nAbs(0–20 nM), 3 medium-affinity nAbs(20–100 nM), and 3 low-affinity nAbs(>100 nM). No significant difference of KD values of all nAbs from two groups was observed, however, the affinity of nAbs targeting the S1 antigen in the Ad5-nCoV group was statistically higher than that in the ICV group (p = 0.0163) (Fig. 2J, K and Additional file: Figure S5).

Overview profile of single-cell sequencing

To elucidate the cellular basis of enhanced immunity, we performed scRNA-seq on sorted memory T cells, memory B cells, and plasmablasts from six participants (three per group) at Days 0, 14, 28, and 180 (Fig. 3A). The proportion of plasmablasts in the Ad5-nCoV group at Day 14 was 1.95-fold higher than that in the ICV group (4.2% vs. 2.1%, p = 0.074; Fig. 3B). No significant differences were observed in the frequencies of memory B or T cells between groups (Figure S7).

After quality control, 190,324 single cells were retained for analysis. Unsupervised clustering identified major subsets, including CD4+central memory (Tcm), CD4+effector memory (Tem), CD8+activated T cells, plasmablasts, and memory B cells (Fig. 3C, Figure S6). Memory B cells were further subclustered into four subsets based on immunoglobulin expression profiles: Bm_IGHD (less mature), Bm_IGHM (early class-switched), Bm_LTB (high IGHA1 expression), and Bm_EBF1 (EBF1-driven) (Fig. 3F and Figure S8). All B cell subsets expanded by Day 14, with Bm_LTB cells representing the largest population. CD8+Tem cells constituted the most abundant T cell cluster post-vaccination (Fig. 3E and Figure S9).

Immunological pathway analysis of plasmablasts, memory B cells and T cells

Pathway analysis of plasmablasts revealed that the Ad5-nCoV group upregulated genes involved in TNF, Toll-like receptor, and MAPK signaling pathways at Day 14, indicative of robust immune activation (Fig. 4A). At day 28, the Ad5-nCoV group also showed effects on oxidative phosphorylation pathways (Figure S10A). By contrast, the ICV group primarily upregulated oxidative phosphorylation pathways, suggesting a metabolic program supporting sustained antibody secretion (Fig. 4B).

Comparative analysis of memory B cell gene expression at days 14 and 28 post-vaccination revealed distinct transcriptional programs between the Ad5-nCoV and ICV groups. At day 14, the Ad5-nCoV group exhibited significant enrichment of pathways related to immune response regulation, B cell activation, Fc receptor signaling, and B cell differentiation (Fig. 4C). These immune-related pathways remained activated at day 28 (Figure S10B). In contrast, up-regulated genes in the ICV group were primarily associated with energy metabolism pathways (Figure S10C).

Subclustering analysis further delineated the functional status of specific B cell subsets. Pathways related to inflammatory factor production, T cell activation, and lymphocyte activation were suppressed prior to vaccination across the IGHD_BM, IGHM_BM, and LTB_BM subpopulations in both groups. Following vaccination, these pathways were strongly activated at day 14, subsequently decreasing by day 28 and continuing to decline through day 180. Notably, the Bm_IGHM subpopulation in the Ad5-nCoV group, alongside the Bm_IGHM and Bm_LTB subpopulations in the ICV group, exhibited particularly pronounced activation of pathways involved in T cell activation, T cell differentiation, Th cell differentiation, the TNF signaling pathway, and CD4-positive alpha-beta T cell differentiation (p < 0.05). Interestingly, the Bm_EBF1 subpopulation demonstrated activated immune-related pathways prior to vaccination; however, these pathways were suppressed afterward, specifically at day 28 in the Ad5-nCoV group and at day 180 in the ICV group (Fig. 4D). This indicated a differential regulation of B cell immune function by the two vaccine regimens.

Analysis of total CD4+and CD8+T cells identified up-regulated genes associated with immune response-regulating and activating cell surface receptor signaling in the Ad5-nCoV group. Specifically, differentially expressed genes(DEGs) highly expressed in the CD4+TCM subgroup were concentrated on T cell activation and immune response activation through cell surface receptor signaling pathways. In the CD8+TEM subgroup, up-regulated DEGs in the Ad5-nCoV group enhanced immune response-regulating cell surface receptor signaling, Fc receptor signaling, T cell receptor signaling, and lymphocyte costimulation. In contrast, the ICV group primarily enhanced the expression of energy metabolism genes in total CD4+and CD8+T cells. These findings suggested that CD4+TCM and CD8+TEM cells contributed significantly to the enhanced immune response observed in the heterologous boosting group (Figs. 4E, F; Figures S10D and S10E).

B cell adaptations and BCR VJ genes rearrangement

BCR analysis showed that the ICV booster induced the expansion of small-sized clones (2–3 cells), whereas the Ad5-nCoV booster promoted the generation of more diverse and unique clones (Fig. 5A, B).

Longitudinal analysis of immunoglobulin isotype frequencies revealed distinct class between the two vaccine regimens. In the Ad5-nCoV group, IgG3 expression was significantly up-regulated at days 14 and 28 compared to baseline, whereas IgG4 levels decreased (p < 0.05). Conversely, the ICV group exhibited increased IgD and decreased IgG4 expression during the same period (p < 0.05). Comparative analysis further highlighted divergent isotype profiles; at day 14, the Ad5-nCoV group exhibited higher levels of IgM, IgG1, and IgG2, but lower levels of IgA1, IgA2, IgG3, and IgD compared to the ICV group. However, by day 28, IgA1 expression in the Ad5-nCoV group had increased, while IgG3, IgG4, and IgD levels were lower than those in the ICV group (Fig. 5C; Figure S11A).

Both groups preferentially used IGHV3-53 at Day 14 (Figure S11B). However, the Ad5-nCoV group exhibited higher usage of IGHV1-2, IGHV1-3, and IGHV3-23 (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, Ad5-nCoV boosting induced a shift toward shorter CDR3 regions in the heavy chain (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, we discovered the IGHJ4 was the most frequently used paired with IGHV in both group (Figure S11C). Light chain analysis revealed preferential usage of IGKV3-15 in both groups, with the ICV group showing skewed usage of IGKV1D-13 and IGKV3-20 (Figure S12).

T cell adaptations and TCR VJ genes rearrangement

Analysis of T cell clonal dynamics revealed modest clonal expansions (ranging from 2 to 10 cells per clone) in both groups following booster vaccination (Fig. 6A). While both regimens exhibited a decrease in the percentage of unique clonal types at day 14, a subsequent increase was observed at day 28, which was particularly pronounced in the Ad5-nCoV group (Fig. 6B).

At day 14, the Ad5-nCoV group demonstrated a significantly higher frequency of TRAV3-1, TRAV3-2, TRAV20, TRBV20-1, and TRBV27 lineage genes compared to the ICV group. In contrast, the ICV group was characterized by enriched usage of TRAV14/DV4, TRAV38-2/DV8, TRAV8-3, TRBV3-1, and TRBV7-8lineages (Fig. 6C).

Furthermore, analysis of the top five clonal combinations at each time point revealed distinct biases. The Ad5-nCoV group preferentially enriched combinations including TRAV13-1.TRBV27, TRAV13-2.TRBV15, TRAV17.TRBV27, TRAV20.TRBV5-1, and TRAV12-1.TRBV3-1. Meanwhile, the ICV group selectively expanded distinct TCR clonotypes, primarily TRAV29/DV5.TRBV6-6, TRAV38-2/DV8.TRBV11-2, TRAV38-2/DV8.TRBV7-8, and TRAV40.TRBV7-8 (Fig. 6D).

Discussion

Consistent with our previous other relevant studies [10–12], we also confirmed that heterologous prime-boost immunization yielded superior adaptive immune responses. However, a significant decline in antibody levels was observed in two regimen groups by day 180. Hence, we constructed the specific mAbs originated from two populations at day 180. We successfully generated seven mAbs (No. 1, 6, 11 from the ICV group; 265, 22, 119 and 61 from the Ad5-nCoV group) that demonstrated improved breadth of neutralization potency against variants as Beta, Gamma, Alpha, Delta, and Omicron, and No.265 mAb could also neutralize variants of BF.7 and XBB. An interesting point was that our mAbs from the Ad5-nCoV group tended to be co-dominated by NTD-binding and RBD-binding specific antibodies with greater pan-variant neutralization. A similar co-dominance of RBD and NTD also occurred in mAbs isolated with natural SARS-CoV-2 infections [13, 14], which hinted that the NTD served as a critical neutralizing epitope as the RBD, and supported the advantage of a combination of the NTD and RBD cocktail conceptual design. The multi-domain synergistic immunization strategy integrating optimized NTD, potent neutralizing RBD and broadly conserved S2 holds great promise for next-generation SARS-CoV-2 vaccine development and offers valuable implications for designing universal influenza vaccines [15].

By scRNA-seq, we identified that DEGs in plasmablasts were significantly enriched in the TNF signaling and Toll-like receptor signaling in the Ad5-nCoV group at day 14 compared to day 0, while ICV group activated the pathway of oxidative phosphorylation, indicating that two prime-boost immunization groups were more active in producing and secreting antibodies. Enrichment analysis of DEGs in memory B cells between the two groups showed that sustained upregulation of B cell immune response-related pathways in the Ad5-nCoV group at days 14 and day 28, which might explain the stronger antibody responses post the boost vaccination. Notably, this study firstly revealed significant upregulation of the Fc-gamma receptor signaling pathway involved in phagocytosis within CD4+ and CD8+ T cells following Ad5-nCoV vaccination when compared with ICV immunization.

Clonal analysis of BCR and TCR sequencing represented the expansion of T cell clones were observed in both groups, but small-sized clones of B cells were only observed in ICV group. We supposed that the ICV boosting provided a more substantial expansion advantage, with TCR clonal expansion demonstrating a significant difference, as previously reported [16]. Conversely, Ad5-nCoV could produce more unique clones of BCR and TCR. We also discovered that Ad5-nCoV boosting generated higher expressions of IgG1 and IgG2 than the ICV boosting, a finding also corroborated by our previous study [6]. Additionally, the HCDR3 length was shorted after the booster dose of Ad5-nCoV. Previous studies gave the evidence that antibodies with shorter CDR3 lengths and a higher number of SHMs were derived from mature B cells with higher affinity [17–20]. These affinity-enhanced antibodies are more likely to recognize the common conserved epitopes shared by variants [21]. Interestingly, our Ad5-nCoV boosting exhibited superior breadth in cross-neutralizing antibodies and the mAbs we constructed from the Ad5-nCoV vaccinees exhibited the higher SHM rate. Therefore, we conclude that Ad5-nCoV boosting tends to generate more broadly neutralizing antibodies.

IGHV3-53 has been acknowledged as the preferential usage germline in SARS-CoV-2 convalescent patients exhibiting broadly neutralizing activity against SARS-CoV-2 [22–24], which was strongly utilized 14 days after the booster dose of Ad5-nCoV or ICV in comparison to pre-immunization. Skewed genes of IGHV1-2, IGHV1-3 and IGHV3-23 used in COVID-19 patients [25, 26] were also observed in Ad5-nCoV group in comparison to ICV group, and germline IGHV3-23 was also utilized in No.265 mAb which was derived from the Ad5-nCoV group with the broadest neutralization spectrum. Another study concluded that IGHV1-2 was the most enriched IGHV genes utilized among a panel of RBD-targeting antibodies across 32 studies [27], suggesting possibility of an advantage in cross-reactivity to variants by the Ad5-nCoV boosting. Furthermore, the dominant IGV genes of IGHV3-7 and IGKV3-15 in severe patients were supposed to facilitate the vaccines design [28] which were also preferentially enriched following the Ad5-nCoV immunization(the VJ pairing of IGHV3-7.IGHJ4 and the usage of IGKV3-15). Notably, the germline IGKV1-39 frequently shared in our mAbs from the Ad5-nCoV group was increasingly used at day 14 post the Ad5-nCoV boosting. Moreover, TRAV3, TRAV20 and TRBV20-1 were reported as the top used TRBV genes in recovered patients [28, 29], with a preference for usage following Ad5-nCoV immunization. Although COVID-19 mRNA recipients always exhibit a positive bias toward TRAV29/DV5 [30, 31], which served as a potential marker for autoimmune diseases [32], hinted that individuals receiving ICV with frequent usage of TRAV29/DV5 should be carefully monitored, particularly those with underlying health conditions.

Limitations of the study: Firstly, convalescent patients or healthy participants who did not receive the third dose of the vaccination were not included as a comparison in our study. Additionally, functional evaluation tests for non-neutralizing antibodies are needed for further analysis. Furthermore, antigen epitopes screening was limited to RBD and NTD, and structure identification and animal experiments should be considered for deeper exploration. The small sample size for scRNA-Seq and immunological repertoire analysis also represents a limitation.

Conclusions

Above all, our study demonstrated that heterologous boosting with Ad5-nCoV resulted in greater cross-neutralization capacity, and more robust cellular immune responses than the homologous boosting with CoronaVac did. Ad5-nCoV boosting was more active in the immune response activation-related pathways, and the antibodies with shorter CDR3 lengths, a higher number of SHMs and a higher usage frequency of germline genes with neutralization activity might explain the immune enhancement mechanism of heterologous boosting.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was conducted as a sub-analysis of a previously reported phase 4 clinical trial (NCT04892459) that evaluated the safety and immunogenicity of heterologous boosting with Ad5-nCoV versus homologous boosting with CoronaVac in healthy adults aged 18–59 years. Briefly, participants who had completed a primary series of two doses of the inactivated COVID-19 vaccine (CoronaVac) within the past 3–6 months were randomized 1:1 to receive either a heterologous booster (Ad5-nCoV) or a homologous booster (CoronaVac). Blood samples were collected immediately prior to booster administration (Day 0) and at Days 14, 28, and 180 post-boost. For this immunological sub-study, 20 participants from each group were randomly selected, stratified by sex (male:female = 1:1) and age (18–45 years:46–60 years = 1:1), with serum and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) available for analysis (Fig. 1A). The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Jiangsu Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (Approval No. JSJK2021–A007-01).

Quantification of SARS-CoV-2-specific antibodies

Serum levels of IgG and IgA antibodies targeting the spike protein were measured using commercial ELISA kits (Nanjing Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Assays were performed for the wild-type (WT) strain and the variants Alpha (B.1.1.7), Beta (B.1.351), Gamma (P.1), Delta (B.1.617.2), and Omicron (B.1.1.529). The limit of quantification was determined to be 1.56 ng/mL.

Isolation of antigen-specific memory B cells and monoclonal antibody generation

At Day 180 post-boost, PBMCs from 12 participants (six per group) were thawed and CD3− CD19+B cells were isolated using the EasySep™ Human B Cell Isolation Kit (Stemcell, 17,954). Isolated B cells (1 × 106 cells/100 μL) were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies: PE anti-human IgG Fc, PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-human IgD, PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-human IgM, APC anti-human CD27, and 1 μg of Alexa Fluor 647-labeled SARS-CoV-2 wild-type spike protein. After incubation on ice for 30 min, S-specific memory B cells were defined as IgG+/IgM—/IgD—/CD27+/antigen+and sorted using a Sony/Beckman cell sorter into 96-well PCR plates containing lysis buffer.

Single-cell cDNA was synthesized using the Sc Reverse Transcriptase (Vazyme, N721) and oligo(dT) primers. The variable (V) regions of the heavy (H) and light (L) chains were amplified via nested PCR. Amplicons were cloned into a human IgG1 expression vector. Paired H and L chain plasmids were co-transfected into Expi293F or ExpiCHO cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher). Supernatants were harvested 5–7 days post-transfection. The germline gene usage, somatic hypermutation (SHM) rates, and complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) lengths were analyzed using IgBLAST.

Evaluation of mAb binding and neutralizing activity

Binding activity was assessed by ELISA. Plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with 2 μg/mL of recombinant WT SARS-CoV-2 trimeric spike protein, RBD, or NTD. Plates were blocked, and mAb supernatants were added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Bound antibodies were detected using HRP-conjugated anti-human IgG. Optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm and 630 nm. Antibodies with an OD450-630 > 0.2 (at least three times the background) were considered positive. High-titer mAbs (OD ≥ 1.0) were purified using Protein A MagBeads (GenScript) and quantified via BCA assay. Binding EC50 values were determined using a 4-fold serial dilution starting at 5 μg/mL.

Neutralizing activity was evaluated using a vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV)-based pseudovirus system expressing the spike protein of WT SARS-CoV-2 or variants (Table S1). mAbs were serially diluted 3-fold starting at 10 μg/mL and incubated with pseudovirus (13,000 TCID50) at 37 °C for 1 h. HEK293–ACE2 cells (2 × 104 cells/well) were added, and luciferase activity was measured 24 h later using the Bright-Lite Luciferase Assay System (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., DD1204). The 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50) was calculated using GraphPad Prism 9.5.0, with a cutoff of 10,000 ng/mL defining positive neutralization.

Antibody affinity

Antibody affinity (KD) was determined using biolayer interferometry (Octet RED 96, ForteBio). Purified mAbs (5 μg/mL) were immobilized on Protein A sensors. RBD, NTD, or S1 antigens were serially diluted 2-fold in PBST and associated with the sensor tips. Data were analyzed using Octet Data Analysis Software.

Single-cell immune profiling

For single-cell sequencing, PBMCs from six participants (three per group) collected at Days 0, 14, 28, and 180 were stained with the Zombie NIR™Fixable Viability Kit(Biolegend, 423,105) and a panel of surface markers to identify memory CD4+T cells, memory CD8+T cells, memory B cells, and plasmablasts(Alexa Fluor 700 anti-human CD4(Biolegend, 557,922), Brilliant Violet 510 anti-human CD8(Biolegend,563256), PE/Cyanine5 anti-human CD28(Biolegend,302910), PE anti-human CD95(Biolegend,305608), Brilliant Violet 421TM anti-human CD38(Biolegend, 303,526), APC anti-human CD27(Biolegend, 302,810), and PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-human CD19(Biolegend, 348,208)). Live cells were sorted using a FACSAria Fusion (BD Biosciences) and counted. Equal numbers of sorted cells from each time point were pooled for library preparation.

Single-cell RNA and V(D)J libraries were generated using the Chromium Next GEM Reagent Kit v2 (10X Genomics) and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq platform by Novogene (Beijing, China), targeting 17,000 reads/cell for RNA and 5,000 reads/cell for V(D)J.

Bioinformatics analysis

Raw scRNA-seq data were processed using the NovoMagic platform. Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human reference genome using Cell Ranger (v4.0) and STAR. Low-quality cells (<200 genes detected or >20% mitochondrial content) and doublets were filtered out. Data normalization, scaling, and clustering were performed using Seurat (v4.0). Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was used for dimensionality reduction. Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis was performed using the FindMarkers function (|log2FC| >0.2, FDR < 0.05). Functional enrichment was conducted using Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases.

TCR and BCR repertoires were analyzed using Cell Ranger (v4.0) and the R package Immunarch. Only productive rearrangements with CDR3 lengths of 5–27 amino acids were retained.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means±standard deviations (SD) or medians (interquartile ranges, IQR). Comparisons between groups were performed using Student’s t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Categorical variables were analyzed using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact tests. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 19.0, GraphPad Prism 8, or R (v4.2.1), with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
