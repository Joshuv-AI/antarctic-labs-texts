Aptamers in cancer therapy: Why has clinical translation lagged behind preclinical promise?
Authors: Thoa T. Tran, Vu L. Tran, Melissa L. Fishel, Ba-Thong Nguyen, Jianjun Zhang, Salvatore Condello, Xiaoling Zhong, Max Schmidt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aptamers have attracted substantial interest as cancer therapeutics due to their high target specificity, chemical programmability, and versatility in drug and RNA delivery; however, despite extensive preclinical development, clinical translation in oncology has remained extremely limited, with no aptamer-based therapies approved for cancer indications to date. This gap reflects not a failure of binding affinity, but a fundamental misalignment between aptamer selection and evaluation paradigms and the biological complexity of human tumors. Most aptamers are selected against purified proteins or homogeneous cell lines that fail to capture tumor heterogeneity, dynamic target regulation, and microenvironmental influences encountered in patients, while inconsistent assessment of pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and functional activity further limits translational predictability. Through analysis of representative clinical programs, including the systemic nucleolin-targeting aptamer AS1411, the microenvironment-modulating CXCL12 inhibitor NOX-A12, and the locally administered personalized platform AM003, this review highlights how delivery strategy, target context, and clinical deployment critically shape therapeutic outcomes. Recurrent translational barriers related to systemic exposure, tumor accessibility, regulatory pathways, and competition with established modalities are identified, together with lessons from both failed and emerging programs. Finally, we discuss practical strategies to improve clinical alignment, including human-relevant selection models, localized or combination therapies, and AI-assisted design, positioning aptamers for context-appropriate roles in future precision oncology.

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of death globally, with timely diagnosis and effective treatment essential to improving outcomes [1]. While current therapies such as surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy have advanced cancer care, they face challenges including resistance, non-specificity, toxicity, and recurrence [2,3]. Although targeted and immune-based therapies offer more precision, they are often limited by tumor resistance, toxic side effects, and short-lived responses [1]. In this context, aptamers have emerged as a promising class of therapeutic molecules for cancer treatment [4–9].

Aptamers are short, single-stranded DNA or RNA molecules that are selected using the Systematic Evolution of Ligands by Exponential Enrichment (SELEX) technique [10–12]. These molecules exhibit high specificity and affinity for their targets, which can include proteins, cells, or small molecules [11,13,14]. Aptamers offer several distinct advantages over traditional therapeutic agents, including their small size, ease of chemical synthesis, low immunogenicity, and potential for chemical modification to enhance stability and functionality [13, 15–17]. Aptamers offer immense potential to overcome critical challenges in cancer treatment by acting as targeted therapeutic agents, precise drug delivery vehicles, and modulators of the tumor microenvironment [13,15–17]. Their versatility has driven advancements in therapeutic aptamers against cancer cells, cancer stem cells for targeted therapy [18,19], aptamer-mediated immunotherapies [20,21], and aptamer-drug conjugates/ aptamer-mediated drug delivery systems [4, 5,22] (Fig. 1).

Despite several aptamers progressing to clinical trials for various diseases, their application in oncology remains significantly underexplored. The approval of the first aptamer-based drug, Pegaptanib (Macugen) in 2004 by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for neovascular age-related macular degeneration has resulted in substantial growth of aptamer-based therapeutics [23,24]. Nearly two decades later, in 2023, Avacincaptad pegol (Izervay) marked a milestone as the second FDA-approved aptamer-based drug [25,26]. Izervay is indicated for the treatment of geographic atrophy (GA) secondary to age-related macular degeneration (AMD), a progressive and irreversible retinal condition that leads to vision loss. However, no aptamer-based therapeutic has achieved regulatory approval for cancer, and most oncology-focused aptamers have stalled at pre-clinical or early clinical stages. This disparity highlights a critical gap between the apparent preclinical potential of aptamers and their practical performance in human cancer therapy.

Importantly, this gap does not primarily reflect a failure of target binding or molecular specificity. Rather, emerging evidence suggests that translational limitations arise from deeper structural and methodological issues, including the use of simplified selection models that inadequately capture tumor heterogeneity, dynamic target regulation, and microenvironmental complexity, as well as inconsistent evaluation of aptamer structure, binding mechanisms, pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, and functional activity in vivo. In addition, oncology clinical trials involving aptamers have faced challenges related to insufficient tumor exposure, unpredictable pharmacokinetics, limited efficacy relative to standard-of-care therapies, and unclear regulatory positioning.

Accordingly, this review does not aim to comprehensively catalog aptamer technologies or reiterate their theoretical advantages. Instead, we critically examine the current clinical landscape of aptamer-based cancer therapeutics, with particular emphasis on failed and discontinued oncology programs, recurrent translational bottlenecks, and lessons learned from clinical trial experience. We analyze how selection strategies, evaluation methodologies, and biological assumptions have contributed to the disconnect between preclinical success and clinical outcomes. Finally, we discuss emerging strategies, including physiologically relevant selection platforms, standardized pharmacokinetic and functional assessment, and rational chemical and delivery optimization, that may better align aptamer development with clinical and regulatory realities. By focusing on the present limitations and actionable paths forward, this review aims to clarify the realistic role of aptamers in precision oncology.

Overview of Aptamers

Aptamers are short, single-stranded nucleic acid molecules-either DNA or RNA-that have the remarkable ability to fold into intricate three-dimensional structures [10,12]. This folding enable aptamer to bind specific molecular targets with high affinity and specificity, akin to the way antibodies interact with antigens [7,27,28]. Aptamers are emerging as versatile tools in biotechnology, diagnostics, and therapeutics due to their unique properties, such as high stability, ease of synthesis, low immunogenicity, and modifiability for enhanced functionality [13,17,29,30]. In oncology, these properties have driven extensive preclinical exploration of aptamers as targeting ligands, immune modulators, and delivery vehicles for small molecules and RNA therapeutics.

However, despite decades of development and the generation of thousands of cancer-targeting aptamers, clinical translation has been extremely limited. This discrepancy highlights that aptamer performance is not determined solely by binding affinity or specificity, but by how selection, characterization, and evaluation strategies align with the biological and clinical realities of human tumors and aptamer pharmacology.

Aptamer Selection: Strengths and Translational Limitations

Aptamer selection is fundamentally enabled by the direct genotype-phenotype linkage of nucleic acids, whereby sequence composition directly encodes binding function. Aptamers are generated through SELEX-based selection processes that exploit the direct relationship between nucleic acid sequence and binding function. While SELEX enables the identification of high-affinity ligands under controlled conditions, the choice of selection model critically shapes translational outcomes [10,13,17,30]. Unlike antibody generation, SELEX begins with a highly diverse and unbiased synthetic oligonucleotide library, often exceeding 10 13−10 17 unique sequences, allowing target recognition to emerge through iterative in vitro evolution rather than prior structural assumptions [13,15,17,30]. In this process, the library is exposed to a defined target under controlled conditions, and sequences capable of target binding are partitioned from non-binders, recovered, and enzymatically amplified to generate progressively enriched libraries [13,15,31]. Repeated selection cycles apply increasing selective pressure and stringency, thereby enriching functional binders based solely on performance while preserving successful sequence-structure relationships across rounds. At the conclusion of selection, enriched aptamer candidates are identified by high-throughput sequencing and further characterized using biophysical and biochemical assays to confirm target recognition, binding affinity, and specificity [4,7,8,29, 32]. This unbiased evolutionary process (Fig. 2) enables the discovery of ligands capable of recognizing a broad spectrum of molecular targets, including those that are difficult to access using conventional protein-based approaches.

After selection, thoroughly validation of aptamer–target interactions require integration of multiple biophysical and structural techniques. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) [33,34] and microscale thermophoresis (MST) [35] are commonly used to quantify binding affinity and kinetics under near-physiological conditions, while isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) [14,36] provides thermodynamic characterization of binding interactions.

Structural and conformational analyses are typically performed using circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy to assess secondary structure formation, particularly G-quadruplex or stem-loop configurations [14, 37]. In addition, computational modeling and molecular dynamics simulations are increasingly employed to predict aptamer folding and optimize target interactions at atomic resolution [35].

Functional validation is further supported by cell-based binding assays, internalization studies, and downstream pathway analyses. However, variability in assay conditions and lack of standardized validation pipelines remain key challenges, limiting cross-study comparability and translational predictability.

Aptamers and Therapeutic Platforms: A Translational Perspective

Aptamers are frequently compared with monoclonal antibodies due to their shared capacity for high-affinity and target-specific binding [4,9,30,38]. However, within the landscape of clinical oncology, aptamers must be considered in the context of a broader spectrum of therapeutic platforms, including nanobodies, peptides, and lipid nanoparticle (LNP)-based RNA systems [5]. Each of these modalities exhibits distinct pharmacokinetic properties, delivery mechanisms, and translational constraints that influence their clinical performance.

Aptamers and cancer

Recent advances in aptamer engineering and delivery have expanded their applicability in cancer-related research, particularly in contexts where conventional antibody-based approaches face practical limitations. Rather than serving as direct replacements for antibodies, aptamers are increasingly viewed as complementary molecular tools whose strengths-such as modular design, chemical tunability, and target accessibility-may address specific unmet needs in cancer therapeutics and diagnostics [32,40–42]. In oncology, aptamers have been explored across a range of functional modalities, including receptor antagonism or activation, multivalent or bispecific targeting, and combination strategies designed to enhance localization or reduce off-target effects.

In cancer immunotherapy, aptamers have demonstrated the ability to modulate immune pathways in preclinical models through mechanisms analogous to immune checkpoint antibodies, while offering greater structural flexibility and control over valency and engagement kinetics. However, the clinical relevance of these approaches remains contingent on overcoming persistent challenges related to pharmacokinetics, delivery, and durability of target engagement. Consequently, the potential contribution of aptamers to precision oncology will depend not on theoretical versatility alone, but on the successful integration of rational design, clinically relevant delivery strategies, and performance benchmarks aligned with existing standard-of-care therapies [32, 40–42]. This section examines current applications of aptamers in cancer with an emphasis on where they add distinct value, where limitations remain, and what lessons emerge from preclinical and early translational studies [32].

Targeting Cancer Cells, Cancer Stem Cells, and the Tumor Microenvironment

Therapeutic resistance, tumor relapses, and metastasis are frequently driven by heterogeneous cancer cell populations, including cancer stem cells (CSCs), as well as supportive components of the tumor microenvironment. Aptamers have been explored as precision targeting ligands capable of selectively recognizing these resistant populations and delivering therapeutic payloads while sparing normal tissues.

Targeting Cancer Cells

Aptamers have been used to target cancer cells by recognizing cell-surface or nuclear-associated proteins and enabling intracellular delivery of functional nucleic acids. The nucleolin-binding aptamer AS1411 has served as a prototypical platform for nuclear targeting, enabling delivery of splice-switching oligonucleotides and transcription-modulating RNAs into nucleolin-expressing tumor cells, resulting in altered gene expression and antitumor effects in preclinical models [43].

In parallel, Small activating RNAs (saRNAs) have emerged as promising tools to selectively enhance silenced tumor-suppressor gene expression without altering DNA sequences. These ~21-mer duplex RNAs enter the nucleus via Argonaute proteins and activate gene transcription at promoter sites [44]. Notable targets include tumor-suppressor genes C/EBPα and DPYSL3, both implicated in cancer suppression. Systemic delivery of C/EBPα-saRNA using dendrimers or aptamers like P19 and TR14 showed strong antitumor activity in hepatocellular carcinoma and pancreatic cancer models, outperforming standard treatments [45]. Similarly, saV2–9-saRNA targeting DPYSL3v2 conjugated to PSMA-binding aptamer A10–3.2 inhibited prostate cancer metastasis in vivo [46]. These studies demonstrate the functional integrity and efficacy of aptamer-saRNA conjugates in tumor-specific gene reactivation. Although thus far limited to two gene targets, this approach offers a powerful framework for developing epigenetic RNA therapeutics.

Targeting Cancer Stem Cells (CSCs)

CSCs represent a subpopulation of tumor cells with self-renewal capacity, intrinsic therapy resistance, and a central role in relapse and metastasis [47]. Despite their recognized importance in disease relapses and metastasis, effective clinical targeting of CSCs has remained elusive, and multiple CSC-directed strategies have entered clinical testing only to show limited success or modest benefit. Monoclonal antibodies against putative CSC surface markers such as CD44, including the humanized anti-CD44 antibody RG7356, demonstrated acceptable safety but only modest clinical efficacy, with limited disease stabilization and no clear tumor regression in early phase trials [48]; small-molecule inhibitors of CSC-associated pathways-including Notch, Hedgehog, and Wnt-have largely failed in solid tumors. DLL4-targeting Notch antibodies like demcizumab achieved preliminary pathway modulation but no durable clinical benefit [49], Hedgehog inhibitors such as vismodegib showed limited efficacy beyond basal cell carcinoma [50] and the Wnt porcupine inhibitor WNT974 produced only stable disease with dose-limiting dysgeusia in Phase I studies [51].

These outcomes underscore key limitations of current CSC-directed therapies, including poor tissue penetration, marker redundancy and plasticity, adaptive signaling, and toxicity from shared stemness pathways, highlighting the need for more selective, context-aware approaches. Aptamers targeting CSC-associated surface markers, including CD133 [47,52,53], EpCAM [54], and CD44 [55], have been developed to selectively deliver chemotherapeutic agents or RNA therapeutics to these populations.

CD133-targeting aptamers have been used to deliver doxorubicin and other payloads to CSC-enriched tumors, resulting in reduced tumor growth and improved therapeutic indices in immunocompetent mouse models and patient-derived organoids [53,56]. Similarly, EpCAM- and CD44-targeting aptamers demonstrated selective tumor inhibition in colorectal, ovarian, and breast cancer models, with bispecific targeting strategies partially addressing tumor heterogeneity [55,57].

Despite these promising results, CSC-targeted aptamer strategies remain preclinical, limited by variability in marker expression, delivery efficiency, and the absence of clinically validated CSC biomarkers.

Targeting the Tumor Microenvironment

Beyond cancer cells, aptamers can also be designed to target components of the tumor microenvironment, which plays a critical role in tumor progression and resistance to therapy. The tumor microenvironment consists of genetically stable non-malignant cells, such as tumor-associated stromal cells and immune cells, which are vital for tumor support and immune evasion. [58] These cells represent alternative and attractive therapeutic targets.

One noteworthy advancement is the development of RNA aptamers targeting tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells (TIMCs). TIMCs contribute to tumor growth and metastasis by suppressing immune responses. Using the cell-SELEX process, researchers identified four TIMC-specific aptamers [38,58]. These aptamers do not bind to peripheral myeloid cells, ensuring selectivity for the tumor microenvironment.

Collectively, aptamer-based targeting of cancer cells, CSCs, and the tumor microenvironment demonstrates high specificity and modularity in preclinical systems. However, heterogeneous target expression, delivery limitations, and lack of standardized clinical biomarkers have constrained progression toward clinical evaluation.

Enhancing Immunotherapy with Aptamers: Opportunities and Translational Constraints

Immune checkpoint blockade therapies targeting CTLA-4 [20,59] and PD-1/PD-L1 [60,61] have transformed cancer treatment, producing durable responses in select patient populations. However, limited response rates, acquired resistance, and immune-related toxicities highlight the need for alternative or complementary immunomodulatory strategies. Aptamers have been explored as flexible immunotherapeutic agents capable of antagonizing inhibitory pathways, activating costimulatory receptors, and enabling spatially restricted immune modulation through multivalent or bispecific designs (Fig. 3) [62].

Aptamers can modulate immune checkpoint pathways through multiple mechanistic modalities that differ from or complement monoclonal antibodies. These include competitive inhibition of ligand-receptor interactions, steric hindrance at binding interfaces, and induction of receptor internalization, which can result in functional receptor downregulation [16,20,59,63]. In addition, multivalent or engineered aptamer constructs can promote receptor clustering, enabling tunable activation or inhibition of immune signaling pathways depending on their structural design.

Functionally, these mechanisms allow aptamers to act either as antagonists that block inhibitory signaling (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 axis) or as agonists that enhance immune activation through receptor crosslinking [60,61]. In contrast, monoclonal antibodies primarily exert their effects through sustained receptor blockade and, in some cases, Fc-mediated effector functions such as antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity [5, 62,64,65]. While aptamers offer greater structural programmability and reversible binding dynamics, their shorter half-life may limit sustained immune modulation, highlighting a key trade-off between mechanistic flexibility and pharmacokinetic durability.

Antagonistic Aptamers Targeting Immunosuppressive Pathways

Antagonistic aptamers have been developed against multiple immune checkpoint and immunosuppressive targets, including CTLA-4 [20,59], PD-1/PD-L1 [61], TIM3 [66,67], LAG3 [68,69], IL-10R [70], C5aR [71,72], TGF-β receptors [73,74], and CD47 [75]. In preclinical tumor models, these aptamers restored T-cell activity, reduced immunosuppressive cell populations, and enhanced antitumor immunity, often achieving effects comparable to monoclonal antibodies, with reduced systemic toxicity in some settings.

Despite these encouraging findings, none of these antagonistic aptamers have advanced into late-stage oncology trials. Translational barriers include rapid systemic clearance, insufficient and transient receptor occupancy, and high sensitivity of aptamer activity to multimerization state and dosing schedule. Moreover, immune checkpoint antibodies have established a high clinical benchmark, requiring aptamer-based alternatives to demonstrate not only comparable efficacy but also clear advantages in safety, durability, or patient stratification.

Agonistic and Bispecific Aptamers: Precision with Practical Limitations

Agonistic aptamers targeting costimulatory receptors such as 4–1BB [76], OX40 [77], CD28 [78], and CD40 [79,80] have demonstrated potent immune activation in preclinical models when appropriately multimerized, recapitulating antibody-like receptor clustering effects. Bispecific aptamers further extend this concept by coupling immune activation with tumor-localized targeting, for example by linking costimulatory aptamers to ligands recognizing PSMA, VEGF, or tumor-associated transporters [21,55,81].

While these designs offer elegant solutions to reduce systemic immune activation, their clinical translation is constrained by structural complexity, manufacturing scalability, and the need for precise spatial and temporal control of receptor engagement in vivo. To date, these constructions remain largely confined to preclinical evaluation.

Combinatorial Aptamer Platforms and siRNA Chimeras.

Aptamer-based chimeric platforms, particularly aptamer–siRNA conjugates, have been developed to simultaneously target tumor cells and modulate oncogenic or immunosuppressive pathways. PSMA-targeted, EpCAM-targeted, CD40-targeted, and AS1411-based aptamer–siRNA constructs have demonstrated efficient tumor targeting, gene silencing, and immune activation in mouse models [82–84]. Notably, AS1411–SMG1 chimeras [85] enhanced tumor inflammation and sensitized tumors to immune checkpoint blockade without overt toxicity.

However, these multifunctional constructs introduce additional translational challenges, including pharmacokinetic unpredictability, intracellular delivery efficiency, regulatory complexity, and batch reproducibility, which have so far limited their progression toward clinical evaluation.

Collectively, immunotherapy-focused aptamer strategies illustrate both the conceptual strengths and practical limitations of the platform. While aptamers provide unmatched modularity and precision in preclinical systems, their clinical impact will depend on achieving durable target engagement, predictable pharmacokinetics, and efficacy benchmarks comparable to established antibody-based immunotherapies. These considerations underscore the importance of aligning aptamer design with translational constraints early in development.

Aptamers as Delivery Platforms for RNA Therapeutics and Chemotherapeutic Agents

The efficacy of anticancer therapies, such as mRNA, siRNA, miRNA, and chemotherapeutic agents’ replacement treatments, is often limited by their off-target effects in healthy tissues. These unintended interactions can lead to significant toxicity, limiting the dosage and overall effectiveness of these treatments. Addressing this challenge, targeted delivery strategies using aptamers have emerged as promising solutions (Fig. 4) [60,83–85]. By improving specificity and promoting intracellular uptake within tumors and the surrounding tumor microenvironment, aptamers enhance the therapeutic index of anticancer agents [39].

Aptamers operate through their ability to bind specific extracellular receptors on the surface of cancer cells. This targeted binding triggers receptor-mediated endocytosis, allowing the delivery of conjugated therapeutic agents, such as small drugs and short therapeutic oligonucleotides, directly into cancer cells [9,16,29,38,86]. By leveraging these receptor-mediated pathways, aptamers achieve greater precision in drug delivery, reducing systemic exposure and minimizing collateral damage to healthy tissues.

Direct Aptamer-Drug and Aptamer-RNA Conjugates

Direct conjugation of aptamers to chemotherapeutic agents and therapeutic RNAs is being tested as a means to enhance tumor-selective delivery [9,16,29,38,86,87]. Representative examples include the sgc8 DNA aptamer targeting PTK7 [88], which enabled selective delivery of 5-fluorouracil to cancer cells. Aptamer–siRNA chimeras targeting oncogenic and immune-related pathways further demonstrated efficient gene silencing and antitumor activity in vivo [83–85,89].

While these studies establish proof-of-concept for aptamer-mediated delivery, translational challenges persist, including pharmacokinetic instability, intracellular trafficking efficiency, and regulatory complexity of multifunctional constructs.

Aptamer-Functionalized Nanoparticle Systems

Nanoparticles have revolutionized drug delivery by providing a versatile platform for transporting therapeutic agents with enhanced stability, controlled release, and improved bioavailability. Aptamer-functionalized nanoparticles further augment this potential by adding a layer of specificity, enabling targeted delivery to cancer cells or specific components of the tumor microenvironment [90].

Liposome-Based Delivery.

Liposomes, spherical vesicles formed by lipid bilayers, are commonly used as nanocarriers for therapeutic agents. Functionalizing liposomes with aptamers significantly enhance their specificity and efficiency. For instance, researchers utilized the Apt1 aptamer, which targets the CD44 receptor-a cell-surface marker overexpressed in many tumors-to functionalize siRNA-loaded liposomes [91]. This system effectively silenced the luciferase reporter gene in vitro and in vivo in a triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) model. The combination of aptamer specificity and liposome delivery systems exemplifies the potential of aptamer-driven nanoparticle platforms.

Polyamide-Amine (PAMAM) Dendrimers.

PAMAM dendrimers are synthetic, hyperbranched polymers extensively studied as carriers for small RNAs and drugs. Researchers have developed a theragnostic (therapeutic and diagnostic) platform using persistent luminescence nanoparticles (PLNPs) conjugated to PAMAM dendrimers. To ensure cancer specificity, the AS1411 aptamer, which binds nucleolin (a protein overexpressed on the surface of cancer cells), was incorporated into this platform. This aptamer-functionalized nanocomposite successfully delivered doxorubicin to tumor sites and enabled real-time monitoring of drug biodistribution through luminescence imaging. In HeLa tumor-bearing mice, the PLNP-PAMAM-AS1411/Dox nanocomposite outperformed free doxorubicin and untargeted nanocomposites in inhibiting tumor growth [92].

However, nanoparticle-based aptamer delivery introduces additional variables related to manufacturing reproducibility, biodistribution, and regulatory approval, which have limited advancement beyond preclinical studies. Aptamers offer a versatile and programmable interface for targeted drug and RNA delivery, particularly in preclinical models of resistant and heterogeneous tumors. Nevertheless, their successful clinical deployment will require improved pharmacokinetic control, scalable manufacturing, and demonstration of clear benefit over existing delivery technologies.

Clinical Trial Experience and Lessons from Oncology Aptamer Programs

Clinical translation of aptamer-based therapeutics in oncology has been limited, despite extensive preclinical promise, and a small number of clinical studies provide critical insight into the factors underlying this gap. Among these, systemic therapeutic trials with AS1411 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT00512083, NCT00881244, NCT00740441, NCT01034410), the a Microenvironment-Targeted Therapeutic Aptamer NOX-A12 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01194934, NCT03168139, NCT01486797, NCT04121455) and more recent personalized or locally administered approaches such as AM003 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06258330), illustrate distinct translational trajectories shaped by pharmacokinetic constraints, target accessibility, and clinical deployment strategy.

Translation of Aptamers from Preclinical Validation to Clinical Trials

The successful transition of RNA aptamers from preclinical investigation to clinical evaluation requires a multi-layered and rigorously validated developmental framework [5,7,17,39]. Unlike conventional small molecules or monoclonal antibodies, aptamers must demonstrate not only high binding affinity but also durable biological activity, pharmacologic stability, tumor-selective biodistribution, and reproducible therapeutic efficacy across experimental systems before clinical consideration [4,5,7,9,27].

The translational pathway begins with biologically grounded target selection. Following selection, candidates undergo detailed in vitro mechanistic validation. Beyond demonstrating nanomolar binding affinity, functional studies confirm pathway inhibition and downstream biological effects. The next translational threshold is in vivo validation in tumor-bearing models [4,5,7,9,27]. Importantly, these studies included biodistribution and safety assessments showing tumor-selective accumulation and minimal systemic toxicity. However, strong preclinical tumor reduction does not guarantee clinical success. To address the intrinsic instability of molecules, chemical optimization is required prior to clinical advancement. Modifications such as 2′-fluoropyrimidine substitution and PEGylation enhance nuclease resistance and prolong half-life but must preserve structural integrity and target affinity [17,93–96]. Without such stabilization, promising candidates may exhibit rapid clearance and insufficient therapeutic exposure in humans.

Only aptamers that demonstrate mechanistic clarity, in vivo efficacy, and pharmacologic optimization proceed to clinical trials. NOX-A12 progressed to Phase 1/2 evaluation after preclinical studies demonstrated its ability to disrupt CXCL12-mediated chemotaxis, inhibit vasculogenesis, and enhance immune cell infiltration following radiotherapy [22,94,95]. Clinical trials subsequently confirmed immune modulation and disease stabilization in selected patient populations, validating the biological rationale established preclinically. Similarly, AS1411 entered Phase II trials based on its mechanistic interference with nucleolin-associated oncogenic signaling. Although objective responses were limited in unselected cohorts, durable benefit observed in rare responders highlighted the importance of biomarker-driven patient stratification, an insight that now informs next-generation translational strategies [5,9,82,85,97].

Despite numerous aptamers demonstrating robust preclinical tumor suppression, only a small fraction has reached clinical evaluation. The progression of RNA aptamers from preclinical validation to clinical trials is a rigorous and selective process. While high-affinity binding and tumor suppression are necessary foundations, successful translation ultimately depends on pharmacologic stabilization, biologically validated target dependency, appropriate patient selection, and integration with rational therapeutic strategies. The limited but informative clinical experiences to date underscore both the promise of aptamer therapeutics and the critical importance of overcoming translational barriers to achieve consistent clinical benefit.

Systemic Therapeutic Aptamer Translation: Lessons learned from AS1411

The most extensively evaluated therapeutic aptamer in oncology is AS1411, a G-rich DNA aptamer targeting nucleolin that advanced into multiple clinical trials, including a randomized Phase II study in patients with relapsed or refractory acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [98]. In this trial, AS1411 was administered systemically in combination with high dose cytarabine and compared with cytarabine alone. Despite promising preclinical activity and evidence of target engagement, the study failed to demonstrate a significant improvement in response rate, remission durability, or overall survival, and the trial was ultimately terminated.

The clinical failure of AS1411 does not invalidate aptamers as therapeutic platforms but rather highlights the limitations of stoichiometric inhibition strategies [97,98]. Recent work repurposing AS1411 as a tumor-targeting and E3-recruiting module within PROTAC architectures demonstrates how aptamers can regain clinical relevance when embedded in catalytic, event-driven mechanisms such as targeted protein degradation [82]. The repurposing of AS1411 as an intrinsic tumor-targeting and E3-recruiting component of PROTACs illustrates how aptamer failures in conventional therapeutic formats can be overcome by embedding aptamers within catalytic degradation frameworks rather than relying on direct inhibitory activity alone.

Similarly, AS1411 has been successfully integrated into nanocomposite platforms for photothermal and photodynamic therapy, where it confers tumor selectivity rather than direct cytotoxicity. When conjugated to photosensitizers or photothermal nanomaterials, AS1411 enables spatially controlled, light-triggered generation of heat or reactive oxygen species, promoting ferroptosis, immunogenic cell death, and stress pathway activation. In these contexts, transient tumor residence and limited systemic exposure are no longer liabilities but become compatible with localized therapeutic activation [99].

Quantitative pharmacokinetic data further illustrate the translational limitations of AS1411 in systemic oncology applications [100]. Clinical studies reported steady-state plasma concentrations in the range of approximately 1–6 μM or less during continuous infusion, while a Phase II study showed rapid blood clearance with a half-life of 1.71 h and a maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) of 25.4 μg/mL (~3 μM). Reported GI50 values for AS1411 in many cancer cell lines fall within the range of 1–10 μM [100,101]. Taken together, these data suggest that clinical exposure levels may only partially overlap with concentrations required for biological activity, potentially contributing to limited therapeutic efficacy in unselected patient populations.

Mechanistically, the limited clinical efficacy of AS1411 can be attributed to multiple translational constraints that were not fully addressed during early clinical development [101]. Although high-affinity binding to nucleolin was demonstrated, the dosing strategy may not have achieved sufficient and sustained systemic exposure to maintain effective receptor occupancy. In addition, the absence of validated pharmacodynamic biomarkers limited the ability to monitor target engagement and optimize dose selection in patients. Tumor uptake efficiency was not quantitatively assessed, raising the possibility that inadequate intertumoral accumulation contributed to suboptimal therapeutic activity. Furthermore, patient selection was not stratified based on nucleolin expression or tumor dependency, potentially diluting clinical response across heterogeneous populations.

Olaptesed Pegol (NOX-A12): Lessons from a Microenvironment-Targeted Therapeutic Aptamer.

Olaptesed pegol (NOX-A12) is a pegylated L-oligoribonucleotide aptamer that neutralizes the chemokine CXCL12, a key regulator of tumor–microenvironment interactions, immune cell trafficking, and malignant cell retention within protective niches. By disrupting CXCL12–CXCR4/CXCR7 signaling, NOX-A12 reduces microenvironment-mediated survival signals and mobilizes tumor cells from stromal compartments, thereby increasing their susceptibility to cytotoxic and radiation-based therapies [94,95].

In a Phase I/II clinical study in relapsed or refractory chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT01486797), NOX-A12 was evaluated in combination with bendamustine and rituximab [95]. The regimen was well tolerated, exhibited predictable pharmacokinetics, and induced rapid mobilization of leukemic cells into the peripheral circulation, consistent with effective CXCL12 blockade. Clinically meaningful response rates were observed, including in genetically high-risk patients, supporting the concept that disrupting protective microenvironments can enhance therapeutic efficacy without introducing significant additional toxicity.

Clinical evaluation of the CXCL12-neutralizing aptamer NOX-A12 further illustrates the importance of pharmacokinetic optimization for therapeutic efficacy. In the GLORIA phase I/II trial combining NOX-A12 with radiotherapy in newly diagnosed glioblastoma (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT04121455) [94], dose escalation from 200 to 600 mg per week resulted in a corresponding increase in plasma drug levels, demonstrating dose-dependent systemic exposure. Treatment was well tolerated with no dose-limiting toxicities, and the maximum tolerated dose was not reached, establishing 600 mg/week as the recommended phase II dose. Notably, 9 of 10 patients (90%) exhibited radiographic responses, including 4 partial remissions. Median progression-free survival (PFS) was 174 days, with a 6-month PFS rate of 40%, and median overall survival (OS) reached 389 days. In addition, reductions in tumor perfusion (rCBV) were observed in 90% of patients, indicating biological activity within the tumor microenvironment [94]. These findings contrast with earlier aptamer programs such as AS1411 and suggest that improved pharmacokinetic exposure, combined with mechanism-driven patient selection, may enable meaningful clinical responses [100].

Collectively, these studies illustrate why NOX-A12 represents a rare translational success among oncology aptamers. Its extracellular, microenvironment-focused mechanism aligns well with aptamer pharmacokinetics, avoiding the need for sustained intracellular delivery or prolonged target occupancy. Rather than functioning as a direct cytotoxic agent, NOX-A12 acts as a sensitizing therapy that reshapes tumor niches to improve responses to established treatments. This case underscores that aptamer-based therapeutics are most likely to succeed when their molecular properties are matched to context-appropriate biological roles within combination strategies.

Personalized and Locally Delivered Aptamer Therapies: Lessons learned from AM003.

A distinct translational strategy is illustrated by AM003, a first-in-human, individualized aptamer-based immunotherapy developed by Aummune and currently evaluated in a Phase I basket trial of advanced solid tumors (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT06258330). Unlike conventional aptamer therapeutics selected against predefined molecular targets, AM003 incorporates a patient-specific “variable domain” identified de novo based on its ability to induce cytotoxicity in autologous tumor cells cultured ex vivo. This variable domain is combined with a T-cell–engaging domain and a CpG-rich immunostimulatory domain, forming a multifunctional construct designed to promote local tumor cell killing and immune activation.

Notably, AM003 is administered intratumorally or locally, thereby circumventing several systemic barriers that have historically limited aptamer translation, including rapid renal clearance, unpredictable pharmacokinetics, and insufficient tumor exposure. In the ongoing Phase I study (AM003-FIH-001), dose escalation across multiple solid tumor indications demonstrated acceptable safety and tolerability without dose-limiting toxicities, supporting the feasibility of this localized, personalized approach. While therapeutic efficacy remains under evaluation, AM003 exemplifies a design paradigm that aligns aptamer biology with clinical constraints, emphasizing individualized selection, local delivery, and immune modulation rather than prolonged systemic exposure.

Barriers to Clinical Advancement of Aptamer-Based Cancer Therapies: Current Status and Challenges

Aptamers have demonstrated compelling potential as highly specific molecular ligands for therapeutic and diagnostic applications. The clinical approvals of pegaptanib (Macugen) [23] for age-related macular degeneration and avacincaptad pegol (Izervay) 25,26for geographic atrophy provide clear proof-of-concept that nucleic acid–based ligands can achieve regulatory approval, clinical safety, and therapeutic efficacy. These successes validate aptamer chemistry, manufacturing feasibility, and regulatory acceptability in human use.

Despite these precedents, the translation of aptamers into oncology has remained limited. Although numerous aptamer candidates have demonstrated strong preclinical activity and several have advanced into early-phase clinical evaluation, particularly in solid tumors such as pancreatic, liver, and colorectal cancers, no aptamer-based therapy has yet received approval for cancer treatment [97,102]. This persistent gap reflects intersecting biological, pharmacological, technical, and commercial challenges that must be addressed to meet the stringent requirements for durable exposure and sustained functional efficacy in systemic cancer therapy, which exceed those of localized or ocular applications (Table 2).

Scientific and Technical Barriers

Pharmacokinetics and Systemic Exposure

One of the most fundamental barriers to therapeutic aptamer translation is unfavorable pharmacokinetics. Due to their small molecular size, unmodified aptamers are rapidly cleared via renal filtration and are highly susceptible to serum nuclease degradation, resulting in short circulating half-lives and limited tumor exposure [9,29,32,103]. These characteristics severely restrict sustained target engagement in systemic cancer therapy.

Multiple chemical strategies, such as PEGylation, backbone modification (e.g., 2′-fluoro, 2′-O-methyl), or conjugation to larger carriers, have been developed to extend half-life and improve stability. However, these modifications introduce new clinical risks. PEGylation, while effective at increasing molecular weight and circulation time, has been associated with anti-PEG antibody formation, altered pharmacokinetics, reduced efficacy, and, in rare cases, hypersensitivity reactions or anaphylaxis [3,9,13,31,42]. Similarly, certain chemical modifications may compromise binding affinity, alter aptamer folding, or raise toxicity concerns, underscoring the delicate balance between stability and functional integrity.

Potency and Clinical Efficacy in Humans

Clinical experience with AS1411, a nucleolin-targeting DNA aptamer, illustrates a recurrent translational challenge. Across multiple trials, AS1411 demonstrated an excellent safety profile but produced limited therapeutic benefit in broader patient populations (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT00512083, NCT00881244, NCT00740441, NCT01034410) [97]. These outcomes suggest that favorable safety alone is insufficient for clinical success and that aptamer efficacy may depend on more precise patient stratification, higher local exposure, or more potent functional constructs. The AS1411 experience highlights the need to align aptamer mechanism, dosing strategy, and biological context more closely with human tumor biology.

Targeting and Delivery Complexity in Human Tumors

Many therapeutically relevant aptamer targets are intracellular or embedded within complex tumor microenvironments, presenting substantial delivery challenges. While aptamers can be efficiently internalized in vitro or in simplified animal models, human tumors exhibit pronounced heterogeneity, altered endocytic pathways, dense stromal barriers, and variable receptor accessibility [9,30,42]. These features can markedly impair intracellular delivery and functional activity in clinical settings. Consequently, preclinical efficacy frequently overestimates real-world performance unless delivery constraints are explicitly addressed during development [104,105].

Tumor microenvironmental factors, such as hypoxia, acidic pH, enzymatic activity, and extracellular matrix density, may further alter aptamer conformation, reduce binding efficiency, or limit penetration [2,31,42]. These influences complicate optimization and necessitate evaluation in clinically relevant models that better reflect human tumor architecture.

Discovery and Selection Bottlenecks

Although aptamers are selected for high specificity, target discovery and selection remain challenging. Many SELEX pipelines rely on purified proteins or simplified targets, which may not capture post-translational modifications, conformational dynamics, or context-dependent epitope presentation observed in vivo. This limitation increases the risk of off-target binding or reduced functional relevance in clinical settings [30,31,106]. Furthermore, the absence of standardized assays for functional validation complicates cross-study comparison and translational benchmarking.

In addition, physical transport barriers within solid tumors further limit aptamer distribution. Tumor penetration of aptamer-based therapeutics is governed by multiple biophysical factors, including extracellular matrix density, elevated interstitial fluid pressure, and binding-site barrier effects. While the small size of aptamers can facilitate diffusion, high-affinity binding near perivascular regions may restrict deeper tissue penetration, resulting in heterogeneous intratumoral distribution and incomplete target engagement [9,42].

However, this limitation may be partially mitigated by receptor-mediated uptake mechanisms. Many aptamers are capable of binding cell-surface receptors and promoting internalization via receptor-mediated endocytosis [9,22]. Targets such as the transferrin receptor, which mediates transcytosis across the blood-brain barrier, and VEGF receptors involved in angiogenic signaling, have been exploited to facilitate intracellular delivery and enhance tissue-specific uptake of aptamer-based therapeutics [4,5,9].

Competition with Established Therapeutic Modalities

Monoclonal antibodies remain the dominant modality in targeted cancer therapy due to their well-characterized pharmacology, established manufacturing pipelines, and extensive clinical validation [62]. Decades of standardized protocols, economies of scale, and regulatory familiarity make antibodies a lower-risk investment. In contrast, SELEX optimization, aptamer modification, and delivery engineering are time-intensive and costly, with fewer late-stage clinical successes to de-risk development [9,31].

Regulatory, Manufacturing, and Commercial Barriers

The regulatory pathway for aptamer-based oncology drugs remains less clearly defined than that for antibodies, contributing to hesitancy among developers and investors. Large-scale, consistent chemical synthesis of aptamers, particularly modified or conjugated constructs, adds manufacturing complexity relative to established biologic systems.

Although pegaptanib represented a major milestone, its limited commercial competitiveness against anti-VEGF antibodies dampened enthusiasm for broader aptamer investment. The absence of highly successful oncology aptamers has further constrained funding, with many programs remaining confined to early-stage trials.

Market and Research Landscape Considerations

Aptamer research has increasingly focused on diagnostics, biosensors, imaging agents, and targeted delivery vehicles rather than standalone therapeutics. While these applications are scientifically valuable, they attract less investment than modalities such as CAR-T cells, gene editing, or mRNA-based therapies. Recent advances in these competing platforms have further diverted attention and resources away from aptamer therapeutics.

These challenges underscore that the limited clinical success of oncology aptamers does not reflect a lack of molecular potential, but rather a misalignment between aptamer properties and the biological, pharmacological, and commercial demands of cancer therapy. Addressing these barriers will require integrated advances in selection strategies, delivery systems, patient stratification, and translational study design, rather than incremental optimization alone.

Strategies to Overcome the Limitations of Aptamers

Using Proper Target Models for Selection

The clinical translation of aptamers for therapeutic applications has been delayed due to limitations in target model selection during development. Most therapeutic aptamers are selected against purified proteins, recombinant domains, or homogeneous cell lines under artificial buffer conditions [9,13,30,31]. These systems fail to capture key features of clinical tumors, including heterogeneous target expression, post-translational modification, cofactor dependence, and microenvironmental regulation [7,107]. As a result, aptamers optimized for simplified targets may exhibit reduced binding, alter conformation, or deliveries failure, diminish functional activity in vivo.

Importantly, selection stringency often prioritizes affinity over functional relevance, pharmacokinetic behavior, or target accessibility in human tissue. Consequently, SELEX frequently produces aptamers with excellent in vitro performance but poor in vivo durability or tumor exposure. This selection-translation mismatch represents a foundational barrier contributing to the low clinical success rate of oncology aptamers. Utilizing advanced systems like primary tissues, human-based organoids, or organ-on-a-chip platforms to replicate human-like microenvironments more accurately during preclinical evaluations could better predict the successful application of aptamer-based therapies [7, 30]. Furthermore, researchers should incorporate high-throughput screening techniques that account for physiological relevance and variability in human disease models as they test the efficacy of aptamers.

Addressing Unpredictable Pharmacokinetics

Aptamers exhibit highly variable pharmacokinetics in systemic circulation, complicating the optimization of their therapeutic profiles [6, 9]. To circumvent this issue, the use of pharmacokinetic modeling during early development to predict behavior in vivo is important [6,7,30,66]. We can also employ conjugation strategies such as attaching aptamers to carriers like nanoparticles or hydrogels to stabilize their systemic circulation profiles [6,7].

Structural and Chemical Modifications for Enhanced Stability and Performance

Aptamers face several challenges that limit their therapeutic efficacy, including rapid renal clearance, vulnerability to enzymatic degradation, and the need for improved binding affinity and stability. Their small size makes them susceptible to rapid clearance from systemic circulation, while unmodified aptamers are prone to degradation by serum nucleases, significantly reducing their functional lifespan [94,107,108]. Additionally, optimization is required to ensure robust functional performance in complex biological environments. The following are some strategies currently used to address these issues. To enhance the stability of aptamers without compromising their specificity, chemical modifications can be introduced to the sugar moieties or backbone linkages. Substitutions such as 2′-fluoro or 2′-O-methyl groups help protect aptamers from enzymatic degradation [31,108]. Additionally, the development of nuclease-resistant analogs, such as spiegelmers [96], offers a promising strategy to further improve durability under physiological conditions.

Enhancing Half-Life

Aptamer stability varies markedly with backbone chemistry and biological environment, spanning more than four orders of magnitude across reported conditions. Unmodified RNA degrades rapidly in serum (half-life~1–2 min) [109], whereas chemically modified RNA backbones (2′-aminopyrimidine, 2′-fluoro, 4′-thio) and DNA aptamers exhibit substantially prolonged stability, with half-lives ranging from several hours to multiple days [25,108] in serum or urine. Notably, L-nucleic acid aptamers (L-DNA and L-RNA) demonstrate exceptional nuclease resistance, can be stable in solution up to 3 months [96], and maintaining half-lives exceeding 3600 min in human serum [25], underscoring how backbone selection critically determines in vivo durability. Aptamers can be conjugated with stabilizing moieties such as polyethylene glycol (PEG) or cholesterol to extend circulation time [23]. An alternative is to explore fusion with albumin-binding domains or encapsulation within long-acting delivery systems like liposomes or nanoparticles to improve bioavailability [6,32,75].

Delivery Strategies and Nanocomposite Approaches

To overcome intrinsic delivery limitations, aptamers are increasingly incorporated into nanocomposite and multifunctional delivery platforms designed to enhance nuclease resistance, circulation stability, tumor accumulation, and intracellular access [29,31,75,110]. Aptamer-functionalized nanoparticles, liposomes, dendrimers, and hybrid nanocomposites have demonstrated improved tumor targeting and inhibition of tumor growth in multiple preclinical models, including reports showing enhanced therapeutic efficacy of aptamer-guided nanocomposites in suppressing tumor progression [6,29,31,75]. Recent advances in nanoparticle-based delivery systems have enhanced the pharmacokinetics of nucleic acid therapeutics by increasing systemic stability, prolonging circulation half-life, and improving tumor accumulation through enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effects and active targeting strategies [110,111]. These systems leverage aptamer-mediated specificity while benefiting from the pharmacokinetic and payload-protective properties of nanoscale carriers.

Despite these advantages, nanocomposite formulations introduce additional layers of translational complexity. Parameters such as formulation stability, batch-to-batch reproducibility, biodistribution, and controlled payload release must be tightly regulated to ensure consistent in vivo performance. Moreover, systemic administration of aptamer-based nanocomposites raises regulatory considerations related to manufacturing scalability, critical quality attributes, safety profiling, and pharmacokinetic predictability, which may exceed those required for free aptamers or conventional biologics.

In parallel, aptamers selected against defined target proteins or signaling pathways can be integrated with single-cell transcriptomic annotation datasets to interrogate target expression across heterogeneous tumor cell populations. This strategy enables identification of tumor subpopulations with enriched target expression and improved accessibility, providing a rational framework for patient stratification and for aligning delivery platform design with clinically relevant tumor biology. Such data-driven integration may help mitigate mismatches between preclinical targeting efficiency and clinical tumor heterogeneity.

Optimizing Structure and Binding Affinity

Conduct structure-activity relationship studies to guide precise chemical and structural modifications for improved performance. Design advanced aptamer platforms incorporating unnatural nucleotide analogs or secondary structural motifs to enhance binding specificity and functional efficacy in therapeutic applications.

Overcoming Intellectual Property Restrictions

Intellectual property protections on foundational aptamer technologies have historically limited accessibility and innovation in the field. We can now capitalize on the expiration of foundational SELEX patents to enable broader research, optimization, and application opportunities. Collaborations among academia, biotech companies, and regulatory agencies will help to foster open innovation and shared resources. Advancements in aptamer design and chemical modification continue to mitigate these limitations, with a focus on improving stability, reducing clearance rates, and enhancing target specificity. As these developments progress, additional clinical trials will be critical to unlocking the full therapeutic potential of aptamers in oncology. With their unique attributes, adaptability, and precision in targeting, aptamers have the potential to revolutionize precision medicine, reshaping the future of cancer treatment.

AI-Assisted Aptamer Design and Target Prioritization

Advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning are increasingly being incorporated into aptamer discovery and optimization pipelines to address limitations of conventional SELEX, including lengthy selection cycles, experimental redundancy, and inefficient enrichment of clinically relevant binders [112–114]. Deep learning frameworks trained on high-throughput SELEX–NGS datasets, such as convolutional neural networks and recurrent architectures, enable systematic identification of sequence motifs, structural features, and enrichment patterns associated with high-affinity binding, thereby improving candidate prioritization beyond frequency-based selection alone. Models such as DeepAptamer [112] integrate sequence information with DNA shape and contextual features to distinguish functional binders from background sequences, reducing experimental noise and accelerating convergence toward optimized candidates.

Complementing these data-driven approaches, structure-informed AI pipelines, exemplified by AIoptamer [113], combine machine learning–based affinity prediction with molecular modeling and molecular dynamics simulations to refine aptamer–target interactions at the atomic level. By evaluating large numbers of in silico sequence variants prior to experimental validation, these approaches reduce reliance on trial-and-error optimization and support rational modification of existing aptamers to enhance stability, affinity, or specificity.

Importantly, AI-based optimization is increasingly being integrated with single-cell transcriptomic and spatial profiling data to inform target and pathway selection. By leveraging cell-type–resolved expression patterns within heterogeneous tumors, these combined approaches support prioritization of aptamer targets that are enriched in clinically relevant subpopulations, including resistant tumor cells or microenvironmental niches [114–116]. While these computational strategies do not replace experimental validation, they provide a scalable and complementary framework to improve alignment between aptamer design, tumor biology, and translational relevance.

Conclusions

Aptamers offer high specificity, chemical flexibility, and modularity that make them attractive candidates for precision oncology; however, their clinical translation has lagged behind preclinical promise. Available clinical evidence indicates that this gap reflects a mismatch between aptamer properties and the pharmacological requirements of effective cancer therapy rather than inadequate target recognition. Systemic programs such as AS1411 illustrate that favorable safety and binding affinity do not ensure sufficient tumor exposure or durable clinical efficacy. In contrast, emerging strategies, including microenvironment-targeted agents such as NOX-A12 and localized or personalized approaches such as AM003, demonstrate that aptamers can achieve clinical relevance when pharmacokinetic constraints are minimized, or extracellular mechanisms are exploited. Recent advances in physiologically relevant selection platforms, including 3D tumor models, organ-on-chip systems, patient-derived tissues, and AI-assisted design, are beginning to address longstanding translational bottlenecks. Future success will depend on clinically informed aptamer design, rational deployment strategies, and alignment with therapeutic contexts where aptamer biology confers a clear advantage.
