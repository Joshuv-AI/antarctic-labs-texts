Ten‐Year Update of Nurse Practitioner Service Impact on Patient and Health Service Outcomes in Emergency Care Settings—A Systematic Review
Authors: Amanda Fox, Suzanne Williams, Natasha Jennings, Ella Marie Tomkins, Rosie Glynn, Corinne Dunningham, Kathleen Tori

Introduction

Healthcare systems face ongoing challenges, including emergency department overcrowding, patient access block to healthcare, workforce shortages, increasing service complexity, rising burden of chronic disease, and limited patient access to primary healthcare (Jones and Dolsten 2024; Sartini et al. 2022). Access block has been identified as a critical issue exacerbating emergency department congestion, contributing to ambulance ramping, and placing sustained strain on emergency services (Australasian College for Emergency Medicine 2022). In response, innovative models of care, such as emergency nurse practitioner services, continue to gain momentum globally. Nurse practitioners are now embedded across the full spectrum of emergency care, including acute and minor injury management, triage, whole‐of‐department models, and targeted care for specialist populations (Horvath et al. 2023). Integrating nurse practitioner services has been touted as an evaluable, holistic, and complementary approach to patient care, improving service delivery and clinical outcomes (Horvath et al. 2023). However, despite widespread adoption of nurse practitioner models in this context, previous systematic reviews have highlighted methodological limitations and a lack of robust evidence reporting service outcomes, patient satisfaction, and quality of care (Jennings, Clifford, et al. 2015; Wilson and Shifaza. 2008).

Background

In 2025, the nurse practitioner role marks its 60th anniversary in the United States. First introduced in Colorado in 1965 to improve access to paediatric care (Berg 2020; Silver et al. 1967), the role has since expanded significantly into other health care contexts. Professional nursing organisations such as the International Council of Nurses and the World Health Organisation have both advocated for the expansion and enhanced recognition of nurse practitioner services to meet evolving and escalating healthcare demands (World Health Organisation 2025; International Council of Nurses 2025). In 2020, the International Council of Nurses released the ‘Guidelines on Advanced Practice Nursing’, urging governments, healthcare authorities and key stakeholders to support nurses in working to their full scope of practice and advocating for the implementation of more nurse‐led services (International Council of Nurses 2020). The World Health Organisation's second ‘State of the World's Nursing’ report 2025 highlighted the importance of advanced practice nurses, including nurse practitioner models of care in addressing global health challenges and called for their increased adoption across multiple health service contexts (World Health Organisation 2025).

In countries such as Australia, Canada, New Zealand, Norway and the United States of America, the title of Nurse Practitioner is protected and requires consistent educational preparation and scope (De Raeve et al. 2023). Title protection allows for health care delivered by nurse practitioners to be evaluated as a collective and distinct health service. In 2015, Jennings et al. conducted a systematic review suggesting that emergency department nurse practitioner services improved patient satisfaction, reduced patient waiting times, and provided care quality comparable to medical practitioners (Jennings, Clifford, et al. 2015). However, the review identified a lack of robust evidence regarding cost‐effectiveness and called for further investigation into the economic impact of these services. Subsequent studies have reinforced the clinical benefits of nurse practitioner services in emergency settings, demonstrating improved patient outcomes, shorter lengths of stay, and enhanced satisfaction (Woo et al. 2017; Bazavluk et al. 2022). Despite these positive findings, the literature continues to highlight the need for more comprehensive research into the financial implications of nurse practitioner‐led models of care. A recent global scoping review found that advanced practice nurses, including nurse practitioners, are integrated into diverse emergency care models across countries such as Canada, the United Kingdom, and the United States (Horvath et al. 2023). These models involve fast‐track, minor injury, paediatric, geriatric, and triage services and consistently demonstrate reduced patient waiting times, improved length of stay, lower left without being seen rates, and equivalent or better outcomes compared to traditional medical models (Horvath et al. 2023).

The healthcare landscape has evolved significantly since the Jennings, Clifford, et al. (2015) review. Nurse practitioners provide care across diverse clinical and community settings, encompassing varying levels of complexity and spanning the primary, public, and private healthcare sectors from metropolitan centres to rural and remote regions (Horvath et al. 2023; National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine 2021; Rossiter et al. 2023). But while nurse practitioners have gained increased recognition, their full and consistent integration into health systems remains incomplete (Currie et al. 2020). Even in mature markets such as the United States, it has been reported that nurse practitioners face barriers like limited scope of practice, organisational resistance, and insufficient support mechanisms (Porat‐Dahlerbruch et al. 2025). According to the American Medical Association (2017), in some parts of the United States, nurse practitioners operate under reduced practice authority where they can perform several clinical tasks but must also maintain collaborative agreements with physicians for prescribing or diagnostics. Other barriers to full integration include professional resistance, funding limitations or misalignments to models of care, and restricted access to system‐level rebates, alongside legal and regulatory constraints imposed by frameworks (McCullough et al. 2021; Peters et al. 2024; Schorn et al. 2022; Smith et al. 2019). Policy reforms have been introduced in some countries to strengthen the profession and promote nurse practitioner autonomy by removing legislated collaborative arrangements, enabling service delivery to rural and remote Australian communities (Australian Government Department of Health and Aged Care 2025). Despite this, ongoing challenges nurse practitioner services are facing highlight the importance of revisiting the evidence to understand the impact and viability of nurse practitioner‐led services.

Given the rapid evolution of healthcare delivery, the expansion of nurse practitioner services, and ongoing policy reform, this updated systematic review has incorporated both emergency departments and urgent care clinics. This review will evaluate current available evidence reporting emergency nurse practitioner services and provide a contemporary analysis of the evidence on the effectiveness of emergency nurse practitioner services in improving patient‐centred outcomes and health system efficiency.

Methods

Aim and Design

This systematic review aimed to provide a 10‐year update on the best available evidence regarding the impact of emergency nurse practitioner services by exploring the question: What is the best available evidence from the past 10 years regarding the impact of nurse practitioner services on cost, waiting times, patient satisfaction, representation rates and length of stay in emergency and urgent care settings?

This systematic review was guided by the JBI methodology (Lizarondo et al. 2025) and was registered with PROSPERO [2025 CRD420250645148]. A protocol was prepared but was not published, the review used narrative synthesis methods and has been reported following the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta‐Analyses) guidelines (Page et al. 2021).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies were included if they (1) were available in full‐text English, (2) were an empirical evidence source, (3) were published between 1 Jan 2014 and 31 Dec 2024, (4) were set in the emergency or urgent care setting*, (5) were conducted in settings where health services were provided by title‐protected nurse practitioners, and (6) measured cost, waiting times, patient satisfaction, representation rates, or length of stay.

*Depending on the country of origin, emergency and urgent care settings may also be referred to as critical care services or accident and trauma centres and may operate under varying models and levels of care. For the purposes of this review, emergency and urgent care refers to services that provide immediate or time‐sensitive care to patients in an outpatient setting, without the need for an appointment, for conditions that cannot safely wait for assessment by a community general practitioner. These services often operate for extended hours and are delivered by nurse/medical practitioners, with support from other staff, such as imaging and pathology personnel. This definition was developed by considering the core aspects of definitions from various countries (American College of Emergency Physicians 2020; American College of Emergency Physicians 2022; Australasian College for Emergency Medicine 2025; Centres for Disease Control and Prevention 2022; National Health Service 2025).

Studies were excluded if they (1) reported outcomes of nurse practitioner services that were not title‐protected, (2) were abstracts, editorials, presentations, or review papers, or (3) did not have an English full‐text available.

Search Strategy

A three‐step search strategy was developed with and executed by a health librarian as recommended by Aromataris et al. (2024). A scoping search was completed in Embase (Elsevier) and Medline (EBSCOhost) with all applicable subject headings and keywords applied to the search before being checked by a second health librarian using the PRESS Checklist (McGowan et al. 2016). The search was completed in seven databases on the 28th of January 2025; these included: Embase (Elsevier), Medline (EBSCOhost), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), Cochrane Library (Wiley), Emcare (Ovid), Web of Science Core Collection (Clarivate), and Scopus (Elsevier). The data range (2014–2024) was used as a limiter to the search query. Table S1 provides the final search queries. The reference lists of the included articles were manually screened, using SpiderCite, against the search criteria and included in the review if relevant (Clark et al. 2020).

Study Screening and Selection

Records from the search were imported into Covidence (Covidence systematic review software 2025). The system removed duplicates before screening. Two independent reviewers screened each title and abstract against the inclusion criteria [AF, SW, NJ, ET, RG, CD, KT]. The full‐text sources were retrieved and screened by two independent reviewers [AF, SW, NJ, ET, RG, CD, KT]. Discrepancies were reviewed by a third independent reviewer or discussed with the whole research team.

Data Extraction

Data extraction was completed in Covidence systematic review software (Covidence systematic review software 2025) using a pre‐determined extraction tool. The data extracted included title, author, publication year, aims, study type, setting, population, recruitment information, methods, outcome measures (including cost, waiting times, representation rates, length of stay and patient satisfaction), additional measures related to the research question (i.e., any measures of nurse practitioner service impact on delivery or patient care), limitations and interpretations. Two independent reviewers completed data extraction [AF, SW, NJ, ET, RG, CD, KT]. One reviewer completed data consolidation [ET].

Quality Appraisal

The Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (Hong et al. 2018) was used to evaluate the quality of the included studies. The tool was uploaded into Covidence (Covidence systematic review software 2025) and completed at the time of data extraction by the same reviewer extracting the data for each study. One reviewer completed data consolidation [ET]. Conflicts in quality appraisal were reviewed by two reviewers [AF, ET].

Data Synthesis and Analysis

Data from the included records have been summarised and explained according to narrative synthesis methods (Campbell et al. 2018). Narrative synthesis was chosen as it is an appropriate systematic method of integrating and interpreting findings from studies that are heterogeneous and report complex research questions (Campbell et al. 2018). This process involved initial synthesis of the data according to outcome measures by research team members; these outcomes were reviewed and confirmed by the whole research team for accuracy and completeness. Each outcome was then written narratively to describe and explain the findings by one researcher and reviewed by a second research team member. The findings were then compared to those of the 2006–2013 review conducted by Jennings, Clifford, et al. (2015) and other current literature to provide an update on new evidence or variations and disparities from previous findings in the discussion. Finally, recommendations for future research based on this review have been provided.

Ethical Clearance

Not required for this review paper.

Quality Appraisal

All studies clearly stated their research aims and met the minimum criteria for inclusion. Most studies were determined to be of high methodological quality, and no records were excluded based on quality appraisal outcomes. Further detail is provided in Appendix S2.

Study and Population Characteristics

Studies published between 2014 and 2024 were conducted in Australia (n = 10), Canada (n = 1), and the United States of America (n = 5). One study did not state the country where it was undertaken; however, the authors' affiliations are within the United States of America. Most studies were retrospective cohort study designs, with others using randomised controlled trials, observational methods, survey methods or qualitative designs with individual or group interviews. Cost

None of the included studies measured the cost of nurse practitioner services either directly or indirectly. No studies reported economic data such as cost‐effectiveness, cost savings, or the broader financial impact of nurse practitioner care.

Waiting Times

Several studies reported the positive impact that nurse practitioner services have on reducing emergency department waiting times (Gardner et al. 2018; Jackson et al. 2024; Payne et al. 2023; Tucker and Bernard 2015; Jennings, Gardner, O'Reilly, Mitra, Gerson 2015; Mowbray et al. 2020). Gardner et al. (2018) reported a notable decrease in patient wait time (26 min) for health service provider assessment for patients following the implementation of a nurse practitioner service in triage compared to a control (non‐nurse practitioner service) period (42 min). Jackson et al. (2024) noted that patients seen by nurse practitioners were generally attended to more rapidly than the department's average regardless of patient acuity. Findings in a study by Payne et al. (2023) demonstrated greater nurse practitioner service efficiency compared to medical practitioner service groups (interns, junior medical officers, and registrars), indicating that nurse practitioner services were faster in time to initial patient review and significantly faster in decision‐making regarding patient admission or discharge. Tucker and Bernard (2015) reported decreased patient waiting times associated with nurse practitioner care. Jennings, Gardner, O'Reilly, Mitra, Gerson (2015) reported statistically significant differences in wait times for analgesia between nurse practitioner and medical practitioner groups, with a higher proportion of nurse practitioner patients receiving analgesia within 30 min (49.2% vs. 29.7%, p = 0.001). Jennings, Kansal, et al. (2015) reported similar times to analgesia, but this is not compared against other care models. In contrast, Middleton et al. (2019) noted longer waiting times in emergency departments for patients attending nurse practitioner services. Studies by Jennings, Gardner, O'Reilly, and Mitra (2015) and Roche et al. (2017) noted that there was no significant difference in waiting times between patients who received standard care and those who received nurse practitioner care.

Mowbray et al. (2020) reported that nurse practitioners managed the highest proportion of non‐urgent patient cases and delivered treatments such as receiving medications faster than other service providers. Multiple studies (Middleton et al. 2019; Muller et al. 2018; Gardner et al. 2018; Tucker and Bernard 2015) found reductions in the rate of patients leaving without being seen in departments delivering nurse practitioner services.

Patient Satisfaction

Measures of patient satisfaction were reported in four records. Two studies (Lutze et al. 2014; Roche et al. 2017) identified clear and measurable patient satisfaction results by comparing nurse practitioner and medical practitioner models of care, and two studies (Tucker and Bernard 2015; Jackson et al., 2024) report patient satisfaction as documented in medical notes. Patients managed in a metropolitan emergency ‘fast track’ area were asked to complete a survey of satisfaction questions on a 5‐point Likert scale (Lutze et al. 2014). Lutze et al. (2014) report that the median satisfaction scores were higher in the nurse practitioner service group compared to the medical service group (4 vs. 3, p < 0.01) and that nurse practitioner services were 2.5 times more likely to receive higher patient satisfaction scores. Lutze et al. (2014) also report that while both patient groups would prefer to see a general practitioner nearby, the patients in the nurse practitioner group had a lower preference. Roche et al. (2017) compared nurse and medical practitioner models of care in three rural emergency departments. In this study, 61 participants were surveyed; 23 were seen by the nurse practitioner service and 38 by the medical practitioner service. Results demonstrated that patients were either highly satisfied or satisfied with care, and no difference was found in satisfaction between the models of care (Roche et al. 2017). A case study of the impact of nurse practitioner models of care over 2 years by Tucker and Bernard (2015) reports that patient satisfaction remained high (> 90%) during the study on Physician Quality Reporting System measures. Jackson et al. (2024) reported that one complaint from a cohort of 1443 patients was received about the nurse practitioner service (0.0006%) by a patient who had been asked to leave due to verbal abuse.

Representation Rates

Four studies examined representation rates (Jackson et al. 2024; Middleton et al. 2019; Jennings, Gardner, O'Reilly, Mitra, Gerson 2015; Roche et al. 2017) with varied definitions and rates of patient representation. A retrospective study of patients treated in a nurse practitioner service (n = 1443) reported a small portion (n = 12, 0.8%) of patients represented with concerns such as medical certificate requests, worsening symptoms and pain (Jackson et al. 2024). An extensive cohort study (n = 2,463,543) across Australia found that rates of unplanned patient representations were no different between hospitals with and those without nurse practitioner services (Middleton et al. 2019). A randomised controlled trial (Jennings, Gardner, O'Reilly, Mitra, Gerson 2015) found higher re‐presentation rates in patients attending a nurse practitioner group (6/130, 0.5%) compared to a standard care group (1/128, < 0.1%); however, all revisits were scheduled follow‐up appointments. Furthermore, in a prospective, longitudinal nested cohort study examining patients presenting with chest pain, patients treated by a standard care model were more than twice as likely to have an unplanned re‐presentation within 7 days compared to those treated by nurse practitioner services (Roche et al. 2017).

Length of Stay and Impact on Discharge

After adjusting for factors such as age and case severity, nurse practitioner care was associated with a lower odds of hospital admission (Kurtzman et al. 2023). Higher rates of discharge home are also associated with nurse practitioner care (Bouchibti et al. 2022). It was found that 98% of patients treated by nurse practitioner services were discharged home, compared with 69% for physicians and 64% for mixed teams (p < 0.001), though the study did not collect data on the severity of cases between groups, which may have contributed to the ability to discharge home (Bouchibti et al. 2022). Several studies reported the impact of nurse practitioner services on length of stay, with mixed but generally favourable outcomes (Begaz et al. 2017; Bouchibti et al. 2022; Gardner et al. 2018; Jennings, Gardner, O'Reilly, and Mitra 2015; Kurtzman et al. 2023; Middleton et al. 2019; Mowbray et al. 2020; Muller et al. 2018; Roche et al. 2017; Tucker and Bernard 2015). Bouchibti et al. (2022) highlight that the median patient length of stay was significantly longer if medical practitioners worked alone (149 min) compared to when nurse practitioners worked alone (137 min), however, when medical and nurse practitioners were working together the median length of stay was longest at 182 min. Gardner et al. (2018) reported a statistically significant reduction in length of patient stay in the emergency department (147 min) for patients who received care by nurse practitioner services compared to a whole of emergency department control cohort (291 min). Kurtzman et al. (2023) reported a decrease (75.5 min) in boarding time (time to leave the emergency department once the care decision is made) for nurse practitioner teams compared to medical teams and a reduction of 23.3 min for overall length of patient stay. However, Begaz et al. (2017) reported only a 4‐min difference between the length of stay for patients receiving care by nurse practitioner services when compared to medical practitioner services. Other studies reporting decreases in length of stay for nurse practitioner groups included Middleton et al. (2019), Mowbray et al. (2020), Tucker and Bernard (2015), Roche et al. (2017) and Muller et al. (2018). The latter, Muller et al. (2018), reported a relative reduction of 10% in length of stay for shifts where a nurse practitioner was on duty.

Other Measures of Interest

From the included studies, several also reported on diagnostic data. Two studies showed comparable or higher diagnostic accuracy for nurse practitioner services. Roche et al. (2017) reported higher accuracy in electrocardiogram interpretation (91.7%) compared to standard care (82.8%). In fracture identification, Lee et al. (2014) reported comparable sensitivity and specificity for nurse practitioner services and emergency physician services. Studies also reported that patients seen by nurse practitioner services were likely to have fewer diagnostic tests and procedures than those seen by physician services. Findings were reported by Begaz et al. (2017), with fewer test categories per patient (1.54 vs. 1.75, p = 0.001) ordered by nurse practitioners, including significantly fewer urine tests and ultrasounds. Bouchibti et al. (2022) and Kurtzman et al. (2023) also reported lower diagnostic test utilisation by nurse practitioners. In addition, Jackson et al. (2024) reported that over 12 months, no incidents were attributed to nurse practitioner care, and no deaths were reported in nurse practitioner cases.

Discussion

This review reflects a robust and contemporary analysis of the evidence reporting the effectiveness of emergency nurse practitioner services in improving patient outcomes and health system efficiencies. The literature demonstrates some variability in reporting clinical outcomes and operational efficiencies; however, consistent themes emerge. Evidence suggests emergency nurse practitioner services contribute to reductions in hospital admissions and increased rates of discharge home (Bouchibti et al. 2022; Kurtzman et al. 2023), reflecting more efficient and patient‐centred care transitions. These outcomes reflect holistic assessment, early intervention, and preventive care—all hallmarks of nurse practitioner practice. Several studies report that the diagnostic accuracy of emergency nurse practitioners is equivalent or higher than medical officers (Lee et al. 2014; Roche et al. 2017). This not only challenges traditional assumptions about professional boundaries but also reinforces the clinical competence of nurse practitioners. Furthermore, emergency nurse practitioner models of care have been associated with fewer diagnostic tests and procedures (Begaz et al. 2017; Bouchibti et al. 2022; Kurtzman et al. 2023), suggesting more efficient use of resources without compromising quality. Importantly, improvements in timeliness of treatment have been demonstrated with no adverse events reported (Jennings, Gardner, O'Reilly, Mitra, Gerson 2015; Jennings, Kansal, et al. 2015; Mowbray et al. 2020; Roche et al. 2017; Jackson et al. 2024). Lower rates of patients leaving without being seen (Gardner et al. 2018; Muller et al. 2018; Tucker and Bernard 2015) further support the accessibility, responsiveness and safety of emergency nurse practitioner services.

Collectively, the evidence positions emergency nurse practitioner services as clinically safe, resource‐efficient, and patient‐centred, enhancing both patient and health system outcomes in emergent care settings. Building on these findings, the following section explores the specific impact of nurse practitioners on patient satisfaction and patient outcomes within emergency and urgent care settings, highlighting measurable contributions to both individual patient care and broader health system efficiency.

Reduction in emergency department waiting times remains one of the most frequently reported and beneficial outcomes associated with nurse practitioner service models. Numerous studies have demonstrated improvements in wait times, whether to key treatment milestones in nurse practitioner‐led triage models or to initial provider assessment. These benefits have been shown to endure even as models of care evolve and service demands increase. While Middleton et al. (2019) reported longer wait times associated with nurse practitioners, these findings may have been context‐dependent, influenced by factors such as workforce configuration, local protocols, or regional health system pressures.

Patient satisfaction is a globally recognised indicator for assessing the effectiveness and quality of healthcare service delivery (Bull et al. 2022). In the context of nurse practitioner care, satisfaction reflects patient experiences and is a testament to nurse practitioner service accessibility and clinical impact. Although this review frequently reported patient satisfaction, the methods of assessment varied considerably across studies. Of the four studies that mentioned satisfaction, only two (Lutze et al. 2014; Roche et al. 2017) provided clear definitions and direct comparisons between nurse practitioner and medical practitioner models. Lutze et al. (2014) reported significantly higher satisfaction scores for nurse practitioner‐led care, while Roche et al. (2017) found no statistically significant difference. Tucker and Bernard (2015) reported lower satisfaction levels, though these were primarily attributed to factors unrelated to clinical care, such as patient behaviour. Overall, the evidence suggests that patients have confidence in nurse practitioner services, though satisfaction outcomes remain inconsistently measured. However, patient satisfaction data should be interpreted with caution. Satisfaction is an inherently subjective measure, influenced by individual perceptions, expectations, and emotional responses, and may not directly reflect the quality or safety of clinical care. The variability in measurement approaches across studies further limits comparability and interpretation of satisfaction outcomes.

Representation rates provide additional insights into the quality and continuity of nurse practitioner models of care. Although definitions varied across studies, representation rates under nurse practitioner‐led care were generally low and may reflect greater patient satisfaction, confidence in initial management, and effective communication. Lutze et al. (2014) found that patients treated in nurse practitioner services were less likely to seek further care, with a reduced preference for general (medical) practitioner follow‐up. Jackson et al. (2024) similarly reported low representation rates, with common reasons including pain, symptom progression, and requests for medical certificates, suggesting that most returns were related to evolving clinical needs rather than inadequate initial care. This is corroborated by Roche et al. (2017), who reported lower representation rates for chest pain under nurse practitioner care, suggesting that comprehensive, person‐centred management may reduce the need for urgent revisits. Evidence demonstrates comparable outcomes between nurse and medical practitioner models, reinforcing the clinical safety, effectiveness, and value of nurse practitioner‐led services.

Length of stay is another key metric of system efficiency and patient outcomes. Length of stay is a critical indicator of emergency department performance, with prolonged durations associated with increased morbidity and mortality, heightened patient dissatisfaction, and intensified operational strain (Lauque et al. 2022). Andersson et al. (2020) identified patient time in the emergency department as a key indicator of crowding, service efficiency, and system performance. Accordingly, reductions in emergency department length of stay represent a clinically and systemically significant improvement—enhancing patient outcomes, alleviating workforce pressures, and contributing to more efficient resource utilisation across the broader health system. Generally, nurse practitioner‐led care was associated with reduced patient length of stay, although results varied across contexts (Begaz et al. 2017; Bouchibti et al. 2022; Gardner et al. 2018; Kurtzman et al. 2023; Middleton et al. 2019; Mowbray et al. 2020; Muller et al. 2018; Roche et al. 2017; Tucker and Bernard 2015). This disparity highlights the complexity of attributing system‐level outcomes to a single role and suggests that the nurse practitioner's impact is influenced by contextual factors such as staffing models, triage responsibilities, and patient acuity (Jeyaraman et al. 2022). Studies such as Bouchibti et al. (2022) suggest that factors including hospital capacity, emergency department crowding, and patient severity may contribute to observed differences in length‐of‐stay outcomes between provider groups; however, these variables are not accounted for in their analysis. Similarly, Gardner et al. (2018) report that nurse practitioners tend to manage patients with lower overall acuity than medical practitioners, yet this difference is also not controlled for in their analysis.

The impact of nurse practitioner services on cost was determined by the absence of economic evaluation for emergency nurse practitioner services. Clearly representing a critical blind spot in the current healthcare literature, undermining efforts to fully integrate this role into mainstream emergency care. In the decade since the initial review by Jennings, Clifford, et al. (2015), there has been a notable absence of rigorous studies evaluating the cost‐effectiveness of these services. This persistent gap is concerning, particularly given that economic evaluation is a critical factor in policy development, service design, and the implementation and expansion of nurse practitioner models across healthcare systems (Contandriopoulos et al. 2024). Without robust, contextually relevant economic data, health services risk making policy and workforce decisions without a clear understanding of the financial implications (Brownie 2018). These decisions may fail to account for the potential impact on patient outcomes, workforce sustainability, and broader policy directions related to the integration of nurse practitioners in emergency departments (Ismail et al. 2025). Ultimately, this lack of economic evidence may hinder the full integration of the nurse practitioner role as an independent health care provider into mainstream healthcare, limiting its potential to contribute to system‐wide improvements in access, efficiency, and quality of care.

A central limitation emerging from this synthesis is the heterogeneity of research and the absence of standard indicators for evaluating emergency nurse practitioner services. Inconsistent outcome measures particularly related to patient satisfaction, representation rates, and length of stay, impede meaningful comparisons across care models and settings, restricting the ability to assess impact and recognise the full contribution of nurse practitioner services to population emergent care. To support the strategic development and integration of these services, future research must prioritise the establishment of evaluation measures and frameworks. This should include validated metrics for cost‐effectiveness, patient experience, clinical outcomes, and system efficiency. Such standardisation would enable rigorous benchmarking, strengthen comparative analyses, and equip policymakers with the evidence needed to inform funding, workforce planning and service design (Jennings, Clifford, et al. 2015).

The findings of this review echo conclusions reported by Jennings, Clifford, et al. (2015), who identified the potential of nurse practitioner services to improve emergency care delivery while calling for more robust evaluation frameworks. Little progress has been made in the intervening years. The continued absence of standardised metrics and limited evidence around economic impact a decade later underscores the urgency of addressing this gap to fully realise the benefits of nurse practitioner services in emergency settings.

Develop an emergency nurse practitioner service evaluation framework with standardised professional benchmarks and validated evaluation tools to enable meaningful and actionable comparisons across diverse settings, generate robust evidence of the impact of the nurse practitioner role, and support strategic integration into emergency department models. Conclusion

The mixed findings across studies highlight the need for more robust, homogeneous research to be conducted on the impact of nurse practitioner services, particularly in areas such as cost‐effectiveness, patient satisfaction, and system‐level outcomes. However, consistent evidence demonstrates improvements in access to care, reduction of patient waiting times, and enhanced patient engagement as a result of nurse practitioner services. These patient‐centred outcomes reflect the value of nurse practitioners not only as clinically competent professionals but also as catalysts for more responsive, person‐centred healthcare delivery.

To fully realise the potential of emergency nurse practitioner services, there is an urgent need to establish professional benchmarks that provide clear frameworks for the evaluation of patient‐centred outcomes and operational impacts in emergency departments. These should encompass metrics for clinical effectiveness, patient experience, cost‐efficiency, and system performance. Consistent frameworks would enable meaningful comparisons across models, support evidence‐based policy and funding decisions, and provide rigorous evidence on the effectiveness of nurse practitioner services in emergency care. Advancing this agenda is essential not only to strengthen the evidence base but also to ensure nurse practitioner services receive sufficient support to be strategically integrated and assist with future healthcare delivery.
