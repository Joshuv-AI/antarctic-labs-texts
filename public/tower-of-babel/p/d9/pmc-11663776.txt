Genetic and vascular risk factors for ischemic stroke and cortical morphometry in individuals without a history of stroke: A UK Biobank observational cohort study
Authors: Jiawei Liu, Yingying Xie, Feng Liu, Wen Qin, Chunshui Yu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Stroke risk factors were associated with 18 CT and SA phenotypes, which could explain portions of variances in these phenotypes.•PRSIS and DBP had more extensive and complex effects on reliable cortical phenotypes than SBP, HbA1c, and TG.•Vascular risk factors within normal ranges still influence cortical phenotypes.•Cortical phenotypes mediated the associations of PRSIS and SBP with FIQ.Keywords: Stroke risk factors, MRI, Cortical morphometry, Cognition, UK Biobank

Introduction

Stroke has a high prevalence in adults worldwide with one in four people affected over their lifetime (GBD 2016 Lifetime Risk of Stroke Collaborators et al., 2018). Most strokes are ischemic, which are associated with both genetic and modifiable risk factors (Boehme et al., 2017). Based on genome-wide association studies (GWASs), polygenic risk score (PRSIS) can evaluate an individual's genetic risk for ischemic stroke by combining the effects of multiple risk variants (Thompson et al., 2022). The modifiable risk factors account for nearly 90 % of strokes, of which vascular risk factors are most potent, such as high systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) blood pressures, blood triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and glucose (Cho et al., 2022, Dave et al., 2010, Kloppenborg et al., 2008, Knopman et al., 2001, Lee et al., 2017, Mishra et al., 2022, Wei et al., 2022, Winkler et al., 2010). Although they are considered as independent risk factors for stroke, several studies reported that ischemic stroke were better predicted by the interactions between risk factors, such as the interaction between dyslipidaemia and hypertension (Wei et al., 2022) and the interaction between glucose and lipid metabolisms (Lee et al., 2017). Furthermore, the synergistic effect of genetic and vascular risk factors is supported by the improved prediction performance for ischemic stroke by combining PRSIS with vascular risk factors (Cho et al., 2022, Mishra et al., 2022).

In addition to the association of ischemic stroke, especially recurrent stroke, with an increased risk of dementia (Desmond et al., 2000), vascular risk factors are also associated with cognitive decline and dementia (Kloppenborg et al., 2008, Knopman et al., 2001), possibly by affecting brain tissue integrity. The mechanism is supported by the findings that vascular risk factors show significant effects on brain structure and function in individuals without a history of stroke (Leritz et al., 2011, Williamson et al., 2018). If their effects on the brain are large enough, the pre-existing brain differences caused by different levels of exposure to stroke risk factors between patients and controls may bias the cross-sectional case-control studies that compared brain differences between patients with ischemic stroke and healthy controls. The issue is not trivial in the clinical setting because brain regions with post-stroke reorganization identified in the case-control neuroimaging studies are considered as potential targets for stroke rehabilitation with the non-invasive brain stimulation techniques, such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) (Khedr et al., 2005). In addition, the effects of vascular risk factors on brain also indicate that they should be considered as the targets for dementia prevention. Therefore, it is important to characterize the effects of stroke risk factors on brain structure and function in individuals without a history of stroke.

The brain structure and function can be in vivo assessed by the neuroimaging techniques, such as the structural and functional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Since structural MRI measures have much higher heritability than functional MRI measures, structural measures are more susceptible to genetic variation (Elliott et al., 2018). The radial unit hypothesis posits that differential neurodevelopment programs will shape and regulate cortical thickness (CT) and surface area (SA), predicting different genetic architectures of these two cortical measures (Rakic, 1988). Accordingly, CT and SA can provide more details on cortical morphometry than volume, an integrated reflection of CT and SA, and thus we focused on these two cortical measures in this study (Winkler et al., 2018). In the past decades, several studies have investigated the associations between vascular risk factors and cortical morphometry in different populations with varied sample sizes (Coutinho et al., 2017, Leritz et al., 2011, Shen et al., 2023, Williamson et al., 2018). Although most of these studies reported that higher blood pressure, glucose, and TG were associated with cortical thinning and higher cholesterol was associated with cortical thickening, non-significant or even opposite associations were also reported. In these association studies between vascular risk factors and cortical morphometry, few studies have considered the potential confounding interaction effects between risk factors on their findings. More importantly, no studies have tested the effects of individual's genetic risk for ischemic stroke on CT and SA.

The fluid intelligence score (FIQ) tests an individual's capacity for problem-solving, logical reasoning, and abstract thinking rather than knowledge obtained from learning and experience (Cattell, 1963, Unsworth et al., 2014). As a composite cognitive measure, FIQ is often used to identify cognitive decline (McHutchison et al., 2019, Raz et al., 2008). Although FIQ has been associated with vascular risk factors (Deary et al., 1998, Elias et al., 2004, Ly et al., 2011, Muldoon et al., 1997, Spyridaki et al., 2014) and cortical morphometry (Schnack et al., 2015), it is still unclear which cortical phenotypes mediate the associations between stroke risk factors and FIQ.

In this observational study, we examined the main and interaction effects of genetic and vascular risk factors for ischemic stroke on CT and SA phenotypes, and FIQ in 27 120 stroke-free participants from the UK Biobank (Sudlow et al., 2015). We first assessed the impact of individual risk factors and their interactions on these phenotypes. To ensure robustness, we conducted five sensitivity analyses to eliminate multicollinearity and adjusting for potential confounders. We also investigated whether vascular risk factors within the normal range affect cortical and cognitive phenotypes. We further compared the differences in variables and associations (significant in both main and sensitivity analyses) between females and males. To establish the clinical relevance, we test the effects of cortical phenotypes on FIQ and applied causal mediation analysis (CMA) (Zhang et al., 2016) to explore whether cortical phenotypes mediate the relationship between stroke risk factors and FIQ (Fig. 1 and Fig. S1).

Flowchart of the study design. Six variables are used to assess risk factors for ischemic stroke. Methods

Study design and participants

In this observational study, we used data from the UK Biobank cohort, a population-based prospective study that recruited 502 415 people aged 37–73 years between 2006 and 2010 from 22 assessment centers in England, Scotland, and Wales (Sudlow et al., 2015). Among them, 43 026 participants had qualified brain structural MRI data. The data are openly accessed for researchers, and we accessed under application number 75556. The study was approved by the National Health Service (NHS) Research Ethics Service (21/NW/0157), and all participants provided written informed consent. After excluding non-Caucasian participants and those with missing data, unqualified brain images, or a history of brain diseases determined based on both baseline and follow-up data before December 8, 2021, we finally included 27 120 participants (Table 1).

Procedures

PRSIS was used to assess individual’s genetic risk for ischemic stroke by incorporating multiple genetic variants across the genome (Martin et al., 2019). Individual’s PRSIS data were provided by the UK Biobank PRS Release (Thompson et al., 2022), and PRSIS was calculated based on genetic variants with information score > 0.8, minor allele frequency > 0.05, and Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (p > 1 × 10−10) in both base and target datasets. The base dataset was constructed by meta-analyzing three GWASs for ischemic stroke totally including 88 563 patients and 849 056 controls that were not overlapped with the UK Biobank samples (the target dataset). The effect sizes of genetic variants in the base dataset were estimated, which were then used to estimate PRSIS for each participant in the target dataset using a Bayesian approach (Thompson et al., 2022).

Blood pressure was measured by an automated device (Omron HEM-7015IT digital blood pressure monitor), and manual sphygmomanometer was employed only if the automated device was unavailable. Blood pressure was measured two times within a short interval, and the average values were defined as SBP and DBP. Blood lipids (mmol/L) were measured on Beckman Coulter AU5800 with GPO-POD for TG, enzyme protective selection for LDL, and enzyme immunoinhibition for high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL). HbA1c (mmol/mol), an index of stable blood glucose level (Gillett, 2009), was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) on Bio-Rad Variant II Turbo analyzer. Details are available at the website (biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/ukb/ukb/docs/serum_biochemistry.pdf). In this study, we only used the baseline assessments for vascular risk factors (Table1, Supplementary Table 1).

T1-weighted brain structural images were acquired by a three-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid acquisition with gradient-echo (3D-MPRAGE) sequence on 3.0-Tesla Siemens Skyra MR systems (Simens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) with a 32-channel head coil and a harmonized protocol (Alfaro-Almagro et al., 2018). The scanning parameters were repetition time = 2 000 ms, echo time = 2.01 ms, inversion time = 880 ms, flip angle = 8 degrees, field of view = 256 × 256 mm, matrix = 256 × 256, acceleration factor = 2, slice thickness = 1 mm, no gap, and 208 sagittal slices. FreeSurfer (https://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) was used to preprocess structural images, and quality control and preprocessing procedures are provided at the website (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/showcase/docs/brain_mri.pdf). Based on the Desikan-Killiany-Tourville (DKT) atlas (Klein and Tourville, 2012), individual's cerebral cortex was divided into 62 cortical regions, and CT and SA values of each region were available as imaging-derived phenotypes (Supplementary Table 2). FIQ was assessed at the same time as MRI data acquisition using a 13-item questionnaire (Fawns-Ritchie and Deary, 2020). The sum of correct answers in two minutes was defined as the FIQ score of each participant.

As for covariate data, age at MRI and cognitive data acquisition, genetic-informed sex, imaging site, genotyping batch, first ten genetic principal components (PC1-10) were directly downloaded from the UK Biobank dataset. The interval years between vascular risk factor (2006–2010 years) and MRI (2014 + years) assessments were obtained by subtraction. The mean CT (MCT) of the left (right) cerebral cortex was defined as the average of CT values of the 31 left (right) cortical regions, and the total SA (TSA) of the left (right) cerebral cortex was the sum of SA values of the 31 left (right) cortical regions. Body weight was measured by Tanita BC-418 MA body composition analyzer, and standing height was measured with Seca 202 measure. Body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) was calculated as weight in kilogram (kg) divided by height in meter square (m2). The question (In the past, how often have you smoked tobacco?) was used to assess past tobacco smoking (PTS). The answers of never, once or twice, occasionally, and most or all days were coded as 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively. Alcohol intake frequency (AIF) was coded as 1–6 grades, which corresponded to never, special occasions only, one to three times a month, once or twice a week, three or four times a week, and daily or almost daily. We used BMI, PTS and AIF assessed at the time of MRI data acquisition and HDL measured at baseline as covariates (Table1 and Supplementary Table 1).

In UK Biobank, diseases were determined by self-report, standardized interview, or hospital records (Supplementary Table 3). To test whether blood pressure within the normal range also affects cortical structure, we further excluded patients with hypertension and participants with SBP beyond 90–140 mmHg or DBP beyond 60–90 mmHg (Chobanian et al., 2003). To test whether blood glucose within the normal range also affects cortical structure, we further excluded patients with diabetes and participants with HbA1c level outside of 20–48 mmol/mol (Kristensen et al., 2016). To test whether blood lipid within the normal range also affects cortical structure, we further excluded patients with hyperlipidemia and participants with TG ≥ 11.1 mmol/L (Skulas-Ray et al., 2019).

Statistical analyses

Correlations among stroke risk factors

Since the highly correlated predictors may bias the linear regression analysis, we assessed the correlation structure of predictors included in the model by calculating Spearman correlations between each pair of these genetic and vascular risk factors and their interaction terms. Spearman rank correlation (rho) was defined as low (0.1–0.3), moderate (0.3–0.5), and strong (> 0.5). Predictors with strong correlation were considered in the sensitivity analysis.

Multivariate linear regression model

A multivariate linear regression model was used to investigate the associations between predictors and outcomes, while controlling for age at data acquisition of outcomes, genetic-informed sex, imaging site, genotyping batch, genetic PC1-10, and interval years between predictor and outcome assessments. We also adjusted the hemisphere-specific TSA for SA phenotypes and the hemisphere-specific MCT for CT phenotypes to remove the global effects (Eyler et al., 2012, Shaw et al., 2006). In each model, predictors were PRSIS, SBP, DBP, HbA1c, LDL, TG, and interaction terms between every two risk factors, while outcome was CT or SA phenotype, or FIQ (Fig. S1). Before the regression analysis, all predictors were grand-mean centered to minimize the impact of multicollinearity on model interpretation. We calculated the standardized coefficient (β) for each predictor to show the magnitude and direction of the effect of predictor on outcome.

For each phenotype, we ran the full model by including all predictors and covariates and the reduced model by only including covariates, based on which we calculated the sum of squares of errors (SSE) of the full (SSEfull) and reduced (SSEreduced) models with Eq. (1). Then we conducted F-test (p < 0.05/125 = 4.0 × 10−4, Bonferroni-correction for the total number of phenotypes) to identify the phenotypes that were significantly associated with stroke risk factors after excluding the effects of covariates (Eq. (2)). For each model, we also estimated the partial variance (R2) of each phenotype that could be explained by stroke risk factors rather than covariates (Eq. (3)).

We used two methods to test whether stroke risk factors show similar effects on CT and SA. First, we compared the partial R2 distribution across cortical regions between CT and SA using a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test (p < 0.05). The test generated V-statistic to represent the sum of the ranks of differences between the two measures. Second, we calculated the Spearman rank correlation of partial R2 between CT and SA across the 62 cortical regions.

Sensitive analyses

We conducted sensitivity analyses to test the reliability of the multivariate linear regression model. Considering for the potential relations of BMI with vascular risk factors, we re-performed the analyses by including BMI as an additional covariate. Due to the opposite effects of LDL and HDL on the risk of ischemic stroke, we reconducted the analyses by including HDL as an additional covariate. As PTS and AIF, other risk factors for stroke, may also affect cortical morphometry, we reconducted the analyses by including PTS and AIF as additional covariates to focus on the impact of genetic and vascular risk factors for ischemic stroke. For each pair of highly correlated predictors (rho > 0.5), we reconstructed the regression model by in turn including one of the two predictors in the model to eliminate multicollinearity. The significance of sensitivity analyses was defined as p < 0.05/125 = 4.0 × 10−4, and only consistently significant results in both main and all sensitive analyses were considered as reliable associations.

Specific reliable associations

For each phenotype showing reliable association with stroke risk factors, we investigated which stroke risk factor had consistent associations with the phenotype in the multivariate linear regression model. The significance of association was defined as p < 0.05/the total number of phenotypes, and the reliable associations were defined as those showing significant in both main and all sensitive analyses.

Normal range analyses for specific reliable associations

For each specific reliable association between vascular risk factor and phenotype, we tested whether the vascular risk factor within the normal range also affects the phenotype by excluding patients with related diagnosis and participants with abnormal measurements. For instance, we tested whether blood glucose within the normal range affects the phenotype by excluding patients with diabetes and participants with abnormal HbA1c levels. Significance in these analyses was defined as 0.05 divided by the number of the reliable associations under consideration.

Sex differences in variables and sex-stratified analyses for significant associations

We compared the differences (p < 0.05/28 = 0.0018, Bonferroni-corrected) in the 28 included variables between males (n = 12 978) and females (n = 14 142) using the ‘tableone’ package (version 0.13) in R 4.3.3. We compared the sex differences in continuous variables using two-sample t-test (normality assumption was met) or Mann-Whitney U test (normality assumption was not met), and the sex differences in categorical variables using chi-square test. For the reliable multivariate linear regression models (significant in both main and sensitivity analyses) in the total sample, we re-constructed the models using the same covariates (not including sex) as the main analyses in males and females, respectively. We applied the same thresholds as the main analyses in the total sample to identify the overall and specific associations in these sex-stratified analyses.

Associations between stroke risk factors and FIQ and the mediation analyses

For the cortical phenotypes showing reliable associations with stroke risk factors, we used the linear regression model to test the association between each cortical phenotype and FIQ (p < 0.05/the total number of these cortical phenotypes), while controlling for age, sex, and imaging site. If a stroke risk factor was robustly associated with both FIQ and cortical phenotype, a single mediator CMA was used to test the mediation of the cortical phenotype (mediator) on the association between stroke risk factor (independent variable) and FIQ (dependent variable). CMA was conducted in R (Windows version 4.1.3; mediation package v4.5.0) to compute the average direct effect (ADE) and average causal mediation effect (ACME). Two regression equations (mediator model and outcome model) were fitted to compute ACME. The mediator model was built with stroke risk factor as the independent variable and the mediator (CT or SA phenotype) as the dependent variable. The outcome model was built with stroke risk factor and mediator as the independent variables, and FIQ as the dependent variable. The covariates were the same as those used in the multivariate regression analyses. Bias-corrected confidence interval (CI) of each mediation model was estimated by 1 000 simulations. The significant mediation was confirmed if the 95 % CI of ACME did not include zero. As the univariate regression models in CMA may generate associations different from the multivariate regression models, we only included consistent predictor-phenotype associations (significant at the same level and in the same effect direction) in both univariate and multivariate regression models in the CMA analyses.

Results

Among the 43 026 participants with qualified brain MRI data from the UK Biobank cohort, we excluded 15 906 (37.0 %) participants including 6 525 non-Caucasian individuals, 6 489 individuals with a history of stroke, malignant brain neoplasms, mental and behavioral disorders, or nervous system diseases, and 2 892 individuals with missing data for PRSIS score (n = 80), blood pressure (n = 4), LDL (n = 1 480), TG (n = 18), and HbA1c (n = 1 310) (Fig. S2). The exclusions resulted in a final sample of 27 120 participants (Table 1). In the sensitivity analyses, due to missing covariate data, we further excluded 888 individuals for BMI, 536 for PTS or AIF, and 2 382 for HDL adjustments, respectively (Fig. S2). We tested whether blood pressure, glucose, lipid within the normal range affected cortical and cognitive phenotypes by excluding 5 139 patients with hypertension and 8 563 participants with abnormal SBP or DBP, 580 patients with diabetes and 162 participants with abnormal HbA1c, and 2 383 patients with hyperlipidemia (all the remaining participants with normal TG), respectively (Fig. S2).

Significant correlations between stroke risk factors

We calculated Spearman rank correlations between 21 predictors (six risk factors and 15 interaction terms) in the total sample (n = 27 120), and found strong correlations (rho = 0.65–0.72, all p < 0.0001) between SBP and DBP and between their interaction terms (Fig. S3). Since high correlation between predictors may bias the regression analysis, we reconstructed the regression model by only including one of the two factors (SBP and DBP) in the model in the sensitivity analysis. We also calculated Spearman rank correlations of BMI, HDL, PTS and AIF with the five vascular risk factors. We found that HDL had moderate correlation with TG (rho = −0.45, p < 0.0001) (Fig. S4).

Overall associations of stroke risk factors with cortical and cognitive phenotypes

In each of the 125 multivariate regression models, we included each cortical phenotype or FIQ as the outcome, PRSIS, SBP, DBP, LDL, TG, HbA1c, and 15 interaction terms as predictors, and age at MRI and cognitive data acquisition, genetic-informed sex, imaging site, genotyping batch, genetic PC1-10, hemisphere-specific TSA for SA or hemisphere-specific MCT for CT phenotypes, and interval years between predictor and outcome assessments as covariates. We calculated the partial R2 values of each phenotype that could be explained by stroke risk factors in both main and sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table 4). In the robustness assessments of these models, we found that the partial R2 values of the 124 cortical phenotypes from the five sensitivity analyses showed strong correlations (rho = 0.90–1.00, all p < 0.0001) with those from the main analyses (Fig. S5). The strong correlations between main and sensitivity analyses were also observed for the partial R2 values of CT (rho = 0.89–1.00) and SA (rho = 0.92–1.00) phenotypes, respectively. Stroke risk factors were consistently associated with 16 CT and two SA phenotypes (p < 0.0004) in all analyses, which explained portions of variances (partial R2, median 0.62 % [IQR 0.44–0.75 %] in main analyses) in these phenotypes with the largest proportion (1.22 %) in the CT of the right superior parietal lobule (Fig. 2A). Specifically, stroke risk factors were associated with CT values of the bilateral cuneus, precuneus, insula, lateral occipital, middle and superior temporal, superior parietal cortices, and the left isthmus of cingulate cortex and pericalcarine cortex, and SA values of the bilateral insula cortices (Table 2 and Fig. 2B). In addition, we also found significant association between stroke risk factors and FIQ (all p < 0.0004; partial R2, range 0.2–0.3 %) in all analyses. We also tested whether stroke risk factors had different effects on CT and SA. In the 62 cortical regions, partial R2 values of CT were weakly correlated with those of SA (rho = 0.26, p = 0.04), and the former (0.21 % [0.13–0.37]) was much larger (V = 253, p < 0.0001) than the latter (0.12 % [0.09–0.15]) (Fig. 2C).

Specific associations of stroke risk factors with cortical and cognitive phenotypes

For the one cognitive and 18 cortical phenotypes showing reliable associations with stroke risk factors, we also tested the effect of each risk factor on each phenotype (p < 0.0026, Bonferroni corrected) in both main and sensitivity analyses (Figs. S6–S12 and Supplementary Table 5). For the 18 cortical phenotypes, we found 51 significant predictor-phenotype associations in the main analyses, of which 26 were significant in all sensitivity analyses, including ten for PRSIS, seven for DBP, three for SBP, three for HbA1c, and three for TG (Fig. 3 and Table 3). However, neither LDL nor interaction terms between stroke risk factors showed reliable associations with these phenotypes. Specifically, PRSIS was negatively correlated with CT in the left (β = −0.029, p = 4.74 × 10−10) and right (β = −0.025, p = 7.76 × 10−8) insular cortices, positively correlated with CT in the bilateral precuneus, superior parietal lobules, and lateral occipital cortices (β = 0.014–0.028, all p < 4.94 × 10−4), and positively correlated with SA in the left (β = 0.024, p = 9.24 × 10−10) and right (β = 0.014, p = 5.50 × 10−4) insular cortices (Fig. 3a and b). DBP was negatively correlated with CT in the bilateral superior temporal gyri and right insular cortex (β = −0.038 to −0.027, p = 8.85 × 10−14–1.82 × 10−7) and positively correlated with CT in the bilateral superior parietal lobules and lateral occipital gyri (β = 0.055–0.065, p = 1.84 × 10−27–2.44 × 10−22) (Fig. 3c). SBP was negatively correlated with CT in the lateral occipital gyri (left: β = −0.030, p = 1.02 × 10−6; right: β = −0.026, p = 5.87 × 10−5) and right middle temporal gyrus (β = −0.02, p = 9.84 × 10−4) (Fig. 3d). HbA1c was positively correlated with CT in the left isthmus of cingulate cortex, left pericalcarine cortex, and right cuneus (β = 0.024–0.03, all p < 1.07 × 10−5) (Fig. 3e). TG was negatively correlated with CT in the bilateral superior temporal gyri and left insular cortex (β = −0.038 to −0.025, p < 1.67 × 10−9) (Fig. 3f). Of these stroke risk factors, only PRSIS (β = −0.027, p = 2.76 × 10−5) and SBP (β = −0.037, p = 1.23 × 10−4) were consistently associated with FIQ. All the above-mentioned statistics were derived from the main analyses.

Specific associations in individuals with normal vascular risk factors

For the reliable associations between vascular risk factors and outcomes (16 cortical phenotypes and FIQ), we tested whether vascular risk factors within normal range also affected phenotypes by excluding patients with clinical diagnosis and participants with abnormal measurements. In 13 418 participants with normal blood pressure and without a diagnosis of hypertension, we found that the four associations of SBP, and two associations of DBP were no longer significant (p < 0.0029, Bonferroni corrected) (Supplementary Table 6). However, we still found significant negative associations (p < 0.0029) of DBP with CT in the bilateral superior temporal gyri (left: β = −0.021, p = 7.95 × 10−4; right: β = −0.028, p = 6.11 × 10−6), and positive associations with CT in the bilateral superior parietal lobules and left lateral occipital gyri (β = 0.032–0.039, all p < 1.42 × 10−5). In 26 378 participants with normal blood glucose and without a diagnosis of diabetes, HbA1c was still positively associated with CT in the left isthmus of cingulate cortex (β = 0.029, p = 6.59 × 10−7) and left pericalcarine cortex (β = 0.016, p = 2.06 × 10−3). In 24 737 participants with normal blood TG and without a diagnosis of hyperlipidemia, TG was still negatively correlated with CT in the bilateral superior temporal gyri and left insular cortex (β = −0.034 to −0.026, all p < 1.53 × 10−9) (Fig. 4).

Sex differences in variables and associations

Among the six predictors, FIQ, and 21 covariates, we found significant sex differences (p < 0.0018) in 12 variables (Supplementary Table 7). Among the 19 reliable overall associations and 28 reliable specific associations, we found that 14 (73.7 %) overall associations (p < 0.0004, Supplementary Table 8) and 14 (50 %) specific associations (p < 0.0026, Supplementary Table 9) were significant in both males and females.

Significant associations between cortical phenotypes and FIQ

To test the effects of the 18 cortical phenotypes reliably associated with stroke risk factors on cognitive decline in the 27 120 participants, we constructed linear regression models with FIQ as the outcome and each of these cortical phenotypes as the predictor, and age at MRI data acquisition, genetic-informed sex, and imaging site as covariates. We found that four CT phenotypes were associated with FIQ (p < 0.0028, Bonferroni corrected; Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 10). The CT values of the left insula cortex and bilateral superior temporal gyri were positively associated with FIQ (β = 0.027–0.061, all p < 6.82 × 10−6), and the CT of the left isthmus of cingulate cortex was negatively associated with FIQ (β = −0.021, p = 3.4 × 10−4).

Mediation of cortical phenotypes on associations between stroke risk factors and FIQ

Based on the consistent associations of PRSIS and SBP with FIQ in all multivariate regression analyses, we further tested whether cortical phenotypes mediated these two associations. Since the univariate and multivariate regression analyses may generate inconsistent associations of PRSIS and SBP with cortical phenotypes, we only included cortical phenotypes showing consistent associations with PRSIS and SBP in the mediation analyses. Of the 13 significant associations of PRSIS or SBP with cortical phenotypes (p < 0.0026, Bonferroni corrected) in the multivariate regression models, 11 showed consistent direction of effects and significant at the same threshold of p < 0.0026. Thus, the 11 cortical phenotypes from the 11 consistent associations were finally used as mediators in CMA. CMA was used to test which cortical phenotypes (mediators) mediated the associations of PRSIS or SBP (independent variables) with FIQ (dependent variable). A significant mediation was defined as the 95 % CI of ACME did not include zero. We found that the CT values of the bilateral insular and lateral occipital cortices mediated the association between PRSIS and FIQ (ACME = −0.0003 to −0.001, all ACME did not include zero, proportion of mediated = 1.04 %–3.11 %), and that the CT of the right middle temporal gyrus mediated the association between SBP and FIQ (ACME = −0.0003, 95 % CI = [−0.0008, −0.0001], proportion of mediated = 0.78 %) (Fig. 5b–f and Supplementary Table 11).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the respective effects and interactions of genetic and vascular risk factors for ischemic stroke on cortical and cognitive phenotypes in 27 120 individuals without a history of stroke. Stroke risk factors accounted for larger variances for CT than SA phenotypes, and significantly affected 18 cortical phenotypes and FIQ. Significant individual effects and non-significant interactions indicate that stroke risk factors affect phenotypes independently. We identified 26 robust associations between stroke risk factors and cortical phenotypes, of which PRSIS and DBP had more extensive (ten and seven phenotypes) and complex (negative and positive) effects than SBP (three negative), HbA1c (three positive), and TG (three negative). Of the 16 reliable associations between vascular risk factors and cortical phenotypes, ten were still significant in participants with normal vascular risk assessments. The associations that lose significance indicate that only abnormal vascular risk assessments affect these phenotypes. We also found that cortical phenotypes mediated the associations of PRSIS and SBP with FIQ, indicating the potential neural mechanisms underlying the associations between stroke risk factors and cognitive decline in middle-aged and older people. These results should be considered in dementia prevention and in identifying target brain regions for stroke rehabilitation with a cross-sectional case-control design.

Almost all previous studies investigate the pair-wise associations of stroke risk factors with cortical or cognitive phenotypes, which cannot account for the correlations between stroke risk factors and their interactions. In this study, we constructed a multivariate regression model for each outcome by including all stroke risk factors and interaction terms in the model, which can simultaneously assess the overall effect of stroke risk factors and the independent effect of each factor or interaction term. As an extension of the reported associations of stroke risk factors with cortical and cognitive phenotypes (Deary et al., 1998, Leritz et al., 2011, Muldoon et al., 1997, Williamson et al., 2018), we found that stroke risk factors were reliably associated with 18 cortical phenotypes and FIQ in both main and sensitivity analyses. These reliable associations may explain why ischemic stroke and its risk factors are associated with cognitive decline and dementia (Kloppenborg et al., 2008, Knopman et al., 2001), indicating that vascular risk factors should be considered in the action of dementia prevention. For instance, in future dementia research, one can test the possibility of combining PRS for ischemic stroke with PRS for Alzheimer's disease (PRSAD) to improve the identification of individuals with high risk for AD (a major type of dementia in elder people) because the combination of PRSAD and PRS for depression has shown better prediction for AD conversion than individual PRS (Xu et al., 2018).

In the past decades, the non-invasive brain stimulation techniques (such as TMS) have emerged as novel strategies for stroke rehabilitation; however, their effectiveness depends on the correctness and precision of the identification of target brain regions (Khedr et al., 2005). The effects of stroke risk factors on cortical phenotypes in individuals without a history of stroke indicate that there are pre-existing cortical differences resulted from different exposure levels to stroke risk factors between patients with stroke and healthy controls, which may bias the intergroup brain differences identified by many cross-sectional case-control neuroimaging studies. For instance, the insular cortical atrophy on the lesion-side is identified as shared brain structural damage in patients with subcortical stroke (Jiang et al., 2017); however, the finding is more likely influenced by the effects of stroke risk factors because we found that high PRSIS, DBP, and TG could result in insular cortical thinning before stroke onset. Therefore, we suggest that the effects of stroke risk factors should be considered when identifying the target brain regions for stroke rehabilitation using a cross-sectional case-control design.

Converging evidence supports that CT and SA can reflect different aspects of cortical morphometry. The radial unit hypothesis posits that the SA of a cytoarchitectonic region is mainly determined by the number of contributing proliferative units (ontogenetic columns), whereas its CT is mainly driven by the number of cell divisions within these units (Rakic, 1988). During postnatal development, the peak time (1.7 years) of mean CT is much earlier than that (11.0 years) of total SA, and then they decline with different rates (Rakic, 1988). Despite CT and SA are highly heritable, they show weak or even inverse genetic correlation (Rakic, 1988). These differences may account for the larger effects of stroke risk factors on CT than SA phenotypes, indicating that CT is more sensitive to stroke risk factors than SA. When inspecting the independent effect of each risk factor or interaction term, we found that stroke risk factors affected cortical and cognitive phenotypes via their individual effects rather than interactions, indicating that different mechanisms may underlie the effects of stroke risk factors on cortical phenotypes and FIQ.

A major contribution of this study is the identification of the effects of PRSIS on both cortical phenotypes and FIQ. Although PRSIS showed mixed effects on cortical phenotypes, the correlations of higher PRSIS with insular thinning and FIQ decline and the mediation of insular thinning for the negative correlation between PRSIS and FIQ may indicate that the insular thinning is a potential neural mechanism underlying the observed association between PRSIS and cognitive decline. As for the positive associations between PRSIS and cortical phenotypes, we are unclear whether they reflect adaptive structural reorganization in response to genetic predisposition to ischemic stroke (Gulyaeva, 2017) or maladaptive cortical development. SBP exhibited detrimental effects on both CT phenotypes and FIQ. However, these effects were no longer significant in individuals with normal blood pressure, suggesting that only abnormal SBP shows adverse consequences on cortical morphometry and cognition, consistent with previous reports (Gonzales et al., 2017, Leritz et al., 2011, Maillard et al., 2012, Shen et al., 2023). Moreover, the mediation analysis indicates that higher SBP may lead to cognitive decline by reducing the thickness of the right middle temporal cortex. The results indicate that the control of abnormal SBP may be an effective strategy for dementia prevention. In contrast to SBP, DBP showed mixed effects on cortical phenotypes and several effects were still significant in individuals with normal blood pressure, which may account for the reported inconsistent associations between DBP and cortical phenotypes. Despite of CT reduction in patients with diabetes (Choi et al., 2020, Shaw et al., 2017, Wennberg et al., 2016), HbA1c was positively associated CT phenotypes in individuals with normal HbA1c, suggesting that the relatively high blood glucose within the normal range may resist cortical thinning in middle-aged and older people. Consisting with previous studies (Palta et al., 2021, Schwarz et al., 2018, Shen et al., 2023), we found consistent negative association between TG and CT, indicating that TG lowering may prevent cerebral cortex from damage. Both main and sensitivity analyses showed non-significant associations between LDL and cortical phenotypes, indicating the lack of independent effect of LDL on cortical morphometry, accounting for the discrepancy (non-significant, positive, or negative) on the association between cholesterol and CT (Gonzales et al., 2017, Krishnadas et al., 2013, Schwarz et al., 2018).

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting our results. First, this was a cross-sectional observation study, preventing us from identifying causal relations between stroke risk factors and cortical phenotypes. A longitudinal design or Mendelian randomization analysis may be helpful in determining the causal relations. Second, although we did not find significant multiplicative interactions between stroke risk factors, the additive interactions may exist because we found that different risk factors affected the same cortical phenotype in the same effect direction. Third, despite common confounders were included in our analyses, our results may be also affected by unobserved confounders. Finally, our analyses were conducted only in Caucasian participants to ensure the accuracy of PRS calculation, but the strategy prevents from extrapolating our results to other ancestral populations.

In conclusion, we used multivariate linear regression to identify the individual effects and interactions of stroke risk factors on cortical and cognitive phenotypes in 27 120 subjects without a history of stroke, and the results were confirmed by five sensitivity analyses. We found 18 cortical phenotypes showing reliable associations with stroke risk factors, which may bias the cross-sectional comparison of brain differences between stroke patients and healthy controls. We provided further insight by finding stronger individual effects than interactions and stronger effects on CT than SA phenotypes. We found 26 reliable associations between stroke risk factors and cortical phenotypes, of which ten were also observed in individuals within normal risk assessments, indicating that DBP, HbA1c, and TG within the normal range also affect cortical morphometry. We also found that several CT phenotypes mediated the associations between stroke risk factors and cognitive decline. These findings indicate that stroke risk factors may contribute to cognitive decline by affecting cortical morphometry and the effects should be taken into consideration in the target identification for stroke rehabilitation with a cross-sectional case-control design.
