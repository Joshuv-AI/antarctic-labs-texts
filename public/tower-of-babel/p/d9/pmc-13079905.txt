Seroprevalence, isolation, comprehensive characterization, and pathogenicity of Clostridium perfringens strain from yak in Xizang, China
Authors: Dongjing Wang, Jiangyong Zeng, Chunfa Liu, Jun Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClostridium perfringens is an anaerobic, toxin-producing pathogen responsible for enteric and systemic disease in livestock, yet its ecology in high-altitude yaks remains poorly understood. We conducted a comprehensive investigation covering eight counties of the Xizang Autonomous Region between July 2021 and October 2024. Serological screening of 922 unvaccinated yaks revealed an individual-level antibody prevalence of 0.76% (95% CI: 0.4–1.6) and a herd-level prevalence of 25% (95% CI: 12.7–43.4). Multivariable logistic regression identified residence below 4000 m as the sole significant risk factor (adjusted OR = 8.75; 95%CI: 1.3–56.8; P = 0.04). From seven seropositive animals, one representative isolate CPTibet-Y1 was obtained and subjected to detailed phenotypic, genomic and virulence analyses. Phenotypic profiling confirmed classic biochemical traits of C. perfringens, while 16 S rRNA, Toxin gene PCR typing and whole-genome sequencing classified the strain as toxin type A, harboring Toxin gene cpa, but lacking cpb, etx, iap, cpe and netB. Antimicrobial resistance profiling revealed broad-spectrum resistance encompassing sulfonamides, lincosamides and fluoroquinolones, underpinned by six acquired resistance determinants. In a murine infection model, intraperitoneal challenge with 5 × 107 CFU resulted in 100% lethality within 48 h accompanied by severe intestinal necrosis, hemorrhage and systemic pathology. This study provides unequivocal evidence for the presence of multidrug-resistant, hypervirulent type A strains on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, underscoring the need for enhanced surveillance and targeted control strategies in yak-farming communities.Keywords: Clostridium perfringens, Qinghai-Tibet Plateau yak, Seroepidemiology, Gene characteristicsSubject terms: Diseases, Microbiology, Molecular biology

Introduction

Clostridium perfringens (C. perfringens) is Gram-positive, anaerobic, spore-forming bacterium. As a significant zoonotic pathogen, it can cause food poisoning in humans and necrotic enteritis in both animals and humans1. This bacterium induces intestinal diseases through the production of various potent toxins, including alpha toxin (cpa), beta toxin (cpb), C. perfringens enterotoxin (cpe), epsilon toxin (etx), iota toxin (itx/iap), and necrotic enteritis B-like toxin (NetB)2. Based on the production of these six major toxins, C. perfringens is classified into seven toxin types (A-G). Type C can cause fatal hemorrhagic enteritis in newborns, while type D primarily leads to enterotoxemia in sheep and goats3.

Infections caused by C. perfringens, such as necrotic enteritis, result in significant economic losses to the livestock industry each year. However, there is still a lack of effective commercial vaccines4. Therefore, conducting regular epidemiological investigations and monitoring is of great importance for the prevention and control of this disease. According to reports, the isolation rate of this bacterium can reach 23.5% (93/395) in lactating animals with mastitis, calf diarrhea, and a history of acute sudden death5. Another study showed that among 40 samples of minced and non-minced mutton collected from slaughterhouses and retail stores in Pakistan, 10% (4/40) tested positive for the alpha toxin gene of C. perfringens6. In wildlife samples from the Amazon region, the carriage rate of C. perfringens was as high as 61.5% (40/65), and all were identified as type A7.

The yak is a unique bovine species endemic to the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and its surrounding regions. It possesses distinctive adaptations to hypoxic environments and serves as an invaluable source of livelihood for local communities, as well as a vital component of the regional economy and culture8,9. However, due to the harsh environmental conditions characterized by severe hypoxia, forage scarcity, and large diurnal temperature fluctuations, the yak’s resistance is compromised, leading to frequent outbreaks of various diseases10,11. These disease occurrences have been demonstrated to inflict substantial economic losses on both herders and livestock enterprises12. A study investigated the prevalence, antimicrobial susceptibility, and genetic diversity of C. perfringens isolated from yaks on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau showed that 75% isolates belonging to types A and 98.61% of isolates exhibiting multidrug resistance13. Despite the significant potential threat posed by this bacterium, systematic and in-depth research on the epidemiological characteristics, molecular typing, and virulence potential of C. perfringens in Tibetan yak populations is still lacking.

To clarify recent trends in C. perfringens infection in Tibetan yaks, this study investigated the seropositivity rate in yaks from certain regions of Tibet between 2021 and 2024. Bacterial strains were isolated and identified from a large number of samples, followed by systematic phenotypic and genotypic characterization of representative strains. Their toxin types and virulence gene profiles were determined, antibiotic resistance was assessed, and pathogenic potential was evaluated via a mouse model. This study provides fundamental genetic resources for elucidating the virulence mechanisms and genetic background of the bacterium. It also offers a scientific basis for assessing the risks posed by C. perfringens in high-altitude yak farming and contributes to the development of targeted prevention and control strategies in the Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau region.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The sample size for the yak serological survey (n = 922) was determined based on convenience sampling across the major yak-rearing prefectures in Xizang to achieve a geographically representative coverage. Between July 2021 and October 2024, a total of 922 blood samples were collected from yak herds that had never been vaccinated against C. perfringens. Sampling covered eight counties across three prefectures of the Xizang Autonomous Region: Dangxiong and Nimu counties in Lhasa City; Nyainrong, Lhari, Nyima, and Biru counties in Nagqu City; Yadong and Sakya counties in Shigatse City (Fig. 1). Information on age, sex, altitude, and husbandry system was recorded for each animal.

Geographic distribution of yak-sampling sites in the Tibet Autonomous Region.

Yellow areas indicate the eight counties (Dangxiong and Nyêmo in Lhasa City; Nyainrong, Lhari, Nyima, and Biru in Nagqu City; Yadong and Saga in Xigazê City). The map was generated by the authors using QGIS software (version 3.12, https://qgis.org).

Serological assay

Blood samples were centrifuged at 4000×g for 10 min to obtain serum. Antibodies against C. perfringens were quantified with a commercial indirect ELISA kit (Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd., lot YJ258920) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm. According to the kit protocol, the cut-off value was defined as the mean OD of the negative controls plus 0.15. Samples with an OD ≥ the cut-off value were defined as positive at the individual level; herds containing ≥ 1 positive animal were considered positive herds.

Bacterial isolation and microscopy

Fresh faeces were inoculated into 1.5mL reinforced clostridial medium (RCM) and incubated anaerobically (37 °C, 5–6 h). Cultures were streaked onto TSC agar and 5% sheep blood agar and incubated anaerobically at 37 °C overnight. Single, black-centered colonies with a surrounding double zone of β-haemolysis were selected, Gram-stained, and examined microscopically for large, Gram-positive rods.

Biochemical identification

Isolates were subjected to 22 conventional biochemical tests in duplicate: acid and gas from lactose, sucrose, melezitose, raffinose, D-ribose, rhamnose, glucose, maltose, xylose, arabinose, mannitol, sorbitol, glycerol, salicin; gelatin liquefaction; milk fermentation test; hydrogen sulfide; MR/VP tests; catalase; urease; and indole production. Reactions were read after 24–48 h anaerobic incubation at 37 °C.

16 S rRNA gene amplification and sequencing

DNA was extracted by the boiling method. PCR was performed with universal primers F (5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′) and R (5′-AAGGAGGTGATCCAGCCGCA-3′) in a 25µL reaction (Green Taq Mix 12.5µL, 10µM primers 1µL each, DNA extract 2µL, ddH₂O 8.5µL). Cycling: 95 °C 5 min; 35 cycles of 95 °C 10 s, 55 °C 20 s, 72 °C 1.5 min; final extension 72 °C 10 min. Amplicons (≈ 1.4 kb) were visualised on 1% agarose gels, purified (Gel Extraction Kit, TsingKe), and Sanger-sequenced (TsingKe Biotech). Sequences were edited in SnapGene, compared with NCBI or using BLASTn, and aligned with MEGA 12 (neighbor-joining, 1000 bootstrap replicates).

Toxin gene PCR typing

Primers targeting cpa, cpb, etx, iap/itx, cpe, netB and cpb2 are listed in Table 18. Multiplex PCR (cpa, cpb, etx, iap/itx) was carried out in 50 µL reactions (Green Taq Mix 25µL, 10µM primers 1µL each, DNA extract 1µL, ddH₂O 16µL) under 94 °C 10 min; 30 cycles of 94 °C 30 s, 51.6 °C 30 s, 72 °C 30 s; 72 °C 10 min. Singleplex PCR (cpe, netB, cpb2) used 20µL volumes (Green Taq Mix 10µL, primers 1µL each, DNA extract 1µL, ddH₂O 7µL) with 94 °C 10 min; 30 cycles of 95 °C 1 min, 54 °C 1 min, 72 °C 1 min; 72 °C 10 min. Products were resolved on 1.5% agarose gels and documented under UV. Following gel documentation, all amplicons representing positive signals were purified (Gel Extraction Kit, TsingKe) and subjected to Sanger sequencing (TsingKe Biotech). The obtained sequences were compared with the NCBI nucleotide database using BLASTn to verify their identity and to exclude false positives.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antibiotic discs (Hangzhou Microbial Reagent Co.) were used according to CLSI (M100-Ed32) guidelines. A 0.5 McFarland suspension was swabbed on 5% sheep blood Mueller-Hinton agar; discs were applied and plates incubated anaerobically at 37 °C for 18–24 h. Zone diameters were measured in duplicate and interpreted using CLSI breakpoints for anaerobes. Quality control employed C. perfringens ATCC13124.

Genomic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted by the cetyl trimethyl ammonium bromide (CTAB) method. A Nanopore library was prepared and sequenced on a MinION Mk1C for 24 h at 400 bp s⁻¹. The filtered reads were assembled by Canu v1.5 software, and then circlator v1.5.5 was taken to cyclizing assembly genome. Coding genes prediction was performed by Prodigal v2.6.3. The GenBlastA v1.0.4 program was used to scan the whole genomes after masking predicted functional genes. Putative candidates were then analyzed by searching for nonsynonymous mutations and frame-shift mutations using GeneWise v2.2.0. Transfer RNA (tRNA) genes were predicted with tRNAscan-SE v2.0, Ribosome RNA (rRNA) genes were predicted with Infernal v1.1.314. Drug resistance genes were identified by BLAST searches against the CARD database.To predict gene functions, the predicted protein sequences were assigned to Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) categories using eggNOG-mapper v2. The circular genome map was subsequently generated for visualization using the Circos software v0.66.

Animal virulence assay

For the mouse virulence experiment, a group size of n = 5 was chosen based on common practice in preliminary pathogenicity studies for bacteria, which provides a clear indication of severe virulence while adhering to the 3Rs principles, particularly Reduction. Ten specific-pathogen-free Kunming mice (18–22 g, 6–8 weeks old, female) were purchased from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Mice were housed in groups of five in individually ventilated cages under a 12/12 h light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2℃ and 50 ± 10% humidity, with ad libitum access to standard rodent diet and water. After a one-week acclimatization period. Mice were randomly allocated to two groups (n = 5) using a random number generator. CPTibet-Y1 was grown in RCM to late log phase. The culture was then centrifuged at 4000 × g for 10 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the bacterial pellet was resuspended in sterile PBS. The suspension was adjusted to 1 × 10⁸ CFU mL⁻¹, and 0.5mL injected intraperitoneally into the test group; controls received sterile PBS. The investigators involved in outcome assessment were blinded to the group allocation. Mice were monitored twice daily for 2 days for predefined humane endpoint criteria, which included severe lethargy, inability to access food or water, rapid weight loss (> 20% of initial body weight), or dyspnea. Moribund animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation under deep anesthesia induced by intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg). At the end of the 48-hour experimental period, all surviving mice in the control group were also euthanized using the same method. Necropsies were performed immediately after euthanasia, and tissues were collected for histopathology.

Statistical analysis

All data were entered into Microsoft Excel 2010 and analyzed in GraphPad Prism V10.1.2. Associations between seroprevalence and age, sex, altitude, or husbandry type were first examined by Fisher’s exact test (p < 0.05). Variables with p < 0.20 were introduced into a multivariable logistic regression; only those with p < 0.05 remained in the final model selected by likelihood-ratio tests and the lowest AIC value. Model fit was assessed with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. The data met the assumptions of the statistical tests used. For logistic regression, the linearity of continuous variables with the logit was assessed and found to be acceptable, and there was no evidence of multicollinearity among the predictor variables. For the mouse toxicity assay, survival curves between the control and treatment groups were compared using the Log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test, with a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant15.

Results

Seroprevalence of C. perfringens antibodies and risk factor analysis

A total of 922 serum samples were collected from yaks across 28 herds. Seropositive antibodies against C. perfringens were detected in 7 samples. The overall individual animal-level seroprevalence was 0.76% (7/922, 95% CI: 0.4–1.6). At the herd level, 7 out of the 28 herds had at least one positive animal, yielding a herd seroprevalence of 25.00% (7/28, 95% CI: 12.7–43.4).

The potential associations between various risk factors and seropositivity were assessed. The results of the univariate analysis are presented in Table 2. Neither age (OR = 2.33, 95% CI: 0.52–10.48, P = 0.27) nor sex (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.22–4.35, P = 1.00) was significantly associated with seropositivity. However, altitude and farming mode were identified as significant at the univariate level (P < 0.2) and were thus selected for further multivariable analysis. Yaks from lower altitudes (< 4000 m) had significantly higher odds of being seropositive compared to those from high altitudes (≥ 4000 m) (OR = 0.22, 95% CI: 0.05–1.00, P = 0.05). Similarly, animals from organized farms showed a higher, though not statistically definitive, odds of seropositivity compared to those from free-ranging households (OR = 3.45, 95% CI: 0.66–18.04, P = 0.16). These two factors were included in a multivariable logistic regression model. After adjustment, altitude remained independently and significantly associated with C. perfringens seropositivity (Table 3). Yaks residing at altitudes below 4000 m had 8.75 times higher odds of being seropositive than those at 4000 m or above (AOR = 8.75, 95% CI: 1.29–56.75, P = 0.04).

Isolation and identification of a C. perfringens

To further investigate, 7 fecal samples were collected from these seven seropositive yaks. A single isolate, designated CPTibet-Y1, was successfully isolated and purified based on typical black-centered colonies with white edges on TSC solid medium (Fig. 2A), as well as characteristic double-zone hemolysis on 5% sheep blood agar (Fig. 2B). Gram staining revealed robust, Gram-positive rods (Fig. 2C). Biochemical characteristics of CPTibet-Y1 confirmed the phenotypic identity of the strain as C. perfringens. CPTibet-Y1 was positive for lactose fermentation, sucrose fermentation, glucose fermentation, maltose fermentation, D-ribose fermentation, rhamnose fermentation, xylose fermentation, mannitol fermentation, sorbitol fermentation, gelatin liquefaction, the milk fermentation test, and urease production. In contrast, it was negative for melezitose fermentation, raffinose fermentation, arabinose fermentation, salicin fermentation, Voges-Proskauer (V-P) test, methyl red (M-R) test, hydrogen sulfide production, peroxidase activity, and indole production (Table 4). Molecular identification via 16 S rRNA gene sequencing and subsequent phylogenetic analysis further confirmed that strain CPTibet-Y1 clustered unequivocally with reference strains of C. perfringens (Fig. 2D).

To gain a comprehensive understanding of its genetic makeup, the complete genome of CPTibet-Y1 was sequenced using Nanopore technology. The genome comprises a single circular chromosome of 3,329,457 bp with 28.4% GC content and 1.4% repetitive sequence content. A total of 2904 coding sequences (CDS), 95 tRNAs, and 30 rRNA operons were predicted (Fig. 3A). Critical analysis of the genome revealed a repertoire of virulence-associated genes. PCR amplification and in silico analysis confirmed the presence of the alpha-toxin gene cpa, classifying CPTibet-Y1 as type A (Fig. 3B and C). Sanger sequencing of the cpa amplicon and subsequent BLASTn analysis revealed 99.63% identity to the reference alpha-toxin gene sequence, providing definitive molecular evidence for the presence of this key virulence determinant. Notably, genes encoding other major lethal toxins (beta-cpb, epsilon-etx, iota-iap/itx, CPE-cpe and NetB-netB) were absent.

Antimicrobial susceptibility profile

The antimicrobial susceptibility of CPTibet-Y1 was determined against a panel of 22 antibiotics belonging to 6 classes (Table 5). The strain demonstrated resistance (R) to sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, clindamycin, enrofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, vancomycin, and exhibited intermediate susceptibility (I) to tetracycline, doxycycline, ampicillin, piperacillin, ofloxacin, cefalexin, and polymyxin B. It was susceptible (S) to minocycline, penicillin, carbenicillin, oxacillin, norfloxacin, ceftriaxone, cefoperazone, chloramphenicol, and erythromycin.

Genomic analysis revealed six acquired resistance genes in CPTibet-Y1: tet44, tetA(P), ermQ, ANT(6)-Ib, AAC(6')-Ie-APH(2")-Ia and mprF. The tet44-encoded ribosomal protection protein and the TetA(P) efflux pump together account for the intermediate susceptibility observed for tetracycline and doxycycline, whereas minocycline retains full susceptibility, likely because it is less affected by these mechanisms. Methylation of 23 S rRNA by ErmQ abolishes clindamycin binding and generates a resistant phenotype, whereas erythromycin remains susceptible, consistent with an inducible MLSB profile. The bifunctional aminoglycoside-modifying enzyme AAC(6)-Ie-APH(2)-Ia and the ANT(6)-Ib nucleotidyltransferase predict high-level resistance to gentamicin and streptomycin, respectively. Finally, MprF-mediated lysinylation of phosphatidylglycerol reduces the affinity of polymyxin B, explaining its intermediate activity, and likely contributes to the vancomycin-resistant phenotype observed in the absence of van genes.

Pathogenicity in a mouse model

The virulence of CPTibet-Y1 was assessed in vivo using a mouse model (Fig. 4A). All mice in the negative control group survived and remained healthy throughout the experiment. In contrast, mice challenged with CPTibet-Y1 culture supernatant exhibited severe clinical signs, including lethargy, ruffled fur, and hunched posture, culminating in 100% mortality within 48 h post-injection (Fig. 4B). Necropsy of the deceased mice revealed significant pathological lesions, including intestinal congestion, hemorrhage, and gaseous distension. Histopathological findings are summarized in Fig. 4C. In the control group, all tissues displayed normal architecture: cardiac myofibers were intact; splenic white pulp, central arteries and red pulp appeared regular; pulmonary alveoli were clear with thin alveolar septa; and intestinal mucosa, muscularis mucosae, muscular layer and goblet cells were unremarkable. In contrast, treated animals exhibited multifocal lesions: the myocardium showed hemorrhage and congestion without myofiber loss; the spleen displayed pronounced red-pulp congestion while white pulp and central arteries remained preserved; lungs presented alveolar congestion and hemorrhage with thickened alveolar walls; and intestines revealed mucosal degeneration and necrosis, characterized by sloughed epithelial cells, whereas goblet cells and the muscular layer were largely intact.

Discussion

C. perfringens is a globally important pathogen in livestock production, yet knowledge of its molecular characteristics and virulence potential in yaks on the Tibet Plateau remains scarce. Here we present the comprehensive characterization of a C. perfringens isolate recovered from Tibetan yaks, integrating serological, microbiological, genomic and experimental virulence analyses to provide new insights into the pathogen’s genetic make-up and pathogenicity in this unique ruminant host.

C. perfringens infections are among the most challenging threats to food safety and agricultural development16. The bacterium infects a broad range of hosts, including cattle, yaks and humans13,17. In Ontario swine farms, C. perfringens was isolated from 64% (225/354) of faecal samples18. In Egypt, 51.5% (103/200) of lamb faecal samples were positive, and the organism was associated with severe diarrhoea and death19. In 2022, 33 C. perfringens strains were recovered from the intestinal contents of 160 water buffaloes and cattle sampled across four major Pakistani dairy regions20. In the present study, 922 yak serum samples collected randomly from Lhasa, Nagqu and Shigatse were screened, yielding seven seropositive herds. Between 2021 and 2024, antibody prevalence in Tibetan yaks showed a downward trend, likely reflecting the progressive improvement of animal disease-control systems. Multivariable logistic regression identified altitude < 4000 m as a significant risk factor: yaks living below this threshold had a higher seroprevalence than those at ≥ 4000 m. This may be attributable to more rapid economic development, greater human mobility and more frequent livestock trading at lower elevations, all of which increase pathogen transmission opportunities. Interestingly, studies in Ethiopia found the opposite relationship for ovine pasteurellosis, with higher positivity at high altitude17, underscoring the complex interplay of environment, management and host species.

CPTibet-Y1 displayed classical C. perfringens traits: double-zone β-haemolysis on blood agar, Gram-positive rods, lactose fermentation and gelatin liquefaction. Whole-genome sequencing confirmed the strain as Type A, harbouring the chromosomal cpa gene encoding α-toxin but lacking the major toxin genes cpb, etx, iap, cpe and netB. This genetic background resembles many environmental or non-pathogenic Type A strains, yet al.so aligns with certain animal enteric disease isolates21. Type A strains are ubiquitous in the environment and the intestinal tract, usually carry no additional plasmid-borne toxin genes, and rely mainly on cpa and perfringolysin O (pfO) for virulence22. The absence of known enterotoxin genes in CPTibet-Y1 suggests it may be incapable of causing classic food-borne illness or necrotic enteritis. Nevertheless, Type A strains can still induce disease in specific hosts or settings through cpa and other uncharacterized virulence factors. Moreover, its spore resistance is expected to be relatively low, since highly resistant spores are typically associated with chromosomal cpe-positive Type F or Type C strains23.

CPTibet-Y1 exhibits a multidrug-resistant (MDR) phenotype with high-level resistance to sulfonamides, lincosamides and fluoroquinolones. Whole-genome sequencing identified six acquired resistance genes located on mobile genetic elements (MGEs), including the tetracycline-resistance determinant tet44, the macrolide-lincosamide-streptogramin B resistance gene ermQ, and the bifunctional aminoglycoside-modifying enzyme gene AAC(6)-Ie-APH(2)-Ia. The presence of these genes fully accounts for the observed MDR profile. This resistance pattern closely mirrors that reported for C. perfringens from yaks on the plateau13, indicating that prolonged, non-prescription use of sulfamethoxypyridazine and fluoroquinolone growth promoters in the region has created sustained selective pressure that has driven horizontal transfer and fixation of MDR determinants. An equivalent gene constellation has been documented in poultry24,25, camels26 and turkeys27 worldwide, suggesting dissemination via Inc18 or pCW3 like plasmids among Gram-positive anaerobes across host species. Limited veterinary drug availability and scarce therapeutic alternatives in remote pastoral areas heighten the risk that CPTibet-Y1, especially when co-infecting with Brachyspira or other clostridial species, will result in treatment failures, thereby threatening the yak industry and public health on the plateau.

In the mouse model, this A-type C. perfringens strain elicited fulminant lethality: all animals succumbed within 48 h of intraperitoneal challenge, exhibiting severe hemorrhagic enteritis, diffuse hepatic and splenic necrosis, and systemic multi-organ edema. Although the isolate harbours only the cpa gene, its virulence markedly exceeds that of reference A-type strains. Secreted hydrolases that are markedly up-regulated in the CPTibet-Y1 genome likely act synergistically to amplify the inflammatory cascade, breach the intestinal barrier and precipitate systemic pathology. C. perfringens pathogenicity is polygenic23. The exceptional virulence of CPTibet-Y1 may therefore arise from cooperation between atypical virulence factors and predicted hydrolytic enzymes. Further study highlighted that host-adaptive evolution and acquisition of mobile virulence determinants can substantially enhance pathogenicity beyond classical toxin typing28. Consistent with this, the research demonstrated marked virulence heterogeneity among A-type strains in necrotic enteritis models, underscoring that toxin profile alone is insufficient to predict disease severity29,30. Thus, the hypervirulence of CPTibet-Y1 strongly indicates that, under specific genetic or ecological pressures, certain A-type lineages can evolve severe disease potential through the acquisition of additional virulence factors or altered virulence-gene regulation. These findings emphasize the need to look beyond toxin typing and to consider individual genomic architecture, host adaptation and the contribution of MGEs when assessing C. perfringens pathogenicity.

A limitation of the present study is that only a single isolate was recovered from a limited number of positive samples, which may not fully represent the population structure of C. perfringens circulating in Tibetan yaks. Nevertheless, the potent virulence and antimicrobial resistance displayed by CPTibet-Y1 highlight a tangible threat to yak health and productivity. The complete genome sequence of CPTibet-Y1 now provides a crucial reference for future comparative genomics and molecular epidemiology studies of livestock-derived C. perfringens in the plateau ecosystem. Broader surveillance is needed to determine the prevalence of such hypervirulent MDR strains in high-altitude regions. In summary, our findings reveal that Tibetan yaks carry C. perfringens strains with significant pathogenic and antimicrobial-resistance potential, thereby enhancing our capacity to monitor, understand and control clostridial disease in this ecologically fragile and economically vital region.
