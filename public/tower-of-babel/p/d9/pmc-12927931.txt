Understanding Treatment Care and Delays in Acquired Hemophilia A: A Population‐Based Study From Nova Scotia, Canada
Authors: Rachelle Blackman, Luke Y C Chen, Mary‐Margaret Keating, Jason Quinn, Sue Robinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesAcquired Hemophilia A (AHA) is a rare, potentially life‐threatening bleeding disorder caused by autoantibodies against factor VIII (FVIII). We aimed to characterize patients with AHA in Nova Scotia, Canada, and assess diagnostic delays.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, population‐based study of adults (≥ 18 years) with AHA treated at the central Hemophilia Treatment Center (HTC) from 2006 to 2024. Demographics, presentation, treatment, outcomes, and diagnostic timelines were reviewed.ResultsTwenty‐seven cases were identified, with a mean annual incidence of 1.46 per million. Median age was 74 years (Interquartile range [IQR] 63.5–80); 26% had malignancy and 22% autoimmune disease. All patients received immunosuppressive therapy, and 63% required bypassing agents. Relapsing or refractory disease occurred in 30%. The 5‐year overall survival was 48%. Diagnosis was delayed ≥ 7 days in 63% of cases, with median times of 32 days (IQR 4.5–75) from first bleeding and 10 days (IQR 6–45.5) from first elevated PTT. Patients had a median of 2 (IQR 2–5) bleeding‐related encounters before diagnosis. Delayed diagnoses were linked to younger age, disease severity, and rural residence.ConclusionDespite centralized care, diagnostic delays were frequent, underscoring the need for early coagulation testing in unexplained bleeding.Keywords: access to care, acquired hemophilia, bleeding disorder, delayed diagnosis, population‐based

Introduction

Acquired hemophilia A (AHA) is a rare bleeding disorder caused by autoantibodies against coagulation factor VIII (FVIII), leading to reduced FVIII activity, spontaneous or excessive bleeding, and high mortality [1]. The annual incidence is estimated at 1.48 cases per million, though underreporting is suspected, likely due to missed or delayed diagnoses and limited population‐based data [2, 3]. AHA most commonly affects older adults, with a median age of approximately 74 years, and presents with spontaneous bleeding and an isolated prolonged activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), confirmed by reduced FVIII activity and inhibitor quantification using the Bethesda assay [4, 5]. Approximately half of the cases are associated with underlying conditions such as malignancy, autoimmune disease, and pregnancy [4].

Management of AHA involves controlling bleeding with hemostatic agents and eradicating inhibitors with immunosuppressive therapy (IST), with remission achieved in 60%–80% of patients [6]. Despite improvements in early recognition and treatment, diagnostic delays remain a major concern [7], and morbidity and mortality remain high due to bleeding, comorbidities, and IST‐related complications. Mortality ranges from 4% in pregnancy‐associated cases to 42% in other populations [6, 8]. In contrast to historical data, recent studies have reported improved mortality rates of approximately 4%, likely due to earlier recognition, treatment, and advancements in available treatment modalities [2, 9, 10].

Poor prognostic factors at time of diagnosis include advanced age, malignancy, poor performance status, low FVIII, ICU admission, and high inhibitor titers [11, 12]. Patients with FVIII < 0.01 IU/mL (< 1%) and/or > 20 BU inhibitors have only a 10% chance of remission with prednisone alone, prompting changes in IST recommendations [6]. Delayed diagnosis is thought to contribute to poor outcomes in up to one‐third of cases [5].

Most data on AHA derive from registries and single‐center cohorts. Population‐based studies are limited, with reported median diagnostic delays ranging 14–19 days, but small sample sizes limit evaluation [7, 13]. Notably, diagnosis at a Hemophilia Treatment Center (HTC) has not consistently shortened these delays, and geographic factors may influence timely diagnosis and access to care [14, 15].

Nova Scotia, Canada, with a population of approximately one million residents served by a single HTC, provides a unique setting to evaluate AHA across both rural and urban populations. We aimed to quantify diagnostic delays, identify contributing factors, and describe treatment and outcomes in a population‐based cohort of adults with AHA in Nova Scotia.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective, population‐based cohort study of all adults (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with AHA in Nova Scotia, Canada, between January 2006 and December 2024. Cases were identified through the provincial HTC and the reference coagulation laboratory at the QEII Health Sciences Centre, the province's sole tertiary care facility. Patients with congenital hemophilia were excluded. The study was approved by the Nova Scotia Health Research Ethics Board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Nova Scotia has 42 hospitals across four health zones. Most provide routine coagulation assays (aPTT, INR, fibrinogen, and D‐dimer), while specialized testing is centralized at the QEII, where assays are typically batched weekly unless urgent. FVIII activity was measured using one‐stage (Synthasil, Werfen) or chromogenic assays, with chromogenic testing reserved for patients on emicizumab. Inhibitors were quantified with the Nijmegen‐modified Bethesda assay. Reflex testing is not routinely performed for abnormal results, and further investigations depend on anticoagulant history, lupus anticoagulant testing, and medical director input. A diagnosis of AHA required FVIII activity < 50% (or < 0.50 IU/mL) with an inhibitor ≥ 0.5 BU/mL, in line with consensus criteria.

Chart reviews captured demographics, including distance to the treatment center, clinical presentation, treatment, and outcomes. Incidence was calculated using annual Statistics Canada population estimates [16]. Continuous variables were summarized as medians (IQR) or means (SD), and categorical data as frequencies. Patients were stratified into low/standard‐risk (FVIII ≥ 1% and inhibitor < 20 BU/mL) and high‐risk groups (FVIII < 1% and/or inhibitor ≥ 20 BU/mL), per GTH 2020 criteria [6].

Bleeding severity followed ISTH definitions [17] major bleeding included hemoglobin drop ≥ 20 g/L, transfusion ≥ 2 units in 24 h, critical‐site bleeding, or fatal hemorrhage; all other bleeds were classified as mild. Relevant bleeding within 12 months prior to diagnosis was included.

Hemostatic therapy included bypassing agents (rFVIIa, FEIBA), FFP, DDAVP, and antifibrinolytics (e.g., tranexamic acid). IST encompassed corticosteroids, cytotoxic or immunosuppressive agents, chemotherapy, and rituximab. Complete remission was defined as FVIII > 50% with inhibitor < 0.5 BU/mL, absence of bleeding, and no IST or hemostatic therapy for ≥ 24 h. Relapse included refractory disease during steroid taper or recurrence > 1 month post‐remission.

Adverse events were captured during IST or hemostatic therapy, including thrombosis, steroid complications, drug toxicities, infections, and sepsis. Mortality was classified as AHA‐related (bleeding with active inhibitor) or IST‐related (treatment complications). Diagnostic delay was measured from the first bleeding presentation and abnormal aPTT to confirmed AHA diagnosis. Sociodemographic and healthcare utilization data were collected, including whether patients first presented at the HTC or community facilities, and the number and type of specialties involved. Barriers to diagnosis were analyzed.

Results

Demographics

We identified 27 cases of AHA in Nova Scotia from 2006 to 2024. During this period, the provincial population ranged from 938,120 (2006) to 1,079,676 (2024), giving a mean incidence of 1.46 ± 0.91 cases per million per year (Table S1) [16]. The median follow‐up was 108 months (IQR 89–118).

The median age at diagnosis was 74 years (IQR 63.5–80); 52% (N = 14) were female. At diagnosis, 59% (N = 16) were idiopathic. Associated conditions included bullous pemphigoid (N = 1), autoimmune disease (N = 6; 22%), and malignancy (N = 7; 26%). Three patients had both autoimmune disease and malignancy. No pregnancy‐ or postpartum‐related cases were identified (Table 1).

Severe AHA (FVIII < 1% and/or BU > 20) was present in 52% (N = 14). At diagnosis, the median aPTT was 84.6 s (IQR 65.5–105), FVIII 0.02 IU/dL (IQR 0.01–0.1), and hemoglobin 98 g/L (IQR 86–117). In 19% (N = 5) of patients, an elevated PTT was detected before bleeding developed.

Bleeding occurred in 96% (N = 26) of patients, most commonly subcutaneous (93%; N = 25) and intramuscular hematomas (74%; N = 20). Multiple sites of bleeding were reported in 89% (mean 2.74 sites/patient, SD 1.38). Major bleeding occurred in 41% (N = 11). Only one patient was asymptomatic at presentation (Figure 1).

Most patients presented to the emergency department (85%; N = 23), and 74% (N = 20) required hospitalization, including 22% (N = 6) ICU admissions. The median hospital stay was 16 days (IQR 3–20.5). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

Treatment and Outcomes

Immunosuppressive Therapy

All patients received IST (Table 2). The most common regimen was prednisone plus cyclophosphamide (44%; N = 12). One frail patient received MMF with prednisone. Rituximab was used as third‐line therapy in 37% (N = 10). One patient with concurrent non‐Hodgkin lymphoma received chemoimmunotherapy with bendamustine, rituximab, and prednisone and achieved remission. Only 11% (N = 3) were treated with prednisone alone. Among those with severe AHA, 36% required three or more IST agents to achieve remission.

Eleven patients received rituximab‐based therapy. Seven achieved complete remission, including two who subsequently died. Of the five patients who died after rituximab exposure, three died from sepsis during ICU admission. Median time to remission was shorter among patients treated with rituximab compared with non‐rituximab therapy (20 days [IQR 0–78.5] vs. 44.5 days [IQR 9.5–83.5]). Median hospital length of stay was longer among patients requiring rituximab as a third‐line agent (15 days [IQR 11.5–28.5] vs. 11 days [IQR 0–17.3]). Five‐year overall survival was lower in patients receiving rituximab‐based regimens compared with those who did not (36% vs. 63%). A summary of rituximab‐based versus non‐rituximab outcomes is provided in Table S2.

Hemostatic Therapy

Bypassing agents were required in 63% (N = 17) of patients, most commonly rFVIIa (37%; N = 10). In 19% (N = 5) of patients, more than one bypassing agent was given. One patient with a low BU titer (1.66 BU/mL) was successfully treated with high‐dose recombinant FVIII. In the latter half of the study, three refractory cases were treated with porcine recombinant FVIII and emicizumab due to sub‐optimal response to initial therapies. Of the 14 patients with severe disease, 71% required at least one bypassing agent to achieve hemostasis. Two patients received FFP at peripheral hospitals before transfer to central HTC.

Adjunctive therapies were given to 11 patients, most often tranexamic acid, with (N = 6) or without IVIG (N = 3). One ICU patient with methicillin‐sensitive Staphylococcus aureus (MSSA) bacteremia and pneumosepsis underwent therapeutic apheresis (PLEX) for refractory high‐titer disease. Of the 60% (N = 16) of patients requiring transfusions, most died (N = 10). The mean number of packed RBC units per patient was 2.7 (SD ± 4.36). First‐line treatment is summarized in Table 2.

Clinical Outcomes

The median time to remission was 51.5 days (IQR 27–90.5). Overall mortality was 52% (N = 14), with a 5‐year overall survival of 48%. Median time to death was 119 weeks (IQR 14–202). Most deaths (79%) occurred within 2 years of diagnosis, including 22% with high‐risk diseases. Three deaths occurred ≥ 2 years from diagnosis, two from relapsed disease (at 556 and 488 weeks).

The leading cause of death was sepsis (15%, N = 4), largely related to IST toxicity. Other causes included bleeding (7%; N = 2), malignancy (7%; N = 2), cardiac arrest (4%; N = 1), and unknown causes (19%; N = 5) (Table 3).

Relapse and Refractory Disease

Of the four (15%) primary refractory patients during prednisone taper, the mortality rate was 50%. Associated conditions included one patient with Crohn's disease and another with IgA deficiency. Of the severe refractory cases, three were treated with emicizumab; two achieved remission, one died. Four patients relapsed at a median of 16.5 months (IQR 8.6–35.8).

Three relapses coincided with new malignancies (melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, or concurrent bladder and prostate cancer) (Table 3).

Barriers to Diagnosis

Trends in Presentation

Most patients (96%; N = 26) presented to the ED with bleeding, but diagnosis often followed multiple visits. The median number of bleeding‐related encounters before diagnosis was 2 (IQR 1–5). Multiple specialties were involved per patient, most often emergency medicine, urology, and surgical subspecialties (Figure 2).

Medical subspecialties consulted for patients presenting with bleeding prior to diagnosis of acquired hemophilia A in Nova Scotia, Canada.

Diagnostic Delays

The median time from first presentation for bleeding to diagnosis was 32 days (IQR 4.5–75); from first abnormal PTT to diagnosis, 10 days (IQR 6–45.5). Delays > 7 days occurred in 63% (N = 17). Ten patients (37%) were diagnosed within 7 days, three (11%) within 8–30 days, and 14 (52%) after > 30 days. Patients with delayed diagnosis (> 30 days) were younger (mean 67.4 ± 16.1) than those diagnosed within 8–30 days (82 ± 5.2) or ≤ 7 days (70.5 ± 10.1). Among early diagnoses (≤ 7 days), 5‐year survival was 55%, compared with 53% for delayed diagnoses (> 8 days). Severe disease was more common in delayed diagnoses (53%), with most detected > 30 days after first bleeding presentation.

Geographic Trends

Ten patients (37%) lived > 100 km from the HTC. Their 5‐year survival was 60% versus 53% for those living closer. Severe AHA was more frequent in rural patients (70% vs. 12%). The median number of visits before diagnosis was similar (remote: 2; local: 3). Of patients diagnosed > 7 days after bleeding, 30% lived rurally. Number of bleeding visits prior to diagnosis was similar in rural and urban patients (Table S3).

Discussion

AHA is a rare but potentially life‐threatening bleeding disorder, and timely diagnosis remains a persistent challenge, even in settings with centralized specialist care. In our population‐based cohort from Nova Scotia, Canada, diagnostic delays were common, with nearly two‐thirds of patients diagnosed more than 7 days after symptom onset. This underscores that, despite specialized infrastructure, AHA continues to be underrecognized, highlighting an ongoing need for awareness among clinicians and streamlined diagnostic pathways.

In this population‐based retrospective cohort, the annual incidence of acquired AHA in Nova Scotia, Canada, was 1.46 cases per million, consistent with large population‐based studies [2]. A recent Canadian study reported 1.78 per million [3], while German data showed an increase from 2.5 to 5.2 per million by 2018, likely reflecting greater diagnostic tools and awareness [18]. Patient characteristics in our cohort mirrored published data, with a median age of 74 years, equal sex distribution, and idiopathic disease in half of the cases [2]. No pregnancy‐associated AHA was identified. Although this is a rare cause of AHA [3, 4], under‐recognition remains possible, as postpartum cases often present with milder disease or spontaneous remission and are often overlooked due to the normalization of postpartum bleeding.

Most patients required IST, commonly prednisone and cyclophosphamide. Rituximab‐based IST was commonly used and frequently resulted in complete remission. While detailed data on disease severity are limited, rituximab is generally reserved for patients with more challenging or refractory disease. Several deaths occurred from sepsis during ICU admission, emphasizing the infectious risks associated with intensified immunosuppression rather than a direct treatment effect. Three patients whose disease persisted despite rituximab‐based IST were successfully treated with porcine FVIII and emicizumab, illustrating the potential role of newer agents in selected cases, although the optimal sequencing and timing of these therapies remain undefined [13, 19, 20]. There is a lack of prospective randomized evidence, and current practice is guided by expert consensus. Thus, further studies are needed to define the role of emicizumab and other adjunctive therapies [13, 19, 20].

Disease severity in our cohort was substantial: nearly half had severe AHA, 63% required bypassing agents, and transfusion needs were high, particularly among those who died. Overall mortality was 52%, higher than recent series reporting short‐term mortality of 3%–4%, likely reflecting extended follow‐up, inclusion of older patients, and absence of postpartum AHA cases with generally better outcomes [3, 6, 21]. Moreover, despite centralized care through a single HTC, initial presentation often occurred at multiple other centers with variable experience, which may have delayed access to optimal therapy.

Diagnostic delay was a persistent challenge. Most patients presented to emergency departments and required multiple consultations, often involving surgical subspecialties. This study identifies the need to improve continuing education to not only primary providers, but also a variety of subspecialties to potentially improve diagnostic acumen. This is one of the first studies that have identified key stakeholders that can be targeted to educate and alter outcomes for patients presenting with AHA.

The median time from symptom onset to diagnosis exceeded 1 month. Prior studies report a median diagnostic delay of 19 (IQR 2–180) to 30 days, often related to absent or delayed coagulation testing [21, 22]. While an association between delays in diagnosis and poor outcomes has been hypothesized, it has not been confirmed in existing literature. Rutherford et al. demonstrated a trend towards worse 3‐month OS (80% vs. 91%) and 1‐year OS (67% vs. 87%, p = 0.40) among patients diagnosed after 7 days [14]. Similarly, our study also suggests that, despite care and/or presentation at a central HTC, diagnostic delays in AHA persist [14].

In 10% of cases, patients presenting with atypical bleeding did not have coagulation studies performed, while 19% had a prolonged PTT identified before presenting with bleeding but without further investigation or diagnosis. We hypothesize that concomitant anticoagulation use may have led clinicians to attribute bleeding or PTT prolongation to therapy itself, limiting further investigation for alternative causes such as AHA. In our study, delays from abnormal PTT to diagnosis were shorter than delays in ordering the PTT itself, emphasizing that timely coagulation testing is a key modifiable barrier. All patients with unexplained bleeding should undergo a coagulation screen, including PTT. Nova Scotia's centralized laboratory system facilitates case capture and comprehensive analysis but may contribute to batching‐related delays, highlighting a potential target for system‐level improvement. Centralized testing has been shown to contribute to longer delays [21].

Geographic factors appeared to influence disease severity at presentation, with patients living > 100 km from the HTC more likely to present with severe AHA, although survival was similar across rural and urban groups. These findings suggest that access to specialized care and timely testing remains important, particularly for rural populations.

This study has limitations, including the small sample size, which restricts the statistical power of analyses, and the inherent constraints of a retrospective design. Additionally, as Nova Scotia is a geographically large province with multiple healthcare centers that lack a unified electronic medical record system, it is possible that the full scope of individual patient care was not captured. Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable insight into the clinical characteristics of patients with AHA in our region and identifies areas for future research aimed at addressing barriers to care and improving outcomes.

Conclusion

The incidence of AHA in Nova Scotia aligns with population‐based data, but delayed diagnosis remains common, particularly in younger patients and those with severe disease. Improving recognition among emergency and surgical providers, promoting early coagulation testing, and streamlining laboratory pathways are critical to earlier diagnosis. As newer therapies such as emicizumab become more accessible, further research is needed to define their optimal role. This study provides real‐world data on AHA incidence, presentation, treatment, and outcomes, and underscores the importance of addressing diagnostic barriers, including targeted key subspecialty stakeholders for further education. These results add to the limited body of population‐based evidence on AHA and provide a foundation for future quality improvement and research initiatives.
