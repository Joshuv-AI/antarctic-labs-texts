Identifying sex-specific genetic effects across 733 traits in UK biobank
Authors: Wei Jiang, Yueqian Jing, James Han, Yixuan Ye, Hongyu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSex-specificity has been reported in a wide range of diseases and complex traits. While sex-specific genetic effects have been documented for certain traits, the genetic mechanisms underlying sex differences in most traits remain largely unexplored. With its large sample size and wide range of diseases and traits, the UK Biobank—a large, prospective cohort study containing health history, phenotypic measurements, and genetic data for over 500,000 individuals—provides an opportunity to explore sexually dimorphic genetic architectures in a large number of traits and diseases. Here, we present a sex-specific analysis of 733 sex-stratified complex trait GWAS for 361,194 white British individuals in the UK Biobank. Among these 733 traits, we detected sex-specific locus in 520 of them. These 520 candidate traits with sex-specific genetic effects were classified to 270 distinct groups. Using a systematic sex-specific discovery-replication analysis, we identify 489 loci showing sex-specific effects on 127 of the candidate trait groups, among which three of them having the most numbers of traits showing replicable signals were further investigated, including fat mass related traits (12 traits), heart disease related traits (7 traits) and body impedance related traits (6 traits). We identify pathways with sex-biased enrichment patterns from exploratory enrichment analyses based on QTL-associated genes. In addition, we present further evidence for significant sex-specific genetic effects in 25 traits by comparing the prediction performance of sex-specific polygenic risk scores (PRS).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13164-1.Keywords: Sex specific effects, Sexual dimorphism, Sex-stratified GWAS, UK Biobank, Complex traits, Polygenic risk score, Pathway analysis

Introduction

There are significant sex differences for many traits and diseases [1], from cardiovascular diseases, asthma, autoimmune diseases, and mental illnesses to anthropometric traits such as BMI, body fat composition, and blood pressure [2–8]. Sex differences can originate from a wide range of factors, from genetic, hormonal, and other biological factors to environmental and sociological factors [9, 10]. Understanding the mechanisms contributing to sex differences in various diseases and traits can aid in our understanding of the biological origins of diseases. These sex differences also present opportunities for improved therapies, where sex-specific disease etiologies may require different treatment strategies, and are an important factor for equitable medical care [1].

Here we focus on the genetic basis of sex differences for a range of common traits. Previous studies have reported sex-specific genetic effects for selected quantitative traits, anthropometric phenotypes, and complex diseases. These include sex-specific genetic architecture for lipid levels, blood pressure, height, basal metabolic rate, waist-to-hip ratio, and other complex traits, as well as sexually dimorphic loci for waist phenotypes, anthropometric traits, body fat distribution, and waist-to-hip ratio adjusted for BMI [9, 11–13, 17, 28]. Sex-specific risk alleles have also been reported for diseases such as asthma, coronary artery disease, diabetes, and Crohn’s disease [14–16].

While sex-specific genetic differences have been discovered for some traits in these studies, a thorough scan of the specific genetic mechanisms conferring sexual dimorphism is still lacking for the majority of common traits and diseases [1]. Even for the traits that have been studied, most were designed to search for global evidence of sex-specific genetic architectures, without identifying loci showing sex-specific effects (SSE) [13, 17], while others were underpowered in detecting sex-specific genetic loci [9, 15], many of which may only have small effect differences through varying disease etiology and mechanism.

The UK Biobank is a large, prospective cohort study of over 500,000 individuals, with rich data including questionnaire responses, phenotypic measurements, disease and health information, and genetic data [18]. This provides an opportunity to scan a large number of common traits and diseases for potential different genetic architectures between males and females and identify loci having SSE with comparatively higher statistical power. Although there were published studies analyzing selected traits within the UK Biobank, these studies either considered a subset of the samples [9] or used methods that are not specifically designed to detect sexually dimorphic loci [13, 19]. With recent releases of more participant genomic and phenotypic data, as well as comprehensive GWAS analyses of these participants, the UK Biobank presents a promising resource for a more comprehensive investigation of sex-specific genetic effects [20, 21].

In this manuscript, we present a sex-specific analysis of 733 complex traits in the UK Biobank. A total of 520 traits has significant sex-specific loci discovered. These 520 traits belong to 270 distinct groups based on hierarchical clustering using their genetic correlations as similarities and a default threshold of 0.5. Using sex stratified GWAS analysis, we initially discovered 18,884 SSE loci across these traits. To validate these findings, we conduct a replication study using an independent set of individuals within the same genetic population from the UK Biobank, yielding 489 replicated SSE loci. In this paper, we particularly emphasize the findings among three trait groups exhibited the most traits being replicated: fat mass related traits (12 traits), heart disease related traits (7 traits) and body impedance related traits (6 traits). We then investigate possible biological interpretations of the SSE loci through pathway enrichment analysis, and report pathways with sex-biased enrichment patterns in the three trait groups. We further present evidence for significant sex-specific genetic effects in 25 traits through polygenic risk prediction. Using two different polygenic risk prediction methods, namely P + T (pruning and thresholdin) [22] and PRS-CS (polygenic risk scores with continuous shrinkage priors) [23], we report significant differences in risk prediction between models trained on male- and female-specific training sets for all the 733 traits. In summary, the objectives of this study are: (i) systematically identify and replicate genetic loci with sex-specific effects across 733 non-redundant complex traits in the UK Biobank using a uniform discovery–replication framework; (ii) investigate the biological pathways implicated by these loci through QTL-based pathway enrichment analysis; and (iii) evaluate the predictive utility of accounting for sex through sex-specific polygenic risk scores. To our knowledge, this is the first analysis to combine systematic replication and trait-wide coverage on this scale, addressing a gap left by previous studies that examined selected traits or tested for sex-specificity only after conditioning on sex-combined significance.

Results

An overview of our analysis procedure, as well as our results, can be found in Fig. 1. Due to the large number of phenotypes presented in the UK Biobank, as well as the presence of redundant phenotypes, we firstly chose 733 traits belonging to 7 categories, including lifestyles, clinical measurements, health and medical status, cognitive functions, biomarkers, and diagnosed diseases. Among them, we conducted primary sex-stratified GWAS analyses and identified candidate sex-specific loci. For these traits with significant sex-specific loci, we cluster them into different groups based on their genetic correlations. Then we used a replication set to validate the findings, and conducted a literature review, QTL analysis, and pathway analysis based on the replicated loci. Finally, we used the discovery and replication sets to compare sex-specific and sex-agnostic PRS models to demonstrate further evidence for sex-specific genetic effects. The methodology details can be found in Methods Section. We note that all analyses reported here are limited to the autosomes; the X and Y chromosomes were not included (see Discussion). Reported effect-size differences between the sexes are generally modest, and the loci described below should be interpreted as candidate sex-specific regions rather than as clinically actionable variants.

Flowchart of analysis procedure. The workflow for our sex-specific analysis. Sex-stratified GWAS summary statistics in an initial discovery set for 733 traits were tested for sex-specific effect differences, and sex-specific loci were selected from 25 traits to identify 843 candidate loci. A replication set (N = 65,464) was used to replicate 54 sex-specific loci, and a literature review, QTL analysis, and pathway analysis were conducted using the replicated loci. Finally, the discovery and replication sets were used to compare sex-specific and sex-agnostic PRS models

Sex-specific loci

Of the 733 traits analyzed, 520 showed evidence of sex-specific SNPs with FDR controlled at 0.05. These 520 traits were then aggregated into 270 trait groups. We performed sex-specific GWAS analysis on each trait group. The largest replicated group comprised 12 fat mass-related traits (FM), including regional and whole-body fat percentages and masses as well as waist circumference (Table 1). These traits are highly correlated with body fat percentage, a more direct measure of adiposity that has been the focus of prior large-scale GWAS (Lu et al. 56). We highlight this group, along with body impedance (BI) and heart disease (HD) traits, because these three groups contained the largest numbers of traits with replicable sex-specific loci. Results for other trait groups can be found in supplementary materials.

Initial clumping within trait groups yielded 708 significant loci for FM traits, 1489 for BI traits and 90 for HD traits (Fig. 3). In the primary analysis, we tested all candidate SSE with p-value smaller than 5 × 10⁻⁸ in only one sex, identified 843 loci showing sex-specific effects with FDR controlled at 10%. Of these, 337 were found in at least one FM trait, 427 in at least one BI trait, and 79 in at least one HD trait. These initial SSE loci can be visualized in Fig. 3, where peaks in only males or females correspond to initial SSE loci. After the replication analysis, a total of 489 replicated loci were identified across all 127 candidate trait groups at a loosely controlled FDR of 30%. Among the three focal trait groups, 54 SSE loci were identified: 31 loci for FM traits, 16 loci for BI traits, and 7 loci for HD traits. These loci are denoted in Fig. 3 with their corresponding gene names and associated QTL genes.

Sex-specific loci were identified through a two-stage procedure, where candidate sex-specific loci in the discovery set were tested for sex-specificity in the replication set. After replication, 54 loci were identified as sex-specific, of which 42 were not previously reported to be sex-specific in the traits studied. All identified sex-specific loci are provided in the table below, with columns denoting the locus chromosome, chromosomal band, rsID, Sex* classification, male and female p-values, male and female beta estimates and their standard errors, nearby genes identified using SNP-Nexus, significantly associated eQTL genes, traits in which the locus was identified, and the study identifying sex-specific effects in the trait group, if applicable. Reported statistics (β, SE, p-value) are from the meta-analysis of discovery (N = 361,194) and replication cohorts (N = 65,464), corresponding to the trait with the minimum p-value in one of the sexes. Of the 31 SSE loci identified in the FM group, several overlapped with loci previously reported for body fat percentage in a meta-analysis of > 100,000 individuals (Lu et al. [56]) or for body fat distribution (Rask-Andersen et al [13]). Ten had prior evidence of sex-specific effects, whereas the remaining 21 were known adiposity-related loci but had not previously been highlighted as sex-specific. Among the 16 BI loci, two had prior evidence of sex-specific effects in fat distribution, and the remaining loci were previously associated with fat distribution, anthropometric traits, or hematological traits, consistent with the close relationship between body impedance and body composition. All seven HD loci were novel with respect to sex-specificity but mapped to regions previously associated with cardiovascular traits, including coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, and lipid levels. Double Manhattan plots for FM, BI, and HD traits with annotated SSE loci are shown in Fig. 3, and all SSE loci, neighboring genes, and classifications of other significant loci are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Pathway analysis

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed to provide exploratory biological context for the replicated loci in the FM, BI, and HD trait groups. Across the QTL-based gene sets, we observed 2413 pathways enriched in at least one gene set with p < 0.05, including 659 pathways with sex-biased enrichment patterns. Of these 659 pathways, 514 were enriched in the female-specific gene sets and 145 were enriched in the male-specific gene sets. Male-enriched pathways were related to immune and inflammatory response (GO:0042608, GO:0005164, GO:0036037, among others), cellular transport (GO:0015606, GO:0015203, GO:0008504, among others), and cell signaling and regulation (GO:0050678, GO:0050858, GO:0090257, among others). Female-enriched pathways were related to immune and inflammatory response (GO:0004896, GO:0045619, GO:0045580), cell signaling and regulation (GO:0038023, GO:0060089, GO:0007167, among others), and cellular process and development (GO:0010001, GO:0098636, GO:2000147). These enrichment patterns suggest that sex-specific genetic effects may influence fat-mass, body-impedance, and heart-disease-related traits through pathways involved in immune regulation, inflammatory response, cellular signaling, and tissue-level developmental or transport processes. For fat-mass and body-impedance traits, these pathways may reflect sex-biased regulation of adipose tissue distribution, body composition, and related metabolic processes. For heart-disease-related traits, enrichment of immune and inflammatory pathways is consistent with the known contribution of inflammation and cellular signaling to cardiometabolic risk. Although the pathway analysis does not establish causality, it provides biological context for the replicated sex-specific loci and suggests that sex differences in these traits may arise from coordinated differences in regulatory and inflammatory mechanisms.

Polygenic risk prediction

We compared the predictive accuracy of male-trained, female-trained, combined sex-specific, and sex-agnostic PRSs (P + T and PRS-CS) on the replication cohort (Table 3; Fig. 5). Consistent with expectations from simulation studies under modest inter-sex genetic correlation (Zhang et al. [71]), sex-specific and combined PRSs outperformed the sex-agnostic models for the majority of fat-mass-related and body-impedance-related traits, especially when evaluated in females (17 of 18 traits at p < 0.05 for P + T; all 18 traits for PRS-CS). Improvement was less consistent for the seven heart-disease traits, where sex-agnostic models performed comparably or better. Overall, these results align with the replicated locus-level findings and indicate that accounting for sex can enhance risk prediction for anthropometric and impedance traits in this cohort.

Discussion

In this paper, we have presented a systematic investigation of sex-specific genetic effects in 733 traits in the UK Biobank. We reported 25 traits in three genetically related trait groups with significant sex-specific loci and identified 54 (42 novel) sex-specific effects (SSE) loci. Through exploratory pathway enrichment analysis, we identified pathways with sex-biased enrichment patterns. Finally, through polygenic risk score analysis, we showed further evidence for sex-specific effects in 19 of the 25 traits analyzed.

All loci identified in the FM group have prior associations with fat-related traits in the literature, consistent with the meta-analysis of Lu et al. [56] and subsequent work on body fat distribution. Many of these signals were first reported in diverse populations and meta-analyses [27, 28, 30, 32–40], yet our sex-stratified approach in the UK Biobank revealed additional sex-specific patterns not emphasized in those studies. Similarly, all novel sex-specific BI loci were also shown to be associated with fat distribution, anthropometric traits and hematological traits [13, 17, 28, 31, 41–48]. Previous studies identifying these associations, as with fat-mass-related traits, were diverse in study designs and populations. These studies include GWASs, meta-analyses, whole genome sequencing and fine-mapping studies in European, East Asian and trans-ethnic populations, identifying associations between the loci and anthropometric traits as well as hematological traits in adults and children. As with the novel fat-mass-related trait loci, these loci were not shown to have sexually dimorphic effects in body-impedance-related traits in any previous analyses. Novel SSE loci for HD traits were also shown to be associated with heart-disease related traits without discovering their sex specificity [49–55, 61]. Those early studies include multi-ancestry GWAS and meta-analyses in European, South Asian ancestry and trans-ethnic populations. Notice that many of the novel loci have actually been studied for their sex specific effects, but most of them weren’t discovered due to the lack of power, which will be discussed below.

Our methods build upon previous studies reporting sex-specific genetic effects in two main ways. First, by using almost all genetically European individuals available within the UK Biobank in either the discovery or replication study, we significantly increased the sample size of our analysis, relative to many previous studies investigating sex-specific effects. Second, we presented a systematic and comprehensive study of sex-specific genetic loci, making use of both the discovery and replication sets to discover and further validate them. Many previous studies that reported sex-specific loci investigated sex-specificity after the identification of loci significantly associated with a trait overall [13, 28, 31]. While such designs can be informative, particularly for reinforcing strong sex-agnostic signals, they are under-powered for detecting one-sex effects (where the combined estimate is diluted) and especially for opposite-direction effects (where estimates cancel). For example, the locus rs13322435, an SSE locus significantly associated in females for FM, does not reach genome-wide significance in non-stratified GWAS for LAFP and RAFP, with p-values of 0.00109 and 0.00114, respectively. However, the locus surpasses genome-wide significance, with a p-value of 9.24e-10 and 4.77e-10 in female-specific GWAS. Loci such as these would not be identified in an approach investigating sex-specificity after assessing overall significance. Our genome-wide sex-difference testing strategy therefore provides greater sensitivity to the divergent genetic architectures that motivate this study. Other studies lacked rigorous replication procedures for sex-specific analyses, relying only on sex-specificity in the primary analysis [17]. Whether or not the identified sex-specificity can be replicated in different studies becomes unknown.

In their work, Rask-Andersen et al. only tested for sex-specificity in the replication cohort, and focused on overall significant loci in the multiple testing correction procedure [13]. In analyzing the role of sex bias in anorexia nervosa, Hubel et al. performed sex-specific GWASs and compared differences in significance but did not include replication methods [29]. Lu et al. performed sex-stratified GWASs for both all-ancestry and European ancestry groups on a comparatively smaller dataset, with the total number of participants at 100,716 individuals [56]. In addition, they also did not include a replication stage for identified sex-specific loci. Randall et al. also used a z-score in the same form with zβ to identify loci with single sex effects for anthropometric traits using summary statistics released from the GIANT consortium, but they included the sample covariance between male and female marginal effects in calculating the standard error of the marginal effect difference. Since the sex-specific marginal effects are computed only based on either male or female individuals and there are no overlaps between these two groups, the actual covariance of sex-specific marginal effects should be zero. The inclusion of sample covariance term decreased the standard error of the effect difference and inflated the test statistic [12]. Shungin et al., in searching for adipose-associated genetic loci, tested the differences in male and female effect sizes for identified significant loci in a single meta-analysis cohort, again accounting only for the significant loci in the multiple testing procedure [31]. Pulit et al. used the same sex-specific effect test with our methods, but did not include a replication procedure for the sexually dimorphic results [17]. Other studies reporting sex-specific effects in fat distributions also suffered from the previously described shortcomings—either low sample size, lack of systematic sex-specificity analysis, or both [28, 57, 58]. While prior large-scale efforts such as Pulit et al. have leveraged much larger sample sizes and provided seminal insights into sex-differential anthropometric traits, our contribution is complementary: by conducting a systematic and uniform scan across a broad set of non-redundant UK Biobank traits, we provide a trait-wide catalog of sex-difference loci, highlighting both consistent and trait-specific patterns of sex-differential genetic effects.

In addition to the 54 replicated sex-specific loci, polygenic risk score analyses offered further, albeit modest, support for sexually dimorphic genetic effects. For fat-mass and body-impedance traits, sex-specific and combined PRSs improved prediction accuracy relative to sex-agnostic models on the replication set, particularly in females (Table 3; Fig. 5). These findings are consistent with our prior benchmark showing that combined sex-specific PRSs are advantageous when inter-sex genetic correlations are modest and sample sizes are adequate (Zhang et al. [71]). No systematic advantage was observed for heart-disease traits, highlighting that the benefit of sex-stratified modeling is trait-dependent. These findings suggest that incorporating sex-specific genetic effects may improve risk stratification for selected traits, particularly adiposity- and body-composition-related phenotypes where sex-specific PRS models showed stronger performance. Such improvements could help identify individuals who are genetically predisposed to unfavorable fat distribution patterns, increased adiposity, or related cardiometabolic complications in a sex-specific manner. For example, sex-informed genetic risk models may contribute to earlier identification of women or men who are at elevated risk for obesity-related metabolic disorders, thereby supporting more targeted screening, lifestyle interventions, and preventive strategies [70]. Our results indicate that sex should be considered not only as a demographic covariate but also as a biological factor that can modify the effects of genetic variants on complex traits.

The pathway results also provide biological context for the replicated sex-specific loci. The enrichment of immune and inflammatory pathways suggests that sex-specific genetic effects may act partly through differences in inflammatory regulation, which is relevant to both adiposity-related traits and cardiovascular disease risk. Enrichment of cell signaling and cellular transport pathways further suggests that sex-biased genetic regulation may influence adipose tissue remodeling, body composition, and impedance-related phenotypes, which are closely related to fat and lean mass distribution. Together, these results suggest that the observed sex-specific loci may converge on biologically meaningful processes involved in adiposity, body composition, and cardiometabolic disease.

Despite the strengths of our framework, several limitations exist. First, all analyses were restricted to white British/European-ancestry participants in UK Biobank to reduce confounding from population stratification, and therefore the generalizability of these findings to other ancestral populations remains limited. Secondly, the observed improvements in predictive performance were relatively modest, and the effect-size differences between sexes were generally small. Therefore, these findings should be viewed primarily as evidence supporting the biological relevance of sex-specific genetic architecture rather than as a basis for immediate clinical application. Additional validation in large, independent, and ancestrally diverse cohorts will be necessary to determine whether incorporating sex-specific genetic effects can meaningfully improve clinically actionable risk prediction, patient stratification, or personalized prevention and treatment strategies. Moreover, we excluded the X chromosome in our analysis due to imputation limitations, QC challenges, and the need for specialized sex-aware statistical models. Pathway enrichment results should also be interpreted cautiously. The QTL-based gene sets were not independently validated and pathway databases contain overlapping and hierarchically related terms, which can inflate the apparent number of enriched pathways and contribute to redundancy among enriched categories. Therefore, the reported enrichments should be viewed as broad biological themes rather than confirmed causal pathways. Future studies incorporating independent cohorts, sex-stratified molecular QTL datasets will be needed to determine whether these pathways mediate sex-specific effects.

Additionally, discovery power is limited in SNP-level association analysis, due to the absence of fine-mapping frameworks for identifying SNPs with sex-specific causal effects. Since we are conducting sex-stratified analyses for all traits without pre-screening by heritability difference test, a stringent threshold is adopted for sex-stratified analyses in order to overcome the multiple comparison problem, which may degrade the overall power. While our clumping-based approach provides a practical and transparent strategy for large-scale locus definition, we note that closely linked variants may not represent fully independent signals, and future work applying fine-mapping frameworks could refine the resolution of sex-specific loci. Two specific caveats follow from this clumping-based design. First, the lead SNPs reported here were selected as the most significant variant within each clump and are not guaranteed to be causal; the true causal variant(s) may lie elsewhere within the same linkage-disequilibrium block. Second, because clumping was performed per trait group on aggregated summary statistics, the same underlying signal can be tagged by slightly different lead SNPs across genetically correlated traits, which may inflate the apparent count of independent loci. Accordingly, the locus counts reported throughout this paper should be read as operational, clumping-based regions rather than as fully resolved independent signals, and statistical fine-mapping or conditional/joint analysis will be required to delineate credible causal variants and confirm independence—an important direction for future work.

At last, our SNP-level analysis also does not take advantage of all information available for analysis, such as linkage-disequilibrium information and annotation data. The presence of loci with significant sex-specific differences in the primary analysis that were unable to be replicated, as well as unidentified loci in the Double Manhattan Plots shown in Fig. 2 and Supplementary materials that seem to be significant in one sex but not the other, suggest that our methods may still lack the sensitivity to identify loci with sex-specific effects which may require more powerful statistical frameworks. Future methods may be developed and implemented to uncover both novel sex-specific loci in traits with known sex-specific genetic effects and novel traits with sex-specific genetic effects.

Future work should extend this analysis in several directions. First, fine-mapping approaches that incorporate linkage disequilibrium structure, functional annotations, and sex-stratified effect estimates will be helpful to refine candidate loci and prioritize putative causal variants or genes. Second, analyses in more diverse ancestral populations are essential to evaluate the generalizability of these sex-specific genetic effects and to identify ancestry-specific patterns that may not be detectable in the current UK Biobank sample. Third, studies incorporating the X chromosome and other sex chromosomes using dedicated sex-aware pipelines will be helpful. Together, these extensions will provide a more complete understanding of the genetic architecture underlying sex differences in complex traits. Finally, future studies should develop and implement sex-specific PRS methods that integrate shared and sex-specific genetic effects, incorporate sex chromosomes, and assess performance across diverse ancestral populations.

Methods

Study cohort

The UK Biobank (UKBB) represents a comprehensive, population-based prospective cohort comprising approximately 500,000 UK residents aged 40–69 years at recruitment (2006–2010), offering an invaluable resource for genome-wide association studies (GWAS) aimed at uncovering sex-specific genetic loci underlying complex traits and diseases. Participants provided extensive baseline data through questionnaires on lifestyle, medical history, environmental exposures, as well as physical assessments (e.g., anthropometry, blood pressure), biospecimen collection (blood, urine, saliva) for biomarker profiling, and high-throughput genotyping of around 800,000 variants, with imputation to over 90 million variants. Full quality control process can be found in following sections.

Sex-stratified GWAS analysis

We selected phenotypes directly provided by UK Biobank and included in the Neale Lab GWAS framework. We restricted analyses to non-redundant, directly measured traits that fall into seven categories: including lifestyles, clinical measurements, health and medical status, cognitive functions, biomarkers, and diagnosed diseases. Derived traits such as waist-to-hip ratio were excluded to maintain a consistent analysis framework based on directly measured UK Biobank phenotypes. Because derived traits combine multiple correlated measurements, their inclusion could complicate interpretation of sex-specific effects across the broad phenotype scan. Instead, we analyzed directly measured component traits, including waist circumference and DXA-based measures of regional and whole-body fat distribution, which capture related biological dimensions of adiposity. However, this design choice may limit direct comparability with prior studies that specifically analyzed derived anthropometric indices such as waist-to-hip ratio. All 733 traits analyzed and their corresponding field number can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

In the discovery stage, we used the GWAS summary statistics proposed by Neale et al. to identify primary sex-specific loci [20]. The GWAS by Neale et al. were performed on a subset of UK Biobank data composed of white British individuals, who were selected through both a genetic criterion and their responses to self-reported ancestry. In the genetic criterion, the means and standard deviations for the first 6 principal components from genotype data in a curated set of white British individuals were calculated, and individuals within 7 standard deviations of these principal components were initially selected for analysis. Principal components (PCs) provided by UK Biobank for the full European cohort were used to account for population structure; we did not recompute PCs within the analysis subset, as the UKBB-derived PCs capture the major axes of genetic variation with higher stability and ensure consistency across discovery and replication datasets. The individuals were further selected according to their responses to ancestry (UK Biobank Data-Field 21000), where only individuals who responded with “British”, “Irish”, or “White” ethnic background were selected. This restriction was applied to minimize confounding due to population stratification. Consequently, the findings may have limited generalizability to individuals from other ancestral backgrounds. Finally, related individuals were removed from the analysis, yielding 361,194 unrelated individuals (194,174 females and 167,020 males). To be more specific, only unrelated individuals were included in both discovery and replication cohorts, defined as removal of all pairs up to third-degree relatives based on kinship coefficients provided by UK Biobank. We considered variants following Neale et al. with a relatively loose quality control procedure, selecting the variants with the imputation information score at > 0.8, minor allele frequency exceeding 0.001, and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium (HWE) p-values > 1e-10 following the UKBB GWAS pipeline. HWE p-values for all reported sex-specific loci are provided in Supplementary Table 2. After these quality control procedures, 13.7 million SNPs remained in the analysis. All genetic analyses in this study were restricted to autosomal variants. Variants on the sex chromosomes, including the X chromosome, were not included because their analysis requires additional sex-aware quality control, genotype coding, imputation assessment, and association models that account for differences in chromosome copy number between males and females. We therefore focused the present genome-wide discovery and replication analyses on autosomes only. As a result, any sex-specific genetic effects located on the X or Y chromosomes were outside the scope of this study.

The 733 traits were first tested for sex-specific effect using summary statistics. Sex-stratified GWAS analyses were performed separately in males and females for continuous and binary traits. Continuous traits were transformed prior to analysis to stabilize variance and approximate normality. Discovery-stage covariates included age, age², and principal components to control for population structure. Sex-agnostic GWAS conducted for comparison and PRS construction additionally included sex and age-by-sex interaction terms. Detailed software versions, covariates, and implementation settings are provided in Supplementary Methods.

SSE loci identification

To filter out SNPs without significant effect size differences between both sexes, the following statistic

was calculated for each SNP, in which βfemale and βmale were effect size estimates for each sex of the SNP, and se(βfemale) and se(βmale) were the standard errors of the effect size estimates. Sex-specificity was tested using a two-tailed z-test with the FDR controlled at 5%. Unlike approaches that first screen for genome-wide significant loci in sex-combined analyses and then test for heterogeneity, we applied the sex-difference test genome-wide. Screening on overall genome-wide significant loci can be under-powered even for unidirectional effects (present in one sex but not the other), as the combined estimate is diluted, and it is particularly under-powered for opposite-direction effects where estimates cancel across sexes. Running the sex-difference test genome-wide ensures uniform multiple-testing control, avoids selection bias, and provides greater sensitivity to both one-sex and opposite-direction effects.

If a trait had SNPs that passed the threshold, it was considered a candidate trait. A total of 520 candidate traits were identified. Each candidate trait was then analyzed at the SNP level for potential sex-specific associations. The candidate traits were clustered into groups based on their genetic correlations. To obtain unified SNP clumping results for each trait group, the summary statistics for each trait within the group were aggregated into a single set of summary statistics by taking the minimum p-value across all traits and both sexes within the group. These aggregated summary statistics were then LD-clumped to define candidate genomic regions. The LD structure was calculated from European-ancestry individuals in the 1000 Genomes project [60]. Detailed software settings, LD reference panel, and clumping parameters are provided in Supplementary Methods. Loci with genome-wide significance in only one sex were considered candidate SSE loci.

The same test statistic was calculated again for each SNP of the candidate SSE locus, Sex-specificity was tested using a two-tailed z-test with the FDR controlled at 10% in order not to miss SNPs that may be sex-specific in only a subset of traits within the group. Candidate SSE loci that had significant sex-specificity were then designated SSE loci. The reported ‘independent loci’ are clumping-based operational sex-specific regions and should not be interpreted as fine-mapped causal variants or guarantees of complete statistical independence. We did not perform fine-mapping at this screening stage because sex stratification reduces effective sample size and power, which widens credible sets and lowers per-variant posterior probabilities, limiting resolution across 733 traits at scale.

We used a hierarchical thresholding strategy because the analysis involved two related but distinct questions: whether a variant showed evidence of association with a trait in at least one sex, and whether the estimated effect differed between males and females. The genome-wide significance threshold of 5 × 10⁻⁸ was used to identify candidate associated loci from the sex-stratified GWAS results. This conventional GWAS threshold was intended to ensure that candidate loci had robust evidence of association in at least one sex before being evaluated for sex-specificity. In contrast, sex-specificity was assessed using the male–female effect-size difference z-test, with FDR control applied to account for multiple sex-difference tests.

Replication analysis

Implicated traits and loci were then tested on a replication dataset, which was constructed to be independent at the individual-sample level while retaining a similar genetic background as the discovery dataset. In accordance with the criterion for individuals chosen by Neale et al. in the discovery set, individuals within 7 standard deviations of the means in self-identified British individuals of the first 6 principal components were considered genetically suitable for the replication set. To construct an independent replication set, all individuals used by Neale et al. were removed. Additional sample- and variant-level quality control was then applied as described in Supplementary Methods. After these quality control measures, 65,464 individuals (35,802 females, 29,662 males) and 10,556,444 variants were included in the replication dataset. We note, however, that this design constitutes an internal replication within UK Biobank rather than replication in an external cohort. Therefore, shared cohort-level factors, including genotyping and imputation pipelines, recruitment characteristics, and potential residual batch effects, may still be present. Future studies in independent cohorts and diverse ancestral populations will be needed to confirm the generalizability of these sex-specific loci. European ancestry was defined based on genetic principal components rather than self-reported ethnicity to ensure consistency across datasets. To confirm the suitability of the replication set, the discovery and replication sets were compared along the first two principal components (Fig. 3).

Sex-stratified GWAS were then performed for each implicated sex-specific trait in the replication dataset, with age, age², and the first 10 principal components as covariates. Continuous traits were transformed with an inverse rank normal transformation before regression analysis [59], and individuals who reported taking blood pressure medications in Data-Field 6153 were removed from GWASs for blood pressure traits. GWASs were performed with a linear regression model for both continuous and binary traits, following the Neale Lab framework.

To confirm the sex-specificity of the discovered SSE loci, each locus was first tested for association within each trait group in the replication dataset, selecting loci with p-value smaller than 5 × 10⁻5 in either sex. The replicated loci were then classified to be SSE in a similar manner as in the primary study, with one exception about the direction of the sex-difference. The two-tailed z test used for determining sex-difference in the primary study was not suitable in the replication study, as the direction of sex-specific effect was also being tested. Thus, a modified version of the one-tailed z test, as described by Jiang et al., was performed in favor of the two-tailed test used in the discovery analysis, with looser FDR threshold set at 30% [25] to maintain power in the smaller dataset. Only the loci that were identified as SSE in both the primary and replication dataset were considered as the final SSE loci. The more permissive FDR threshold in replication was chosen to balance reduced sample size and statistical power under sex-stratified analysis, while still requiring directionally consistent effects across datasets to mitigate false positives.

In the discovery stage, an FDR threshold of 10% was used for candidate SSE loci after genome-wide screening and clumping. This threshold was intended to retain loci with potential sex-specific effects that may occur in only a subset of traits within a genetically correlated trait group, while still controlling the expected proportion of false discoveries among candidate loci. In the replication stage, we used a looser FDR threshold of 30% because the replication sample was substantially smaller than the discovery sample and was further divided into male and female subsets, reducing power for detecting sex-difference effects.

Analysis of sex-specific loci

All identified loci were first examined in the primary literature for published associations. Literature review was performed using the Data Integrator tool from the UCSC genome browser [62] to query the GWAS Catalog at specific genomic regions. Previously reported associations in related traits were queried around each identified SSE locus. Related traits for each trait group included adiposity, anthropometric traits, body fat mass/percentage and distribution, leg fat mass, trunk fat mass, and obesity for fat traits, and hypertension, diastolic blood pressure, and systolic blood pressure for blood pressure traits. Of particular interest were previously identified sex-specific loci for body fat-related traits by Rask-Andersen et al. [13], Hubel et al. [29], Lu et al. [56] , Randall et al. [12] , and Winkler et al. [28] . Additional query windows and implementation parameters are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Genes neighboring all replicated loci were identified using SNP-Nexus [63]. Gene names reported in association tables correspond to the nearest annotated gene to the lead variant and are used solely as positional labels; they do not imply that the annotated gene is necessarily causal for the association. All SSE loci were also analyzed for known QTL associations using QTLbase [64]. Significant QTL genes were used to generate five gene sets for pathway analysis: (1) a sex-agnostic set of all neighboring genes; (2–3) male- and female-specific sets, each combining QTL genes from non-SSE loci with the male or female SSE genes; and (4–5) male- and female-specific sets containing only genes linked to the SSE loci in QTLbase. Pathway analysis was performed using g: Profiler [65] with Gene Ontology (GO) [66, 67], Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) [68], and Reactome [69] gene sets. Detailed gene-window definitions, QTL significance thresholds, and pathway-correction settings are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Polygenic risk prediction

To obtain orthogonal evidence for sex-specific genetic effects beyond the replicated loci, we constructed polygenic risk scores for the 25 traits that showed replicable sex-specific signals. We followed the benchmark framework and implementation details previously established by Zhang et al. [71] Briefly, two summary-statistic-based PRS methods—pruning and thresholding (P + T) and PRS-CS—were applied to (i) sex-stratified summary statistics (male-only and female-only) and (ii) sex-agnostic summary statistics from the discovery set (N = 361,194). We also constructed optimal linear combinations of male- and female-specific scores as described in Zhang et al.71 All PRSs were evaluated on the independent replication set (N = 65,464) separately in males and females. Predictive performance was quantified by R², and differences between sex-specific/combined models versus the sex-agnostic model were tested with two-tailed z-tests. Detailed P + T thresholds, LD settings, PRS-CS settings, and significance thresholds are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Conclusion

We have described a thorough screening of 733 complex traits and diseases in the UK Biobank, and presented a systematic method for the analysis of sex-specific loci in 25 complex traits identified to have possible sex-specific effects. We reported 42 novel sex-specific effects loci in fat-mass related traits, body-impedance-related traits and heart-disease related traits, and identified pathways with sex-biased enrichment patterns using exploratory pathway analysis. Finally, we demonstrated further evidence for sex-specific genetic effects in these traits through sex-specific polygenic risk score analysis.

The workflow for our sex-specific analysis. Sex-stratified GWAS summary statistics in an initial discovery set for 733 traits were tested for sex-specific effect differences, and sex-specific loci were selected from 25 traits to identify 843 candidate loci. A replication set (N = 65,464) was used to replicate 54 sex-specific loci, and a literature review, QTL analysis, and pathway analysis were conducted using the replicated loci. Finally, the discovery and replication sets were used to compare sex-specific and sex-agnostic PRS models.
