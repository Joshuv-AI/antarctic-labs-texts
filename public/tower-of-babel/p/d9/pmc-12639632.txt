A comprehensive study on the effects of drying methods on the color, structure, flavor, and nutritional properties of green peppers (Piper nigrum L.)
Authors: Xinyi Wang, Hui Su, Ruiyang Gao, Hongchen Li, Ying Lyu, Haiming Chen, Qiuping Zhong, Rongrong He, Congfa Li, Wenxue Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Optimal drying method is important for the quality preservation of green pepper (GP). This study assessed the effects of five drying methods on the color, structure, flavor, and nutritional properties of dried GP. Results indicated that freeze drying (FD) preserved excellent color (L⁎ = 56.27, a⁎ = −3.76) and microstructure through low-temperature sublimation, while thermal drying (hot air drying (HAD) and far-infrared drying (FIRD)) enhanced the aroma release via melanoidin and lipid oxidation reactions. Notably, FD-FIRD significantly reduced the energy consumption and drying time by 50.98 % and 55.6 %, respectively, compared to FD alone. This combined drying method also maintained a high level of piperine (6.75 mg/g) and flavonoids retention ratio (370.53 %), as well as preserving good antioxidant activity. Notably, the 25 dominant VOCs exhibited inverse correlations with piperine and phenols (r = −0.54 to −0.92). In short, FD-FIRD is a promising dehydration process.

Introduction

Green pepper (Piper nigrum L., GP), a woody perennial evergreen climber, is not only one of the oldest known spices but also holds a significant position in the world of flavoring and is widely used in various cuisines. Flowers, roots, fruits, and leaves are the various components of GP (Wulandari et al., 2021). The fruit is the most widely used of them, mostly due to its flavor and fragrance (Megat Ahmad Azman et al., 2020), but it also has significant nutritional benefits. Furthermore, GP is well known for its biological qualities, which include anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, hypoglycemic, anti-cancer, anticonvulsant, and antioxidant effects (Haq et al., 2021). In order to gain a substantial portion of the worldwide seasoning market, GP is thus processed into a variety of agricultural goods, such as white pepper, black pepper, and green pepper. Meanwhile, GP-based products are becoming more and more well-liked.

However, GP is mainly grown in warm and humid environments, which results in a high moisture content and water activity. Because of these factors, GP is vulnerable to mold growth, odor, and deterioration when being stored (Dhulipalla et al., 2023). Thankfully, GP may be processed in a number of methods, such as pickling, dehydration, powdering, oil production, and sauce creation (Balasubramanian et al., 2016). Dehydration is the most straightforward and widely applied deep processing approach for increasing the shelf life of GP. Hot air drying (HAD), infrared drying (IFD), and freeze drying (FD) are generally used techniques for drying GPs. Because of its affordability and convenience of use, HAD is extensively used in the food sector. However, the foods may lose important nutrients as a result of their protracted drying process (Ren et al., 2021). IRD utilizes the penetrating properties of infrared rays to directly heat the internal material, significantly reducing drying time and nutrient loss. However, this technology requires a higher initial investment, and uneven heating can be an issue when processing certain materials (Li et al., 2023). Conversely, FD is carried out at low temperatures, which helps to maintain the food materials' nutritional value and organizational structure. It therefore has a lot of potential for use in biomedical and luxury food applications. However, its main disadvantages are that it requires a lot of energy and takes a long time to dry, which raises the cost of manufacture and restricts its use (Liu, Zhang, & Hu, 2022). As a result, it appears that a single drying technique is no longer adequate to satisfy the food industry's contemporary demands for high efficiency, environmental protection, and variety. Researchers have investigated the impact of mixing thermal and non-thermal drying techniques on the overall quality of food items in order to address these industrial concerns (Calín-Sánchez et al., 2020). According to Hou et al. (2021), the combined hot air-freeze drying method effectively preserved the volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and gave shiitake mushrooms new flavor attributes, thereby surpassing the drawbacks of single drying. In addition, Li et al. (2024) discovered that the microwave-freeze drying method for fermented cabbage not only produced outstanding organoleptic quality and high rehydration capacity, but also greatly shortened the drying cycle and used less energy. However, previous research has primarily concentrated on individual drying methods, with limited investigation into the specific impacts of FD-HAD and FD-FIRD technologies on GPs.

Thus, the primary goal of this study was to compare how the organoleptic and nutritional qualities of GPs—such as color, texture, flavor, phenolic contents, and antioxidant activity—were affected by single drying techniques (HAD, FIRD, and FD) and combined drying techniques (combined freeze-hot air drying (FD-HAD) and combined freeze-far infrared drying (FD-FIRD)). The relationships among these indices were also investigated. Moreover, the optimal drying mode mechanism of GP was analyzed. This study can provide a theoretical basis for the development of dehydrated GP products.

Materials and methods

Materials

In Dapo Town, Haikou, China, fresh and uniform GP berries (diameter = 4 mm) were picked. They had an initial moisture content of 66 % ± 0.57 (w/w) on average. The berries were carefully cleaned after the stems were physically removed. Prior to the trials, the GP samples were kept in a refrigerator (DW-86L578J, Haier, Qingdao, China) at 4 °C for no more than 24 h.

Chemicals and reagents

Methanol and ethanol were of high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) grade (purity ≥99 %), supplied by the LabServ (USA). Piperine, gallic acid, rutin, catechin, chlorogenic acid (CGA), isochlorogenic acid A (ICGA), caffeic acid (CA), 1,5-dicaffeoylquinic acid (1,5-diCQA), and 5,7-dihydroxy-3′,4′,5′-trimethoxyflavone (TMF) were all analytical grade (purity ≥98 %), obtained from YuanYe Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Sodium hydroxide (NaOH), sodium nitrite (NaNO3), aluminium nitrate (Al(NO3)3), and sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) were provided by XiLong scientific (China). The kits used to measure antioxidant abilities, including 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2, 2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS), and (Ferric ion reducing antioxidant power) FRAP), were purchased from Suzhou Grace Biotechnolgy Co.,Ltd. (China).

Fixation treatment

The GPs were immersed in boiling water (100 °C) for 5 min to preserve their unique flavor and color (Huang et al., 2025).

Drying procedures

The GP samples (3.6 kg) were divided into six equal parts, with one part used as a blank control group and the remaining five parts used for 5 different drying methods. The drying methods were stopped when the moisture content of the dried GPs reached a safe level of less than 12 %.

Hot air drying (HAD)

The GP samples flattened on a baking tray were dried in an electrically heated air blast-drying oven (DGH-9140 A, Shanghai YIHENG, Shanghai, China) at 60 °C with an air velocity of 1.5 m/s.

Far infrared drying (FIRD)

The GP samples were neatly placed on a baking tray and dried in a far infrared drying oven (HYHG-II-72, Shanghai YUEJIN, Shanghai, China) at 60 °C with an output of 1200 W.

Freeze drying (FD)

The GP samples were pre-frozen in a refrigerator (DW-86L578J, Haier, Qingdao, China) at −80 °C for 8 h, then dried in a freeze dryer (FD-1 A-50, BIOCOOL, Beijing, China) at ambient temperature with a vacuum of 20 P. The temperature of the cold trap was −54 °C.

Combined FD–HAD

The GP samples were initially freeze-dried for 12 h until the moisture content reached 49.5 ± 0.96 % (w/w), and then subjected to HAD at 60 °C with an air velocity of 1.5 m/s.

Combined FD–FIRD

The GP samples were initially freeze-dried for 12 h until the moisture content reached 49.5 ± 0.96 % (w/w). Afterwards, the samples were dried in a far-infrared dryer (HYHG-II-72, Shanghai YUEJIN, Shanghai, China) at 60 °C.

Powder preparation

The different dried GP samples were ground into fine powders of 0.50 mm diameter using a kitchen grinder (JYL-C50T, Joyoung, Jinan, China), which were stored at −80 °C before the experiments.

Physical properties analysis

Moisture content

The moisture content of both fresh and dried pepper was measured using a rapid moisture meter (DHS-20 A, LICHEN, Shanghai, China), which has a precision limit of 0.0001. Specifically, 2 g of fresh and dried GP samples were precisely weighed and placed in the tray of the moisture meter. The measurement was conducted at 135 °C under the autodetect mode, and the resulting moisture content values were recorded.

Energy consumption evaluation

Energy consumption was calculated by equals (1):(1)Et=P×twhere Et represents the total energy consumption (kWh); P is the output power (kW), and t is the drying time (h).

The color of the GP sample was measured using a hand-held colorimeter (WSC-1B, INESA, Shanghai, China) equipped with a test kit (NH-A-1508, 3NH, Shenzhen, China). L⁎: color lightness (0: black, 100: white), a⁎: red-greenness (−60: green, +60: red), and b⁎: yellow-blueness (−60: blue, +60: yellow) were the three dimensions used to represent each sample's color (Goztepe et al., 2022). For pre-measurement calibration, the matching standard black and white boards were utilized. The chroma (C) and hue (H) were calculated from the equations as referenced in Li et al. (2023).(3)H=arctanb∗a∗(4)C=a∗2+b∗2

Hardness and fracturability.

The hardness and fracturability of the GP were determined by using a texture analyzer (TA.XT PlusC, SMS, Surrey, UK) as described by Li et al. (2023). A 1 kg weight was used for force and height calibration before the test. The sample was placed flat under the P50 cylindrical probe (50 mm diameter) for testing. The other test conditions were set as follows: test mode (TPA), pre-forecast speed (1 mm/s), test speed (1 mm/s), post-test speed (5 mm/s), trigger force (5 g), and target mode (deformation80%).

Microstructure

The microstructure of the GP samples was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (Verious G4 UC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), following a reference protocol with modifications (Djordjević et al., 2023). The fresh and dried GP samples were fixed on the conductive discs with conductive adhesive and coated with a layer of gold, which was then scanned under the SEM. The samples were observed by adjusting the focal length with zoom and the images were finally captured at 500× magnification.

Flavor

The VOCs in the GP samples were detected using the Headspace Solid Phase Micro-extraction gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS), which was borrowed from the method described by Li et al. (2024) with minor modifications.

HS-SPME extraction parameters

To prevent any enzyme reaction, precisely 500 mg of the GP powder was transferred right away to a 20 mL head-space vial (5188–2753, Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) that contained a saturated NaCl solution. A 120 μm DVB/CWR/PDMS fiber (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) was then exposed to the sample's headspace at 60 °C for 15 min after each vial had been in the temperature for 5 min.

Chromatographic conditions

Following sampling, VOCs were thermally desorbed from the fiber in the GC injector at 250 °C for 5 min in splitless mode. Separation and identification were performed using an Agilent 8890 GC system coupled with a 7000D mass spectrometer equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). Helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. The oven temperature program was as follows: held at 40 °C for 3.5 min, increased to 100 °C at 10 °C/min, to 180 °C at 7 °C/min, then to 280 °C at 25 °C/min, and finally held for 5 min. Mass spectrometry was operated in electron impact mode (70 eV) with selected ion monitoring. The ion source, quadrupole, and transfer line temperatures were set to 230 °C, 150 °C, and 280 °C, respectively.

Qualitative and quantitative analysis

An independent database was established by integrating multi-species metabolite information, literature data, authentic standards, and retention indices. Each entry included predefined retention times, one quantitative ion, and 2–3 qualitative ions. Compound identification was confirmed in Selected Ion Monitoring (SIM) mode when the detected retention time matched the database reference and all predefined ions were present in the background-subtracted spectrum (Yuan et al., 2024).

Retention indexes (RIs) were computed using the retention times of n-alkanes, as outlined below.(5)RI=100×n+100×ta−tntn+1−tnwhere ta represents the retention time of the measured VOCs, while tn and tn+1 denote the retention times of the n-alkanes with n and n + 1 carbon atoms, respectively.

Relative odor activity value (rOAV) analysis

The rOAV was used to elucidate the contribution of each aroma compound to the overall aroma profile of the sample. The calculation formula is as follows:(6)rOAVi=CiTi

Where Ci and Ti are the concentration and the threshold value of the compound i.

Sensory evaluation

For the sensory assessment of the color, taste, look, and odor, ten panelists between the ages of twenty and twenty-five were chosen. As shown in Table S1, three tiers of criteria were created for every characteristic. Every 2 g sample was put onto a single-use petri plate. Normal illumination was used to evaluate color. Using the procedure described by Cheng et al. (2021), about 1 g of GP was dissolved in 100 mL of water at 60 °C to create the liquid, which was then used to test for odor and pungency.

Bioactive substances analysis

Piperine

Extraction process

The piperine was extracted from the GP sample using the ultrasonic-assisted method outlined by Vieira et al. (2022), with a few minor adjustments. In an ultrasound bath (YM-031 T, YUMENG, Shenzhen, China), a test tube containing exactly 0.5 g of GP powder and 20 mL of HPLC-grade ethanol was ultrasonically sonicated for 18 min at 50 °C. A 10 mL brown volumetric flask that had been diluted 1:100 with ethanol was then filled with precisely 0.1 mL of the supernatant.

Qualitative analysis

Piperine was analyzed using an Agilent 1260 Infinity II HPLC system equipped with a ZORBAX SB-C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of ultrapure water and methanol (HPLC-grade), delivered at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min over a 15 min run time. Detection was performed at 343 nm with a 10 μL injection volume. Compound identification was based on the retention time of authentic standards.

Quantitative analysis

A standard curve was generated by dissolving 10 mg of piperine in 10 mL of ethanol. The content of piperine in the samples was determined by comparing their chromatographic profile with the standard curves. Distilled water was utilized as a blank for the experiment.

Total phenols content (TPC), monophenol content, and total flavonoids content (TFC)

The TPC, monophenol, and TFC of the GP samples were assayed using the method as reported by Pereira et al. (2023) and Del Burgo-Gutiérrez et al. (2023), respectively.

Extraction process

TPC and monophenol: Precisely 0.5 g of GP powder sample was mixed with 10 mL of EtOH/H2O (0.1 % formic acid) (1:1, v/v), which was then sonicated for 1 h at a temperature of 40 °C. Following sonication, the mixture was centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 10 min to separate the supernatant. Finally, 20 μL of the supernatant was accurately dispensed into an enzyme-labeled plate for further analysis.

TFC: Taking the same steps as above to obtain the supernatant. Accurately 1 mL of the extract was added to a 10 mL volumetric flask and mixed thoroughly with 0.4 mL of NaNO3 solution (5:100, w/v) for 4 min. Afterwards, exactly 0.4 mL of Al(NO3)3 solution (10:100, w/v) was added and stood for 6 min, followed by the addition of 4.0 mL of NaOH solution (5:100, w/v). The mixture was allowed to stand at ambient temperature for 15 min and a blank reagent (EtOH/H2O (0.1 % formic acid) (1:1, v/v)) was used as a reference solution.

Determination process

TPC: A standard stock solution (0.1 mg/mL) was prepared by dissolving gallic acid in ethanol/water (1:1, v/v, with 0.1 % formic acid). A series of working standards (0.02–0.08 mg/mL) were then diluted. For analysis, 20 μL of standard or sample was mixed with 80 μL of 10 % Folin–Ciocalteu reagent in a microplate and incubated for 4 min. Then, 100 μL of 7.5 % Na2Co3 (w/v) was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark for 2 h at ambient temperature. Absorbance was measured at 765 nm, and TPC was expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per 100 g dry weight (mg GAE/100 g, d.b.). The retention ratio was calculated accordingly.(7)TPR%=CTPCCTPC0

Monophenol: Sample extracts were analyzed using a high-performance liquid chromatograph (1260 Infinity II, Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) at a temperature of 25 °C. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile (A) and water containing 0.1 % formic acid (B) in a ratio of 5:95, delivered isocratically at a flow rate of 1 mL/min. The injection volume was 10 μL, and detection was performed at 326, 278, 285, and 330 nm. Compounds were identified by comparing retention times and UV spectra with those of reference standards (CGA, catechin, CA, 1,5-diCQA, ICGA, TMF). Quantification was based on external calibration curves prepared from serial dilutions of each standard.

TFC: A standard stock solution (1 mg/mL) was prepared by dissolving 25 mg of rutin in 25 mL of 70 % ethanol. A series of working standards (0.2–0.8 mg/mL) were then diluted accordingly. The absorbance was measured at 500 nm using an enzyme marker (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, USA). The TFC of GP samples was quantified based on the rutin standard curve (concentration vs. absorbance) and expressed as mg rutin equivalents per 100 g dry weight (mg RE/100 g, d.b.). Antioxidant capacity

The antioxidant activities of the GP samples were assayed using testing kits (Grace, Shanghai, China) by Zhu et al. (2022) method.

Extraction process

About 0.1 g of GP powder was weighed and added into 1 mL of methanol solution (80:20, v/v), then extracted by ultrasonic extraction in an ultrasonic cleaner (YM-031 T, YUMENG, Shenzhen, China) at 60 °C for 30 min with shaking and mixing at 5 min intervals. Subsequently, the supernatant was centrifuged at 12,000 rmp for 10 min at ambient temperature.

ABTS+ radical scavenging capacity

The ABTS solution was prepared by mixing 0.25 mL ABTS diammonium salt and 0.25 mL potassium persulfate (1:1), reacted at ambient temperature for 12 h (protected from light), then diluted 23-fold with anhydrous ethanol to achieve a blank absorbance of 0.70 ± 0.1 at 734 nm. For assay tubes, 10 μL sample and 190 μL anhydrous ethanol were mixed, incubated for 6 min, and absorbance measured at 734 nm. The results were expressed as μg Trolox/g.

DPPH radical scavenging capacity

After precisely mixing 150 μL of sample solution with 150 μL of DPPH-methanol solution, the mixture was let to stand for 30 min at ambient temperature, shielded from the light, and centrifuged for 5 min at 12,000 rpm. An enzyme labeling device (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, USA) was then used to measure the absorbance value at 517 nm after 200 μL of the finished solution had been added to an enzyme labeling plate. The results were expressed as μg Trolox/g.

Reducing power of ferrous ions (FRAP method)

The color development solution mixed with acetate buffer, TPTZ solution, and FeCl₃ (10:1:1, v:v:v) was pre-warmed at 37 °C and stored under dark conditions before use. Exactly 5 μL of extract, 25 μL of distilled water, and 170 μL of color development solution were sequentially placed into the plate. After mixing, the reaction was carried out at ambient temperature for 10 min and the absorbance value was read at 590 nm. The results were expressed as μmol Trolox/g.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

The Infrared spectroscopy was carried out on GP samples using a Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometer (T27, Bruker, Billerica, Germany) at 20 °C to analyse the changes in phenolics. The method was referenced from Djordjević et al. (2023). For sample preparation, GP powder was homogeneously mixed with KBr (potassium bromide) and squeezed into flakes by applying appropriate pressure. Each spectrum collected 256 interferograms with a resolution set to 4 cm−1 and scanned 32 times. The range of wave numbers scanned covered a wide area from 400 cm−1 to 4000 cm−1. The background spectra were measured in the air for reference.

Statistical analysis

All physicochemical analyses, except for texture analysis, were conducted using three independent biological replicates. The data were presented as mean ± standard error and were processed and analyzed using Origin 2024 and IBM SPSS Statistics 26. Statistical significance (p < 0.05) was assessed by Duncan's test in SPSS.

Results and discussions

Moisture ratio analysis

As depicted in Fig. S1(a), (b), (c), (d), and (e), single FIRD (5 h) and HAD (9 h) demonstrated higher drying efficiency, whereas FD (36 h) was the least efficient. The combination of FD-HAD (17.42 h) and FD-FIRD (16 h) reduced the FD duration by 51.6 % and 55.6 %, respectively, highlighting the advantages of energy-saving and time-saving of combined drying. The drying process of GP berries initially experienced a lag, attributed to dense structural barriers that hindered water transfer (Aziz et al., 2019). The high efficiency of FIRD was due to IR-induced reverse thermal gradients and the softening effect on cell walls (Li et al., 2023). Conversely, forced convection in HAD is constrained by surface boundary layer drag. Despite its lower dehydration efficiency due to the energy demand for ice sublimation, a porous skeleton is formed during the process (Sun et al., 2023). Building on this, a methodology for the FD-HAD/FIRD technique was proposed: the GP was dehydrated to a critical point (0.45 g/g) using FD, after which the process transitioned to HAD/FIRD mode. This approach accelerated water migration through the synergistic effect of thermal and non-thermal processes, while preserving the stability of the porous skeleton.

Energy consumption analysis

Fig. S1(f) illustrated the impact of various drying methods on the energy consumption of GP. In the experiment, the corresponding energy consumption using HAD, FIRD, FD, FD + HAD, and FD + FIRD was 18.45 kWh, 6 kWh, 30.6 kWh, 21.3 kWh, and 15 kWh, respectively. Compared with single FD, the energy consumption of FD + HAD and FD + FIRD was significantly reduced by 30.39 % and 50.98 %, respectively. Hence, these combined drying technologies had enormous potential in reducing energy consumption and environmental protection.

Physical properties analysis

Color, texture, microstructure, and sensory evaluation are the most critical indicators of GP's physical quality and will be addressed individually.

Color analysis

Color, as a fundamental sensory attribute, is directly linked to consumers' purchasing decisions and value assessments. As illustrated in Fig. 1 (a), the drying process had a significant impact (p < 0.05) on the color parameters (L⁎, a⁎, b⁎, and C) of GP. The FD-treated samples displayed considerably higher brightness (L⁎ = 56.27), greeness (a⁎ = −3.76), and hue angle (H = 105.76), suggesting a brighter and more natural green color, which aligns with consumers' sensory expectations of “high quality”. This is because of FD's low-temperature vacuum preserves chlorophyll/carotenoids by inhibiting enzymatic degradation (e.g., polyphenol oxidase) and blocking pigment oxidation (Gao et al., 2024). Additionally, non-enzymatic browning reactions must be considered, particularly given the presence of reducing sugars in GP. The interactions between these different degradation pathways warrant further investigation. Oppositely, HAD damages cell walls, releasing phenolic acids (such as catechins) that serve as browning precursors, thereby exacerbating color deterioration through enzymatic and non-enzymatic pathways (Lyu et al., 2021). Generally, smaller a⁎ values are associated with greener hues. As illustrated in Fig. 1 (a), the a⁎ value remained high (HAD: −2.43; FIRD: −1.72) for samples subjected to a single heat treatment. This increase was attributed to heat-induced chlorophyll degradation. The ΔE values of the samples treated by HAD, FIRD, FD, FD-HAD, and FD-FIRD were 18.90, 21.49, 13.95, 14.18, and 17.37, respectively, clearly indicating the influence of the drying process and temperature on the color retention of GP. All samples that had undergone FD treatment had lower ΔE values (13.95–17.37), attributed to FD's low-temperature processing preserving tissue integrity and bioactive components. In conclusion, the drying process significantly influenced the color properties of GP. The FD process achieved the minimum color difference (ΔE = 13.95) with optimal greenness (a⁎ = −3.76) by utilizing the low-temperature vacuum environment as its core advantage, while the combined FD-HAD process offers a feasible solution for the production of brightly colored GP.

Textural (Hardness and fracturability) analysis

Fig. 1(b) illustrated the effects of various drying techniques on the hardness and fracturability of GP. It is evident that the hardness of FS was approximately 5000 g. This is because FS contains a large amount of water and has an intact cellular structure, which aids in maintaining tissue firmness. However, the hardness decreased significantly after drying treatment. The hardness of the FD-treated sample decreased to 3688.47 g, which is a 32.45 % reduction. This may be due to the sublimation process of the FD ice crystals, which leaded to the formation of a porous structure, resulting in a loose spongy texture (Zhao et al., 2023). Conversely, thermal drying treatment caused cell wall shrinkage and the formation of a tighter structure (Boateng et al., 2021), which increased the hardness values (4471.88 g ∼ 4852.82 g) compared to FD and its combinations. However, the channels formed during the FD stage of the combined drying process facilitated accelerated water migration during thermal drying, which intensified the inhomogeneity of cell wall collapse and further compromised the stability of the structure. Hence, significant differences in the hardness values (3403.47 g ∼ 3916.28 g) between the combined drying samples and the FD-treated sample (3688.47 g) were observed.

The results for the fracturability of various dried GPs followed a trend consistent with those for hardness. Notably, FD-treated GP displayed the lowest fracturability (1546.38 g), which represented a 67.90 % reduction compared to FS. This outcome contradicted the expectation that the porous structure created by FD would enhance fracturability, indicating that other factors might be counteracting the impact of porosity. The low-temperature conditions of FD optimally preserve pectin and hemicellulose within the cell wall, and the hydrophilic and gelatinous characteristics of these components impart a level of elasticity, which helps prevent brittle fracture even after drying (Xu et al., 2021). Conversely, the highest fracturability was observed in HAD (4712.78 g), followed by FIRD (4122.67 g), FD-HAD (3146.98 g), and FD-FIRD (3165.53 g) among the five drying. These results indicated that heat can result in coarser or unevenly distributed pores, with the resulting stresses creating localized points of stress concentration, thereby increasing fracturability. Overall, the GP samples treated with single thermal drying exhibited greater hardness and fracturability.

Microstructure analysis

Fig. 1(d) systematically revealed the ultrastructural similarities and differences between FS and five dried samples using SEM at 500× magnification. FS exhibited a smooth, continuous, and wavy epidermal structure. The HAD-treated samples displayed an irregular morphology and texture of the epidermis, with numerous convex and concave areas, closely related to the rapid dehydration caused by the convection of hot air. The FIRD accelerated water evaporation through far-infrared radiation, triggering rapid epidermal cell shrinkage and pore formation (Zhang et al., 2022). These pores enhanced moisture removal, expediting drying while altering GP's texture and appearance. The structural defects observed in the single thermal drying method were not merely cosmetic, they provided a direct microstructural explanation for the macroscopic results discussed earlier. Irregular, collapsed surfaces correlate with deeper colors and higher hardness, as the damaged structure lost elasticity and becomes brittle. In the case of the FD-treated samples retained a smoother surface with minor undulations and occasional bumps. This could be attributed to the slower rate of ice sublimation from the frozen sample tissue during FD (Zhang et al., 2023). This structural superiority was the foundational reason for FD's optimal performance in color. The intact epidermis acted as a barrier, reducing exposure to oxygen, which explained the lower ΔE. The FD-HAD-treated samples exhibited irregular folds, and reduced texture sharpness and groove depth compared to HAD-treated counterparts. The epidermis of the FD-FIRD-treated samples exhibited concave and convex areas, and the overall roughness was greater than that of the FD-HAD-treated samples. However, it lacked the dry-crack-like texture typical of FIRD-treated samples. Overall, FD and its combined drying methods better preserved GP microstructure, particularly epidermal integrity and uniformity.

Sensory evaluation analysis (SEA)

The significant differences in the results of SEA were illustrated in Fig. 1(c). The FD-treated samples (4.31) displayed light green (Fig. S2), preferred by consumers, while co-dried groups resembled FS's emerald green hue. In contrast, single thermal-dried samples (HAD: 2.63; FIRD: 0.88) exhibited dark brown tones, indicating lower visual appeal. This indicated that its color was visually less appealing to consumers compared to the FD-treated samples and co-dried treated samples. Regarding odor, the thermal dried samples had marginally higher taste scores (3.94–4.15). This was because of the high-temperature treatment accelerated the Maillard reaction, which enhanced the release of the GP's characteristic flavor compounds (Ren et al., 2021). The overall flavor profile may align more closely with the taste preferences of some consumers, thus potentially having greater market appeal. When it comes to taste, HAD-treated samples scored highest in taste (3.84), attributed to prolonged thermal excitation enhancing pure pungency without off-flavors (Marseglia et al., 2020). In contrast, the FD samples scored the lowest (2.48) due to the loss of VOCs through tissue pores induced by FD (Zhao et al., 2023). The co-drying methods balanced flavor retention and generation via stepwise modulation, achieving intermediate scores (3.3–3.07), suggesting a low-energy strategy for flavor optimization. In terms of morphology, GPs from the FD (4.2) were notably superior to other drying methods, followed by FD-HAD treated samples (3.93), FD-FIRD treated samples (3.65), HAD-treated samples (1.91), and FIRD-treated samples (1.44). These results were corresponding to the aforementioned microstructural analysis concluded in Fig. 1 (b) and (c). Overall, FD-treated samples proved optimal in terms of color and appearance. Thermal drying excelled in terms of odor, whereas co-drying showed more potential for application by balancing energy consumption and flavor. This strong correlation between instrumental measurement (SEM) and SEA (morphology score) significantly strengthened the validity of both datasets.

These physical indicators may affect flavor release and perception to a certain degree, highlighting the necessity of flavor analysis.

Although 354 VOCs were identified from the Venn diagram, only those with a relative odor activity value (rOAV) ≥ 1 were considered aroma-active compounds (Wang et al., 2025). Thus, a total of 27 aroma-active compounds were selected and presented in Table S2. In HAD-treated samples, 6-undecanol (1.34 × 104), 3-mercapto-3-methylbutyl formate (6462.31), 2-acetylthiazole (1263.24), and (+)-δ-cadinene (26.38) emerged as the primary aroma contributors based on their high OAV values. Among these, 6-undecanol imparted a grassy flavor through oxidative degradation of long-chain fatty acids (Wu et al., 2024), while 3-mercapto-3-methylbutyl formate generated strong sulphurous, burnt, and tropical fruit notes via thermal degradation of thioglycoside precursors during HAD processing (Cai et al., 2024). Additionally, 2-acetylthiazole contributed popcorn-like and roasted nut aromas through Maillard reactions. The terpene (+)-δ-cadinene formed a characteristic pungent base, complementing the overall aroma profile. Collectively, these compounds reflected the thermal degradation and Maillard reaction pathways induced by HAD treatment, shaping the distinct flavor attributes of dried GP.

As for the samples treated by FIRD, (2E,6Z)-2,6-nonadien-1-ol (7.40 × 105), (6Z)-nonen-1-ol (2.31 × 104), 4-(1,1-dimethylethyl)benzenepropanal (1.42 × 104), 2-aminomesitylene (1.27 × 104), β-phenylethyl isovalerate (2040.10), p-methylacetophenone (326.87), and dodecanenitrile (229.71) exhibited the highest rOAV values, and the first four were recognized as key aroma compounds. These compounds contribute to various aroma characteristics, including a “fresh and green” scent, fatty and subtly green notes, grassy-nutty-caramel complexity, and fruity undertones, respectively. Additionally, other compounds such as germacrene D, α‑irone, and cedrol play a role in shaping the overall aroma profile through their terpenoid contributions.

In FD-treated samples, 2-methyl-5,7-dihydrothieno[3,4-d]pyrimidine (1190.86), 2,4-diisopropylphenol (227.54), and humulene (14.67) demonstrated the highest OAV values. Notably, 2,4-diisopropylphenol, in combination with isomeric phenolic compounds, constituted a characteristic “smoky-cokey” aroma base. Although 2-methyl-5,7-dihydrothieno[3,4-d]pyrimidine exhibited the highest OAV value, it lacked a specific aroma description. These compounds collectively form the core aroma profile of FD-treated samples, reflecting both pyrolytic transformations and lipid-derived terpene generation during the drying process.

In contrast, the rOAV values of the 27 volatile organic compounds identified in the FD-HAD and FD-FIRD samples were all within the mid-range, suggesting that the contribution of each aroma component was more evenly distributed in these two composite drying systems. Furthermore, compared to single drying methods, these samples lacked distinct aroma characteristics.

The thermograms demonstrated that under various drying techniques, there were differences in the relative amounts of each volatile chemical in GP. The frequency of distribution of volatile chemicals in the six groups was demonstrated by row comparisons. The cluster structure revealed that the species composition of volatiles was remarkably similar across drying groups. Based on the clustering characteristics, the majority of terpenoids, alcohols, aldehydes, and ketones could be classified into separate taxa, indicating that different drying techniques regulate different volatiles differently.

Due to the fact that changes in flavor often imply changes in nutritional indicators, it is crucial to conduct relevant chemical composition analysis.

Bioactive substances analysis

Piperine analysis

Piperine, the primary active compound in GP, is responsible for the characteristic pungent and stimulating flavor of GP. As shown in Fig. 3 (a), FS showed the lower piperine content (3.73 mg/g) compared to dried samples, which may be due to its dense cellular structure. The cell walls of FS were composed of a tight network of polysaccharides, including cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin, which created a physical barrier that prevented the intracellular piperine from leaching out. The piperine contents in FD-treated samples (37.28 mg/g), FD-HAD treated samples (34.42 mg/g), and FD-FIRD treated samples (33.73 mg/g) samples were significantly higher than that in HAD-treated samples (25.32 mg/g) and FIRD-treated samples (23.04 mg/g). This was due to the fact that FD simultaneously preserved the cellular pore structure through sublimation, enhancing the solubility of piperine (Ghafoor et al., 2020). During the combined drying process, most of the free water is effectively removed in the initial freeze-drying (FD) stage, which significantly shortens the time required for subsequent thermal drying and thus minimizes the hindrance of piperine efflorescence caused by the hardening of the skin. In contrast, when HAD and FIRD were utilized separately, the prolonged high temperatures led to significant shrinkage and surface hardening of the GP, hindering the extraction of piperine from the tissue. Notably, although FIRD enhanced drying efficiency via radiative heat transfer, its infrared radiation also elevated epidermal hardness (Boateng et al., 2021), which obstructed the leaching of piperine, accounting the lowest content in the group treated solely with FIRD. Notably, samples treated with HAD exhibited the lowest piperine content yet the highest pungency rating (3.84). Conversely, FD samples with the highest piperine content received the lowest pungency rating (2.48). TPC, monophenol content, and TFC analysis

Fig. 3(b) presented a comparative analysis of TPC, monophenolic contents, and TFC in various samples. The FS sample showed the lowest TPC retention ratio (100 %) as compared with dried GP (216.60 % ∼ 531.22 %), which aligned with the previous findings reported by Huang et al. (2025) that pre-boiling prevents degradation of polyphenols. Notably, thermal drying samples exhibited lower retention ratios (HAD: 315.65 %, FIRD: 216.60 %) due to the combined effects of high temperatures, prolonged dehydration and oxidative conditions. In contrast, FD-FIRD treated sample exhibited the highest retention ratio (531.22 %), followed by FD (514.80 %), and FD-HAD (490.41 %). This was attributed to cellular disruption caused by ice crystal formation during the pre-freezing process, resulting in improved polyphenol extraction (Ghafoor et al., 2020). Furthermore, this can be attributed to low temperature and vacuum conditions. Subsequent HAD or FIRD involve short-term high-temperature treatment, but due to increased cell permeability, polyphenol release efficiency remains high. These results emphasized the crucial role of drying methods in regulating the retention of phenolic compounds through physical and chemical mechanisms.

As depicted in Fig. 3 (c), the total content of six monophenols ranged from 144.51 to 1372.35 μg/g. The highest total content of monophenols was observed in FD-HAD treated sample, followed by FD-FIRD treated samples, FD-treated samples, HAD, and FIRD-treated samples, with the lowest total content of monophenols found in the FS samples. These experimental results reaffirmed that longer drying times and higher drying temperatures accelerated the degradation of the phenolic acids, while vacuum and freezing environments helped to retain their contents. Notably, FD-HAD treated (1372.35 μg/g) samples and FD-FIRD treated samples (1299.32 μg/g) initially preserved the cellular structure and minimized oxidative loss of phenolics. Afterwards, the subsequent thermal drying at 60 °C facilitated the degradation of bound-state phenolics (Chao et al., 2023), resulting in the highest phenolic content. The high retention ratios of CGA, Catechin, and CA in FD samples were verified from the findings of Periche et al. (2016) that catechin is prone to non-enzymatic oligomerization at elevated temperatures, leading to the formation of high molecular weight polymers (Tu et al., 2023), which also explained the high level of catechin in the FD-treated samples (81.23 μg/g) (P < 0.05). On the other hand, the infrared radiation penetration may disrupt the structure of the monophenols, leading to the reduction in contents. Additionally, it was observed that the content of TMF remained relatively consistent across the five dried samples, a phenomenon attributed to the spatial site-blocking effect of methoxyl groups, which inhibited thermally-induced C-ring cleavage (Štellerová et al., 2022).

The retention ratios of TFC were presented in Fig. 3 (d). The trend of the values was similar to that of TPC. The highest retention ratio was found in FD-HAD treated samples (370.53 %), followed by FD-FIRD treated samples (356.00 %), FD-treated samples (353.09 %), HAD-treated samples (316.46 %) and FIRD-treated samples (167.81 %). This result suggested that low temperature and vacuum conditions facilitated the preservation of flavonoids. The thermal drying stage of combined drying processes (60 °C) activated β- glucosidase, which converted glycosides such as rutin into highly active quercetin (Baqer et al., 2024), resulting in the enhancement of TFC. Although FD (353.09 %) inhibited the enzyme activity, the release of flavonoids was 17.44 % and 2.91 % lower than that of the FD-HAD and FD-FIRD respectively. This was due to the insufficient cell wall fragmentation. The severe loss of flavonoids as antioxidant compounds is usually caused by high temperatures or prolonged thermal processes (Xu et al., 2020), which explained the lower retention ratios of HAD-treated samples (316.46 %) and FIRD-treated samples (167.81 %). In conclusion, the combined FD-HAD process had the best performance in phenolic and flavonoid retention through the synergistic mechanism of cryoprotection-thermal activation.

In conclusion, by regulating moisture migration, FD-FIRD optimally balanced key quality attributes: it inhibited excessive Maillard reactions (preserving color), retained pigments and volatiles (enhancing color and aroma), and protected phenolics and cellular structure (maintaining antioxidant activity and texture), establishing it as the superior method for comprehensive quality enhancement.

Comprehensive evaluation for the FD-FIRD sample

This study comprehensively evaluated five drying techniques for GPs, identifying FD-FIRD as the optimal method. Compared to freeze-drying alone, FD-FIRD reduced process time by 55.60 % and energy consumption by 50.98 %, while simultaneously preserving quality by retaining color, flavor, phenolics, and microstructure. The specific mechanism of changes in the drying process involved in FD-FIRD was shown in Fig. 7. First, the low temperature during the freeze-drying process fixed the structure by forming ice crystals, sublimating and removing free water, retaining heat-sensitive components like polyphenols and piperine, and delaying browning by inhibiting the activity of oxidative enzymes. The radiation activation phase (FIRD) was then applied to the sample. An inside-out temperature gradient was created during this time as the radiation permeated the material and induced heat production and molecular vibrations. Through thermochemical reactions, this gradient improved the scent, sped up water migration, and weakened the cell wall to avoid local overheating. Lastly, the porous structure created during the FD stage decreased the chance of thermal damage and offered effective water diffusion channels for the FIRD that followed. Conclusion

Five different drying techniques have a substantial impact on GP's physico-chemical and nutritional characteristics. Because of its low-temperature sublimation method, FD performed exceptionally well in maintaining color, microstructure, and bioactive ingredients. However, its lengthy drying period and significant energy consumption make it impractical for industrial use. Through Maillard and thermochemical reactions, thermal drying techniques like HAD and FIRD improved flavor profiles, but they also degraded color and nutrients. Notably, FD-HAD samples had the highest lowering power in the FRAP test (1297.06 μmol Trolox/g) and the highest retention ratio of TFC (353.09 %). The most promising strategy, however, was the combined FD-FIRD method, which reduced drying time and energy usage by 55.6 % and 50.98 %, respectively, in comparison to the FD method while retaining a significant retention of phenolics (531.22 %) and piperine (6.75 mg/g). In addition, 25 primary scent components had substantial negative correlations with color parameters L⁎ and a⁎, while b⁎, C, and textural markers associated favorably with these odors, with the exception of 3-mercapto-3-methylbutyl formate and 2-acetylthiazole. Conversely, there was a notable antagonistic relationship between the 25 fragrance chemicals and nutritional indices (piperine, TPC, TFC, ABTS, and FRAP). These results prove the effectiveness of the FD-FIRD method in processing GP.
