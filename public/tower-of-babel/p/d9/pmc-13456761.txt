Intravesical migration of an intrauterine contraceptive device complicated by giant bladder calculus formation: a case report
Authors: Dagmawi Anteneh Teferi, Masresha Solomon Dino, Seid Mohammed Hassen, Ali Ibrahim Ussman, Nigus Hagos Kahsay, Aklilu Kedru Nega
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:An intrauterine device (IUD) perforating the uterus and migrating into the bladder to become a nidus for stone formation is an uncommon scenario with a considerable management challenge.Case presentation:A 28-year-old woman presented with lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) along with pelvic pain of 3 years’ duration after 7 years of IUD insertion. A computed tomography scan and cystoscopy confirmed the presence of an IUD with a large stone formation inside the bladder. The stone was successfully fragmented using a pneumatic lithotripter, and the IUD, along with the stone fragments, was extracted without complications using a nephroscope.Clinical discussion:IUD migration into adjacent structures is a rare entity, occurring in one to two cases per 1000 insertions. The urinary bladder represents the second most common site of IUD migration, following the intestine. Affected patients often present with non-specific LUTS and usually report treatment for recurrent urinary tract infections, which causes a diagnostic delay, ultimately leading to calculus formation.Conclusion:Endoscopic management of an intravesically migrated IUD associated with calculus formation is the preferred therapeutic approach, offering favorable outcomes with reduced morbidity compared to more invasive interventions.

Introduction

Intrauterine contraceptive devices (IUCDs) are the most widely used and preferred contraceptive methods due to their efficacy, safety, reversible effect, longer duration, and cost-effectiveness[1]. Despite all the advantages, patients with intrauterine devices (IUDs) might rarely develop uterine perforation and migration into adjacent structures in one to two cases per 1000 insertions[2].

The most common sites of IUD migration are the intestine (32%), bladder (24%), and peritoneum (18%). Rarely, it might migrate to the stomach (2%), ureter (2%), and diaphragm (1%)[2]. We report the clinical presentation, diagnostic modalities, and management of a rare case of a migrated IUD into the bladder, which resulted in the formation of a large bladder stone. We report our case in line with the SCARE criteria[4].

Case presentation

A 28-year-old para-II mother, with both deliveries by spontaneous vaginal delivery, presented with lower abdominal pain associated with cyclic hematuria during menses for the past 3 years. She also reported urinary frequency, urgency, and dysuria of a similar duration, for which she had received multiple courses of treatment for recurrent urinary tract infections. She had undergone IUD insertion 7 years ago but had not been checking the IUD thread regularly. Otherwise, she had no history of abnormal vaginal bleeding, discharge, or any known chronic medical illness. A trial of IUD removal had been attempted using alligator forceps, a manual vacuum aspirator, and hysteroscopy at a referring hospital three times, with the most recent attempt performed 1 week prior to her current presentation. All attempts were unsuccessful, and she was subsequently referred to our institution for further management.

On physical examination, the patient had stable vital signs, and the genitourinary examination revealed the absence of the IUD thread. Laboratory investigations, including a complete blood count, organ function tests, and urinalysis, were within the normal range. Abdominopelvic ultrasound demonstrated an echogenic focus in the urinary bladder suggestive of a stone, prompting further evaluation with a computed tomography (CT) scan. The CT imaging showed a single hyperdense lesion measuring 3.1 × 2.9 × 2.5 cm, with a 3 cm long thin rod-like projection attached to it within the urinary bladder. The lesion had a density of 1426 Hounsfield units and appeared attached to the right posterior bladder wall (Fig. 1). Subsequent cystoscopic evaluation confirmed a single large bladder stone formed over one arm of a T-shaped IUD partially attached to the bladder wall (Fig. 2). A diagnosis of intravesically migrated IUD with associated large bladder stone formation was made. The patient subsequently underwent cystolithotripsy using a nephroscope, along with IUD removal under spinal anesthesia. Stone fragmentation was performed using a pneumatic lithotripter (Fig. 3). The procedure was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on her first postoperative day with a transurethral catheter in situ. The catheter was removed 5 days after the procedure, and the patient had a smooth postoperative course during her follow-up visits at the first week, 1 month, and 3 months. Discussion

The majority of patients with IUD migration into the urinary tract are symptomatic, with dysuria reported in 33.04%, hematuria in 26.1%, pelvic pain in 20%, and urinary tract infections in 15.6%. In approximately 4.3% of the cases, migrated IUDs into the urinary tract are detected incidentally[3]. Rarely, patients might present with cyclic hematuria [5,6]. Our patient’s presentation was similar to those described in previous reports.

Migration of an IUD can occur over a highly variable time frame, ranging from as early as 1 week to as late as 35 years after insertion[3]. Factors contributing to the displacement and subsequent migration of the IUD include uterine contractions, inflammatory responses, first-time use, a history of multiple abortions, poor healing of uterine scars following cesarean section, inexperienced operators, and anatomical variations[7–9]. In our patient, migration was detected 7 years after insertion, with no identifiable risk factors predisposing to this event. Urinary bladder stone formation following IUD migration is reported as early as 6 months after insertion[3,10].

Patients suspected of having a migrated IUD should initially be evaluated with abdominal or transvaginal ultrasonography to determine the location of the device and assess for the presence of complications[9]. Cystoscopy plays a crucial role as both a diagnostic and therapeutic modality for IUDs migrating into the bladder. It allows assessment of the presence of foreign bodies, bladder stones, fistula formation, and the extent of partial or complete migration. Cross-sectional imaging modalities are often required to evaluate the extent of migration into adjacent structures and the presence of complications such as fistula formation, stones, and hydronephrosis[10]. In our case, the presence of an intravesical IUD with an associated stone was confirmed using cystoscopy and CT.

Migrated IUDs should be removed as early as possible, even in asymptomatic patients, due to a wide range of potential complications. In general, the choice of management for migrated IUDs depends on the location, presence of complications, the availability of equipment, and expertise[3,10]. Management options for IUDs migrated into the bladder range from cystoscopic removal for completely migrated IUDs to laparotomy with partial cystectomy and possible reconstruction in complex cases[3,10,11]. A single management approach may not always be sufficient, particularly given the complexity of these cases. Whenever feasible, minimally invasive management options are the preferred choice of modalities, given the fact that they are associated with lower morbidity and favorable outcomes [3,10].

Completely migrated IUDs associated with bladder stone formation can often be managed with simple fragmentation of the stone using forceps or lithotripters, followed by cystoscopic removal. Endoscopic stone fragmentation can be performed using laser energy sources[12]. However, partially migrated IUDs with associated bladder stones are better managed with combined approaches, such as cystoscopy + hysteroscopy, cystoscopy + laparoscopy, or laparotomy + cystolithotomy[3,10,13–15]. The selection and combination of approaches should be guided by the accessibility of the IUD and stone while ensuring minimal morbidity and postoperative complications. Laparoscopy is generally preferred for partially migrated IUDs requiring bladder repair to reduce the risk of fistula formation. Laparotomy may serve as a second-line management option for partially migrated IUDs, either in cases with significant adhesion that are not amenable to minimally invasive modalities or when resources and expertise are limited[10,13,15].

In our case, the IUD, along with the stone, is located entirely within the bladder, with only minimal attachment to the bladder wall. The stone was successfully fragmented using a pneumatic lithotripter, followed by cystoscopic retrieval of both the stone fragments and the IUD.

Conclusion

Women using IUDs should be properly counseled on the potential complications and taught how to check them by themselves. In patients with missing IUD threads presenting with long-standing urinary complaints, migration of the IUD into the urinary tract should be considered as a top differential.

Minimally invasive approaches should be considered as the first-line treatment modality for intravesically migrated IUDs associated with stone formation, even in the presence of large and dense calculi.
