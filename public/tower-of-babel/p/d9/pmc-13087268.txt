Optimizing injector nozzle configuration for high efficiency and low emissions in diesel engines fueled with biodiesel and n-butanol blends
Authors: N. Sathiesh Kumar, Debabrata Barik, Santhosh Velmurugan, Milon Selvam Dennison, Ayyar Dinesh, Saravanan Rajendran, Viatkin Pavel Alekseevich
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Biodiesel, being a renewable fuel, offers significant ecological advantages with low harmful gas emissions and a reduced carbon footprint. This underscores the importance of optimizing the injector nozzle, which plays a key role in enhancing combustion efficiency. This study enhances the performance, emission, and combustion characteristics of a diesel engine operating in dual-fuel mode with an electronic injection system using 3, 4, and 5-hole nozzles. The tested fuels include conventional diesel, blended chlorella vulgaris oil (Extracted from algae), and waste cooking biodiesel (CWB20), and CWB20 mixed with 30% n-butanol. The biodiesel was synthesised using the transesterification method with a graphene heterogeneous catalyst. The synthesised biodiesel thermophysical properties were identified and compared with ASTM standards. Among the tested configurations, the CWB20 + 30% n-butanol blend paired with a 5-hole nozzle demonstrated the most favourable balance between efficiency and fuel economy, achieving a peak BTE of 33% and the lowest BSFC of 0.215 g/kWh at 5 kW BP, alongside substantial reductions in CO and HC emissions. Although the 5-hole with CWB20 fuel configuration exhibited higher NOx emissions due to enhanced premixing and elevated in-cylinder temperatures (up to 14.35 g/kW hr at high BP), these can be effectively mitigated through after-treatment techniques such as EGR and SCR. At full BP, the ignition delay and combustion duration of the engine fuel with CWB20 + 30% n-butanol were observed to be longer by approximately 1°CA and 2°CA, respectively, compared to diesel. Therefore, the CWB20 + 30% n-butanol 5 H combination represents a technically viable, low-carbon alternative for both large-scale and domestic diesel applications.

Introduction

Increasing environmental concerns and the finite quantity of fossil fuels have intensified the search for alternative fuel sources1. These alternatives are being explored as potential substitutes to help reduce the negative environmental effects linked to conventional fossil fuels, including high greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution2. The extreme climatic conditions observed today are largely a consequence of global warming, primarily driven by greenhouse gas emissions3. Since the Industrial Revolution, rapid industrialization and increased mobility have led to a significant rise in the number of automobiles. Emissions from these vehicles now account for a major portion of overall greenhouse gas emissions4.

As one of the most densely populated regions in the world, we bear a significant responsibility in addressing global warming5. Extensive research is underway to identify viable alternatives to diesel for use in CI engines6. Countries in Europe and North America are already making considerable progress in adopting vegetable oils as sustainable fuel substitutes7. Raw vegetable oils, when used as fuel, can hinder efficient combustion due to their maximum viscosity, which primarily results from the presence of glycerol. To overcome this issue, a synthesis process known as transesterification is involved to reduce the viscosity of raw oils. In the early stages of biofuel development, fuels were predominantly synthesised from edible oils like groundnut oil and peanut oil8. Primarily, biodiesels were considered merely as a source of alternative to reduce fossil fuels. However, further research revealed it is possible to increase the combustion process, owing to atomization9.

The fuel injection process plays a key role in achieving the chosen parameters of the CI engine cycle. The creation and disintegration of the fuel during spraying, followed by the development of the fuel-air mixture, are influenced by the fuel’s thermophysical properties, pumping pressure, injector opening, air swirl, the design of the injection nozzle hole, and its capacity of atomization10. Imperfection atomization and wear leading to incorrect operation of the injector in CI engines have a significant impact on engine performance, resulting in high consumption of fuel, higher toxic emissions, and greater smoke opacity in exhaust gases11. The key factors governing the mixing ratio of air and fuel, and subsequently fuel atomization and combustion within the engine, are the inside nozzle flow and spray characteristics12. The differences in the thermophysical properties of diesel and biofuel, the nozzle flow and spray characteristics are expected to undergo significant changes, which in turn affect the output power and emission in the diesel engine13. The fuel injector nozzle is a crucial component in diesel engines; including the nozzle holes numbers, higher injection pressure, and orifice size greatly influence combustion and engine performance by affecting spray characteristics like droplet size and penetration length14. Alterations in the surface condition and dimensions of the nozzle spray hole have an impact on the flow behaviour of the injection system. This, in turn, influences the shape and size of jet fuel delivery to each cylinder in an engine, thereby affecting the superiority of atomization15. Numerous studies have confirmed that the geometry of the nozzle hole number and its flow dynamics play a vital part in spray characterisation and the combustion process16.

Regarding the flow dynamics of the injector nozzle, Payri et al. (2009)17 described that higher viscosity and density of biodiesel notably influence the injection system, particularly affecting needle drive and the flow through the orifice of the nozzle. Their experimental results showed that biodiesel sprays tend to have longer penetration lengths and narrower spray cone angles, especially during extended injection durations. In contrast, during short injection events or under low-temperature conditions, a reduction in the mass of injected fuel was observed, primarily due to delayed needle lift caused by the increased viscosity. Additionally, Rajak et al. (2024)18 numerically demonstrated that a wider spray cone angle enhances combustion by promoting higher temperatures.

Battistoni et al. (2012)19 conducted a study examining internal flow dynamics and spray behaviour of injectors using both diesel (D100) and soybean methyl ester. The investigation involved two different nozzles with radii of 0.1 mm and 0.02 mm. The results showed that, compared to SME, diesel produces a smaller Sauter mean diameter and a lower penetration length. Mohan et al. (2014)20 employed a seven-hole injection nozzle, each with a nozzle hole of 0.175 mm, operating at an injection pressure of 600 bar and an ambient pressure of 30 bar. Their observations indicated that WCO biodiesel generated higher exit velocities at the nozzle, leading to longer penetration lengths and narrower spray cone angles. However, as the injection pressure improves, the differences in macroscopical spray characteristics between fuels become less pronounced. Jiang et al. (2015)21 investigated the nozzle flow behaviour of several fuel blends using transparent nozzles of different sizes. Their study concluded that fuels with lower viscosity and increased vapor pressure promote greater cavitation formation. Among the tested blends, ethanol-diesel mixtures exhibited higher cavitation intensity under identical injection pressure conditions.

Nandakumar et al. (2020)22 identified that the effect of increasing nozzle holes numbers from 3 to 5 on engine performance using a higher biodiesel blend ratio (B50). At maximum BP condition, increasing the number of nozzle holes from 3 to 5 resulted in a rise in heat rejection rate and cylinder pressure. The increased nozzle holes also shortened ignition delay, enhanced the combustion rate. The nozzle with 5 holes demonstrated better performance compared to the 3 H nozzle. Additionally, the increase in nozzle orifices led to decreases in CO, HC, and smoke emissions, but increases in NOx emissions. Patrick Rorimpandey et al. (2024)23 experimentally investigated hydrogen and diesel pilot jet combustion using two converging single-hole nozzles. The results indicated that with increased time separation between injections, the emission from diesel products cooled down, requiring a lengthier jet-to-jet interface duration to successfully ignite the hydrogen. Prabhu Kishore et al.24(2024) utilized a fuel combination consisting of higher reactivity jatropha oil with pure diesel and lower-reactivity 1-hexanol. This alternative fuel approach led to a 36.83% increase in BTE and a 19.48% reduction in BSFC. Additionally, significant reductions in harmful emissions were observed: smoke by 55.86%, unburned hydrocarbons (UHC) by 30%, carbon monoxide (CO) by 52.33%, and carbon dioxide (CO₂) by 46.3%.

Zhiyong Li et al. (2022)25 demonstrated that the nozzle hole number increases while decreasing its diameter improves fuel efficiency and reduces exhaust pollutant emissions. Recent studies have shown growing interest in modifying the geometry of injection nozzle holes to improve engine performance and support future applications. An investigational study was conducted on a diesel engine fuel with different butanol diesel volume ratios. Combustion characteristics showed that the butanol-diesel blends resulted in lower cylinder pressure, reduced pressure rise rates, and decreased heat release rates26. Mass fraction burned analysis further indicated a longer ignition delay and an extended combustion duration. Wavelet power spectrum analysis revealed higher cyclic variations, with the DBu15 blend exhibiting the most pronounced effect. Emission evaluation confirmed that butanol–diesel blends significantly reduced NOx, CO, and unburned hydrocarbons (UHC)27.

Rajesh et al. (2022)28 investigated the performance of pyrolyzed polypropylene-based plastic oil blended with pure diesel. At a 4% plastic oil blend, the indicated power differed by 0.01 kW at a low BP of 0.04 kg, increasing to about 0.02 kW at a higher BP of 17.04 kg. The brake power at 0.04 kg BP was 0.02 kW for both diesel and the 4% plastic oil blend, indicating similar performance under low-BP conditions. However, under higher BPs, the brake power for the plastic oil blend varied by approximately 0.02 kW compared to diesel.

Summary of diesel engine feedstocks with performance and emission.

Table 1 gives a summary of the literature on diesel engine feedstocks with performance and emissions for various studies. Although existing studies have mainly focused on specific nozzle parameters, limited research has explored the effects of injector nozzle number on performance, emission, and combustion. In this context, the present experimentation seeks to address this gap by examining how variations in nozzle hole number impact engine performance, emission, and combustion analysis.

From the literature, it is observed that many researchers have worked on the blending of diesel with biodiesel originated from various sources such as non-edible vegetable oil, waste cooking oil, microalgae, and animal fat to optimize the best performance condition of the engine to reduce emissions and enhance combustion. Some researchers have used various additives in biodiesels for certain advantages, such as to control the ignition delay, to enhance the cetane index, and to reduce the trade-off between NO and smoke emission. The present research explores the effect of varying the injector nozzle hole numbers with oil originating from Chlorella vulgaris and waste cooking oil (1:1), and blended with diesel and n-butanol as an additive. In addition to this, during the synthesis of biodiesel, graphene oxide was used as a catalyst, which is a reusable catalyst with 90% catalytic activity up to three times of its use. This novel graphene catalyst offered simpler biodiesel recovery, reduced soap formation, aligning with the ASTM standard yield. As a part of an engine experimental investigation, the present research uses CWB20 + 30% n-butanol blend as an injected fuel using an electronic unit injection system having multi-holes injector, such as 3H, 4H, and 5H injector holes. For a deeper analysis on the usability of the fuel blend, the combustion, performance, and emission of the engine were tested with the produced fuels and explained their effect on the engine and on the environment in detail in the subsequent sections.

Materials and methods

Selection of materials

Chlorella vulgaris oil and waste cooking oil were selected as raw materials for biodiesel production. Chlorella vulgaris was chosen due to its abundance, invasive nature, high oil yield, non-edible status, and potential for sustainable utilization. Waste cooking oil (WCO) was selected for its low cost, wide availability, role in effective waste management, sustainability, and its ability to balance the FFA profile when blended with chlorella vulgaris oil. Graphene oxide size 100 nm was selected as a nano-heterogeneous catalyst owing to its high surface area, dual acid-base functional sites, reusability, and effectiveness in processing high FFA feedstocks. N-Butanol was selected as an additive due to its capacity to improve the cold flow properties and reduce the viscosity of biodiesel blends, enhancing fuel atomization and combustion efficiency. The presence of n-butanol in biodiesel improved the polarity compatibility between diesel and n-butanol, ensuring good miscibility and oxidation stability of the final blend suitable for engine applications36. The n-butanol was selected for this study because its properties are best suited for diesel and biodiesel blending and conventional CI engine operation compared with the other butanol isomers. Its moderate cetane number, lower vapor pressure, and controlled evaporation behaviour helps maintain stable ignition and prevent excessive charge cooling during spray development. Importantly, n-butanol exhibits superior miscibility and long-term phase stability with both diesel and biodiesel, whereas iso-butanol, sec-butanol, and tert-butanol commonly show phase separation or poor compatibility under typical storage and operating conditions. In addition, n-butanol’s lower auto ignition temperature and more predictable reactivity profile support reliable combustion. These combined attributes make n-butanol the most appropriate and widely validated alcohol additive for improving spray breakup, reducing viscosity, and enhancing combustion efficiency in diesel biodiesel fuel blends37.

Biodiesel preparation

In the present study, a novel approach was explored by utilizing Chlorella vulgaris and waste cooking oil (1:1) as a raw material for biodiesel synthesis through transesterification. This was a widely used method for converting oils into biodiesel. The 1:1 ratio of Chlorella vulgaris oil and waste cooking oil was chosen based on preliminary pilot trials aimed at balancing the free fatty acid (FFA) content, viscosity, and yield during transesterification. Chlorella vulgaris oil alone exhibited higher FFA (> 5%), which can hinder biodiesel conversion, while waste cooking oil showed lower FFA but poorer oxidative stability. The 1:1 ratio provided the optimum compromise, resulting in a conversion efficiency of 85.6%, acceptable viscosity (2.9 cSt), and improved fuel stability comparable to ASTM standards. This optimized blend ensured smooth transesterification and desirable thermophysical properties for engine testing. The extracted Chlorella vulgaris and waste cooking oil (1:1) mixed raw bio-oil (before transesterification) exhibited a higher viscosity of 7.8 cSt, indicating the need for transesterification to reduce viscosity and improve fuel suitability for engine applications. The process involves reacting the extracted Chlorella vulgaris and waste cooking oil (1:1) with methanol in the occurrence of graphene oxide as a catalyst 2w/w% (weight% of the oil) and at this stage is maintaining an excess amount of alcohol, 3:1 molar ratio of methanol to oil, to ensure sufficient contact between reactants and promote efficient conversion of the oil mixture into biodiesel. The transesterification reaction also involves heating the oil with graphene oxide as a catalyst to a temperature of 75 °C for one hour. Continuous stirring at 300 rpm was maintained throughout the process to ensure effective reaction to complete conversion of the oil. After each transesterification cycle, the graphene catalyst was separated by filtration, washed with methanol, and dried at 80 °C for 2 h before reuse. The catalyst retained more than 90% of its initial activity up to three successive cycles, beyond which a gradual decline in conversion efficiency was observed due to surface fouling and minor structural degradation. Compared with conventional homogeneous catalyst NaOH, the graphene oxide offered simpler recovery, reduced soap formation, and cleaner biodiesel separation, aligning with the study’s sustainability goal of using reusable and low-waste catalytic systems.

The steps involved in biodiesel production are mentioned in Fig. 1. Typically, the transesterification reaction results in a two-layered product: the upper layer contains the desired methyl ester, commonly known as biodiesel, while the lower layer consists of excess glycerine. Once the reaction is complete, the clear biodiesel rich top layer is separated from the glycerine bottom layer. The biodiesel found from the transesterification process requires further purification, as impurities may remain after the initial separation. This is achieved using calcium chloride, which helps to dry the biodiesel and remove all residual water content.

The selection of 30% n-butanol for this research was based on optimization trials conducted for fuel miscibility, lower viscosity, and engine stability with CWB20. The 30% addition offered the best balance, reducing viscosity from 2.9 cSt to 2.7 cSt, improving volatility and oxygen content, and maintaining storage stability without separation. The blends above 30% of n-butanol show phase separation and reduces heating value of the fuel blend, while blends below 20% of n-butanol reduce the dissolved oxygen concentration, leading to higher emissions of unburned HC and CO; however, it does not affect the combustion efficiency. During the blending and storage of CWB20 + 30% n-butanol, no gum or deposit formation was observed. The addition of n-butanol actually helped in reducing polymerization tendencies by improving oxidation stability and lowering the viscosity of the biodiesel component. The blend remained homogeneous and free from surface films or residues throughout preparation and four weeks of observation. This stability is attributed to the balanced polarity between biodiesel and n-butanol, which minimizes the formation of peroxides or gums typically seen in long chain fatty esters under oxidation.

Experimental test setup

A Kirloskar EA10 G1 series commercial engine (5.5 kW, vertical, water-cooled, four-stroke, compression ignition) equipped with an electronic engine management system (EES) and combustion analysis system, variable BP, and 210 bar injection pressure. The test engine was directly connected to an eddy current dynamometer to control the electrical BP during the tests. To maintain a stable intake air temperature, a thyristor-controlled, closed-loop air preheater (1500 W coil power) was installed downstream of the air surge tank. K-type thermocouples were installed in the intake and exhaust manifolds and continuously monitored using a temperature controller. The layout of the experimental setup is shown in Fig. 2. An EGR system with an integrated cooler and control valve was connected to the exhaust manifold to regulate the hot or cold recirculating gas depending on the test conditions. The cylinder head was modified with an adapter to accommodate indirect fuel injectors, and conventional mechanical injectors were replaced with electromagnetic injectors. The test engine specifications are listed in Table 2. The physical model of the fuel injector with varying nozzle hole numbers is shown in Fig. 3.

A Kistler piezoelectric pressure sensor installed near the injectors was used to measure the in-cylinder pressure in real time. The mechanical fuel injector was upgraded to a high-pressure single direct injection system, managed by a PID-controlled DIDS. The 3 H, 4 H, and 5 H injector configurations were selected to systematically analyse the effect of atomization and air-fuel mixing intensity in the combustion chamber, which is directly related to the emission behaviour of the engine. These nozzle types represent a progressive increase in fuel dispersion while maintaining a constant hole diameter (0.28 mm), ensuring that performance variations are solely due to hole number. The chosen range (3 H to 5 H) also aligns with commercial injector designs commonly used in small and medium engines. The camshaft and crankshaft positions were detected using rotary encoders mounted on the crankshaft and dynamometer shaft extension. Fuel consumption was quantified by measuring the mass flow with a digital weighing scale and recording the time required to dissipate 20 g of test fuel using a digital stopwatch. A Kistler crankshaft angle encoder (with a resolution of 0.1 °C) provided precise crankshaft position data. The fuel pressure was maintained continuously at 210 bar using a common-rail system. A piezoresistive pressure sensor within the rail provided instantaneous pressure feedback to the control unit, which adjusted the safety valve and maintained a constant rail pressure. The control unit controlled the precise injection timing and quantity by referencing the compression TDC signal from the camshaft position sensor and governor. The injection timing and quantity can vary with changes in the program in the control system. The engine was stabilized under steady-state conditions before data acquisition. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and the average value was reported for analysis.

Fuel properties

The fuel thermophysical properties required for effective use in diesel engines are displayed in Table 3. These properties play a crucial role in influencing engine performance and emission characteristics and to avoid issues related to fuel atomization and spray behaviour. To ensure compatibility with standard diesel engine operation, the fuel properties must lie within the acceptable limits defined by recognized biodiesel standards. The measured thermophysical properties of the tested blends were found and it was to be comparable with pure diesel properties with specified ASTM values. The influence of fuel properties such as viscosity, density, and cetane number strongly affects atomization, ignition delay, and heat release rate, which in turn govern the combustion efficiency and emission characteristics. Lower viscosity and density enhance spray breakup and fuel-air mixing, promoting complete combustion and reducing CO and HC emissions. However, the higher oxygen content of biodiesel and butanol tends to raise combustion temperature, slightly increasing NOx formation. The optimized blend (CWB20 + 30% n-butanol) achieved a good balance between combustion stability, emission control, and fuel economy.

Result and discussion

Brake thermal efficiency (BTE)

Figure 4 represents the variation of BTE with engine BP for diesel, CWB20, and CWB20 + 30% n-butanol blends fuels tested with 3H, 4H, and 5H injection nozzles. For all injection nozzles, BTE increases with BP from 1 kW to 5 kW. At lower BPs, BTE values are in the range of 18 to 24%, while at maximum BP, efficiencies reach 26 to 33% on average. This is due to elevated cylinder temperatures, better fuel atomization under higher injection pressures, and reduced relative heat losses. At identical injector configurations, diesel consistently shows superior BTE at low BPs compared to CWB20, owing to its higher calorific value and superior ignition quality. However, at higher BPs, CWB20 + n-Butanol blends outperform diesel and CWB20. At 5 kW with a 4 H injector, CWB20 + n-Butanol achieves 33% BTE, compared to 29% for diesel and 27% for CWB20. This is due to n-Butanol blending, which is attributed to its inherent oxygen content, improved volatility, and superior atomization characteristics, which collectively promote complete combustion. Emissions of nitrous oxides (NOX)

Figure 8 illustrates the variation of NOX emissions with engine BP 1 to 5 kW for three fuel types diesel, CWB20, and CWB20 + 30% n-butanol, and three nozzle configurations 3H, 4H, and 5H. Across all configurations, NOX emissions increased steadily with BP due to higher combustion temperatures and in-cylinder pressures. Diesel fuel, NOX emissions increase from 9.44 g/kW hr at 1 kW to 13.26 g/kW hr at 5 kW using the 3H nozzle. Similarly, CWB20 recorded values from 10.58 g/kW hr to 14.03 g/kW hr, and CWB20 + 30% n-butanol from 10.05 g/kW hr to 13.32 g/kW hr under the same nozzle. The increase in NOX with PWB blends is attributed to enhanced oxygen availability, promoting complete combustion and elevated flame temperatures. Among nozzle types, the 5 H nozzle consistently produced the highest NOX levels for all fuels, followed by 4H and 3H. The 5H nozzle’s finer atomization and better air-fuel mixing led to improved complete combustion but also led to higher peak temperatures, thereby increasing NOX formation. The CWB20 + 30% n-butanol blend showed the highest NOX emissions at each BP and nozzle configuration. At 5 kW with a 5 H nozzle, it peaked at 14.35 g/kW hr, likely due to both higher oxygen content and improved atomization, promoting peak combustion temperatures. Conversely, the 3 H nozzle consistently exhibited lower NOX values, particularly with diesel 9.44 g/kW hr at 1 kW to 13.26 g/kW hr at 5 kW, as coarser spray and lower mixing efficiency reduced combustion temperatures slightly, limiting thermal NOX formation. Notably, at higher BPs (4–5 kW), the difference between fuel types became more pronounced, indicating the stronger influence of oxygenated fuel and nozzle design on NOX emissions under higher combustion pressures. Overall, although CWB20 and CWB20 + 30% n-butanol promote better combustion efficiency, they lead to increased NOX emissions, especially with high-performance 5 H nozzles. The experimental results, at 5 kW BP with the 5-hole (5 H) nozzle, the CWB20 + 30% n-butanol blend produced 14.35 g/kW hr of NOX, compared to 11.05 g/kW hr for conventional diesel under the same operating conditions. The increasing NOX emissions are 17% compared to conventional diesel. This trade-off must be considered in emission control strategies. To mitigate this, the study considers the implementation of standard after-treatment techniques, particularly: EGR to lower peak combustion temperature by diluting the intake charge with inert exhaust gases, and SCR using urea or ammonia-based catalysts to convert NOX into N₂ and H₂O in the exhaust stream. These are well-established technologies in diesel engine emission control and can effectively reduce NOX levels by 60–90%, bringing emissions close to or below those of baseline diesel operation.

The rise in NOX emissions with increasing biodiesel content follows the thermal Zeldovich mechanism, which is highly temperature and oxygen-dependent. The fuel-bound oxygen in biodiesel enhances local oxidizer concentration, accelerating the Zeldovich reactions. Moreover, higher adiabatic flame temperatures and slightly advanced combustion phasing elevate peak in cylinder temperatures, further intensifying NOX formation. Changes in nozzle geometry also influence droplet size and spray distribution, affecting the temperature field and the residence time of gases in high-temperature zones. Collectively, these effects explain the observed increase in NOX with biodiesel blends, linking chemical kinetics and in-cylinder fluid dynamics.

Heat release

Figure 10 illustrates the variation of heat release rate (HRR, kJ/m³·°CA) with crank angle for diesel, CWB20, and CWB20 + 30% n-Butanol under different nozzle hole configurations (3H, 4H, and 5H). The general trend shows negligible HRR during compression, followed by a sharp premixed combustion spike immediately after TDC, and then a gradual decline in the diffusion/post-oxidation phase. Across all nozzle configurations, diesel exhibits the highest HRR peak values, ranging from 43 to 45 kJ/m³·°CA. The sharper spike and quicker decay confirm its higher cetane number, superior volatility, and greater calorific value, which shorten ignition delay and concentrate energy release within a narrow crank-angle window. For CWB20, the HRR peaks are lower, in the range of 28 to 30 kJ/m³·°CA. This reduction is primarily due to the higher viscosity and slower atomization of biodiesel, which extends the diffusion burning phase and moderates the premixed combustion intensity. However, the oxygen content of biodiesel sustains post-oxidation, thereby flattening the tail of the HRR curve. The CWB20 + 30% n-Butanol blend exhibits intermediate premixed spikes of 38 to 40 kJ/m³·°CA, lower than diesel but higher than CWB20. The longer ignition delay, caused by butanol’s lower cetane number and higher latent heat of vaporization, allows greater premixed fuel accumulation before ignition, which explains the stronger premixed spike compared to biodiesel. However, the diffusion phase remains weaker (15 to 20 kJ/m³·°CA) due to butanol’s lower energy density and cooling effect. Peak HRR increases with nozzle hole number for all fuels. For example, CWB20 improves from 28 kJ/m³·°CA with the 3 H nozzle to 30 kJ/m³·°CA with the 5H nozzle. This clearly demonstrates that increasing nozzle holes enhances atomization and air–fuel mixing, thereby compensating for biodiesel’s higher viscosity. Overall, increasing nozzle holes intensifies and better phases the premixed burn, while fuel oxygenation (CWB20 and butanol) shifts energy release from the diffusion phase to cleaner premixed and post-oxidation pathways. Diesel with a 5 H nozzle maximizes HRR and efficiency, but at the cost of higher NOx and pressure rise rates. In contrast, oxygenated blends with 5 H nozzles deliver cleaner combustion with manageable efficiency. The heat release characteristics of the CWB20 + 30% n-butanol blend is strongly influenced by the linear molecular structure of n-butanol, which produces slower evaporation, a moderate laminar flame speed, and a longer ignition delay compared with diesel38. These properties allow a larger premixed charge to accumulate, resulting in a higher premixed HRR spike but a slower and more extended diffusion burn phase.

Ignition delay

Figure 11 illustrates the variation of ignition delay with BP for diesel, CWB20, and CWB20 + 30% n-Butanol blends under three nozzle configurations (3H, 4H, and 5H). A clear decreasing trend is observed as the engine BP increases. At low BP 1 kW, ignition delay ranges between 16–18° CA, whereas at high BP 5 kW, it reduces to 11–13° CA. This reduction in ignition delay with increasing BP can be attributed to higher in-cylinder pressure and temperature at elevated BPs, which promote faster fuel evaporation and accelerate chemical kinetics. Thus, the ignition lag period shortens significantly at higher power outputs. Diesel consistently shows the shortest ignition delay across all test conditions, varying from 17° CA at 1 kW to 11° CA at 5 kW. This superior performance is due to its higher cetane number, which ensures rapid auto-ignition. The CWB20 blend shows a slightly higher ignition delay compared to diesel, with differences of about 0.5 to 1° CA. This increase is due to its higher viscosity and density, which reduce atomization efficiency and slow down vaporization. However, the inherent oxygen content in biodiesel is compensated for partially by improving combustion stability. The CWB20 + 30% n-Butanol blend records the longest ignition delay, with 18° CA at 1 kW and 13° CA at 5 kW. The reasons are its low cetane number, which weakens ignition quality, and its high latent heat of vaporization, which absorbs cylinder heat during evaporation, lowering the local temperature and delaying ignition. Nozzle hole number also influences ignition delay. An increase from 3 H to 5 H reduces ignition delay by 0.5 to 1° CA, mainly due to improved atomization, reduced droplet size, and enhanced mixing. The effect is more significant in biodiesel and butanol blends, where physical property limitations are partially offset by finer spray distribution. Overall, the results confirm that BP, fuel properties, and nozzle configuration strongly affect ignition delay.

The oxygen content of CWB20 narrowed the gap with diesel, while butanol’s longer ignition delay and cooling effect limited pressure rise. Overall, the 5H nozzle provided the most efficient and stable combustion, particularly beneficial for biodiesel and its blends.

Among the tested fuel nozzle configurations, the CWB20 + 30% n-butanol blend with a 5-hole nozzle exhibited the most favourable trade-off between combustion efficiency and emissions, showing higher BTE and lower CO and HC emissions compared to baseline diesel. However, the blend also produced increased NOX emissions, attributable to its oxygen-enriched combustion and higher local flame temperatures. Although these can potentially be mitigated through EGR and SCR systems, such measures entail additional cost and design complexity that must be considered in practical implementation. Therefore, while the blend demonstrates technical feasibility as a cleaner-burning alternative to diesel, its overall “low-carbon” potential can only be fully validated through a comprehensive life-cycle. Overall, this configuration supports cleaner, sustainable, rural power generation, irrigation pumping, microgrid systems, and energy-efficient operation for both domestic and industrial diesel applications.

Future scope

Further research can focus on optimizing injection timing and pressure in combination with multi-hole nozzles to further enhance combustion efficiency. Integration of exhaust after-treatment systems such as SCR or EGR can be explored to mitigate the increased NOX emissions observed with high-efficiency configurations. Long-term durability and wear analysis of engine components operating on CWB 20 + 30% n-butanol blends can be conducted. Techno-economic feasibility studies are recommended to evaluate large-scale adoption in large and rural power sectors. Life cycle assessment (LCA) and carbon footprint analysis can be performed to quantify the environmental benefits over conventional diesel.
