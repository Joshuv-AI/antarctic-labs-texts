Knowledge gap in dysphagia: a study among nursing students in Nepal
Authors: Prabha Dawadee, Isha Wosti, Pratima Dawadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDysphagia is a swallowing difficulty that is primarily observed in patients with neurological, surgical, and geriatric conditions, posing significant risks for malnutrition, aspiration, and reduced quality of life (QoL). As part of the multidisciplinary team, nurses play a critical role in the early identification, monitoring, and management of dysphagia. This study aimed to identify the knowledge of dysphagia among nursing students in Nepal.MethodDescriptive cross-sectional study was conducted among the bachelor level’s nursing students of Maharajgunj Nursing Campus (MNC). Complete enumerative sampling method was adopted to collect the data from 206 nursing students. Data collection was done through self-administered questionnaire developed by Andrea Pickle Voight (2015). Data were analyzed using SPSS version 20. Descriptive (frequency and percentage) and inferential statistics (chi-square) were used to analyze the data.ResultThe findings revealed that students had a moderate level of knowledge of dysphagia (61%), and the knowledge has a significant association with age (p = 0.01), types of nursing program (p = 0.002), and no prior experience with dysphagia patients (p = 0.41).ConclusionThe nursing students at MNC demonstrated a moderate level of knowledge, which was significantly associated with their age and types of nursing program. Given that nurses play a critical role in dysphagia care, gaps in their knowledge may compromise patient management. Therefore, there is a clear need to strengthen dysphagia education within the Nepalese nursing curricula.Keywords: Deglutition, Swallowing, Nurses, Knowledge, Nepali

Introduction

Dysphagia refers to difficulty swallowing or struggling to keep food and liquids in the mouth [1]. It can involve various parts of the upper digestive tract, including the oral cavity, pharynx, esophagus, or gastroesophageal junction.

The global prevalence of dysphagia ranges from 2 to 20% [2–4]. Dysphagia can arise from various sources, including anatomical, neuromuscular, infectious, and inflammatory diseases, which may either manifest with dysphagia or contribute to its development [5].

Dysphagia can have numerous adverse outcomes including risks of aspiration pneumonia, dehydration, malnutrition, reduced pulmonary function, and in severe cases, mortality [6]. When viewed through the lens of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF), dysphagia is not merely a medical condition; it is a consequence of an underlying disease that can result in various restrictions on daily activities, as well as feelings of isolation and depression consequently affecting patients’ quality of life, often leading to diminished enjoyment of meals [7] Early identification combined with efficient management of dysphagia could reduce the occurrence of aspiration pneumonia and mortality while enhancing oral movements and nutritional status, thereby resulting in improved outcomes [8, 9].

Speech-language pathologists (SLPs) are the primary professionals sought out for dysphagia services and they play a crucial role as essential members of an inter-professional team dedicated to diagnosing and treating oral and pharyngeal dysphagia. Consultation from SLPs is considered the gold standard in dysphagia assessment [10] The SLP collaborates with physicians, dieticians, occupational therapists, physical therapists, respiratory therapists, families, and particularly nurses when caring for patients with dysphagia [11]. Taking an interdisciplinary approach is crucial for effectively assessing and managing dysphagia and ensuring optimal patient care [12]. The World Health Organization (WHO) also emphasizes the importance of educating speech-language pathologists (SLPs) and collaborating with other healthcare disciplines to enhance the understanding and management of dysphagia, recognizing its complex impact on patients.

Nurses have a distinct opportunity to positively impact the health outcomes of inpatients, given the significant time they spend interacting with patients [13] This allows them ample opportunities to identify swallowing difficulties, screen for the presence of dysphagia, make appropriate referrals, and also initiate interventions aimed at preventing further complications until a formal assessment can be conducted. They are also involved in dysphagia management as they are responsible for administering both oral and non-oral feeds, maintaining oral hygiene, and ensuring that prescribed swallowing techniques and maneuvers are performed as directed by the SLP [13].

Additionally, nurses are also responsible for providing counseling to patients with dysphagia [14, 15]. A previous study that explored the knowledge of dysphagia in nurses in Namibia found a moderate level of knowledge of signs and symptoms as well as complications of dysphagia but poor knowledge of the management [16]. A similar study done at Kathmandu University revealed that Nurses at KU had a moderate level of knowledge of dysphagia [17]. This knowledge was significantly associated with the nurses’ prior experience with dysphagia patients but not with their educational qualifications or years of experience in the field. The knowledge of dysphagia among nursing students in Nepal is still unexplored.

It is essential to understand the knowledge of dysphagia among nursing students and develop plans to improve dysphagia care in Nepal. Such training should target both healthcare professionals and home caregivers, equipping them with the knowledge to perform simple screening tools like the bedside swallow test and to implement proper patient positioning during mealtimes.

Method

Study design

Descriptive cross-sectional design was used in the study to assess knowledge of dysphagia a mong nursing students.

Study site and sampling method

The study was carried at Maharajgunj Nursing Campus (MNC), situated within the premises of Institute of Medicine (IOM). Using complete enumerative sampling, all nursing students enrolled in the B.Sc. Nursing and Bachelor of Nursing (BNS) programs at MNC were invited to participate. The total number of participants enrolled for the study were n = 206.

Data collection instrument

The instrument used in this study consisted of two components, the first section captured sociodemographic information, and the second section comprised a set of questions designed to assess knowledge related to dysphagia.

The study utilized the “Knowledge of Dysphagia Questionnaire,” developed by Andrea Pickle Voight (2015). It’s a self-reported tool consisting of 30. Questions assessing dysphagia-related knowledge. It is a reliable and valid instrument for assessing knowledge of dysphagia in patients, with Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC) of 0.716 for Section B1 (Signs and Symptoms), 0.704 for Section B2 (Complications), and 0.714 for Section B3 (Management). The tool has also been shown to be sensitive to changes in knowledge following educational interventions for nurses. The questionnaire covers three key domains: knowledge of signs and symptoms (13 questions), knowledge of complications (10 questions), and knowledge of management (7 questions). Each question offers three response options. Agree, Disagree, and Unable to Decide. A score of 1 was assigned for correct answers, while incorrect answers and “Unable to Decide” responses received a score of 0. The total score was calculated by summing the points from all sections. Participants scoring more than 50% of the total score were considered to have ‘knowledge present’, whereas those scoring below 50% were categorized as having ‘knowledge absent’. Among individuals who demonstrated knowledge (i.e., scored above 50%), the level of knowledge was further classified as follows: 75% and above was categorized as high, 50% to 74% as moderate, and below 50% as low [16].

Statistical analysis

All responses were numerically coded and analyzed using IBM SPSS version 20. Descriptive statistics, including percentages, were used to summarize demographic characteristics and knowledge levels. The Chi-square test was applied to examine associations between the outcome variable (knowledge) and the demographic variables (age, program type, and previous experience with dysphagia patients). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data collection

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee of the Institute of Medicine (IOM). Informed written consent was secured from all participants before data collection. Total 208 nursing students were approached and 2 of them declined to participate. Hence, 206 nursing students consented to take part and signed the written consent form. A specific date and time were scheduled for each year group of students. According to the schedule, the questionnaire was distributed inside the classroom to all students who provided consent to participate. They were asked to complete the form, and the responses were checked simultaneously for completeness. After collection, the questionnaires were taken for data entry. Data were collected in the same classroom setting over a three-month period to ensure consistency across all groups.

Ethical considerations and informed consent

The study was carried out in alignment with the ethical principles outlined in the 2013 Declaration of Helsinki. Approval for the study was granted by the Institutional Review Committee (IRC) of the Institute of Medicine, Tribhuvan University, Nepal (Reference number: [6–11] E2). Permission was obtained from head of the nursing institution prior to data collection. Written consent was used due to cultural and institutional norms, as the study was low-risk and non-invasive. Participants were informed about the study’s purpose and their right to withdraw any time during data collection. Data confidentiality was ensured, and all information was used solely for research purposes. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Among all the participants, most of them agree that coughing while eating (65.5%), feeling of food stuck in throat (97.1%), chocking saliva during non-meal times (67.5%), food remain in the mouth (66%), patient always cough if they aspirate (68.9%), weight loss (79.6%) and frequent throat clearing (72.8%) are symptoms of dysphagia while closing lips (59.2%) of them considered is not a sign of dysphagia.

Similarly, the participants agree that increased mortality (49.5%), pneumonia (69.4%, general weakness (88.8%), problem with digestion (67.5%), dehydration (80.1%), malnutrition (88.8%), chocking (94.7%) and difficulty breathing (66.5%)are the complications and 90.3% of the participants are not sure if sudden heart attack is the complication of dysphagia.

Similarly, most of them agree that maintaining oral hygiene (85%), avoiding thickened liquid (77.20%) and referring to SLP (50%) are considered as appropriate management consideration for dysphagia. However, all the patient with dysphagia (54.9%), positional consideration while feeding (78.2%), patient always being able to eat hospital food (65.5%), participants do not agree that these are dysphagia management strategies.

The overall correct response rate across all three knowledge sub-sections, signs and symptoms, complications, and management of dysphagia, was 61%, indicating a moderate level of overall knowledge among the participants.

Discussion

This study employed a descriptive cross-sectional study design to assess the level of knowledge about dysphagia and examine its association with factors such as age, prior experience with dysphagia patients, and types of nursing program (B.Sc. Nursing and BNS) among nursing students at Maharajgunj Nursing Campus.

Findings revealed that the nursing students at MNC had a moderate level of knowledge regarding dysphagia. A significant association was observed between knowledge levels and both the age of the students and the types of nursing program. However, no significant association was found between knowledge of dysphagia and prior experience with dysphagia patients.

Most participants demonstrated knowledge of common signs and symptoms of dysphagia, such as the sensation of food getting stuck in the throat, weight loss, and frequent throat clearing, choking on saliva during non-meal times, and coughing while eating. However, of participants disagreed with the statement “Patients always cough if they aspirate,” indicating a lack of knowledge about silent aspiration. This finding aligns with previous studies that have highlighted limited awareness of silent aspiration among nurses. If silent aspiration goes undiagnosed due to the absence of obvious signs of aspiration pneumonia, it can lead to increased morbidity and mortality [18]. Early recognition and appropriate management are therefore critical to prevent serious health outcomes. Hence as a major predictor of mortality in stroke patients, nurses need to recognize its signs and symptoms accurately [19].

The majority of participants (88.3%) acknowledged that dysphagia can lead to complications and correctly identified specific complications such as increased choking aspiration, dehydration, malnutrition, and general weakness. The higher scores in identifying complications indicate that participants possess a basic understanding of the common issues associated with dysphagia. Although there was relatively good awareness of pneumonia and difficulty breathing as frequent complications, knowledge about silent aspiration remained limited. This result aligns with previous studies that also emphasized the limited awareness among nurses regarding the connection between oral hygiene, silent aspiration, pneumonia, and mortality [16, 20].

In the management section of the questionnaire, participant responses varied across different questions. A majority of the participants disagreed with keeping the patient in a flat position while feeding and oral hygiene, while a significant number gave incorrect responses related to food consistency and feeding tubes. Additionally, only 50% of participants knew that patients with dysphagia should be referred to a Speech-Language Pathologist (SLP). The high rate of discrepancy in answers in this section may be attributed to limited nurse involvement in dysphagia management [21] These findings suggest that while nurses may possess general knowledge about dysphagia, they still lack evidence-based practice in its management.

Prior experience with dysphagia was not significantly associated with the level of knowledge, possibly due to inadequate exposure, brief duration of experience, and limited hands-on involvement in the care of patients with dysphagia. Most nursing students who indicated that they had prior experience with dysphagia patients reported only a few days of exposure during short-term postings at a rehabilitation center.

On the other hand, a significant association was found between students’ level of knowledge and their age, which in turn correlates with years of experience. This finding is consistent with previous studies that have also reported a significant relationship between knowledge levels and years of experience, suggesting that nurses with more clinical exposure tend to demonstrate higher knowledge due to the accumulation of practical experiences over time [16] A significant association was also observed between the types of nursing program and level of knowledge, with BNS students showing higher knowledge (91.6%) compared to B.Sc. Nursing students (74%). This difference can be attributed to the fact that BNS students are required to complete a 3-year PCL nursing course and have at least one year of work experience before enrolling in the program, whereas B.Sc. Nursing students typically enter the field without prior professional experience. Consequently, B.Sc. Nursing students may have fewer opportunities to acquire practical knowledge about dysphagia due to their limited clinical exposure. Additionally, the inclusion of first-year B.Sc. Nursing students in the study, who have limited clinical exposure, may have further contributed to the lower level of knowledge regarding dysphagia among B.Sc. Nursing students.

Overall, it indicates the need for dysphagia knowledge in the curriculum of the nursing students so that when they transition from student to practitioner they at least bear the minimum knowledge to handle dysphagia patients firsthand. The moderate level of knowledge in a nursing student working with stroke in KU [17] and the findings of this study clearly suggest that nursing students need Continuing Education Programs in dysphagia management.

Conclusion

The nursing students at MNC demonstrated a moderate level of knowledge regarding dysphagia, and this knowledge was found to be significantly associated with their age and types of Nursing Program. Considering that nurses serve as frontline professionals in identifying, monitoring, and managing patients with dysphagia, any gaps in their knowledge during student level can adversely affect timely intervention, patient safety, and overall quality of care. As educating nursing students has positive impact on nursing student on dysphagia knowledge [22], these findings highlight an urgent need to integrate more structured and comprehensive dysphagia-related content within Nepalese nursing curricula. Strengthening theoretical instruction, enhancing clinical exposure, and promoting interdisciplinary teaching particularly through collaboration with speech-language pathologists would help ensure that nursing graduates are better equipped to recognize dysphagia early and contribute effectively to its management in clinical settings.

Establishing a more dysphagia-oriented curriculum across different nursing programs, along with incorporating educational sessions delivered by speech-language pathologists (SLPs) as part of regular teaching–learning activities, can be considered effective strategies to enhance awareness and improve knowledge about dysphagia.

Limitation

The present study employed a relatively small sample size, further restricting the extent to which the results can be considered representative of the broader nursing student population across Nepal. Moreover, due to the study design, we were unable to explore differences in dysphagia-related knowledge based on academic year.

Future studies could be conducted with a larger sample size to enhance representativeness and allow for the assessment of knowledge progression across different academic years, providing insight into year-wise growth in dysphagia-related knowledge among nursing students.
