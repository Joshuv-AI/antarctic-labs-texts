Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota and Osteoporosis Risk in Chinese Postmenopausal Women: A Case–Control Study
Authors: Xiaofeng Yin, Yiyun Guan, Weixiang Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiet influences bone health through modulation of gut microbiota. The current study aimed to assess the association between the dietary index for gut microbiota (DI-GM) and osteoporosis in Chinese populations.MethodsWe conducted a case-control study among 500 postmenopausal Chinese women (250 osteoporosis and 250 controls). Controls were postmenopausal women matched or frequency-matched to cases on key characteristics (eg, age ± 2 years, time since menopause) who had BMD measurements within the normal or osteopenic range (ie, T-score > −2.5) at the same skeletal sites. The DI‑GM score was calculated based on 14 components, yielding a total possible score range of 0 to 14. The DI‑GM was analyzed as both a continuous variable and as categorical variables (0–3, 4, 5, ≥6). Logistic regression models adjusted for confounders were used to examine associations.ResultsIn the fully adjusted logistic regression, when DI-GM was analyzed as a continuous variable, the association with osteoporosis was not statistically significant (OR per 1-point increase: 0.96; 95% CI, 0.92–1.01). However, the analysis of categorical variables revealed women with DI-GM ≥6 had significantly lower odds of osteoporosis compared with scores 0–3 (OR 0.59; 95% CI, 0.36–0.97). Associations were stronger among women with BMI < 25 kg/m2 and serum 25(OH) D ≥ 20 ng/mL, although the interactions were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).ConclusionA high DI-GM score is significantly associated with a lower prevalence of osteoporosis in Chinese postmenopausal women, particularly when comparing the highest versus the lowest score categories.Keywords: osteoporosis, postmenopause, diet, gut microbiota, bone density

Introduction

Osteoporosis is a common metabolic bone disease characterized by low bone mass and deterioration of bone microarchitecture, leading to increased fragility and fracture risk.1 It imposes a heavy global health burden. Low bone mineral density (LBMD, encompassing osteoporosis and osteopenia) affects roughly 200 million women worldwide,2 and one in three women over age 50 will sustain a fragility fracture in her remaining lifetime.3 Women are especially vulnerable after menopause, since the sharp decline in estrogen accelerates bone loss.2 In China and other aging societies, the impact is particularly acute. Osteoporosis prevalence rises steeply with age and is higher in women, reflecting both demographic shifts and generally lower peak bone mass in Asian populations.4

Established osteoporosis risk factors include both non-modifiable factors (older age, female sex, genetics) and modifiable factors such as diet, smoking, excess alcohol, and physical inactivity.5–7 Recently, the gut–bone axis has emerged as a new paradigm in osteoporosis research. Mechanistically, the gut microbiota influences bone metabolism through microbial metabolites, immune modulation, and endocrine pathways.8 Short-chain fatty acids produced from dietary fiber fermentation enhance calcium absorption and stimulate osteoblast activity, while gut microbiota–regulated immune responses affect osteoclastogenesis.9 Preclinical and clinical evidence indicates that the gut microbiota significantly influences bone remodeling and osteoporosis.10 Experimental models show that altering gut microbes (for example via antibiotics or fecal transplants) can change bone mass.11,12 Diet is a primary driver of microbiota composition, and specific dietary patterns can promote a “beneficial” gut ecosystem.13 To capture this effect, a literature-derived Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) has been developed.14 In validation analyses, higher DI-GM scores correlated with biomarkers of beneficial microbial metabolism (eg lignan-derived metabolites in urine).14 This index assesses the intake of specific foods, scoring them based on their established or proposed beneficial versus unfavorable effects on gut microbiota composition and function. For instance, foods rich in fiber and fermented products (eg, whole grains, fruits, vegetables, yogurt) are typically scored favorably, while those high in processed ingredients, saturated fats, and artificial sweeteners (eg, sugary drinks, processed meats, refined snacks) are scored unfavorably. The total possible score for the DI-GM ranges from 0 to 14.14 Although the DI-GM was originally developed and validated in Western populations, its components include several foods commonly consumed in Asian diets (eg, soy products, tea, whole grains, and fermented foods), supporting its applicability in Chinese populations; nevertheless, further validation in diverse Asian dietary contexts is warranted.15

While conventional diet quality indices, such as the Mediterranean Diet (MedDiet) or DASH, address overall nutrient balance linked to various chronic diseases,16,17 the DI-GM offers added value by specifically targeting components known to modulate gut microbiota composition, providing a more direct, mechanistic insight into bone health. Few studies have examined DI-GM in relation to osteoporosis. In a recent large US cohort (NHANES 2007–2020), higher DI-GM scores were significantly associated with lower osteoporosis prevalence.18 Consistently, smaller clinical studies in postmenopausal women report that those with osteoporosis tend to consume more microbiota-disrupting foods (eg red meat, alcohol) and fewer fermented and plant foods.19 Those patients also had lower calcium and vitamin D intake, paralleling the known diet–bone associations.4,5 There are studies in Chinese populations looking at gut microbiota composition and diversity in relation to bone mineral density or bone loss.20 Another recent study from Beijing used 16S rRNA, metagenomics, and metabolomics to map microbiota and its metabolites in postmenopausal osteoporosis vs healthy controls.21 To the best of our knowledge, no study in a Chinese population has investigated the association between DI-GM and osteoporosis.

To address this gap of knowledge, we investigated the association between the DI-GM and osteoporosis in a Chinese postmenopausal population using a matched case–control design. We hypothesized that higher DI-GM scores, reflecting a diet richer in gut microbiota–promoting foods, would be associated with lower odds of osteoporosis, independent of established osteoporosis risk factors. Unlike single-nutrient approaches, the DI-GM integrates multiple dietary components known to act synergistically on gut microbial composition and metabolic activity, thereby providing a more comprehensive assessment of diet–microbiota interactions relevant to bone health.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This was a hospital-based case-control study conducted among postmenopausal Chinese women to examine the association between a DI-GM and osteoporosis. Recruitment took place between January and December 2024 at the Second Hospital of Longyan City, China. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Longyan City (Ethical code: LYEY-KY-2023-227) and all participants provided written informed consent.

Participants

Cases were postmenopausal women whose BMD measurement met the diagnostic criteria for osteoporosis: T-score ≤ −2.5 at the lumbar spine (L1–L4), femoral neck, or total hip (as measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry [DXA]).22 BMD was measured using the DXA-800F Bone Densitometer (Pinyuan Medical, Jiangsu, China). Subjects were eligible if they were ≥50 years old, at least 1 year postmenopausal, had no history of conditions or medications known to greatly affect bone metabolism (eg long-term corticosteroid use, hyperthyroidism, chronic kidney disease, etc).

Controls were postmenopausal women frequency-matched to cases on key characteristics (eg age ± 2 years, time since menopause) who had BMD measurements within the normal or osteopenic range (ie T-score > −2.5) at the same skeletal sites. Controls had the same exclusion criteria as cases (no history of bone-affecting diseases or treatments). The rationale for selecting controls from the same population was to ensure comparability to cases on access to healthcare, measurement methods, and socioeconomic background.

Sample Size and Power Calculation

Sample size was calculated based on the method developed by Fleiss et al.23 Accordingly, 500 participants (250 cases, 250 controls) were calculated based on sample size estimation (power) to detect an odds ratio of approximately 0.60 per 1 standard deviation increase in DI-GM,24 with 80% power and α = 0.05. Allowing for potential exclusions, missing data, or dropouts, we targeted a slightly higher recruitment.

Exposure

The primary exposure was the DI-GM, a summary dietary measure incorporating both “beneficial” and “unbeneficial” diet components relative to their effects on gut microbiota. Beneficial components included fermented dairy, chickpeas, soybean, whole grains, fiber, cranberries, avocados, broccoli, coffee, and green tea. Unbeneficial components included red meat, processed meat, refined grains, and a diet high in fat (≥40% of energy).14 Habitual dietary intake is measured via a valid 149-item Chinese-formatted, culturally adapted Food Frequency Questionnaire (FFQ) that covers consumption frequency and portion size of the relevant food groups over one-year retrospective period.25 To account for measurement error, the FFQ data were calibrated against a subset of participants (N = 150) who completed three non-consecutive 24-hour dietary recalls (24HRs). This calibration was performed using a regression calibration model to estimate usual intake. Each DI-GM component is scored in a binary manner (0 or 1) based on whether the intake of a person is above (for beneficial items) or below (for unfavorable items) the sex-specific media. For the high-fat diet component, a fixed energy threshold is used.

Confounders and Effect Modifiers

Pre-specified potential confounders were selected based on established literature regarding risk factors for osteoporosis in postmenopausal women26,27 and included age, body mass index (BMI), education level, household income, smoking status, physical activity levels, calcium and vitamin D intake, supplement use, serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25-OH D), serum calcium, and parathyroid hormone (PTH). Effect modifiers considered in stratified analyses included BMI (<25 vs ≥25 kg/m2), vitamin D status (<20 vs ≥20 ng/mL), physical activity level, supplement use, and education status.

Height and weight measured using calibrated instruments; BMI calculated as weight (kg)/height (m2). Waist circumference measured at the midpoint between the lower border of the ribcage and the iliac crest. Fasting blood samples were collected from all participants to assess serum 25-OH D, calcium, and PTH levels. Serum 25-OH D concentrations were quantified using the 25-OH Vitamin D Total ELISA Kit (KA6138; Abnova Corporation, Taiwan), a validated assay for measuring both D2 and D3 forms. Serum calcium levels were determined using the Calcium Assay Kit (KA1644; Abnova Corporation, Taiwan), which employs a colorimetric method for accurate calcium quantification. PTH concentrations were measured using the Monkey PTH ELISA Kit (EMK0061; Fine Biotech Co., Ltd., China). All assays were performed in duplicate with internal standards included in each batch to ensure quality control and minimize inter-assay variability. Each batch consisted of up to 96 samples, and the inter-assay coefficients of variation were <8% for 25-OH D, <6% for Calcium, and <10% for PTH, reflecting high assay precision.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Baseline differences between osteoporosis cases and controls were assessed using independent samples t-tests for normally distributed continuous variables and Chi-square tests for categorical variables. Non-normally distributed variables were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U-test as appropriate.

The primary exposure, DI-GM score, was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable (0–3, 4–5, ≥6). Logistic regression models were employed to examine the association between DI-GM and osteoporosis status. Three models were constructed: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, education level, household income, BMI, and smoking status), and Model 3 (further adjusted for calcium and vitamin D intake, supplement use, serum calcium, serum 25-OH vitamin D, and parathyroid hormone). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. P for trend was calculated using the median value of each DI-GM category as a continuous variable in the regression models.

Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate potential effect modification by BMI (<25 vs ≥25 kg/m2), serum 25-OH vitamin D (<20 vs ≥20 ng/mL), physical activity (low vs moderate/high), supplement use (yes vs no), and education status (<high school vs ≥high school). Interaction terms were included in logistic regression models to test for statistical significance of interactions (P for interaction >0.05 considered not significant).

All tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data visualization and descriptive summaries were generated using SPSS, including tables for participant characteristics, odds ratios, and subgroup analyses.

Calcium and vitamin D intake, as well as supplement use, were also lower in cases (all p ≤ 0.02) (Table 1).

Association Between DI-GM and Osteoporosis

In multivariable logistic regression analyses (Table 2), DI-GM expressed as a continuous variable was negatively associated with osteoporosis (OR, 0.93; 95% CI, 0.90–0.98). However, after full adjustment for confounders, this association became non-significant (OR, 0.96; 95% CI, 0.92–1.01), suggesting a modest effect size in the continuous model. In a categorical analysis, in the fully adjusted model, women with DI-GM ≥6 showed a significantly lower likelihood of osteoporosis compared to those with 0–3 (OR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.36–0.97). Additionally, higher beneficial-to-gut-microbiota scores were associated with lower osteoporosis prevalence (OR, 0.92; 95% CI, 0.85–1.00), whereas higher unbeneficial scores were positively associated (OR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.00–1.21).

Stratified Analyses by Selected Factors

Subgroup analyses showed that the inverse association between DI-GM and osteoporosis was stronger among women with a BMI < 25 kg/m2 (OR = 0.94; 95% CI: 0.88–1.00; P = 0.045), those with low physical activity levels (OR = 0.92; 95% CI: 0.86–0.99; P = 0.028), women with less than a high school education (OR = 0.92; 95% CI: 0.86–0.99; P = 0.031), and those with serum 25-OH vitamin D concentrations <20 ng/mL (OR = 0.91; 95% CI: 0.84–0.99; P = 0.022). No statistically significant interactions were observed for BMI (P-interaction = 0.08) or supplement use (P-interaction = 0.09). However, statistically significant interactions were detected for physical activity (P-interaction = 0.04), education status (P-interaction = 0.05), and serum 25-OH vitamin D levels (P-interaction = 0.03) (Table 3).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that in this sample of 500 Chinese postmenopausal women, those with osteoporosis had significantly lower DI-GM scores and BMI, along with lower BMD and nutrient intakes. Women scoring ≥6 had lower odds than those with scores 0–3. Overall, these results suggest an inverse association between diets promoting a diverse, gut microbiota–friendly dietary pattern and osteoporosis risk.

Clinically, this underscores the potential of diet-based strategies in bone health. A gut‐targeted dietary pattern, rich in prebiotics, probiotics and phytonutrients, could be a low-cost adjunct to osteoporosis prevention. In our study, the DI-GM–osteoporosis association held across most subgroups, implying broad relevance. Standard prevention emphasizes calcium and vitamin D, and indeed our cases had lower Ca/D intake, consistent with evidence that Ca+D supplementation reduces fracture risk by 15%.28 However, our findings point to an additional, gut‐mediated pathway. Notably, a randomized trial (PREDIMED-Plus) showed that older adults on a Mediterranean‐style diet (rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains and fermented products) lost less bone density over 3 years than controls.29 Likewise, meta‐analyses confirm that improving overall diet quality can measurably improve bone outcomes.30 Taken together, while causal inference is precluded by the case–control design, the consistency of these associations with prior Western studies suggests that diet–gut–bone relationships may extend to Chinese populations.18,29 Although the observed effect sizes at the individual level are modest, the widespread adoption of a beneficial dietary pattern, as reflected by a high DI-GM score, holds substantial potential for meaningful impact on population-level osteoporosis prevalence. Prospective cohort studies and randomized interventions are warranted to further evaluate causality.

Our results align with a growing literature linking gut‐healthy diets to skeletal health. Zhan et al reported that higher DI-GM scores correlated with 9% lower odds of osteoporosis in US adults.18 Similarly, Li et al found an inverse DI-GM–osteoporosis relationship in NHANES data.30 Beyond DI-GM, other studies implicate microbiota-friendly nutrients eg, higher fiber intake is associated with increased hip BMD,10 and probiotic or fermented-food consumption has been linked to reduced bone loss in postmenopausal women.31 Our findings extend these observations to an Asian population, highlighting that the “beneficial” components of the DI-GM such as soy, fermented dairy, and vegetables commonly appear in diets that support bone health, whereas “unfavorable” foods, including red and processed meats, refined grains, and excessive fat, are associated with lower bone protection.

The growing understanding of the gut-bone axis underscores the therapeutic potential of modulating the gut microbiota. Dysbiosis, characterized by an imbalance in gut microbial communities, is increasingly implicated in the pathogenesis of chronic inflammatory states that negatively impact bone health,32 such as those reflected by a higher DI-GM score. Therapeutic strategies targeting the gut microbiome offer promising adjunctive treatments for metabolic and skeletal disorders. For instance, Rifaximin, a non-absorbable antibiotic, selectively reduces the burden of small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) and certain pathogenic bacteria, which can mitigate systemic inflammation and potentially improve outcomes in conditions linked to dysbiosis.33,34 Similarly, probiotic and synbiotic supplementation introduces beneficial live microorganisms (probiotics) often combined with their preferred fuel source (prebiotics). Biologically, multiple mechanisms could explain this link. Diet profoundly shapes gut microbiota composition and its metabolites. Dietary fiber and complex carbohydrates (emphasized in DI-GM) are fermented by colonic bacteria into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs, eg butyrate, propionate). Animal experiments show that butyrate can directly stimulate bone formation and treated mice exhibit higher bone volume and serum osteocalcin.35 Gut-derived polyamines (spermidine/spermine) have been shown to upregulate osteogenic genes (RUNX2, osteocalcin, etc.) and inhibit osteoclastogenesis in cell and animal studies.36 Furthermore, the gut microbiota modulates the immune and endocrine environment that influences bone metabolism. Key immune cells including Th17 and Treg cells are influenced by gut microbes. Segmented filamentous bacteria and others can induce Th17 cells that produce IL-17 and RANKL, promoting osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption.10 Conversely, beneficial commensals (eg Clostridia, Lactobacilli) promote regulatory T-cells that secrete IL-10 and TGF-β. These cytokines suppress osteoclasts and stimulate osteoblasts, for example by downregulating RANKL and increasing osteoprotegerin.10 Probiotic supplementation (eg L. reuteri, L. rhamnosus, Bifidobacteria) has been shown to reduce inflammation and bone loss in estrogen-deficient models.37,38 Gut bacteria directly influence hormonal systems pivotal to bone integrity. A key mechanism is the microbial production of β-glucuronidases, enzymes that deconjugate and reactivate estrogens in the enterohepatic circulation.39 From an epidemiological perspective, these mechanisms provide biological plausibility for the observed associations between gut microbiota–oriented dietary patterns and bone health outcomes at the population level. Given the high prevalence of suboptimal diet quality and age-related bone loss worldwide, adherence to diets that support a favorable gut microbial profile may represent a modifiable and scalable strategy for reducing osteoporosis risk. Importantly, these findings reinforce the relevance of dietary interventions not only at the individual clinical level but also for public health nutrition policies aimed at healthy aging and fracture prevention.

This study has limitations. Dietary intake was assessed by recall, so misreporting (especially underestimating unhealthy foods) could bias results. As a case–control study, reverse causation and residual confounding cannot be excluded. In particular, unmeasured or imperfectly measured socioeconomic factors and genetic susceptibility related to bone metabolism or gut microbiota composition may have influenced the results. In addition, DI-GM is an indirect indicator of gut microbiota health; direct microbiome profiling or metabolomic measurements were not available. Accordingly, DI-GM should be interpreted as a population-level dietary indicator rather than a clinical diagnostic tool. However, dietary patterns captured by this index may influence bone health through both microbiota-dependent and microbiota-independent pathways. Strengths of this study include the use of DXA-measured BMD, matched case–control design, and adjustment for key nutritional and biochemical covariates. Future longitudinal or intervention studies should test whether increasing DI-GM (for example, through diet counseling or pre/probiotic supplementation) can causally improve bone health.

Conclusion

A high DI-GM score is significantly associated with a lower prevalence of osteoporosis in Chinese postmenopausal women, particularly when comparing the highest versus the lowest score categories; however, causal relationships cannot be inferred from this case–control study. There is a necessity for longitudinal or interventional studies to confirm causality. Stratified analyses suggested potentially stronger associations within subgroups (eg, those with lower BMI or inadequate vitamin D status), but these warrant confirmation in future, larger studies due to limitations in power and the need to further clarify interaction effects. This adds to a growing body of evidence that the gut–bone axis is clinically important and that nutritional strategies targeting the microbiome may complement traditional osteoporosis prevention. However, it must be noted that DI-GM should be considered a potential dietary indicator rather than a clinical diagnostic tool.
