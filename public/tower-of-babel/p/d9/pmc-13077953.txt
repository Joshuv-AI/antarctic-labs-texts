Waist-to-height ratio and hypertension risk: a retrospective cohort analysis of the CHARLS
Authors: Li Ma, Wei He, Dandan Wang, Bo Ma, Jianjun Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension represents a significant global health concern, with central obesity widely acknowledged as a pivotal risk factor. The objective of this study was to investigate the association between waist-to-height ratio (WHTR) and hypertension risk, identify specific thresholds.MethodsWe analyzed data from 7,712 participants of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), aged ≥ 45 years and free of hypertension at baseline (2011–2012),with follow-up until 2020. Waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) was the primary exposure and incident hypertension the main outcome. Covariates included demographic, lifestyle, and metabolic factors. To flexibly model the association between WHtR and hypertension risk, we used restricted cubic spline regression. Threshold effect analysis was conducted using two-piecewise linear regression, with the inflection point determined via log-likelihood maximization and assessed using the likelihood ratio test.ResultsThere was a non-linear association between WHtR and incident hypertension, with an inflection point (cut point) at WHtR = 0.443. Below this threshold, higher WHtR was not associated with increased risk of hypertension (HR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.71–0.89). However, above the cut point, each 0.1-unit increase in WHtR was associated with an 82% increased risk of developing hypertension (HR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.68–1.96). The risk difference before and after the cut point was statistically significant (HR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.96–2.66).ConclusionHigher WHtR is independently associated with a greater risk of hypertension in Chinese adults. The optimal cut-off identified in our cohort (0.438) is lower than commonly used Western thresholds. Population-specific cut-offs may improve screening and prevention efforts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05691-2.Keywords: Waist-to-height ratio, Hypertension, Threshold effect, Retrospective cohort study, Chinese adults, Anthropometric measurements

Introduction

Hypertension remains one of the most significant public health challenges worldwide and is a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [1]. In China, recent national surveys indicate that over 23% of adults are affected by hypertension, with prevalence rates rising especially among younger individuals [2]. Globally, hypertension is the most prevalent cardiovascular risk factor, affecting approximately 26% of the adult population [3]. The rapid pace of socioeconomic development and changes in lifestyle in China have further exacerbated the hypertension burden, with some regions maintaining persistently high incidence rates [4].

There is growing evidence that the demographic profile of hypertension is shifting, with younger adults increasingly presenting with elevated blood pressure, often remaining undiagnosed [5–7]. This underscores the urgent need for effective and accessible population-level strategies to enable early identification and intervention, particularly among high-risk youth. Despite this need, current large-scale screening efforts are often limited by the absence of simple, reliable, and scalable anthropometric indicators suitable for diverse populations.

The waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) has emerged as a practical and robust measure for the assessment of metabolic and cardiovascular risk [8–10]. Compared to the traditional body mass index (BMI), WHtR provides a more accurate estimate of cardiometabolic risk related to abdominal obesity [11–13]. Multiple studies have demonstrated strong associations between WHtR, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and the risk of hypertension [14]. However, most existing studies are based on cross-sectional data, and longitudinal cohort evidence from representative populations is limited. Although many studies have reported on the relationship between BMI and hypertension, evidence from longitudinal cohort studies on WHtR and incident hypertension in large Chinese cohorts remains limited [15]. In addition, the lack of standardized WHtR threshold values restricts direct comparison among studies.

This study aims to assess the relationship between baseline WHtR and hypertension risk based on longitudinal data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). By analysing a nationwide representative population cohort in a systematic manner, the aim is to provide scientific evidence for the early identification of hypertension risk and the development of precise prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and population

This study is a retrospective cohort analysis based on data from the CHARLS registry, a nationally representative cohort study that began in 2011 and followed participants through 2020 [16]. Participants were selected from 28 provinces (excluding Tibet) using a four-stage, stratified, probability sampling method, which included stratifying provinces by geographic region and urban/rural status, selecting counties and communities by probability-proportional-to-size sampling, and randomly sampling households within each community. At baseline (Wave 1, 2011–2012), 17,215 adults aged 45 years and older were enrolled. Follow-up surveys were conducted every two to three years (Wave 2 in 2013, Wave 3 in 2015, Wave 4 in 2018, and Wave 5 in 2020) by face-to-face interviews and telephone calls. For this analysis, we included participants who were aged ≥ 45 years and had complete data on waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) and hypertension at all waves. We excluded individuals with baseline hypertension (n = 134), missing baseline WHtR (n = 7,579), or missing outcome data during follow-up (n = 7,918), resulting in 7,712 participants for the final analysis (Fig. 1). Hypertension was defined as physician-diagnosed hypertension, self-reported use of antihypertensive medication, or blood pressure ≥ 140/90 mmHg.(CHARLS variable "da007_1_").

Flow chart of the study population

To assess potential selection bias due to loss to follow-up, we compared baseline characteristics between participants included in the final analysis and those lost to follow-up, after excluding individuals with hypertension at baseline. Standardized differences were used for continuous variables, and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Results are presented as standardized differences and P values (see Supplementary Table S1).

Data resource

Variables and measurements

The primary exposure variable was the baseline waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), which was calculated as waist circumference (cm) divided by height (cm) [17]. Waist circumference was measured using a non-elastic tape at the midpoint between the lower border of the last palpable rib and the iliac crest, with participants standing and breathing normally. Height was recorded using a calibrated stadiometer while the participant stood barefoot with the head positioned in the Frankfurt plane [16]. The optimal threshold for high-risk classification was determined by Restricted cubic spline regression analysis within the study population [18]. Outliers were excluded from the curve fitting and threshold effect analyses. To minimize the influence of extreme outliers on curve fitting and threshold effect analyses, two samples with WHtR10 values ≤ 0.12 and three samples with values ≥ 0.85 were excluded based on distributional characteristics and visual inspection. Subsequent analyses were performed on the data set after outlier removal.

The primary outcome was incident hypertension during the nine-year follow-up, which was ascertained according to the CHARLS protocol. Hypertension was defined as a self-reported physician diagnosis (variable "da007_1_"), the use of antihypertensive medication, or a measured systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, following the criteria of the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension [19].

Covariates were selected based on socio-ecological models and previous literature. Each variable was measured and classified as follows

Demographic characteristics

Age was recorded at baseline and treated as a continuous variable in the analysis. Sex was self-reported as either male or female.

Socioeconomic factors

Education level was self-reported and categorized as: (1) elementary school and below, (2) middle school, high school, or vocational school, and (3) college and above, according to the CHARLS data coding. Health insurance type was grouped into four categories: none, New Cooperative Medical Scheme, Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme, and other. Marital status was classified as married or single (including widowed, divorced, separated, or never married). Residence was coded as urban or rural, based on the official CHARLS registration [20].

Lifestyle factors

Physical activity was assessed by self-reported frequency of participation in exercise during the past year, according to the CHARLS questionnaire. The responses were categorized as: 0 = never, 1 = seldom (< 1 time/week), 2 = sometimes (1–2 times/week), 3 = often (3–5 times/week), and 4 = almost every day (6–7 times/week) [21]. Smoking status was classified as 0 = never, 1 = former, and 2 = current smoker; alcohol consumption was categorized similarly as 0 = never, 1 = former, and 2 = current drinker, in line with the coding scheme in the CHARLS dataset [21].

Physiological indicators

Blood pressure was measured three times at one-minute intervals after five minutes of seated rest using a validated automated device; the mean of the three readings was used for analysis. Fasting blood samples were collected to determine glucose, triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), which were classified according to the thresholds recommended by the Chinese Guidelines for the Prevention and Treatment of Hypertension and relevant epidemiological studies [19].

Ethics statement

CHARLS datasets are available for download at the CHARLS home website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en).The Biomedical Ethics Review Board of Peking University gave its ethical approval for the gathering of CHARLS data (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants signed an informed consent form.

Statistical analyses

All Statistical analyses were performed in R version 4.2.0 and EmpowerStatsRCH version 4.2 (www.empowerstats.com; X&YSolutions Inc.). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

To assess the association between waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) and the incidence of hypertension, the following analytical approach was utilized:

Restricted cubic spline regression was used to flexibly model and visualize the potential non-linear relationship between waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) and the risk of hypertension.

Threshold effect analysis was conducted using a two-piecewise Cox proportional hazards regression model to identify inflection points in the association.

Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the incidence of hypertension: Given the baseline differences observed between WHtR quartile groups (Table 1), all multivariable models were adjusted for major demographic, lifestyle, and clinical variables to minimize potential confounding and selection bias.

Results

A total of 7,712 participants were classified into four quartiles based on waist-to-height ratio (WHtR). Detailed baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Demographic Characteristics:Participants in the highest WHtR quartile (Q4) were on average older (60.93 ± 9.39 years) compared to those in lower quartiles (59.08–59.66 ± 9.13–9.28 years, P < 0.001). The proportion of female participants increased markedly with higher WHtR, from 35.04% in Q1 to 75.91% in Q4 (P < 0.001). Educational attainment decreased with increasing WHtR: the proportion with elementary education or below rose from 70.08% (Q1) to 76.56% (Q4), while those with college education declined from 1.03% to 0.58% (P < 0.001). Urban residence was also more common in Q4 (13.15%) compared to Q1 (7.44%, P < 0.001). Distribution by marital status did not differ significantly among groups (P = 0.362).

Metabolic and Clinical Parameters:Higher WHtR was associated with adverse metabolic profiles. Fasting glucose increased from 104.70 ± 28.97 mg/dL (Q1) to 115.98 ± 38.41 mg/dL (Q4), total cholesterol from 185.57 ± 36.79 mg/dL to 201.49 ± 40.01 mg/dL, LDL-cholesterol from 110.36 ± 32.15 mg/dL to 121.97 ± 37.88 mg/dL, and HbA1c from 5.13 ± 0.65% to 5.47 ± 0.96% (all P < 0.001). TG and HDL did not differ significantly across quartiles (P = 0.896 and P = 0.397, respectively).The prevalence of hypertension increased sharply with increasing WHtR, from 23.19% in Q1 to 54.93% in Q4 (P < 0.001).

Lifestyle Factors:About 60% of the participants reported engaging in physical activity almost every day, with no significant difference across WHtR quartiles (P = 0.965). The prevalence of never smokers and never drinkers both increased with WHtR, from 44.36% to 77.93% and from 57.78% to 79.30%, respectively (both P < 0.001).

Association between WHTR10 and the log relative risk (Log RR) of hypertension

The relationship between waist-to-height ratio multiplied by 10 (WHTR10) and the risk of hypertension was further examined using a restricted cubic spline regression model. After removing five outliers (as described above), the restricted cubic spline analysis demonstrated a robust non-linear association between WHTR10 and hypertension risk. As shown in Fig. 2, a clear non-linear association was observed. The risk of hypertension, presented as the log relative risk, remained relatively stable at lower WHTR10 values but increased sharply beyond a threshold of approximately 4.43 (i.e., WHtR = 0.443). The fitted curve, together with its 95% confidence interval, demonstrates the statistical significance and robustness of this non-linear trend. These findings suggest the existence of a critical WHTR10 threshold, above which individuals are at markedly higher risk of developing hypertension, highlighting the importance of early screening and targeted intervention for those with elevated WHTR10.

Threshold effect analysis of the association between WHTR10 and hypertension risk

The association between waist-to-height ratio multiplied by 10 (WHTR10) and hypertension risk was analyzed using both linear and threshold effect models. Following the exclusion of five outliers (as previously detailed), the threshold effect analysis yielded stable results supporting a non-linear relationship. In the linear model (Model I), WHTR10 was significantly associated with an increased risk of hypertension (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.47, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.38–1.57, P < 0.0001), indicating that each one-unit increment in WHTR10 (equivalent to a 0.1 increase in WHtR) was associated with a 47% higher risk of hypertension.

However, the threshold effect model (Model II) demonstrated a significant non-linear relationship, with a breakpoint identified at WHTR10 = 4.43 (equivalent to a conventional WHtR of 0.443). Below this threshold, the risk of hypertension decreased with increasing WHTR10 (HR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.71–0.89, P < 0.0001); above the threshold, the risk increased sharply (HR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.68–1.96, P < 0.0001). The difference in effect between the two segments was statistically significant (ΔHR = 2.28, 95% CI: 1.96–2.66, P < 0.0001).

The log-likelihood ratio test comparing the threshold and linear models was highly significant (P < 0.001), underscoring the superiority of the threshold effect model in describing the association. Clinically, these findings indicate that there is a critical threshold for WHtR (0.443), below which an increase in WHtR does not increase, and may even decrease, hypertension risk, whereas above this threshold, the risk escalates considerably. This non-linear association suggests that targeted interventions should be considered when individuals approach or exceed a WHtR of 0.443 (Table 2).

The association between WHTR10 and incident hypertension was assessed using Cox proportional hazards models with WHTR10 expressed both as a continuous variable and by quartiles (Table 3). In the continuous analysis, each 0.1-unit increase in WHTR10 was associated with a 45% higher risk of hypertension after full adjustment for confounders (HR = 1.45, 95% CI: 1.36–1.55, P < 0.0001).

When analyzed by quartiles, participants in the highest quartile (Q4) had a 2.62-fold higher risk of hypertension compared to those in the lowest quartile (Q1), based on the fully adjusted model (HR = 2.62, 95% CI: 2.26–3.03, P < 0.0001), with a clear dose–response trend observed across quartiles (P for trend < 0.0001; HR per quartile increase = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.32–1.44).

These results indicate that higher WHTR10 is robustly and positively associated with increased hypertension risk, in both continuous and categorical analyses, independent of demographic, lifestyle, and metabolic factors.

Subgroup analysis of the association between WHTR10 and hypertension risk

This study evaluated the association between WHTR10 and hypertension risk in subgroups, including age, sex, education level, marital status, physical activity, and place of residence (Fig. 3). The results showed a significant positive association between WHTR10 and hypertension risk, with hazard ratios (HRs) consistently greater than 1.0 in all subgroups and no significant interactions observed (all P for interaction > 0.05).

In the age subgroups, the HR for individuals aged ≤ 60 years was 1.52 (95% CI: 1.38–1.67), which was higher than that for individuals aged > 60 years (HR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.29–1.51). In the sex subgroups, the HR for men was 1.59 (95% CI: 1.42–1.77), slightly higher than that for women (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.35–1.59). For the subgroups of educational level, marital status, physical activity, and residence, HRs ranged from 1.29 to 2.18, with overall HRs ranging from 1.48 to 1.50 (P < 0.0001). Interaction tests showed no significant modifying effects of these stratification variables on the relationship between WHTR10 and hypertension risk.

Discussion

In this large-scale retrospective cohort study of 7,712 Chinese adults from CHARLS, we observed a non-linear association between waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) and incident hypertension, with a critical inflection point at WHtR = 0.443. Below this threshold, each 0.1-unit increase in WHtR was not associated with higher hypertension risk (HR 0.79; 95% CI 0.71–0.89), whereas above it, every 0.1-unit increase corresponded to an 82% greater risk (HR 1.82; 95% CI 1.68–1.96). The difference in risk estimates before versus after the threshold was statistically significant (HR ratio 2.28; 95% CI 1.96–2.66).Both continuous and categorical analyses further confirmed this robust dose–response relationship. These findings suggest that even moderate increases in central adiposity, as reflected by WHtR, may substantially elevate hypertension risk, and indicate that the actionable cut-off for WHtR may be lower than the widely used value of 0.5 [22, 23].

Subgroup analyses further demonstrated the consistency and robustness of these associations across various demographic and behavioral characteristics, including age, sex, educational attainment, marital status, physical activity, and residence. The absence of significant interactions suggests that the observed relationship between WHTR and hypertension risk is broadly applicable across diverse subpopulations. Exclusion of extreme outliers was performed to ensure the robustness of the non-linear modeling. Sensitivity analyses with and without outlier removal yielded similar threshold values, suggesting minimal impact on the main findings.

Our findings are consistent with and build upon recent studies. For example, Wang et al. [24] observed a significant association between WHtR and new-onset hypertension in Chinese adults, with each 0.1 unit increase in WHtR linked to a 30.4% increase in risk. Similarly, Liu et al. [25, 26] confirmed in a decade-long longitudinal cohort that central obesity indices, including WHtR, are reliable predictors of future hypertension. Compared to many previous investigations, our study benefits from a larger sample, comprehensive covariate adjustment, and the use of flexible modelling to identify an optimal and lower WHtR threshold. Moreover, unlike earlier studies with cross-sectional or short-term follow-up, our research design enables a stronger temporal inference.

It is worth noting that some studies have reported weaker or inconsistent associations, possibly due to differences in population structure, sample size, outcome definitions, or follow-up duration. For instance, Wu et al. [26] found only a modest association between WHtR ≥ 0.5 and hypertension among middle-aged women in CHARLS, likely attributable to the inclusion of only female participants and a shorter follow-up. Results from Ge et al. [27] further indicate the impact of age and sex on the strength of this association, highlighting the necessity for stratified analyses.

The biological plausibility of our findings hinges on the well-established mechanisms by which abdominal adiposity promotes hypertension. Central obesity contributes to insulin resistance, a pro-inflammatory state, and neurohormonal activation—including both the sympathetic nervous system and the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system—all pathways that increase blood pressure and cardiovascular risk [28]. These mechanisms underscore the value of WHtR as a metric for central obesity and cardiometabolic risk.

Our research provides valuable clinical and public health insights. WHtR is simple, non-invasive, and captures both central adiposity and body stature, making it preferable to conventional indices such as BMI or waist circumference, especially for diverse populations or resource-limited settings [22, 23]. The identification and application of a lower WHtR threshold (~ 0.44) enables targeted risk stratification and timely intervention strategies, such as weight management and lifestyle modification, for those at heightened risk. Its integration into primary care and public health guidelines for hypertension prevention may enhance early case identification and resource allocation.

This study’s strengths include its retrospective cohort design, which minimizes recall and selection bias; the large sample size, which provides robust statistical power and generalizability; the use of non-linear models to identify optimal WHtR cut-offs; and adjustment for an extensive range of confounders, which reinforces the validity of the observed associations.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, exclusion of participants due to missing baseline data on hypertension diagnosis or anthropometric measures may have introduced selection bias. Additionally, as data were unavailable for excluded individuals, we could not compare their baseline characteristics with those of the analytic sample. Second, our reliance on self-reported, physician-diagnosed hypertension during follow-up may have led to some outcome misclassification. Third, although we adjusted for multiple confounding factors, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be ruled out due to the observational nature of this longitudinal cohort study. Finally, as our study population consisted of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older, the generalizability of these findings to younger populations or other ethnic groups remains to be determined.

In conclusion, this study establishes WHtR as a practical and effective screening tool for hypertension risk in Chinese adults, with a lower actionable threshold (WHtR ≥ 0.443) than commonly recommended. Individuals at or above this cut-off face a sharply elevated risk of developing hypertension, underscoring the importance of timely weight management and lifestyle intervention. These findings support the integration of WHtR-based screening into clinical and public health strategies, and call for further validation of this threshold in diverse populations and settings.

Conclusion

In summary, this retrospective cohort analysis of Chinese adults shows that WHtR is independently associated with hypertension risk, with an optimal threshold of 0.438—lower than values typically proposed for Western populations. Applying population-specific cut-offs may enhance early detection and prevention strategies. Future research should validate these thresholds in other demographic groups and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms.
