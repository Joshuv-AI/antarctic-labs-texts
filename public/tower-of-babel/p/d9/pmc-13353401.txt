Emerged N193S mutation of PA-X protein disabled the immunity of mucosal dendritic cells for regulating virulence of clade 2.3.4.4b H5 subtype virus
Authors: Xinyu Miao, Xing Xu, Yixuan Han, Yiling Wang, Wenqing Wang, Jinyuan Wang, Yuncong Yin, Sujuan Chen, Daxin Peng, Tao Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn recent years, H5 subtype highly pathogenic avian influenza viruses (HPAIVs), especially clade 2.3.4.4b, have posed a global threat to poultry, cattle, and public health. Bioinformatics analysis of H5 subtype AIVs from 2000 to 2023 revealed a progressive increase in the PA-X N193S mutation, which became predominant in both avian and mammalian isolates of clade 2.3.4.4b. Using reverse genetics, we generated viruses with PA-X 193N (rWT) and 193S (rWT-N193S). The PA-X N193S mutation significantly inhibited viral polymerase activity while enhancing host shutoff. In vitro and vivo, rWT-N193S showed attenuated replication in avian and mammalian cells, reduced pathogenicity in mice, and suppressed cytokine storms. However, it enhanced uptake by dendritic cells (DCs), impairing DC maturation, activation, cytokine secretion, and CD4+ T cell proliferation. In murine nasal mucosal experiments, the PA-X N193S mutation reduced CCL5 expression, altered DC recruitment, and suppressed IL-17/MAPK signalling. These findings reveal a viral trade-off: the mutation attenuates epithelial replication and immunopathology but enhances DC uptake and disables mucosal immune functions, a strategy that likely contributed to the global predominance of PA-X N193S mutation in epidemic H5 subtype viruses.

Introduction

Influenza A viruses (IAVs) pose a persistent and significant global threat to public health and animal husbandry with H5 subtype highly pathogenic avian influenza viruses (HPAIVs) being of particular concern due to their severe impact on poultry and their proven zoonotic potential [1]. Since H5N1 HPAIV was first isolated in China in 1996 [2], the HA gene of the H5 viruses has evolved into multiple clades, from clade 0 to clade 9 [3]. The global co-circulation of the H5 subtype viruses has diversified into multiple distinct subclades, with clade 2.3.4.4 emerging as a dominant and globally disseminated lineage [4]. Since late 2020, clade 2.3.4.4b H5 subtype viruses have caused one of the largest recorded panzootic, devastating poultry and wild bird populations across Europe, the Americas, Asia, and Africa [5]. In the spring of 2024, an unprecedented outbreak of clade 2.3.4.4b H5N1 in bovine herds occurred in the USA, with virus spread within and between herds, infections in poultry and cats, and spillover into humans, collectively indicating an increased public health risk [6]. In 2013, a novel reassortant H5N8 virus was isolated in Eastern China [7] and later detected in Korea and Japan [8]. Since then, the H5N8 virus within clade 2.3.4.4b has spread to Europe, North America, and Africa via migrating birds [9]. Since the beginning of 2020, the H5N8 viruses have been extensively detected in various avian species across numerous countries, giving rise to two distinct branches within clade 2.3.4.4b based on HA gene analysis [10]. Alarmingly, human infections with clade 2.3.4.4b H5N8 viruses have been reported [11], underscoring the pandemic risk of 2.3.4.4b H5 viruses and the urgent need to understand the molecular determinants of their pathogenesis and host adaptation.

The pathogenicity of IAVs is modulated by multiple viral proteins, among which PA-X, a fusion protein expressed via ribosomal frameshifting in the PA gene segment, plays a multifaceted role [12]. PA-X possesses host shutoff activity, suppresses host protein synthesis, and regulates viral polymerase activity, apoptosis, and the host immune response, thereby significantly influencing viral virulence [12-14]. Studies have demonstrated that the PA-X site may regulate host shutdown activity by altering the binding or degradation efficiency of the PA-X protein to host mRNA, thereby affecting viral fitness and pathogenicity. Its function, however, appears to be virus subtype- and host context-dependent. For instance, PA-X deficiency or truncation attenuates the H5N1 virus in mice, whereas it enhances the pathogenicity of H1 subtype viruses [13, 15, 16]. In addition, our recent study has also found that PA-X played a role as a virulence factor in H9N2 subtype AIV, which enhanced pathogenicity in mammals by helping H9N2 subtype AIV escape the immune response [17]. It has been demonstrated that the adaptation mutation of the molecular marker of PA-X regulated the pathogenicity of AIV and host shutoff activity [18]. A previous study has demonstrated that truncation of the PA-X C-terminus of H1N1, H5N1, and H9N2 influenza viruses by 20 amino acids effectively reduced host shutoff activity, resulting in a diminished capacity for viral replication [19]. Recent studies have identified mammalian-adaptive PA-X mutations, including R195 K, K206R, and P210L. Among them, PA-X R195 K, prevalent in human H7N9 and H1N1/2009 viruses, enhances replication, transmission, and pathogenicity in mammalian models [18]. Our recent studies further demonstrated that PA-X proteins of H1N1 and H9N2 viruses regulated the pathogenicity of the virus in mammals and avians by influencing the innate immunity of dendritic cells (DCs) [16, 17, 20]. However, the impact of prevalent PA-X mutations in the currently circulating clade 2.3.4.4b H5N8 viruses on viral fitness and immunomodulation remains poorly defined.

DCs are the most potent antigen-presenting cells, serving as sentinels that bridge innate and adaptive immunity [21]. Upon respiratory infection, mucosal DCs are pivotal in initiating immune responses; however, they can also be subverted by viruses to exacerbate immunopathology or facilitate immune escape. Our previous study found that H5 subtype AIVs induced the “cytokine storm” regulated by DCs, which contributes to high pathogenicity [22]. Our prior findings indicate that H1N1 and H9N2 PA-X proteins evade DC immunity, thereby increasing viral pathogenicity [16]. Notably, several adaptive mutations in PA-X (e.g. R195 K, K206R, and P210L) have been identified to modulate viral pathogenicity and host adaptation in other subtypes [18]. Nevertheless, whether and how the emerging PA-X mutation of the prevalent H5N8 virus modulates DC-mediated innate immunity is entirely unknown, representing a significant gap in understanding H5N8 pathogenesis.

Here, we comprehensively investigated the impact of the prevalent PA-X N193S mutation in H5 subtype AIVs on viral pathogenicity, host shutoff activity, and dendritic cell-mediated innate immune responses.

Results

Increased frequency of PA-X n193s mutation in H5 subtype AIVs during 2000–2023

To assess the evolutionary trend of the PA-X mutation in H5 subtype AIVs, we performed a temporal and subtype-specific analysis of available H5 viruses sequences collected from 2000 to 2023. In avian-origin H5 subtype AIVs (Figure 1A), the frequency of the 193S mutation exhibited a substantial increase, rising from 32.7% during 2000–2005 to 69.9% during 2016–2023. A more pronounced trend was observed in mammalian-origin H5N1 subtype AIVs (Figure 1B), where the 193S frequency surged from 6.5% to 74.6% over the same periods. These data demonstrated that the PA-X N193S mutation has become the prevalent mutation in both avian and mammal-origin H5N1 isolates in recent years. We next focused on the currently predominant H5N8 clade 2.3.4.4b viruses, which have diverged into two groups, with Group II becoming the predominant branch [23]. As shown in Figure 1C, the PA-X N193S mutation was highly prevalent and characteristic of Group II viruses, being found in most avian-origin isolates and all analysed mammalian-origin isolates. The H5 subtype influenza virus has undergone genetic evolution from clade 2.3.4 to clade 2.3.4.4. Currently, the circulating H5N1 isolates belonged to clade 2.3.4.4b [24]. We also analysed the evolutionary pattern of the PA-X N193S mutation of isolates belonging to the above clade. As shown in Supplementary Figure 1, the proportion of the PA-X N193S mutation gradually increased with the evolution of the isolates. Given its notable rise to dominance across major H5 subtypes, we concluded that the PA-X N193S mutation was a high-priority candidate for further study. Together, these findings demonstrated that the PA-X N193S mutation has undergone a progressive increase in frequency across major H5 subtypes, culminating in its dominance in contemporary clade 2.3.4.4b H5N8 viruses. The PA-X n193s mutation attenuated viral pathogenicity in vitro and in vivo.

To experimentally investigate the functional impact of the PA-X N193S mutation, we used the reverse genetics system based on A/goose/Eastern China/CZ/2013 (H5N8), an early clade 2.3.4.4b Group I isolate that carries the ancestral PA-X 193N residue, allowing us to introduce the N193S mutation into a relevant genetic background. To determine the impact of the PA-X N193S mutation on polymerase activity, recombinant RNP complexes containing PA-X 193N or PA-X 193S were measured through a luciferase reporter assay in 293 T cells. As shown in Figure 2A, the polymerase activity of the RNP complex containing PA-X 193S was significantly lower than that of the wild-type complex containing PA-X 193N (P < 0.01), indicating that PA-X N193S mutation significantly impaired the polymerase activity of H5N8 subtype AIV. Importantly, the N193S mutation is located in the PA-X-specific +1 frameshifted reading frame and corresponds to a synonymous mutation in the PA polymerase ORF. Therefore, the mutation does not alter the amino acid sequence of the PA protein, and the observed differences in polymerase activity between rWT and rWT-N193S are attributable solely to the amino acid substitution in PA-X, not to any change in the PA polymerase subunit. Moreover, PA-X protein played a major role in host shutoff activity, which suppressed host protein synthesis in infected cells [12]. Through co-transfection of a GFP-reporter plasmid with PA-X expression vectors, flow cytometry and immunofluorescence demonstrated that PA-X 193S suppressed host exogenous protein synthesis more efficiently than PA-X 193N, evidenced by significantly lower GFP expression (P < 0.05) (Figure 2B). The host shutoff activity mediated by influenza virus PA-X is closely linked to the nuclear accumulation of poly(A)-binding protein cytoplasmic 1 (PABPC1), a well-established molecular hallmark of global host mRNA degradation, the greater the host shutoff activity, the more accumulation of PABPC1 protein in the nucleus of the cell [25, 26]. Consistent with the established correlation between host shutoff activity and nuclear accumulation of PABPC1, western blot analysis confirmed a marked increase in nuclear PABPC1 in cells expressing PA-X 193S compared to PA-X 193N (Supplementary Figure 2). To determine the influence of impaired polymerase activity and host shutoff activity induced by the PA-X N193S mutation on the viral replication capacity of H5N8 subtype AIV, H5N8 subtype AIV (A/goose/Eastern China/CZ/2013) (rWT) and its PA-X N193S mutant virus (rWT-N193S) were successfully generated by site-directed mutagenesis and reverse genetics. Mammal (A549 and MDCK cells) and avian (CEF cells) origin cells and were infected at a dose of 0.1 MOI to detect the viral replication capacity of rWT and rWT-N193S. As shown in Figure 2C, the viral titre of rWT was significantly higher than that of rWT-N193S after infection for 36 h on CEF cells (P < 0.01). This difference of replication capacity was more significant on mammal origin cells (MDCK and A549 cells) (P < 0.01). Those results indicated that PA-X N193S mutation significantly inhibited the viral replication capacity of H5N8 subtype AIV in vitro, especially on mammalian cells.

In order to examine the effect of PA-X N193S mutation on mammalian pathogenicity in vivo, BALB/c mice were intranasally inoculated with 101–106 EID50/50 μL of rWT or rWT-N193S. When 106 EID50/50 μL was used for inoculation, all infected mice died within 7 days, whereas the mice infected with rWT-N193S died later than that with rWT. When the challenge dose ranged from 103 to 105 EID50/50 μL, mice of rWT infection group all died within 10 days, while the survival rate of rWT-N193S infection group remained at 60%−80%. The mouse median lethal dose (MLD50) results showed that rWT (MLD50 = 3.5 EID50/50 μL) displayed a stronger pathogenicity to mice than rWT-N193S (MLD50 = 5 EID50/50 μL) (Figure 2D and E). Furthermore, as shown in Figure 2F, obvious pathological changes were observed in lung. In detail, histopathological analysis based on H&E staining showed that rWT caused severe lesion in the lungs, which were characterized by the disappearance of alveolar structure and severe infiltration of inflammatory cells in the alveolar interstitium. However, the pathological changes caused by rWT-N193S were alleviated. Viral titre was detected through EID50 assay to evaluate the replication levels of virus in lung and nasal tissues at 3 and 5 days post infection (d.p.i.). As shown in Figure 2G, viral titres in the lungs and nasal tissues were higher in mice infected with 106 EID50 of the rWT virus than in that infected with the rWT-N193S virus at 3 and 5 d.p.i. (P < 0.01), which demonstrated that PA-X N193S mutation inhibited the viral replication in the upper respiratory tract of mouse.

Given the critical role of cytokine storm in the pathogenicity of H5 subtype AIVs, we detected the expression of cytokines in infected lungs by qRT-PCR. As shown in Figure 2H, the lower expression levels of IL-8, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, TNF-α, and IFN-β in rWT-N193S-infected mice were detected compared with that in rWT-infected mice, suggesting that the PA-X N193S mutation weakened the cytokine storm, resulting in the low pathogenicity in mice. Collectively, these data showed that PA-X N193S mutation reduced viral replication and pathogenicity in vitro and vivo by impairing viral polymerase activity, enhancing host shutoff function, and mitigating the infection-induced cytokine storm.

The PA-X n193s mutation escaped immune response through breakthrough infection and downregulation of DC activation

To assess the functional impact of the PA-X N193S mutation on innate immune response, maturation and antigen uptake of mouse bone marrow-derived DCs were evaluated. Although rWT-N193S exhibited low replication efficiency in epithelial cells, interestingly, its infectivity in DCs was significantly higher than that for rWT (Figure 3A and Supplementary Figure 3, P < 0.05). The capacity of antigen uptake in immature DCs is efficient but lost during the maturation stage, which can reflect the maturation of DCs [16]. FITC-Dextran was used to determine the endocytosis capacity of virus-infected DCs by flow cytometry. The results showed that DCs in the 4°C control group were dormant and the endocytosis levels of FITC-Dextran remained at a low level, which were lower than those of DCs in the 37°C group. The mean fluorescence intensity of FITC-Dextran+ LPS-treated DCs was significantly decreased compared with that of MOCK group. Notably, DCs infected with the rWT-N193S virus exhibited a significantly higher level of FITC-dextran uptake compared to those infected with the rWT virus (Figure 3B and C, P < 0.05). The above results suggested that the PA-X N193S mutation maintained DCs in a state of enhanced endocytic activity, potentially indicative of delayed or impaired maturation. To determine the effect of PA-X N193S mutation on the activation ability of DCs, the expression of CD69 was detected by flow cytometry after infection with rWT and rWT-N193S. The expression of CD69 was significantly downregulated after infection with rWT-N193S at an infection dose of 1 MOI compared with that of rWT infection group (Figure 3D and E, P < 0.01), which indicates that the PA-X N193S mutation significantly inhibited the ability of H5N8 subtype AIV to activate DCs. DCs' phenotypic markers, including CD80, CD86, CD40, and MHCII, played important roles in the process of DCs antigen presentation, and were also important markers of DCs' function and maturation [16]. To further understand the effect of PA-X N193S mutation on H5N8 subtype AIV-induced DCs phenotypic markers, DCs were infected with rWT or rWT-N193S, and the expression of phenotypic markers was detected by flow cytometry. As shown in Figure 3F and G, it was found that when the virus infected DCs at a dose of 1 MOI, the expression of CD80, CD86, CD40, and MHC II was significantly inhibited when DCs were infected with rWT-N193S compared with the rWT infection group. The above results indicated that PA-X N193S mutation significantly inhibited the phenotypic maturation and immune function of DCs.

PA-X n193s mutation reduced DCs recruitment to the nasal submucosa by inhibiting the expression of CCL5

H5 subtype AIV mainly infects hosts via upper respiratory tract tissues like the nasal cavity. DCs, key in mucosal immunity against viruses, are vital for regulating host innate immune responses [27]. To investigate the mechanism underlying differences in DC recruitment, we collected nasal tissues from mice at 3 h.p.i. qRT-PCR analysis of the nasal mucosa revealed that the expression of the chemokine CCL5 was significantly higher in rWT-infected mice than in rWT-N193S-infected mice (Supplementary Figure 4, P < 0.01). We further developed a flow cytometry-based strategy (Figure 4A) to detect the CCL5 expression in nasal mucosal epithelial cells (CD326 + CD45−). Consistent with the qRT-PCR result, the expression level of CCL5 by flow cytometry was also significantly elevated in rWT-infected mice compared to the rWT-N193S group (Figure 4B and C, P < 0.01). PA-X n193s mutation reduced DCs' migration to the CLNs for suppressing cascade immune response of CD4+ T cells

When DCs mature, they can highly express chemokine receptor CCR7, which plays an important role in the process of DCs migrating to nearby draining lymph node [28]. To determine the effect of PA-X N193S mutation on the migration ability of DCs, the expression of CCR7 was detected by flow cytometry after infection with rWT and rWT-N193S. The expression of CCR7 was significantly downregulated after infection with rWT-N193S (Figure 5A and B, P < 0.05), which indicated that PA-X N193S mutation significantly inhibited the ability of H5N8 subtype AIV to activate DCs. Mature DCs can stimulate the proliferation of downstream T cells for extensive immune response. To test fully functional maturation of DCs, DCs were assessed for their ability to stimulate CD4+ T cells (Figure 5C). In comparison to the rWT infection group, the ability of rWT-N193S-infected DCs to stimulate the proliferation of CD4+ T cells was remarkably inhibited when the ratio of DCs to CD4+ T cells was 1:5 (Figure 5D and E), implying that the PA-X N193S mutation inhibited the proliferation of CD4+ T cells induced by infected DCs.

We next examined DC migration to cervical lymph nodes (CLNs) in vivo. Using the flow cytometric strategy shown in Figure 5F, we found that at 6 h.p.i., the number of DCs (CD11c + CCR7+) that had migrated to CLNs was significantly higher in rWT-N193S-infected mice than in rWT-infected mice (Figure 5G and H, P < 0.01). Given the predominance of CD11b+ DCs in the nasal mucosa, we specifically analysed the migration of this subtype (CD11c + CD11b + CCR7+) to CLNs. The results demonstrated that rWT-infected mice had significantly more CD11b+ DCs in CLNs (Figure 5I and J, P < 0.01).

In summary, the PA-X N193S mutation disrupts early mucosal immunity by attenuating CCL5-dependent recruitment of CD11b+ DCs to the nasal mucosa and then inhibiting their migration to draining lymph nodes to suppress the cascade immune response of CD4+ T cells. This altered DC trafficking pattern demonstrates a dual mechanism of immune modulation that contributes to the attenuated yet distinct immune response observed with the mutant virus.

PA-X n193s mutation inhibited IL-17/MAPK signalling pathway to impair the immune function of DCs

To further elucidate the molecular mechanism by which the PA-X N193S mutation regulated the immune function of DCs, we performed the RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) on DCs infected with two recombinant viruses. The top 10 most significant pathways from the KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were presented, which suggested that the PA-X N193S mutation might influence changes in DC immune function by regulating the IL-17 signalling pathway (Figure 6A). We then screened and analysed the significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs) within the IL-17 signalling pathway. As shown in Figure 6B, the top 10 most significantly altered DEGs in the IL-17 pathway showed that compared to the rWT infection group, the expression of these DEGs was significantly downregulated after rWT-N193S infection. Further analysis revealed that these top DEGs were predominantly enriched in the MAPK signalling pathway, which is a crucial pathway for regulating innate immune functions within the IL-17 signalling pathway [29]. The IL-17/MAPK pathway served as a core switch enabling DCs to execute rapid, potent, and coordinated innate immune responses at mucosal and skin barrier sites [30]. Its activation also promoted the migration of DCs to lymph nodes, thereby bridging innate and adaptive immunity [31]. We subsequently validated the expression of chemokines and cytokines in these top ten DEGs by qPCR. The results showed that, compared to the rWT group, the rWT-N193S infection group downregulated the expression of chemokines, including CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL5, and cytokines, including COX2, IL-1β, and TNF-α, associated with the IL-17/MAPK signalling pathway, consistent with the result of RNA-seq (Supplementary Figure 5, P < 0.05). We next assessed the activation status of the IL-17/MAPK pathway by detecting the phosphorylation levels of JNK, ERK1/2, and p38. The results demonstrated that infection with rWT-N193S significantly inhibited the activation of the IL-17/MAPK pathway within DCs, as evidenced by markedly reduced phosphorylation levels of JNK and p38 (Figure 6C and D, P < 0.01). The IL-17/MAPK pathway can critically influence DC maturation and activation by regulating the secretion of key polarizing cytokines, such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [29]. These cytokines constituted the essential signal s that derived the phenotypic and functional transition of DCs from an antigen-capturing state to a fully activated, antigen-presenting state capable of priming T-cell responses [29, 32]. The expression of cytokines by ELISA showed that, compared to the rWT group, the rWT-N193S infection group significantly downregulated the expression of these cytokines including IL-17, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 (Figure 6E, P < 0.05).

Collectively, these results indicated that the PA-X N193S mutation significantly impaired the innate immune function of DCs by inhibiting the IL-17/MAPK signalling pathway and downregulating the expression of key cytokines.

Discussion

The global circulation of H5 subtype AIVs, particularly the clade 2.3.4.4b H5N8 viruses, continued to pose a significant threat to poultry and public health due to its high pathogenicity and zoonotic potential [1, 10]. Therefore, understanding the molecular determinants of its virulence and host adaptation was crucial. The PA-X protein served as a key virulence modulator in influenza virus, regulated host shutoff activity, viral polymerase function, and immune responses [12, 13, 20]. A previous study has identified several mammalian-adaptive mutations in PA-X, including R195 K, K206R, and P210L that altered viral pathogenicity [33]. Our study demonstrated that the PA-X N193S mutation has risen to dominance in H5 subtype AIVs, especially within the currently circulating clade 2.3.4.4b lineage. However, the functional impact of the prevalent PA-X N193S mutation in contemporary H5N8 viruses remained unclear. A potential limitation of this study is the temporal gap between our backbone virus (A/goose/Eastern China/CZ/2013, CZ) and contemporary clade 2.3.4.4b viruses that predominantly carry the PA-X N193S mutation. The CZ strain belongs to early clade 2.3.4.4b Group I, while current N193S-carrying isolates mostly fall into Group II [23]. However, the PA gene segment of CZ shares >85% nucleotide identity with recent Group II isolates and contains the ancestral 193N residue, making it a suitable platform for functional assessment of the N193S mutation. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that other lineage-specific mutations accumulated in contemporary viruses may modulate the observed phenotype. Future studies using more recent backbone viruses or introducing the N193S mutation into multiple clade 2.3.4.4b isolates would further validate the generality of our findings. We also noticed the amino acid at PA-X position 195 is arginine (R195), corresponding to the avian-like residue rather than the mammalian-adaptive K195 mutation previously identified in H7N9 and H1N1/2009 viruses [33]. Future studies constructing double mutants including N193S and R195 K in different backbone backgrounds would help determine whether synergistic or antagonistic interactions exist between these two positions.

A key outcome of this work was the specific impact of the PA-X N193S mutation on DC-mediated innate immunity. HPAIV primarily infected the host through the respiratory mucosa, where the mucosal immune system, serving as the primary defense barrier, frequently encounters the virus [34]. Within nasal-associated lymphoid tissues, DCs acted as vigilant sentinels, patrolling beneath the mucosal epithelium. Upon invasion of pathogenic microorganisms like influenza viruses, DCs promptly gathered beneath the epithelium, migrated to the epithelial cell layer, and extended into the lumen to capture antigens [35]. In contrast, macrophages resided locally within the mucosa, making it difficult for them to phagocytose pathogens and migrate rapidly to draining lymph nodes to orchestrate a broader immune response, as DCs can [36, 37]. DCs possessed robust phagosomes, endosomes, and enzyme systems, allowing them to rapidly engulf, internalize, and process exogenous pathogenic microorganisms [38]. Therefore, DCs played a crucial role in the mucosal immune response against viruses, such as AIV, that invaded mucosal surfaces. Infection with rWT-N193S inhibited DC maturation, as indicated by the downregulated expression of surface markers and key activation/migration molecules. This impaired maturation state was accompanied by reduced secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Importantly, this DC dysfunction translated into a diminished capacity to stimulate CD4+ T cell proliferation in vitro. The immunomodulatory effect extends to the early mucosal immune response in vivo. The N193S mutation attenuated the initial antiviral response in the nasal mucosa by reducing the epithelial production of the key chemokine CCL5. This led to decreased recruitment of CD11b+ DCs to the nasal submucosa, subsequently, to a reduced migration of these DCs to the draining cervical lymph nodes. This disruption in early DC trafficking and priming likely contributes to the overall attenuated immune response observed against the mutant virus.

PA-X was demonstrated to be a negative virulence regulator and decreased the virulence of HPAIV by inhibiting viral replication and the host innate immune response [13]. Our study demonstrated that the PA-X N193S mutation further exacerbated the suppression of the immune response of DCs, thus reducing the virulence of HPAIV by suppressing a cascading cytokine storm. HPAIV has been closely associated with its ability to induce excessive and dysregulated host immune responses, commonly referred to as a cytokine storm [39]. Notably, DCs are not only the most important antigen presenting cells for antigen-specific immune responses, but also play a leading role in regulating the cytokine storm [22]. In this context, the PA-X N193S mutation-mediated suppression of DC activation and subsequent downregulation of cytokine production represents a strategic viral adaptation that reduces excessive inflammation while preserving host viability, thereby promoting viral persistence and evolutionary fitness in the host population. Highly pathogenic viruses enhance their adaptability within the host by reducing virulence through site mutations during evolution [40], which is also reported in AIV [41].

The IL-17 pathway was a pivotal regulator of mucosal immunity, orchestrating rapid innate immune responses and facilitating the transition to adaptive immunity by promoting DC maturation, cytokine production, and migratory capacity [29, 31]. Specifically, IL-17 signalling activated the MAPK pathway by inducing phosphorylation of p38, JNK, and ERK1/2, which in turn derived the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines and upregulated critical markers for DC activation [32, 42]. In this study, we demonstrated that the PA-X N193S mutation significantly inhibited the phosphorylation of p38 and JNK. This suppression mechanistically accounted for the DC dysfunction by impairing MAPK activation, which reduced the synthesis of cytokines crucial for DC autocrine/paracrine signalling and T cell proliferation, ultimately blunting DC maturation and the initiation of immune responses [43, 44].

A seemingly paradoxical observation emerged from our study: the PA-X N193S mutation enhanced host shutoff activity, yet the mutant virus exhibited attenuated pathogenicity in vivo. This paradox can be resolved by considering the unique pathogenesis of H5 subtype HPAIVs. Unlike low pathogenic influenza viruses, H5N1 viruses cause severe disease and mortality primarily through inducing an excessive cytokine storm rather than solely through viral replication levels [39]. DCs are central regulators of this cytokine storm [22]. Our data demonstrate that the PA-X N193S mutation significantly suppresses DC immune functions, including maturation, activation, and the IL-17/MAPK signalling pathway, leading to reduced production of key pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-17. While enhanced host shutoff might theoretically favour viral replication, its dominant in vivo consequence in the context of H5N1 infection is the dampening of DC-driven cytokine storm. Because the cytokine storm is a major determinant of H5N1 mortality, its attenuation results in reduced tissue damage, less severe immunopathology, and lower overall pathogenicity. Thus, the PA-X N193S mutation represents a viral adaptation that trades off direct replicative capacity for subversion of DC-mediated inflammation, ultimately reducing virulence while potentially favouring host survival and viral persistence.

Our data also revealed a cell-type-specific strategy of the PA-X N193S mutant: attenuated replication in epithelial cells contrasted with enhanced infectivity in DCs. This dichotomy suggested a sophisticated viral trade-off. This cell-type-specific phenotype may be explained by cell-type-specific regulation of the IL-17/MAPK signalling axis. The IL-17 pathway is known to be a key regulator of DC function [29] and by the fact that MAPK signalling in response to influenza virus activation, function, and pathophysiological consequences are known to differ greatly between different cell types [45]. Such differences could determine why the mutant virus preferentially suppresses p38/JNK in DCs while having less impact on ERK, and why epithelial cells, which lack the same IL-17/MAPK-mediated immune control machinery, are more permissive to wild-type replication. We proposed that reduced replication in respiratory epithelia minimized early cytopathic damage and excessive innate immune activation, potentially prolonging host infectiousness, which was a known virulence-adaptation trade-off [46, 47]. Conversely, the increased uptake by DCs enabled targeted immune subversion. Within DCs, the mutation’s heightened host shutoff and suppression of the IL-17/MAPK pathway crippled DC maturation, cytokine production, and migration, effectively disarming the host’s immune command centre [16, 34]. Thus, the virus appeared to divide its labor: lowering systemic virulence while deploying a “Trojan horse” strategy to incapacitate mucosal DCs. This dual approach through curtailing immunopathology while selectively evading frontline immunity likely optimized host adaptation [48], explaining the dominance of PA-X N193S mutation in clade 2.3.4.4b H5N8 viruses.

In conclusion, this study elucidated a critical mechanism by which the emerging PA-X N193S mutation modulates the pathogenesis of H5N8 avian influenza virus (Figure 7). The mutation attenuates general viral fitness and cytokine-driven pathogenicity while specifically disarming the dendritic cell arm of the host's innate and adaptive immune response. These insights underscore the importance of continuous surveillance of PA-X evolution and highlight DC-mediated immunity as a key determinant of H5N8 pathogenicity. Materials and methods

Ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals by the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People’s Republic of China. The Administrative Committee for Laboratory Animals of Yangzhou University affiliated with Jiangsu Province (Permission number: SYXKSU-2023–0089) approved all of the animal studies according to the guidelines of Jiangsu Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics of Jiangsu Administrative Committee of Laboratory Animals. All animal euthanasia and anesthesia procedures were performed in accordance with the Guidelines of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA). All experiments involving HPAIVs were performed in the authorized animal biosafety level 3 (ABSL-3) facilities at Yangzhou University.

Virus and cells

H5N8 subtype AIV (A/goose/Eastern China/CZ/2013) (CZ) was isolated and stored in our laboratory. Madin-Darby canine kidney cells (MDCK) and adenocarcinoma human alveolar basal epithelial cells (A549) were purchased from ATCC and cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37°C and 5% CO2. Chicken embryo fibroblast cells (CEF) were prepared from 10-day-old specific pathogen-free (SPF) embryonated chicken eggs (Zhejiang Lihua Agricultural Science and Technology Co., Ltd) and cultured in M199 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 4% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) at 37°C and 5% CO2. Mouse bone marrow-derived DCs were isolated from 4-week-old female C57BL/6 mice purchased from the Comparative Medicine Center of Yangzhou University according to our reported method [22]. DCs were cultured in the complete medium containing RPMI 1640 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco), 10 ng/ml IL-4, and GM-CSF (Peprotech) at 37°C and 5% CO2. After culturing for 60 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Mature DCs with a purity greater than 90% were harvested on the fifth day and used in subsequent experiments.

Virus rescue

The plasmid of the PA gene from the CZ strain was used as a template, and the sequence encoding the PA-X ORF frame was mutated using a point mutation kit to obtain the PA gene with the PA-X N193S mutation. The primer sequences were as follows: (F: TTTCGTCAGTCCGAAAGAGGCGAAGAAACAAT, R: CTTTCGGACTGACGAAAGGAATCCCATAGGCC). Based on the previous establishment of reverse genetic technology for AIVs [49], the wild-type virus and the recombinant virus with the PA-X N193S mutation were successfully rescued using the eight-plasmid system after amplification of plasmids for the other seven gene fragments of the CZ strain, and were named rWT and rWT-N193S, respectively.

Animal experiments

6-week-old female BALB/c mice (n = 5/group, purchased from Experimental Animal Center of Yangzhou University) were intranasally inoculated with 101–106 EID50 of rWT of rWT-N193S in 50 μL PBS to determine the 50% murine lethal dose (MLD50) by recording survival and body weight for 14 days. 6-week-old female BALB/c mice were infected intranasally with 200 μL 104 EID50 of virus diluted by PBS. Lung tissues from each group of mice were collected at 5 d.p.i. to detect pathological changes. Lungs of mice from each group were collected at 3 and 5 d.p.i. to detect the viral replication by quantitative reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR). The expression of cytokines in the lungs, including IFN-β, IL-8, IL-1β, IL-6, IL-12, and TNF-α, was detected by qRT-PCR at 5 d.p.i. The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR were listed in Table 1. At 3 and 5 d.p.i., mice were euthanized. Lungs and nasal tissues were collected aseptically. Each sample was homogenized in 1 mL of PBS containing 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin using a tissue homogenizer. After centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, the supernatant was collected and serially diluted 10-fold in PBS. For each dilution, 200 μL was inoculated into the allantoic cavity of five 10-day-old SPF embryonated chicken eggs. The eggs were incubated at 37°C for 72 h and then chilled at 4°C overnight. Allantoic fluid was harvested, and the titre of virus was determined by hemagglutination (HA) assay using 1% chicken red blood cells. The EID50 was calculated according to the Reed-Muench method [50]. Viral titres in lung and nasal tissues were expressed as log10 EID50 per 0.1 g of tissue. Host shutoff assay

The eGFP plasmid was transfected with PA-X or PA-X N193S into 293 T cells for 24 h. The expression of eGFP was detected by fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry to analyse the host shutoff activity of PA-X and PA-X N193S. The plasmid expressing PA-X or PA-X N193S was constructed and transfected into 293 T cells respectively. 293 T cells were collected at 24 h.p.i. to detect the expression of PABPC1 by western blot.

Luciferase assay

The polymerase activity of the viruses was detected using a dual-luciferase assay as previously described [51]. 293 T cells cultured on a 12-well plate were co-transfected with 200 ng/well of pcDNA3.1 encoding PB1, PB2, PA (from rWT)/PA (from rWT-N193S), and NP genes and a Firefly luciferase reporter plasmid (p-Luci), or an empty plasmid (stored in our laboratory), together with 20 ng/well of an internal control Renilla plasmid (pTK-RL). After 24 h, luciferase activity was determined using the dual luciferase assay system kit (Vazyme, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Polymerase activity was calculated as relative luciferase activity, which is presented as the ratio of firefly luciferase activity to Renilla luciferase activity. The results are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments.

Virus growth curve

To evaluate the effect of the PA-X N193S mutation on the replication capacity of viral cell lines in vitro, monolayer CEF, MDCK, and A549 cells were infected with the virus at a dose of 0.1 MOI in DMEM or M199, then cultured in maintenance medium at 37°C and 5% CO2. The supernatant samples were collected to determine the median tissue culture infective dose (TCID50) on CEF cells at 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, or 72 h.p.i. TCID50 was calculated as previously described by Reed and Muench [52].

Detection of DCs phenotype, activation, and migration

The collected DCs were detected by flow cytometry for uptake, phenotype, and maturation. DCs were stained with the various fluorescent mAbs, including FITC-NP, PE-CD80, FITC-CD86, PE-CD40, FITC-MHCII, Percp-Cy5.5-CD69, PE-Cy7-CCR7, or isotypes (BD Bioscience), and then detected by flow cytometry. Data were analysed with FlowJo software (Tree Star).

Mixed lymphocyte reaction

Stimulatory capacity of DCs to T cells was reflected in the mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR) assay as previously described [35]. Spleens were aseptically collected from naïve BALB/c mice (6-8 weeks old), and a single-cell suspension was prepared by gently pressing the tissue through a 70-μm cell strainer. Red blood cells were lysed with ACK lysis buffer. CD4+ T cells were then purified from the splenocytes using the CD4+ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Purified CD4+ T cells were labelled with 5 μM carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE, Invitrogen) in PBS for 10 min at 37°C in the dark, followed by quenching with ice-cold RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum. After three washes with PBS, the CFSE-labelled T cells were resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 medium. Then the cells as responder cells were cocultured in duplicate with viral infected DCs (DC: T cell ratios of 1:5). After 5 days of co-culture at 37°C with 5% CO2, cells were harvested and the dilution of CFSE fluorescence was detected by flow cytometry. CD4+ T cell proliferation was assessed by the dilution of CFSE fluorescence in the CD4+-gated population.

Detection of DCs recruitment under the nasal mucosal epithelium in vivo

C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were intranasally inoculated with recombinant viruses rWT or rWT-N193S at 106 EID50/50 μL for 1 h or 3 h, and then euthanized. The nasal epithelial cells were isolated as described previously [17]. The individual cells were isolated by Type XIV protease (0.5%), Type IV collagenase (0.1%) and hyaluronidase (0.1%) (Sigma–Aldrich) at 37°C for 0.5 h, and then were passed through a 100-µm cell strainer. The achieved epithelial cells were incubated with APC-Cy7-FVS-780, Percp-Cy5.5-CD45, APC-CD326, PE-CCL5, APC-CD11c, PE-Cy7-CD11b, PE-CD103 and their respective isotypes (BD Bioscience) at 4°C in the dark for 30 min, washed twice with PBS and analysed by flow cytometry.

Cryosection and confocal microscopy

C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were intranasally inoculated with recombinant viruses rWT or rWT-N193S at 106 EID50/50 μL for 3 h, and then euthanized. Briefly, after removing excess tissues, the nasal cavity was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Sangon Biotech) overnight, decalcified in decalcification solution (10% EDTA, pH = 7.4) for 7 days and frozen in embedding medium (OCT, California, USA). Next, cryosections (8 µm) were cut and processed for immunofluorescence below. Cryosections were permeabilized in 0.2% Triton X-100 (MP Biomedicals) for 5 min, then blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h, then incubated with primary antibodies including anti-CD11c mAb anti-CD11b, and anti-CCL5 pAb (Abcam) overnight at 4°C. After washing three times, Fluorescent IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 649, Alexa Fluor 594, and Alexa Fluor 488) (Abcam) was incubated for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, Cryosections were incubated with 2-(4-Amidinophenyl)−6-indolecarbamidine dihydrochloride (DAPI, Abcam) at room temperature for 5 min. After washing, cryosections were performed by a Leica TCS SP8 STED confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems).

Detection of DCs migration to the CLNs in vivo

C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were intranasally inoculated with recombinant viruses rWT or rWT-N193S at 106 EID50/50 μL for 6 h, and then euthanized. Then they were euthanized, and the cervical lymph nodes (CLNs) of mice were taken, ground with PBS, and passed through a 100-µm cell strainer [17]. The individual cells were incubated with APC-Cy7-FVS-780, APC-CD11c, PE-CCR7, PE-Cy7-CD11b, and their respective isotypes (BD Bioscience) at 4°C in the dark for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, and detected by flow cytometry.

Transcriptome analysis and validation

DCs were infected with viruses at a dose of 1 MOI for 12 h. DCs were collected and extracted for total RNA using the Trizol method. RNA sequencing and bioinformatic data collection were performed by Genedenovo. For the validation of transcriptome analysis, DCs were collected to detect the expression of DEGs in the IL17/MAPK signalling pathway by qRT-PCR. The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table 1. DCs were denatured by boiling in loading buffer (Cytiva) for 10 min, followed by the separation of the extracted proteins via SDS-PAGE. The separated proteins were then electrophoretically transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Cytiva). The membranes were blocked with blocking buffer (NcmBlot) and incubated with rabbit anti-p-ERK1/2, anti-ERK1/2, anti-p-p38, anti-p38, anti-p-JNK, and anti-JNK antibodies, or mouse anti-β-actin antibody (Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. After primary antibody incubation, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam) for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using an electrochemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Biobest).

Statistical analysis

The results were expressed as the means ± standard deviations (SD) and analysed with GraphPad Prism 6 software (SanDiego). Normality and homogeneity of variances were confirmed for all datasets before performing parametric tests. Unpaired Student’s two-sided t-tests were used to determine the statistical significance between the two groups. One-way ANOVA was used to compare the variance between different groups by using SPSS 17.0. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01.
