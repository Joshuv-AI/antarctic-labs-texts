Effect of chronic heat stress on the carbonylation of glycolytic enzymes in breast muscle and its correlation with the growth performance of broilers
Authors: Yingsen Liu, Zhen Liu, Tong Xing, Jiaolong Li, Lin Zhang, Liang Zhao, Feng Gao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic heat stress has detrimental effects on the growth performance of broilers, and the potential mechanism is under exploration. In this study, the protein carbonyl modification was introduced to glycolytic enzymes to evaluate its relationship with the growth performance of heat-stressed (HS) broilers. A total of 144 male 28-day-old broilers were assigned to 3 treatments: the normal control group (NC, raised at 22°C with free access to feed and water), the HS group (raised at 32°C with free access to feed and water), and the pair-fed group (PF, raised at 22°C with an amount of feed equal to that consumed by the HS group on a previous day). Results showed that heat stress decreased the average daily growth, increased the feed-to-gain ratio (F/G), decreased breast muscle rate, and increased abdominal fat rate compared with the NC and PF groups (P < 0.05). Higher cloacal temperature and serum creatine kinase activity were found in the HS group than those of the NC and PF groups (P < 0.05). Heat stress increased the contents of carbonyl, advanced glycation end-products, malonaldehyde, and the activities of catalase, glutathione peroxidase, and total antioxidant capacity compared with the NC and PF groups (P < 0.05). Heat stress increased the contents of glucose and lactate, declined the glycogen content, and lowered the relative protein expressions of pyruvate kinase muscle type, lactate dehydrogenase A type (LDHA), and citrate synthase compared to those of the NC group (P < 0.05). In contrast to the NC and PF groups, heat stress intensified the carbonylation levels of phosphoglucomutase 1, triosephosphate isomerase 1, β-enolase, and LDHA, which were positively correlated with the F/G (P < 0.05). These findings demonstrate that heat stress depresses growth performance on account of oxidative stress and glycolysis disorders. It further increases the carbonylation of glycolytic enzymes, which potentially correlates with the F/G by disturbing the mode of energy supply of broilers.Key words: chronic heat stress, growth performance, carbonylation, glycolytic enzyme, broiler

INTRODUCTION

The poultry industry is currently experiencing a growing obstacle due to the rise in ambient temperatures caused by global warming. Broilers are vulnerable to heat exhaustion due to their inability to sweat and the hindrance of heat dissipation caused by their feathers. As the ambient temperature exceeds the thermoneutral zone of birds, they are compelled to disperse internal heat into the environment by convection (e.g., spreading wings) and evaporative cooling (e.g., panting, gular fluttering, and excretion) (Saeed et al., 2019). Broilers are susceptible to chronic heat stress as a result of prolonged heat exposure since they bear a negative equilibrium in releasing internal energy in terms of energy produced by the system (Goel, 2021). Heat stress is known to reduce production performance, muscle mass, and economic efficiency, while the precise mechanisms and relationships involved remain largely unknown (Zhang et al., 2012; Al-Sagan et al., 2020; Park et al., 2022).

Earlier research demonstrated that broilers exposed to heat stress showed a time-dependent energy bioavailability, with glucose shifting from aerobic metabolism to glycolysis to fulfill the energy demands over the course of a 14-day heat exposure (Lu et al., 2017). In addition, the heat-stressed (HS) birds exhibit incremental fat deposition and glycogenesis, which suggests that they are in a metabolically inflexible state and prefer glucose as the fuel to generate ATP in an inefficient manner (Baumgard and Rhoads, 2013; Nawaz et al., 2021). This stumbling block is generally attributed to the oxidative stress triggered by heat stress, as Lu et al. (2017) postulated the accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) under heat stress mainly lies in the downregulation of the expression of avian uncoupling protein. Overproduction of ROS is intertwined with mitochondrial dysfunction, which further blocks pyruvate from entering the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Belhadj et al., 2016; Emami et al., 2021b). This argument sheds light on the way ROS affects glucose metabolism. However, the potential influence of the reactive carbonyl species (RCS) derived from ROS remains underappreciated.

RCS is a category of chemical compounds with 1 or more functional carbonyl groups that are extremely electrophilic and active (e.g., aldehydes and ketones). They are largely produced by glycation, lipid peroxidation, and amino acid oxidation and are prone to triggering carbonyl stress (Tian and Zhen, 2019). RCS has the ability to nonenzymatically add carbonyl moieties to proteins, allowing the targeted proteins to alter in both function and conformation (Akagawa, 2021). Several approaches to protein carbonylation are explored. Proteins can be directly oxidized by free radicals at their backbones and side chains through the Fenton reaction. They may also be formed by Michael-type addition reactions with lipid peroxidase to produce advanced lipoxidation end-products (ALEs) or by nonenzymatic glycosylation to produce Amadori and Heyns products, which further generate advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) (Akagawa, 2021). Nonetheless, the rule of carbonyl modification of glucose metabolic enzymes and its relationship with the growth performance of heat-challenged broilers remain ambiguous. In this study, the carbonylation rule of glycolytic enzymes in the breast muscle is examined in order to acquire a novel perspective on its potential impact on the growth performance of broilers under HS conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Birds and Experimental Design

All experimental protocols adhered to the ethical guidelines that were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Agricultural University (approved protocol number: SYXK 2021-0086). A total of 144 male Arbor Acres broilers aged 28 d with an average weight of 1.47 ± 0.02 kg were purchased from Changfeng poultry hatchery (Suqian, China), thereafter randomly assigned to 1 of 3 treatments. The birds in the normal control (NC) group were kept at a temperature of 22°C with unlimited access to feed and water. The birds in the HS group were kept at a temperature of 32°C with unlimited access to feed and water. Those in the pair-fed (PF) group were kept at a temperature of 22°C with an amount of feed equal to that consumed by the HS group on a previous day. Eight birds were housed in each of the 6 duplicate cages in each treatment (1.2 m × 0.8 m × 0.45 m). The composition and nutritional values of the basic diet were developed in accordance with the recommendation of the National Research Council (1994) and are summarized in Table 1. The lighting schedule followed a 23-h cycle of light and a 1-h cycle of darkness throughout the entire period. A steady 55 to 60% relative humidity level was maintained in the atmosphere. The trial was held for 14 d.

Growth Performance and Carcass Characteristics

The body weights of broilers were recorded in replicate cages at the beginning and end of this trial. Simultaneously, the feed consumption for the whole period of the NC group was recorded. The feed intakes of the HS and PF groups were strictly weighed each day. The average daily growth (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed-to-gain ratio (F/G) were calculated in accordance. Twelve broilers from each group, with 2 birds weighing close to the mean in each pen, were selected and weighed on d 14. The birds were then instantly killed by exsanguination on the left jugular vein after being stunned with carbon dioxide. Subsequently, the birds were defeathered in hot water, then dissected and weighed to determine the dressing percentage, yield of semi- and eviscerated carcasses, and the percentages of breast muscle and abdominal fat following the procedures provided by Zhang et al. (2019).

Cloacal Temperature

The cloacal temperature was recorded by measuring 2 birds from each cage. Specifically, the birds in a quiet state were individually selected and gently taken out in succession. A digital thermometer (accurate to 0.1°C) was used to measure the temperature of subjects by inserting about 2 cm into the rectum. The temperatures were recorded on d 1, 3, 7, and 14 in this trial.

Serum Parameters

Blood samples were drawn from the jugular vein, placed in nonheparinized tubes, and centrifuged at 3,500 rpm at 4°C for 10 min. The serum samples were stored at −20°C. The serum corticosterone content (CORT, #H205-1-1) and creatine kinase activity (CK, #A032-1-1) were measured using commercial kits that were bought from Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China).

Biochemical Assays for Breast Muscle

A piece of de-epidermal muscle tissue measuring roughly 3 cm × 3 cm × 1 cm was separated from the middle-upper part of the right pectoralis major. The tissues were collected in cryotubes and immediately frozen at −80°C for preservation. For further investigation, samples were homogenized with ice-cold normal saline (0.75%, w/v) at a ratio of 1:9 (w/v) and centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 10 min at 4°C to collect the supernatant. The levels of ROS (#E004-1-1), carbonyl (#A087-1-2), AGEs (#H250-1-1), malondialdehyde (MDA, #A003-1-1), glucose (#F006-1-1), and lactate (#A019-2-1), in addition to the activities of total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, #A015-1-2), total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD, #A001-1-1), catalase (CAT, #A007-1-1), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-PX, #A005-1-2), were measured using assay kits (Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). The protein concentration was determined simultaneously for standardization using the Micro BCA protein assay kit (#C503061, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). For the determination of glycogen content, 75 mg of muscle samples were chipped and hydrolyzed with lye at 1:3 (w/v), then boiled for 20 min to prepare the testing solution. The following steps were conducted on the basis of manufacture instruction (#A043-1-1, Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Protein Extraction and Western Blot Analysis

Muscle samples were mixed with RIPA lysis buffer (#P0013B, Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China, 1:9, w/v) containing 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 0.8 μM aprotinin, and 0.015 μM pepstatin, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. Once the supernatant was gathered, the Micro BCA protein assay kit (#C503061, Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) was used to determine the protein concentrations. The concentrations were then standardized to 4 ug/μL by utilizing RIPA lysis buffer. Following this, the supernatants were combined with 5 × loading buffer at a ratio of 4:1 (v/v) and then heated at 100°C for 5 min. The samples containing 20 μg protein were separated in 10% SDS polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and then transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Thereafter, the membranes were blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) dissolved in Tris-buffered saline with 0.05% Tween-20 (TBST). The primary antibodies against the target proteins included hexokinase 2 (HK2, #22029-1-AP), phosphofructokinase muscle type (PFKM, #55028-1-AP), fructose bisphosphate aldolase C (ALDOC, #14884-1-AP), triosephosphate isomerase 1 (TPI1, #10713-1-AP), pyruvate kinase muscle type (PKM2, #15822-1-AP), and citrate synthase (CS, #16131-1-AP), which were obtained from Proteintech (Rosemont, IL). In addition to lactate dehydrogenase A type (LDHA, #A1146), phosphoglucomutase 1 (PGM1, #A19904), and β-enolase (ENO3, #A3852), which were obtained from ABclonal Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The primary antibodies were applied to incubate membranes at the prescribed dilutions at 4°C overnight. The secondary anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated antibody (#7074, Cell Signaling Technology Inc., Beverly, MA) was used to incubate membranes with a dilution of 1:3,000 for 1 h at room temperature after being washed by TBST for 5 times. Finally, the blots were captured by the Tanon-3900 Chemiluminescent Imaging System (Tanon Science & Technology, Shanghai, China) after they had reacted with ECL Plus detection reagents (#E411-05, Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). The relative intensities of target proteins were normalized by β-actin (#81115-1-RR, Proteintech, Rosemont, IL).

Immunoprecipitation Analysis

Approximately 2 μg specifically targeted primary antibodies and 20 μL protein A/G (#sc-2003, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Inc., Dallas, TX) were successively added to the produced supernatants containing 1.5 mg protein for overnight incubation at 4°C. The immunoprecipitates were harvested by centrifuging at 2,500 rpm for 5 min and discarding the supernatant. After being washed 4 times with RIPA buffer, the precipitates were resuspended in 40 μL of 1 × loading buffer and boiled at 95°C for 5 min prior to Western blotting. The preparation of each sample for electrophoresis and transmembrane was done twice. In the following steps, 1 was incubated with 2 N HCl for 5 min and a 0.5 mM 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine solution for another 5 min in the dark. The membrane was sequentially washed by 2 N HCl for 3 times and 100% methanol for 5 times with 5 min each. Then it was incubated with rabbit antidinitrophenyl antibody (#D9656, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) diluted at 1:1,500 in 5% BSA in TBST overnight at 4°C after being blocked with a 5% BSA-TBST solution. Another replicate, which was used as the input, was immediately blocked with a 5% BSA-TBST solution before a specific primary antibody was added and incubated at 4°C overnight, along with subsequent incubation with a secondary anti-rabbit HRP-conjugated antibody (#7074, Cell Signaling Technology Inc., Beverly, MA). A chemiluminescent immunoassay was implemented to compare the blot intensity of the 2 replicates. The input blot intensity was used to normalize the amount of carbonylation of targeted proteins.

Statistical Analysis

The data were analyzed by 1-way analysis of variance using SPSS statistical software (version 20.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL). Duncan's multiple range tests were performed to examine the differences among the 3 treatments. The mean of 2 birds in each pen was taken as the replicate value (n = 6), except for the replicate value taken from the mean of 4 birds from 2 cages in immunoprecipitation analysis (n = 3). The results were presented as mean values with a standard error. The differences were judged statistically significant at P < 0.05. The Pearson coefficient and hierarchical cluster analysis were performed in heatmap using the pheatmap package of R software (Version 4.1.2).

DISCUSSION

Modern broilers are vulnerable to high ambient temperatures. Aslam et al. (2021) and Zampiga et al. (2021) illustrated that broilers exposed to HS had decreased body weight gain, decreased feed intake, and a poor feed conversion ratio during grower and finisher periods. Our earlier research showed that elevated temperature reduced broiler feed consumption, and attributed this issue to decreased orexigenic neuropeptide Y expression in the hypothalamus (He et al., 2019). Although there was a lower heat increase, the growth performance was hampered by the reduced feed intake. Zuo et al. (2015) found that the ADG and feed conversion ratio of the HS group were significantly impaired compared with the PF group based on the same feed intake. Similar results were found in this trial. This indicates that heat exposure encompasses an adverse effect on growth performance apart from the decreased feed intake.

HS damages the economic value of broilers by lowering the breast muscle yield (Ma et al., 2018; Teyssier et al., 2022a). Emami et al. (2021a) observed that cyclically HS broilers had reduced breast yield and greater leg and wing yields than controls, which may be ascribed to the reallocation of nutrients to the extremities as a heat-dissipating mechanism. Moreover, the breast meat from broilers exposed to a constant high temperature had lower protein synthesis and higher fat deposition (Zhang et al., 2012). As found in this trial, HS caused a reduction in breast yield and an increase in abdominal fat percentage compared to those of the NC and PF groups. It is postulated that the protein reservoirs of breast muscle are mobilized to enhance the glucogenic potential, which compensates for the inability to extract energy from stored fat (Teyssier et al., 2022b).

Cloacal temperature is employed to assess chicken heat tolerance and performance (Chen et al., 2013). It was reported by Lu et al. (2019) that HS broilers had a greater cloacal temperature than the thermoneutral group, which was consistent with our findings. Chronic stress stimulates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and pituitary gland to secrete CORT. Excess circulating CORT in broilers inhibits growth rate and increases proteolysis in muscle, which features increased energy expenditure with enhanced gluconeogenesis and protein oxidation during HS (Dong et al., 2007). Additionally, serum CK activity is recognized as a diagnostic marker of damaged muscle (Brancaccio et al., 2010). In this study, an increased serum CK activity was found in the HS group compared with the NC and PF groups, which may be linked to the oxidative damage of breast muscle.

Hyperthermia may aggravate an unbalanced oxidative state by increasing the levels of critical ROS, such as O2·−, H2O2, and HO·− (Slimen et al., 2014). Accordingly, the formation of AGEs is accelerated via the covalent binding of proteins with reactive aldehydes that are created by the oxidation of sugars, lipids, and amino acids (Uribarri et al., 2015). MDA is the oxilipin carbonyl with α,β-unsaturated bond and could be decomposed from lipid peroxides that developed during HS (Mano, 2012). In this study, HS increased the amounts of carbonyl, AGEs, and MDA in breast muscle, implying that HS broilers experienced enhanced muscular carbonyl stress. Previous studies demonstrated that high environmental temperature contributed to dysregulated antioxidant system and chaotic enzyme activities (Huang et al., 2015; Liu et al., 2022). It is proposed that oxidative stress affects antioxidant enzyme activity differently depending on its duration and intensity (Garaschuk et al., 2018). Herein, the activities of CAT and GSH-Px in breast muscle were enhanced by HS compared with the NC and PF groups, suggesting that birds underwent eustress with upregulated antioxidant capacity induced by RCS. In addition to regulating antioxidant defense, RCS also has an impact on the structural stability and biological function of proteins controlling glucose metabolism (Curtis et al., 2012).

The breast muscle of broilers largely consists of type IIb fibers, which favor glycolysis for energy production (Petracci and Cavani, 2012). In spite of the decreased feed intake, glucose as the preferred fuel is influenced by the intensity and duration of heat stress (Belhadj et al., 2016). In contrast to a prior research showed that HS decreased the glucose level in the broiler breast muscle (Zampiga et al., 2021), we found a greater muscular glucose content in HS birds in this trial. The probable cause is the increased glycogenolysis generated by glycogen phosphorylase and gluconeogenesis from pyruvate, lactate, and amino acids, which are linked to excessive CORT secretion (Rui, 2014). Broilers subjected to heat experience metabolic inflexibility and fail to meet energy demands. Siddiqui et al. (2021) reported that broilers suffering from HS had depleted ATP storage and accelerated glycogen utilization. In this trial, broilers from the HS group contained lower glycogen content and higher lactate content in muscle than those from the NC group, which is consistent with Chang et al. (2020). These results indicate that the function of the enzymes participating in glycolysis was altered during HS.

Under heat exposure, there is an increased energy expenditure of breast muscle, which relies heavily on anaerobic glycolysis to produce ATP (Lu et al., 2017). Meanwhile, the activities of glycolytic enzymes are speculated to be modified by RCS under enhanced electron leakage during oxidative phosphorylation. In this research, the carbonylation degrees of PGM1, TPI1, ENO3, and LDHA were intensified by HS when compared with the NC and HS groups. PGM1 is the major evolutionary conserved enzyme in catalyzing glycogen catalysis. Its carbonylation by heat exposure may induce further dephosphorylation at peripheral residues via conformational change, which accelerates glycogenolysis (Doello et al., 2022). TPI is responsible for the net gain of glycolysis-derived ATP by catalyzing the interconversion between glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (GA3P) and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) (Olivares-Illana et al., 2017). It was noted by De Waal et al. (2013) that the function of TPI was activated after being insulted by massive protein carbonylation alteration. Since TPI is susceptible to the toxic methylglyoxal (an RCS derived from excessive DHAP), the activity of TPI is assumed to improve when broilers suffer from HS (Segal et al., 2019). The downstream ENO3 is the metal-ion-activated enzyme that regulates glucose metabolism in response to steady-state concentration fluctuations under stress conditions (Pancholi, 2001). An interesting observation was found by Gomes et al. (2006) that the methylglyoxal-modified enolase of yeast was reduced in its activity, which virtually had little impact on glycolytic flow. In contrast, there was increased production of lactate along with intensified carbonylated ENO3 in the HS group. Considering the spatial location of carbonylated residues in the folded structure of ENO3, further investigation of the link between the modification site and the catalytic activity is required. As the respiratory chain is disturbed by HS, LDHA enhances the reduction of pyruvate to lactate to provide NAD+ for the oxidation of GA3P and to stimulate glycolysis for producing ATP. In this study, the carbonylation level of LDHA was increased by heat exposure, possibly promoting the formation of active tetrameric conformations and expanding the binding domain for NADH substrate (Valvona et al., 2016). Therefore, it was assumed that carbonyl modification boosted the catalytic activity of glycolytic enzymes by modifying the original conformation of proteins.

In the present study, a higher F/G was observed in the broilers exposed to HS than those subjected to PF. This implied that HS resulted in a lower efficiency of protein deposition and insufficient energy supply, apart from the decreased feed intake. Zhang et al. (2017) reported that glycolysis progression was promoted when there was exhaustion of muscle ATP generated by mitochondrial oxidative respiration. CS is one of the key enzymes regulating glucose aerobic metabolism, the activity of which was found to be attenuated after 14 d of HS (Lu et al., 2017). This arguably mediates the decarbonylated CS that affected the F/G in this trial. Earlier research documented that the breast muscle of broilers in the constant high-temperature group had higher activities of PK and LDH and produced more lactate than those in the standard temperature group. This indicates an adverse impact of strengthened glycolic processes on growth performance (Zhang et al., 2012). In the present study, F/G was found to be positively correlated with the carbonylation levels of PGM1, TPI1, ENO3, and LDHA. Accordingly, we hypothesize that the carbonylation alteration of glycolytic enzymes tends to enhance their catalyzing activities, further impairing the growth performance by shifting the mode of energy supply of broilers under HS condition.

CONCLUSIONS

Broilers subjected to HS had impaired growth performance and carcass characteristics, accompanied by a high cloacal temperature and peripheral stress response. HS disturbed redox status, accelerated glycolysis progress, and further strengthened the carbonylation levels of glycolytic enzymes in breast muscle that were correlated with the increased F/G of HS broilers.
