3D-bioprinted mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes reprogram macrophages via PI3K/AKT to accelerate nerve regeneration
Authors: Tao Sun, Lixin Huang, Qin Sun, Yibo Zhao, Zhimin Wu, Xi Luo, Jun Sun, Mingyang Li, Qingyi Huo, Tao Xu, Hui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeripheral nerve injury (PNI) regeneration involves complex immunoregulatory mechanisms, particularly M2 macrophage‑mediated immunomodulation. Promoting macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype represents a potential therapeutic strategy. Exosomes can create favorable microenvironments for tissue regeneration, but their clinical application is often limited by low concentration and purity. Three‑dimensional‑bioprinted exosomes (3D‑Exos) have recently gained attention due to their enhanced concentration and purity. MethodsIn this study, 3D‑Exos were applied to treat PNI. The regenerative effects of 3D‑Exos were compared with conventionally produced exosomes (2D‑Exos) in vivo. In vitro experiments, RNA sequencing, and miRNA microarray analyses were performed to investigate the underlying mechanisms. The mechanism was further validated in vitro and in vivo.Results3D‑Exos significantly enhanced nerve regeneration after PNI and outperformed 2D‑Exos. In vitro studies revealed that 3D‑Exos promoted macrophages polarization toward the M2 macrophages. RNA sequencing indicated that the PI3K/AKT pathway was activated by 3D‑Exos, and miRNA microarray analysis identified miR‑26b‑5p within 3D‑Exos as the key mediator targeting PTEN to activate PI3K/AKT signaling. Subsequently, M2 macrophages facilitated Schwann cell migration, elongation, and myelination, thereby accelerating nerve regeneration. Inhibition of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway abolished the therapeutic effects of 3D-Exos on nerve regeneration after PNI.ConclusionsThis study elucidates a novel mechanism by which 3D‑Exos promote nerve regeneration through reprogramming macrophages polarization via the PI3K/AKT pathway. The work establishes a paradigm‑shifting therapeutic framework that integrates 3D-Exos with immunomodulatory strategies for the treatment of PNI.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07845-4.Keywords: Macrophages reprogramming, 3D-bioprinted mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes, PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, Peripheral nerve injury, Nerve regeneration

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) is common in daily life and clinical settings. Even mild cases may result in localized sensory and motor deficits, while more severe injuries can lead to complete loss of limb function, imposing a substantial burden on patients, families, and healthcare systems [1]. Currently, the primary treatment options for PNI include nerve anastomosis, autologous nerve grafting, and nerve transfer [2, 3]. However, only a minority of patients treated with these methods achieve satisfactory effects, and up to one-third of patients receive minimal benefit from surgical interventions [4].

Studies demonstrated the regenerative processes following PNI are intricately linked to immune regulation mechanisms [5–7], especially the immune response mediated by M2 macrophages. The macrophages involve in tissue reorganization and the maintenance of homeostasis [8]. Additionally, macrophages are key players in immunological responses and act as the first responders to injury. Study found that macrophages could delay axon degeneration and preserve function [7]. Following PNI, abundant macrophages derived from the blood are recruited to the injured nerve [9]. On the one hand, these macrophages phagocytize damaged cells and tissue debris, and secrete a variety of bioactive substances, creating a favorable environment for nerve regeneration and accelerating regeneration process. This has garnered significant attention. On the other hand, there is a substantial accumulation of M1 macrophages after PNI [10]. These cells secrete large amounts of inflammatory substances, which stimulate inflammatory responses and impede nerve regeneration. As a result, the regulation of macrophages reprogramming has become a focal point and challenge of PNI, specifically by inhibiting M1 polarization and promoting M2 polarization. While hydrogel-based therapeutic strategies, particularly drug-eluting hydrogel, have garnered considerable attention for their demonstrated potential in immunomodulation [11], they remain constrained by suboptimal biocompatibility profiles and a substantial translational chasm between preclinical investigations and clinical implementation. Concurrently, emerging research have identified exosomal components as novel biological regulators capable of orchestrating macrophages polarization dynamics, offering a paradigm shift in precision immune modulation through endogenous nanoscale mechanisms [12–18].

Exosomes are natural membrane vesicles secreted by various cells, including mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), which contain proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids [19]. As membrane-bound vesicles secreted by cells, exosomes play crucial roles in cellular communication, exerting multiple biological effects in various diseases, such as myocardial infarction, bone fracture [21], acute kidney injury [22]. Moreover, the aforementioned functions of the exosomes are deeply related to macrophages [23, 24]. However, their application are significantly limited by low concentration and purity. Consequently, newly developed 3D-bioprinted exosomes attract increasing attention for their high concentration and purity. Drawing from our initial studies on PNI, exosomes, and macrophages [25–27], 3D-bioprinted MSCs-derived exosomes (3D-Exos) were utilized to explore their effects on nerve regeneration after PNI, and investigate the underlying mechanism. Through this study, we aspire to expand the research horizons of exosomes and treatment of PNI.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

Schwann cells (SCs) and macrophage line RAW264.7 were purchased and cultured with DMEM in 37℃ incubator with 5% CO2. Human adipose-derived MSCs, obtained from the Chinese Academy of Sciences, were maintained in MSC culture medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. In case of RSC96 cells were cultured under oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) conditions, the cells were cultured under glucose free medium, 37℃, 1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94% N2.

Isolation and characterization of 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos

MSCs used for 2D-Exos extraction were cultured by conventional methods. The coaxial bioprinting technology was used for the cultivation of MSCs and the production of 3D-Exos. Consistent with our previous experimental protocol [28], MSCs used for 3D-Exos extraction were co-extruded with alginate bioink through a dual-channel coaxial nozzle, generating cell-laden microfibers. Subsequent to microfiber fabrication, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free medium and maintained for 24–48 h prior to conditioned medium harvest. What’s more, the 2D-Exos were isolated from MSCs cultured under traditional two-dimensional monolayer conditions. Then, we isolated exosomes from the supernatant of same number of MSCs (1 × 109) according to the manufacturer‘s instruction (BL920A, Biosharp, China). We characterized the exosomes by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis, and measurement of their zeta potential. Protein concentrations of 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos were determined using a BCA assay (P0010, Beyotime, China) and further analyzed by Western blotting.

CCK-8 assay

Cell viability was assessed using CCK-8 kit according to the instruction (C0041, Beyotime, China). In brief, cells were cultured in a 96-well plate at a density of 5000 cells each well. After treatment, cells were washed and CCK-8 reagent was added. Following a 2-hour incubation at 37 °C, the maximum absorbance at 450 nm were determined.

Migration, proliferation assay

The transwell migration, 5-ethynyl-2-deoxyuridine (EDU) staining, wound healing and clonogenic formation assays were used to evaluate the migration, and proliferation abilities of the cells. For transwell assay, cells suspended in 200 µl culture medium in the upper compartment of the transwell chambers and 600 µl of culture medium were added to the lower chamber. The cells in the lower chambers were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and then the stained with crystal violet and observed using a bright field microscope. For the EdU assay, cells were cultured with EdU (C0076S, Beyotime, China) for 2 h after treatment, then the cells fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained. The ratio of EdU-positive cells to DAPI was used to calculate the cell proliferation rate. For wound healing assay, 6-well plates were used to culture macrophage till a density of 90%. Cell migration distances were measured under a microscope at 0 and 24 h after a wound zone was created. For clonogenic assay, 1000 cells were seeded in the 6-well plate and cultured for 2 weeks. Colonies were stained with 0.25% crystal violet after fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde.

In vitro macrophages polarization assay

To characterize the effect of 3D-Exos on macrophages M2 polarization, RAW264.7 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured in DMEM medium with or without 3D-Exos for 24 h. To detect macrophages polarization, the cells were fixed and blocked at room temperature, then the cells were incubated with mouse anti-CD68 antibody and rabbit anti-iNOS antibody/rabbit anti-CD206 antibody overnight at 4 ◦C. Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse IgG and Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti-rabbit IgG were then incubated with the cell for 1.5 h at room temperature. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI and observed. The antibodies used in this study were listed in Table S1.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The supernatants of macrophages or SCs received different treatments were collected. The levels of the released cytokines were measured by ELISA kits based on the instructions, the expressions of the cytokines were normalized to control group.

Evaluation of elongation and spreading of SCs

The macrophages were firstly incubated with 3D-Exos, then the SCs were cultured with macrophages for 48 h in coculture systems. The longest branch length, number of branches, and mean branch length were measured under bright field. The SCs were also stained with Phalloidin (G1249, Servicebio, China) to better evaluate the branch.

Neurite outgrowth assay of PC12 cells

An indirect co-culture system was established for SCs and PC12 cells. SCs subjected to different treatments were seeded into the upper chamber and incubated for 24 h to ensure firm adhesion. Subsequently, PC12 cells were seeded into the lower chamber and cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 1% B27 (17504044, GIBCO, USA), 50 ng/mL nerve growth factor (NGF) (450-01, PeproTech, USA), and 2 mM L-glutamine (G6203, Macklin, China) to induce differentiation and neurite extension for 14 days. The PC12 cells were stained with phalloidin to observed the neurites, the number of neurite, longest and total neurite lengths of the PC12 cells were also calculated.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and ATP assay

Firstly, the cells were seeded and subjected to corresponding treatments. Then, the medium was replaced with a non-buffered medium and incubated under non-CO2 conditions for 60 min. At specific time points, oligomycin, FCCP, and rotenone/antimycin A were added to the medium. The levels of ATP was determined using a ATP determination kit (S0026, Beyotime, China) according to manufacturer’s protocol.

Experimental animals and PNI model

Six-week-old male C57BL/6 N mice, weighing about 20 g, were purchased from the Guangdong Sijia Jingda Biotechnology Co. LTD. The animal experiment was performed in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Huateng Technology Co., Ltd. (No. C202505-3). The mice were kept in a room with a constant humidity of 55 ± 5%, temperature of 24 ± 2℃, and a 12 h light/dark cycle. They were randomly grouped and the sciatic nerve crush model was established like our previously study [27, 29]. In brief, After anesthesia, the sciatic nerve was cleanly exposed, the sciatic nerve underwent three 10-second compressions in three distinct directions. This was performed 5 mm distal to the sciatic notch using a medium-sized straight hemostatic forceps, which was applied with approximately 54 N of force to press the nerve to its final ratchet. The 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos (3 × 109 particles) were administered intraperitoneally on the surgical day for 7 consecutive days.

Detection of exosomes delivery in vivo and in vitro

To detected the uptake of 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos, the macrophages were cocultured with PKH-67 (C3635S, Beyotime, China)-labelled exosomes for different time, then the cells were visualized. At 24 h after injection of PKH-67-labeled exosomes, sciatic nerve tissue was collected and the macrophage marker protein CD86 was labeled. The uptake of exosomes by macrophages was evaluated through the co-localization of PKH-67 and CD86.

Measurement of zeta potential

The zeta potentials of exosomes were measured using zeta Potential Analyzer. Under an applied electric field, the migration velocity of exosomes in solution is measured to determine their electrophoretic mobility, subsequently converted into the Zeta potential.

Exosome microarray

To pinpoint the key regulatory molecules within exosomes, we tested the miRNA profiles of 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos. After isolating exosomes as described above, total RNA was extracted and subjected to Agilent miRNA expression profiling microarray analysis (600583, Agilent, USA). Raw data were acquired using Agilent Feature Extraction software.

Evaluation of the sensory and motor functions

Sciatic functional index (SFI) measurement and paw withdraw threshold (PWT) assay were respectively used to evaluate the motor and sensory functions, thereby assisting the access nerve regeneration. The specific methods have been detailed in our previously studies [30]. The footprints were recorded to assess the bearing ability.

Electrophysiological assessment

The electrophysiological assessments were done 4 weeks later by employing the BL-420 S Biological Function Experiment System (BL420N, TECHAMN, China), two stimulating hooked platinum bipolar electrodes were placed around the site of crush. The negative one and positive one were proximal and distal to the site of injury, respectively. The peak amplitude of compound muscle action potential (CMAP) and the time to deflection (latency) were recorded.

Histological evaluation, Immunofluorescence

The muscles or nerves were fixed, embedded in paraffin and then cut, the sections were stained after deparaffinization and dehydration. For immunofluorescence, slide-mounted tissues and plate-mounted cells were blocked for 1 h and then incubated overnight with primary antibodies, followed by staining with secondary antibodies for 1 h. Then the images were captured and/or quantified, and the antibodies used in this study were listed in Table S1.

TEM

In this assay, the samples were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (P1126, Solarbio, China) and stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate. Subsequently, the morphology of the myelin was observed by TEM, the diameter of the axon and thickness of the myelin were also measured.

Western blotting

The proteins were collected, and the concentrations were quantified using BCA Protein Assay Kit (P0010, Beyotime, China). The proteins were firstly separated at 5–15 µg of protein per lane using sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes followed by blocking. The membrane was then incubated with primary antibodies overnight, finally it was incubated with the secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. The signal was visualized and quantified. The antibodies used in this study were listed in Table S1.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (9108, TaKaRa, Japan), and reverse transcription was performed using a PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (TaKaRa) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. qRT-PCR was performed using TB Green™ Premix Ex Taq™ II (TaKaRa). The primers sequences used in this study are listed in Table S2.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from macrophages followed by library preparation based on the Illumina standard instruction. The concentration and size distribution of cDNA library were evaluated before sequencing. The protocol of high-throughput sequencing was fully according to the instructions. The raw reads were filtered and the fragments of genes were counted and normalized. Significant differential expressed genes were identified as those with a False Discovery Rate (FDR) value above the threshold (Q < 0.05) and fold-change > 2.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Luciferase vectors containing wild-type or mutant 3ʹ-untranslated region (3ʹ-UTR) of the PTEN gene, along with miR-26a-5p mimics or control, were constructed by Umine Biotechnology (Guangzhou, China). These luciferase vectors were transfected into macrophages using Lipofectamine 3000 (Life Technologies, USA). After 24-hour transfection, luciferase activity was determined using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (RG088S, Beyotime, China).

Statistical analysis

The measurement data were presented as means ± standard deviation (mean ± SD) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9. Student’s t-test was used to assess the differences between two groups, multiple group comparisons were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA analysis of variance with Tukey’s test. Statistical significance was established as P < 0.05, the minimum significance levels were defined as follows: ns, no significance, *, P < 0.05, **, P < 0.01, and ***, P < 0.001.

Results

Extraction and characterization of 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos

Mesenchymal stem cells used for 2D-Exos extraction were cultured by conventional methods. The coaxial bioprinting technology was used for the cultivation of MSCs and the production of 3D-Exos. Consistent with our previous experimental protocol [28], MSCs used for 3D-Exos extraction were co-extruded with alginate bioink through a dual-channel coaxial nozzle, generating cell-laden microfibers. Subsequent to microfiber fabrication, the culture medium was replaced with serum-free medium and maintained for 24–48 h prior to conditioned medium harvest. What’s more, the 2D-Exos were isolated from MSCs cultured under traditional two-dimensional monolayer conditions. Then, we isolated exosomes from the supernatant of MSCs according to the manufacturer’s instruction (Fig. 1A), the TEM showed that the exosomes exhibited a circular-like morphology (Fig. 1B), and the Nanosight Tracking Analysis (NTA) results revealed that the diameters of the 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos primarily range around 150 nm (Fig. 1C). Then, we found that both 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos present the surface markers of CD81, CD63, and CD9, while the calnexin and GM130 were not detected in both the 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos (Fig. 1D). Consistent with these results, the NTA revealed a significant increase in the number of particles secreted by 3D-cultured cells compared with 2D-cultured cells (Fig. 1E), and the total proteins in the 3D-Exos were significantly higher than 2D-Exos (Fig. 1F), demonstrating that the 3D-culture caused dramatic increases in the number and proteins concentration of the exosomes. The zeta potentials of both the 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos were measured, the results showed that similar potential of the exosomes(Fig. 1G). The stabilities and integrities of both the 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos were investigated by monitoring their particle size (Fig. 1H), zeta potential (Fig. 1I) and relative particle concentration (Fig. 1J) over 48 h at room temperature. No significant variations were observed over this period, indicating their excellent stabilities under the condition. Collectively, we successfully extracted 3D-Exos, the 3D culture conditions enhanced both the production and proteins content of exosomes (3D-Exos), and both of them exerted excellent stabilities.

3D-Exos exert better therapeutic effects on PNI than 2D-Exos

Numerous studies have confirmed the significant role of MSC-Exos in the treatment of various diseases [20, 21]. Our previous research indicated that SCs-derived exosomes can regulate the function of endothelial cells, promote angiogenesis after PNI, and subsequently modulate nerve regeneration [27]. However, there is currently no research specifically investigating the regulatory mechanisms of 3D-Exos on PNI. In this study, we explored and analyzed the role of 3D-Exos on nerve regeneration (Fig. 2A). We first labeled the 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos with PKH-67 to further verify whether intraperitoneally injected exosomes can reach the sciatic nerve and be internalized by macrophages. We found that both the 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos could be internalized by the macrophages (Fig. 2B and C). The mice were then divided into four groups, including sham, PNI, PNI+2D-Exos, and PNI+3D-Exos, the PNI+2D-Exos and PNI+3D-Exos groups received exosomes administration intraperitoneally from the surgical day for 7 consecutive days. We found that both the 2D and 3D exosomes treatments accelerated myelination and axonal regeneration of the mouse after PNI, which were indicated by S100 and NF200, respectively, and the 3D-Exos demonstrated significantly superior therapeutic efficacy compared to 2D-Exos (Fig. 2D). It also could be seen that the morphology of the regenerated axon fibers, including the axon diameter and thickness of the myelin sheath, were significantly greater after treatment, and 3D-Exos showed better efficacy than 2D-Exos (Fig. 2E), thereby promoting the recovery of nerve electrical impulses (Fig. 2F). This significantly improved the strength of the lower limb muscles, resulting in more forceful, clear, and orderly paw prints (Fig. 2G), which is closely related to the role of 3D-Exos in promoting nerve regeneration and enhancing the nerve’s nutritional and regulatory effects on muscles. More quantitative assessments also showed that exosomes significantly improved the motor (Fig. 2H) and sensory functions (Fig. 2I) of the mice, and the 3D-Exos also showed better treatment efficacy. Further staining of the sciatic nerve confirmed the promoting effect of exosomes on nerve regeneration. It can be seen that HE staining, Masson staining, and LFB staining collectively indicated that 3D-Exos more obviously accelerated nerve regeneration than 2D-Exos after PNI (Fig. 2J-L), making the arrangement of nerve fibers in the damaged nerves more regular and order, with fewer vacuoles and edema, less fibrosis, and more pronounced vascular regeneration. Based on the above mentioned results, we initially concluded that 3D-Exos can promote nerve regeneration, and demonstrated a markedly more pronounced therapeutic effect than 2D-Exos. However, the specific mechanisms required further investigation.

Macrophages phenotype is correlated with nerve regeneration after PNI

Given that the potential role of macrophages polarization in tissue repair, along with our previous study found the significant role of macrophages polarization in nerve regeneration after PNI [30]. This study specifically focuses on the mechanistic role of macrophage polarization in nerve regeneration. We collected samples of the sciatic nerve, facial nerve, and trigeminal nerve at different time points after crush injury. We analyzed the dynamic changes in macrophages phenotypes-including total macrophages (CD68), M1 macrophages (CD86/iNOS), and M2 macrophages (CD206)-and assessed their correlation with myelin regeneration. We found that there were less macrophages in intact nerve compared to injured nerve. After nerve injury, the cell-free DNA (cfDNA) increased in sciatic nerve, trigeminal nerve, and facial nerve (Fig. 3A-C), it served as a danger signal in intrinsic immunity activation to promote macrophages recruitment and polarization, then inducing a dramatic increase in macrophages. What’s more, the macrophages were predominantly M1 macrophages, with fewer M2 macrophages in the early stage after PNI, indicating that in the early stages, there is a predominance of proinflammatory. With nerve regeneration, the M1 macrophages gradually decreased, while M2 macrophages gradually increased (Fig. 3D-F). This phenomenon was demonstrated by more intuitive data, and correlation analysis indicated that M1 macrophages were negatively correlated with nerve repair, while M2 macrophages were positively correlated with nerve regeneration (Fig. 3G-I). These findings demonstrated that regulating macrophages reprogramming played a critical role in modulating nerve regeneration following PNI.

3D-Exos promote macrophages reprogramming

Macrophages reprogramming plays an important role in various diseases, and our previous research has also confirmed the impact of macrophages reprogramming on nerve regeneration [30]. We first investigated whether the exosomes could be internalized by macrophages. PKH-67 was used to label exosomes, we found that both the 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos were significantly internalized by macrophages, suggesting their cellular incorporation (Fig. 4A and B). We used 3D-Exos to treat macrophages for 48 h to investigate whether 3D-Exos facilitate macrophages M2 polarization, and the level of Arg-1, IL-10, CD206, IL-4, Fizz1, iNOS, IL-6,TNF-α,IL-1β, CD86, TGF-β, and CCL22 were detected. We found that the levels of M1 markers were decreased and the levels of M2 markers were increased after the treatment of 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos (Fig. 4C). Then, CD206 and iNOS were chosen for further confirm the optimal concentration of 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos. Several concentrations of exosomes (0, 50 µg/mL, 75 µg/mL, 100 µg/mL, 125 µg/mL, 150 µg/mL, 175 µg/mL, 200 µg/mL) were used to treat macrophages, we found that the concentration of CD206 were increased and the concentration of iNOS were decreased after the treatment of 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos till 150 µg/mL. For that, 150 µg/mL exosomes were chosen for following study (Fig. 4D and E). Subsequently, immunofluorescence suggested that both the 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos treatment promoted the expression of CD206 (M2) (Fig. 4F) and suppressed the expression of iNOS (M1) (Fig. 4G), the 3D-Exos exhibited pronounced superiority over 2D-Exos in both dimensions. We further confirmed this in vivo. After PNI, 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos were injected intraperitoneally, and the proportions of different macrophages subtypes in the sciatic nerve were detected by immunofluorescence. The results indicated that 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos can promote macrophages polarization, increasing the number of M2 macrophages while decreasing the number of M1 macrophages (Fig. 4H and I). Consistent with the in vitro findings, 3D-Exos demonstrated marked superiority over 2D-Exos. These findings demonstrated that 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos treatment effectively orchestrates macrophages reprogramming, as evidenced by a significant macrophages reprograming, while the underlying molecular mechanisms required further in-depth investigation.

3D-Exos promote macrophages M2 polarization through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway

To investigate the specific mechanisms by which 3D-Exos promote macrophages reprogramming, we performed mRNA sequencing on macrophages cultured with or without 3D-Exos. All the detected genes were listed in the volcano plot (Fig. 5A), and the different expressed genes were also presented (Fig. 5B). Moreover, Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) statistics were used to found the pathways that were most obviously influenced by the 3D-Exos. We found that the 3D-Exos mainly influenced the aldosterone-regulated sodium reabsorption, mTOR signaling pathway, longevity regulating pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, longevity regulating pathway-multiple species pathways, among them, the PI3K/AKT pathway is associated with macrophages M2 polarization [31, 32] (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, signature genes for PI3K/AKT were upregulated in M2 macrophages indicated by gene set enrichment analysis (Fig. 5D). For further verification, we detected the proteins levels of the PI3K/AKT pathway, and found that the expression of p-PI3K and p-AKT were significantly increased after the treatment of 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos, and the 3D-Exos showed better efficiency (Fig. 5E). These results revealed that 3D-Exos promote the macrophages M2 polarization by activating the PI3K/AKT pathway.

3D-Exos regulated PI3K-AKTpathway via delivery of miR-26a-5p

MicroRNA (miRNA), an approximately 18–22 nucleotides in length single-stranded non-coding RNA, regulates target gene expression by interacting with their 3’ untranslated regions (3’-UTRs) to exert biological effects, and it is well-established that exosomes deliver their miRNAs to target cells for this function. We conducted a microarray analysis of 3D-Exos and detected 78 miRNAs (Fig. 6A), with the 15 most significantly expressed miRNA including miR-124-3p.2, miR-130c, miR-26a-5p, miR-145a-5p, miR-135b-3p, miR-130b-3p, miR-144-3p, miR-135a-2-3p, miR-130a-3p, miR-3474, miR-5624-3p, miR-3068-5p, miR-3087-5p, miR-3082-3p and miR-3096-5p (Fig. 6B). Current research confirmed that 3D-Exos regulates macrophages polarization via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and it is widely acknowledged that PTEN exerts its functions by targeting the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [33–35]. Subsequently, TargetScan (https://www.targetscan.org/vert_80/) [36], miRTarBase (https://miRTarBase.cuhk.edu.cn/) [37], and miRDB (http://mirdb.org/) [38] were used to predict the target miRNA of PTEN. To obtain a more comprehensive and systematic prediction, we intersected the results from three prediction tools (Fig. 6C), which yielded 17 candidate miRNAs, including miR-92a-3p, miR-494-3p, miR-486a-5p, miR-374b-5p, miR-369-3p, miR-29a-3p, miR-26b-5p, miR-26a-5p, miR-25-3p, miR-23b-3p, miR-23a-3p, miR-216a-5p, miR-212-3p, miR-19b-3p, miR-19a-3p, miR-141-3p, and miR-132-3p. Among these, miR-26b-5p was the only one that showed both a higher expression level in 3D-Exos and a statistically significant difference in expression between 2D-Exos and 3D-Exos (Fig. 6D). This indicated that miR-26b-5p was the most likely responsible for mediating the regulatory effect of 3D-Exos on macrophage polarization. To validate this hypothesis, we designed a miR-26b-5p mimic and inhibitor, and examined PI3K/AKT pathway activation in macrophages under different treatment conditions. It’s showed that mimic significantly activated the PI3K/AKT pathway in macrophages while the inhibitor suppressed it (Fig. 6E). Dual-luciferase reporter assays was further used to confirm miR-26b-5p’s regulatory role (Fig. 6F). Compared to NC mimics, co-transfection with plasmids carrying the PTEN 3’-UTR sequence and the miR-26b-5p mimic significantly enhanced luciferase activity, while co-transfection with a mutated PTEN 3’-UTR sequence and the mimic had no significant effect (Fig. 6G), demonstrating PTEN as a direct target of miR-26b-5p. These results collectively indicated that 3D-Exos activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway via its miR-26b-5p, which inhibits PTEN, thereby activating the pathway and promoting macrophages M2 polarization.

3D-Exos protect SCs against OGD injury by promoting macrophages M2 polarization via PI3K-AKT pathway

Our study revealed that 3D-Exos promote the nerve regeneration, we further identified the PI3K-AKT pathway as the target pathway through which 3D-Exos promote macrophages M2 polarization. However, whether the pro-regenerative effects of 3D-Exos on nerve regeneration were mediated by promoting macrophages M2 polarization via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway remains to be elucidated. Next, we explored whether 3D-Exos enhance SCs vitality by promoting macrophages M2 polarization. We established SCs OGD model and cultured macrophages with SCs. In this system, SCs were cultured in the bottom chamber, while macrophages were placed in the top chamber for a period of 48 h (Fig. 7A). As LY294002 has been widely shown to be the efficient inhibitor of PI3K/Akt [39], it was used to inhibit the PI3K-AKT pathway and assess the impact of 3D-Exos, and the efficiencies of LY294002 were tested by WB (Fig. 7B). We found that pre-treated the SCs with 3D-Exos treated macrophages significantly improved their tolerance to OGD (Fig. 7C). Then, to determine the proliferation effect of 3D-Exos treated macrophage on SCs, EdU and colony formation assays were performed. The results showed significant decrease of EdU positive cells after OGD, and the co-culture of pretreated macrophages partially reversed this (Fig. 7D), and the colony formation assays also showed similar results (Fig. 7E). Which indicated that 3D-Exos treated macrophages could promote the proliferation of SCs. It’s was suggested that the use of LY294002 could counteract the effects of 3D-Exos (Fig. 7D and E). To investigate whether 3D-Exos affect migration and invasion abilities of SCs by promoting macrophage M2 polarization, transwell assay and scratch assay were performed. As shown in results (Fig. 7F and G), both the results of transwell assay and scratch assay indicated that 3D-Exos treated macrophages significantly increased the migration abilities of SCs, and the use of LY294002 also offset the effects of 3D-Exos. Taken together, the abovementioned results showed that 3D-Exos treated macrophages significantly promoted the cell proliferation and migration ability of SCs. To further verify the positive effects of 3D-Exos treated macrophage on SCs, the OCR and ATP production of SCs were tested. Unexpectedly, OGD decreased the mitochondrial OCR and ATP production in SCs, but these decreases were blunted after the treatment of 3D-Exos pre-treated macrophage, which were reversed by LY294002 (Fig. 7H and I). These results indicated that 3D-Exos can promote macrophage M2 polarization through the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to enhance SCs vitality.

3D-Exos enhance SCs morphology and promote neurite outgrowth in vitro by promoting macrophages M2 polarization

The microenvironments play critical role in tissue regeneration, as are certainly true in the context of PNI. Cytokines secreted by macrophages are key components of the lesion environment after PNI [40]. To assess whether the 3D-Exos could promote the myelination of SCs by promoting the macrophages M2 polarization after PNI (Fig. 8A), the coculture systems involving macrophages and SCs were used (Fig. 5A). Observations from bright field and immunofluorescence indicated that macrophages treated with 3D-Exos significantly promoted the elongation and spreading of SCs, but inhibited the PI3K/AKT pathway with LY294002 could offset the effects of 3D-Exos (Fig. 8B and C). As the myelin proteins, the S100 and myelin basic protein (MBP) of the SCs were tested, we found that the protective effects of 3D-Exos were obliterated after inhibiting PI3K/AKT pathway (Fig. 8D-F). Given that NGF, secreted by SCs, is commonly recognized as the trigger for SCs elongation and spreading, we used ELISA to evaluate the concentrations of NGF. As anticipated, the levels of NGF in the 3D-Exos treated macrophages group were significantly higher compared to other groups, which were vanished with the treatment of LY294002 (Fig. 8G). Furthermore, considering growth-associated protein 43 (GAP43) and peripheral myelin protein 22 (PMP22) are the key proteins involved in neurite outgrowth and cytoskeletal remodeling, their expressions were assessed via immunofluorescence. Our findings revealed that GAP43 and PMP22 expressions in the 3D-Exos treated macrophages group were significantly higher than other groups (Fig. 8H). This was further substantiated by protein evaluation and RNA evaluation (Fig. 8I and J). As SCs are indispensable in essential substrates and guidance of axons regeneration. The PC12 cells were cultured with different treated SCs, SCs treated with 3D-Exos-preconditioned macrophages significantly promoted the neurite growth of PC12 cells, which were abolished by LY294002, the inhibitor of PI3K-AKT pathway (Fig. 8K). In summary, these results suggested that 3D-Exos promote macrophage M2 polarization through PI3K/AKT pathway, thereby fostering the morphology and promoting neurite outgrowth of SCs, and also the neurite outgrowth of PC12 cells.

The promoting effect of 3D-Exos on nerve regeneration after PNI relies on PI3K/AKT pathway-dependent macrophages M2 polarization

Thus far, we found that 3D-Exos promote macrophages M2 polarization via the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, thereby facilitating nerve regeneration. We further evaluated the effects of 3D-Exos on nerve regeneration by modulating the activity of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway through the inhibitor of the pathway. We used the LY294002 to inhibit the PI3K, and the efficiency was tested (Fig. 9A). And the mouse were divided into four groups, including sham, PNI, PNI+3D-Exos, and PNI+3D-Exos+LY294002. We discovered that after inhibiting the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway, the proportion of M2 macrophages after 3D-Exos treatment was decreased and the proportion of M1 macrophages was increased (Fig. 9B and C). Moreover, the promotion effect of 3D-Exos on nerve regeneration was significantly reduced. This was evident in more loosely organized and disordered nerve fibers, worse myelin regeneration (Fig. 9D-F), less diameter and thickness of the myelinated nerve fibers (Fig. 9G), as well as a significant decrease in sciatic nerve electrical activity (Fig. 9H). The more disorganized and weaker footprints (Fig. 9I), along with more pronounced atrophy and fibrosis in the gastrocnemius muscle (Fig. 9J), further support the findings. These results indicated that the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is the target through which 3D-Exos promote macrophages M2 polarization to accelerate nerve regeneration.

Discussions

In this study, we innovatively employed 3D bioprinting technology to obtain high-quality and highly efficient exosomes, and exploring their potential effects in promoting nerve regeneration and the underlying mechanisms. Our research demonstrated that the 3D-Exos effectively promote macrophages M2 polarization, thereby facilitating the nerve regeneration after PNI. Moreover, 3D-Exos exhibit a significantly superior therapeutic effect compared to 2D-Exos. This discovery demonstrated significant application potential of 3D-bioprinted exosomes orchestrate tissue regeneration.

As the crucial components of the immune system, macrophages play significant roles in tissue regeneration and cellular repair [7]. Numerous studies have highlighted their importance in myocardial injury [41, 42], bone tissue regeneration [15], and wound healing [14]. The deficiency of M2 macrophages and the sufficiency of M1 macrophages can result in sustained secretions of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1, which not only impedes the polarization of M2 macrophages but also inflicts damage to surrounding tissues. By mitigating pro-inflammatory signals, decreasing the proportion of M1 macrophages, and promoting M2 macrophages polarization, these approaches hold considerable potentials for facilitating tissue repair [43]. An increase in the proportion of M2 macrophages in wound tissue can promote the secretion of M2 macrophage-derived cytokines such as IL-10 and VEGF, and inhibit the secretion of TNF-α and IL-6. This improvement in endothelial cell function leads to enhanced angiogenesis, migration, and chemotaxis, thereby accelerating wound healing [43]. Nevertheless, efficiently and rapidly inducing macrophages M2 polarization remains a critical challenge.

Various approaches, such as loading drugs hydrogels [11, 44], and small molecules [45, 46], have been shown to facilitate macrophages M2 polarization. Intraperitoneal administration of M2 macrophages effectively mitigates kidney injury and inflammatory responses in ischemia-reperfusion injury by activating the TGF-β/Smad7 pathway, thereby promoting the cells proliferation [47]. What’s more, sequential release of immunomodulatory cytokines can promote the transition of macrophages from M1 to M2 and enhance vascularization of bone scaffolds, thereby creating a favorable environment for the regeneration and repair of bone [48]. Our previous research demonstrated that inhibition of M1 macrophage polarization ameliorates the inflammatory response after and facilitates nerve regeneration [30]. However, drug loading, tissue compatibility, and potential systemic damage may limit their application. Exosomes, which comprise membrane-bound small vesicles secreted by cells, exhibit excellent tissue compatibility and confer substantial advantages in various biological applications. This includes their considerable potential in promoting macrophages M2 polarization [15, 17].

Exosomes can also directly or indirectly interact with macrophages, promoting their polarization and consequently exerting their functions. Study found that miR-181b-5p in the M2 macrophages derived vesicles, induces macrophage polarization, exerts antifibrotic effects, reduces cardiomyocyte hypertrophy and death, promotes angiogenesis, myocardial regeneration, and improves cardiac function [13]. Yili Wang found that M2 macrophages derived exosomes can promote macrophages M2 polarization via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, thereby facilitating bone repair [15]. It’s also found that exosomes derived from M2 macrophages can improve glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity both in vivo and in vitro. Knocking out miRNAs weakens the functional capacity of these exosomes, it was discovered that miR-690 is an effective component. Which suggested that miR-690 could be a new therapeutic insulin-sensitizing agent for metabolic disease [18]. Moreover, exosomes derived from hypoxia-treated glioma cells can enhance glioma resistance and progression by promoting macrophage M2 polarization through the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [17]. Macrophages also play crucial roles in nerve regeneration. They initially contribute to regeneration by eliminating inhibitory signals from myelin debris, thereby facilitating axonal regrowth [6]. Additionally, they secrete many factors, including proteases and growth-promoting cytokines, and stimulate extracellular matrix remodeling to support nerve regeneration [49]. Also, study indicated that M2 macrophages secrete Ephrin-B2 to activate SCs, thereby fostering axon regeneration [50]. Furthermore, modulating macrophages function can regulate nerve regeneration by influencing SCs activities after PNI, such as dedifferentiation, and migration [5]. Our previous study demonstrated that inhibiting macrophages M1 polarization could accelerate nerve regeneration [30]. However, the encouraging advances in exosome-driven M2 macrophage polarization are tempered by a critical bottleneck: the lack of standardized methods to obtain exosomes that are simultaneously high-yield, highly pure, and sufficiently concentrated remains an urgent, unmet need.

Here, we delved further into the mechanism by which 3D-Exos promote macrophages M2 polarization to promote nerve regeneration after PNI. We isolated 3D-Exos and validated that the exosomes can induce macrophages M2 polarization by activating PI3K-AKT signaling pathway by inhibiting PTEN through miR-26b-5p. In vivo investigations further substantiated that 3D-Exos enhances nerve regeneration by fostering macrophages M2 polarization, and the 3D-Exos demonstrate superior efficacy compared to 2D-Exos. These comprehensive findings indicate that 3D-Exos significantly augment the local extracellular environment after PNI, enabling macrophages polarization to create a conducive microenvironment that is pivotal for nerve regeneration. This study not only elucidates a more critical role of 3D-Exos than 2D-Exos in nerve regeneration but also underscores the therapeutic potential of modulating macrophages polarization for PNI. This technology has, to some extent, overcome the significant limitations in suboptimal concentration and compromised purity of traditional exosomes.

While we have made clear that the 3D-Exos could accelerate nerve regeneration after PNI by promoting the macrophages M2 polarization, it has certain limitations in our study. Although this study has confirmed that M2 macrophages can promote SCs repair and, consequently, facilitate myelin regeneration, the underlying pathways and molecular mechanisms remained unclear and require further in-depth investigation. In future research, we should focus on exploring the components in 3D-Exos that induce macrophages M2 polarization and delve into their molecular mechanisms to elucidate the detailed processes by which M2 macrophages promote myelin regeneration. Which will contribute to the advancement of the practical application and development of relevant therapies. What’s more, the observation period in the study was 28 days, longer-term observations with functional and histological outcomes would provide a more comprehensive assessment of the durability of therapeutic effects and the potential for recovery. Future studies could extend the follow-up duration.

Conclusions

In summary, we investigated the significant role of 3D-Exos in nerve regeneration after PNI. Our study demonstrated that 3D-Exos showed a better therapeutic effect compared to 2D-Exos. The 3D-Exos could regulate macrophages M2 polarization and then promote nerve regeneration. Specifically, 3D-Exos inhibits PTEN through miR-26b-5p, then activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway to boost macrophages M2 polarization, thereby creating an advantageous microenvironment for SCs regeneration and subsequently enhancing nerve regeneration. This study provides compelling evidence in 3D-Exos orchestrate tissue regeneration through targeted modulation of cellular crosstalk.
