Tachinid Endoparasitoid Flies do not Rely on Domesticated Viruses, Unlike Multiple Hymenopteran Lineages
Authors: Sara Oukkal, Benjamin Guinet, Paul D N Hebert, Daniel Janzen, Winnie Hallwachs, A J Fleming, Sylvain Charlat, Julien Varaldi, Virginia Sanchez Puerta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractViral domestication, the co-option of integrated viral genes for host functions, has been repeatedly documented in endoparasitoid Hymenoptera. Whether this phenomenon extends to other parasitoid insects remains to be assessed. Here, we tested this hypothesis in tachinid flies, the largest group of non-hymenopteran parasitoids. To this end, we investigated patterns of viral endogenization in 52 genomes, including 37 newly sequenced species. Similarly to hymenopteran endoparasitoids, tachinid genomes were found to display numerous endogenous viral elements (EVEs), primarily derived from insect-infecting viruses with either DNA or RNA genomes. However, the majority of integrated sequences were species-specific, with no evidence of major ancient gene domestication events. In addition, the EVE content did not differ between dipteran parasitoids and their free-living Diptera counterparts. These findings contrast with the recurrent viral domestication observed in endoparasitoid Hymenoptera. This pattern is discussed in light of important attributes of tachinid flies including their lack of venom and perforating ovipositor, and their avoidance of the host immune system.Keywords: viral domestication, endogenous viral elements, parasitoids, Tachinidae, viruses, diptera

Several ancient and independent domestication events have been well characterized in hymenopteran endoparasitoids, where multiple integrated viral genes contribute to parasitic success. These multiple evolutionary convergences may be driven by their endoparasitoid lifestyle, in which immature stages develop inside the host and must survive its immune response. However, endoparasitoid species are also found beyond the Hymenoptera clade, and whether viral domestication also occurs in these groups remains to be investigated. Here we tested this hypothesis on tachinid flies, the largest group of non-hymenopteran endoparasitoids. We analyzed a total of 52 tachinid genomes, including 37 new assemblies, to investigate their content in endogenous viral elements (EVEs). Our results reveal abundant and diverse EVEs, but no evidence of ancient nor multiple gene viral domestication. Furthermore, EVE content is similar between parasitoid and non-parasitoid flies. These findings suggest that viral domestication is not a systematic feature of endoparasitoid evolution and may be constrained by clade-specific morphological and physiological features.

Introduction

Parasitoid insects harbor one of the most remarkable molecular innovations driven by symbiotic interactions. Endoparasitoid wasps—hymenopteran insects whose larvae develop inside their host—face the challenge of overcoming the host's immune response. While most parasitoid wasps rely on the injection of immune-suppressive proteins along with the eggs (Moreau and Asgari 2015), some lineages have evolved a more extraordinary strategy involving the hijacking of viral machinery. Indeed, these wasps produce and inject particles that resemble viruses, built from proteins encoded by viral genes that were horizontally acquired by these parasitic wasps millions of years ago, in a process known as viral domestication (Drezen et al. 2022; Guinet et al. 2025).

The endoparasitoid lifestyle is ancestral and appeared once within Hymenoptera, with six independent viral domestication events documented so far in this group. Three major cases involve species in Braconidae and Ichneumonidae, which harbor PolyDNAviruses (PDVs) (Edson et al. 1981; Espagne et al. 2004; Bézier et al. 2009; Volkoff et al. 2010; Béliveau et al. 2015). In these hybrid systems, the structure of the particles is encoded by virally-derived genes encoded in the wasp genome, while the inside consists of circular DNA containing typical wasp genes. These DNA circles encode virulence factors that, once injected into the host during oviposition, can insert into the host genome (Chevignon et al. 2018). There, they can be expressed to suppress immune response and modulate host development, thus promoting the parasitoid's larval survival (Chevignon et al. 2014). Other wasp lineages, such as Venturia, Fopius and the common ancestor of Eucoilini and Trichoplastini, have also independently domesticated viral elements to produce virus-like particles (VLPs) (Pichon et al. 2015; Burke et al. 2018; Di Giovanni et al. 2020; Guinet et al. 2025). In contrast to PDVs, these particles are loaded with immuno-suppressive proteins instead of DNA. They specifically target host immune cells, ultimately suppressing their ability to orchestrate an effective immune response (Rizki and Rizki 1992). However, these systems are restricted to these groups, with all other endoparasitoid wasps relying on venom or evasion strategies to overcome host defences, without the involvement of domesticated viruses.

A large genome-wide analysis covering most of the Hymenopteran diversity showed that the endoparasitoid lifestyle is associated with frequent viral integrations (Guinet et al. 2023). EVEs derived from double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) viruses and viral domestication events were more frequent in endoparasitoids than in ectoparasitoids or free-living species, suggesting that endoparasitoidism promotes viral acquisition and domestication. Beyond Hymenoptera, well-supported cases of viral domestication remain scarce. A few instances have been reported in diverse lineages such as fungi, nematodes, and algae, but they appear to be isolated and lineage-specific, in contrast to the pattern in Hymenoptera (Dawe and Nuss 2001; Delaroque et al. 2001). Altogether, these results support the hypothesis that strong selective pressure on the immune interaction of endoparasitoids with their hosts has driven the convergent co-option of viruses in multiple hymenopteran clades. In turn, such convergence may extend to other insects that have independently evolved a similar lifestyle. The present study aims at testing this hypothesis in the dipteran family Tachinidae that is the largest and most diverse group of non-hymenopteran obligate endoparasitoids, with over 10,000 described species and an extraordinary diversity of host range, oviposition modes, and parasitic strategies (Eggleton and Belshaw 1992; Stireman et al. 2006). However, in contrast to hymenopteran endoparasitoids, tachinid flies generally lack a perforating ovipositor and therefore do not inject venom factors directly into the host during oviposition. Despite this biological difference between the two endoparasitoid clades, co-option of viral genes could still confer a selective advantage, for instance through the release of viral elements by the developing larva itself.

To this end, we identified and characterized EVEs in the genomes of 52 Tachinidae species, investigating their viral origins and potential domestication. We sequenced 37 of these genomes as part of a broader project on parasitoids and their hosts collected in the Área de Conservación Guanacaste (ACG) located in Costa Rica (Janzen et al. 2009; Janzen and Hallwachs 2016; Quicke et al. 2025). This effort is part of a long-term biodiversity inventory in which species are being formally named and described based on integrative taxonomic approaches (Fleming et al. 2023). The dataset was complemented by 15 publicly available genome assemblies from the Darwin Tree of Life project (Blaxter et al. 2022). Furthermore, we tested whether the EVE content differs between Tachinidae and their free-living close relatives, within the Muscomorpha clade.

Results

Comprehensive EVE Detection Across Tachinidae Genomes

The dipteran genome assemblies average 466.84 Mb in size, with considerable variation (153 to 961 Mb), consistent with the known range for the group (Kelley et al. 2014; Morales-Hojas et al. 2018; Bayega et al. 2020; Cornette et al. 2020). All assemblies included at least 70% of the expected universal Diptera genes present in single copies (BUSCO). The DTOL genomes (assembled with long-read and Hi-C technologies) exhibited a higher completeness (97.88%) than the Illumina-based assemblies from Tachinidae and free-living Muscomorpha (76.82% and 76.51%, respectively) (Fig. S1). The inferred phylogenetic tree, based on 300 shared BUSCO Diptera genes, was very well resolved (91% of nodes with aLRT > 99). Consistent with previous studies, Tachinidae formed a monophyletic group within Muscomorpha (Fig. S2). DTOL and newly sequenced tachinid species are interspersed in the tree, collectively covering all four Tachinidae subfamilies (Exoristinae, Tachininae, Dexiinae, Phasiinae) (Stireman et al. 2019).

To detect EVEs, we first identified genomic loci that were not taxonomically assigned to insects but showed homology to known viral proteins (Fig. 1 Step 1). To exclude fragments from free-living viruses that may have been co-sequenced with the flies (that is; to only retain only loci integrated into the nuclear genome), we restricted our analyses to scaffolds with a sequencing depth consistent with that of the nuclear genome and/or carrying insect genes (BUSCO) or transposable elements (Fig. 1 Step 2; see Methods for details).

Overview of the homology-based approach used to identify endogenous and domesticated viral elements in Tachinid genomes. (1) Homology search: each genome was searched against a RefSeq viral database (excluding phages and misannotated PDV genes and including IVSPER sequences). Overlapping viral hits were merged into candidate loci. Taxonomic validation: each candidate was searched against a generalist RefSeq database to confirm its viral origin and exclude those with a eukaryotic first hit. (2) Endogenization status validation: sequencing depth and genomic context were examined to confirm endogenization. Scaffolds were classified as nuclear if their coverage matched that of BUSCO-containing scaffolds and/or contained BUSCO genes or transposable elements. (3) Clustering: EVEs were clustered with viral and dipteran RefSeq proteins. (4) Phylogenetic analysis: Clusters containing both viral and EVE sequences were aligned and analyzed phylogenetically to infer ancestral integration events using both gene tree and species tree information. (5) Detection of domesticated events: for shared integrations across species (identified by comparing genomic neighborhoods), we tested for a signature of purifying selection (dN/dS < 1).

Using this approach, EVEs were detected in all Tachinidae genome assemblies, with an average of 32 per species, ranging from 39 to 538 in DTOL genome assemblies and from 5 to 44 in our new assemblies (Fig. 2). The assembly quality gap between DTOL and our assemblies was the main explanation for this difference (phylogenetic generalized linear mixed model, fixed effect estimate = 2.24, P < 2.2e-16). Thus, unsurprisingly, high-quality assemblies allowed for more exhaustive EVE detection, although a comparable number of candidate loci were initially detected in both high-quality and fragmented assemblies The subsequent filtering step, designed to retain only loci integrated into the nuclear genome (EVEs), penalizes fragmented assemblies, as it relies on the sequencing depth and the presence of insect BUSCO genes on the same scaffold as the candidate.

The identified EVEs derive from multiple viral families, with a strong predominance of Hytrosaviridae (33.95%) (Fig. 3a). This is consistent with their host spectrum, as these large dsDNA viruses are known to infect Diptera, including Musca domestica and Glossina pallidipes, where they cause chronic infections (Jaenson 1978; Lietze et al. 2007). Other highly represented viral families are also known to infect insects. Xinmoviridae (14.71%) are ssRNA(-) viruses, some of which, like Drosophila unispina virus 1, specifically infect Diptera (Sharpe and Paraskevopoulou 2023). Baculoviridae (12.26%) are typically associated with Lepidoptera but have also been reported in Diptera, such as Culex nigripalpus nucleopolyhedrovirus (Perera et al. 2007). Phasmaviridae (11.42%) include ssRNA(-) viruses like Jonvirus, that infect Diptera (Marklewitz et al. 2015; Kuhn and Hughes 2024). Less abundant families include Poxviridae (4.83%), dsDNA viruses that replicate in the cytoplasm (rather than the nucleus) and are known to infect insects. Rhabdoviridae (4.75%) and Iridoviridae (4.67%) also infect a variety of insects (Delhon et al. 2006; Walker et al. 2022; McInnes et al. 2023). Finally, Spinaereoviridae (3.45%) are dsRNA viruses with a broad host range including arthropods, mammals, birds, and even plants (Mohd Jaafar et al. 2014; Suzuki et al. 2015). Thus, all viral families represented in our dataset are known to infect insects, with the exception of Phycodnaviridae, that are thought to solely infect algae (Phycodnaviridae ICTV report 2009). However, as our knowledge of viral diversity is largely incomplete, these sequences may derive from yet unsampled insect-infecting viruses, only distantly related to Phycodnaviridae.

While the strong representation of insect-associated viral families suggests that the EVE content reflects the natural array of viruses infecting tachinids, it seems likely that some viruses are more prone to viral integration, depending on their genomic structure. Although we found that all forms of viral genomes can be endogenized, a marked over-representation of dsDNA viruses was observed. These represent nearly half of all EVEs (Fig. 3b), while single-stranded and double-stranded RNA viruses—although frequent among insect-infecting viruses—are underrepresented. This pattern diverges sharply from their prevalence in viral databases (χ2 = 5246.2, df = 5, P < 2.2 × 10⁻16), where RNA viruses account for over 90% of the known viral diversity infecting insects, while dsDNA viruses account for only 8% (Shi et al. 2016; Wu et al. 2020).

Viral Domestications in Tachinidae are Rare and Lineage-Specific

While the abundance of EVEs provides an overview of viral infections in flies, it does not reveal their evolutionary or functional relevance. In a canonical event of viral domestication, we expect EVEs to be retained over time, conserved in their coding sequence by purifying selection as a consequence of co-option for a given function. To test for the presence of such cases, we clustered homologous EVEs (with a minimum of 30% sequence overlap and an e-value threshold of 10⁻4) resulting in 139 clusters (Fig. 1 Step 3) and plotted each gene presence or absence across the species phylogeny (Fig. 1 Step 4; Fig. 4). An ancient and conserved viral integration event involving multiple genes (i.e. as observed in Hymenoptera) should be revealed as blocks in the heatmap, indicating the continuous presence of multiple groups of orthologous EVEs (columns), across closely related fly species (rows). However, our data reveal mostly scattered patterns. A few EVEs are shared between some species, but the analysis shows no clear evidence of ancient integration events. Specifically, 63.4% of EVE clusters are unique to a single species, confirming that most integrations are recent and lineage-specific. An intriguing case, further detailed below, involves 52 EVEs homologous to genes from Musca domestica salivary gland hypertrophy virus (MdSGHV, Hytrosaviridae) (Garcia-Maruniak et al. 2008), identified in two Tachina species: 52 in Tachina lurida and 25 in Tachina fera.

Clusters homologous to DNA viruses (blue) are on the left, RNA viruses (red) on the right. Colored cells indicate the presence of one or more EVEs; numerical values show the number of copies/paralogs in the cluster for each species.

Beyond this visual inspection, we tested more formally for the existence of viral domestication cases. This was done by (i) searching for EVEs inserted at the same genomic location in several species (likely implying a single integration event in their common ancestor) and by (ii) searching for signs of purifying selection (Fig. 1 Step 5). A shared genomic location was inferred when an orthologous insect gene was found upstream and/or downstream of the viral insertion, while purifying selection was inferred when the dN/dS ratios was significantly below 1.

Under these criteria, the MdSGHV-like EVEs identified in T. lurida and T. fera do not qualify as a domestication event, although the number of viral genes is reminiscent of the large, domesticated gene sets described in Hymenoptera. Tachina grossa, which is sister species of T. lurida and T. fera, lacks these EVEs. Moreover, the EVEs are inserted at different genomic locations in the two species, suggesting at least two independent viral integration events. In addition, we also observed that some portions of this virus are present in several copies in each genome, explained either by multiple integration events or intra-genomic duplications.

In contrast, eight events (each involving a single gene) met both criteria, suggesting potential cases of domestication (Table S1; Fig. S3). One of these events (Cluster 7928) involves an EVE homologous to a hypothetical protein from an unclassified RNA virus (Wuhan cricket virus 2), which likely integrated into the genome of the common ancestor of three closely related species within the Phasiinae subfamily. All other cases involve dsDNA viruses. One event (Cluster 47101) involved an aspartyl protease, homologous to a gene from a Steigviridae family, and was likely acquired by the ancestor of two species from the Tachininae subfamily. The six other integration events involved hypothetical proteins with no defined function: one from a lymphocystis disease virus-China gene (Iridoviridae; Cluster 8404) found in Tachininae; two from a chlorovirus gene (Phycodnaviridae; Cluster 12273), identified in species from Exoristinae and Tachininae, and thus corresponding to two distinct integration events; and three from an Apis mellifera filamentous virus gene (Unclassified DNA Virus; Cluster 27313), detected in some Tachininae, Exoristinae, and Phasiinae species, suggesting three independent integration events. While these cases represent interesting candidates for the potential domestication of individual viral genes, awaiting further investigation, they do not exhibit the patterns expected under a scenario of convergent evolution with hymenopteran endoparasitoids. Overall, this pattern stands in stark contrast with large-scale domestications observed in hymenopteran endoparasitoids (Guinet et al. 2023).

No Association Between Lifestyle and EVE Abundance

Hymenopteran endoparasitoids, beyond their numerous cases of viral domestication, are also characterized by a higher number of EVEs than their free-living counterparts (Guinet et al. 2023). To test whether this association between lifestyle and EVE abundance also holds in Diptera, we examined whether the endoparasitoid lifestyle correlates with a higher EVE content, comparing Tachinidae to other free-living Muscomorpha (Dipterans), bearing in mind that Tachinidae are themselves a subgroup of the Muscomorpha section (Table S2a/S2b). We selected assemblies of similar quality, thus excluding high-quality DTOL assemblies to avoid any bias. In Tachinidae species, we detected between 2 and 23 EVEs each (mean = 10.05), while in free-living Muscomorpha species we detected 2 to 53 EVEs per genome (mean = 12.29). To assess the effect of lifestyle on EVE abundance, while accounting for evolutionary relatedness between species, we applied a phylogenetic generalized linear mixed model (PGLMM). The test revealed no significant difference in EVE abundance between the two groups (fixed effect estimate = −0.21; Std. Error = 0.17; P = 0.227), leading to the conclusion that EVEs abundance is not associated with the parasitoid lifestyle in Diptera (Fig. 5a). The vast majority of viral families homologous to EVEs are shared between Tachinidae and free-living Muscomorpha, with more than 90% of EVEs assigned to families present in both groups. Thirteen viral families were shared, while five were found exclusively in Tachinidae and eight exclusively in free-living Muscomorpha (Fig. 5b). However, these group-specific families represent only a minor fraction of the total EVE content. The respective proportions of the different viral genomic structures were also highly similar between groups, with dsDNA viruses dominating in both Tachinidae (64.9%, DTOL excluded) and free-living Diptera (77.0%). In conclusion, these results reveal a high similarity in EVE content between Tachinidae and free-living Muscomorpha, both in terms of abundance, taxonomic diversity, and viral genome structures.

Discussion

In this study, we performed a comprehensive analysis of EVEs across 52 species of Tachinidae, the largest family of parasitoid Diptera. The identified EVEs were analyzed in relation to host phylogeny, to investigate their origins, conservation, and potential domestication. All genomes were found to contain EVEs, that predominantly derived from insect-infecting viruses, with an overrepresentation of dsDNA viruses. However, most integrations were recent and restricted to single tachinid species, with limited conservation across taxa and no evidence of large-scale domestication events. Moreover, the EVE abundance and composition did not significantly differ between Tachinidae and free-living Diptera.

The detected EVEs are homologous to viral families that are known to infect insects, with the exception of Phycodnaviridae, which are though to solely infect algae (see ICTV report for details). However, as our knowledge of viral diversity remains largely incomplete, these EVEs may originate from yet unsampled insect-infecting viruses only distantly related to Phycodnaviridae. We detected EVEs derived from viruses with all major viral genome structures, including DNA and RNA viruses. This may sound surprising, as none of the viral families identified in our dataset possess natural integration mechanisms. dsDNA viruses typically replicate episomally in the nucleus without integrating into the host genome (Herniou et al. 2013), while insect RNA viruses lack the integration machinery found in retroviruses (Foil and Issel 1991; Gilbert and Belliardo 2022). Yet, both DNA and RNA viruses are known to integrate accidentally in insect genomes (Gilbert and Belliardo 2022). Regarding the mechanisms at play, we can speculate that DNA viruses may be endogenized through non-homologous end joining (NHEJ), which represents the predominant double-strand break repair pathway in eukaryotes (Pannunzio et al. 2018). It is plausible that this machinery is involved in the incorporation of viral DNA fragments into the host genome during double-strand break repair, especially for viruses with a nuclear replication. Additionally, transposable elements may also be involved in viral integration, from both DNA and RNA virus donors, although the exact molecular dynamics remain poorly understood. This idea is supported by the fact that TE-containing regions tend to be enriched in EVEs (Whitfield et al. 2017). Template switching constitutes one possible mechanism, in which the reverse transcriptase shifts from the original TE RNA to a different RNA (Tan et al. 2016).

Interestingly, the bias toward dsDNA-derived EVEs observed in our study was previously reported in Hymenoptera (Guinet et al. 2023) and, more broadly, across insects (Gilbert and Belliardo 2022), suggesting that very general mechanisms are at play. One possible explanation for the observed patterns—i.e., fewer integrations from RNA viruses than expected from databases—would be a sampling bias toward RNA viruses, making them over-represented in viral databases. Such a bias could stem from the fact that many viral discovery efforts rely primarily on transcriptomic data, which inherently underrepresents DNA viruses. While this limitation may apply to poorly studied taxa, well-characterized systems such as Drosophila melanogaster and the honeybee Apis mellifera indicate that RNA viruses are indeed more abundant in insects than DNA viruses, suggesting that the trend observed in our analysis is biologically meaningful rather than artifactual. Indeed, in both species, RNA viruses consistently dominate in terms of prevalence and diversity (Chen and Siede 2007; Webster et al. 2015; Wallace et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2022). In a study from our group investigating both the DNA and RNA viromes within a Drosophila-parasitoids community, RNA viruses were again found to be markedly more diverse and abundant than DNA viruses (Varaldi et al. 2024). This pattern is further supported by large-scale surveys that revealed the high prevalence and diversity of RNA viruses in insects (Wu et al. 2020). This apparent dominance of RNA viruses is also observed at broader evolutionary scales, as similar trends have been reported across diverse eukaryotic taxa (Koonin et al. 2015; Irwin et al. 2022). The overrepresentation of dsDNA viruses among EVEs is thus likely to reflect differences in integration potential rather than a bias in viral discovery efforts. One plausible explanation is that accidental integration is more likely for dsDNA viruses due to their structural and functional similarity to host genomes. In addition, their replication often takes place in the nucleus, where viral DNA is in direct contact with the host chromatin, facilitating integration. In contrast, most insect-infecting RNA viruses lack a DNA phase and replicate exclusively in the cytoplasm, making their integration less likely.

Our results contrast with those from hymenopteran parasitoids, where multiple lineages have co-opted large sets of viral genes to manipulate host immunity (Bézier et al. 2009; Volkoff et al. 2010; Béliveau et al. 2015; Pichon et al. 2015; Burke et al. 2018; Di Giovanni et al. 2020; Guinet et al. 2023). Thus, the striking pattern of convergent viral domestication seen in several hymenopteran lineages does not appear to extend beyond this order. However, it is worth noting that viral domestication has so far been documented in only six hymenopteran clades, while the vast majority of parasitoid wasps successfully diversifies without a viral contribution. Moreover, parasitoidism in Hymenoptera traces back to more than 200 million years ago (Peters et al. 2017), compared with around 70 million years for Tachinidae (Stireman et al. 2019), suggesting that tachinid flies may simply have had fewer evolutionary opportunities for such contingent events to occur.

An alternative is that the absence of viral domestication may stem from differences in viral exposure, i.e., tachinids being less exposed to viruses than hymenopteran parasitoids. Although direct records of past infections are inaccessible, the presence of EVEs can serve as a proxy to explore this hypothesis. Based on this approach, the EVE content of tachinid genomes appears quantitatively comparable to that of hymenopteran parasitoids (Guinet et al. 2023). This observation appears to rule out the hypothesis that differences in viral exposure could account for the contrasting patterns of viral domestication.

A more plausible explanation lies in the lack of maternally transferred immune-modulating factors in Tachinidae, which may limit opportunities for viral co-option. Hymenopteran parasitoids possess a perforating ovipositor, an ancestral feature of the Parasitoida clade (Peters et al. 2017), allowing them to inject eggs directly into the host along with venom or virus-like particles that actively modulate host immunity (Moreau and Asgari 2015). By contrast, most tachinid flies lack a perforating ovipositor and thus have alternative and diversified oviposition strategies (Dindo and Nakamura 2018). Some species lay macrotype eggs directly on the host cuticle, while others deposit microtype eggs on foliage; these are either ingested by the host or hatch into mobile larvae that actively seek out their host (Stireman et al. 2006). Notably, in certain taxa, females scratch the host's surface and lay their eggs inside, a moment during which maternal injection of viral factors could potentially occur. However, a few exceptional species, such as Compsilura, are known to inject their eggs directly into the host (Culver 1919), but none of these were included in our dataset. The absence of viral domestication in Tachinidae may thus reflect the general lack of maternally transferred factors, reducing the opportunities for the release of viral immunosuppressive factors in the host, unless the developing larvae have the ability to do so. Another possibility that could not be explored with the present dataset, is that tachinid parasitoids could rely on non-endogenized mutualistic viruses. A notable example of such interactions in Hymenoptera is a vertically transmitted poxvirus in the braconid Diachasmimorpha longicaudata, which replicates both in the wasp and in its fly host, where it suppresses the immune response and enhances parasitoid development (Coffman et al. 2020) While the absence of a perforating ovipositor in tachinids most likely constrains the co-option of free viruses either, this remains a hypothesis for future investigation.

The absence of viral domestication is consistent with the immune interaction strategies adopted by tachinids. Hymenopteran endoparasitoids often adopt an active host immune suppression strategy, directly manipulating host immunity through injected venom and teratocytes. This creates a context favorable to the co-option of viral functions that act similarly on host physiology, enhancing the parasitic efficiency (Pennacchio and Strand 2006; Moreau 2013; Strand 2004). While some hymenopteran lineages also rely on avoidance or evasion, these remain minor compared to the widespread active immunosuppressive strategies (Moreau 2003). In contrast, tachinids have predominantly evolved passive mechanisms minimizing host immune detection, likely because females lack a mechanism to inject protective venoms into the host. Tachinid larvae can hide in inaccessible areas, such as glands and muscle tissues, thus limiting exposure to the host's immune system (Caron et al. 2008). Some species form a protective shield around their larvae, preventing melanization thanks to a structure composed of hemocytes and fat cells (Yamashita et al. 2019). Others secrete cytolytic saliva that causes tissue necrosis and creates a breathing tunnel that prevents complete encapsulation by the host (Valigurová et al. 2013). This reliance on stealth and evasion contrasts sharply with the active immune manipulation observed in Hymenoptera. It is possible that an early commitment to immune evasion in tachinids reduced the selective pressure for viral co-option. Conversely, the absence of viral domestication may have constrained the evolution of Diptera parasitoidism toward alternative immunosuppressive strategies, i.e. avoidance-based strategies.

In summary, the present analysis demonstrates that despite their endoparasitoid lifestyle, tachinid flies do not show signs of viral domestication, in contrast to hymenopteran endoparasitoids. This result suggests that viral domestication is not an inherent consequence of adopting an endoparasitoid lifestyle, but rather the outcome of a specific combination of biological traits (e.g. presence of a perforating ovipositor), ecological interactions with the host immune system, and contingency in viral exposure.

Materials and Methods

The pipeline used for this analysis is available on GitHub git@github.com:SaraOukkal/EVEs_in_Tachinids.git, and the corresponding workflow is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Data

We analyzed genomes from three sources (Table S2a/S2b). First, Illumina HiSeq 2 × 150 bp data were generated from 37 Tachinidae species. The specimens were originally sampled for DNA barcoding and ecological studies of host-parasitoid interactions (Janzen et al. 2009; Janzen and Hallwachs 2016). Collected in Costa Rica from the Área de Conservación Guanacaste (ACG), over several decades of field work, their reuse for this analysis was allowed under permit R-007-2922-OT-CONAGEBIO. The list of specimens, with corresponding metadata, are available in the BOLD database as HORIZON1 dataset under doi: http://dx.doi.org/10.5883/DS-HORIZON1. DNA was extracted from legs and sequenced at ∼20× depth. We also included all high-quality Tachinidae genomes available at that time from the Darwin Tree of Life (DTOL) project (Blaxter et al. 2022). These 15 genomes were sequenced using long-read and Hi-C technologies. Third, we retrieved Illumina paired-end reads for 24 non-parasitoid Muscomorpha species from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA), aiming to cover a wide diversity of this clade. Parasites are considered as “free-living” in this study since we aim to separate endoparasitoids that develop inside their hosts from all other lifestyles. To match sequencing depth across groups (∼20X), reads exceeding 25× were subsampled using seqtk (v1.2-r94). Non-parasitoid and newly sequenced Tachinidae reads were filtered with Fastp (v0.12.4) and assembled using Megahit (v1.2.9) (Li et al. 2015; Chen et al. 2018). Heterozygous regions were collapsed using Redundans (Pryszcz and Gabaldón 2016). Assembly quality was assessed with QUAST (v5.0.2) (Gurevich et al. 2013), and gene completeness with BUSCO (v5.4.5) using the diptera_odb10 dataset (Simão et al. 2015). Assemblies with BUSCO scores below 70% were excluded.

Species Phylogeny

Phylogenetic relationships among species were inferred using 300 genes identified in the BUSCO analysis as complete in all studied species. Genes were aligned using Clustal Omega v1.2.3, trimmed with TrimAL v1.4.rev15 and concatenated into a single supergene alignment file (Capella-Gutiérrez et al. 2009; Sievers et al. 2011). Phylogenetic trees were inferred with IQ-TREE v2.0.3, employing the -m MFP option to select the best-fit substitution model for each gene (Minh et al. 2020). Parameters included -alrt 2500 and -bb 2500 for robust support values, -bnni for non-standard data optimization, and –symtest-remove-bad to exclude poorly symmetric partitions (Nguyen et al. 2015; Chernomor et al. 2016; Hoang et al. 2018). Three Hymenoptera species, Microplitis demolitor (GCF_026212275.2), Cotesia congregata (GCA_905319865.3), and Leptopilina boulardi (GCF_019393585.1) were used as outgroups.

Candidates Identification

EVEs were identified using a homology-based pipeline. The RefSeq virus database was used for the first homology search (683,337 sequences—released 09/09/23) (Brister et al. 2015). Phages were excluded from the database because of their irrelevance when searching for insect-infecting viruses. Genes annotated as PDVs were also removed because they are mistakenly classified as viral sequences despite being derived from wasp genes. In contrast, structural genes of viral origin from Ichnovirus (IVSPER) were added to the database, despite them not being assigned to any known viral family yet (Volkoff et al. 2010). This resulted in a refined database of 141,805 sequences that was used for homology search with MMseqs2 (version 5daca424b162cc5fdf0b9cd151aebed86975cbf6), with the following options: -a, -s 7.5, -e 0.01 (Steinegger and Söding 2017). Candidate loci were defined by regrouping overlapping hits.

Our goal was to determine whether these candidates, while being sequences from dipteran genomes, show greater similarity to viral sequences than to those of eukaryotic origin. To this end, each candidate was compared with a generalist RefSeq database (release 09/09/2023) converted for taxonomy using MMseqs2 createtaxdb with –ncbi-tax-dump and –tax-mapping-file. This dataset was curated to exclude proteins from Diptera clades present in our dataset (taxid 43738). Taxonomic assignment was performed using easy-taxonomy with –lca-mode 4 (Top-hit Last Common Ancestor (LCA) approach, taxonomy based on the best hits common ancestor) and e-value threshold 1e-5 (Mirdita et al. 2021). Only sequences whose top-scoring hits placed their last common ancestor within viral taxa were retained. However, an additional homology search against the nucleotide database (NCBI NT) revealed widespread presence of some candidate sequences across diverse eukaryotic lineages, including insects. As these patterns are inconsistent with a virus-to-fly horizontal transfer, but rather the contrary, such candidates were excluded from downstream analyses. Viral homologs were annotated from RefSeq viral proteins and VirusHostDB.

To filter out sequences homologous to transposable elements (TEs), we used MMseqs2 against the RepeatPeps database (18,011 proteins, release 26 Oct 2018), merging hits with >80% Tcov. Despite this, some Adintoviridae, Retroviridae and Metaviridae-like sequences were retained. Given their high copy number and TE-like features, these were removed, leading to the exclusion of 47.4% of initial candidates.

Arguments for Endogenization

To distinguish true EVEs from free viral fragments, we validated candidates based on sequencing depth and genomic context. Reads were mapped using Bwa-mem2 v2.2.1, and sequencing depth calculated with samtools v1.19.2 (Li et al. 2009). Candidate scaffolds were compared with nuclear genome scaffolds containing BUSCO genes. A scaffold was considered nuclear if it met at least one of the following criteria: (i) Presence of a BUSCO gene, (ii) sequencing depth within the 20th to 80th percentile of the BUSCO-containing scaffolds' depth distribution, and (iii) depth within the 5th–95th percentile and presence of transposable elements, since viruses generally lack TEs (Miller and Miller 1982; Gilbert et al. 2014; Loiseau et al. 2020).

Assembly Quality and EVE Detection

To test the influence of assembly quality on EVE detection, we applied a phylogenetic generalized linear mixed model (PGLMM) with a Poisson distribution, using the pglmm function from the phyr R package, and our Diptera phylogeny (Ives et al. 2023). We compared high-quality DTOL assemblies to our lower-quality ones to assess differences in: (i) viral homolog detection and (ii) validation as integrated EVEs.

Clustering and EVEs Phylogenies

To investigate ancestral viral integrations, we clustered EVEs and their homologous viral proteins. We first retrieved RefSeq Virus proteins, RefSeq Diptera proteins, and EVEs. Diptera sequences were obtained from RefSeq using mmseqs filtertaxseqdb (Taxid 43738—Schizophora [subgroup of Diptera]). Clustering was performed using MMseqs2 with the –cluster-mode 1 parameter, bidirectional coverage (−cov-mode 0) and requiring a minimum of 30% overlap between sequences. An e-value threshold of 10-4 was set to retain significant matches. Only clusters containing both EVEs and viral sequences were retained. Clusters composed of >90% Refseq Diptera sequences were excluded, as they likely reflect host-to-virus transfers or ancient events predating Diptera diversification. For retained clusters with more than two sequences, phylogenetic trees were constructed. Sequences were aligned using Clustal Omega v1.2.3 and trimmed with TrimAl v1.4.rev15, with automated1 option (Sievers et al. 2011). Maximum likelihood phylogenies were generated using IQ-TREE multicore version 2.0.3 with -m MFP and -alrt 1000 (Nguyen et al. 2015; Chernomor et al. 2016; Hoang et al. 2018).

Search for Domestication Event Signature

To detect potential shared viral domestication events, we searched for EVEs meeting two criteria: insertion in the same genomic region across multiple related species (implying an ancestral integration) and being under purifying selection (dN/dS < 1). Shared loci were inferred by comparing the genomic context around EVEs. For each candidate, we extracted its scaffold and predicted coding sequences using MetaEuk with the UniRef90 database (Levy Karin et al. 2020). Homology searches with MMseqs2 search were used to identify shared upstream/downstream genes. If flanking genes were also of viral origin, we extended the analysis to a 200 kb window upstream and downstream until non-viral genes were encountered, as viral sequences are often co-integrated in blocks. Purifying selection was evaluated using dN/dS estimates. Codon alignments were generated with MACSE v1.02 (Ranwez et al. 2018), trimmed using TrimAl v1.4.rev15 with options: -automated1, -backtrans, -resoverlap 0.30, -seqoverlap 30, -fasta, and -ignorestopcodon. Analyses were run in PAML (codeml via ETE3) with parameters: CodonFreq = 4, estFreq = 1, getSE = 1 (Yang 1997). Significance was assessed using chi-squared tests and FDR correction (0.05) via Multipy (Puoliväli et al. 2020). Alignments with dS > 10 were excluded to avoid substitution saturation artifacts. Integration events were then placed on the species phylogeny when the EVE phylogeny was congruent with the species tree topology.

Method Sensitivity Assessment

To evaluate the sensitivity of our method, we used Leptopilina boulardi (GCF_019393585.1) as a positive control, since it is known that this parasitic wasp harbors a domesticated virus composed of 14 viral genes (Di Giovanni et al. 2020; Table S3). All 14 were detected in the homology search, and 9 remained after viral taxonomic assignment. These nine were subsequently confirmed as endogenized and domesticated.

Lifestyle and Group Effect on Viral Endogenization and Domestication

To assess whether parasitoid lifestyle influences EVE abundance, we applied the same pipeline we used on Tachinidae on 24 free-living Muscomorpha (Table S2b). We excluded DTOL assemblies to avoid quality bias. We used a PGLMM with a Poisson distribution (pglmm function, phyr R package), modeling EVE count as a function of lifestyle (parasitoid vs. free-living) while accounting for phylogenetic relatedness using our Diptera species tree (Ives et al. 2023).
