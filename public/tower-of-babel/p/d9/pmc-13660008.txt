Neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy versus chemotherapy for locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Yixing Li, Haozhe An, Fan Yang, Hanyu Li, Zhiyuan Wang, Yizhao Sun, Xingzhuo Zhu, Jinteng Feng, Guangjian Zhang, Hongyi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (NCIT) has shown promising activity in locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), but comparative evidence against neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NCT) alone remains limited. This study aimed to compare the pathological response and perioperative safety of NCIT versus NCT in patients with locally advanced ESCC.MethodsThis single-center retrospective cohort study included 199 patients with locally advanced ESCC who underwent neoadjuvant therapy followed by esophagectomy between 2017 and 2023. Among them, 131 patients received NCIT and 68 received NCT alone. Pathological response was assessed using major pathological response (MPR), pathological complete response (pCR), and tumor regression grade (TRG). Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) and postoperative outcomes, including 30-day and 90-day mortality, were evaluated. To reduce confounding from baseline imbalance, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was performed as the primary adjusted analysis.ResultsIn the overall cohort, MPR was achieved in 51 of 131 patients in the NCIT group and 17 of 68 patients in the NCT group (38.93% vs. 25.00%; absolute risk difference [ARD], + 13.93%; 95% CI, + 0.02% to + 26.18%; odds ratio [OR], 1.91; 95% CI, 1.00–3.67; P = 0.049). The distribution of TRG also favored NCIT (P = 0.015). After 1:1 propensity score matching, 67 matched pairs were analyzed, and the NCIT group continued to show a higher MPR rate than the NCT group (44.78% vs. 25.37%; ARD, + 19.40%; 95% CI, + 3.24% to + 34.23%; OR, 2.39; 95% CI, 1.15–4.96; P = 0.019). The pCR rate was numerically higher in the NCIT group but did not reach statistical significance. Treatment-related adverse events were comparable between the two groups (80.2% vs. 83.8%; P = 0.528). No deaths occurred within 30 days, whereas one death occurred within 90 days in each group.ConclusionNCIT was associated with improved major pathological response compared with NCT alone in patients with locally advanced ESCC, while maintaining comparable treatment-related toxicity and short-term postoperative safety. These findings support NCIT as a promising neoadjuvant strategy for locally advanced ESCC and provide a rationale for further prospective validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16569-z.Keywords: Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, Neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Pathological response, Retrospective cohort study

Introduction

Esophageal cancer remains a major global health burden, ranking among the leading causes of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is the predominant histological subtype in East Asia and is frequently diagnosed at a locally advanced stage. Although surgery remains the cornerstone of curative treatment, surgery alone is often insufficient for locally advanced disease because of high risks of recurrence and distant metastasis. Neoadjuvant treatment has therefore become an important strategy to improve tumor downstaging, resectability, and long-term outcomes [3, 4]. The CROSS trial demonstrated a sustained survival benefit of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy followed by surgery compared with surgery alone [5–7], supporting the value of preoperative treatment. Nevertheless, recurrence remains common, highlighting the need for more effective neoadjuvant strategies in locally advanced ESCC.

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting the programmed death-1/programmed death ligand-1 (PD-1/PD-L1) pathway have substantially changed the treatment landscape of esophageal cancer. The biological rationale for integrating ICIs into neoadjuvant therapy is supported by the immunogenic features of ESCC, including PD-L1 expression and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, as well as evidence that chemotherapy may modulate the tumor immune microenvironment by increasing PD-L1 expression and CD8-positive T-cell infiltration [8, 9]. Clinically, several pivotal trials have demonstrated the efficacy of ICIs in advanced esophageal cancer [10–12]. KEYNOTE-181 showed improved survival with pembrolizumab in patients with advanced esophageal cancer and high PD-L1 expression [13]. The ESCORT trial confirmed the survival benefit of camrelizumab as second-line therapy for advanced or metastatic ESCC [14], while ESCORT-1st and ORIENT-15 further demonstrated the benefit of combining PD-1 inhibitors with chemotherapy in the first-line setting [15, 16]. In addition, JUPITER-06 and CheckMate 648 established survival advantages for toripalimab- or nivolumab-based regimens in advanced ESCC [17, 18], and CheckMate 577 supported the role of adjuvant nivolumab after neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy and resection in patients with residual pathological disease [19]. These findings provide a strong clinical basis for exploring ICIs in the perioperative management of ESCC.

Recently, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (NCIT) has shown encouraging pathological response and acceptable perioperative safety in patients with locally advanced ESCC [20–29]. Several early-phase and retrospective studies have reported pathological complete response rates of approximately 25% – 35% after PD-1 inhibitor plus chemotherapy regimens [30–35]. These ICIs, including camrelizumab, pembrolizumab, sintilimab, toripalimab, tislelizumab, durvalumab, and atezolizumab, have demonstrated promising activity in this setting [31–41]. However, comparative data between NCIT and neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NCT) alone remain limited [20], and concerns persist regarding treatment selection bias, heterogeneity of immunotherapy agents, pathological surrogate endpoints, and perioperative safety.

Therefore, this single-center retrospective cohort study compared the pathological response and safety of NCIT versus NCT alone in patients with locally advanced ESCC who underwent subsequent esophagectomy. To reduce potential confounding, propensity score matching was performed as the primary adjusted analysis. This study aimed to provide further evidence on the efficacy and feasibility of NCIT as a neoadjuvant strategy for locally advanced ESCC.

Methods

Patients

Patients with ESCC who received NCT or NCIT between 2017 and 2023 at a single center were screened. Inclusion criteria were: Ⅰ. Pathologically confirmed ESCC; Ⅱ. Preoperative neoadjuvant therapy with 2–4 cycles of immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy or chemotherapy alone; Ⅲ. Normal hematological, renal, hepatic, cardiac, and pulmonary function; Ⅳ. Underwent radical surgery for esophageal cancer. The exclusion criteria were: Ⅰ. Preoperative radiotherapy; Ⅱ. Missing postoperative pathological data; Ⅲ. Substantial missing medical records of tests and examinations; Ⅳ. Diagnosis of cM1 before the neoadjuvant treatment.

These clinical data were collected from the Biobank of First Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University, and were collected by professionals. All patients were diagnosed by chest and abdominal CT, esophageal endoscopy, and tumor biopsy. Patients who discontinued preoperative neoadjuvant therapy due to intolerance to immunological or chemotherapeutic drugs or for other reasons were excluded. From January 2017 to December 2023, 199 patients with ESCC were retrospectively included in this study. The median age was 64 years and 161 patients were male (80.9%). All patients had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score of ≤ 1. Among 199 patients, 131 received NCIT, and 68 received NCT alone. The process of patient screening is shown in Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of patient enrollment

Neoadjuvant treatment

For patients who received NCT only, the chemotherapy regimen was mainly platinum combined with paclitaxel. Patients in the NCIT group received ICI therapy in addition to the chemotherapy regimen. After completion of neoadjuvant therapy, all patients underwent preoperative reassessment by the multidisciplinary team. Surgery was generally scheduled within 3–4 weeks after the last cycle of neoadjuvant therapy. Preoperative examinations, including CT scans, were completed within 2 weeks before surgery to evaluate operative feasibility and the suitability of minimally invasive esophagectomy. The detailed treatment plan distribution can be found in Supplementary (Table S1). The neoadjuvant and surgical treatment plans for all patients were evaluated and implemented by the same multidisciplinary medical team.

Clinical outcome

The efficacy of treatment was measured with major pathological response (MPR) and pathologic complete response (pCR) as the observational endpoints. Pathological examination was performed on the resected esophageal specimens and regional lymph nodes after operation, and the effect of chemotherapy was further evaluated by recording MPR and pCR. Pathological biopsy showed that the viable tumor cells were less than 10%, which was MPR, and no residual cancer cells were found in pathology, which was pCR. After each chemotherapy, the general conditions of the patients were recorded and blood biochemical examinations were carried out. The adverse reactions were recorded according to the American Common Adverse Reaction Terminology Evaluation Standard Version 5.0, and the mortality rate of the patients 30 days and 90 days after the operation was recorded to evaluate the safety of the treatment.

Pathological response to neoadjuvant therapy was evaluated using the College of American Pathologists (CAP) tumor regression grade (TRG) system based on examination of resected primary tumor specimens. Systematic long-term postoperative follow-up was not part of this study design; therefore, recurrence-free survival and overall survival were not analyzed. Tumor regression was graded as TRG 0 (no residual cancer cells), TRG 1 (single cells or small groups of cancer cells), TRG 2 (residual cancer with evident tumor regression but more than single cells or small groups), and TRG 3 (extensive residual cancer with no evident tumor regression). pCR was defined as TRG 0.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range) and compared with the Mann–Whitney U test; categorical variables as counts (%) and compared with Pearson’s chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test when any expected count was below five. Ordinal endpoints (including TRG) were compared with the Mann–Whitney U test. For the primary efficacy endpoints (MPR and pCR), absolute risk differences (ARD) were reported with Newcombe–Wilson hybrid score 95% confidence intervals, odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals, and the number needed to treat, calculated as 1/ARD.

Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed as the primary adjusted analysis. The propensity score was estimated by logistic regression on eight prespecified covariates (sex, age, BMI, smoking, alcohol, tumor location, chemotherapy regimen, and number of cycles; Table S2), and one-to-one nearest-neighbor matching without replacement was conducted with a caliper of 0.2 SD on the logit propensity-score scale; balance was assessed by standardized mean differences (Table S3). The association between treatment and MPR was additionally examined in the full cohort using a multivariable logistic-regression model adjusting for clinical N stage (modelled as an ordinal variable, with a binary node-positive versus node-negative coding used as a robustness check). For the exploratory comparison of pathological response among individual ICIs, agent-specific MPR and pCR rates are presented with 95% Wilson score confidence intervals, and between-agent differences were assessed using the Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test owing to small per-agent cell counts. All tests were two-sided; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed in Python 3.11 (NumPy, SciPy, scikit-learn, pandas).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 199 patients with locally advanced ESCC were included, with 68 in the NCT group and 131 in the NCIT group. The median age of the overall cohort was 64 (57–68) years. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the two groups except clinical N stage (Table 1). No significant differences were observed between the two groups with respect to sex, age distribution, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, alcohol consumption history, tumor location or clinical T stage (all P > 0.05). These findings indicate that the baseline characteristics of the two treatment groups were well balanced. Neoadjuvant treatment regimens

All patients received platinum-based chemotherapy regimens as neoadjuvant treatment. Chemotherapy regimens were categorized as doublet or non-doublet (including triplet and quadruplet combinations) according to the number of agents used. All patients received platinum-based neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Regimens were classified by the number of cytotoxic drug classes as doublet (two classes) or non-doublet (three or more classes). Doublet regimens predominated in both groups, and the proportion of patients receiving doublet chemotherapy was comparable between the NCT and NCIT groups (94.1% [64/68] vs. 94.7% [124/131]; P = 1.000). Detailed chemotherapy regimens for each category are provided in Supplementary (Table S1). In the NCIT group, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) were administered concurrently with chemotherapy. The majority of patients in both groups received two cycles of neoadjuvant therapy prior to surgery (NCT: 80.9%; NCIT: 59.5%). The number of neoadjuvant cycles differed significantly between the two groups (Table 2).

Treatment-related adverse events

Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were observed in 162 patients in the overall cohort. The incidence of TRAEs was comparable between the two groups, occurring in 57 patients (83.8%) in the NCT group and 105 patients (80.2%) in the NCIT group. Gastrointestinal reactions were also common. Among them, decreased appetite and nausea occurred significantly less frequently in the NCIT group compared with the NCT group (P = 0.005; P = 0.033). Other adverse events, including vomiting, leukopenia, neutropenia, thrombocytopenia, and liver function abnormalities, did not differ significantly between the two groups (all P > 0.05) (Table 3). Overall, the addition of immunotherapy did not result in a significant increase in treatment-related toxicity.

Tumor response after neoadjuvant therapy

Tumor response was evaluated using both radiological measurements and pathological assessment. For radiological evaluation, the longest tumor diameter and tumor length were measured on chest CT scans before and after neoadjuvant therapy. Baseline radiological tumor measurements were similar between the two groups. After neoadjuvant therapy, the NCIT group showed a statistically significant greater reduction in both the longest tumor diameter and tumor length in transverse section compared with the NCT group (Table 4). Furthermore, pathological response was assessed in the entire cohort of 199 patients. In the NCIT group, MPR was achieved in 51 of 131 patients (38.93%), compared with 17 of 68 patients (25.00%) in the NCT group (Table 4). The absolute risk difference was + 13.93% (Newcombe 95% CI + 0.02% to + 26.18%; Pearson chi-square = 3.86, P = 0.049), corresponding to an unadjusted odds ratio of 1.91 (95% CI 1.00–3.67) and a number needed to treat of 7.2 patients (95% CI 3.8 to ∞; the wide upper bound mirrors the closeness of the lower bound of the ARD 95% CI to zero) to achieve one additional MPR. The pCR rate was 23.66% (31/131) in the NCIT group versus 16.18% (11/68) in the NCT group, with an ARD of + 7.49% (95% CI -4.85% to + 18.02%; P = 0.220). In addition, the distribution of TRG compared significantly between the two groups (P = 0.015), indicating improved pathological tumor regression in the NCIT group.

Propensity score matching was adopted as the primary adjusted analysis to reduce baseline imbalance between the NCT and NCIT groups. Because clinical N stage differed significantly between the two groups at baseline, we first performed logistic regression analyses to evaluate whether this imbalance could substantially influence the primary pathological endpoint, MPR. When clinical N stage was modelled as an ordinal covariate together with treatment, the adjusted odds ratio for NCIT versus NCT was 1.92 (95% CI, 0.99–3.72; P = 0.054), whereas clinical N stage itself was not associated with MPR (per-category odds ratio, 1.01; P = 0.95). Similar findings were obtained when clinical N stage was coded as a binary node-positive versus node-negative variable, with an adjusted odds ratio for NCIT versus NCT of 1.84 (95% CI, 0.95–3.58), and no association between nodal positivity and MPR (odds ratio, 0.84; P = 0.57). These results suggested that the baseline imbalance in clinical N stage was unlikely to have a major impact on the observed difference in MPR. Based on these findings and the covariate-selection diagnostic, 1:1 nearest-neighbour propensity-score matching without replacement was performed as the primary adjusted analysis using the eight Brookhart-selected covariates (sex, age, BMI, smoking, alcohol, tumour location, chemotherapy regimen and number of cycles), yielding 67 well-balanced matched pairs with all post-matching standardized mean differences within the acceptable range (≤ 0.25; Table S3). After matching, MPR was achieved in 30 of 67 NCIT patients (44.8%) versus 17 of 67 NCT patients (25.4%), an absolute risk difference of + 19.40% (Newcombe 95% CI + 3.24% to + 34.23%; P = 0.019) with a matched odds ratio of 2.39 (95% CI 1.15–4.96) and a matched number needed to treat of 5.2 patients (95% CI 2.9 to 30.9). The matched pCR rate was 28.4% (19/67) versus 16.4% (11/67) (OR 2.02, 95% CI 0.87–4.69; P = 0.097; Table 5).

Because the NCIT group received several different ICIs, we performed an exploratory subgroup analysis of pathological response by agent (n = 131). Two patients who received more than one ICI during neoadjuvant therapy were each assigned to the agent used in their predominant regimen. Camrelizumab (n = 47) and tislelizumab (n = 32) were the most frequently used agents, followed by sintilimab (n = 19), pembrolizumab (n = 17) and toripalimab (n = 12), with the remaining agents grouped together (n = 4). MPR and pCR rates were broadly comparable across agents, with no significant between-agent difference (MPR, P = 0.76; pCR, P = 0.14; Fisher–Freeman–Halton exact test), and the 95% confidence intervals overlapped substantially (Figure S2 and Table S4). As all agents were PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors targeting the same immune checkpoint axis, these findings support pooling the ICIs in the primary analysis, although the small number of patients per agent limits the power of this comparison.

Surgical outcomes

All 199 patients successfully underwent esophagectomy following neoadjuvant therapy. The overall distribution of surgical approaches did not differ significantly between the two groups (P = 0.889), and all operations were performed with radical (R0) intent by the same multidisciplinary surgical team. Minimally invasive McKeown esophagectomy was the most commonly performed procedure in both groups. Among patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery, three cases in the NCT group required conversion to open surgery due to intraoperative findings, including tumor proximity to the left inferior pulmonary vein, excessive intraoperative bleeding, and lymph node encasement of the left gastric vessels; no conversions occurred in the NCIT group. Pleural effusion was the most common postoperative complication in both groups of patients, and there was one postoperative death case in each group within 90 days (P = 1.000) (Table 6). Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study of patients with locally advanced ESCC who underwent neoadjuvant therapy followed by esophagectomy, NCIT was associated with improved pathological response compared with NCT alone [20]. The improvement was most evident for MPR, which remained higher in the NCIT group after propensity score matching [42]. The TRG distribution also favored NCIT, indicating a greater degree of tumor regression. These findings suggest that NCIT may enhance pathological tumor regression, particularly MPR, while the strength of inference should remain proportional to the retrospective design and the use of pathological surrogate endpoints [42, 43].

ICIs provide a strong biological rationale for neoadjuvant treatment in ESCC. PD-1/PD-L1 signaling suppresses antitumor T-cell activity and contributes to immune escape, while PD-L1 expression and immune-cell infiltration are frequently observed in locally advanced ESCC [44–46]. Moreover, NCT may modulate the tumor immune microenvironment by increasing PD-L1 expression and CD8-positive tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, potentially enhancing sensitivity to subsequent or concurrent immunotherapy [47, 48]. Clinically, ICIs have demonstrated survival benefits in advanced esophageal cancer, including pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy in KEYNOTE-590 and nivolumab-based therapy in CheckMate-649 [10–12, 49, 50]. These findings support the extension of ICI-based strategies from advanced disease to the neoadjuvant setting.

Accumulating studies have reported encouraging pathological responses with neoadjuvant ICI plus chemotherapy in locally advanced ESCC [20–29]. Previous studies using ICIs-based regimens have reported pCR rates of approximately 25%–35.3% [20–41]. In the present study, the NCIT group achieved a pCR rate of 23.7% and an MPR rate of 38.9%, which are broadly consistent with previous evidence [20–29, 42].

The heterogeneity of ICI agents is another important issue. Several PD-1 or PD-L1 inhibitors were used in this cohort, mainly camrelizumab and tislelizumab, reflecting routine clinical practice and drug availability. Although these agents share the same PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint axis, they differ in pharmacologic characteristics and supporting clinical evidence [20–29]. Exploratory subgroup analyses did not show statistically significant differences in MPR or pCR among individual agents; however, due to the small sample size of the subgroups, the pooling of immune checkpoint inhibitors is a practical approach in this retrospective, practice-based cohort study. Therefore, these results should not be interpreted as evidence that the efficacy of different drugs is comparable. In addition, treatment period differed between groups because NCIT was introduced later than NCT in clinical practice. Owing to the limited sample size and insufficient overlap across calendar years, we were unable to perform a robust year-restricted or year-adjusted analysis without causing model instability or substantial loss of matched patients. Thus, residual confounding related to treatment era cannot be excluded, and larger-scale cohort studies are warranted to further clarify.

Safety is an important consideration when integrating immunotherapy into neoadjuvant treatment strategies [20–29]. In our cohort, the overall incidence of treatment-related adverse events was comparable between the NCIT and NCT groups, and the most common adverse events were hematologic toxicities and gastrointestinal symptoms, which were generally manageable. Previous studies have reported that immune-related adverse events associated with PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors are usually mild to moderate, although grade ≥ 3 adverse events may occur in a subset of patients receiving neoadjuvant ICI-based regimens [33, 39, 51, 52]. Consistent with these findings, the addition of immunotherapy did not appear to increase treatment-related toxicity in our cohort. One postoperative death occurred within 90 days in each group, due to esophageal bleeding and severe infection, respectively. These findings suggest that NCIT was feasible and did not show an obvious increase in short-term safety risk in this cohort.

The potential impact of neoadjuvant immunotherapy on surgical difficulty also deserves attention [20–29]. In this study, surgical approach and anastomotic technique were comparable between groups, and postoperative complications were not significantly increased in the NCIT group. Three patients in the NCT group required conversion to open surgery, whereas no conversion occurred in the NCIT group; however, this observation should not be overinterpreted because conversion events were uncommon and may be influenced by tumor location, local invasion, lymph node status, and surgeon judgment. With respect to tissue adherence, most patients in the NCIT group did not show severe intraoperative adhesion, which is consistent with previous reports suggesting that esophagectomy after neoadjuvant ICI plus chemotherapy is generally feasible [20–29, 53–55]. Overall, NCIT did not appear to substantially increase perioperative complexity in this cohort, but further prospective studies are needed to clarify its impact on operative difficulty and postoperative morbidity.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective single-center design may lead to selection bias and residual confounding despite the use of PSM. Treatment allocation may also have been affected by temporal changes in clinical practice, immunotherapy availability, perioperative management, and surgeon experience. Second, the exclusion of patients with incomplete medical records, particularly in the chemotherapy cohort, may have influenced cohort representativeness. Third, multiple PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors were used in the NCIT group, and the exploratory subgroup analysis was limited by small sample sizes. Finally, this study mainly evaluated pathological response and short-term safety, and systematic long-term postoperative follow-up was not performed. Prospective studies with standardized follow-up are needed to determine whether the improvement in pathological response translates into better recurrence-free or overall survival.

Conclusion

In this single-center retrospective cohort of patients with locally advanced ESCC, NCIT was associated with a higher MPR rate than NCT alone, particularly in the propensity score-matched analysis, without evidence of increased treatment-related toxicity or short-term postoperative mortality. However, pCR did not differ significantly between groups. These findings should therefore be interpreted as supportive but not definitive evidence for NCIT, and require validation in larger multicenter prospective studies with long-term survival follow-up.
