Weakened macrophage antibacterial capacity in myelodysplastic syndrome
Authors: Johnathon B Schafer, Kenneth C Malcolm, Jessica Dell-Martin, Maria L Amaya, Daniel A Pollyea, Scott Alper
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPatients with myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) are immunocompromised and are therefore susceptible to fatal infection. While neutropenia and neutrophil dysfunction account for much of this immunodeficiency, other immune cells likely also contribute. In contrast to the extensive study of neutrophils in MDS, there has been very little investigation of macrophage host defense function in MDS. In the current study, we find that macrophage differentiation and macrophage phagocytosis of bacteria are greatly weakened in patients with MDS, regardless of patient genotype. Moreover, we find that killing of those bacteria that are ingested is diminished in MDS patients with high-risk disease. Using a mouse model that expresses the MDS-associated U2AF1-S34F mutation, we find that this mutation is sufficient to induce macrophage functional defects. We conclude that macrophage host defense defects likely contribute to the immunodeficiency present in MDS.

Introduction

Myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) is a hematological malignancy characterized by arrested myeloid cell differentiation and dysplastic blood cell production, with many MDS patients progressing to acute myeloid leukemia (AML).1–3 Patients with MDS are at increased risk of both acquiring and dying from severe infection, with bacteria the most common pathogen type recovered from these patients.4–11 MDS patients often are neutropenic, and those neutrophils that are generated exhibit numerous functional defects.12–17 While neutropenia and neutrophil dysfunction in MDS patients account for some of the increased infection susceptibility present in MDS, dysfunction in other immune cells likely also contributes.6,7,12,14,17–19 Thus far, the host defense functions of immune cells other than neutrophils in MDS patients have garnered much less study.

Macrophages from patients with myeloid malignancies are reported to have diminished phagocytosis capacity,20–25 but these studies have mostly been performed in patients with the more severe myeloid malignancy AML.20–23 Moreover, these AML studies have focused on ingestion of leukemia cells, rather than on ingestion of bacteria. The few studies investigating macrophages in MDS patients have likewise analyzed phagocytosis in the context of tumor killing, rather than host defense, or have analyzed expression levels of markers of macrophage function.24–28 These studies are consistent with the hypothesis that the host defense functions of macrophages in MDS patients are compromised, but this has not been directly assessed. Moreover, to our knowledge, the ability of macrophages from MDS patients to kill bacteria once ingested has never been monitored. Finally, the effects of MDS-associated mutations on macrophage host defense functions have not been investigated previously.

Here, we investigated peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC)–derived macrophages isolated from a large cohort of patients with MDS. We found that monocytes from MDS patients differentiate into macrophages less efficiently than monocytes derived from healthy donors. Those macrophages that are generated are severely compromised in the ability to phagocytose bacteria. Interestingly, macrophages from MDS patients exhibited a range of bacterial killing capacities, with macrophage killing capacity severely compromised in patients with high-risk disease and macrophage killing capacity largely unaffected in individuals with lower-risk disease.

Numerous somatically acquired mutations have been identified in MDS patients.29–32 These mutations fall into a variety of broad classes including mutations in genes that encode epigenetic regulators (DNA or histone modifiers), transcription factors, and spliceosome components.29–32 Spliceosome mutations are the most common class of mutation in MDS, with roughly half of all MDS patients having somatically acquired mutations in the pre–messenger RNA (mRNA) splicing machinery.33 We and others have found that spliceosome mutations alter innate immune signaling by altering pre-mRNA splicing in immune signaling pathways,34–37 making this class of genes prime candidates to drive immune dysfunction. Notably, we previously found that the MDS-associated U2AF1-S34F spliceosome mutation was able to induce immunodeficiency by compromising neutrophil chemotaxis.38 In contrast, the molecular drivers of macrophage dysfunction in MDS patients have not been determined. Here, we find that bacterial phagocytosis is defective in macrophages from MDS patients with spliceosome gene mutations as well as other classes of mutations. Using a mouse model that expresses the MDS-associated U2AF1-S34F mutation, we determined that U2AF1-S34F was sufficient to drive macrophage phagocytosis defects in mice engineered to express this mutation.

Methods

Collection of PBMCs from MDS patients

The collection and analysis of samples from untreated MDS patients and healthy donors was approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (IRB), the Rocky Mountain Regional VA IRB, and the National Jewish Health IRB. All subjects gave informed written consent. Peripheral blood samples were obtained from patients with MDS at the University of Colorado Cancer Center and the Rocky Mountain Regional VA. Patient clinical parameters were collected through retrospective chart review; genotyping was performed with a next-generation targeted sequencing panel used for patient diagnostics at the University of Colorado Cancer Center.39 Peripheral blood samples from age- and sex-matched healthy control subjects were obtained from the National Jewish Health Human Cell Core and Charles River Cell Solutions. These healthy volunteers answered questionnaires to ensure that they were not experiencing signs of acute infection, did not have underlying chronic conditions, and were not taking corticosteroids. Generation of human PBMC-derived macrophages and quantification of differentiation efficiency

PBMCs were incubated in the presence of RPMI (ATCC modified) containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, and 50 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) (PeproTech; 300-25) for 7 d to induce differentiation of monocytes into macrophages, with media changes 6 h after initial plating and again after 5 d. To quantify differentiation, samples were taken from the thawed patient PBMC samples before and after differentiation; cells were counted using an automated cell counter. Cell differentials were determined by staining cytospin slides with Hema 3 (Fisher Scientific; catalog numbers 23-122929, 23-122937, and 23-122952) following the manufacturer’s instructions and subsequently analyzing cell morphology by microscopy. The efficiency of monocyte differentiation was calculated comparing the number of monocytes before differentiation to the number of viable macrophages after differentiation.

Mouse studies

All mouse studies were approved by the National Jewish Health Animal Care and Use Committee. To examine the effects of 1 MDS-associated mutation in mice, we compared rtTA, U2AF1-S34F mice to control rtTA, U2AF1-WT mice.40 These mice are engineered for doxycycline-inducible expression of either U2AF1-S34F or U2AF1-WT. Mice were fed chow containing doxycycline for 10 d to induce U2AF1 transgene expression prior to analysis. To generate thioglycolate-elicited peritoneal macrophages, 1.5 mL of 4% thioglycolate was injected via intraperitoneal injection 3 d before the mice were analyzed. Macrophages were collected via peritoneal lavage; cytospin staining confirmed that the cells were >95% macrophages.

Analysis of macrophage phagocytosis

Macrophages were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (mouse cells) or RPMI (ATCC modified, human cells) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. Macrophages were seeded onto 96-well plates at either 25,000 cells per well for human cells or 100,000 cells per well for mouse cells. Cells were allowed to adhere overnight, and phagocytosis of fluorescently labeled Escherichia coli particles was assayed using the Vybrant phagocytosis assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Uptake of fluorescent E. coli particles was monitored using a plate reader and is reported in arbitrary fluorescence units.

Analysis of bacterial killing by macrophages

Macrophages were seeded onto 96-well plates at either 25,000 cells per well for human cells or 100,000 cells per well for mouse cells. Following overnight incubation, the macrophages were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and media lacking antibiotics was added to the wells. E. coli strain H904941,42 that had been grown overnight in LB, centrifuged, and resuspended in PBS was then added to the macrophages at a multiplicity of infection of 5:1. Uptake of the bacteria was allowed to proceed for 2 h. Further incubation was then performed in the presence of 50 µg/mL gentamicin to kill extracellular bacteria. The percentage change in colony-forming units between 5 and 90 min after addition of gentamicin was quantified by lysing macrophages with 1% Tween 20 in PBS and plating serial dilutions of lysate.

Quantitation of candidate gene expression

RNA was isolated using RNAeasy mini kits (Qiagen). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) was performed using the QuantiTect SYBR-Green RT-PCR kit (Qiagen) with a QuantStudio 7 Flex (Applied Biosystems). Gene expression was normalized relative to GAPDH using the ddCt method. Sequences of primers used for qPCR are listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software). Each dot presented in the figures represents a single individual (mouse or human). Phagocytosis and bacterial killing were analyzed using ≥3 technical replicates per mouse or 1 to 3 technical replicates per human sample (depending on sample availability). qPCR was performed using technical replicates for all samples. t tests were used for the analysis comparing U2AF1-WT to U2AF1-S34F mouse macrophages. The t tests with Welch’s correction were used to compare macrophages generated from healthy human donors to macrophages from MDS patients. The t tests with Welch’s correction of log-transformed data were used to analyze the qPCR data comparing healthy human donors to MDS patients. Statistical significance was considered P < 0.05. In some studies, MDS patient samples were stratified by the class of mutation present in those patients. We chose to analyze 4 possible mutation classes: spliceosome mutations (Splice), mutations in DNA-modifying enzymes (DNA), mutations in histone modifiers (Histone), and mutations in transcription factors (Transcription).29–32 Because many MDS patients harbor several mutations, some patients appear in multiple categories. In other studies, MDS patients were stratified by International Prognostic Scoring System–Revised (IPSS-R) score. Statistical comparison between two groups of MDS samples using t tests with Welch’s correction are marked with a bar over the two groups in figures. For comparison between MDS groups that significantly deviated from normal distribution (P < 0.01 by D’Agostino and Pearson omnibus normality test), a Mann-Whitney test was used.

Results

Differentiation of monocytes into macrophages is impaired in MDS patients

The fraction of monocytes present in the peripheral blood from MDS patients was similar to that in peripheral blood from age- and sex-matched healthy donors (Table 1, Fig. 1A). PBMC-derived macrophages from MDS patients were generated by incubating PBMCs with M-CSF. Even though monocytes were present in the peripheral blood of MDS patients, these cells were much less efficient at differentiating into macrophages when compared with monocytes from healthy control subjects, with a roughly 10-fold decrease in mean differentiation efficiency (Fig. 1B). To determine if specific classes of MDS-associated mutations might impact differentiation efficiency, we stratified the patient samples into 4 categories that encompassed almost all patients: patients with mutations in the spliceosome machinery (Splice), mutations in epigenetic regulators that modify DNA (DNA), mutations in epigenetic regulators that modify histones (Histone), and mutations in transcription factors (Transcription). The decreased differentiation efficiency of monocytes was observed in MDS patients with mutations in any of these categories (Fig. 1B), suggesting that multiple MDS-associated mutations can impair monocyte to macrophage differentiation.

The number of mutations present in MDS patients has been implicated as a possible measure of disease severity with 3 or more mutations associated with worse outcome.43,44 Consistent with the possibility that more MDS-associated mutations might lead to worsened disease, PBMCs from patients harboring ≥3 mutations differentiated about half as efficiently as PBMCs from patients harboring <3 mutations, although both groups differentiated less efficiently than PBMCs from healthy donors (Fig. 1B).

Macrophages from MDS patients have weakened ability to phagocytose bacteria

To examine macrophage host defense function, we first monitored phagocytosis of FITC-labeled E. coli particles. PBMC-derived macrophages from patients with MDS exhibited decreased ability to phagocytose fluorescently labeled bacteria compared with macrophages from healthy control subjects, with macrophages from many MDS patients phagocytosing fluorescent bacteria close to the background reading (Fig. 2). Upon stratification of these data by MDS-associated mutation class, we found that macrophages from patients with mutations in genes encoding the spliceosome, DNA modifiers, histone modifiers, or transcription factors all exhibited significant phagocytosis defects (Fig. 2). This is consistent with the hypothesis that multiple MDS-associated driver mutations weaken macrophage phagocytosis. Further, macrophages from patients with <3 or ≥3 MDS-associated mutations both were severely compromised in their ability to phagocytose bacteria.

Macrophages from MDS patients with high-risk disease have a decreased capacity to kill ingested bacteria

Although the macrophage phagocytosis defects in MDS patients occurred in almost all MDS patient samples, we observed a varied ability of patient macrophages to kill live bacteria once ingested (Fig. 3A). To control for differences in bacterial uptake, we quantitated the percent of ingested bacteria killed in these assays. Macrophages from some patients exhibited near-complete bacterial killing, while macrophages from other patients failed to kill not only the bacteria, but also the bacteria actively replicated inside the macrophages (Fig. 3A). Although there was a small number of patient samples analyzed in some mutation categories, we note that macrophages derived from patients with mutations in the DNA modification machinery efficiently killed ingested bacteria while macrophages from patients with mutations in transcription factors failed to kill ingested bacteria (Fig. 3A).

The IPSS-R is a clinical measure of MDS severity used for prognostication. The IPSS-R incorporates patient hemoglobin levels, platelet counts, cytogenetics, bone marrow blast percentages and morphology, and absolute neutrophil counts.45,46 We observed that worsened disease status, as assessed by higher IPSS-R score, produced macrophages with weaker bactericidal capacity (Fig. 3B). Thus, high-risk MDS patients are more likely to produce macrophages that are unable to kill bacteria efficiently. In contrast, the IPSS-R score did not correlate with the ability of macrophages from MDS patients to phagocytose bacteria (not shown), suggesting that both low- and high-risk MDS patients produce macrophages with phagocytosis deficiency.

The MDS-associated U2AF1-S34F mutation is sufficient to induce macrophage phagocytosis defects

Our data demonstrated that the presence of multiple classes of MDS-associated mutations correlated with diminished macrophage phagocytosis (Fig. 2); however, by their nature, these association studies cannot demonstrate causality. To determine if any MDS-associated mutations were sufficient to drive these macrophage phagocytosis defects, we chose to investigate the function of macrophages isolated from mice expressing the MDS-associated U2AF1-S34F spliceosome mutation.40 Spliceosome mutations, including U2AF1 mutations, alter innate immune signaling pathways34–37; our studies suggest that this is because innate immune signaling is particularly sensitive to spliceosome perturbation.47–50 Moreover, we recently reported that the U2AF1-S34F mutation is sufficient to compromise neutrophil function.38

Bacterial phagocytosis was greatly weakened in macrophages from U2AF1-S34F mice compared with U2AF1-WT mice with uptake of fluorescently labeled bacteria roughly halved on average in the U2AF1 mutant samples (Fig. 4A). This indicates that the U2AF1-S34F mutation is sufficient to impair macrophage phagocytosis. We next examined the capacity of these macrophages to kill live E. coli bacteria. While macrophages harboring the U2AF1-S34F mutation phagocytosed fewer bacteria, the mutant macrophages were able to kill ingested E. coli with similar efficacy to macrophages from wild-type mice (Fig. 4B). Thus, even though the U2AF1-S34F mutation is sufficient to hinder phagocytosis, this mutation does not impair all macrophage host defense functions.

Misexpression of multiple genes likely contributes to the phagocytosis defect in macrophages from MDS patients

To identify candidate gene expression changes that might drive the phagocytosis defects in U2AF1 mutant macrophages, we extracted the list of “Phagocytosis” genes in the Gene Ontology Database51,52 and compared this gene list with a study that reported the effects of U2AF1-S34F mutation on gene expression, albeit in cell types other than macrophages.53 Several phagocytosis genes exhibited altered expression in mouse bone marrow cells harboring the U2AF1-S34F mutation.53 We further stratified these data to identify genes that were differentially expressed, with either a >30% increase or a >40% decrease in gene expression in U2AF1 mutant cells. Three genes that modulate phagocytosis met these criteria (YES1, THBS1, and CCL2). We reasoned that CCL2 expression changes are likely occurring in cells other than macrophages, so we chose to investigate YES1 and THBS1 further. We also identified additional phagocytosis genes in a literature review, and found 1 additional gene (PRKCA, the gene that encodes PKCα) whose expression was altered in this same dataset.53 Finally, in a prior study that reported phagocytosis defects in macrophages from MDS patients (although without stratification by patient genotype), the authors observed decreased expression of 2 other genes that impact phagocytosis, SIRPA and CD206.24 We therefore quantitated expression of these 5 genes (CD206, SIRPA, PRKCA, THBS1, and YES1) using qPCR in mouse macrophages harboring the U2AF1-S34F mutation and in human macrophages from MDS patients harboring different classes of mutations.

All 5 genes exhibited altered expression in macrophages from U2AF1-S34F mice compared with macrophages from U2AF1-WT mice, with Cd206 and Sirpa expression decreased and Prkca, Thbs1, and Yes1 expression increased (Fig. 5A–E). Thus, the U2AF1 mutation is sufficient to alter expression of all 5 of these genes that are known to impact phagocytosis. Strikingly, macrophages from MDS patients, regardless of mutation status, exhibited very similar changes in gene expression, with greatly decreased mRNA levels of CD206 and SIRPA and increased mRNA levels of PRKCA, THBS1, and YES1 (Fig. 5F–J).

Genes that regulate bacterial killing are misexpressed in macrophages from MDS patients

To identify candidate genes that might mediate the bactericidal defects in macrophages from MDS patients, we took a similar approach to that used in our phagocytosis analysis. We mined 3 published RNA sequencing studies that reported gene expression changes in MDS patient samples,54–56 albeit not in macrophages. Notably, we did not limit this analysis to patients with U2AF1 mutations, as U2AF1-S34F mutant macrophages did not have impaired bactericidal capacity (Fig. 4B). We compared these RNA sequencing results to the “Defense Response to Bacterium” GO category in the Gene Ontology Database.51,52 We selected genes with at least a 45% decrease in expression in MDS patient samples, that were reported to be expressed in macrophages, and that a literature review suggested were likely to act cell autonomously (i.e. cytokines were excluded). Ten genes met these criteria. We selected 5 of these genes to test further: LYZ, MAVS, AKIRIN2, PRKCD, and ANXA3 (the other 5 genes were PPP1R11, RAB14, and 3 S100 family members).

Four of these 5 candidate genes exhibited altered gene expression in macrophages from MDS patients (Fig. 6). Expression of LYZ and MAVS was decreased in macrophages from MDS patients and in most patient subsets when stratified by mutation class (Fig. 6A, B). Because macrophage bactericidal capacity was only decreased in patients with high-risk disease, as assessed by higher IPSS-R score, we examined if gene expression was different in macrophages from patients with low- or high-risk disease. LYZ gene expression was decreased in MDS patients in both IPSS-R risk categories (Fig. 6A). In contrast, MAVS expression was lower in the high-risk group but not in the low-risk group (Fig. 6B). Similarly, AKIRIN2 expression was lower in the high-risk but not the low-risk MDS patients (Fig. 6C). In contrast, despite its reported decreased expression in monocytes from MDS patients,55 ANXA3 expression was dramatically increased in macrophages from MDS patients, regardless of mutation group or IPSS-R risk group. Finally, PRKCD expression was not altered in macrophages from MDS patients (Fig. 6E).

Discussion

In summary, we found that monocytes from MDS patients exhibited a decreased ability to differentiate into macrophages, regardless of patient genotype. Our results using M-CSF as a differentiation factor are consistent with a prior study that determined that differentiation of monocytes into macrophages using GM-CSF was also impaired in MDS patients.24 Those macrophages that were generated exhibited substantial defects in bacterial phagocytosis, again regardless of patient genotype. These results suggest that multiple MDS-associated mutations can drive these macrophage functional defects. Our studies using mice that express the U2AF1-S34F mutation indicate that this 1 MDS-associated mutation is sufficient to drive these phagocytosis defects. Because U2AF1 mutations are only present in a subset of MDS patients, we assume that at least several other MDS-associated mutations would likewise be sufficient to weaken bacterial phagocytosis.

We also found that bactericidal capacity of macrophages from MDS patients varied considerably, suggesting that only certain MDS-associated mutations (or combinations of mutations) impact this macrophage host defense function. Nevertheless, the net result of diminished phagocytosis capacity and normal or weakened killing of ingested bacteria represents an overall decrease in macrophage bactericidal activity. Notably, worsened disease status as evidence by higher IPSS-R score correlated with decreased macrophage anti-bacterial capacity. These results suggest that patients with high-risk disease are more likely to produce macrophages with functional defects.

Prior studies demonstrated that neutropenia and neutrophil dysfunction contribute to immunodeficiency in MDS. Our current results suggest that defects in macrophage differentiation, phagocytosis, and bactericidal capacity also contribute to this immunodeficiency, although future studies examining the association of these defects with infection risk in patients are needed to confirm this proposition. Our data also suggest that multiple classes of MDS-associated mutations can drive these macrophage defects. Our mouse studies indicate that the U2AF1-S34F mutation is one such mutation that is sufficient to induce macrophage bacterial phagocytosis defects. In contrast, U2AF1-S34F mutation did not hinder killing of bacteria once ingested, suggesting that other MDS-associated mutations, alone or in combination, do so, and that these other mutations impact gene expression and splicing distinctly from U2AF1 mutation.

To identify potential mediators of the bacterial phagocytosis defect, we mined a published study53 that examined the effects of U2AF1 mutation on gene expression, albeit in nonmacrophage cell types. We determined that the U2AF1-S34F mutation was sufficient to decrease expression of Cd206 and Sirpa and increase expression of Prkca, Thbs1, and Yes1. Moreover, we observed similar changes in expression of these phagocytosis genes in macrophages from MDS patients, whether or not these patients harbored U2AF1 mutations. These results suggest that multiple MDS-associated mutations may impact phagocytosis by altering expression of a common set of phagocytosis genes.

CD206 is a scavenger receptor57 that enhances phagocytosis58,59; thus, the observed decrease in CD206 expression could directly hinder bacterial phagocytosis. SIRPα signaling can prevent phagocytosis.60–63 However, there is one study that demonstrates that in some tumor models, macrophages with downregulated SIRPα exhibit impaired phagocytosis,64 which may reflect effects of chronic SIRPα signaling on innate immunity. THBS1 is produced by both immune and nonimmune cells and exerts complicated effects on the immune system.65,66 Several studies suggest that THBS1 promotes phagocytosis,67–69 possibly by serving as a bridge between macrophages and apoptotic cells. However, these studies involved THBS1 provided from other cell sources. Studies investigating THBS1 produced in macrophages have shown that macrophage-derived THBS1 inhibits macrophage phagocytosis,70,71 which suggests that the increased THBS1 expression in MDS patients that we observed could likewise be inhibitory. PKCα57 and YES172 are signaling molecules that enhance phagocytosis. We speculate that overexpression, like underexpression, of these factors may perturb signaling pathways that impact phagocytosis.

Similarly, our studies identified several gene expression changes that may contribute to the bactericidal defects in MDS patients. These include downregulation of LYZ, which encodes lysozyme, an antibacterial protein that primarily affects Gram-positive, and to a lesser extent, Gram-negative bacteria.73–75 We likewise identified downregulation of AKIRIN2, a nuclear factor that bridges chromatin and nuclear factor κB activation that is required for robust defense against Gram-negative bacteria.76–78 Expression of MAVS, an RNA sensor that drives antiviral responses,79,80 was also decreased in macrophages from MDS patients, particularly those with high-risk disease. Viral infection does occur in MDS patients, although much more rarely than bacterial infection, and this defect in MAVS production could contribute to viral susceptibility.4,8,10 Moreover, under some circumstances, MAVS can influence the response to some bacteria.81,82 Finally, expression of ANXA3, which encodes a signaling protein that localizes to antibacterial granules in phagocytes,83–85 was massively increased in macrophages from MDS patients.

These expression changes in phagocytosis and bactericidal genes were present in patients harboring different classes of mutations. This broad gene expression defect observed across multiple genotypes suggests that multiple MDS-associated mutations can alter phagocytosis and bacterial killing by macrophages. Thus, our data suggest that MDS patient macrophage functional defects likely result from misexpression of multiple genes. Thus, normalizing the expression of no single gene likely would be sufficient to restore macrophage function in these patients.
