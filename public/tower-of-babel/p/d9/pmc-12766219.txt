Restitution or substitution after loss of a human hand: A personal view
Authors: Simon PJ Kay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe author summarises the differences between prosthetic care and allotransplantation in managing upper limb loss.Keywords: Upper limb, Prosthesis, Amputtaion, Allotransplantation of upper limb, Hand transplant

In 2010, a group of surgeons in Leeds,1 prompted by colleagues in solid organ transplantation, began the process of building support for the establishment of a hand transplantation service. None of us knew much about the field, and indeed, even a decade after the first successful hand transplants, there was little in the way of firm, reliable, evidence-based knowledge. That has changed only slowly, in large part due to the sporadic and uncontrolled nature of the small series of hand transplants conducted globally to date. The Leeds service was initiated with the dual intentions of treating patients and curating and reporting a prospective cohort over decades within a uniform, protocol-driven system.

Developing the service began with securing the confidence of key stakeholders, including NHSBT,i BAPRAS,ii NICE,iii and BSSH.iv Under the commissioning arrangements of the time, the most crucial support came from Leeds Teaching Hospitals, who generously agreed that the first two procedures would be undertaken ``at risk''—that is, with the Trust absorbing the costs upfront, hoping to recover them from the purchaser (primary care) post hoc.

We received generous and unstinting support from the hand transplant team in Lyon, France, under the leadership of Aram Gazarian and Emmanuel Morelon, who at that time had the greatest European experience in vascularized allotransplantation.2

Shortly after Christmas 2012, we performed our first transplant: a unilateral graft replacing a functionless hand in a patient who had lost almost all mobility following severe infection. His contralateral hand was compromised by gouty arthritis.

Since that first case, we have transplanted 20 limbs in 11 patients—now the largest and most diverse experience in the Western world. We have encountered our first failure (one limb of a bilateral transplant, abandoned intraoperatively due to idiopathic systemic shock) and one long-term, unrelated death three years post-transplant.

Emerging fields in surgery inevitably challenge our assumptions and offer fresh lessons. In this article, I outline some of the lessons we have learned and attempt to convey the profound excitement this field offers.

As a trainee, I learned the reconstructive ladder: a conceptual ascent in technical complexity to restore form and function. I also learned the distinction between the two Rs: Repair (healing wounds and perhaps enabling some functional return) and Reconstruction (restoring both form and function). Now, a third R enters the scene: replacement.

Faced with an upper limb stump, neither Repair nor Reconstruction (aside from the rather grotesque Kruckenberg procedure) is applicable and historically the only option for the patient was prostheses. For decades prosthesis designs changed very little, being either “cosmetic” (i.e. a usually rather risible attempt to produce a limb facsimile in a small range of skin shades and yet totally inert: like a toupée for the arm), or body-driven, which is to say animated by shoulder movement via cables. The latter, surprisingly effective, were often visually unacceptable due to the piratical cartoonish appearance of the split hook, even when draped in silicone skin.

Nonetheless, these cable-actuated devices remain the most effective, cheapest, and reliable solutions for many upper limb amputees. They offer modularity—varied peripherals can be attached for specific tasks—and continue to serve many patients well.

Then came a leap: powered prostheses, electronically actuated and capable of multi-axial motion. When combined with osseointegrated fittings, they represent a significant advance. But a fundamental issue remains: bandwidth. The link between brain and device remains narrow and with poor definition. Most transcutaneous sensing yields four high-noise channels. This may change as direct central implantation to the cerebral cortex of transducers (as exemplified by Neuralink™,3 BrainGate™,4 and others) may eventually offer thousands of channels with greater specificity and finer control.

So why is the UK hand transplant service persisting, rather than ceding the future to increasingly sophisticated prosthetics, instead of the pharmacologically riskier world of transplantation and immunosuppression?

Before I answer that, we may take a brief diversion into the speculative realm of evolutionary anthropology. When, around six million years ago, hominin species adopted bipedalism,5 abandoning the arboreal life for erect living on the edge of the African savannah, the hands were freed to evolve differently6 and to become dedicated to tasks other than brachiating and locomotion. The eyes, raised higher above the ground, developed stereoscopic distance vision. It may be supposed that these adaptations led to the development of gesturing, pointing (a uniquely human attribute7,8), communication, language, and tool making which, together with the emergence of larger and larger brains, heralded the emergence of symbolic and cultural behaviors.

Whatever the causative links between these changes, what is not often considered is how the hands and eyes cooperate in a ring of face-hand-hand-face. The hands are supervised by the eyes, and the eyes are fed objects for inspection by the hands, whilst each hand collaborates with the other to derive information and execute procedures. This collaboration between hand and hand, and between the hands and the center (consisting of the mouth, eyes, and brain) relies upon a complexity of afferent and efferent signaling supervised by a highly evolved central nervous architecture. It is difficult to imagine how any prosthetic limb could play a role in this arrangement comparable to that of a normal upper limb. We can consider the hands as paired organs, mutually cooperating and combining in such a way that it may even be better to think of them as a single organ, of which each hand is an essential half. More akin to the left and right heart which beat as one, than to the mutually independent kidneys, say. The hands dwell in an ecosystem with the eyes, and so with the brain, in a far more complex arrangement than for the legs and feet, whose duties are more automatic, less communicative and need less supervision, and are more satisfactorily replaced by a lower limb prosthesis.

Many people view the hands as mechanical effectors at the ends of multi-articulated reaching arms, and even hand surgeons have a more mechanistically focused lexicon for the hands than is deserved. We speak of angles and grasp patterns, power and range of motion, whilst also mentioning some afferent qualities like sensibility. But all these descriptors and metrics bypass the integrated, complex and cultural functions which characterize the human hand. The gesturing, the stroking and embracing, the comforting of a poorly child by the warmth and soft caress of a parent’s hands, their role in intimacy and sexual behavior and a myriad of other higher functions that we construct from attributes of the evolutionarily liberated upper extremities. We can all reflect and identify many other complex composite behaviors that use the human hands9 in collaboration with each other and with our eyes, directed by, and deriving from, that master of all behavior, the paired halves of the human cerebral cortices. Despite decades of research across many disciplines we are only now beginning to appreciate the complexity of the mutually interdependent processes in which the hands participate and which contribute to the essential qualities of being human.

Prostheses can so far only really address the mechanical properties of the upper limbs, and they manage those poorly. Their capacity to emulate the complexity of the evolved and integrated organ described above is limited by our incomplete understanding of the prototype, the biological hand and its interface with brain and eyes. That does not mean that these devices do not do a lot of good, and as acknowledged earlier are the preferred option for the great majority of upper limb losses. They substitute well in many ways for the executive lower-order roles of the hand. But they do only substitute. Hand transplants restitute. When successful they return the upper limbs to the full gamut of human function. I derive joy from seeing our long-term transplant patients casually and unconsciously gesturing, posturing, and using in very natural human ways their new hands, in a manner less evident in prosthesis wearers.10

The transplanted limbs are always attached, are silent as they act, and never need to be donned or doffed. They never run out of power: no charger is required, and they never need disguising with silicone “skin.” They are warm, and comforting, and sensate, and have proprioception, and when they are damaged there is no need to return them to base, instead they repair themselves in the remarkable process of healing. They truly do provide a restitution, not simply a substitution. In confirmation of that, our patients consistently and anticipatedly report a sensation after transplantation of being complete again. This completeness is not simply an aesthetic quality, appreciated by external observers, but a true appraisal of themselves from their own perspective, centered behind their eyes which now have before them the restored and completed circle of interaction between two hands. So, the capability of these peripheral agents, these reporters for the mind, is recreated and the organism is whole again, in a way it is hard to imagine could be achieved by prostheses.

We can muse on where this comparison between prostheses and transplants will be in the future. It is certain that prosthetic design and competence will continue to develop exponentially, especially with the advent of machine learning, peripheral processing, and haptic feedback. So too will their costs. Transplants are unlikely to develop in terms of the attributes of the part, which after all is the product of more than a million years of specific evolution. But the field still awaits the advent of immune tolerance and the obviation of the risks of immunosuppression, even allowing that these risks are no greater (and may be less) than for renal transplantation. It is absorbing to speculate, but impossible to know, whether humans will ever accept a prosthesis as a true replacement for a human hand, but I doubt that the apex collusion that has evolved between the two halves of the brain (each with its distinct role) and the eyes and upper limbs will ever be restored by mechanical devices.

Perhaps one day we will accept that the only restitution for the loss of a hand is to acquire a new hand, and this will free us to stop trying to create prostheses modelled on the quirk of pentadactyl limbs, and we can then think of substitute limbs with different forms and functions. Perhaps we might envy the octopus with its limbs that have such wonderful mechanical repertoires, whilst also being adhesive, and able to sense touch, to smell and to taste. But for now, and for many lifetimes to come, only a human hand is human.
