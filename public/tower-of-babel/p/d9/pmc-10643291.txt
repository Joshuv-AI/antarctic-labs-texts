Interactive effects of the built environment and rail transit on commuting behavior: Evidence from Hefei, China
Authors: Ran Yan, Bo Yang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA deep understanding of the underlying mechanisms of the built environment on commuting behavior along with rail transit is considered of great importance for both TOD land use and formulation of transport policies. The impact of the built environmental factors on commuting behavior has been already explored in the literature. However, the main focus has been laid on the separate effects of each factor and the interactions among these factors have been neglected. Along these lines, taking Hefei, China as the case, this work filled this gap by employing a social ecological model to systematically investigate the interactive effects of the built environment and urban rail transit on commuting behavior. From our analysis, it was demonstrated that land-use intensity was negatively correlated with car commuting, and mixed land use was positively related to metro commuting. Additionally, rail access near the workplace plays a key role in reducing car commuting than residential neighborhoods. This work revealed also some interesting findings on the association between rail transit and commuting behavior, which were significantly affected by land use intensity and mixed land-use. Our work provides valuable insights for the TOD land use to effectively reduce car commuting.Keywords: Commuting behavior, Built environment, Interactions, Social ecological model, Urban rail transit

Introduction

Urban issues, such as traffic congestion and air pollution, are becoming increasingly prominent against the growing backdrop of the yearly increase in the usage and ownership of motor vehicles.

For instance, by the end of 2020, China had already 243 million private cars (an increase of 8 % from 2019) and 79 cities had more than a million vehicles (an increase of 11.39 % from 2019) [1]. Instead, only 15 % of commuting was covered by rail transit within the buffer zone of 800 m in 2021 [2], while the length of the rail transit lines (2021) under operation is 2.4 times larger than it was in 2015. Urban rail transit is regarded as an efficient means of reducing traffic congestion and private vehicle usage [3,4]. There are many factors that affecting residents' choice of mode of transportation, mainly accessibility [5], environment and the individual's attributes [6]. Among them, the impact of the built environment on travel behavior has been extensively investigated in the literature [7,8]. There is a general consensus that the built environment in rail transit-supported areas with higher densities and higher mixed land use, as well as higher transit accessibility, could lead to a reduction in car use [9,10]. On top of that, it has been pointed out that the built environment could affect travel behavior not only directly but also indirectly by influencing other factors [11]. However, most of the existing works in the literature have focused on the direct effects of the built environment on travel behavior, while the interactive effects have been scarcely examined [12,13]. As a result of this interaction, the influence of one built environment factor on travel behavior may be affected by other built environment factors. In other words, one built environment may strengthen or weaken the relationship between other built environments and travel behavior. If this interaction is ignored, the role of the built environment in guiding travel behavior might not be effectively leveraged. This is also the main contribution of this work to the existing literature. More specifically, it was examined whether the relationship between the built environment and the travel behavior was strengthened or weakened by rail transit.

Based on the rapid development of rail transit and automobiles, as well as the role of rail transit in reshaping both the urban space and land use [14], it is necessary to thoroughly investigate the interactive effects of the built environment factors on travel behavior. Therefore, this work was guided by the following two questions: (1) After controlling the influence of the other built environment and eliminating the interference of the self-selection problem, does the rail transit affect individuals’ metro and vehicle commuting behavior? (2) Do the built environment and rail transit have interactive effects on commuting behavior? To this end, taking Hefei, China as a case study, this work enriched the scientific understanding of the relationship between urban rail transit and built environment and travel behavior, and enabled transport planners to have a clearer perception of the impact of built environment on travel behavior within the context of the urban rail transit.

Literature review and theoretical framework

Built environment and travel behavior

The built environment was measured by 5Ds, which were developed from 3Ds (i.e., density, diversity, design, distance to city centers, and distance to transit). Over the past three decades, numerous works in the literature on the relationship between the built environment and travel behavior have been conducted [10,11]. The majority of these works have focused on relatively low-density urban contexts in North American and European countries [15]. However, the inconsistency of the existing findings might be due to the difference between the high-density and low-density urban built environments [16].

It has been also demonstrated that high density and high land-use mix in communities’ built environments can reduce car trips [10,17]. For example, an empirical study in Beijing found that both the increasing population and employment densities in urban fringe communities can reduce the likelihood of commuting to downtown areas [18]. Some other works have reported that mixed land use encourages people to walk or take buses instead of driving [19,20]. For instance, in a recently published work in the literature, a significant positive correlation between mixed land use and the use of public transportation in the USA was proved, where in the area of higher mixed-land people tended to use public transportation more often [21]. On top of that, there is a non-linear inverted-U relationship between mixed land use and travel behavior [22,23].

However, some works in the literature have also found that mixed land use has a marginal effect on commuting behavior [24]. Similarly, no links have been found between active and passive commuting and mixed land use [21]. A relationship between population density, employment density, mixed land use, and auto travel at the neighborhood level has also been reported [8,[25], [26], [27]]. Using a meta-analysis of the literature before 2010, Ewing and Cervero demonstrated that the population density and employment density had a negligible impact on travel behavior after other built environment variables were controlled [11]. In addition, a recent study also found the presence of a car in the household has a strong impact on car travel behavior, while being negligibly affected by the built environment [28].

Rail transit and travel behavior

Just as the European and American scientific communities have diverse views on the merits and shortcomings of urban rail transit, the existing works in the literature present mixed results on the impact of urban rail transit on travel behavior. The positive impact of rail transit on travel behavior in the literature includes, first, the construction of rail transit that leads to an increase in metro commuting trips [29]. For example, Cao and Schoner revealed that light rail transit can significantly promote individuals' choice of transit trips after excluding the influence of self-selection [9]. Second, urban rail transit can reduce auto ownership [30]. Third, urban rail transit can reduce car travel [31]. For instance, the growth of car-based travels of residents within 0–3 km of Shenzhen's rail transit has been mitigated [32]. However, the opponents argue that urban rail transit has a much smaller impact than the built environment on both car ownership and use [33]. Moreover, by examining various cities in developing countries, it has been also found that diverse land use and easy access to recreational amenities in the vicinity of the metro are more important in improving the efficacy of the urban rail transit system and reducing automobile reliance [26]. On top of that, a recent meta-analysis pointed out that the impact of local access on transit use in the built environment decreased in post-2010 studies [20].

As far as residential rail transit and workplace rail transit are concerned, which have a greater impact on travel behavior, the existing works in the literature remain controversial. For example, it has been reported that living near light rail did not increase the likelihood of non-car commuting trips, while working near light rail can decrease the likelihood of car trips [34]. Nonetheless, in other works in the literature, it was reported that living near local rail transit led to a reduction of vehicles and boosted metro trips, whereas working near rail transit encouraged car trips and had no impact on metro trips [35].

Interactive effects on travel behavior

Although many works have explored the relationship between the built environment and commuting behavior, the majority of these works have almost entirely focused on the influence of a single variable [36]. Nevertheless, the interactive influence of the built environment factors on commuting behavior has been neglected [13]. As far as the few works in the literature are concerned where the interactive effects have been explored, the built environment factors interacted with the third variable of the non-built environment [37,38]. For example, in a work about the interaction effects of the price variable with neighborhood land use on reducing car dependence, it was found that the higher density of sidewalks and the closer to mixed-use activity centers, rendered the pricing policies more effective in reducing driving [37].

Additionally, the interactive effects of the built environment factors have also been addressed. For example, by examining the interactive effect between the built environment at the city and neighborhood level on commuting time, it was found that the greater density of the city's population indicated a more pronounced impact of the proximity to transit stations on reducing commuting time [39].

In addition, several works have examined the interactive effects of urban rail transit with other factors on travel behavior. The significant differences in VMT (vehicle miles traveled) originated from the different household income categories between inside rail station area and outside rail station area, whereas urban rail transit could reduce VMT more for higher income households [40,41]. Although, Shao et al. (2020) reported that the higher station centrality has a greater impact on the employment density on metro ridership, and a moderating impact on land use of metro ridership. However, further research on the interactive effects of urban rail and built environment factors on commuting behavior is undoubtedly required [42].

The theoretical framework

The social ecology theory was first proposed by psychologist Bronfenbrenner in 1977, and focused on the interactive effects of the human and the environment, and emphasized that the social ecological environment has a significant impact on human behavior. Based on this approach, Mcleroy et al. divided the factors influencing an individual's behavior into five levels including micro, meso, and macro [43]. However, the influence of the physical environment on health behavior was not specified. Stokols in 1996 proposed an interactive relationship between the dimensional factors [44], while Sallis et al., in 2006, based on the above-mentioned two scholars, clarified the influence of the physical environment and multiple factors at different levels, as well as the interaction between the various factors [45]. Therefore, the socio-ecological model breaks through the limitations of the theoretical model of single-level factors and reveals the relationship between the interaction of the multiple factors. The social-ecological model can be applied to environmental, social, economic, and other scientific fields based on the above model characteristics [46], as well as other behavioral areas, such as outdoor activities [47] and research on active travel [48]. For example, the interactive effects of the city-built environment and neighborhood-built environment on commuting duration have been examined [39].

Under this perspective, in this work, a theoretical framework was proposed based on social ecological model as our theoretical basis for exploring the interactive effects of urban rail transport and the built environment on travel behavior (Fig. 1). First, based on the theoretical framework, individual travel preferences and socioeconomic attributes that directly influence travel behavior were formulated; Second, according to the existing works in the literature, the urban rail transit and the built environment also directly affect commuting behavior (their direct impact is shown as the solid line in Figure); theoretically, the urban rail transit includes both residential rail and workplace rail transits. In addition, it was assumed that the urban rail transit not only directly influences residents' commuting behavior, but also indirectly through its interaction with the built environment (depicted as the dashed line in Fig. 1).

Data and methods

Data sources

By the end of 2021 (and since 2016), Hefei City had opened five urban rail transit lines. The “well” network skeleton of rail transit has been developed and is in the process of rapid network formation, with a total length of 156 km and 500 km planned for the 14th Five-Year Plan (i.e., by the end of 2025). As a representative of China's fast-growing cities, the scale and speed of rail transit development in Hefei are rapid. The case of Hefei is used as a reference for both land use and formulation of transport policies in cities that expect to reshape their urban spatial patterns and transform residents' travel modes based on rail transit.

In the current work, residents along and off rail transit lines were surveyed regarding their commuting characteristics and travel preferences. The commuting characteristics of the respondents include travel behavior before and after the epidemic of Covid-19. Demographic data, such as individuals’ home and workplace addresses, as well as their socioeconomic status, were also collected. Before the formal research, 35 copies of the questionnaire were distributed in the communities surrounding the schools as a pre-questionnaire. When the reliability of the questions was greater than 0.70 and the details of the questionnaire were revised, the formal questionnaire was administered. The questionnaire survey was conducted online and offline from March to May 2020. The offline survey was conducted by urban and rural planning college students as questionnaire volunteers, and the questionnaire was conducted by face-to-face in large communities, shopping malls, parks and other places with high pedestrian traffic in the city. The online survey was performed through the questionnaire platform of Wenjuanxing.

Before starting the questionnaire, informed consent from the participants and their parents or guardians when the participants are minors was obtained, informing them that the results of the survey would be used only for academic purposes and that the survey would not be analyzed for individuals and would not have any impact on them. The follow-up analyses would be also conducted only with their permission. The outliers samples were excluded, such as cases where the direct distance between an individual's residence and the subway station in the GIS software was greater than 1 km but the respondent's answer reflected that the walking time between their residence and the subway station was within 15 min. Finally, 982 valid questionnaires were obtained, and the pre-epidemic travel behavior of the residents was mainly analyzed in this work. Variables

Based on the literature, the key variables in this work included rail transit, built environment, and individual attribute variables [49]. Gender, age, dwelling type, annual household income, number of cars owned by the household, mode of transportation, and travel preference are examples of individual attribute characteristics.

The rail transit variables included living and working along with the subway. According to the specific location of a respondent's residence, the 1-km range from the rail transportation station was used to define whether living along the subway or not. In terms of working along with the subway or not, based on the “walking time from your workplace to the nearest opened subway station”, working along with the rail transit line was established based on whether the walking time from a respondent's workplace to the nearest opened subway station was within 15 min.

As far as the built environment variables are concerned, first, the population number, POI number, road intersection number, and bus stop number of each fishing network were counted in the GIS software with a scale of 200 m × 200 m, where the population density was based on the 2020 Chinese population data from WorldPop (https://www.worldpop.org/) and corrected using Hefei City's seventh census data.

Using GIS software, valuable pieces of information on the population density, land-use intensity, land-use mix, intersection density, and straight-line distance from the city center were then collected from the 800-m buffer zone (approximately a 15-min living circle) centered on the sample residence (Table 2), based on the data of 2020 Gaode POI, OSM road network, and Hefei public transportation network.

Since the car and the metro are competitive, in terms of travel distance, the travel behavior in this work focused on the choice of car and metro commuting modes, whereas the travel attitude preference was only considered for metro preference and car preference. The self-selection effect was primarily treated by controlling for travel preference [10], where the relevant items on the questionnaire included “I need a car to do many things I like to do”, “I like to travel by car”, and “It is inconvenient to go to work without a car”. The items for metro travel preference included “I like to travel by metro”, “I would rather take the metro than drive as long as the conditions allow”, and “It is sometimes easier for me to travel by subway than by driving”.

All items were measured on a five-point Likert scale, and respondents were asked to judge the items from strongly agree to strongly disagree. Finally, car preference and metro preference were extracted using factor analysis, and the reliability of the travel preference variables was greater than 0.8 after excluding the item “inconvenient to go to work without a car”. The reliability of the travel preference measures was found to be strong (Table 3).

Methods

As “whether to travel by vehicle” and “whether to travel by metro” are categorical data, this study used logistic regression models, as well as models with interaction terms. The model's basic expression as Eq. (1). In this equation, P refers to the likelihood that residents will choose to commute by vehicle or by subway, and 1-p represents the likelihood that they will not choose to commute by vehicle or by subway. The odds ratio, or Oodds=P1−P, is the proportion of a given transport mode's likelihood to its probability of not being chosen.

The interaction terms XBE×XRC were added to Eq. (1), after decentralizing the variables affecting rail transit and the built environment to investigate the impact of the built environment on the relationship between the rail transit and commuting mode, the interactive effects model as Eq. (2).

in this equation, XRC represents the rail transit variable, XBE denotes the built environment variable, XSE is the socio-economic attribute variable, and the interaction term XBE×XRC represents the moderating effect of the built environment on the relationship between the rail transit and commuting mode choice. Furthermore, βm is the coefficient of the moderating effect, and the maximum likelihood method was used to estimate the model's parameters.

Controlling the influence of individual socioeconomic attributes and travel preference, the built environment was found to have a significant impact on commuting by metro and by car in models 1 and 3, respectively. Moreover, urban rail transit was found to have a significant impact on two different travel modes in models 2 and 4, further controlling the built environment. The specific effects are as follows.

(1)The impact of individual socioeconomic characteristics

The impact of individual socioeconomic characteristics

Age was inversely related to metro trips and positively related to auto trips, indicating that as people's ages increase, the likelihood of taking metro trips is reduced, and the likelihood of taking car trips is increased. Men were 1.43 times more likely than women to select car travel but gender was not found to affect subway transit.

Education and income had a significant impact on car commuting, while there was no significant influence on metro commuting. Furthermore, as education and income levels increased, the probability of residents choosing a car also increased. Household ownership of a private car had a significant positive impact on car commuting (i.e., households with a car were 10.4 times more likely to choose a car for commuting than households without a car, while the presence or absence of a private car did not affect metro travel).

(2)The impact of travel preference

The impact of travel preference

Travel preferences had a considerable impact on the probability of the preferred travel mode, with 1.96 times the probability of preferring auto travel for car commuting than for no-car travel, and 1.98 times the probability of preferring subway travel for metro commuting than for non-subway travel to our respondents. This demonstrates the significant influence of travel preference on travel behavior; therefore, it was important to control for the influence of travel preference on travel behavior to derive unbiased estimates of the built environment and rail transportation on the travel mode.

(3)The impact of the built environment

The impact of the built environment

The impact of population density on both car and subway travel was not significant, which may be related to the high-density homogeneous development in the built-up area of Hefei, where the differences in population density in spatial distribution are not very large. This is in contrast to typical Western cities, where urban density decreases from the urban center to the suburbs [50,51]. No significant correlation between land-use intensity and metro trips was detected when the impact of the rail transit was considered. On the contrary, land-use intensity was significantly negatively correlated with auto trips. Each unit increase in land-use intensity was associated with a 20 % decrease in the likelihood of auto trips. This finding is also consistent with the results of Zegras [25].

Although mixed land use did not significantly affect car commuting, it was found to significantly promote subway commuting, with each unit increase in mixed land use being 30 times more likely to take the subway for our respondents. The mixed land use can promote metro travel but also affects car travel, which is consistent with previously reported outcomes in the literature [52,53]. More specifically, it was found that while road intersection density had a small influence on metro travel, it can dramatically increase car travel. The density of the public transportation stations was negatively correlated with metro travel and positively correlated with car travel, indicating that conventional public transportation and metro travel compete. Because bus stops are typically located near road intersections, places with dense bus stops also have higher road-intersection density, which in turn promotes car travel. This is consistent with the findings by Li [26].

(4)The impact of rail transit

The impact of rail transit

The influence of rail transit was found to be greater than the impact of the built environment for metro commuting, whereas the influence of the built environment was greater than the impact of rail transit for car commuting, based on the findings from models 2 and 4. Rail transit can greatly increase the likelihood of metro trips, while also decreasing the likelihood of car travel when the impact of individual socioeconomic characteristics, travel preference, and the built environment are controlled.

Living and working along a rail line, in particular, was found to significantly promote metro travel. More specifically, living along a rail line increased the likelihood of taking the metro by 2.4 times compared with not living near one, and working along a rail line versus not working near one increased individuals’ likelihood of taking the metro by 2.2 times for our respondents.

To avoid the influence of multi-collinearity on the regression results, the quantitative variables need to be centered, whereas data centering refers to each score subtracted from its average. Then, these interaction terms were separately added to the regression models. Models 5 and 6 were generated by adding the interaction term of the rail transit variable and built environment to model 2—the regression results only retained the component that passed the significance test. The regression coefficient of the interaction term between rail transportation and land-use intensity in model 5 in Table 6 was 0.51, with a positive direction, and passed the 10 % significance test. This result indicates that the built environment had a strong positive impact on the link between rail transit and metro commuting behavior. In particular, the higher land-use intensity indicates a more effective workplace along with the rail transport line to promote metro travel. The regression coefficient of the interaction term between the mixed land use and rail transit in model 6 was 1.925; however, the direction was negative and passed the 10 % significance test. This outcome indicates that greater mixed land use will weaken the promotion effect of workplace along rail transit on metro commuting.

Model 7 was based on model 4, in terms of the relationship between rail transit, built environment, and car commuting mode, with the interaction term of the rail transit variable and the built environment included. The coefficient of the interaction term in model 7 was 0.486, with a negative sign and significance at the 5 % level. This implies that the larger land-use intensity led to a greater influence of the workplace along with rail transit on reducing automobile travel (i.e., increasing the extent of workplace along with rail transit on reducing car commuting).

Discussion and limitation

Discussion

The main goal of this work was to thoroughly investigate the impact of living and working along the subway and built environment, and the interactive impact of them on commuting behavior, while controlling for commuters' travel preferences and socioeconomic attributes from a perspective of social-ecological theory. The contributions of this work are as follows.

First, the results showed that living and working along a rail line in particular, significantly promoted metro commuting, which is consistent with the reported results in the literature [8]. For car commuting, contrary to the findings [35], this work found that residing near a rail transit route did not significantly reduce the likelihood of car commuting, which is in direct line with the results of Li and Zhao [26]. However, working along with rail transit significantly reduced the probability of car commuting, which is consistent with Kwoka [34]. This means that rail access near the workplace is more central in reducing car commuting than in residential areas. Rail transit should prioritize addressing the amount of employment, while simultaneously covering the high-density residential population. Using rail transit to connect the high-density residential population and jobs can boost metro commuting, while also lowering car commuting, which is crucial to achieving a job-housing balance and reducing traffic congestion.

Second, the findings add to the existing literature exploring the interactive impact of the built environment and rail transit on commuting behavior. From our analysis, it was demonstrated that the association between rail transit and commuting behavior was significantly impacted by land-use intensity. Particularly, the higher land-use intensity led to a stronger contribution of rail transit to metro travel and a greater weakening effect on car commuting. This was because the increased land-use intensity can cause more pedestrian and vehicular traffic, which worsens congestion in the community and on adjacent roads [54,55]. This aligns with the findings of Shao [42], where land-use intensity including population density and employment density, had a positive moderating impact on metro ridership. However, many cities in China, like Hefei, have almost across-the-board existing control indicators for land use, with little regard for differences in the presence or absence of rail transit. For this reason, policymakers should consider the interactive effects of rail transit and land use on metro travel.

Third, this study found the moderating effect of mixed land use and rail transit on metro travel. The greater mixed land use will weaken the promotion effect of working along rail transit on metro commuting. Since the TOD theory emphasizes the effect of mixed land use, the results of this paper differ from our expectations. One possible reason is the non-linear relationship between mixed land use and transit travel. For example, Ding et al. found an inverted U-shaped relationship between mixed land use and metro travel [22]. Despite the fact that the TOD theory emphasizes a high degree of mixed land use, a high concentration of mixed land use around rail transit may weaken the promotion of metro travel if pursued excessively. To effectively leverage the role of rail transit and the built environment in promoting metro travel and reducing automobile travel, urban and transport planners should consider the weakening effect of the mixed land use on the relationship between rail transit and metro commuting, as well as the promoting effect of the mixed land use itself on metro travel. The mixed land use of neighborhoods near metro stations should be within a particular range to successfully promote metro travel. The amount of mixed land use should be specifically analyzed based on employment and residence, as well as the urban built environment and rail transit conditions.

Finally, this study applied the social ecological theory to deeply investigate the relationship between the built environment and travel behavior, and the results also confirmed that the interaction between the built environment and rail transit had significant effects on travel behavior. The socio ecological theory can explain the complex mechanism between the built environment and an individual's travel behavior. This issue of the interactive effects of the influencing factors on individual travel behavior, such as the built environment factors, is worth exploring in future research.

Limitations

It is important to note that the presented study has certain limitations that warrant further research. Given that the data in this empirical study were cross-sectional, the findings do not establish a causal relationship between interactive effects on travel behavior. Similarly, the findings of this work pertain specifically to the case study of Hefei. Future work should encompass other cities and compare any differences between the them.

Conclusions

This work explored the impact of urban rail transit, the built environment, and their interactive effect on metro commuting and car commuting based on the social ecological model. The main findings can be summarized as follows.

(1)Living and working along a rail line can enhance metro commuting, while working near a rail line has a greater impact than living along a rail line, in terms of reducing car commuting.

(2)A negative correlation was found between land-use intensity and car commuting, and a positive correlation was found between mixed land use and metro commuting.
