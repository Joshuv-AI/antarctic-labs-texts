Occupational and Nonoccupational Physical Activity and Their Association With All‐Cause, Cardiovascular, and Cancer Mortality in US Adults: A Prospective Cohort Study from the NHANES 2007 to 2018
Authors: Jinli Mahe, Kai Guo, Liyang Guo, Li Liu, Zhengyu Yang, Ao Xu, Pengyuan Xu, Yujia Huo, Jian Wang, Garon Perceval, Yang Zhao, Junjie Huang, Xiangyi Kong, Guannan Bai, Aimin Xu, Chaopin Du, Feng Shi, Hanbin Cui, Shengfeng Wang, Zhihui Li, Liang Wang, Lei Zhang, Lin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt remains unclear whether achieving the physical activity guidelines through occupational activity alone, nonoccupational activity alone, or combined occupational and nonoccupational activity has different health consequences.MethodsAdults aged 18 years or older in the NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) from 2007 to 2018 were prospectively observed. Mortality data were acquired from the National Death Index through December 31, 2019. Time of self‐reported occupational and nonoccupational activity was calculated. All‐cause mortality was the primary outcome, with cardiovascular and cancer mortality as secondary outcomes. Cox proportional hazards models were used to analyze the association of occupational and nonoccupational activity with mortality.ResultsDuring a median follow‐up of 6.92 years, 23 752 participants were included in our analysis, with 1367 deaths recorded, including 367 cardiovascular and 328 cancer deaths. Compared with inactive participants, those achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone (hazard ratio [HR], 0.75 [95% CI, 0.60–0.94]), nonoccupational activity alone (HR, 0.58, [95% CI, 0.47–0.71]), or combined activity (HR, 0.43 [95% CI, 0.33–0.56]) had lower all‐cause mortality. Additionally, those achieving recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity alone (HR, 0.51 [95% CI, 0.36–0.71]) or combined activity (HR, 0.32 [95% CI, 0.20–0.57]) had lower cardiovascular mortality. However, occupational activity alone (HR, 0.89 [95% CI, 0.61–1.29]), nonoccupational activity alone (HR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.66–1.46]), and combined activity (HR, 0.65 [95% CI, 0.38–1.11]) were not associated with cancer mortality.ConclusionAchieving recommended activity levels through any domain was associated with lower all‐cause mortality. Nonoccupational activity alone or combined activity was associated with lower cardiovascular mortality. Participants in occupational activity alone may benefit more if they also achieve recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity.

Physical activity is a well‐established determinant of health, with numerous studies demonstrating its significant effects on reducing risk of mortality and chronic diseases, such as cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, depression, and cancer. 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 The World Health Organization guidelines on physical activity recommend that adults engage in at least 150 minutes of moderate‐to‐vigorous‐intensity physical activity per week. 6 These guidelines promote physical activity across domains, including work, home, leisure‐time, and transportation activity. However, in the United States, physical activity recommendations are achieved by only 24% of adults, 7 leading to a substantial economic burden of $24.7 billion, which accounts for 45.9% of global health care costs. 8 Thus, it is crucial to increase physical activity to prevent and reduce the risk of morbidity and mortality, ultimately lowering global health care costs and productivity losses.

Although the benefits of physical activity are well documented, the relationship between different domains of physical activity—particularly occupational and nonoccupational physical activity—and mortality remains unclear. For example, a study from China found that higher occupational physical activity was associated with lower all‐cause mortality, 9 whereas a study from Denmark indicated that greater levels of occupational physical activity correlated with increased all‐cause mortality risk. 10 Similarly, research on the association between nonoccupational physical activity and mortality has also been inconsistent. Although a study in the United States found that higher nonoccupational physical activity was associated with lower cardiovascular mortality, 11 a study in Norway reported no such association. 12 Findings regarding the associations between nonoccupational physical activity and cancer mortality also remain mixed. 13 , 14 A possible explanation for these discrepancies may lie in the physical activity paradox, which suggests that although nonoccupational physical activity is generally associated with health benefits, high levels of occupational physical activity may have neutral or even adverse effects on mortality. 15 Differences in intensity, duration, and physiological impact between occupational and nonoccupational physical activity may contribute to these varying associations. 15 Therefore, it is important to consider the context of occupational physical activity when exploring the relationship between nonoccupational physical activity and health outcomes, as well as the context of nonoccupational physical activity when examining the relationship between occupational physical activity and health outcomes. Although a study from Norway revealed that only joint very high occupational and vigorous nonoccupational physical activity reduced both all‐cause and cancer mortality, 16 it remains unclear whether achieving recommended physical activity levels through occupational and nonoccupational physical activity reduces mortality. Furthermore, based on the intensity, frequency, and duration of physical activity, it is still uncertain if the impact on mortality differs when achieving recommended physical activity levels through occupational and nonoccupational physical activity.

Therefore, our study aimed to examine the associations of occupational and nonoccupational physical activity with all‐cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality. Furthermore, our study also investigated whether these associations varied based on the intensity, frequency, and duration of physical activity.

METHODS

Availability of Data and Materials

The data sets generated and analyzed during the current study are available in the: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/Default.aspx. Access to the data is permitted openly.

Study Population

The details of the research design and data description for the NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) were obtained from the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention’s National Center for Health Statistics. 17 Among 59 842 participants, 30 169 adults (≥18 years old) without self‐reported cardiovascular disease or cancer at baseline met the inclusion criteria for this prospective cohort study, which covered 6 cycles from 2007 to 2008 to 2017 to 2018. Among them, pregnant women (n=274), adults without follow‐up (n=119), physical activity (n=117), and potential covariate (n=5907) data were excluded. Eventually, 23 752 participants were included in our analysis (Figure S1). Missing data on follow‐up, physical activity, and potential covariates were attributed to participants’ uncertainty, difficulty in understanding questions, or refusal to provide answers. The distribution of missing data is shown in Table S1. The NHANES was approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Physical Activity Measurements

The intensity, frequency, and duration of sessions in occupational (Table S2) and nonoccupational (Table S3) activity were measured from 15 questions using Global Physical Activity Questionnaire. 18 Based on the NHANES, occupational activity includes paid or unpaid work, studying or training, household chores, and yard work, which differs from the broad definition of occupational activity that excludes household chores. 9 , 16 However, some previous NHANES studies categorized household chores as part of occupational activity, 19 , 20 as this distinction is not possible within the NHANES framework. Transportation activity refers to bicycling and walking for commuting purposes, 20 including travel to work, shopping, and school. Leisure‐time activity excludes the aforementioned work and transportation activities and involves sports, fitness, and recreational activity. 20 Nonoccupational activity includes transportation and leisure‐time activity. 21 According to the NHANES Codebook, metabolic equivalent for vigorous‐intensity activity is double that of moderate‐intensity activity, which has been widely employed in previous research. 20 Among them, transportation activity is classified as only moderate‐intensity activity. Total minutes of occupational and nonoccupational moderate‐to‐vigorous‐intensity activity were calculated from multiplying the frequency by duration. Therefore, time of moderate‐to‐vigorous‐intensity activity in a week (min/wk) was calculated using the formula: 2×frequency of vigorous−intensity activity×duration +frequency of moderate−intensity activity×duration. Participants were then categorized into 4 groups: inactive–not achieving recommended activity levels (<150 min/wk of moderate‐to‐vigorous‐intensity activity in both occupational and nonoccupational activities, considered separately); occupational activity alone–achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone (≥150 min/wk of moderate‐to‐vigorous‐intensity activity in occupational activity but <150 min/wk in nonoccupational activity); nonoccupational activity alone–achieving recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity alone (<150 min/wk of moderate‐to‐vigorous‐intensity activity in occupational activity but ≥150 min/wk in nonoccupational activity); and combined activity–achieving recommended activity levels through both occupational and nonoccupational activities (≥150 min/wk of moderate‐to‐vigorous‐intensity activity in each domain separately). Activity intensity was calculated with moderate‐intensity activity <150 and ≥150 min/wk, and vigorous‐intensity activity <75 and ≥75 min/wk. Activity subgroups were further categorized based on each session duration ≤60 minutes, >60 to ≤150 minutes, and >150 minutes.

Mortality Data

Cause of death data were ascertained from the US National Center for Health Statistics through the National Death Index to December 31, 2019. 22 All‐cause mortality was the primary outcome, with cardiovascular and cancer mortality as secondary outcomes. The International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes used to classify cause of death were I00–I09, I11, I13, I20–I51, and I60–I69 for cardiovascular disease, and C00–C97 for cancer.

Covariates Measurements

Characteristics of participants at baseline (age, sex, race or ethnicity, education, marital status, body mass index, smoking, and drinking) were included as covariates. 14 Age (18–59 or ≥60 years old), sex (men or women), race or ethnicity (Hispanic, non‐Hispanic White, non‐Hispanic Black, or other), education (≤high school or >high school), marital status (married or unmarried), smoking (no or yes), and drinking (no or yes) were obtained from questionnaire. Weight (kg) and height (m) of participants were measured, and body mass index was calculated by dividing the weight by the square of the height. Body mass index was then categorized into normal (≤24.9 kg/m2) and overweight/obese (≥25.0 kg/m2).

Statistical Analysis

In accordance with NHANES analytic tutorials, all analyses in our study applied sample weights, clustering, and stratification to ensure the US national representation and accurate variance estimation for the US population. Chi‐square test was performed to compare categorical variables in the 4 groups: inactive, occupational activity alone, nonoccupational activity alone, and combined activity. Cox proportional hazards models with calendar time (months) as the time variable were used to estimate the associations of occupational and nonoccupational activity with all‐cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality risk. Three models were used: Model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and race or ethnicity; Model 2 included additional adjustments for education and marital status; and Model 3 further accounted for body mass index, smoking, and drinking. To evaluate mortality over time, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated. Pairwise comparisons of hazard ratios were conducted using a Z‐test to evaluate the differences of hazard ratios between the 3 groups (occupational activity alone, nonoccupational activity alone, and combined activity). To examine dose–response relationships between occupational and nonoccupational activity with all‐cause, cardiovascular, and cancer mortality, restricted cubic spline functions were conducted.

Subgroup analyses were performed to ensure whether there were potential interaction effects between covariates and activity on mortality. Five sensitivity analyses were conducted to examine the robustness of our results. The first sensitivity analysis excluded individuals who died within the first 24‐month follow‐up to deal with the possible effects of preexisting diseases. The subsequent 4 sensitivity analyses excluded participants with high activity levels to account for exposure variability and enhance comparability among groups. The second and third sensitivity analyses excluded participants with ≥2500 and ≥2700 min/wk of occupational activity. The fourth and fifth sensitivity analyses excluded participants with ≥600 and ≥800 min/wk of nonoccupational activity. Statistical analyses were conducted in September 2024 using R software (version 4.4.0), with all tests being 2 tailed and statistical significance defined as P<0.05.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics of Participants

A total of 23 752 adults (median age, 28 [interquartile range, 32–60] years; 12 103 [51.0%] women; and 11 685 [49.2%] non‐Hispanic participants) were included in our analysis, with 4130 (17.4%), 6305 (26.5%), and 4282 (18.0%) participants achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone, nonoccupational activity alone, and combined activity, respectively. Percentages of these 3 groups decreased with age in both sexes (Figure S2). Compared with inactive participants, those achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone tended to be younger, men, Hispanic, married, thinner, smokers, and drinkers but had lower educational levels; those achieving recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity alone tended to be younger, men, Hispanic, unmarried, thinner, nonsmokers, and drinkers and had higher educational levels; and those achieving recommended activity levels through combined activity tended to be younger, men, Hispanic, unmarried, thinner, smokers, and drinkers and had higher educational levels (Table 1).

Occupational Activity Alone, Nonoccupational Activity Alone, and Combined Activity Versus Inactive Participants

During a median follow‐up of 6.92 years, 1367 deaths were recorded, including 367 cardiovascular and 328 cancer deaths. Compared with inactive participants, occupational activity alone, nonoccupational activity alone, and combined activity participants had lower all‐cause mortality by 25% (hazard ratio [HR], 0.75 [95% CI, 0.60–0.94]), 42% (HR, 0.58 [95% CI, 0.47–0.71]), and 57% (HR, 0.43 [95% CI, 0.33–0.56]). Compared with inactive participants, nonoccupational activity alone and combined activity participants had lower cardiovascular mortality rates by 49% (HR, 0.51 [95% CI, 0.36–0.71]) and 68% (HR, 0.32 [95% CI, 0.20–0.57]), but occupational activity alone participants did not (HR, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.53–1.19]). However, occupational activity alone (HR, 0.89 [95% CI, 0.61–1.29]), nonoccupational activity alone (HR, 0.98 [95% CI, 0.66–1.46]), and combined activity (HR, 0.65 [95% CI, 0.38–1.11]) participants did not have lower cancer mortality than inactive participants (Table 2). The Kaplan–Meier curves indicated similar results (Figure S3). Pairwise comparisons showed that there were differences in HRs for all‐cause (P=0.002) and cardiovascular (P=0.007) mortality between occupational activity alone and combined activity participants (Table S4).

Nonoccupational Activity in Combined Activity Versus Inactive Participants, by Intensity, Frequency, and Duration of Activity

Compared with inactive participants, those achieving recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity among those achieving recommended activity levels through combined activity had lower all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality across nearly all levels of intensity, frequency, and durations of sessions of activity. Subgroup Analyses

Subgroup analyses of occupational and nonoccupational physical activity with mortality were presented (Tables S5 through S7). It was observed that almost all the associations between physical activity and mortality risk were not influenced by age, sex, education, marital status, body mass index, smoking, or drinking. Only the association between physical activity and cardiovascular mortality varied by age (P value for interaction=0.016), with slightly better reduction effects in older adults. In other words, almost all the interactions between these variables and physical activity were not statistically significant (almost all P values for interaction >0.05).

Sensitivity Analyses

Five sensitivity analyses were performed to ensure the stability of our results. All results were similar to the complete case analysis. The first sensitivity analysis found that occupational activity alone, nonoccupational activity alone, and combined activity reduced all‐cause mortality after removing participants who died within the first 24 months of follow‐up. Meanwhile, nonoccupational activity alone and combined activity decreased cardiovascular mortality. The second and third sensitivity analyses excluding participants with ≥2500 and ≥2700 min/wk of occupational activity showed that occupational activity alone, nonoccupational activity alone, and combined activity reduced all‐cause mortality, when nonoccupational activity alone and combined activity decreased cardiovascular mortality. The fourth and fifth sensitivity analyses excluding participants with ≥600 and ≥800 min/wk of nonoccupational activity indicated similar results (Table S8).

DISCUSSION

This large prospective cohort study found that participants who achieved the World Health Organization guidelines on physical activity through occupational activity alone, nonoccupational activity alone, and combined activity had lower all‐cause mortality risk by 25%, 42%, and 57%, respectively. Moreover, participants who achieved recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity alone and combined activity had lower cardiovascular mortality by 49% and 68%. Findings from this study demonstrated that participants who achieved the physical activity recommendations experienced lower all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality risk. It is worth noting that among 23 752 US adults, only 14 717 (62.0%) participants achieved recommended activity levels through occupational, nonoccupational, or combined activity, and only 4282 (18.0%) participants achieved recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity after achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity. This emphasized that public health organizations and policymakers should encourage inactive adults to achieve the physical activity guidelines at least through occupational or nonoccupational activity and also encourage those achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone to achieve recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity as well. As far as we know, previous research mainly focused on effects of occupational or nonoccupational activity on mortality. 23 , 24 Consistent with our findings, a meta‐analysis demonstrated that the highest occupational and nonoccupational activity decreased the risk of all‐cause mortality by 17% and 26% compared with the lowest activity. 23 In addition, a prospective study involving Chinese adults indicated that nonoccupational activity reduced all‐cause mortality by 10% compared with no nonoccupational activity, whereas occupational activity did not reduce all‐cause mortality compared with no occupational activity. 25 However, our study found that occupational activity alone, nonoccupational activity alone, and combined activity lowered all‐cause mortality by 25%, 42%, and 57%. This discrepancy may result from difference in study population, reference groups, definition of occupational and nonoccupational activity, and classification of activity groups. Moreover, in our study, for all‐cause mortality, the HR differed between occupational activity alone and combined activity. This suggested that participants achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone may benefit more by also achieving recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity. Therefore, our study implied that achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone, nonoccupational activity alone, and combined activity, may continuously decrease all‐cause mortality, which could be quantified and achieved in daily life, with greater benefits from achieving recommended activity levels through combined activity.

Our study found that achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone did not decrease cardiovascular and cancer mortality. Consistent with our results, a meta‐analysis from adult workers demonstrated that high occupational activity did not lower cardiovascular mortality. 26 This suggested that achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone may not reduce cardiovascular and cancer mortality, aligning with another study of US adults, which showed that high occupational activity did not decrease cardiovascular and cancer mortality risk. 27 Three reasons may explain our findings. First, occupational activity alone participants achieved recommended activity levels through their daily occupational activity, meaning they engaged in little or no nonoccupational activity, 28 which was known to reduce cardiovascular and cancer mortality. 11 Second, occupational activity alone participants frequently involved prolonged static tasks, repetitive and uncomfortable postures, and other nonexercise activity for several hours a day without enough recovery time, which did not improve their cardiorespiratory fitness and health. 15 Third, vigorous physical activity jobs always involve shift work, which can lead to altered sleep patterns, including irregular sleep duration and disrupted circadian rhythms. 29 These have been associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes. 30 Therefore, policymakers and public health institutions should be aware of this “physical activity paradox” and consider different nonoccupational activity contexts when providing occupational recommendations. Moreover, in our study, for cardiovascular mortality, the HR differed between occupational activity alone and combined activity. This indicated that participants who achieved recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone may experience lower cardiovascular mortality if they also achieved recommended activity levels through nonoccupational physical activity.

Our study also found that nonoccupational activity alone and combined activity lowered cardiovascular mortality by 49% and 68%. Consistent with our study, a meta‐analysis and 2 cohort studies demonstrated that recommended nonoccupational activity reduced cardiovascular mortality. 21 , 31 , 32 Although achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone did not decrease cardiovascular mortality, achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity among participants achieving recommended activity levels through combined activity decreased cardiovascular mortality. The dose–response analyses showed nonlinear relationships between occupational activity and cardiovascular mortality, supporting this finding. This might be because participants achieving recommended activity levels through combined activity had better baseline characteristics (younger, more educated, leaner, and less smokers) than those achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone. This suggests that if achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone is not associated with lower cardiovascular mortality, achieving them through nonoccupational activity is likely to reduce cardiovascular mortality risk. Additionally, our study found that achieving recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity alone and combined activity was not associated with cancer mortality, which was consistent with previous research. 13 This may be because patients with cancer often have weakened health, making it difficult for physical activity to reverse or significantly affect their condition. However, another study indicated that higher nonoccupational activity was associated with lower cancer mortality. 14 This conflict may result from different study population, covariates adjustment, and classification standards for physical activity. Further research should be conducted to address this conflict.

Strengths and Limitations

Our study had several strengths. First, our study used a large and representative sample from nationwide US adults. Second, our study included almost all activity types in daily life, which encouraged the public to meet the World Health Organization guidelines through daily activity. Third, 5 sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure the robustness of our results. However, our study also had several limitations. First, compared with objectively measured activity using equipment, both occupational and nonoccupational activity were self‐reported, which introduced recall bias. Second, the measurement of occupational activity in our study included household chores, which may lead to potential misclassification. Although household chores involve physical exertion, they do not fully align with the conventional definition of occupational activity, potentially affecting the interpretation of our findings. Third, our study did not analyze the association between specific activity types and mortality, such as walking, running and lifting, which may not completely reveal detailed relationship between specific activity types and mortality. Fourth, only baseline activity was measured without repeated measurements, which may not reveal the association between physical activity and mortality over time. Fifth, our study examined whether nonoccupational and occupational activity met the physical activity guidelines, without exploring cases where participants engaged in some activity (>0 to <150 min/wk), which should be further investigated. Finally, the combined activity might have a higher total activity volume than the single‐domain groups, which might partly account for the observed health benefit. Nonetheless, among participants who engaged in ≥300 min/wk of total activity, the proportion engaging in combined activity (34.3%) was slightly lower than those engaging in nonoccupational activity alone (35.7%). Future studies could consider total activity to better isolate the effects of activity domain combinations.

CONCLUSIONS

Participants who achieved the World Health Organization guidelines on physical activity for occupational activity alone, nonoccupational activity alone, and combined activity had lower all‐cause mortality risks. Those achieving recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity alone and combined activity had lower cardiovascular mortality. Moreover, those achieving recommended activity levels through occupational activity alone may benefit more if they also achieved recommended activity levels through nonoccupational activity. However, both occupational and nonoccupational activity were not associated with cancer mortality rates. Our study highlighted that achieving the World Health Organization guidelines on physical activity benefited health and reduced all‐cause and cardiovascular mortality.
