Linking environmental adaptation and genetic structure to the biogeography of flower colour lineages in Lysimachia monelli (Primulaceae)
Authors: M. Sánchez‐Cabrera, F. J. Jiménez‐López, P. L. Ortiz, M. Talavera, E. Narbona, M. Arista
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract Reproductive traits such as flower colour can have a profound influence on the diversification of plant species, making understanding their evolutionary ramifications one of the foremost challenges for evolutionary biologists. Lysimachia monelli is a Mediterranean endemic with two markedly divergent flower colour lineages, blue and orange, which do not coexist within the same populations.We examined the relationship between lineage divergence and environmental niche differentiation for the two lineages. Genetic relationships among individuals from 21 blue and six orange populations were assessed using seven nSSR markers. Environmental niche models were projected for the present and three past periods.Low gene flow between populations and elevated heterozygosity were pervasive across L. monelli populations. Western populations of both orange and blue lineages had higher genetic diversity than their eastern counterparts, with blue lineages displaying higher diversity than orange lineages. There was conspicuous genetic differentiation between colour lineages, and populations were aggregated into five nSSR clusters. Colour lineages occupied distinct environmental niches, with lineage‐specific habitat suitability primarily influenced by variations in annual precipitation and solar radiation. Bioclimatic fluctuations in the region appear to have driven the divergence of L. monelli populations along a west‐to‐east gradient.The findings suggest comprehensive geographic and genetic separation of the two L. monelli colour lineages, suggesting an advanced stage of speciation.

INTRODUCTION

The Mediterranean region is among the most extensive and thoroughly investigated biodiversity hotspots in the world (Myers et al. 2000; Nieto‐Feliner 2014), encompassing a diverse array of heterogeneous environments, related to its complex topography and paleoclimatic configuration (Thompson 2005; Rundel et al. 2016). The formation of Betic and Rifean corridors during the Oligocene and Miocene (33.9–5.3 kya) facilitated intercontinental biotic exchanges and expansions (Lonergan & White 1997; Gueguen et al. 1998). Subsequent contraction dynamics and dispersal events from Quaternary refugia during the Last Glacial Maximum (LGM) were pivotal in shaping current phylogeographic patterns of numerous lineages (Avise et al. 2016; Riddle 2016). Additionally, these historical biogeographic events often resulted in complex patterns of genetic structuring, (e.g., hybridization and introgression; Thompson 2005; Nieto‐Feliner 2014). Such intricacies add layers of complexity to the evolutionary narratives of Mediterranean species. Thus, these geologic and climatic transformations, in conjunction with well‐documented phylogeographic trends in a multitude of Mediterranean taxa, shed light on the diversification and spatial distribution of many species (e.g., Jakob et al. 2007; Kadereit & Yaprak 2008; Casimiro‐Soriguer et al. 2010; García‐Castaño et al. 2014; Nieto‐Feliner 2014; Martín‐Hernanz et al. 2019).

The added complexity of these evolutionary factors in Mediterranean species not only highlights the historical and ecological diversity of the region but also underscores the importance of phenotypic variations in the evolutionary dynamics of these species. Genetic variation, biogeographic events, reproductive traits, and local environmental changes significantly influence the distribution of floral lineages, whose divergence may result from divergent selection and pleiotropic reproductive isolation (Rausher 2008; Ellegren & Galtier 2016). Variability in flower colour, mediated by ecological and biological factors, can influence population dynamics, gene flow, and geographic distribution of these species, as documented in studies on pollinator interaction and ecological adaptation to different environments (Arista et al. 2013; Ortiz et al. 2015; Jiménez‐López et al. 2020). Therefore, their current distribution must be the result of a set of processes operating at different temporal scales that are essential for understanding phylogeographic and evolutionary trends in the Mediterranean Basin (Hamrick et al. 1992; Vellend et al. 2014). Hence, combining Ecological Niche Models (ENMs) and molecular tools allows us to link these genetic divergence patterns with species distribution projections (Alvarado‐Serrano & Knowles 2014), providing a comprehensive understanding of spatial genetic structure and niche evolution in the Mediterranean biogeographical context, which still requires more research (Benítez‐Benítez et al. 2018).

This comprehensive understanding of genetic structure and niche adaptation is particularly relevant for endemic species that exhibit flower colour polymorphism, such as Lysimachia monelli (L.) Manns and Anderb. Lysimachia monelli is a perennial, self‐incompatible (Gibbs & Talavera 2001) and diploid (2 n = 20; García et al. 1997) endemic of the Mediterranean Basin, with a blue–orange flower colour polymorphism (Ferguson 1972; Pujadas 1997). The phenotypic variability and reproductive dynamics of this species highlight the need to explore how these factors interact with the biogeography of the Mediterranean Basin. Most populations are distributed across the West‐Central Mediterranean. Currently, these lineages inhabit different regions: the blue lineage is restricted to the Iberian Peninsula, eastern Algeria, Tunisia, and western Libya, whereas the orange lineage is found in Morocco, west of Algeria, Sardinia, and the northeastern Iberian Peninsula (Sánchez‐Cabrera et al. 2024). So far, no sympatric populations have been identified, and reproductive isolation via intrinsic post‐zygotic barriers has been reported (Sánchez‐Cabrera 2023). A recent phylogenetic study established the common ancestral divergence of flower lineages at 2.1 Mya (Jiménez‐López et al. 2022), coinciding with the advent of Pleistocene climate oscillations (Combourieu‐Nebout et al. 2015). The Pleistocene is well known for its profound impact on floristic composition and genetic diversity of European–Mediterranean flora (Comes & Kadereit 1998). In Lysimachia arvensis, a closely related species, flower colour lineage distribution is associated with abiotic conditions (Arista et al. 2013), but this is not resolved for L. monelli (Sánchez‐Cabrera et al. 2024).

We employed environmental predictive models in conjunction with a molecular phylogeography based on nuclear microsatellite (nSSR) loci to examine lineage differentiation, genetic structure, potential glacial refugia, and ecological niche shifts in L. monelli. Specifically, our aims were to: (1) define the genetic structure and diversity of the two L. monelli flower colour lineages; (2) establish current and past distribution of these lineages through environmental niche modelling (ENM); and (3) elucidate the biogeographic pattern of the two lineages to hypothesize their origin and interpret the current demographic dynamics.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Plant material and nSSR genotyping

Fresh leaves were collected from 21 blue and six orange populations of L. monelli (324 individuals; 11–13 individuals per population) across its natural range (Table S1). Leaf samples were dried and preserved in silica‐gel until molecular analysis. The spatial genetic structure of L. monelli was assessed using nuclear microsatellites (nSSR), a highly polymorphic co‐dominant marker. Seven primers (Lys12, Lys24, Lys28, Lys29, Lys30, Lys31) specifically developed for L. arvensis (Jiménez‐López et al. 2015), were applied to 324 samples of L. monelli (252 blue and 72 orange). Fluorescence emitted by each sample was analysed following Jiménez‐López et al. (2020). To exclude unreproducible bands and calculate the error rate (Bonin et al. 2004), 10% of the samples were replicated.

The fluorescence‐labelled amplification products were separated using capillary gel electrophoresis on an automated sequencer (3730 DNA Analyser; PE Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA) with an internal size standard (GeneScan 500 LIZ; Applied Biosystems) at STABVIDA (Oeiras, Portugal). Raw data were processed using GeneMarker 1.9 (SoftGenetics, State College, PA) for manual fragment scoring after profile normalization. Peaks were validated when fluorescence intensity exceeded the fluorescence threshold (set at 100 relative fluorescence units). Amplified fragments from 100 to 500 base pairs (bp) were scored and exported to a matrix with fragment sizes for each locus. To evaluate the adequacy of the seven loci used, a simulated analysis of multilocus genotype accumulation curves was conducted with the R package “poppr” (Kamvar et al. 2014), applying 1000 random permutations to generate the distribution, ensuring sufficient loci sampling.

Genetic variability

Genetic diversity was assessed using standard parameters, including observed and expected heterozygosity (Ho and He) and inbreeding coefficient (F is), using GENODIVE v. 3.06 (Meirmans & Van Tienderen 2004). The frequency of a null allele (No) was calculated with GENEPOP v. 4.0.10 (Raymond & Rousset 1995; Rousset 2008). Isolation‐by‐distance between populations of each lineage was evaluated by correlating the pairwise population genetic distance matrix with the geographic distance matrix, followed by a Mantel test with 10,000 permutations using GENODIVE.

We used BOTTLENECK v. 1.2.02 (Piry et al. 1999) to detect recent bottlenecks in the studied populations. Wilcoxon tests were performed under three mutation models of microsatellite variation: the infinite alleles model (IAM), stepwise mutation model (SMM), and two‐phase mutational model (TPM).

Population structure

To elucidate the number of independent genetic and evolutionary entities, overall population structure was inferred using Bayesian clustering implemented in STRUCTURE v. 2.3.4 (Pritchard et al. 2000, 2010), which allows identification of the optimal number of clusters (K) within the nSSR dataset. Individual clustering was performed without incorporating geographic origin of samples as prior information. Analyses were based on an admixture ancestry model with correlated allele frequencies across a range of K genetic clusters from 1 to 27. A burn‐in period of 1,00,000 and a run length of the Monte Carlo Markov Chain (MCMC) of 1,000,000 iterations were used. The most plausible number of genetic clusters (K) was identified based on ∆K and Mean LnP(K) (Evanno et al. 2005; Pritchard et al. 2010), using STRUCTURE Harvester (Earl & vonHoldt 2012). In addition, K‐mean clustering implemented in GENODIVE was also used, relying on the Euclidean distances derived from allele frequencies within populations, with simulated annealing of 50,000 steps and 20 random starts for K genetic clusters ranging from 2 to 25. Optimal clustering was determined using the Calinski and Harabasz pseudo‐F and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC).

We examined patterns and levels of genetic differentiation for three groups using analyses of molecular variance (AMOVA) in ARLEQUIN v. 3.01 (Excoffier et al. 2005): (1) population level, (2) flower colour level, and (3) first optimum from Bayesian clustering analysis (K = 5; see Results). The AMOVA‐derived fixation index (F ST) was also calculated with ARLEQUIN as an indirect measure of gene flow (Wright 1951), describing the heterozygosity reduction within populations relative to the total population. The OSx‐statistic (Goudet 1995) was used to evaluate the squared differences across all group pairwise combinations, with statistical significance tested with 9,999 randomizations using GENODIVE. Principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) among individuals from different populations and lineages was conducted in IBM SPSS Statistic 24 (IBM, Armonk, NY). Two neutrality tests, Tajima's D‐test (T's D) and Fu's Fs‐test (Fu'S), were also performed using ARLEQUIN.

Environmental niche modelling and projections to past climates

Present and past distributions of the two flower colour lineages of L. monelli in the Mediterranean Basin were modelled using the maximum entropy algorithm (MaxEnt v. 3.4.4; Phillips & Dudík 2008). Occurrences were collected from field campaigns, the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF 2022; https://doi.org/10.15468/dl.a8ngjc), and verified herbarium vouchers from several Herbaria: Herbario de la Universidad de Sevilla (SEV), Herbario del Real Jardín Botánico de Madrid (MA), Herbario del Jardín Botánico de la Universidad de Valencia (JBV), and Herbario del Instituto Botánico de Barcelona (BC). A total of 174 presence points (142 from blue‐flowered and 32 from orange‐flowered plants) were used, covering the reported geographic distribution of the species throughout the Mediterranean Basin.

Four environmental layers (bio9, bio12, srad3, srad7) from WorldClim (Fick & Hijmans 2017) and one edaphic variable (cec) from SoilGrids (Poggio et al. 2021) were selected from a broader dataset (Table S2) based on ecological relevance (Pujadas 1997 and pers obs), variance inflation factor (VIF) <10 (López‐Jurado et al. 2018), and Spearman correlation (<0.6). The selected layers were rescaled to 5 km2 with the Resample function in R software (R Core Team 2021) and clipped to the Mediterranean region extent using QGIS 3.10 (QGIS Development Team 2021). Model accuracy was evaluated using the subsample method with 50 replicates, training 70% of the data and testing 30%, together with 10,000 background points and a maximum of 5,000 iterations. The model performance was assessed using AUC values and permutation importance of each variable. The models were also projected onto past climate scenarios: Mid Holocene (MH; ~6 kya; WorldClim; CNRM‐CM5), Last Glacial Maximum (LGM; ~20 kya, WorldClim; CCSM4) and Last Interglacial (LIG; ~125 kya; WorldClim; Otto‐Bliesner et al. 2006), adding present‐day edaphic and solar radiation variables. The optimum model for each past period was selected based on high AUC scores from all 12 tested models (Table S3).

RESULTS

Genetic variability

The seven nSSR markers analysed across all samples revealed a total of 108 alleles (10–25 per locus), with a mean number of alleles per locus and population ranging from 2.57 to 5.14. Genotype accumulation curves indicated that five to six loci sufficed to adequately capture genetic diversity of the studied populations (Fig. S1). Observed heterozygosity (Ho) and expected heterozygosity (He) ranged from 0.512–0.821 (mean 0.641) and 0.457–0.681 (mean 0.574), respectively (Table 1). The inbreeding coefficient (F is) varied from −0.477 to 0.197 (mean − 0.117). Blue‐flowered plants had similar mean values for F is compared to orange‐flowered plants, but with a slightly higher Ho and He (Ho: 0.650 blue vs. 0.610 orange; He: 0.580 blue vs. 0.552 orange; F is: −0.127 blue vs −0.112 orange). The 12 studied populations deviated from Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE; P‐value < 0.05) after Bonferroni correction, mainly associated with high F is values, indicating either heterozygote excess (negative F is) or deficiency (positive F is). Low null allele frequencies (No) were estimated (0.000 to 0.122; mean 0.039; Table 1). Hence, there was no evidence of linkage disequilibrium for each locus pair across all populations.

Comparison of genetic parameters between flower colour lineages and geographic groups (see Bayesian analysis below) revealed no significant differences in Ho, He, or F is. However, Ho (OSx = 0.291, P‐value = 0.024) and F is (OSx = 0.556 P‐value = 0.011) differed significantly across geographic clusters (K = 5; Table S4): populations from Portugal (LM1‐5) had the highest Ho mean (0.733) and lowest F is mean (−0.299), while those from southern Spain (LM6‐10 and LM14) had the lowest Ho mean (0.560) and highest F is mean (0.020).

Pairwise genetic distance indicated that orange‐flowered populations (excluding LM25) were more genetically similar to each other than to blue‐flowered populations (PhiST<0.3; Fig. S2). In contrast, blue‐flowered populations from Portugal had greater internal genetic divergence than other blue populations (Pairwise PhiST>0.3). Only six populations (LM6, LM10, LM11, LM12, LM20 and LM27) showed evidence of genetic impoverishment via a recent bottleneck in some of the applied models (Table S5).

Geographic versus genetic distance

Mantel tests revealed a significant correlation between genetic and geographic distances across all pairs of L. monelli populations (r = 0.435, P‐value = 0.000). This pattern persisted within each colour lineage, with blue‐flowered populations (r = 0.480, P‐value = 0.000) and orange‐flowered populations (r = 0.592, P‐value = 0.023) having similar associations. Thus, the isolation‐by‐distance hypothesis was corroborated. Within geographic groups, there was a significant association between genetic and geographic distances, except in populations from the southwestern Iberian Peninsula, Tunisia, and Sardinia (Table S6). Furthermore, genetic diversity did not differ significantly among populations of varying sizes, regardless of flower colour (blue‐flowered plants F 2,18 = 0.724, P‐value = 0.498; orange‐flowered plants F 2,3 = 0.491, P‐value = 0.654) or overall (F 2,24 = 0.851, P‐value = 0.440).

Population structure

Phylogeographic analyses

Bayesian clustering identified K = 5 as the first optimum number of clusters, capturing most genetic variation (DK = 66.535, Mean LnP(K) = −6,715.1; Fig. S3), delineating five distinct clusters (Fig. 1A): (1) five blue‐flowered populations from Portugal (LM1–5); (2) six blue‐flowered populations from SW Spain (LM6–10 and LM14); (3) eight blue‐flowered populations from central Spain (LM11–13 and LM15–19); (4) orange‐flowered populations from NE Spain and Morocco (LM20‐24); and (5) a mixed colour cluster comprising one orange‐flowered population from Sardinia (LM25) and two blue‐flowered populations from Tunisia (LM26, 27). Bayesian mean F ST values for each group were 0.299, 0.2587, 0.192, 0.301, and 0.219, respectively. Furthermore, both the Calinski and Harabasz pseudo‐F and the Bayesian Information Criterion suggested K = 25 as optimal clustering, indicating considerable genetic independence among populations.

The PCoA revealed clear separation between the flower colour lineages, both at individual and population level (Fig. 1B, C). Individual‐level analysis accounted for 22.20% of total variance (Fig. 1B), while population‐level analysis explained 39.42% of variance (Fig. 1C). These ordination results highlighted two prominent patterns: separation of the SW Iberian Peninsula populations from all other populations, and separation between blue‐ and orange‐flowered plants.

A non‐hierarchical AMOVA (Table 2) indicated that more than half of the total genetic variation was attributable to within‐populations components (67.3%, F ST = 0.329). Nested AMOVA found 11.4% (F ST = 0.490) of variation between flower colour lineages, and 17.8% (F ST = 0.248) of variation among the five clusters (Table 2). The F ST values suggest that the genetic structure of L. monelli is better explained by flower colour lineage differences than by the five clusters.

Demography and migration

Tajima's D‐test did not provide significant results for any population (mean 0.097 ± 0.347), while Fu's Fs‐test revealed significant negative values (mean − 4.235 ± 0.807) in almost all populations (except LM4 and LM9; Table 1), indicating excess low‐frequency alleles. There was a unimodal mismatch distribution pattern for both flower colour lineages (Fig. S4). Significant (P < 0.05) sums of squared deviations (SSD) supported the sudden expansion model tested. However, at population level, seven populations (LM2, LM5, LM6, LM8, LM17, LM20 and LM27) exhibited clear signs of demographic expansion (Fig. S5), and only two populations (LM6 and LM19) gave significant SSD test results (Table 1). These results align more closely with Tajima's D index than with Fu's index.

Environmental niche model (ENM)

The ENM results (Fig. 2) had exceptional predictive accuracy, with AUC scores exceeding 0.9 for both the current and the three past periods (Table S7), indicating negligible rates of false negative and false positive habitat suitability predictions.

Predicted environmental suitability for blue and orange lineage of Lysimachia monelli, and their overlap region, according to the present (Pr.), Mid Holocene (MH), Last Glacial Maximum (LGM), and Last Interglacial (LIG) conditions. Colour gradient in each lineage represents different levels of predicted suitability, where blue is the lowest and red is the highest level. Overlapping areas are indicated in red in the overlap region maps.

Habitat suitability differed markedly between lineages. For example, the blue lineage preferred solar radiation in March (srad3) between 10,000 kJ and 16,000 kJ, solar radiation in July (srad7) >24,000 kJ, with a maximum ca. 30,000 kJ, average annual precipitation (bio12) around 600 mm, a broad mean temperature range of the driest quarter (bio9; 10–40°C), and soil cation exchange capacity (cec) between 100 and >500 (Fig. S6, Table S8). Conversely, suitable habitats for the orange lineage were characterized by a mean temperature of the driest quarter (bio9) around 20°C, March solar radiation (srad3) from 14,000 kJ to 24,000 kJ, July solar radiation (srad7) <28,000 kJ, a wide range of mean annual precipitation (bio12; 100–2000 mm), and soil cation exchange capacity (cec) from 0 to >300 (Fig. S6, Table S8). Therefore, current ENMs revealed clear habitat suitability differentiation between colour lineages (Fig. 2). Habitat suitability for the blue lineage was the Iberian Peninsula, north Morocco, north Tunisia, Corsica, and parts of Italy, while the orange lineage optimal conditions were east and west coasts of the Iberian Peninsula, north Africa, coastal France, Corsica, Sardinia, and Sicily.

Over time, habitat suitability for both lineages of L. monelli has expanded within the Mediterranean Basin (Fig. 2). In the past, the blue lineage was restricted to Tunisia and the Iberian Peninsula, with a significant contraction during the LGM. Regarding the orange lineage, it was previously confined to the NE Iberian Peninsula, vanished during the LGM, and was limited to areas of North Africa. There were few overlapping suitable habitats for the two lineages (Fig. 2). During the LIG and MH periods, these areas were in the central–eastern Iberian Peninsula. Currently, the two lineages are more widespread in the Iberian Peninsula (S and SW coasts and some central spots), southern Corsica, and northern Sardinia.

DISCUSSION

This study attempted to reconstruct evolutionary relationships between the two flower colour lineages of L. monelli by integrating nSSR data with niche modelling analyses. The pronounced genetic differentiation between these lineages implies a long period of isolation. Moreover, the high genetic diversity in the blue‐flowered populations in the western groups suggests that this species likely originated in the western Iberian Peninsula.

Lineage distribution and niche modelling

Environment niche models (ENMs) demonstrated that, at a broad spatial scale, the two lineages of L. monelli occur in the Mediterranean Basin. Their presence across western and central regions suggests that a common ancestor also inhabited the Mediterranean Basin. Climate and soil characteristics had a pivotal role in lineage distribution. However, at a finer scale, the blue and orange lineages exhibit different projected area distributions, influenced by differences in environmental conditions. Probability of the presence of the blue L. monelli increases in habitats with consistent annual precipitation around 600 mm, and a wide temperature range during the dry season, while orange L. monelli distribution relied on a broader range of annual precipitation and a more stable temperature during the drier quarter (Fig. S6). In addition, the blue lineage tolerates a wider soil cation exchange capacity (cec) above 200 mmol(c)/kg, whereas the orange lineage favours a narrower cec range (70–200 mmol(c)/kg) (Fig. S6). Moreover, the two lineages had contrasting responses to solar radiation in March and July, with no overlap: blue responding to low solar radiation in March and high in July, whereas orange lineages display an opposite pattern (Fig. S6). These results suggest that differences in climatic, edaphic, and solar radiation properties could be key for maintenance of this flower colour polymorphism in L. monelli and explain the allopatric distribution of the two lineages. This pattern has been reported in a closely related species, L. arvensis, where the blue lineage tolerates more xeric conditions than the orange lineage, with a latitudinal pattern from Central Europe (exclusively orange) to Mediterranean environments (predominantly blue) (Arista et al. 2013; Ortiz et al. 2015). Abiotic factors could play an important role in the maintenance of flower colour polymorphisms (Strauss & Whittall 2006). Some studies have suggested that pelargonidin derivatives (low‐hydroxylated anthocyanins of red–orange colour) are less effective in protection against drought and high irradiance stress than delphinidin or cyanidin derivatives (high‐hydroxylated anthocyanins of blue–purple colour) (reviewed in Narbona et al. 2018). Similarly, rhamnose, the specific glycoside stabilizing malvidin anthocyanidin in blue petals of L. arvensis and L. monelli (Sánchez‐Cabrera et al. 2021, 2024) is reported to confer stress tolerance (Jiang et al. 2021).

Past niche models predicted near‐complete separation of the two lineages from LIG to the present time, but current ENMs reveal potential overlap areas for blue and orange L. monelli. The central and southern coast of Portugal, and the south coast and central‐eastern region of the Iberian Peninsula appear most suitable for coexistence of the lineages (Fig. 2). Despite historical records of orange L. monelli in central Portugal (Sintra; Coutinho 1913), it no longer exists (Jiménez‐López observations). Current ENMs suggest a larger potential area for both lineages than they currently occupy (Sánchez‐Cabrera et al. 2024), possibly overestimating the overlap. MaxEnt, assuming pseudo‐absence, can be influenced by the number of occurrence points in the analysis (VanDerWal et al. 2009). Although the number of presence points adhered to recommendations (Wisz et al. 2008), the spatial distribution of these points could impact the model predictive capacity. The shorter that distance, the greater the predictive ability of this method (Kiedrzyński et al. 2017). In our study, the orange lineage had three highly disjunct centers (NE Iberian Peninsula, Morocco, Sardinia), which might affect precision for the areas projected by MaxEnt, because predictive models can struggle to discern fine‐scale conditions that constrain distribution of a species, as they are dominated by parameters that only coarsely discriminate regional conditions (VanDerWal et al. 2009). In addition, factors not considered, such as anthropogenic pressure (particularly intense along the Mediterranean coast of the Iberian Peninsula and Mediterranean islands) may have influenced current distribution of the species (Díez‐Garretas et al. 2019).

All molecular analyses indicate that L. monelli populations have diversified along a longitudinal gradient, with western populations having higher genetic diversity than eastern populations. The same pattern has been reported in other Mediterranean species, such as Atripex halimus, Taxus baccata, and Rosmarinus officinalis, where western Mediterranean populations differ from central or eastern populations (Ortiz‐Dorda et al. 2005; González‐Martínez et al. 2010; Mateu‐Andrés et al. 2013). Bioclimatic changes during the Quaternary (Comes & Kadereit 1998) and geographic isolation appear to have driven divergence in L. monelli. Potential long‐term refugia from the Quaternary correspond to the southern Iberian Peninsula and Morocco, and coastal areas of the Central Mediterranean Basin (Médail & Diadema 2009). Genetic diversity tends to be higher in refugia and contact zones (Bonin et al. 2007). Niche models for the blue and orange lineages showed a reduction in suitable habitats during the LGM, suggesting these areas may have served as climate refuges.

Genetic diversity and structure through L. monelli distribution

Lysimachia monelli has high genetic diversity, comparable to or exceeding that of other Mediterranean Lysimachia species (Calero et al. 1999; Jiménez‐López, Ortiz, et al. 2020; Kwiecińska‐Poppe et al. 2020). The low F is values in most populations suggest a heterozygote surplus, likely related to the species RNAse‐based self‐incompatibility system (Gibbs & Talavera 2001; Ramanauskas et al. 2025). This excess of heterozygotes may help stabilize allele frequencies, enhancing genetic diversity, mating opportunities, and fertility (Wright et al. 2008), as reported in other self‐incompatible species, e.g., Leavenworthia alabamic (Koelling et al. 2011), Linaria vulgaris (Bartlewicz et al. 2015), and Bromelia hieronymi (Godoy et al. 2018). Moreover, the blue lineage had the highest genetic diversity, suggesting it is older than the orange lineage. This aligns with previous studies on colour flower polymorphic systems, where evolutionary shifts generally occur from delphinidin/cyanidin production (blue) to pelargonidin production (red/orange) (Rausher 2008; Wessinger & Rausher 2013). A recent study revealed the key genes underlying the single shift from blue to orange in Lysimachia species (Sánchez‐Cabrera et al. 2024).

The genetic diversity suggests three different structuring levels. First, several indicators point to high independence among populations: (1) elevated F priv, (2) a significant proportion of variability explained within populations, (3) Calinski and Harabasz’ pseudo‐F suggesting K = 25 as the optimal number of clusters, and (4) isolation‐by‐distance supported by the Mantel test. In fact, gene flow appears minimal between most populations. Second, nSSR data reveal geographic structuring among populations, indicating a disjunctive distribution with five groups, strongly supported by the PCoA. Third, F ST values provide a better explanation of the genetic structure of L. monelli by flower colour lineage differences than by the five clusters. However, the five clusters can also reflect flower colour groupings, since there are three blue, one orange and one mixed cluster. Three clusters divide the blue lineage populations of the Iberian Peninsula based on location: two from the Atlantic coast and one from the center and Mediterranean coast of the Iberian Peninsula. Similar patterns of divergence between Atlantic and Mediterranean coasts have been reported in other perennial plant species (Kadereit et al. 2005; Juan et al. 2012). The orange cluster includes populations from Morocco and the Northeast Iberian Peninsula. During the Quaternary, a fluctuating ‘stepping stone’ scenario likely facilitated multiple expansion pathways from Morocco and other Central Mediterranean areas (Ortiz et al. 2008; Del Valle et al. 2019). Finally, a mixed cluster of two blue populations from Tunisia and one orange population from Sardinia could indicate a possible secondary contact zone between lineages. Although with low probability, past niche models suggest possible coexistence of the two lineages in Sardinia (Fig. 2), making this island a plausible past contact zone.

CONCLUSIONS

Currently blue and orange flower colour lineages of L. monelli differ markedly in distribution, seemingly driven by environmental and edaphic factors. Both lineages display pronounced genetic divergence, with minimal gene flow between the two. The largest genetic similarity between lineages was in the Central Mediterranean, suggesting historical gene flow in this region. Overall, our findings indicate an almost complete geographic and genetic isolation, reflecting that these lineages could be nearing the culmination of a speciation process. However, more genomic evidence is necessary to finally elucidate the speciation status of L. monelli flower colour lineages. This study underscores the profound impact of the unique topography and climate of the Mediterranean Basin on the diversification and distribution of angiosperms.
