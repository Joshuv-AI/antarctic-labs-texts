Prevalence and risk factors for hepatitis B virus infection among expectant mothers in Jirapa Municipality, Upper West Region, Ghana: a facility-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Yeri Monica
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic HBV infection remains a global health priority, contributing to over 800,000 annual deaths. In sub-Saharan Africa, high endemicity persists despite the availability of preventive vaccines. This study assessed the HBsAg seroprevalence and identified independent socio-demographic and clinical correlates among pregnant women in the Jirapa Municipality, providing the evidence base required for targeted maternal-child health interventions in the UWR of Ghana.MethodsA facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 235 pregnant women in the Jirapa Municipality using a mixed purposive-consecutive sampling approach. HBV infection status was determined via prospective data abstraction of laboratory-confirmed HBsAg rapid diagnostic test results. Firth’s penalized logistic regression was employed to minimize small-sample bias and ensure parameter stability. Model performance was validated through sensitivity analyses comparing penalized likelihood with complementary log–log regression. Final model selection was guided by Akaike (AIC) and Bayesian (BIC) Information Criteria, with specification verified via the link test. All analyses were performed in Stata 16.0, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe clinical prevalence of HBsAg seropositivity among the sampled population was 10.6% (95% CI: 7.0–15.3%), categorizing the Jirapa Municipality as a region of high HBV endemicity per WHO criteria. Bivariate analysis identified maternal age, marital status, number of co-wives, parity, religion, prior blood transfusion, hospital admission, gestational age, and ANC visit frequency as significant correlates (p < 0.05). However, in model 2, only the number of co-wives remained a persistent independent correlate. Specifically, having exactly one co-wife was associated with significantly lower odds of HBV infection compared to other marital structures (AOR = 0.05; 95% CI: 0.00–0.72; p = 0.027). Other socio-demographic and clinical factors did not retain statistical significance after adjustment for confounding variables.ConclusionThe 10.6% HBsAg prevalence documented in the study classifies Jirapa Municipality as a high-endemicity cluster. The independent association between HBV infection and spousal marital structure suggests antenatal care should serve as a gateway for family-centered interventions. The study recommends adopting a “Triple-Linkage” model integrating universal screening, reflex HBeAg risk stratification, and partner-inclusive counseling. Transitioning to this proactive care continuum is essential to strengthen the clinical infrastructure required to mitigate the regional burden of chronic hepatitis B.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27966-1.

Background

Hepatitis can be defined simply as an inflammation of the liver [1]. Hepatitis B virus infection is a serious chronic liver disease that affects 1 million people worldwide [2]. Globally, 257–296 million people are chronically infected and thus, approximately 820,000 to about 877,000 people die annually from hepatitis B virus-related complications and hepatocellular carcinoma [3, 4]. As of 2019, the global prevalence of chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection across all age groups was estimated at 4.1%, corresponding to approximately 284–351 million people living with chronic infection worldwide. During the same period, HBV-related causes accounted for an estimated 555,000 deaths (487,000-630,000) globally [5]. Hepatitis B virus is an entirely vaccine-preventable liver disease with an effective vaccine available for over 4 decades [6, 7]. Hepatitis infection is grouped into phases, progressing from mild to acute, and then to chronic [8]. The disease is also marked by an intermediary phase that plays a vital role in its transmission. Individuals infected do not show any symptoms but rather harbour the virus, potentially spreading it to others [9]. Usually, because not all newly infected persons present with symptoms, others infected with the virus may experience symptoms such as fatigue, loss of appetite, stomach ache, nausea, and jaundice [10]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) and Terrault et al., the hepatitis B virus can persist outside the human body for extended periods and typically follows a non-linear clinical course, ranging from acute infection to chronic disease. HBV endemicity is classified based on HBsAg prevalence as high (> 8%), intermediate (2–7%), or low (< 2%) [11, 12].

According to the hepatitis B Foundation, the hepatitis B virus is readily transmitted through exposure to infected blood and certain body fluids, including semen and vaginal secretions. Common routes include unprotected sexual contact, sharing of needles or syringes, unsafe injections, and the use of contaminated instruments or equipment. Transmission can also occur from an infected mother to her child around the time of birth [13]. Globally, perinatal transmission and early childhood exposure remain the dominant pathways of infection, particularly in high-endemic settings, where both mother-to-child and close household contact contribute substantially to ongoing spread [14]. The term “Mother-to-Child Transmission” is often used to replace “Perinatal Transmission”, and includes all hepatitis B virus infections in a neonate acquired at or before birth, during delivery, and also during early childhood, either by transplacental, in utero, and or via postnatal and breast milk [15]. Mother-to-child transmission is characterised by the presence of viral surface antigen positivity at six to twelve months of age in a young child born to a carrier mother [16]. Hepatitis B prevalence shows marked geographic variation across WHO regions, with the highest burden concentrated in the African and Western Pacific regions. Current global estimates indicate that these two regions carry a disproportionately large share of chronic HBV infections, with population prevalence levels of roughly 6.1% in the African Region and about 6.2% in the Western Pacific Region [17]. Ginzberg and colleagues reported a HBV prevalence of about 10% in their study population. Hepatitis also remains a major contributor to mortality in several high-burden settings. It’s ranked among the leading causes of death in sub-Saharan Africa, parts of the Pacific region, and Eastern Europe [18].

Furthermore, Spearman and colleagues reported that hepatitis B remains endemic across much of Africa, with an estimated HBsAg seroprevalence of about 6.1% (95% CI: 4.6–8.5). The persistently high burden observed in sub-Saharan Africa has been linked to gaps in maternal and child health services, particularly limitations in prevention of mother-to-child transmission and early childhood infection control measures [19]. The prevalence of the virus and infection rate among pregnant mothers in Ghana are 12.3% and 13.1%, respectively [20]. Ghana has been earmarked as highly endemic to the hepatitis B virus, and this has raised a greater public health problem in Ghana [21]. Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3 aims to take immediate action to combat hepatitis and address epidemics of communicable diseases as a priority across all age groups by 2030 [22]. Infants are projected to be at the highest rate, around 90%, progressing to a more chronic infection [23]. Therefore, the World Health Organisation has reported that mother-to-child transmission is the principal mode of transmission and has raised the alarm, noting that its prevention and management during pregnancy are vital [14]. Nationally, the prevalence of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection among pregnant women in Ghana has been estimated at approximately 7.4%; significant regional disparities persist, particularly within the northern belt [24]. Within the Upper West Region, existing literature reveals a concerning and heterogeneous burden; for instance, recent research in the neighboring Wa Municipality reported a staggering HBsAg seroprevalence of 20.2% [25]. Additional reports from the Jirapa Municipal Health Directorate, together with recent regional studies, indicate an increasing number of liver-related conditions among both males and females, raising concern that a substantial proportion of HBV infections may remain undetected and unmanaged [26–28].

Despite this high regional endemicity, a critical research gap persists within the Jirapa Municipality. To date, there is a marked lack of dedicated, municipality-specific data regarding the isolated burden of HBV infection among expectant mothers. Previous investigations in this locality, notably those by [27], focused primarily on a broad range of sexually transmitted blood-borne infections (STBBIs), providing a composite prevalence but failing to isolate independent factors specifically for HBV. Furthermore, while regional research has traditionally prioritized traditional risk factors such as scarification and gravidity [16], the influence of domestic social structures such as polygamous and monogamous unions remains largely unexplored in the Jirapa context. Although national health policies in Ghana mandate integrating HBV screening into routine antenatal care, significant gaps persist in post-screening management. Many pregnant women in both urban and rural settings remain uninformed of their status following routine testing, reflecting a systemic challenge in result communication and patient counseling. This failure to effectively link HBsAg-positive mothers to essential follow-up services such as specialist consultation, antiviral therapy, or neonatal immunoprophylaxis remains a critical barrier to eliminating mother-to-child infection. Consequently, the persistent risk of vertical transmission in the Jirapa Municipality is driven less by a lack of initial screening and more by the breakdown in the clinical cascade of care that should follow a positive diagnosis. Given these limited health services and information gaps, this study assessed the prevalence and risk factors for HBV infection among pregnant women in the Jirapa Municipality to provide the evidence base necessary for targeted intervention.

Methods

Study design and population

This facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 235 pregnant women seeking antenatal care. Rather than a purely probability-based municipal survey, the study employed a mixed purposive-consecutive sampling approach. This design was chosen to systematically capture all pregnant women receiving routine clinical care at the participating facilities during the study period. Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection status was determined through a prospective data abstraction approach linked to mandatory routine screening. No additional blood samples were drawn for research purposes; instead, the study used laboratory-confirmed HBsAg results obtained at the point of care by the facilities’ laboratories. These results were systematically abstracted from the participants’ Maternal and Child Health (MCH) Books. Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics were collected using structured questionnaires administered alongside the clinical record review. While this approach provides high internal validity for the maternal population at these facilities, it was recognized as a non-probability-based sample that represents the clinical population of the Jirapa Municipality rather than the general municipal population.

Study settings

The study was conducted in the Jirapa Municipality, located in the Upper West Region of Ghana (see Fig. 1). The municipality has an estimated population of approximately 91,240 in 2024 and is served by a network of healthcare facilities, including hospitals, health centers, and community-based health planning and services (CHPS) compounds [30]. The St. Joseph’s Hospital serves as the only hospital and the primary clinical and diagnostic hub. By sampling from this central referral hospital and a randomly selected health center, the study captured a population representative of the main points of care for the majority of the municipality’s maternal population [31]. According to the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS) and the GHS 2022 annual report for the Upper West Region, the area maintains exceptionally high antenatal care coverage. Approximately 98% of pregnant women in the region attended at least one ANC visit with a skilled provider, and 82.6% completed the recommended four or more visits [32]. Because nearly the entire pregnant population in the municipality interfaces with the formal health system, a facility-based sample in this context captures the majority of pregnant women from both the urban core and the peripheral rural sub-districts who are referred to the hospital for laboratory screenings and specialized care.

Sampling technique and procedure

A multistage sampling method was used to recruit sub-districts, health facilities, and respondents. First, all sub-districts in the municipality were listed. Of the seven sub-districts offering antenatal care (ANC) services, only one was randomly selected. Next, in the second stage, health facilities within the chosen sub-district were identified. A list of all facilities providing ANC services was compiled, and, due to limited resources and time, only one health facility was selected by simple random ballot. In addition, St. Joseph’s Hospital was purposively included because it is centrally located, serves a large population, and acts as the main referral hospital for all sub-districts in the municipality; most women seeking ANC services use this hospital when either referred to carry out a lab test. These selected facilities are better resourced with personnel and equipment, such as hepatitis testing instruments, compared to others, and the sampling frame captures a significant proportion of the total ANC attendees in the Jirapa Municipality, as the two selected facilities serve as the primary and secondary health hubs for the district. In the third stage, quota sampling was applied to select respondents at each facility. The sample size was calculated and allocated per facility based on prior ANC attendance records. Both facilities were typically visited on ANC days. Expectant mothers waited in queues by arrival time for assessment and counseling. Finally, to ensure a diverse participant profile, the study employed a mixed purposive-consecutive sampling strategy rather than a purely probability-based municipal sample, selecting participants until the quota for each facility was reached, with multiple visits as needed to achieve the required sample size. This approach was chosen to maximize the inclusion of women across different levels of the healthcare referral chain within the municipality. Four unemployed nurses were recruited and trained as research assistants to collect the data. These nurses were specifically trained to translate the questionnaire from English to Dagaare and back to English. Prior to data collection, a pre-test was conducted on 5% of a similar population outside the study location to ensure the questionnaire was validated and standardized.

Sample size allocation

Participants were selected using a multi-stage sampling technique. The study area was stratified by hospital and sub-district, and quotas were assigned based on antenatal care attendance (see Table 1).

Laboratory analysis and hepatitis B testing procedures

HBsAg screening was performed at the first antenatal booking visit using the SD BIOLINE HBsAg WB rapid diagnostic test (RDT) (Abbott Diagnostics, Republic of Korea). This WHO-prequalified qualitative immune-chromatographic assay was the standard-of-care kit supplied by the Ghana Health Service (GHS) in 2022. According to the manufacturer’s specifications, the kit demonstrates 100% sensitivity and specificity. Strict quality control protocols were maintained throughout the study. Every rapid diagnostic test strip was inspected for the appearance of a procedural control line to validate sample migration. In accordance with 2022 facility protocols, external validation was performed weekly using known HBsAg-positive and HBsAg-negative serum panels to ensure batch consistency. Any indeterminate results were cross-checked by a senior laboratory technologist to ensure diagnostic accuracy. All tests were performed by trained laboratory technologists in accordance with GHS standard operating procedures. Results were interpreted within the recommended 15–20-minute window and recorded in both the ANC laboratory register and the study data form. While resource constraints in the municipal setting precluded the use of Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) or Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) for confirmation, the high field validity of the WHO-prequalified RDT justified its use for determining clinical prevalence. This rapid testing approach was prioritized for its feasibility and its ability to facilitate same-visit counseling and referral for seropositive women.

Privacy and data collection

To ensure data integrity and minimize social desirability bias, all interviews were conducted in private consulting rooms to guarantee auditory privacy. Participants were explicitly informed of the study’s anonymity and assured that the disclosure of sensitive behavioral information would not influence the quality of clinical care received at the facility. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire tailored to the local context and informed by established literature on maternal hepatitis B epidemiology. While the researcher extracted HBsAg results and clinical history directly from maternal health records and ANC laboratory registers, the questionnaire was used to capture additional socio-demographic characteristics, obstetric history, and exposure-related risk factors. The instrument was administered in Dagaare and English by trained data collectors to ensure participant comprehension. Although the comprehensive instrument covered a wide range of variables (see Additional file 1), the present analysis focuses strictly on those directly relevant to the study’s primary objectives. This approach enabled rigorous triangulation between self-reported behavioral data and retrospective clinical data recorded in the facility’s maternal books.

Statistical analysis

All 235 enrolled participants completed the questionnaires with no withdrawals. Data were initially cleaned and validated in Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft Corporation) before being imported into STATA version 16.0 for analysis. Descriptive statistics summarized participants’ socio-demographic and clinical characteristics. To ensure stability of the multivariable model, multicollinearity among the candidate variables (p < 0.20 in univariable analysis was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF); p < 0.05 was considered indicative of the absence of significant multicollinearity. Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants

A total of 235 pregnant women were enrolled in the study (see Table 2). The study population was predominantly young, with the highest concentration in the 20–24 year age bracket (32.8%), and followed by the 25–29 year group (27.2%). Maternal HBsAg seropositivity demonstrated significant variation across age cohorts. The highest prevalence was observed among women aged 20–24 years, who accounted for 36.0% of all infections, whereas the lowest prevalence (8.0%) was observed among those aged < 20 years. Pearson’s chi-square analysis confirmed a statistically significant association between maternal age and HBV infection (p < 0.001). Regarding marital status, the majority of participants were married (71.1%). The distribution of HBV infection was notably higher among married women (72.0%) compared to those who were single or divorced (20.0%) or cohabiting (8.0%). This disparity was statistically significant (p = 0.040), suggesting that marital status is a significant correlate of HBV infection within this clinical population. The majority of participants resided in urban areas (59.1%); however, residential status did not significantly influence HBV prevalence (urban: 10.8% and rural: 10.5%; p = 0.735). Educational attainment was diverse, with 35.0% having completed Junior High School (JHS) and 25.9% holding tertiary qualifications. While HBV prevalence was highest among those with a JHS education (48.0% of the positive cohort), this association was not statistically significant (p = 0.157). Occupational analysis showed that beauticians and dressmakers constituted the largest group (33.6%), followed by government employees (20.4%). Although the highest infection rates were observed among beauticians/dressmakers (36.0%) and housewives (24.0%), occupational category was not a significant correlate of infection (p = 0.194). Similarly, while 80.0% of HBV-positive women earned ≤ 500 GH₵ monthly, income level did not demonstrate a statistically significant association with serostatus (p = 0.363) (see Table 2).

Given the low frequency of certain outcome events, a multivariable Firth’s penalized logistic regression was performed to ensure parameter stability and reduce bias. Table 3 presents the bivariate (Model I) and multivariable (Model II) analyses of factors associated with HBsAg seropositivity.

Bivariate analysis (model 1)

In the bivariate analysis, maternal age emerged as a significant factor. Specifically, mothers in the oldest age cohort (40–44 years) had remarkably higher odds of infection than those under 20 years (COR = 53.00; 95% CI: 1.95–1439.4; p = 0.018). Marital status and household structure emerged as notable correlates. Single or divorced women were over six times more likely to be HBsAg-positive compared to those in cohabiting arrangements (COR = 6.59; 95% CI: 1.33–32.74; p = 0.021). Interestingly, women in polygynous households where the husband had only one other wife demonstrated a 98% reduction in the odds of infection (COR = 0.02; 95% CI: 0.00–0.17; p < 0.001) compared to those in households with two or more other wives. Significant associations were also observed for religious affiliation and obstetric history. Participants identifying as Muslim or with other religions exhibited higher odds of infection (COR = 3.54; p = 0.005) compared to Christians. Regarding parity, grand multiparous women (those with 5 or more births) were at the highest risk; conversely, multiparous (COR = 0.17; p = 0.028), nulliparous (COR = 0.09; p = 0.006), and primiparous (COR = 0.16; p = 0.025) women all demonstrated significantly lower odds. Clinical history variables also showed a three-fold increase in risk within the crude model, specifically regarding a history of blood transfusion (COR = 3.69; 95% CI: 1.47–9.29; p = 0.005) and previous hospital admission (COR = 3.23; 95% CI: 1.01–10.30; p = 0.048) (see Table 3).

Adjusted analysis (model 2)

Upon adjustment in the final multivariable Firth’s penalized regression model, several variables that demonstrated strong associations in the bivariate analysis failed to retain independent statistical significance. This loss of statistical significance suggests that their initial effects were likely confounded by factors in the socio-demographic and clinical landscape. Notably, while the point estimate for maternal age (40–44 years) remained high (AOR = 16.45), it did not achieve statistical significance (p = 0.305). This lack of significance is directly attributable to numerical instability and sparse data bias, as evidenced by the exceptionally wide 95% confidence interval (0.08–3479.26). Such a wide interval reflects the limited sample size within this specific age sub-cohort, and thus, the estimate is interpreted as a potential trend rather than a precise measure of effect. Despite these shifts, the number of other wives remained a persistent and significant independent correlate of HBsAg seropositivity. Specifically, having only one other wife continued to serve as a significant protective factor, associated with a 94.9% reduction in the odds of HBV infection (AOR = 0.051; 95% CI: 0.00–0.72; p = 0.027) compared to women in households with multiple other wives. These findings underscore that once broader confounding variables are accounted for, specific household structures emerge as primary drivers of maternal infection risk in the Jirapa Municipality. Similarly, a history of hospital admission was associated with a fourfold increased likelihood of HBsAg seropositivity (AOR = 4.147); however, this relationship was only marginally significant (95% CI: 0.92–18.55; p = 0.063). Other factors that appeared significant in the bivariate analysis, including a history of blood transfusion (AOR = 1.669; p = 0.404) and various parity levels, did not demonstrate independent associations with HBV infection after adjustment for other covariates (see Table 3).

Discussion and summary of key findings

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants

In the present study involving 235 pregnant women, the overall HBV prevalence was 10.6% (25/235), indicating a high endemicity based on World Health Organization thresholds (> 8%). This estimate aligns with prior research from Ghana’s Eastern Region, which also reported a 10.6% HBsAg positivity among antenatal attendees, suggesting persistent high endemicity within several Ghanaian populations [36]. The age distribution in this cohort was skewed toward younger women, with those aged 20–24 years constituting the largest subgroup (32.8%). Notably, HBV positivity was highest in this age category (36.0%) and lowest among women under 20 years (8.0%), with a statistically significant association between increasing age and HBV infection (p < 0.001). This finding reflects patterns observed in other local settings; for example, a study in peri-urban Ghana reported a similar age distribution in pregnant women and a comparable HBV prevalence of 10.2%, though the highest infection rates were observed in slightly older age brackets (26–30 years) [37]. Such age-related variations may reflect cumulative exposure over time, differences in immune response dynamics, or cohort effects related to vaccination coverage. Internationally, age has shown heterogeneous associations with HBV status; some studies show higher positivity among younger pregnant women, whereas others report either non-significant or reversed trends, indicating the influence of context-specific behavioral and clinical risk factors [38]. Furthermore, this age distribution is consistent with the reproductive age profile commonly observed in the Upper West Region and other West African settings. Similar age patterns have been reported among ANC attendees, with the majority falling within the 20–30-year age group [39]. Comparable findings have also been documented in sub-Saharan Africa, with a mean maternal age of 31 years in a study conducted at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital [40].

Marital status was also significantly associated with HBV infection (p = 0.040), with married women representing the majority of positive cases (72.0%). While some studies have not observed significant relationships between marital status and HBV positivity, others have reported elevated HBV prevalence among women in polygamous unions or those with multiple sexual partners, possibly due to increased horizontal transmission risk [25]. The significant association in the current study may thus reflect underlying partner characteristics or sexual network dynamics. A large proportion of participants were urban residents (59.1%), but HBV prevalence did not differ significantly by residence (p = 0.735). This finding contrasts with some studies in sub-Saharan Africa, where rural residence has been linked with higher seroprevalence, potentially due to disparities in healthcare access and vaccination uptake [38]. The lack of a significant difference here may indicate relatively uniform access to antenatal services across the study area, or other unmeasured confounders such as community HBV awareness. While educational attainment and occupation descriptively varied, neither showed statistically significant associations with HBV infection (p = 0.157 and p = 0.194, respectively). Interestingly, although women with a Junior High School education had the highest prevalence (48.0%), this did not reach statistical significance. This observation parallels findings from several Ghanaian studies where education level failed to significantly predict HBV status among pregnant women. This lack of association persists despite broader literature suggesting that higher education is often linked with improved HBV knowledge and more robust preventive behaviors [41]. Income level similarly did not show a meaningful association (p = 0.363), though a high proportion of HBV-positive women were in the lowest income category (≤ 500 GH₵), suggestive of potential socioeconomic vulnerabilities that may influence health outcomes through complex pathways. This finding is comparable to reports from northern Ghana, where persistent poverty has been associated with increased vulnerability to infectious diseases [42]. Mkandawire et al. further described the clustering of socioeconomic and environmental stressors that contribute to localized patterns of vulnerability, a context that is relevant to the Upper West Region. Also, studies have highlighted the role of maternal education in facilitating engagement with preventive health interventions in Ghana [43].

Religious affiliation was associated with HBV infection (p = 0.004), and most participants were identified as Christians 197 (83.8%). Though the observed differences were modest, such associations may proxy for culturally linked behaviors or community norms influencing health service utilization or risk behaviors. These findings correlate with those reported by [44]; a majority, 91.1% of the participants, were Christians. Unlike the present study, where the majority were Christian, Mutaru and colleagues reported a largely Muslim study population (164/195; 84.1%), reflecting contextual differences in religious composition across study settings [45].

Obstetric and clinical history factors

Obstetric characteristics also demonstrated significant associations. The number of other wives of the husband showed a strong relationship with HBV infection (p < 0.001); women whose husbands had ≥ three other wives exhibited the highest HBV positivity, echoing evidence that polygamous or high partner turnover contexts may elevate exposure risk [25]. Parity was significantly associated with HBV status (p = 0.028), with multiparous women having the highest infection rates. This pattern is consistent with several regional studies demonstrating increased seroprevalence with higher gravidity and parity, possibly due to repeated antenatal exposures or other cumulative behavioral risks, although the literature remains mixed on this point [38]. The high proportion of women in the first trimester (108, 45.9%) and the predominance of multiparous participants (78, 33.2%) highlight the importance of early and repeat screening strategies during pregnancy. National and international policy guidance emphasized that the need for timely identification of infection in early gestation, coupled with continued engagement throughout antenatal care, is critical for interrupting mother-to-child transmission of hepatitis B virus [46, 47]. Leveraging the established use of antenatal care services may therefore strengthen efforts to eliminate vertical HBV transmission in this setting. Gestational age at first antenatal care (ANC) visit was also significantly associated with HBV infection (p = 0.048), with women in the second trimester showing the highest positivity (40.0%). This may reflect differences in timing of exposure, health-seeking behavior, or immunologic changes across trimesters. Previous studies have also reported varying associations between gestational age and HBV status, highlighting the need for early ANC engagement and routine screening regardless of gestational timing [38].

Finally, a higher frequency of ANC visits was significantly associated with HBV positivity (p = 0.028). Women who attended 4–6 visits had the highest HBV prevalence (52.0%). While frequent healthcare contact provides more opportunities for case detection, this finding could likely reflects detection bias or reverse causality. Since this study utilized results from maternal health books, it is probable that women identified as HBsAg-positive were scheduled for more frequent follow-up visits for counseling and monitoring. Consequently, the higher number of visits may represent a clinical consequence of diagnosis rather than an independent risk factor for infection. Similar observations have been reported in recent facility-based studies where antenatal care serves as a key entry point for screening and subsequent monitoring of HBV-positive pregnant women [48, 49]. Antenatal services provide an important opportunity to identify infected mothers and link them to appropriate care pathways aimed at reducing vertical transmission and improving maternal outcomes. In addition, routine antenatal screening has been emphasized as a critical strategy for detecting maternal HBV infection and initiating preventive interventions, including counseling, antiviral prophylaxis where indicated, and timely neonatal vaccination [50]. This underscores the importance of the ANC platform for intensive maternal surveillance once a status is established. Regardless, this finding underscores the importance of integrated screening and education throughout ANC. The pattern of observed socio-demographic and obstetric correlates reinforces the need for routine antenatal HBV screening, culturally tailored health education, and strengthened vaccination strategies to reduce risks of vertical and horizontal transmission.

A critical component of the care cascade is the clinical linkage of HBsAg-positive women to long-term management. While immediate antiviral therapy for the mother is often deferred until post-delivery or post-weaning to accommodate exclusive breastfeeding, the ‘linkage’ triggered by antenatal screening is vital for neonatal immunoprophylaxis. Specifically, identifying a mother’s status ensures the infant receives the HBV birth-dose vaccine within the 12-hour window required to interrupt vertical transmission. Furthermore, this linkage initiates a monitoring pathway for maternal liver health, allowing clinicians to track postnatal viral flares and determine the appropriate timing for maternal treatment initiation after the breastfeeding period is complete.

HBsAg seroprevalence and endemicity classification

While this study was facility-based, the findings reflect the clinical reality of the Jirapa Municipality. St. Joseph’s Hospital serves as the primary referral hub for the entire district; consequently, it functions as a “catch-all” point for pregnant women referred from even the most remote rural sub-districts. By capturing this central cohort, the observed HBsAg prevalence of 10.6% (95% CI: 7.0–15.3%) provides a high-fidelity indicator of the municipal disease burden. From an epidemiological perspective, this figure exceeds the World Health Organization threshold of 8%, formally categorizing the Jirapa Municipality as a region of high HBV endemicity. Overall, the prevalence of 10.6% in the current study is consistent with previous reports of high endemicity among pregnant women in Ghana and underscores the continued public health challenge posed by HBV in this vulnerable population [20]. This finding underscores a substantial public health burden and identifies a persistent reservoir for infection within the maternal population of the Upper West Region. Statistically, the observed distribution of 10.6% reactive versus 89.4% non-reactive cases represents a low-frequency outcome, and standard Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) was applied to address small-sample bias and parameter instability, and to avoid overestimation of effect sizes by applying Firth’s penalized likelihood approach in the multivariable analysis. This study estimate remains broadly consistent with wider national trends in Ghana. A recent pooled analysis of HBsAg prevalence among pregnant women nationwide reported an overall prevalence of approximately 12.3% [51]. Given the overlapping confidence intervals between our municipality-specific findings and the national average, there appears to be no meaningful epidemiologic divergence. This suggests that the drivers of HBV infection in Jirapa are not isolated anomalies but rather reflect broader national patterns of maternal endemicity.

Temporal and sub-national comparisons

At the sub-national level, this study’s estimate of 10.6% is consistent with previous data from the Jirapa Municipality, where HBsAg prevalence among pregnant women was reported at 9.8% [27]. The marginal difference between these two figures suggests a stable, persistent transmission pattern in the Upper West Region over time. Both estimates firmly position the municipality within the high endemicity category, highlighting a critical public health gap in maternal care. This local stability in prevalence, despite national efforts to scale up screening, underscores the need for more aggressive community-based interventions and suggests that the drivers of infection, such as household structure and limited access to birth-dose vaccination, remain largely unaddressed in this district.

Intra-national heterogeneity and localized epidemiology

Beyond the regional context, our findings highlight a significant degree of intra-national heterogeneity in HBV seroprevalence across Ghana. While our observed rate of 10.6% indicates high endemicity, other facility-based studies in southern populations have reported moderate endemicity levels, including 7.7% at Korle-Bu Teaching Hospital and approximately 6.0% in the Volta Region [52]. The disparity is even more pronounced within the Northern belt itself. Notably, our estimate is substantially lower than the 20.2% documented in the nearby Wa Municipality [25]. Such stark variation underscores the localized epidemiology of HBV within Northern Ghana. This divergence may be driven by several factors, including district-level differences in socio-economic status, cultural practices surrounding household structure, and the uneven distribution of specialized hepatology services [42]. Furthermore, the higher prevalence in the Northern regions compared to the South suggests that delayed diagnosis and geographical barriers to care remain significant drivers of persistent transmission. These localized “hotspots” of infection, like Wa and Jirapa, warrant a decentralized public health approach that prioritizes high-burden districts for the immediate scale-up of maternal-child health interventions. When situated within the broader continental and global epidemiological landscape, the observed 10.6% prevalence in the Jirapa Municipality significantly exceeds current pooled estimates. A recent meta-analysis of HBsAg seroprevalence among pregnant women across Africa reported a pooled prevalence of approximately 5–6%, a figure markedly lower than the burden documented in this study [53].

Global and continental perspectives on maternal HBV burden

This disparity is even more pronounced at the global level, where the pooled prevalence of chronic HBV among pregnant populations typically ranges between 3% and 5% [54]. These lower global averages might be largely driven by regions with long-standing universal neonatal vaccination and mature routine antenatal screening programs, infrastructure that remains suboptimal in many parts of sub-Saharan Africa. These study findings align closely with those of other high-burden African nations, such as Ethiopia (11.6%) [55] and Mauritania (10.7%) [56]. However, the 10.6% prevalence in Northern Ghana remains substantially elevated compared to the most recent continental pooled estimate of 6.77% for pregnant women [57]. This sustained elevation relative to the African average suggests that Northern Ghana represents a high-prevalence cluster within the continent.

In summary, these comparative analyses reveal a critical dual interpretation of the HBsAg seroprevalence in the Jirapa Municipality. From a methodological standpoint, the 10.6% prevalence is a low-frequency outcome; a distribution that supports the use of Firth’s penalized likelihood approach to ensure the stability and reliability of our multivariable estimates. Epidemiologically, however, this burden remains markedly elevated relative to global and continental benchmarks. Our findings are consistent with the highest-endemic estimates within the Northern Ghanaian belt, reinforcing the public health significance of this maternal cohort. This intersection of statistical rarity and high-endemicity in epidemiology is a defining feature of this study. It underscores the necessity for specialized analytical techniques in maternal health research while simultaneously providing a compelling evidence base for the targeted scale-up of screening and birth-dose vaccination programs. Addressing this high-prevalence cluster is essential for Ghana to align with the WHO’s 2030 targets for the elimination of viral hepatitis as a public health threat.

Bivariate and multivariable correlates of HBsAg positivity

The present study applied both bivariate and multivariable modeling approaches to identify factors associated with HBV seropositivity among pregnant women, with Firth’s penalized logistic regression used in the adjusted analysis due to the relatively low event frequency (10.6%). In the crude (bivariate) analysis, several socio-demographic and clinical variables showed statistically significant associations with HBV infection; however, after adjustment, only the husband having exactly one other wife remained independently associated, suggesting substantial confounding among the initially observed relationships. Maternal age showed a strong crude association, with women aged 40–44 years having markedly higher odds (COR = 53.00; p = 0.018) of HBV seropositivity compared with those aged < 20 years. Although the effect estimate remained elevated in the adjusted model, it lost statistical significance and was accompanied by an extremely wide confidence interval. This pattern is consistent with sparse-data effects in small subgroups and indicates instability in the estimates rather than a confirmed absence of association. Similar studies in Ghana and elsewhere have reported inconsistent age effects. Some Ghanaian antenatal studies found no independent association between maternal age and HBV infection after adjustment [20, 52], while others in sub-Saharan Africa reported higher prevalence among older reproductive-age women, attributing this to cumulative lifetime exposure risk [53]. The lack of stability in the adjusted estimate in the current study, therefore, aligns with the broader literature showing heterogeneous and context-dependent age effects. Again, this pattern is consistent with evidence from sub-Saharan Africa suggesting that cumulative lifetime exposure to HBV risk factors, together with historical gaps in hepatitis B vaccination programmes, contributes to higher prevalence among older women of reproductive age [44, 55]. Conversely, recent data from the Wa Municipality indicate an emerging shift toward higher risk among younger pregnant women, potentially reflecting incomplete coverage during the early phases of the 2002 universal childhood HBV vaccination programme [25].

Marital status was significantly associated with HBV infection in the crude analysis, with single or divorced women showing higher odds (COR = 6.59; p = 0.021) compared with cohabiting women, but this relationship did not persist after multivariable adjustment. This attenuation suggests that the crude association may be mediated through correlated behavioral or household-structure variables. Several antenatal HBV studies in Ghana and Ethiopia similarly reported crude marital status associations that did not remain significant after adjustment [20, 58], indicating that marital status often acts as a proxy rather than a direct determinant. A notable and biologically plausible finding in this study is the role of polygynous household structure. Compared with women whose husbands had three or more other wives, those whose husbands had only one other wife had significantly reduced odds of HBV infection in both crude and adjusted models (COR = 0.02; p = 0.001; AOR = 0.051; P = 0.027), with a 94–95% reduction in adjusted odds. This suggests that increasing spousal network size may elevate HBV exposure risk through expanded sexual transmission pathways. Evidence from West and East Africa has linked HBV infection with multiple sexual partnerships and polygamous unions, reflecting the role of horizontal adult transmission in endemic settings [53, 59]. Although many antenatal studies measure the number of lifetime partners rather than the number of co-wives, the underlying principle of transmission networks remains consistent. This study finding, therefore, fits well within the established epidemiologic framework of HBV sexual and household transmission.

Parity showed significant associations in the crude model, in which nulliparous, primiparous, and multiparous women had lower odds than grand multiparous women, but these associations disappeared after adjustment. This indicates confounding by age and household structure variables. Prior studies show mixed findings regarding parity: some report higher HBV prevalence with increasing parity due to cumulative exposure and repeated healthcare contact, while others find no independent association after controlling for age and behavioral factors [58, 60]. The adjusted results from the current study support the latter interpretation that parity alone is not an independent correlate when other covariates are considered. History of blood transfusion (OR = 3.69, p < 0.005) and prior hospital admission (OR = 3.23, p = 0.048) were significantly associated with HBV seropositivity in the crude model. These associations are epidemiologically credible, given the recognized role of unsafe blood and medical exposure in HBV transmission, especially in historically resource-constrained settings. However, neither variable retained statistical significance after adjustment, although prior hospital admission remained borderline. Similar diminishing after adjustment has been reported in several African antenatal studies, where transfusion history is associated at the crude level but weakens after multivariable control, likely reflecting improvements in blood screening practices and overlap with other exposure variables [53, 61]. These findings suggest that while medical exposure remains relevant, it may no longer be the dominant transmission pathway in many antenatal populations compared with household and sexual transmission routes. This observation again strongly correlates with the recent work of Eduku and colleagues, who similarly reported that both blood transfusion history (p = 0.019) and hospital admission (p < 0.001) were significantly associated with the infection. However, the risk profile in our cohort was more focused than that observed in their study. While their study again reported significant associations with a history of STIs (p = 0.007) and hepatitis B vaccination status (p < 0.001), these factors did not reach statistical significance in our multivariable analysis [44]. Similar associations between medical procedures and HBV status has been documented in the Bono East Region, where previously previous history of blood transfusions (AOR = 6.82; 95% CI: 2.53–8.34; p < 0.001), sharing sharp objects (AOR = 3.24; 95% CI: 2.98–5.71), and unsafe medical practices were independent correlate of infection [62].

Also, patterns have been observed in studies across West Africa, where prior surgery or hospitalization remains a near-significant factor, highlighting the ongoing challenge of infection control in resource-limited clinical settings [53, 63]. The fact that this variable remained prominent in the multivariable model underscores the need for continued vigilance in blood safety and the sterilization of medical equipment in regional facilities [64]. Several factors that were significant in the bivariable analysis did not retain independent associations after multivariable adjustment. These included history of blood transfusion (p = 0.404), maternal age (p = 0.305), and religious affiliation (p = 0.293), indicating that their initial associations were likely confounded by household structure or broader clinical exposures. While religious affiliation showed a significant association with HBV infection in the bivariate analysis (p = 0.004), this relationship did not persist after multivariable adjustment (p = 0.293). This suggests that religious affiliation does not function as an independent determinant of HBV status in this population. Rather, the initial association was likely confounded by other socio-demographic or household-level variables. In this context, religious identity may serve as a proxy for cultural practices or community norms, but it lacks independent predictive value once structural factors, such as spousal marital arrangements, are accounted for. This finding aligns with similar antenatal studies in sub-Saharan Africa, in which initially significant cultural markers often lose statistical significance when adjusted for more direct behavioral and clinical risk factors [58]. The adjusted null effect in the present analysis supports interpreting religion as a contextual rather than a determining factor.

Methodologically, the reduction in statistically significant correlates following multivariable Firth modelling is expected. This refinement reflects the model’s ability to adjust for confounding and provide more stable, penalized estimates for low-frequency outcomes. The fact that only one factor remained independently associated suggests that several crude associations were likely confounded and driven by sparse-cell inflation, reinforcing the appropriateness of the modeling strategy used. Overall, the shift from multiple crude associations to fewer independent correlates is consistent with several antenatal HBV studies across Ghana and sub-Saharan Africa, where adjusted models frequently identify fewer significant factors than bivariate analyses, reflecting the impact of multivariable control on overlapping socio-demographic and behavioral variables [52, 53, 58]. This convergence supports the interpretive validity of the current findings.

Recommendations and public health implications

Health facilities in the Jirapa Municipality should strengthen hepatitis B prevention within antenatal care by ensuring consistent screening, monitoring, and follow-up of HBsAg-positive pregnant women. Incorporating maternal viral load assessment and HBeAg risk stratification where feasible could help identify mothers at higher risk of vertical transmission and guide targeted clinical management. Antenatal services should also promote partner testing and family-based counseling, particularly within polygamous marital settings where household transmission risk may be elevated. The study recommends adopting a “Triple-Linkage” model. This model does not solely prioritize immediate medication, but rather establishes a proactive care continuum: first, by ensuring immediate neonatal vaccination for the infant; second, by providing partner counseling to reduce horizontal spread; and third, by enrolling the mother into a structured hepatology follow-up program for transition to antiviral therapy post-weaning when clinically indicated, this could strengthen prevention strategies and help interrupt transmission pathways sustaining hepatitis B endemicity in the municipality.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study provides facility-based evidence on hepatitis B virus seroprevalence among pregnant women in the Upper West Region of Ghana, a setting with limited published data. The high antenatal care (ANC) coverage in the region (98.1%) enhances the representativeness of the study population. Data were collected within routine ANC services using standardized procedures and laboratory testing, improving measurement consistency. The application of Firth’s penalized logistic regression strengthened the analysis by addressing sparse data and the relatively low-frequency outcome.

However, several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference and the temporal interpretation of associations. Some exposure variables relied on self-reported information and may be subject to recall or social desirability bias. The relatively small number of HBV-positive cases resulted in wide confidence intervals for certain estimates. In addition, viral load and hepatitis B e-antigen testing were unavailable, limiting further clinical characterization of infection status. Finally, data on infant HBV status and birth-dose vaccination were not available, preventing a comprehensive assessment of vertical transmission risk in this population.

Conclusion

Hepatitis B remains an important public health concern in the Jirapa Municipality, with a maternal seroprevalence of 10.6% (95% CI: 7.0–15.3%). This prevalence exceeds reported global and continental averages, suggesting the persistence of localized high-endemicity clusters in northern Ghana. The observed association between HBV infection and spousal marital structure highlights the potential role of household dynamics in sustaining transmission. These findings indicate that reliance on individual antenatal screening alone may be insufficient and support the need for more comprehensive family-centered prevention strategies. Strengthening prevention efforts through universal birth-dose vaccination and integrated antenatal screening, including the proposed “Triple-Linkage” model, may reduce vertical and household transmission and support progress toward the WHO 2030 hepatitis B elimination targets in Ghana.

Areas for future research

This study provides important insights into hepatitis B in the Jirapa Municipality, but several research gaps remain that could strengthen Ghana’s efforts toward viral hepatitis elimination. Longitudinal cohort studies are particularly needed to follow infants born to HBsAg-positive mothers. Tracking these children over time would help quantify actual mother-to-child transmission rates and evaluate the real-world effectiveness of the HBV birth-dose vaccine in the Upper West Region’s unique socio-epidemiological context. In addition, incorporating molecular epidemiology into future research could yield more detailed insights. Measuring HBV-DNA viral load and HBeAg serological status among expectant mothers would clarify maternal infectivity levels and guide the use of antiviral prophylaxis, especially in resource-limited settings. The study also found a strong link between marital structure and HBV status, suggesting a need for socio-behavioral research. Investigating how horizontal transmission occurs within polygynous households could inform culturally sensitive counseling and household-based screening strategies. Finally, health system research is critical for identifying barriers to birth-dose vaccine uptake and assessing the feasibility and cost-effectiveness of expanding the “Triple-Linkage” screening model to the CHPS (Community-based Health Planning and Services) level. Evidence in this area would support the Ghana Health Service in prioritizing high-endemic districts and allocating resources effectively, contributing to the country’s roadmap toward the 2030 viral hepatitis elimination targets.
