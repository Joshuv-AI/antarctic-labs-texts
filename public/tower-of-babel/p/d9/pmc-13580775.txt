CD90 Promotes Gastric Cancer Progression by Regulating SLC1A5‐Mediated Glutamine Metabolism Through YY1
Authors: Zihua Zhou, Siyi Liu, Chenyu Zhang, Jun Deng, Lin Liang, Yanhong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCD90 (THY1) is a cell surface glycoprotein that plays a crucial role in the occurrence and development of various malignant tumors. It affects tumor cell proliferation, metastasis, angiogenesis, stem cell characteristics, and energy metabolism. CD90 is an important biomarker for many malignant tumors and is closely related to tumor prognosis. However, the role and specific mechanisms of CD90 in glutamine metabolism in gastric cancer (GC) cells remain incompletely understood. In this study, we report that CD90 affects glutamine metabolism in GC cells through SLC1A5, ultimately promoting GC progression. Mechanistically, CD90 promotes the binding of YY1 to the SLC1A5 promoter, enhances the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter, and increases its transcriptional level. This, in turn, affects SLC1A5‐mediated glutamine metabolism and ferroptosis in GC cells, ultimately facilitating GC progression. Our results suggest that CD90 plays an important role in regulating glutamine metabolism in GC cells, providing important experimental evidence for elucidating the pathogenesis of GC.Keywords: CD90, gastric cancer, glutamine metabolism, SLC1A5

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC), including gastroesophageal junction cancer, is the fifth most common cancer worldwide and the fifth leading cause of cancer‐related deaths [1]. Globally, the incidence of GC is relatively high in regions such as Asia, Eastern Europe, Central America, and South America, particularly in East Asia [2]. In recent years, immunotherapy, including immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), has opened up new avenues for the treatment of GC [3]. However, the overall survival (OS) rate of GC patients has not significantly improved, especially the 5‐year survival rate of advanced GC patients, which is only about 10% [1]. Therefore, a deeper and broader understanding of the pathological mechanisms of GC and the search for new effective prognostic factors and molecular targets are of great significance for improving the survival rate of GC patients.

CD90, also known as thymocyte antigen 1 (THY1), is a glycoprotein anchored to glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) with a size of 25–37 kDa. It was first discovered in mouse thymocytes [4, 5, 6]. CD90 is expressed in various cell types, including T cells, neurons, endothelial cells, and cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and plays a key regulatory role in cell communication, cell‐extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions, as well as in the immune and nervous systems [7, 8]. The latest research shows that THY1 is overexpressed in cancer stem cells (CSCs) and the tumor microenvironment in various tumors, including GC, thereby enhancing the proliferation and metastatic ability of tumor cells [9, 10]. Serena Mancarella et al. found that in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA), CD90 is a downstream effector molecule of the NOTCH pathway, regulated by Notch1. Patients with higher levels of NOTCH1/HES1/THY1 expression have significantly shortened survival, indicating a more aggressive intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma phenotype [7]. In addition, Jiang et al. identified a population of CSCs with high expression of CD90 in primary gastric tumors. Research has shown that when trastuzumab is used in combination with traditional chemotherapy, it can target the reduction of this CSC population and inhibit tumor growth [11]. However, the specific molecular mechanism of CD90 in the progression of GC still needs further exploration.

As is well known, glutamine is the most abundant non‐essential amino acid in human serum. In addition to glucose, proliferating cancer cells also rely on glutamine as their main source of energy, which can provide carbon and nitrogen for biosynthesis [12, 13]. Therefore, many cancer cells undergo glutamine metabolic reprogramming, increasing their uptake to meet their rapid proliferation needs [14]. In recent years, research on specific glutamine metabolic pathways in cancer cells has been increasing [15, 16, 17]. High levels of glutamine degradation have also been found in GC tissue [18]. Inhibiting glutamine metabolism can suppress tumor growth or enhance the therapeutic effect in GC [19]. For example, Fang et al. [20] found that m5C‐methylated lncRNA NR_033928 can stabilize glutaminase (GLS) mRNA, promote glutamine metabolism reprogramming, and ultimately promote the proliferation of GC cells. Additionally, Liu et al. [21] found that RUNX3‐mediated circDYRK1A can inhibit glutamine metabolism in GC by upregulating the expression of FBXO4, thereby suppressing the in vitro malignant phenotype of GC cells. These studies further emphasize the importance of glutamine metabolism in the progression of GC.

As a hydrophilic amino acid, extracellular glutamine cannot penetrate the plasma membrane without the help of cell surface transporters (i.e., solute carrier proteins) [22, 23]. The uptake of glutamine in cancer cells is primarily mediated by the glutamine transporter families SLC1, SLC6, SLC7, and SLC38 [23]. Among these, ASCT2, encoded by SLC1A5 (solute carrier transporter 1A5), is a sodium‐dependent bidirectional amino acid transporter that primarily mediates the cellular uptake of neutral amino acids, particularly glutamine [24]. Numerous studies have shown that SLC1A5 can affect various cancers, including gastric and lung cancer, by regulating glutamine metabolism [25, 26, 27]. For example, Li et al. [28] found that CircAKT3 activates SLC1A5 expression by targeting miR‐515‐5p, thereby promoting the proliferation, survival, and glutamine metabolism of GC cells. Inhibition of SLC1A5 can hinder glutamine uptake, interfere with mTORC1 signaling, and thereby reduce the growth and viability of lung cancer cells [26]. Additionally, SLC1A5‐dependent glutamine uptake and subsequent metabolism are crucial for apoptosis and autophagy of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) cells, as well as the development of HNSCC tumors [29]. In summary, SLC1A5 plays an important role in glutamine metabolism and the progression of various malignant tumors.

In this study, we found that CD90 can affect glutamine metabolism in GC cells through SLC1A5, thereby promoting GC progression. Specifically, in GC cells, CD90 enhances the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter by promoting the binding of the transcription factor YY1 to the SLC1A5 promoter, thereby increasing the transcription level of SLC1A5. This process further affects SLC1A5‐mediated glutamine metabolism and ferroptosis, ultimately promoting GC progression. The aim of this study is to elucidate the role and specific molecular mechanisms of CD90 in regulating SLC1A5 during GC progression, in order to identify new therapeutic targets, discover effective treatment strategies, and provide new pathways for the treatment of GC.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Specimen

This study collected GC tissues and their corresponding adjacent non‐cancerous tissues from 18 GC patients at the Xiangya Medical College Affiliated Cancer Hospital/Hunan Provincial Cancer Hospital of Central South University between 2024 and 2025. All specimens were collected with the patients' informed consent. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Xiangya Medical College Affiliated Cancer Hospital/Hunan Provincial Cancer Hospital of Central South University and complies with the ethical standards set forth in the Helsinki Declaration.

GC Cell Lines and Cell Culture

The GC (GC) cell lines HGC‐27, AGS, and MFC were all preserved by the Institute of Oncology, Xiangya School of Basic Medical Sciences, Central South University. These cell lines are regularly tested for mycoplasma contamination to ensure their purity. The cell culture medium was RPMI‐1640 (Procell, PM150110, Wuhan, China), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Procell, 164210‐50, Wuhan, China) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (P/S; Macgene, Beijing, China). All cells were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2.

Plasmid and Cell Transfection

In this study, the eukaryotic expression vectors used include pcDNA3.1‐3xFlag‐C, pcDNA3.1‐myc, pcDNA3.1‐3xFlag‐C‐CD90, pcDNA3.1‐3xFlag‐C‐mCD90, and pcDNA3.1‐myc‐SLC1A5. These vectors were all purchased from Changsha Youbao Biotechnology Co. Ltd. Additionally, the shRNA vectors PLKO.1‐PURO‐shCD90, PLKO.1‐PURO‐shSLC1A5, and PLKO.1‐PURO‐shYY1 were purchased from Qingke Biotechnology Co. Ltd. The transfection experiments were conducted using Neofect DNA transfection reagent (Neofect Biotech, China) and were carried out strictly in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions.

Quantitative Real‐Time Polymerase Chain Reaction (qRT‐PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRnaZol Reagent (M5101) (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the All‐in‐One Script RT Premix (with dsDNase) (Kermey Biotech, China). Quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR) was then performed on the cDNA using specific primers and 2× SYBR Green qPCR Premix (Universal) (Kermey Biotech, China), with the Bio‐Rad CFX96 qPCR system. The 2−∆∆CT method was used to calculate fold changes for relative quantification. The detailed sequences of the primers used in this study are shown in Table S1.

Western Blot Analysis

Total cellular protein was extracted using NCM RIPA Buffer lysis buffer (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China) containing protease inhibitors. Samples that require detection of SLC1A5 protein levels must be processed using the PNGase F Deglycosylation Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Beyotime, China). Western blotting analysis was performed according to the previously established procedure [29]. The specific steps are as follows: First, proteins were separated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. Next, the proteins were transferred onto a PVDF membrane. After the transfer was complete, the membrane was blocked with 5% skimmed milk. Subsequently, the membrane was incubated overnight with a specific primary antibody at 4°C, followed by incubation with a secondary antibody at 37°C for 1 h. Finally, the protein bands were visualized and photographed. Detailed information about the antibodies used can be found in Table S2.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP)

Total cellular protein was extracted using IP lysis buffer (New Cell & Molecular Biotech, China) containing protease inhibitors. Subsequently, the supernatant of the lysate was immunoprecipitated using anti‐FLAG magnetic beads or anti‐Myc magnetic beads (Biolinkin, China) pre‐bound with specific primary antibodies at 4°C and incubated overnight. On the second day, the precipitated protein mixture was washed 5–6 times with IP lysis buffer, each time for 5–7 min. After immunoprecipitation was completed, the precipitated protein mixture was used for Western blot analysis. For detailed information on antibodies, please refer to Table S2.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Tumor specimens were fixed and dehydrated, then embedded in paraffin and sectioned according to the previously described method (https://doi.org/10.3892/ijo.2018.4651). The specific steps are as follows: Tissue sections were boiled in citric acid buffer (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval. Subsequently, the sections were blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h. Primary antibody incubation was performed at 4°C, and secondary antibody incubation was carried out at 37°C, with each incubation lasting 30 min. The stained sections were then imaged and analyzed using the Saiviewer software. For detailed information on antibodies, please refer to Table S2.

Cell Viability Assay (CCK8)

Cell viability was evaluated using the CCK8 reagent (Yeasen, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The specific procedure is as follows: Cells were inoculated into a 96‐well plate and processed according to the experimental requirements. After processing, the medium in each well was replaced with 100 μL of fresh culture medium, followed by the addition of 10 μL of CCK8 solution. The 96‐well plate was then placed in a 37°C, 5% CO2 incubator and incubated for 2 h. Finally, the absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm.

EDU Cell Proliferation Assay Combined With Flow Cytometry

Cell proliferation was assessed using the BeyoClick EdU‐594 cell proliferation assay kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The specific procedure is as follows: The EdU working solution was added to the cell culture medium to achieve a final concentration of 10 μM EdU. Cells were then incubated for 2 h. Subsequently, the cells were fixed, washed, and permeabilized, followed by the addition of freshly prepared Click reaction solution. Finally, the fluorescence signal generated by EdU labeling was detected using a flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo software.

Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Experiment

Luciferase activity was measured using the Dual‐Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay (Uelandy, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The fluorescence values were detected using a multi‐functional microplate reader. Renilla luciferase was used as an internal reference. The ratio of the relative light unit (RLU) value measured by Firefly luciferase to the RLU value measured by Renilla luciferase was calculated. This ratio was used to compare the activation levels of target reporter genes between different samples.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Assay Followed by Quantitative PCR (ChIP‐qPCR)

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) experiments were performed using the Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Detection Kit (Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The specific steps are as follows: First, crosslink the target protein with the corresponding genomic DNA. Next, use SDS Lysis Buffer containing 1 mM PMSF to thoroughly lyse the cells. Then, subject the sample to ultrasound treatment and perform chromatin immunoprecipitation. Afterward, remove the cross‐linking between the protein and genomic DNA. Finally, purify the DNA and perform qRT‐PCR analysis (as described above). The fold enrichment of the target gene relative to the negative control was calculated using the 2−∆∆CT method. For detailed information on primers, please refer to Table S1. Detailed information about the antibodies used can be found in Table S2.

Nude Mouse Experiment

All mouse experiments were conducted according to the approved protocol of the Department of Experimental Zoology at Xiangya Medical College, Central South University. Mice were euthanized before the length or width of the tumor exceeded 200 mm or the volume reached 2000 mm3, or before the mice showed any signs of pain. GC MFC cells (1 × 106) transfected with Ctrl or M‐CD90 were subcutaneously injected into female BALB/c nude mice at a volume of 100 μL. After tumor formation, mice were intraperitoneally injected with the SLC1A5 inhibitor V9302 or a control solution, and tumor size measurements were recorded on designated dates. The formula for calculating tumor volume is: length × width2 × (π/6). After the experiment, the tumor tissue was removed from the mice, washed with pre‐cooled PBS, and frozen in liquid nitrogen for preservation.

Bioinformatics and Statistical Analysis

Bioinformatics analysis was conducted using various web‐based bioinformatics tools. The following public databases were searched: JASPAR (https://jaspar.elixir.no/), hTFtarget (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/hTFtarget/), and MotifMap (https://motifmap.ics.uci.edu/). The number of clones formed in the cloning experiment was quantified using ImageJ software, and statistical charts were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. In statistical analysis, Student's t‐test was used to evaluate the degree of difference between the mean values of any two groups of samples; one‐way ANOVA was used to analyze the degree of difference between the means of three or more sample groups; one‐way ANOVA was used to analyze the impact of two factors on the observed variable. The intracellular concentration detection of glutamine, glutamate, and cysteine (Cys) is normalized by cell number, and sample collection, processing, and detection are all completed within a unified time to minimize operational fluctuations. Each experiment was repeated at least three times. Statistical significance was considered when p < 0.05, indicated as follows: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

Results

CD90 Promotes GC Progression by Regulating Glutamine Metabolism in GC Cells

First, we overexpressed or knocked down CD90 in human GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells, respectively, and investigated the effect of CD90 on GC cell proliferation using the EDU cell proliferation assay combined with flow cytometry. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the proliferation rate of GC cells overexpressing CD90 was significantly increased, whereas the proliferation rate of GC cells with CD90 knockdown was decreased (Figure S1A–D). This indicates that CD90 promotes the proliferation of GC cells. Additionally, overexpression of CD90 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells, as well as CCK8 cell proliferation assays, indicated that at the same time point, the OD value of the CD90 overexpression group was higher than that of the control group (Figure S1E). Furthermore, the results of the clone formation experiment also showed that, compared with the control group, CD90 significantly enhanced the clone formation ability of GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells (Figure S1F,G). These results confirm the promoting effect of CD90 on the malignant progression of GC cells.

Furthermore, to further investigate the specific mechanism by which CD90 promotes the malignant progression of GC cells, we overexpressed CD90 in HGC‐27 cells and performed RNA‐seq and energy metabolomics assays. The RNA‐seq results showed that, compared with the control group, genes related to glutamine metabolism were significantly upregulated in GC HGC‐27 cells overexpressing CD90 (Figure 1A). The results of the energy metabolomics assays suggest that, compared with the control group, the glutamine content was upregulated in GC HGC‐27 cells overexpressing CD90 (Figure 1B). Therefore, we speculate that CD90 may affect glutamine metabolism in GC HGC‐27 cells. To test this hypothesis, we overexpressed or knocked down CD90 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells, respectively, and used a colorimetric assay kit to detect the extracellular and intracellular glutamine content in GC cells. The results showed that, compared with the control group, overexpression of CD90 significantly reduced the extracellular glutamine content while significantly increasing the intracellular glutamine content; conversely, CD90 knockdown resulted in opposite outcomes (Figure 1C,D, Figure S2A,B). Additionally, we measured the extracellular glutamate content of HGC‐27 and AGS cells. The results showed that the extracellular glutamate content of the CD90 overexpression group was significantly higher than that of the control group, whereas the extracellular glutamate content of the CD90 knockdown group was significantly lower than that of the control group (Figure 1E, Figure S2C). These results indicate that CD90 regulates glutamine metabolism by affecting the transport of glutamine and glutamate in GC cells.

Subsequently, we overexpressed CD90 in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells and treated the cells with three glutamine metabolic pathway inhibitors at a concentration of 10 μg/mL, including the SLC1A5 inhibitor GPNA (L‐γ‐glutamyl‐p‐nitroaniline), the GLS2 inhibitor Compound 968, and the GOT1 inhibitor GOT1 Inhibitor‐1, whereas setting up a control group treated with DMSO. Changes in GC cell proliferation were detected using the EDU cell proliferation assay combined with flow cytometry. The results showed that the proliferation rate of cells overexpressing CD90 and treated with DMSO (CD90+ DMSO group) was significantly higher than that of the control group (NC+ DMSO group). The proliferation rates of cells in the CD90+ GPNA group, CD90+ Compound 968 group, and CD90+ GOT1 Inhibitor‐1 group were lower than that in the CD90+ DMSO group, with GPNA showing the most significant effect (Figure 1F,G). This indicates that glutamine metabolism inhibitors can partially reverse the promoting effect of CD90 on the proliferation of GC cells. Next, we overexpressed CD90 in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells and treated the cells with DMSO or GPNA. The results of the CCK8 cell proliferation experiment showed that, at the same time point, the OD value of the CD90+ DMSO group was higher than that of the NC+ DMSO group. However, the OD value of the CD90+ GPNA group decreased compared to the CD90+ DMSO group (Figure 1H,I). This further confirms that CD90 affects the proliferation of GC cells through glutamine metabolism. Additionally, the results of the clone formation experiment also showed that the glutamine metabolism inhibitor GPNA can partially reverse the promoting effect of CD90 on the clone formation ability of GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells (Figure 1J,K). In summary, CD90 regulates glutamine metabolism by affecting the transport of glutamine and glutamate in GC cells, thereby promoting the progression of GC.

CD90 Inhibits Ferroptosis Through Glutamine Metabolism and Promotes the Progression of GC

To further investigate how CD90 affects glutamine metabolism in GC cells and promotes GC progression, we analyzed the RNA‐seq results of HGC‐27 cells overexpressing CD90. The results suggested that CD90 may be associated with ferroptosis in GC cells (Figure 2A). The accumulation of iron and lipid peroxidation are two important indicators of ferroptosis, characterized by iron dependence, lipid peroxidation‐driven membrane disruption, and can be inhibited by GPX4 [30]. To verify whether CD90 affects ferroptosis in GC cells, we overexpressed or knocked down CD90 in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells, respectively, and treated them with two classic small molecule ferroptosis inducers—Erastin or RSL3. We then analyzed the relative content of Fe2+ in GC cells using an Fe2+ detection kit. The results showed that, compared with the control group, overexpression of CD90 significantly inhibited the increase in Fe2+ levels in GC cells induced by Erastin or RSL3, whereas knockdown of CD90 significantly promoted this increase (Figure 2B, Figure S3A,B). Meanwhile, we used the fluorescent probe C11‐BODIPY to detect the production of lipid reactive oxygen species (ROS) in GC cells through flow cytometry. The results showed that, compared with the control group, overexpression of CD90 inhibited the increase in lipid ROS levels in GC cells, whereas knockdown of CD90 promoted this increase (Figure 2C,D, Figure S3C,D).

Additionally, malondialdehyde (MDA) is one of the most important end products of lipid peroxidation [31]. Therefore, we treated HGC‐27 and AGS cells overexpressing or knocking down CD90 with Erastin or RSL3 and detected the relative content of MDA in GC cells using an MDA detection kit. The results showed that, compared with the control group, overexpression of CD90 significantly inhibited the increase in MDA levels induced by Erastin or RSL3 in GC cells, whereas knockdown of CD90 significantly promoted this increase (Figure 2E, Figure S3E–G).

Cysteine (Cys) is an essential precursor for the synthesis of glutathione (GSH), which serves as a source of GPX4 reducing equivalents and can detoxify phospholipid hydroperoxides into non‐toxic phospholipids, thereby preventing ferroptosis [32]. Therefore, we also overexpressed or knocked down CD90 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells and detected the levels of Cys and GSH in GC cells using Cys and GSH detection kits. The results showed that, compared with the control group, overexpression of CD90 significantly upregulated the levels of Cys and GSH in GC cells, whereas knockdown of CD90 significantly reduced these levels (Figure 2F,G, Figure S3H,I). These results confirm that overexpression of CD90 can inhibit ferroptosis induced by Erastin or RSL3 in GC cells, whereas knockdown of CD90 can enhance this induction effect. This suggests that CD90 is a negative regulatory molecule for ferroptosis in GC cells.

Previous results have confirmed that CD90 promotes GC progression by regulating glutamine metabolism and negatively regulates ferroptosis in GC cells. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that CD90 may inhibit ferroptosis by affecting glutamine metabolism, thereby promoting GC progression. To verify this hypothesis, we conducted the following experiments: First, we overexpressed CD90 in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells and treated the cells with GPNA, Compound 968 or GOT1 Inhibitor‐1, whereas setting up a control group treated with DMSO. We then analyzed the relative content of Fe2+ in GC cells using an Fe2+ detection kit. The results showed that after DMSO treatment, the level of Fe2+ in GC cells was reduced in the CD90 overexpression group compared to the control group. However, after treatment with the three glutamine metabolism inhibitors, there was no significant difference in Fe2+ levels compared to the CD90+ DMSO group (Figure 2H). This indicates that CD90 affects Fe2+ levels in GC cells through glutamine metabolism. Next, we overexpressed CD90 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells and treated the cells with DMSO or the three glutamine metabolism inhibitors, respectively. We detected the relative content of MDA in GC cells using an MDA detection kit. The results showed that after DMSO treatment, the MDA level in GC cells was reduced in the CD90+ DMSO group compared to the control group. However, after treatment with the three glutamine metabolism inhibitors, there was no significant difference in MDA levels compared to the CD90+ DMSO group (Figure 2I). Additionally, we overexpressed CD90 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells and treated the cells with DMSO or GPNA. We detected the production of lipid ROS in GC cells by flow cytometry using the fluorescent probe C11‐BODIPY. The results showed that the lipid ROS levels in the CD90+ DMSO group were lower than those in the control group, whereas the inhibitory effect of CD90 on lipid ROS levels in GC cells was partially restored after GPNA treatment (Figure 2J,K, Figure S4A,B). These experimental results indicate that CD90 affects ferroptosis in GC cells through glutamine metabolism.

Subsequently, to further confirm that CD90 affects GC progression by inhibiting ferroptosis in GC cells, we overexpressed CD90 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells and treated the cells with DMSO or the ferroptosis inducer RSL3, respectively. We then used the EDU cell proliferation assay combined with flow cytometry to investigate whether CD90 affects the proliferation of GC cells through ferroptosis. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the proliferation rate of GC cells overexpressing CD90 and treated with DMSO was significantly increased. However, after overexpression of CD90 and treatment of GC cells with RSL3, the promoting effect of CD90 on cell proliferation was partially reversed (Figure 2L,M, Figure S5A,B). This indicates that CD90 promotes the proliferation of GC cells by inhibiting ferroptosis.

In summary, CD90 inhibits ferroptosis in GC cells and promotes GC progression by regulating glutamine metabolism.

CD90 Affects Glutamine Metabolism and Ferroptosis Mediated by SLC1A5 in GC Cells

Through the above experiments, we found that CD90 regulates the transport of glutamine and glutamate, affects glutamine metabolism, inhibits ferroptosis in GC cells, and promotes GC progression. To further investigate the specific mechanism by which CD90 affects glutamine and glutamate transport, we overexpressed or knocked down CD90 in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells and detected the mRNA levels of glutamine transporters SLC38A2, SLC7A5, SLC7A11, SLC1A5, SLC3A2, SN1, SN2, and LAT2 using RT‐qPCR. The results showed that, compared with the control group, overexpression of CD90 significantly upregulated the mRNA level of SLC1A5, whereas knockdown of CD90 significantly downregulated the mRNA level of SLC1A5 (Figure 3A,B, Figures S6G,H and S7A). Subsequently, we further investigated the regulation of SLC1A5 protein levels by CD90 using Western blot experiments. The results indicate that CD90 positively regulates the protein level of SLC1A5 (Figure 3C,D, Figure S7B). Additionally, to verify the effect of CD90 on SLC1A5 mRNA levels in vivo, we injected mouse‐derived GC MFC cells transfected with M‐Ctrl or M‐CD90 subcutaneously into nude mice. After tumor formation, the tumor tissue was removed, and the mRNA level of SLC1A5 was analyzed using RT‐qPCR. The results showed that CD90 significantly upregulated the mRNA levels of SLC1A5 in tumor tissues (Figure 3E). The cysteine/glutamate antiporter SLC7A11 mediates the uptake of extracellular Cys in exchange for glutamate, playing an important role in glutamine metabolism. Therefore, to further confirm that CD90 does not mediate glutamine metabolism in gastric cancer cells through SLC7A11, we further used Western blot to investigate the regulation of CD90 on SLC7A11 protein levels. The results indicate that CD90 does not regulate the protein level of SLC7A11 (Figure S7C). SLC1A5 protein usually undergoes glycosylation modification in cells, so we overexpressed SLC1A5 in HGC‐27 cells. After obtaining the protein extract, we treated the sample with PNGase F deglycosylation enzyme. The results showed that after treatment with PNGase F, the band of SLC1A5 shifted from about 60 kDa to about 45 kDa. This result indicates that SLC1A5 protein does indeed exhibit high glycosylation (Figure S7D). At the same time, we also detected the effect of SLC1A5 on CD90 expression by overexpressing or knocking down SLC1A5 in GC cells using RT‐qPCR and Western blot experiments. It was found that SLC1A5 did not affect the mRNA and protein levels of CD90 (Figure S8A–F).

Furthermore, to further confirm that CD90 affects the transport of glutamine and glutamate in GC cells through SLC1A5, thereby regulating glutamine metabolism, we overexpressed CD90 in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells or knocked down SLC1A5 while overexpressing CD90. Subsequently, we measured the extracellular and intracellular glutamine content in HGC‐27 and AGS cells using colorimetric assay kits. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the extracellular glutamine content of HGC‐27 and AGS cells overexpressing CD90 was significantly reduced, whereas the intracellular glutamine content was significantly increased. However, compared with the group overexpressing only CD90, knocking down SLC1A5 while overexpressing CD90 significantly upregulated the extracellular glutamine content of GC cells while reducing the intracellular glutamine content (Figure 3F,G, Figure S9A,B). Additionally, we measured the extracellular glutamate content of HGC‐27 and AGS cells. The results showed that the extracellular glutamate content of HGC‐27 and AGS cells overexpressing CD90 was significantly higher than that in the control group, whereas the extracellular glutamate content of the group overexpressing CD90 and knocking down SLC1A5 was significantly lower than that of the group overexpressing CD90 (Figure 3H). These results confirm that CD90 affects the transport of glutamine and glutamate in HGC‐27 and AGS cells through SLC1A5, thereby regulating glutamine metabolism.

To investigate whether CD90 affects ferroptosis in GC cells through SLC1A5, we overexpressed CD90 in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells or knocked down SLC1A5 while overexpressing CD90, and treated the cells with Erastin or RSL3. Subsequently, we analyzed the relative contents of Fe2+ and MDA in GC cells using Fe2+ and MDA detection kits. The results showed that, compared with the control group, overexpression of CD90 significantly inhibited the increase in Fe2+ and MDA levels in GC cells induced by Erastin or RSL3. However, after knocking down SLC1A5 while overexpressing CD90, the levels of Fe2+ and MDA in GC cells were significantly upregulated compared to the group overexpressing CD90 alone, and the inhibitory effect of CD90 on Fe2+ and MDA content in GC cells was significantly antagonized (Figure 3I,J, Figure S10A,B). Meanwhile, we used the fluorescent probe C11‐BODIPY to detect the production of lipid ROS in GC cells through flow cytometry. The results showed that, compared with the control group, overexpression of CD90 significantly inhibited the level of lipid ROS in GC cells, whereas overexpression of CD90 and knockdown of SLC1A5 simultaneously significantly increased the level of lipid ROS in GC cells compared to the group overexpressing CD90 alone (Figure 3K,L, Figure S10C,D). Additionally, we overexpressed CD90 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells or knocked down SLC1A5 while overexpressing CD90. We detected the levels of Cys and GSH in GC cells using Cys and GSH detection kits. The results showed that, compared with the control group, overexpression of CD90 significantly upregulated the levels of Cys and GSH in GC cells, and after knocking down SLC1A5 while overexpressing CD90, the levels of Cys and GSH were significantly downregulated compared to the group overexpressing CD90 alone (Figure 3M,N). The upregulation of Cys and GSH levels in GC cells by CD90 was antagonized. These experimental results indicate that CD90 inhibits ferroptosis in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells through SLC1A5.

In summary, CD90 affects glutamine metabolism and ferroptosis in GC cells through the glutamine/glutamate transporter SLC1A5.

CD90 Affects the Transcriptional Activity of SLC1A5 Promoter Through YY1, Thereby Promoting the Transcriptional Level of SLC1A5

Previous experiments have confirmed that CD90 positively regulates the expression of SLC1A5, thereby affecting glutamine metabolism and ferroptosis in GC cells. To further investigate the specific molecular mechanism by which CD90 affects SLC1A5 expression, we conducted the following experiments: First, we overexpressed the Flag‐CD90 plasmid in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells and investigated the interaction between CD90 and SLC1A5 through Co‐IP experiments. The results showed that there was no direct interaction between CD90 and SLC1A5 (Figure 4A). Since CD90 can upregulate the mRNA level of SLC1A5 and affect its transcription, we hypothesized that CD90 may regulate the expression of SLC1A5 by affecting the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter. To test this hypothesis, we constructed the SLC1A5 dual‐luciferase reporter gene vector (PGL3‐Basic‐SLC1A5 promoter). After overexpressing or knocking down CD90 and transfecting the SLC1A5 dual‐luciferase reporter gene vector into HGC‐27 and AGS cells for 48 h, we detected the activity of luciferase using a dual‐luciferase reporter gene detection kit. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the luciferase activity of SLC1A5 was significantly enhanced in GC cells overexpressing CD90, and SLC1A5 luciferase activity was significantly reduced in GC cells knocked down CD90 (Figure 4B, Figure S11). This indicates that CD90 enhances the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter. Subsequently, we utilized the hTFtarget (http://bioinfo.life.hust.edu.cn/hTFtarget/) and MotifMap (https://motifmap.ics.uci.edu/) online tools to predict transcription factors that may potentially bind to SLC1A5. By taking the intersection of these two predicted results, we found that there are six transcription factors (YY1, STAT1, USF1, FLI1, STAT3, ETS2) that have potential binding relationships with SLC1A5, with YY1 ranking high (Figure 4C).

Next, we knocked down YY1 in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells and detected the effect of YY1 on SLC1A5 expression through RT‐qPCR and Western blot experiments. The results showed that YY1 positively regulated the mRNA and protein levels of SLC1A5 (Figure 4D,E). Meanwhile, we also overexpressed or knocked down SLC1A5 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells and investigated the effect of SLC1A5 on YY1 expression using RT‐qPCR and Western blot experiments. The results showed that SLC1A5 had no significant effect on the mRNA and protein levels of YY1 (Figure 4F,G, Figure S12A). Additionally, we overexpressed the myc‐SLC1A5 or Flag‐CD90 plasmids in HGC‐27 and AGS cells, respectively. Co‐IP experiments were conducted to explore whether there were interactions between SLC1A5 and YY1, as well as between CD90 and YY1. The results showed that there was no interaction between SLC1A5 and YY1, nor between CD90 and YY1 (Figure S13A,B).

We input the upstream 2000 bp sequence of the SLC1A5 gene as the promoter sequence and set the binding confidence threshold to 85%. The results showed that there were five possible binding sites between YY1 and the SLC1A5 promoter (Table S3). To further verify whether YY1 binds to these five predicted sites, we designed corresponding primers based on the predicted binding sites and conducted ChIP‐qPCR experiments in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells. After ultrasonically fragmenting the collected gastric cancer cells, we used YY1 ChIP‐grade antibody for chromatin pull‐down, followed by RT‐PCR experiments to detect the enrichment of YY1 at the predicted sites of SLC1A5. The results showed significant enrichment of YY1 at all five predicted sites of the SLC1A5 promoter (Figure 4H), confirming the binding of YY1 to the SLC1A5 promoter. At the same time, we overexpressed or knocked down YY1 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells and transfected the SLC1A5 dual‐luciferase reporter gene vector. After 48 h, we used the dual‐luciferase reporter gene detection kit to measure luciferase activity. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the luciferase activity of SLC1A5 in gastric cancer cells with overexpression of YY1 was significantly enhanced, whereas that in gastric cancer cells with knockdown of YY1 was significantly decreased (Figure 4I). This indicates that YY1 enhances the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter. The above results indicate that YY1 binds to the SLC1A5 promoter, upregulates its transcriptional activity, and thereby regulates the expression of SLC1A5.

Since YY1 is a transcription factor, we also investigated whether YY1 regulates the expression of CD90. First, we knocked down YY1 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells and used RT‐qPCR and Western blot experiments to detect the effect of YY1 on CD90 expression. The results showed that YY1 did not regulate the mRNA and protein levels of CD90 (Figure 4J,K). Meanwhile, we also found that CD90 does not regulate the mRNA and protein levels of YY1 (Figure 4L–N, Figure S12B,C). These results indicate that there is no significant regulatory relationship between YY1 and CD90. Additionally, we overexpressed CD90 or knocked down YY1 while overexpressing CD90 in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells. We then explored whether CD90 affects SLC1A5 expression through YY1 using RT‐qPCR and Western blot experiments. The results showed that overexpression of CD90 significantly upregulated the mRNA and protein expression levels of SLC1A5 compared to the control group. However, after knocking down YY1, the positive regulatory effect of CD90 on SLC1A5 was partially antagonized, indicating that CD90 affects the expression of SLC1A5 through YY1 (Figure 4O,P).

Based on the above conclusions, we hypothesized that CD90 enhances the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter by promoting the binding of YY1 to the SLC1A5 promoter, thereby upregulating the transcriptional level of SLC1A5. To verify this hypothesis, we conducted ChIP‐qPCR experiments to detect the enrichment of YY1 at the SLC1A5 predicted sites in HGC‐27 and AGS cells overexpressing CD90. After overexpressing CD90 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells, we subjected the collected CD90‐overexpressing gastric cancer cells to ultrasonic disruption and performed chromatin pull‐down using a YY1 ChIP‐grade antibody. Subsequently, we employed RT‐PCR experiments to detect the enrichment of YY1 at the predicted sites of SLC1A5. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the enrichment of YY1 at the fifth predicted site of SLC1A5 was significantly enhanced in the CD90 overexpression group (Figure 4Q). Subsequently, we overexpressed CD90 or knocked down YY1 while overexpressing CD90 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells and transfected the SLC1A5 dual‐luciferase reporter gene vector. After 48 h, we used the dual‐luciferase reporter gene detection kit to measure luciferase activity. The results showed that, compared with the control group, overexpression of CD90 significantly enhanced SLC1A5 luciferase activity. In contrast, in the group overexpressing CD90 while knocking down YY1, SLC1A5 luciferase activity was significantly reduced compared to the group overexpressing CD90 alone. This indicates that knocking down YY1 partially antagonizes the enhanced transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter by CD90 (Figure 4R). These results indicate that CD90 enhances the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter by promoting the binding of YY1 to the SLC1A5 promoter, thereby upregulating the transcriptional level of SLC1A5.

In summary, CD90 affects the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter through YY1, promoting the transcriptional level of SLC1A5.

In Vivo and In Vitro Experiments Confirm That Anti‐CD90 and SLC1A5 Inhibitors Synergistically Inhibit the Progression of GC

We conducted immunohistochemical experiments on cancer tissues from 18 GC patients. The results showed that in the cancer tissues of 18 gastric cancer patients, 12 cases had high expression of CD90, and the expression of SLC1A5 was significantly enhanced. At the same time, as a transcription factor, YY1 showed increased subcellular localization in the nucleus. This further supports our conclusion that CD90 promotes the nuclear entry of YY1, affects the binding of YY1 to the SLC1A5 promoter, and enhances the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter, upregulating its expression (Figure 5A,B). To confirm whether the synergistic effect of anti‐CD90 and SLC1A5 inhibitors inhibits GC progression, we first overexpressed CD90 or knocked down SLC1A5 while overexpressing CD90 in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells. Subsequently, we used the EDU cell proliferation assay combined with flow cytometry to detect whether CD90 affects the proliferation of GC cells through SLC1A5. The results showed that, compared with the control group, the proliferation rate of GC cells in the group overexpressing CD90 was significantly increased. However, knocking down SLC1A5 while overexpressing CD90 resulted in a significant decrease in the proliferation rate of GC cells compared to the group overexpressing CD90 alone (Figure 5C,D, Figure S14A,B). This indicates that the promoting effect of CD90 on GC cell proliferation is partially antagonized. These results indicate that CD90 regulates the proliferation of GC cells through SLC1A5.

Subsequently, to further confirm whether CD90 regulates GC cell proliferation through SLC1A5 in vivo and promotes GC progression, we subcutaneously injected MFC cells overexpressing CD90 and control cells into nude mice. On the 8th day after injection, tumors formed. Subsequently, we randomly divided the mice into three groups: IgG control group, anti‐CD90 monoclonal antibody treatment group, and anti‐CD90 monoclonal antibody combined with SLC1A5 inhibitor V9302 treatment group. Discussion

GC is a heterogeneous and refractory disease characterized by significant phenotypic diversity including multiple molecular subtypes. This phenotypic diversity leads to resistance to both conventional and immunotherapy. CD90 is a cell surface glycoprotein anchored to the cell surface via GPI and exhibits high N‐glycosylation [11, 33, 34]. CD90 is located on human chromosome 11q23.3 [35]. Zhu et al. [36] found that CD90 is upregulated in GC tissue and inhibits apoptosis of GC cells by regulating the expression of osteopontin. Additionally, CD90 can affect the biological behavior and energy metabolism of GC cells by targeting the PI3K/AKT/HIF‐1α signaling pathway [37]. However, the exact mechanisms by which CD90 contributes to GC progression still need further investigation. In this study, we first revealed that CD90 affects glutamine metabolism in GC cells through SLC1A5, thereby promoting GC progression. Specifically, in GC cells, CD90 enhances the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter by promoting the binding of the transcription factor YY1 to the SLC1A5 promoter, thereby increasing its transcription level. This process further affects SLC1A5‐mediated glutamine metabolism and ferroptosis in GC cells, ultimately promoting GC progression.

First, we conducted EDU cell proliferation experiments, CCK8 cell proliferation experiments, and clone formation experiments in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells. The results confirmed that CD90 promotes the progression of GC. To explore the specific mechanism by which CD90 promotes GC progression, we overexpressed CD90 in HGC‐27 cells and performed RNA‐seq and energy metabolomics assays. The results suggest that CD90 may affect glutamine metabolism in GC cells. Subsequently, we used glutamine and glutamate assay kits to detect intracellular and extracellular glutamine content as well as extracellular glutamate content in HGC‐27 and AGS cells. The results further confirmed that CD90 affects glutamine metabolism in GC cells. Next, we treated HGC‐27 and AGS cells with three glutamine metabolism inhibitors. The results of the EDU cell proliferation experiments showed that these inhibitors can partially reverse the promoting effect of CD90 on the proliferation ability of GC cells, with GPNA showing the most significant effect. Therefore, we further treated HGC‐27 and AGS cells with GPNA and conducted CCK8 cell proliferation and colony formation experiments. The results also confirmed that CD90 can regulate glutamine metabolism and promote the progression of GC by affecting the transport of glutamine and glutamate in GC cells.

Here, we have discovered an interesting metabolic phenomenon: activating the CD90‐SLC1A5 axis promotes glutamine uptake, but does not inhibit and may instead enhance glutamate output. This seemingly contradictory observation may reveal a complex aspect of this pathway in regulating glutamine metabolism that has not been fully recognized. We propose a hypothetical explanation: the increased influx of glutamine drives stronger glutamine catabolism, resulting in a rate of glutamate production that exceeds its consumption rate for biosynthesis (such as entering the TCA cycle and synthesizing glutathione), leading to a “relative surplus” of glutamate. In order to maintain metabolic balance, cells may dynamically adjust the output threshold of glutamate, leading to the observed changes in efflux kinetics. This possible mechanism is consistent with the previous research results of Jain et al. [38]: the consumption of major nutrients is related to the release of their by‐products, and the consumption of glutamine in NCI‐60 tumor cell lines is closely related to the release of glutamate. However, whether and how CD90 signaling directly regulates the activity of glutamate transporters, or indirectly produces effects by changing intracellular metabolite concentrations, remains to be further elucidated in the future. This also highlights the existence of fine feedback and coupling between input and output in the tumor metabolic network.

Subsequently, we further analyzed the RNA‐seq results mentioned above. The results suggest that CD90 may be associated with ferroptosis in GC cells. Ferroptosis is a novel regulatory mode of cell death that involves the accumulation of iron‐dependent lipid peroxides, ultimately leading to fatal cell damage [39]. Numerous studies have shown that ferroptosis plays an important role in GC including mediating tumor development and drug resistance [40, 41, 42]. To verify whether CD90 affects ferroptosis in GC cells, we used Fe2+, MDA, Cys, and GSH detection kits, as well as flow cytometry to analyze the intracellular Fe2+, MDA, the relative content of Cys and GSH, as well as the level of lipid ROS. The results confirmed that CD90 negatively regulates ferroptosis in GC cells. Subsequently, we overexpressed CD90 in GC cells and treated the cells with three different glutamine metabolism inhibitors. The experimental results showed that after treatment with glutamine metabolism inhibitors, the inhibitory effect of CD90 on ferroptosis in GC cells was partially restored. This indicates that CD90 affects ferroptosis in GC cells through glutamine metabolism. Additionally, we overexpressed CD90 in GC cells and treated the cells with the ferroptosis inducer RSL3. The results of the EDU cell proliferation experiments showed that the promoting effect of CD90 on cell proliferation was partially reversed. This further confirms that CD90 promotes the proliferation of GC cells by inhibiting ferroptosis. In summary, CD90 inhibits ferroptosis in GC cells by regulating glutamine metabolism, thereby promoting the progression of GC.

To elucidate the potential mechanism by which CD90 affects the transport of glutamine and glutamate, we conducted the following experiments. First, through RT‐qPCR and Western blot experiments, we found that CD90 specifically regulates the expression of SLC1A5 among various glutamine transporters including SLC38A2, SLC7A5, SLC7A11, SLC1A5, SLC3A2, SN1, SN2, and LAT2. CD90 positively regulates the expression of the glutamine transporter SLC1A5, whereas SLC1A5 does not affect the expression of CD90. Second, we overexpressed CD90 or knocked down SLC1A5 while overexpressing CD90 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells. Using Fe2+ and MDA detection kits, we further confirmed that CD90 affects the transport of glutamine and glutamate in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells through SLC1A5, thereby regulating glutamine metabolism. Additionally, the results from the Cys and GSH detection kits showed that, compared with the group overexpressing CD90 alone, overexpression of CD90 while knocking down SLC1A5 significantly downregulated the intracellular Cys and GSH content. The upregulation of Cys and GSH content in GC cells by CD90 was antagonized. In summary, the experimental results indicate that CD90 inhibits ferroptosis through SLC1A5 in GC HGC‐27 and AGS cells.

To further investigate the molecular mechanism by which CD90 regulates SLC1A5, we first conducted Co‐IP experiments. The results showed that there is no direct interaction between CD90 and SLC1A5. Given that CD90 can upregulate the mRNA level of SLC1A5, affecting its transcription, we hypothesized that CD90 may regulate the expression of SLC1A5 by affecting the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter. To verify this hypothesis, we knocked down CD90 in GC cells and simultaneously transfected the SLC1A5 dual‐luciferase reporter gene vector. The results of the dual‐luciferase reporter gene detection experiment confirmed that CD90 enhances the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter. Subsequently, we utilized the hTFtarget and MotifMap online tools to predict transcription factors that may potentially bind to SLC1A5. After taking the intersection of the predicted results from these two tools, we found that transcription factors with potential binding relationships to SLC1A5 include YY1, STAT1, USF1, FLI1, STAT3, and ETS2, with YY1 ranking high. YY1 is a transcription factor that plays a key role in the occurrence and development of various tumors [43, 44, 45, 46]. In microglia, YY1 can be lactylated at lysine 183 (K183). YY1 with high lactate can directly enhance the transcription of FGF2 and promote angiogenesis [43]. Additionally, the lactylation of YY1 can enhance the function of microglia through inflammatory genes, thereby exacerbating autoimmune uveitis [44].

Subsequently, we investigated the regulatory relationship between YY1 and SLC1A5. The results showed that YY1 positively regulates the mRNA and protein levels of SLC1A5, whereas SLC1A5 has no significant effect on the mRNA and protein levels of YY1. Meanwhile Co‐IP experimental results indicated that there is no interaction between SLC1A5 and YY1, nor between CD90 and YY1. Subsequently, we predicted five possible binding sites between YY1 and the SLC1A5 promoter using the JASPAR database and conducted ChIP‐qPCR experiments in GC cells. The results showed that YY1 was significantly enriched at all five predicted binding sites of SLC1A5, confirming the binding of YY1 to the SLC1A5 promoter. Additionally, we knocked down YY1 in HGC‐27 and AGS cells and conducted dual‐luciferase reporter gene detection experiments. The results showed that YY1 enhances the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter, thereby regulating the expression of SLC1A5. Meanwhile, RT‐qPCR and Western blot experiments confirmed that there is no significant regulatory relationship between YY1 and CD90, whereas CD90 affects the expression of SLC1A5 through YY1. To verify the hypothesis that CD90 enhances the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter by promoting the binding of YY1 to the SLC1A5 promoter, thereby upregulating the transcriptional level of SLC1A5, we conducted ChIP‐qPCR and dual‐luciferase reporter gene detection experiments. The results indicated that CD90 can enhance the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter by promoting the binding of YY1 to the SLC1A5 promoter, thereby upregulating the transcriptional level of SLC1A5. Immunohistochemical experiments on GC tissue specimens showed that the expression level of SLC1A5 was higher in GC tissues with high CD90 expression, and the two were positively correlated. Finally, the EDU cell proliferation experiment results showed that CD90 regulates the proliferation of GC cells through SLC1A5. Meanwhile, similar observations were made in nude mouse experiments, further confirming the synergistic inhibition of GC progression by anti‐CD90 and SLC1A5 inhibitors.

Yoo et al. reported a mitochondrial glutamine transporter variant, SLC1A5. This variant can mediate mitochondrial glutamine metabolism in cancer cells, and its overexpression can promote glutamine‐induced ATP production and glutathione synthesis [47]. In this study, we found that CD90 can affect glutamine metabolism in GC cells by promoting the transcription of SLC1A5. However, it remains unclear whether the SLC1A5 variant plays a role in CD90‐promoted glutamine metabolism in GC cells or if CD90 can affect the expression of SLC1A5 variants. These questions require further exploration in future studies.

In summary, this study delved into the molecular mechanism by which CD90 affects glutamine metabolism in GC cells through SLC1A5, ultimately promoting GC progression. Our research findings suggest that CD90 promotes the binding of YY1 to the SLC1A5 promoter, thereby enhancing the transcriptional activity of the SLC1A5 promoter and increasing its transcription level. This process further affects SLC1A5‐mediated glutamine metabolism and ferroptosis in GC cells, ultimately driving the progression of GC. The findings of this study not only provide important experimental evidence for clarifying the critical role of CD90 in GC but also offer new targets for GC treatment and open up new perspectives for developing effective treatment strategies (Figure 5H).
