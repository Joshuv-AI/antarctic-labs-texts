How should APOBEC3-induced resistance mutations be considered in the management of antiretroviral therapy?
Authors: Marie Gilbert, Valentine Marie Ferré, Romane Guilbaud, Marc Digumber, Gilles Peytavin, Florence Damond, Quentin Le Hingrat, Yazdan Yazdanpanah, Diane Descamps, Charlotte Charpentier, Véronique Joly, Jade Ghosn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCombined antiretroviral therapy (ART) management guided by the cell-associated HIV-DNA genotypic resistance test (GRT) is not standardized, and its interpretation may be difficult in the presence of APOBEC3-induced mutations at drug resistance-associated positions (APOMut). Our objective was to evaluate the virologic outcome of people living with HIV (PLWH) with HIV-DNA provirus carrying APOMuts when switched to or receiving an ART regimen that would theoretically be significantly impacted by those APOMut (potential APOMut-impacted treatment, PAIT).MethodsPLWH under PAIT and with a cell-associated HIV-DNA GRT containing APOMut between April 2010 and December 2023 were included. Treatment failure was defined as two consecutive plasma viral load values >50 c/mL at least 1 month apart after the switch to PAIT.ResultsWe studied 38 PLWH who received 42 distinct PAIT lines. At the time of PAIT introduction, all individuals except one were virally suppressed. Virologic failure occurred in four PLWH; all of these cases were related to either poor ART adherence or ART interruption. Two of them switch to another ART: one for galenic adaptation, and the other due to the emergence of the N155H resistance mutation in integrase. For this individual, virologic suppression was obtained with a PI-containing ART. The two others resumed virologic suppression without any ART switch.ConclusionsDespite the presence of APOMut in cell-associated HIV-DNA GRTs, virologic suppression was maintained in PLWH switching to PAIT. Virologic failures were unrelated to the presence of APOMut. These results suggest that APOMuts should not be considered in the management of ART.

Introduction

Virologically suppressed people living with HIV (PLWH) may still need antiretroviral therapy (ART) adaptation, in order to simplify therapies, lessen treatment toxicity or avoid drug-drug interactions. Immunovirological history, past treatments and cumulative drug resistance mutations (DRMs) should always be considered before switching treatments. However, a previous plasma HIV-RNA genotypic resistance test (GRT) is not always available, especially for PLWH who have been virologically suppressed for a long time. As an alternative, cell-associated HIV-DNA GRTs may be useful to guide therapeutic switches but remain barely recommended in the guidelines.1–3 Indeed, cell-associated HIV-DNA GRTs remain difficult to interpret due to several limitations: (i) peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) do not reflect the whole HIV reservoir and archived DRMs; (ii) sequencing methods have different sensitivities; and (iii) there is insufficient knowledge about the impact of Apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme catalytic polypeptide (APOBEC3)-induced DRMs (APOMuts) on ART activity.4,5

Specifically, APOBEC3 enzymes belong to a family of human proteins involved in different cellular mechanisms, including antiviral innate immunity.5 APOBEC3 is expressed in CD4T-cells and deaminates cytidine in HIV complementary DNA (cDNA) during the reverse transcription viral replication step, leading to guanosine-to-adenosine (G-to-A) mutations in the positive viral DNA strand. This process can induce hypermutation in the proviral sequence, leading to defective proviruses that are non-competent for replication due to the introduction of stop codons.6–9 APOBEC3 editing can also introduce mutations in HIV DNA at positions associated with drug resistance in defective proviruses that do not result from antiretroviral pressure selection and that do not correlate to ART efficacy.7,10–12 However, the impact of APOMuts on ART efficacy has not been examined in real-life clinical studies, and no guidelines are currently available to guide their interpretation.

Overall, the aim of this retrospective study was to evaluate the virological outcomes in PLWH carrying APOMuts detected in cell-associated HIV-DNA when switched to or receiving an ART regimen that would theoretically be significantly impacted by those DRMs.

Materials and methods

Definition of potential APOMut-impacted treatments

In this work, a potential APOMut-impacted treatment (PAIT) is defined as an ART regimen containing at least one molecule predicted to be resistant due to APOMuts found in the HIV-DNA GRT. We restricted the ART regimens regarded as ‘at-risk’ to three types: three-drug regimens containing lamivudine or emtricitabine (3TC/FTC = XTC) in the nucleos(t)ide backbone associated with an NNRTI [with at least one drug impacted by APOMut in the reverse transcriptase gene (RT)]; two-drug regimens containing XTC and an integrase strand-transfer inhibitor (INSTI) [impacted by APOMut in the RT or integrase gene (INT)]; and two-drug regimens containing NNRTI and INSTI (impacted by APOMut in the RT or INT). ART regimens containing protease inhibitors were not considered as PAIT.

HIV genotypic resistance tests

HIV-DNA GRTs were performed on PBMCs, extracted on QIAsymphony instrument (Qiagen, Courtaboeuf, France), using the consensus Sanger protocol of the Agence Nationale de Recherche sur le SIDA et les hépatites virales I Maladies Infectieuses Emergentes (ANRS I MIE) Resistance Study Group (www.hivfrenchresistance.org), and DRMs were interpreted using the ANRS resistance algorithm (version 35, April 2024). All these genotypic resistance tests were carried out as part of the patients’ routine care.

PLWH selection and data collection

This observational retrospective monocentric study was conducted at the University Hospital of Bichat Claude-Bernard, Assistance Publique—Hôpitaux de Paris, France. We included all PLWH who received PAIT between April 2010 and December 2023 with at least 6 months of previous clinical history data and two viral load tests in the follow-up after treatment initiation. Socio-demographic characteristics, a description of the PLWH’s ART therapy and biological data [CD4T-cell count, plasma viral load (pVL)] were retrieved from Nadis®, an electronic medical record designed for PLWH follow-up, with the data quality ensured by regular control and annual assessments. Participants gave written informed consent to have their data recorded in Nadis® [www.dataids.org; Fedialis Medica, CNIL number 1171457 (24 May 2006)].

Cell-associated HIV-DNA sequences were collected between January 2008 and December 2023. For inclusion availability of a PLWH, an HIV-DNA GRT was needed before PAIT initiation or during PAIT and containing APOMut. PLWH were selected if at least one HIV-DNA sequence harboured a potential APOMut on a resistance-related position to the ongoing PAIT or the PAIT initiated subsequently. In cases of multiple nonconsecutive PAIT lines, we considered all of them, omitting the periods with non-impacted ART. All PLWH with a history of DRMs on the plasma HIV-RNA genotypic resistance test impacting a PAIT before its initiation were excluded. Finally, treatment failure was defined as two consecutive pVL values >50 c/mL more than 1 month apart after switching treatment.

APOBEC3-induced hypermutation analysis

For each gene, multiple mappings were carried out between all sequences and the reference HXB2 (K03455.1) using Mafft. In all sequences presenting a degenerate base ‘R’, which can either be an A or G, at the first position of the APOBEC3 trinucleotide HIV context, we assigned an A base to take into account all potential APOMuts (custom R script). Hypermutation induced by APOBEC3 was then tested using the Los Alamos Hypermut 2.0 programme.13 The APOMuts impacting PAIT were as follows: M184I, E138K, G190E, G190S and M230I in RT sequences and G118R, E138K, G140R, G140S and R263K in INT sequences. Stop codons induced by APOBEC were assessed on any tryptophan coding sequences (TGG→TAA, TGG→TAG or TGG→TGA).

Statistical analysis

Continuous and categorical variables were summarized using the median and IQR or frequency and percentage, respectively. The Excel® package was used to set the statistical analysis.

Results

Selection of HIV DNA sequences

From January 2008 to December 2023, 4058 cell-associated HIV-DNA sequences were available in our local virological database with 2683 RT, 3045 protease (PROT) and 3309 INT sequences (Figure 1). Among them, 301 sequences (7.4%) were hypermutated on at least one gene. At least one stop codon was detected for 89% (n = 75/81) and 79% (n = 144/183) of the hypermutated RT and INT sequences, respectively. An APOMut was detected in 72 out of the 81 hypermutated RT sequences (89%) and 93 out of the 183 hypermutated INT sequences (51%). The median ratio of APOBEC3 mutation number/APOBEC3 mutation sites number was 34% (IQR = 14%) and 26% (IQR = 19%) in hypermutated RT and INT sequences, respectively. These APOMuts were associated with at least one stop codon for all the RT sequences and in 91% of the INT sequences. Of note, RT and INT sequences respectively harboured 4 (IQR = 3) and 1 (IQR = 2) stop codons in median while presenting in median 3 (IQR = 2) and 3 (IQR = 2.5) TRP codons by sequence. Overall, 112 PLWH harboured viral RT or INT sequences with APOMuts before or while receiving a PAIT. Among them, 38 PLWH were receiving a PAIT that could potentially be impacted by the APOMut detected.

Population characteristics

The population characteristics are presented in Table 1. In our study, 22 PLWH were males (58%), the majority of the PLWH (74%) originated from France or sub-Saharan Africa and 29 PLWH (76%) were in CDC-stage A or B of the disease. The median nadir of the CD4T-cell count was 240/mm³ (85–370). Twenty-five (66%) PLWH initiated their first ART in the 18 months following diagnosis of HIV infection. Only 8 out of 38 PLWH had viruses harbouring DRMs for INSTI or RT without any impact on the considered PAIT in their historical plasma HIV-RNA GRT. Cell-associated HIV-DNA genotypes were collected before the introduction of PAIT in 55% of cases and during PAIT with sustained virological control in 45% of cases. In median, there was 17 years (11–21) between HIV infection diagnosis and this genotyping test. The median duration of virological suppression before switching to PAIT was 96 months (38–139). At the time of PAIT introduction, five PLWH (13%) had less than 1 year of virological suppression, and only one had a detectable pVL (7044 c/mL, PAIT was the first-line treatment for this patient).

APOMuts in the PLWH sample

Among the 38 PLWH, 4 received two different lines of PAIT. Among the 42 lines of PAIT impacted by RT or INT hypermutations, there were 15 triple-therapy regimens containing XTC and NNRTI, 14 dual-therapy regimens containing XTC and INSTI and 13 dual-therapy regimens containing NNRTI and INSTI (Table 2). The most prevalent APOMuts were M184I (n = 21, 55%) for NRTI, M230I for NNRTI (n = 11, 29%) and E138K (n = 10, 26%) for INSTI antiretroviral classes. If these APOMuts in cell-associated HIV-DNA had been taken into account, all those PLWH would theoretically have been receiving either functional dual therapy without INSTI or PI, functional monotherapy without PI or even no functional therapy at all. PLWH follow-up and outcomes

In our population, the median PAIT duration was 22 months (13–51). Treatment was modified in nine PLWH (24%). In two cases, the decision to switch was based on the detection of APOMuts and the subsequent anticipation of the risk of virological failure, although plasma viral load was still suppressed. In one case, due to two consecutive viral blips, ART was modified to increase the barrier to resistance by switching from TDF-FTC-RPV to TDF-3TC-DOR. However, this change did not take into account the presence of an 184I APOMut in RT. In the six other cases, ART was modified for a different reason than APOMuts (i.e. drug–drug interactions, treatment, simplification, suspicion of poor adherence or side effects).

According to our definition, PAIT was successful in 90% of cases (34/38 PLWH). Only four (10%) virological failures were observed after 23–148 months: three PLWH with a sustainable pVL >200 c/mL and one with a pVL between 50 and 200 c/mL for 14 months. All PLWH with virological failure were found to have either ART interruption or poor adherence according to their low plasma drug concentration (for one) and their clinician’s interview and report (for everyone). Among these PLWH, only two underwent PAIT switches: one for galenic adaptation and the other one because of the emergence of the N155H, an INSTI-related resistance mutation on a plasma HIV-RNA GRT. For this PLWH, virological control was obtained with a boosted PI-containing ART. The remaining two PLWH continued their PAIT and obtained virological suppression again. Discussions of ART management in multidisciplinary team meetings

Out of the 38 PLWH, 21 cases were discussed in multidisciplinary team meetings, and APOMuts were not taken into account for ART management for 95% of them (20/21). Therefore, APOMuts as a risk factor for virological failure were considered for only one PLWH, leading to an ART switch despite current virological suppression.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate virological outcomes in virologically suppressed PLWH harbouring hypermutated HIV-DNA sequences with APOMuts, which may mimic archived DRMs but are often associated with defective proviruses. Among 301 hypermutated sequences identified in our database, a total of 112 PLWH had HIV-DNA sequences with APOMuts, and 38 PLWH had proviruses harbouring APOMuts potentially affecting their ongoing or upcoming PAIT. All but one were virologically suppressed at the time of PAIT introduction, with a median time of 96 months (38–139). Only four experienced virological failure, all related to ART poor adherence or treatment interruption.

In our database, approximately 10% of sequences were hypermutated, consistent with previous studies.14 For the 38 PLWH selected, the median time between HIV diagnosis and cell-associated HIV-1 DNA genotyping was 17 years, reflecting the accumulation of defective proviruses over time in the HIV reservoir of virologically suppressed patients. Most APOMut-containing sequences also harboured stop codons, supporting their defective nature, in line with previous results demonstrating that APOMuts are more frequently detected on defective proviruses than selected DRMs15 and that stop codons and APOMuts are found on the same HIV-DNA viral sequence in 98% of cases.16 Even in the absence of stop codons, HYPERMut software confirmed hypermutation in these sequences, suggesting limited clinical relevance of the APOMuts.

In long-term virologically suppressed patients, HIV DNA genotype was used either because no baseline plasma HIV-RNA GRT was available or because there was a past history of viral replication without an HIV-RNA GRT done at the moment. In our study, an HIV-DNA GRT was performed before switching to PAIT in approximately half of the cases and during PAIT in the remaining cases. Only four PLWH were documented to have treatment failure; however, these failures were related to factors other than resistance emergence. Indeed, these PLWH were poorly compliant with their ART and had long treatment withdrawal periods. Overall, virological failure was never related to the APOMuts detected. HIV-DNA GRT, though helpful as an alternative to plasma HIV-RNA GRTs in virologically suppressed PLWH, is challenging to interpret since APOMuts are frequently present in non-replicative viral forms and may not affect ART efficacy.17 Our findings support that APOMuts do not compromise PAIT since most PLWH maintained viral suppression.

Regarding ART management, multidisciplinary discussions were held for 21 PLWH of this study. The presence of stop codons helped practitioners differentiate APOMuts from archived DRMs and confidently initiate simplified regimens. In only one case, the decision to switch PAIT to a stronger ART was made because of two close consecutive pVL values of 62 c/mL, and the APOMut was taken into account when choosing the new therapy. Finally, a pVL test performed on the day of the switch showed that virological suppression had been obtained again in less than 1 month under the same PAIT. Notably, this case occurred in 2014, at a time when HIV-DNA GRTs were rarely performed and less information on APOBEC mutations was available.

Importantly, 11% and 21% of hypermutated RT and INT sequences, respectively, lacked stop codons, emphasizing the need for bioinformatic analysis to identify hypermutated sequences without stop codons. This is essential to avoid misinterpretation of APOMuts as clinically relevant DRMs, which could unnecessarily restrict therapeutic options, particularly in the modern era of treatment simplification (e.g. the use of two-drug therapies or long-acting injections). These results highlight the value of collaboration between virologists and physicians to clearly interpret cell-associated HIV-DNA GRTs.

Although this study has some limitations, as it was monocentric, retrospective, without control and included a limited number of patients, it is, to our knowledge, the most relevant cohort of PLWH with documented APOMuts receiving PAIT and with virological and clinical follow-up data available.

Overall, our study suggests that APOMuts have no impact on ART efficacy in PLWH who are virologically suppressed for a long time and should not be considered in ART management. Further prospective studies are needed to confirm these encouraging preliminary results.
