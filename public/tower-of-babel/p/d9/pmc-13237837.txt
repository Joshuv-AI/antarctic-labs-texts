Dear Editor,

The conventional biomedical model of health, rooted in 19th-century germ theory and modern pathology, has long employed a deficit-oriented framework that defines health primarily as the absence of disease. While effective in acute and specific disease contexts, this model proves increasingly inadequate for addressing complex challenges such as chronic conditions, preventive care, and the demand for holistic health management. Acknowledging these limitations, the World Health Organization (WHO) proposed in 1946 a broader definition of health as “a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being”—a conceptual advance that has nonetheless remained largely abstract in practical application.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) offers a distinct proactive paradigm, framing health as the dynamic manifestation of physiological robustness and adaptive capacity. Grounded in classical Chinese philosophy and refined through millennia of clinical observation, TCM emphasizes relational networks spanning environmental, somatic, and mental dimensions, prioritizing systemic harmony over reductionist approaches. This perspective converges remarkably with modern scientific developments, as biomedical research undergoes a parallel evolution toward function-oriented perspectives. TCM’s foundational dimensions

Human-environment harmony (天人相应)

This dimension conceptualizes health as emerging from dynamic synchronization between organisms and their environmental contexts, encompassing climatic, seasonal, and geographical adaptations. Representative mechanisms include microbial community shifts in response to seasonal variations.2 Environmental disruptions, such as artificial light exposure, can suppress nocturnal melatonin secretion, significantly delaying sleep onset and raising the risk of metabolic syndrome.3 Interventions such as time-restricted feeding windows and controlled light exposure exemplify practical applications that restore temporal harmony, thereby operationalizing this TCM principle in contemporary health practices.4

While modern environmental medicine quantifies the disruption of circadian and seasonal rhythms by urbanization and artificial light, TCM’s principle of human-environment harmony provides a proactive framework for designing lifestyle and clinical interventions (e.g., time-restricted eating and ecological exposure) aimed at resynchronizing human physiology with natural cycles.

Yin-yang dynamic equilibrium (阴阳匀平)

This principle embodies the dynamic balance between complementary physiological forces operating across multiple organizational levels. Exemplary manifestations include redox homeostasis, maintaining equilibrium between reactive oxygen species (ROS) signaling and antioxidant defense systems. Pathological imbalances, such as disrupted mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR)-AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling cascades, underlie various metabolic and inflammatory conditions. Therapeutic approaches, including metformin administration and chronotherapeutic interventions, demonstrate effective strategies for reestablishing homeostatic balance through multi-target mechanisms, reflecting the dialectical nature of this foundational concept.

While systems biology delineates the molecular feedback loops (e.g., AMPK/mTOR and PER/CRY) underlying physiological balance, TCM’s yin-yang framework conceptualizes this equilibrium as a dynamic and dialectical process, guiding therapeutic strategies that restore balance through multi-target modulation rather than isolated correction.

Body-mind integration (形神合一)

This dimension represents the sophisticated interrelationship between mental processes and physiological functions, characterized by bidirectional regulatory pathways. Scientific investigations have revealed that practices such as mindfulness meditation induce structural plasticity in prefrontal-limbic networks and consequent reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines (interleukin-6 [IL-6] and tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α]), where microbial metabolites, including short-chain fatty acids, influence neurological function and emotional states.5 Mind-body interventions such as Tai Chi and yoga demonstrate practical applications that simultaneously enhance psychological well-being and physiological resilience, embodying the TCM principle of unified mind-body functioning.

While neuroscience meticulously maps the bidirectional gut-brain axis via microbial metabolites and vagal pathways, TCM’s principle of “spirit governing form” offers a holistic clinical model for designing integrated interventions (e.g., Tai Chi and mindfulness) that synchronously regulate cognitive-emotional and physiological states.

Cardiovascular-microcirculatory coordination (气血和调)

This concept emphasizes the coordinated dynamics of circulation, encompassing both macroscopic hemodynamic forces and microscopic tissue perfusion efficiency. Representative mechanisms include endothelial shear-stress-mediated activation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) signaling and microcirculatory regulation, thereby ensuring optimal oxygen delivery.6 Clinical manifestations of dysregulation include fatigue syndromes and exertional intolerance observed in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, despite normal macroscopic hemodynamic parameters. Acupuncture interventions, particularly at specific points such as ST36, demonstrate the capacity to enhance nitric oxide bioavailability and optimize hemodynamic function, reflecting the practical application of this principle in restoring circulatory harmony.7

While modern physiology details the mechanisms of shear stress, microcirculatory flow, and oxygen extraction, TCM’s concept of cardiovascular-microcirculatory coordination emphasizes the functional outcome—ensuring adequate nutritive perfusion to all tissues—and guides therapies aimed at optimizing this macro-micro circulatory integration.

Visceral system coordination (脏腑协调)

This principle highlights the complex network of interactions among organ systems that transcend anatomical boundaries to maintain systemic homeostasis. Illustrative examples include bile acid metabolism regulated through the farnesoid X receptor (FXR) signaling pathway, facilitating gut-liver communication, and the sophisticated renal-calcium phosphate balance mediated by the fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23)-Klotho axis.8 Disruption of these coordinated networks manifests clinically as multi-organ dysfunction syndromes, where failure in one system precipitates compensatory mechanisms and eventual decompensation in others. Network-level therapeutic approaches, including glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, demonstrate the practical application of this principle by simultaneously addressing metabolic, cardiovascular, and renal pathophysiology.9

While molecular biology reveals specific inter-organ signaling pathways (e.g., gut-liver and heart-kidney axes), TCM’s framework of visceral system coordination provides a systemic map of these functional relationships, guiding a therapeutic shift from “single-organ correction” to “network homeostasis reconstruction.”

Systemic adaptive resilience (正气存内)

This dimension encompasses the organism’s comprehensive capacity to withstand stressors and maintain functional integrity through integrated adaptive mechanisms. Scientific investigations have elucidated trained immunity phenomena, in which epigenetic reprogramming enhances immune cell responsiveness, and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)-dependent pathways that support cellular repair and maintenance processes.10 Resilience deficits manifest as increased vulnerability to infections, accelerated aging phenotypes, and impaired tissue regeneration. Interventions including mindfulness practices, moderate physiological challenges, and nutritional approaches that enhance cellular stress resistance operationalize this principle by strengthening the body’s inherent adaptive capacities.

While hormesis research identifies how low-dose stressors activate specific cellular pathways (e.g., trained immunity and NAD+ pathways), TCM’s principle of systemic adaptive resilience conceptualizes this capacity as an integrated, system-wide property, advocating for strengthening it through calibrated challenges rather than mere avoidance of pathogens.

Theoretical convergence and evolutionary perspective

The dialogue between TCM and modern science reveals a remarkable convergence: where Western medicine offers deep mechanistic insights, TCM contributes time-tested clinical wisdom and a holistic lens on functional integrity. This complementarity is particularly significant when considering evolutionary timescales. While TCM’s recorded history spans two millennia, representing only a brief segment of humanity’s evolutionary journey, our core physiological makeup remains consistent with that of our ancestors. The foundational relationships articulated by TCM—including the six dimensions outlined above—thus retain profound relevance, having been shaped by environmental pressures that operated over much longer timescales than those captured by modern biomedical science.

Future perspectives

Looking ahead, the dialogue between TCM and modern science is evolving from validation to co-creation. Three key directions promise to advance this integration.

First, deepening mechanistic research could unravel TCM theories at the molecular level. Exploring concepts such as “kidney governing bones” through osteokine-nephron axes may reveal novel organ communication pathways. Similarly, investigating “digestive system governing transportation” through gut microbiome metabolites could elucidate systemic immune-nutrient interactions.

Second, technological integration offers unprecedented opportunities. Digital health platforms combining TCM diagnostics (tongue imaging and pulse analysis) with wearable sensors can create multi-scale health indices. Artificial intelligence algorithms can analyze these complex datasets to identify patterns linking lifestyle, environment, and health outcomes, enabling truly personalized prevention.

Third, we must work toward an integrated medical paradigm that combines Western medicine’s mechanistic precision with TCM’s holistic resilience. This requires innovative trial designs that test how interventions aligning with TCM principles (e.g., time-restricted eating and vagus nerve stimulation) produce enhanced effects when personalized according to TCM diagnostics. The concept of “precision health” should evolve beyond genetics to encompass dynamic systemic states.

While all three directions are crucial, integration with modern technology may offer the most immediate pathway to translating TCM’s holistic principles into objective, personalized health metrics. Digital health platforms and multi-omics profiling can operationalize TCM concepts, providing the empirical data needed to deepen theoretical research and ultimately inform the development of a truly integrated medical paradigm.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this dialogue is moving from validation to co-creation. The six-dimensional TCM framework presented here does not merely echo modern concepts of health; it provides a proactive, operational model that translates the WHO’s holistic vision of “complete physical, mental, and social well-being” into specific, actionable dimensions for preventive self-cultivation, clinical intervention, and therapeutic innovation. By deepening this integration, we can advance toward a medical paradigm fundamentally oriented toward cultivating lasting health and systemic resilience.
