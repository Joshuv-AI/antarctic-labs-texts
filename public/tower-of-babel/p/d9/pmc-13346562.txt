Switching tumor-derived extracellular vesicles off and on via targeted proteolysis to shift toward immunogenic phenotypes
Authors: Yeongji Jang, Byeongmin Park, Jiwoong Choi, Do Young Jin, Eun Hye Kim, Hochung Jang, Jae-Hyeon Lee, Eunbyeol Ko, Dongwon Shin, Kyungsu Kim, Woohyeong Lee, Ansoo Lee, Myung Chul Lee, In-Cheol Sun, Hong Yeol Yoon, Sangmin Lee, Sun Hwa Kim, Jooho Park, Kwangmeyung Kim, Jun-Seob Kim, Yoosoo Yang, Man Kyu Shim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite compelling evidence that tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (TEVs) exhibit either pro- or antitumorigenic phenotypes, pharmacological efforts have focused primarily on their indiscriminate suppression. Here, we propose a strategy of “switching TEVs off and on” to redirect them toward an immunogenic phenotype. Designed as a nanoproteolysis-targeting chimera (Nano-PROTAC) for TEV reprogramming, EVOTAC is composed of tripartite building blocks that integrate a PROTAC and a photosensitizer via a cancer biomarker-responsive cleavable linker and spontaneously self-assemble into supramolecular nanostructures. Upon biomarker-guided activation preferentially in tumors over normal tissues, EVOTAC initially eliminates TEVs by selectively degrading intracellular proteins involved in extracellular vesicle (EV) biogenesis. Subsequent localized laser irradiation reactivates EV generation, prompting tumor cells to predominantly produce immunogenic TEVs in response to photodynamic therapy (PDT). TEVs generated through this switching-off-and-on strategy independently exert pleiotropic effects by inhibiting tumor growth, migration, and metastasis while increasing mature dendritic cells and cytotoxic T lymphocytes in lymphoid organs and tumor tissues. This TEV-toggling process, therefore, significantly enhances both innate and adaptive immune responses to photoimmunotherapy, which leads to a complete regression of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and prevents metastasis and recurrence. Our study highlights the potential of this therapeutic approach for precise TEV modulation and encourages further exploration, adding new breadth to the growing list of EV-targeting cancer immunotherapy concepts.Subject terms: Drug delivery, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are lipid bilayer-enclosed membrane vesicles produced by most mammalian cells and released into the extracellular microenvironment.1,2 These vesicles participate in intercellular communication by shuttling cargo such as nucleic acids, proteins, and lipids from parental to recipient cells.3,4 In particular, tumor-derived extracellular vesicles (TEVs) play pivotal roles in tumor cell growth, migration, and invasion, thereby contributing to tumor progression and the formation of premetastatic niches.5,6 Moreover, TEVs suppress antitumor immune responses mediated by cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) through direct interactions between programmed cell death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)/programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) while also indirectly impairing effector cells by remodeling the tumor microenvironment (TME).7–9 As TEVs are being increasingly recognized as therapeutic targets, most current strategies focus on halting their biogenesis or eliminating EVs released from tumor cells to neutralize their malignant properties.10–14 However, a growing body of literature provides compelling evidence supporting the existence of antitumorigenic TEVs in addition to those with an oncogenic phenotype.15 EVs released from both human and mouse tumor cells transfer shared tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) to dendritic cells (DCs) for CTL cross-priming.16 A similar phenomenon has been observed clinically, where TEVs isolated from 11 melanoma patients induced the differentiation and expansion of tumor-specific CTLs, even in individuals with poorly immunogenic tumors.17 Furthermore, under specific conditions, such as exposure to immunogenic stimuli, tumor cells release EVs that exert potent antitumor immunity and can function as cancer vaccines.18–20 Collectively, these findings suggest that precisely modulating TEVs to adopt an immunogenic phenotype would be more advantageous than indiscriminate inhibition.

Here, we present a TEV-controlling nanoproteolysis-targeting chimera (Nano-PROTAC; hereafter EVOTAC) to implement a strategy for switching TEVs off and on, thereby reprogramming them toward an immunogenic phenotype. This nanoarchitecture is a supramolecular system that self-assembles through intermolecular interactions among conjugates composed of a PROTAC targeting cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2), a cathepsin B (Cat B)-specific cleavable linker and a photosensitizer (Fig. 1a). Unlike conventional drug delivery systems that rely on carrier materials (e.g., polymeric nanoparticles, micelles, liposomes and inorganic nanoparticles), this formulation has the potential to address long-standing technical and industrial challenges, including (i) low drug-loading capacity (<10%), (ii) adverse events arising from premature drug leakage or an excess of carrier materials and (iii) difficulties in process optimization for quality control (QC) and scalable production, factors that collectively hinder the clinical translation of nanomedicines".21

Various combination strategies have been developed to counteract COX-2 overexpression during photodynamic therapy (PDT).22,23 Tumoral upregulation of COX-2 and its downstream molecule, prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), promotes immune evasion by modulating the abundance and functionality of multiple immune cell populations.24 These signaling pathways further drive intrinsic immune resistance in human tumors through the constitutive expression of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1).25 In contrast, the present work focuses on the recently emerging role of this axis in suppressing EV biogenesis associated with ATP-binding cassette subfamily A member 3 (ABCA3) depletion.12 To effectively regulate the production of intracellular TEVs that continuously maintain homeostasis, we leveraged a PROTAC system to achieve sustained target degradation via iterative pharmacology.26 By integrating targeted protein degradation (TPD) with PDT within a coordinated supramolecular nanomedicine, this study presents the first proof-of-concept implementation for preferentially biasing TEVs toward immunogenic phenotypes.

The TEV toggling process begins with substantial accumulation in tumor regions (Fig. 1b). Upon activation by an enzymatic biomarker, the PROTAC initially removes TEVs through the TPD of COX-2, a key regulator of EV biogenesis.12,27 Subsequent laser irradiation stimulates tumor cells to reactivate EV generation, preferentially producing antitumorigenic phenotypes in response to PDT-induced immunogenic stimuli. This TEV reprogramming improves the outcomes of photoimmunotherapy, which achieves a complete pathological response in a model of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) and confers adaptive immune responses that suppress metastasis and recurrence. These cascading events are selectively triggered in target cells via biomarker-specific activation and localized photoirradiation, preventing potential off-target effects from unintended EV modulation.

Results

Design, preparation, and characterization of EVOTAC

EVOTAC was constructed through the self-assembly of tripartite conjugates, each rationally designed to implement the TEV off-and-on switching strategy. (i) The PROTAC comprises the clinically approved COX-2-selective inhibitor indomethacin,28,29 a single lysine (K) residue as a short carbon chain linker30,31 and the VHL-binding peptide ALAPYIP.32–34 (ii) Verteporfin (VPF), an FDA-approved photosensitizer marketed as Visudyne®, was utilized as the PDT agent.35–37 (iii) The PROTAC and VPF were covalently linked via the Cat B-cleavable substrate peptide KRR.38 The detailed synthetic scheme and characterization data are provided in Supplementary Figs. 1–6.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to investigate the driving forces underlying the self-assembly of the conjugates into EVOTAC. Molecules that were initially distributed randomly within a water box progressively coalesced into a single cluster over time (Supplementary Fig. 7). This aggregation was accompanied by a steady decline in Lennard–Jones potential energy over time, indicating that spontaneous self-assembly promoted progressive structural stabilization (Supplementary Fig. 8). Notably, diverse hydrophobic contacts, including 82 alkyl, 46 hydrogen and 12 π–orbital bonds between the VPF and PROTAC moieties, played key roles in maintaining structural integrity (Fig. 1c).

Self-assembled EVOTAC exhibited a spherical morphology with a hydrodynamic diameter of 101.26 ± 6.39 nm (Fig. 1d). There colloidal stability was confirmed in mouse serum, where particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) remained stable over a 3-day period (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 9). The UV–vis spectrum of EVOTAC revealed characteristic absorption peaks corresponding to indomethacin and VPF, while its FL emission peak at 670 nm closely resembled that of VPF (Fig. 1f, g and Supplementary Fig. 10). Using 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF), we verified the photodynamic activity of EVOTAC, which generated reactive oxygen species (ROS) at levels comparable to those of VPF under laser irradiation (Fig. 1h).

Finally, target biomarker-specific cleavage was assessed following incubation with various enzymes. EVOTAC underwent rapid cleavage into a PROTAC and lysine-conjugated VPF (K-VPF) upon incubation with Cat B (Fig. 1i). In marked contrast, no cleavage was detected when Cat B was co-incubated with its irreversible inhibitor, Z-Phe-Ala fluoromethyl ketone (Z-FA-FMK). Comparable results were observed after incubation with other enzymes (Supplementary Fig. 11). Even under acidic lysosomal conditions, no apparent nanoparticle disassembly or chemical degradation was evident (Supplementary Fig. 12). When incubated with various proteins, such as immunoglobulin G (IgG), complement component 3b (C3b) and apolipoprotein A-1 (ApoA1), the adsorption behavior of EVOTAC was comparable to that of hyaluronic acid (HA) and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles, highlighting the potential to reproduce the in vivo biodistribution of conventional nanoparticulate drug delivery systems despite its supramolecular formulation without additional carrier materials or surface modifications (Supplementary Fig. 13).39,40 Taken together, these findings confirm that EVOTAC selectively releases PROTAC via tumor biomarker-specific cleavage and promotes ROS generation upon photoirradiation.

Proof of concept for switching TEVs off and on with EVOTAC

Cellular internalization was investigated in 4T1 cells, a murine mammary carcinoma model representing a highly aggressive stage IV subtype of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC).41,42 and markedly overexpresses Cat B.43 EVOTAC uptake increased in a time-dependent manner during incubation and reached a plateau at 12 h posttreatment, similar to the behavior of VPF (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 14).

EVOTAC downregulated ABCA3 expression via COX-2 degradation (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 15).27,44 In stark contrast, no significant changes in protein expression were observed following pretreatment with inhibitors targeting key processes in EVOTAC-mediated proteolysis, such as the Cat B inhibitor CA-074-Me, the NEDD8-activating enzyme inhibitor pevonedistat and the proteasome inhibitors epoxomicin and MG132.45 Notably, tumor cells exhibited a progressive decrease in EV release because of TPD (Fig. 2c). These findings indicate that TPD by EVOTAC involves binding between COX-2 and VHL, neddylation and ubiquitin transfer following enzymatic cleavage by the target biomarker, ultimately depleting ABCA3 to reset TEVs. This selective PROTAC activity, controlled by Cat B expression, prevents unintended EV modulation in off-target normal cells that rely on EV-mediated intercellular communication to maintain tissue homeostasis.46–49

Next, we assessed photoactivity and immunogenic cell stress under culture conditions. Considering that at approximately 70% viability, tumor cells significantly increase the stress-induced release of immunogenic substances and EVs, the following experiments were conducted under fixed conditions within this viability range (Supplementary Fig. 16).50,51 Upon photoirradiation (+L), 4T1 cells treated with EVOTAC or VPF generated comparable levels of ROS (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Fig. 17). Intracellular ROS generation induced endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress-mediated immunogenic cell death (ICD), thereby triggering the increased expression of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), including surface calreticulin (CRT) exposure and extracellular release of high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) and heat shock protein 70 (HSP70; Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 18).52–55

We further monitored the number of EVs released from TNBC cells before and after photoirradiation using nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Following laser irradiation 6 h after EVOTAC treatment, TEV release increased to approximately sevenfold relative to the preirradiation level through the upregulation of ABCA3, indicating that PDT can reactivate EV biogenesis in tumor cells (Fig. 2f; Supplementary Fig. 19a, b). In contrast, VPF-treated cells showed no initial decrease in TEV release, followed by a subsequent increase in EV generation postirradiation (Fig. 2g, Supplementary Fig. 19c). As shown in Fig. 2h, changes in TEV production after each treatment correlated directly with the expression of EV markers (e.g., ALIX, TSG101, CD63 and CD9). Since TEV release from cells treated with either EVOTAC (+L) or VPF (+L) plateaued at nearly three times the basal level, we hypothesized that the quantity of immunogenic TEVs and their ratio to the protumorigenic phenotype would be greater in the EVOTAC (+L) group, which replenishes EVs from a lower baseline by switching the TEVs off and on.

These findings were corroborated in vivo in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice, where TEVs derived from naive tumor cells accelerated disease progression, whereas those from EVOTAC (+L)-treated cells delayed tumor growth and significantly increased the number of mature DCs and CTLs in the spleen, tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLNs), and primary tumors, indicating robust innate and adaptive antitumor immunity with vaccine-like effects (Fig. 2i–k; Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21).56,57

Proteogenomic profiling to identify TEV phenotypes

To directly determine TEV toggling toward an immunogenic phenotype, proteogenomic analyses were performed. As EVOTAC reduced EV biogenesis, the release of TEVs carrying protein markers associated with tumor cell motility [phosphorylated Akt1 (p-Akt1), fibronectin, and MMP-9]58 and immunosuppression (PD-L1 and CD47)59,60 from an equivalent number of TNBC cells substantially decreased (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 22). Notably, compared with the competitive inhibition of COX-2 by indomethacin, EVOTAC-induced TPD resulted in more pronounced suppression of TEV release.61 Coculture under these conditions markedly inhibited tumor cell migration and T-cell exhaustion (Fig. 3b, c and Supplementary Fig. 23). In contrast, upon laser irradiation, EVOTAC-treated cells began secreting TEVs enriched in several HMGBs and HSPs, with these immunogenic markers being more abundant relative to the VPF (+L) group (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Fig. 24).18 Importantly, there was no significant difference in tumor cell migration following incubation with EVs isolated from tumor cells treated with EVOTAC (-L or +L), suggesting that PDT preferentially replenished antitumorigenic rather than oncogenic TEVs (Fig. 3e). These findings were further corroborated by increased DC maturation and antigen presentation, as well as enhanced T-cell proliferation and activation, following coculture with TEVs from the EVOTAC (+L) group (Fig. 3f–j and Supplementary Fig. 25). Additionally, mixing TEVs from untreated cells with EVOTAC (+L)-derived TEVs neutralized their antitumor properties in both tumor and immune cells, highlighting the importance of eliminating tumorigenic phenotypes (Supplementary Fig. 26).

The miRNA sequencing analysis uncovered significant differences among the TEVs derived from the laser-only, EVOTAC (+L) and VPF (+L) groups. Each group exhibited unique patterns of miRNA content (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 27). Notably, the levels of several miRNAs associated with oncogenesis (miR-21a-5a, miR-24-3p, miR-25-3p, miR-102-5p and miR-501-3p)62–68 and chemoresistance (miR-423-5p)69 were substantially reduced in the EVOTAC (+L) and VPF (+L) groups. Meanwhile, the well-known antitumorigenic LET-7 family (let-7a-5p, let-7f-5p, let-7i-5p, and miR98-5p),70 along with other antitumorigenic miRNAs (e.g., miR-7b-5p),71 was highly abundant in TEVs from the photoactivated groups (Supplementary Fig. 28; Supplementary Tables 1 and 2).

Beyond this shared trend, EVOTAC (+L) imparted a distinctive miRNA signature in the secreted TEVs. Compared with those in the VPF (+L) group, significant levels of miR-690, miR-6239, miR-6238, miR-3535, and miR-6538 were detected in the TEVs (Fig. 3l and Supplementary Table 3). Except for miR-690, most of these miRNAs remain poorly characterized. Nevertheless, gene ontology analysis of the predicted targets indicated preferential enrichment of pathways linked to antitumor immunity and cell death regulation (Supplementary Fig. 29). Furthermore, a cross-interaction network was constructed to identify shared target genes among these miRNAs. Common target genes between mmu-miR-6238 and mmu-miR-690, including Efnb2, Gnas, and Ikzf2, are involved in DC maturation and T-cell activation, respectively.72–74 Additionally, the negative regulator of GPCR signaling Rgs18 was predicted to be a common target gene of mmu-miR-6238 and mmu-miR-3535, potentially contributing to the limitation of protumor signaling downstream of G protein activation.75 Acer3 and Mdm4 were anticipated to be targets of mmu-miR-690 and mmu-miR-3535, both of which are known to exert antitumor effects through apoptosis induction (Fig. 3m).76,77 Notably, the specific enrichment of miRNAs shown to be involved in DC maturation and T-cell activation provides proteogenomic validation for our earlier phenotypic observations (Figs. 3f–j). Collectively, these results strongly support that EVOTAC (+L) induces a phenotypic switch in TEVs, enriching them with immunogenic miRNAs.

EVOTAC-mediated photoimmunotherapy in the TNBC orthotopic model

To determine the optimal timing for photoirradiation, the tumor accumulation kinetics and biodistribution of EVOTAC were investigated following intravenous injection in an orthotopic TNBC model. Near-infrared fluorescence (NIRF) intensity within the tumors, delineated by bioluminescence (BL) imaging, increased over 3 h and gradually decreased thereafter (Fig. 4a). Although VPF exhibited a similar intratumoral accumulation pattern, its fluorescence intensity was approximately half that of EVOTAC (Fig. 4b). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 30a, compared with VPF, EVOTAC exhibited significantly prolonged in vivo circulation and improved pharmacokinetic (PK) parameters. Time-dependent ex vivo NIRF imaging of major organs and tumor tissues revealed greater tumor accumulation of EVOTAC than VPF at each time point, which is attributable to the EPR effect (Supplementary Fig. 30b). Notably, EVOTAC facilitated the time-dependent elimination of TEVs within tumor tissues, with their levels decreasing to less than half the initial level at 6 h after administration (Fig. 4c). This effect was driven by substantial depletion of tumoral ABCA3 via targeted COX-2 protein degradation (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 31).

The therapeutic efficacy of EVOTAC-mediated photoimmunotherapy was evaluated in mice bearing orthotopic TNBC tumors, with laser irradiation performed 3 or 6 h after injection [EVOTAC (+L3h) or (+L6h)]. The PDT efficiency was anticipated to be maximal when tumor regions were irradiated at 3 h postadministration, corresponding to peak EVOTAC accumulation. However, consistent with the in vitro results, TEV release plateaued in tumor tissues irradiated 3 or 6 h after EVOTAC injection (Supplementary Fig. 32). These results suggest that immunogenic TEVs are enriched in the tumor tissues of mice treated with EVOTAC (+L6h), as TEVs repopulate from a lower baseline following PDT-induced immunogenic stimulation. Accordingly, TEVs isolated from the EVOTAC (+L6h) group promoted more pronounced DC maturation of splenocytes in coculture than those from the EVOTAC (+L3h) group (Fig. 4e and Supplementary Fig. 33).

The exceptional immunogenicity of TEVs derived from the EVOTAC (+L6h) group was attributed to their marked phenotypic differences from naive EVs isolated from tumor tissues in the saline group (Fig. 4f). Several miRNAs with known tumor-suppressive functions (miR-129-5p, miR-144-3p, miR-205-3p and miR-451a)78–82 were significantly enriched in TEVs following EVOTAC (+L6h) treatment, whereas the expression levels of representative oncogenic miRNAs (miR-182-3p, miR-27b-5p, miR-181a-1-3p and mmu-miR-181b-2-3p)83–86 were reduced (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Table 4).

Consequently, all mice in the EVOTAC (+L6h) group achieved complete regression (CR), in stark contrast to those in the EVOTAC (+L3h) group, in which none of the mice exhibited CR (Fig. 4h). BL imaging allowed the direct visualization of TNBC progression in each group, with EVOTAC (+L6h)-treated mice displaying significantly delayed tumor growth among all groups (Fig. 4i and Supplementary Fig. 34). Histological analyses revealed considerable structural abnormalities and apoptosis at tumor sites following EVOTAC (+L6h) treatment (Fig. 4j and Supplementary Fig. 35). These tumor tissues also exhibited a marked reduction in the expression of Ki67, a well-established predictive and prognostic marker in breast cancer.87 Importantly, blockade of EV biogenesis with GW4869 markedly reduced the therapeutic efficacy of EVOTAC (+L6h), providing direct evidence for the therapeutic contribution of PDT-induced immunogenic TEVs (Supplementary Fig. 36).88 We further assessed the therapeutic efficacy of EVOTAC in a highly immunogenic CT26 colon tumor model and observed significant effects, including complete tumor regression along with increased DAMP release and immune cell activation (Supplementary Fig. 37).89

Throughout the study, no treatment-related body weight loss or observable damage to normal tissues was observed in any group (Supplementary Figs. 38 and 39). Despite substantial retention of EVOTAC in off-target tissues, no significant COX-2 degradation was apparent from the early postadministration phase through the clearance period, whereas selective TPD occurred in tumor regions via the target biomarker Cat B (Supplementary Fig. 40). Accordingly, EVOTAC-treated mice showed no significant alterations in biochemical parameters or complete blood counts (CBCs), which are commonly monitored in clinical settings to detect adverse effects related to off-target COX-2 inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 41).90 Collectively, these results underscore the robust antitumor efficacy and safety of photoimmunotherapy combined with TEV redirection via EVOTAC.

Inhibition of premetastatic niche formation

We next investigated whether EVOTAC could inhibit metastasis by eliminating oncogenic TEVs that play a major role in establishing premetastatic niches.91 Metastatic potential was evaluated through invasion assays, which revealed a significant reduction in the number of migrated TNBC cells following the addition of TEVs isolated from EVOTAC-treated tumor cells (Fig. 5a, b). These findings were supported by a pronounced decrease in the expression levels of proteins associated with tumor cell motility in cocultured TNBC cells (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 42).

Thirty after primary tumor inoculation, orthotopic TNBC mice exhibited substantial pulmonary metastases (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 43). In contrast, treatment according to the protocol shown in Fig. 4 resulted in substantial regression of lung metastases in the EVOTAC (+L6h) group (Fig. 5e). Considering that TEVs from the primary lesion play a major role in premetastatic niche formation via fusion with target cells following direct organ-specific colonization, the metastasis-inhibitory effect can be attributed to the mitigation of oncogenic TEVs with a propensity for site-specific metastasis, together with an increase in antitumorigenic phenotypes that facilitate antitumor immunity.91,92 Consistent with these observations, EVOTAC (+L6h) significantly reduced tumor nodules in the lungs (Fig. 5f). These trends were also reflected in the histological analyses, which demonstrated a marked decrease in pulmonary metastatic lesions after EVOTAC (+L6h) treatment, whereas extensive metastatic burden remained evident in the other groups (Fig. 5g). Moreover, pulmonary tissues from EVOTAC (+L6h)-treated mice displayed substantially lower expression of MMP-9, fibronectin and p-Akt1 (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 44). These results demonstrate that combined TEV reprogramming and PDT mediated by EVOTAC effectively prevents premetastatic niche formation and suppresses distant metastasis in the context of primary TNBC.

Immune responses to EVOTAC-mediated photoimmunotherapy

In vivo antitumor immunity was assessed by measuring DAMP levels, immune cell infiltration and cytokines in tumor tissues (Fig. 6a). A significantly greater fraction of CRT-expressing tumor cells was observed in the EVOTAC (+L6h) group, together with enhanced extracellular release of HMGB1 and HSP70 (Fig. 6b, c and Supplementary Fig. 45). Compared with those in the EVOTAC (+L3h) group, the numbers of intratumoral mature DCs and CTLs were greater in the EVOTAC (+L6h) group, whereas the number of regulatory T cells (Tregs) was lower (Fig. 6d–f). Additionally, EVOTAC (+L6h) treatment markedly increased the levels of proinflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IFN-γ, while reducing the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-10 (IL-10) relative to all other groups (Fig. 6g). Although EVOTAC (+L6h) alone achieved CR of the primary tumor, combination with anti-PD-L1 antibody therapy further enhanced CTL recruitment, suggesting increased potential as a clinical intervention for highly aggressive human cancers (Supplementary Fig. 46).93,94 These data demonstrate that EVOTAC photoimmunotherapy reshapes the TME into an immune-responsive milieu, enhancing antitumor immunity by recruiting effector cells and limiting immunosuppressive populations.

One month after EVOTAC (+L6h)-induced CR, mice were subjected to tumor rechallenge to evaluate adaptive immune memory, which resulted in full tumor rejection (Fig. 6h).95 These mice also showed a significant increase in effector/memory T cells (Tem), a hallmark of in vivo immunological memory that confers long-term protection against previously encountered tumors, as evidenced by the striking downregulation of CD62L expression in splenic CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6i).96 Moreover, serum cytokine levels were considerably higher in the CR mice than in the naive mice (Fig. 6j). Taken together, these results validate the ability of EVOTAC to induce both innate and adaptive immune responses, further underscoring the advantage of biasing TEVs toward an immunogenic phenotype to increase photoimmunotherapy efficacy rather than indiscriminately disrupting TEVs or suppressing their biogenesis.

Discussion

This study reports the first implementation of a methodology that specifically redirects them toward an antitumorigenic phenotype via a supramolecular system integrating TPD and PDT. The incorporated PROTAC and photosensitizer are structurally balanced to fine-tune intermolecular forces, thereby promoting their spontaneous self-assembly into a stable cluster.97 By eliminating the need for carrier materials, EVOTAC achieves a high drug-loading content of 80.59%, consisting of 50.57% PROTAC and 30.02% photosensitizer. This supramolecular formulation can address fundamental, practical and technical challenges faced by conventional nanomedicines (e.g., difficulties in manufacturing process optimization, low drug-loading capacity, and potential toxicity).98,99 EVOTAC also controls the always-on bioactivity of the PROTAC by masking the VHL-recruiting domain with a biomarker-cleavable linker, thereby enabling site-selective TPD at target tissues. In a highly aggressive and refractory TNBC model, EVOTAC-mediated TEV toggling combined with photoimmunotherapy led to complete regression of primary, metastatic, and recurrent tumors, highlighting an innovative therapeutic strategy that extends beyond existing approaches focused solely on TEV suppression.10–14

Our study offers key evidence to guide the future development of TEV-targeting therapeutic strategies. Even under conditions that increase EV biogenesis, such as immunogenic stimuli, the overall TEV output from tumor cells tends to plateau (Fig. 2). Given that stress-induced EV secretion is known to exhibit saturable behavior because of cell number, endosomal sorting capacity and membrane trafficking machinery, the observed plateau in TEV production reflects an intrinsic cellular ceiling for EV biogenesis under fixed culture conditions rather than a limitation of photoactivity.3,100–102 This suggests that the production of sufficiently immunogenic TEVs requires an initial clearing step to remove preexisting basal vesicles, which would otherwise dilute the immunogenic population within the capped intracellular EV pool. Consistent with these findings, EVOTAC successfully biased TEVs toward tumor-specific antigenicity and immunostimulatory activity upon PDT after TPD-mediated elimination of basal TEVs with malignant phenotypes. Furthermore, we characterized previously unexplored phenotypic features of immunogenic TEVs through proteogenomic analysis (Fig. 3). TEVs generated following PDT with VPF or EVOTAC exhibited reduced levels of miRNAs associated with oncogenesis and chemoresistance. Notably, enrichment of miRNAs involved in antitumor immunity, regulation of cell death, and immune cell activation was observed exclusively in the EVOTAC-treated group. This direct phenotypic reprogramming of TEVs was demonstrated in both in vitro and in vivo conditions.

The responses of tumor cells to PDT were evaluated by assessing ICD-related DAMP expression and the generation of antitumorigenic TEVs. Because TEV “switching on” ultimately arises after the detection of immunogenic stimuli, the primary role of PDT is to induce tumor cell ICD, which in turn drives the production of EVs with immunogenic phenotypes. Interestingly, photoirradiated tumor cells exhibited significantly upregulated ABCA3 levels without alterations in COX-2 expression. These results provide two important pieces of evidence: (i) PDT does not affect the PROTAC activity of the system, and (ii) although previous studies have demonstrated that PDT induces COX-2 overexpression, TPD counteracts the photoirradiation-induced overexpression of COX-2, while tumor cells restore EV release through the substantial upregulation of ABCA3.103 Given that TEVs carry a broad spectrum of tumor-associated antigens and neoantigens, the EVOTAC-mediated toggling process has a strong ability to selectively enrich anti-neoplastic TEVs, thereby overcoming key limitations underlying the poor efficacy of EV-based immunotherapeutic approaches.104,105 Nevertheless, a limitation of the present study is that direct targeting of ABCA3 was not feasible because no suitable ligand is currently available. Therefore, regulation of ABCA3 was achieved indirectly through TPD of its upstream regulator, COX-2. Future development of PROTACs incorporating ligands capable of directly engaging ABCA3 may provide a more efficient and selective strategy for modulating this pathway and further improve therapeutic outcomes.

Notably, we identified the independent effects of EVOTAC-generated TEVs in suppressing tumor growth, migration and metastasis through the activation of antitumor immune responses (Fig. 2i–k). These TEVs inherently retain the antigenic repertoire of their parental tumor cells and therefore hold significant potential as personalized cancer vaccines. Unlike conventional one-size-fits-all vaccination strategies, EVOTAC induces the generation of patient-specific TEVs that present a unique repertoire of tumor-associated antigens and neoantigens, thereby enabling the development of customized cancer vaccines capable of targeting the heterogeneous antigenic landscape of individual tumors. A major challenge associated with TEV-based cancer vaccines is that these vesicles also inherit immunosuppressive molecules from their parental tumor cells, such as PD-L1, which can attenuate antitumor immune responses. However, a substantial fraction of TEVs carrying such immunosuppressive factors may be selectively depleted during the toggling process, as EVOTAC eliminates intrinsic TEVs through TPD. Therefore, by carefully monitoring batch-to-batch variability and establishing standardized protocols for the high-purity, large-scale production of immunogenic TEVs, EVOTAC may provide a promising platform for the development of personalized cancer vaccines.

In summary, we leveraged PROTAC technology to effectively regulate TEVs by inducing sustained degradation of target proteins via its recyclable mechanism (event-driven pharmacology), in contrast to conventional occupancy-driven inhibitors. Beyond existing TPD research, which has largely focused on identifying oncogenic proteins and optimizing chemical structures, our findings demonstrate the ability of PROTACs to influence physiological and pathological processes. Importantly, the successful regulation of a biological process through PROTAC-mediated protein degradation, despite the inability of the corresponding small-molecule ligand to modulate the same process through conformational blockade, represents a significant conceptual and technical advance. These findings underscore the distinct advantages of event-driven pharmacology and expand the therapeutic potential of ligands previously considered biologically ineffective. With its versatile design, which can also accommodate other moieties to target proteins involved in signal transduction pathways, this study establishes a generalizable platform and highlights the broad therapeutic potential of PROTACs in oncology.

Materials and methods

Preparation of EVOTAC

EVOTAC was generated through the self-assembly of chemically synthesized conjugates. Briefly, KRRPIYPALAK(Fmoc) peptide (100 mg, 0.063 mmol) was reacted with verteporfin (43 mg, 0.063 mmol) in anhydrous N,N-dimethylformamide for 2 h at room temperature in the presence of 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide (EDC; 49 mg, 0.32 mmol), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS; 36 mg, 0.32 mmol) and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (22 mg, 0.17 mmol) as coupling reagents. Following purification through a C18 cartridge to eliminate residual reactants and catalysts, the Fmoc protecting group was removed by incubating in piperidine/DMF. The resulting intermediate, VPF-KRRPIYPALAK (89 mg, 0.043 mmol), was subsequently conjugated to indomethacin (15.4 mg, 0.043 mmol) in the presence of EDC (42 mg, 0.22 mmol) and NHS (25 mg, 0.22 mmol) for 3 h at room temperature. The resulting VPF-KRRPIYPALAK-indomethacin conjugate was purified by liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS). Product identity and purity were verified by LC–MS throughout each synthetic step, and the final compound was further characterized by 1H NMR spectroscopy.

Characterization of EVOTAC

Hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index were measured in mouse serum using a Zetasizer. Morphological features were visualized by transmission electron microscopy following dispersion in distilled water. UV-vis absorption and fluorescence emission spectrum were acquired using a UV spectrometer and a fluorescence spectrophotometer, respectively. The ROS-generating capability of EVOTAC was assessed using a bleaching assay. Briefly, equimolar concentrations of EVOTAC or VPF were mixed with 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran and exposed to a NIR laser. ROS production was subsequently quantified by monitoring changes in DPBF absorbance using UV spectrophotometry. Enzyme-responsive cleavage was evaluated by LC–MS. EVOTAC was incubated at 37 °C in MES buffer containing either Cat B, D, E, L, MMP-9, caspase-3 (10 μg) or Cat B together with the irreversible inhibitor Z-FA-FMK.

Cellular uptake

Murine 4T1 mammary carcinoma cells (American Type Culture Collection) were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with fetal bovine serum and penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Following incubation with EVOTAC or VPF (300 nM), 4T1 cells were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole and analyzed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) to visualize intracellular fluorescence. Quantitative analysis of cellular uptake was performed by flow cytometry, and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was determined.

In Vitro TPD, ICD, and TEV toggling

To evaluate the targeted degradation of proteins associated with EV biogenesis, 4T1 cells treated with EVOTAC for 12 h were stained with an anti-COX-2 antibody, an anti-ABCA3 antibody, and a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody. For mechanistic studies, cells were pretreated for 6 h with the CA-074-ME, pevonedistat, epoxomicin, or MG132 before EVOTAC treatment.

For analysis of intracellular ROS generation, cells treated with EVOTAC or VPF were further incubated with the diacetyldichlorofluorescein probe for 30 min, followed by photoirradiation at 100 mW for 10 min. For cytotoxicity analysis, cells were incubated with culture medium containing 10% Cell Counting Kit-8, followed by measurement of cell viability using a UV‒vis microplate reader at 450 nm. These cells were also counterstained with an anti-CRT antibody and analyzed by flow cytometry. Western blot analysis was performed to assess HMGB1 and HSP70 in the culture medium.

The number of TEVs released from 4T1 cells treated with EVOTAC or VPF before and after laser irradiation was quantified using NTA. Cells were cultured in serum-free RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 1× GlutaMAX, and the culture medium was collected. TEVs were isolated through four sequential centrifugation steps: 10 min at 300 g, 10 min at 2000 g, 30 min at 10,000 g and 2 h at 120,000 g. Before the final centrifugation, samples were passed through 10 K MWCO filters. TEV pellets were resuspended in PBS for NTA and subjected to western blotting to assess EV markers using antibodies against ALIX, TSG101, CD63, CD9, fibronectin, MMP-9, p-Akt1, CD47, PD-L1, HMGB1, HMGB2, HSPA13, HSP70, HSP60, and HSP90B1. The TEVs were normalized either to their particle number (Fig. 4e) or to isolation from an equivalent cell density (Fig. 2h and Fig. 3), depending on the experimental purpose.

Coculture study

For migration assays, 4T1 cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in six-well culture dishes. The cells were cocultured with TEVs isolated from 4T1 cells that had been treated with indomethacin, VPF, or EVOTAC in the presence or absence of photoirradiation. An artificial wound was then created using a sterile 20-μl pipette tip, and images were captured with a microscope after 24 h of incubation.

Animal procedures were carried out following the ethical standards and institutional regulations established by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Korea Institute of Science and Technology (IACUC No. 2023-013). Bone marrow cells collected from mice were passed through a cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. Red blood cells were removed using lysis buffer, and the remaining cells were cultured in bone marrow-derived dendritic cell (BMDC) differentiation medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor, 20 ng/mL IL-4, and 0.1% β-mercaptoethanol. T cells were isolated from the spleen via CD8+ Microbeads. Following coculture with TEVs under the same conditions described above, multiparameter staining was carried out to define the following cell populations: mature DCs (CD11c+CD86+CD40+), proliferating T cells (CD8+Ki67+), and activated T cells (CD8+CD25+ or CD8+CD44+).

miRNA library construction and sequencing

miRNA libraries were constructed using the Small RNA-seq Library Preparation Kit for Illumina V3 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For small RNA isolated from exosomes, a 10-fold diluted 3’ adapter was ligated at 16 °C overnight, followed by depletion of excess 3’ adapter. Subsequently, a 10-fold diluted and denatured 5’ adapter was ligated, and first-strand cDNA synthesis was performed. Indexing PCR (24 cycles) was carried out using the Small RNA Adapter Module 96 Index for Illumina, and the amplified libraries were purified with AFTMag NGS DNA Clean Beads. After purification, double-size selection was performed at 1.3× and 0.9× bead-to-sample ratios. The final libraries were validated with a Fragment Analyzer (Cat. No. DNF-915, Agilent) and quantitative PCR (qPCR). Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeqX Plus platform using paired-end 150 bp (PE150) reads.

miRNA data processing, differential expression, and beta diversity analysis

Raw sequencing data quality was assessed using FastQC (v0.12.1) and MultiQC (v1.3.1).106,107 The 3’ adapter sequences were trimmed using Cutadapt (v5.1), and only the forward reads from paired-end sequencing were used for downstream miRNA analysis.108 The adapter-trimmed reads were aligned to mature Mus musculus miRNA sequences from the miRBase database (v22.1) using Bowtie (v1.3.1).109 Read counts per mature miRNA were obtained from coordinate-sorted BAM files using SAMtools idxstats (v1.22.1).110 This approach was validated to yield consistent results with dedicated small RNA quantification tools. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 (v1.48.2) package in R (v4.5.1).111 miRNAs with an adjusted p value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change | > 2 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Beta diversity was quantified using Bray‒Curtis dissimilarity in R. Differences in community composition among the three groups were tested using permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) in the vegan package (adonis2, 999 permutations; seed = 123).112

miRNA target gene and pathway prediction networks

Predicted target genes of the selected miRNAs were obtained from the miRDB database.113 All predicted target genes were included in the network analysis without applying score-based filtering. The miRNA–mRNA interaction network was visualized using Cytoscape software (v3.10.4).114 To perform pathway enrichment analysis, the putative target genes were analyzed using Metascape (v3.5.20250701).115 Pathway enrichment was conducted with a minimum overlap of three genes, a p-value cutoff of 0.01, and a minimum enrichment factor of 1.5. Enriched Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes were identified and automatically clustered into nonredundant functional groups based on kappa similarity (kappa score > 0.3). The network resulting from the pathway analysis was visualized using Cytoscape software (v3.10.4).

Biodistribution and in vivo TEV toggling

Orthotopic TNBC models were established by inoculating 2 × 105 luciferase-expressing 4T1 cells into the fourth mammary fat pad of BALB/c nude mice. Tumor growth was monitored using the following formula: V (mm3) = longest diameter × (shortest diameter)2 × 0.53. Once tumors reached a volume greater than 50 mm3, EVOTAC or free VPF was administered intravenously at an equivalent dose of 5 mg/kg VPF content. Blood samples for pharmacokinetic analysis were collected, centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 15 min, and fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader. NIRF images were acquired to assess tissue distribution and tumor accumulation using an IVIS system. Major organs and tumor tissues were harvested from mice at the indicated time points and analyzed for COX-2 expression by western blotting. Hematological analyses were performed by KNOTUS Inc. BL and NIRF imaging was performed using an IVIS system. D-luciferin (3 mg per mouse) was administered intraperitoneally prior to BL imaging. NIRF signals within tumor regions were quantified with Living Image® software. For histological analysis, tumor tissues collected 6 h posttreatment were counterstained using anti-COX-2 or anti-ABCA3 antibodies. TEVs were isolated from tumor tissues by removing single cells in RPMI 1640 medium containing DNase I and collagenase D using a tissue dissociator, followed by four sequential centrifugation steps: 10 min at 300 g, 10 min at 2000 g, 30 min at 16,500 g, and 2 h at 120,000 g.116

Therapeutic efficacy and antitumor immune responses

Mice bearing orthotopic TNBC tumors were assigned to six treatment groups: (G1) saline, (G2) laser only, (G3) VPF (+L), (G4) EVOTAC (-L), (G5) EVOTAC (+L3h) and (G6) EVOTAC (+L6h). Once the tumor volume exceeded 50 mm3, EVOTAC or free VPF was administered intravenously at an equivalent dose corresponding to 5 mg/kg VPF content, followed by laser irradiation of the tumor region for 15 min with power of 200 mW. Treatments were performed twice at one-week intervals. For comparison of in vivo effects, TEVs (100 μg of protein) derived from untreated or EVOTAC (+L)-treated 4T1 cells were intravenously administered to orthotopic TNBC mouse models, followed by monitoring of tumor growth and immune cell populations in TDLNs and the spleen.

Tumor tissues were collected on days 1 and 3 following the second treatment for the analysis of DAMPs and immune cell populations, respectively. Single-cell suspensions were prepared using a Tumor Dissociation Kit, followed by FcBlock incubation for 5 min and multiparameter staining to analyze the following cells: CRT-positive tumor cells (CD45-CRT+), mature DCs (CD11c+; CD40+; CD86+), CTLs (CD45+CD3+CD8+) and Tregs (CD3+CD4+CD25+). In parallel, HMGB1, HSP70, IL-10, IFN-γ, and TNF-α levels were analyzed in tumor supernatants. Colon tumor models were established by injecting CT26 cells (1 × 106) into the left flank of mice, followed by treatment and analysis of tumor growth, DAMP expression, and immune cell populations according to the same protocol described above.

To monitor pulmonary metastasis, BL imaging was performed using an IVIS system on days 30 and 37 after the second treatment. Lung tissues from orthotopic TNBC mice were then collected and stained with H&E, anti-p-Akt1, anti-MMP-9 or anti-fibronectin antibodies. Eighteen days post-treatment, 2 × 105 4T1 cells were inoculated into the contralateral mammary fat pad of the primary TNBC tumor. Flow cytometric analysis was performed on day 14 after tumor rechallenge to determine the frequency of effector memory T cells (CD3+CD8+CD44+CD62Llow) in the spleen.

Statistics and reproducibility

All experiments were independently repeated at least three times. The exact number of biological or technical replicates (n) for each experiment is specified in the corresponding figure legends. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Graph generation and statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 10 software (GraphPad). For comparisons between two groups, statistical significance was assessed using a two-tailed Student’s t-test. Differences among multiple groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey–Kramer multiple-comparison testing. Exact P values are provided in the corresponding figures. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001, whereas ns indicates a non-significant difference (nsP ≥ 0.05).
