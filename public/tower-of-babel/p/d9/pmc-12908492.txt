Cranial radiotherapy profoundly affects glia without inducing widespread cellular senescence
Authors: Laura E Kuil, Roan H van Scheppingen, Yoran M Leter, Berend H Röring, Mark C de Gooijer, Caroline L van Heijningen, Stefan Prekovic, Annette Compter, Olaf van Tellingen, Sanne B Schagen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMore than 70% of cancer patients who underwent cranial radiotherapy have cognitive problems, suggesting that they might undergo accelerated brain aging due to the cancer treatment. Radiotherapy is known to induce cellular senescence in tumors and in various cell types in vitro. Therefore, we hypothesized that cranial radiotherapy induces cellular senescence in the brain.MethodsWe treated male C57BL/6 mice with various dosages of (fractionated) CT-guided cranial radiotherapy. The brains were analyzed for various senescence and glial markers using immunohistochemistry, histochemistry, CosMx SMI and RT-qPCR. To contextualize the findings regarding cranial radiotherapy in young animals and assess whether these changes parallel natural aging, we studied the brains from 77-week-old female mice, which is considered middle-aged to old representing early aging.ResultsSurprisingly, we found no increase in markers for cellular senescence in the brain after cranial radiotherapy. However, we did detect profound changes in different types of glia. In early-aged mice we again did not detect an increase in senescence markers, but we observed the same directionality in the effects on glia. These effects on glia were milder compared to those upon cranial radiotherapy.ConclusionOverall, cellular senescence in the healthy brain seems a rather uncommon phenomenon and not induced upon cranial radiotherapy, but profound changes in different types of glia were detected upon cranial radiotherapy.

Importance of the Study

Cranial radiotherapy (RT) induces neurotoxicity in the healthy brain that likely underlies the cognitive impairments experiences by brain cancer patients and survivors. The underlying molecular mechanisms are not completely understood and there is no successful pharmacological treatment available. Cancer therapies including RT are known to induce cellular senescence in the tumor and here we assessed whether this also happens to the healthy brain as a consequence of RT using mice. We extensively assessed a variety of markers for cellular senescence after various radiation dosages and timings after treatment and were unable to find induction of cellular senescence in the brain. This stresses the idea whether cellular senescence is important in RT-induced neurotoxicity. Importantly we identified changes in neurogenesis, oligodendrocyte progenitor cells, microglia and astrocytes, of which some showed resemblance between early aging and radiation effects.

Irradiation causes DNA damage in the receiving cells, which will induce the DNA damage response (DDR) and non-homologous end joining (NHEJ) to attempt to repair the double strand breaks. How well the damage can be repaired determines the fate of the cell, including apoptosis and senescence.1 Senescence is a state of permanent cell cycle arrest accompanied by a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) and resistance to apoptosis. Initiation of senescence requires activation of p53/p21 and p16INK4A tumor-suppressive pathways. DDR will stabilize p53, which in turn increases p21 transcription leading to cell cycle arrest.2 Persistence of this arrest is facilitated by p16INK4A. Senescent cells display β-galactosidase activity at pH 6.0 (SA-β-gal), widely used as a readout for senescence. Since SA β-gal can occur in other contexts a panel of hallmarks of senescence is essential to characterize a cell as being senescent. Senescence hallmarks include SASP markers, reduced lamin B1, relocated HMGB1, prolonged DNA damage signaling, enlarged cell size, telomere shortening, epigenetic changes, induction of anti-apoptotic BCL-2 family members and increased autophagy.3 In tumors it is well known that irradiation induces cellular senescence. However, in the tissue surrounding the tumor cellular senescence is also thought to occur.

Irradiated human brain material from regions close to the tumor was found to contain senescent astrocytes.4 Moreover, several rodent studies showed cellular senescence in the brain after radiotherapy, although the senescent cell types are inconsistent between studies showing senescent post-mitotic neurons,5 neural stem cells in the dentate gyrus,5,6 astrocytes7,8 and endothelial cells.9 The latter two papers rely solely on the P16-3MR reporter construct or P16 as a readout for cellular senescence,8,9 even though it is well known that P16 is not a marker specific to cellular senescence.10,11 In addition, cranial radiotherapy induces an inflammatory response involving mainly astrocytes and microglia. Astrocytes gain a hypertrophic phenotype characterized by GFAP induction.12,13 Microglia, the resident macrophages of the brain, respond by phagocytosis of apoptotic cells and gaining a reactive status with an altered morphology.14–16 Microglial reactivity can be observed by changes in their morphology, either showing excessive branching or acquiring an ameboid phenotype with shorter branches and somatic hypertrophy.17 Other IHC markers for microglial reactivity are the expression of CD68 and the loss of the homeostatic microglia specific marker P2RY12.18–20

Cranial radiotherapy is a central modality in the treatment of brain tumors and brain metastases. However, from six months after radiotherapy patients can experience progressive cognitive problems which significantly affect their daily lives.21 These effects are often seen in patients receiving whole brain radiation, and at a lower number in patients receiving stereotactic radiation surgery (SRS).22,23 These radiotherapy-induced cognitive problems often consist of impaired (verbal) learning and memory, information processing speed, and executive function. Various symptoms are reminiscent of those seen in aging-related neurodegenerative conditions where cellular senescence has been observed.24 Cranial radiotherapy is known to induce other neurotoxic effects such as loss of neurogenesis,25 oligodendrocyte precursor cell loss,25,26 inflammation14,27 and reactive gliosis,28 showing overlap with neurodegenerative conditions. We hypothesized that cranial radiotherapy induces cellular senescence and accelerated aging of the healthy brain, which may underlie the cognitive impairment that occurs after treatment. To assess this, we used non-tumor bearing mice, irradiated their brains and searched for indications of cellular senescence using a combination of readouts and assessed the effects on various other cell types. To contextualize the findings regarding cranial radiotherapy in young animals and assess whether these changes parallel natural aging, we included early-aged mice as a comparative group.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Animals

Experiments were approved by the Animal Experimentation Committee of the Netherlands Cancer Institute (NKI) and all were carried out in accordance with the approved protocols. The principles of laboratory animal care, Dutch law and the guidelines for care and the Code of Practice of lab animals used in oncology were followed. The number of mice per group were determined based on previous experience with immunohistochemistry analysis. All methods are reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines. C57Bl/6JRj mice were housed with 3–5 animals in clear cages containing wood shavings, food and water ad libitum on a fixed 12:12 h light:dark cycle. Dose and duration experiment: 12-week-old males (n = 24). 6 sham-control, 3 per experimental group. Single dose versus fractionated dose: 6-week-old males (n = 9), 3 per group. The animals were arbitrarily (random) selected for radiotherapy or sham-treatments. Aging experiment: female C57Bl/6JRj mice of 10–24 weeks of age (n = 8) or of 77 weeks of age (n = 10), the animals did not undergo any kind of treatment. All animals were sacrificed by CO2 asphyxiation followed by isolation of the brain (Fig. 1B).

Cranial Irradiation

X-RAD SmART (Small Animal Radiotherapy) Ct-guided cranial irradiation under isoflurane anesthesia using a S10 collimator. Two animals were paired and one animal was left in the induction chamber (sham-control) and the other was irradiated. The irradiation beam was targeted at the cerebrum dorsal to the skull base between the cerebellum and olfactory bulb (Fig. 1A). The animals received half the daily dose from one lateral side and the other half from the other lateral side, immediately after each other. Dose determination experiment: From previous studies it is known that cognitive impairment is induced by fractionated radiotherapy using fractions of 0.5 to 8.67 Gy with a total dose of 5 to 45Gy.29 Senescence was found with doses between 5 and 40 Gy.5–9,30 The planned follow-up was 1 week, 4 weeks and 8 weeks after irradiation using two doses (20 Gy/day and 4Gy/day for 3 consecutive days, translating to a BED of 660Gy and 36Gy, respectively). However, the high dose animals showed problems with their skin around the eyes 5 weeks after RT and therefore the high dose (60 Gy) animals were sacrificed at 1 and 5 weeks after irradiation instead. The last group was adjusted to 30 Gy (10 Gy/day, BED 180Gy) and followed up for 10 weeks (Fig. 1B). Single and fractionated dose experiment: 10 Gy/day for either one (single dose, BED 60Gy) or for three consecutive days (fractionated dose, BED 180Gy) follow-up of 30 days.

RT-qPCR

RNA was isolated from irradiated tissue around the midline (Figs. 1B and 3A; 15–20 mg per animal) with the Purelink RNA mini kit (Invitrogen) with an on-column DNA removal step. cDNA was generated with the iScript cDNA synthesis kit (BIO-RAD). Primers are listed in table S1. Bimake 2x SYBRgreen qPCR mastermix was used for the qPCR reaction. Various housekeeping genes (Gapdh, Bactin, Gusb and Oaz1) were tested and Oaz1 was found to be most stable and insensitive to radiotherapy treatment and used for normalization.

X-gal Staining

For the visualization of SA-β-gal activity, the histochemical staining kit by Sigma (CS0030) was used using OCT embedded material cut in 8 µm sections. Sections were counterstained using nuclear fast red.

Immunohistochemistry

Mouse brains were fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 48 hours at room temperature and embedded in paraffin by the animal pathology core facility of the Netherlands Cancer Institute. All slides were stained with DAB chromogen antibodies, with slight differences for each staining, highlighted in table S2. Slides are scanned on a Panoramic P1000 slidescanner.

Quantification of Slides

Manual quantification of cell numbers was performed to quantify the number of microglia (IBA1 and P2RY12) and OPCs (PDGFRA) in 2–6 ROIs of 500 × 500 μ m per brain region using Qupath V0.3.2 (Fig. 1B). Only counting the positive cells of which the nucleus was present on the slide. A quantification method developed by Finney et al. was adapted for use in this project to assess glial staining (if not manually counted). First, stain vectors for color deconvolution were set using the automatic estimation function in Qupath. Then the region annotations were defined, after which positive pixel identification was done. For the nuclear localized proteins the positive cell detection function in Qupath was used for quantification after regions were annotated. For the nuclear membrane marker Lamin B1 the build in H-scoring of Qupath was used.

To detect radiation-induced DNA damage, the subcellular detection feature in Qupath was used to detect yH2AX + spots. After stain deconvolution, nuclei were detected with the automatic cell detection feature, which was followed by the subcellular detection feature to detect spots between 0.5 and 2 microns in size. DNA damage in a ROI was assessed by determining the ratio of total spots/total cell detections in the ROI.

For microglial morphological analysis the mGlia pipeline was used as described by van Weering et al.31 20 squared 500 pixel thalamic microglial images per mouse were selected in Qupath, exported as JPEG and color deconvolution in ImageJ was used to remove the hematoxylin counterstain. AUC calculations were performed on the sholl data using the Linear Trapezoidal Method in R (4.4.1).

CosMx

Three tissue microarrays (TMAs) per paraffin block were generated capturing the cortex, thalamus and hippocampus. 5 µm slices put on slide for spatial single cell transcriptomics using the CosMx Technology Access Program (Nanostring) according to manufacturer’s recommendations with the Mouse RNA Neuroscience panel (1.0), the CosMx Mouse Neuroscience Cell Segmentation Kit, Mouse Neuroscience Supplemental Segmentation kit and MmCD68. Bioinformatic analysis is described in the Supplemental Materials and available on Github (https://github.com/Prekovic-Lab/Kuil2025).

Statistical Analysis

Data visualization and statistical analysis was done in GraphPad Prism (version 9, GraphPad software, Boston, MA). Students t-test or one-way ANOVA analysis with a Dunnett’s multiple comparison test were performed. In case of unequal variances, a Brown-Forsythe Welch ANOVA was performed. If the data were not normally distributed, a Kruskal-Wallis test was performed with a Dunn’s multiple comparisons test. P values below .05 were considered statistically significant, graphs represent the means with standard deviations.

Results

Brain-Targeted Radiotherapy Does Not Induce Cellular Senescence in The Healthy Irradiated Brain

We used a plethora of markers to assess the presence of cellular senescence in the irradiated brain. SA-β-gal activity showed a comparable number of positive cells (on average only 10 cells/mm2) in the different experimental irradiated groups at various doses and times after radiation and in the control group (Fig. 1C; Fig. S1A). We next assessed other characteristics of cellular senescence by RT-qPCR. Cdkn2a (P16), Lmnb1 and Mmp3 expression did not change between any of the groups (Fig. 1D). Only Cdkn1a (P21) was induced at 1 week after radiotherapy at both dosages (12 and 60 Gy) and at 5 weeks after radiotherapy after high dose radiotherapy (60 Gy) (Fig. 1D). Markers of the SASP (Il6, Il1b, Cxcl10 and Cxcl1) did not change after radiotherapy in any of the groups (Fig. 1E).

Next, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) to assess available senescence markers at the protein level, while retaining spatial information. P53 and phospho-P53 are expected to be upregulated early on after DNA damage to induce cell cycle arrest and/or senescence.3 As expected, we observed an increase in both markers at 1 week after radiotherapy showing significance only in the 60Gy high dose group (Fig. S1B and C). However, in lower doses and at later time points, there was no increase in P53 or pP53 + cells. P53 activation is known to activate P21, but P21 IHC did not reveal differences between any of the groups (Fig. 1F; Fig. S2A). Lamin B1 is expressed in the nuclear membrane of all cells, as expected, but did not reveal any overall changes as determined using automated H-scoring in Qupath (Fig. 1G). BCL-2 + cells are common in the brain, in particular in the cortex (60%–90% of cells) and were not changed in abundance after radiotherapy (Fig. S2B). Activation of the DNA repair machinery can be visualized using a γH2AX antibody. As expected, we detected a large increase γH2AX positivity upon radiation, in a seemingly dose and time dependent manner (Fig. S2C). Over time, the number of γH2AX foci decreases, as expected. However, even at 10 weeks after radiotherapy, we still observed a significant increase in γH2AX foci upon radiotherapy, suggesting that the DNA repair machinery remains activated long-term after radiotherapy in the brain (Fig. S2C). Based on the nuclear morphology of the cells containing persistent γH2AX foci these should be neurons or astrocytes (Fig. S3A). Double IHC using NeuN and γH2AX showed that the virtually all foci are located in neurons (arrows) and double IHC using GFAP and yH2AX shows no yH2AX localization in the nuclei of astrocytes (Fig. S3A). Thus, the persistent DNA damage foci induced by RT are located in neurons.

Brain Irradiation Shows Clear Effects on Neurogenesis and Glia

It is known that various cellular processes in the brain are affected after radiotherapy, such as loss of neurogenesis,25 oligodendrocyte precursor cell loss,25,26 inflammation14,27 and reactive gliosis.28 In our model we assessed the time and dose dependency. Ki67, a proliferative marker was used as a proxy for neurogenesis in the subventricular zone (SVZ), one of the neurogenic regions in the mouse brain. We showed a significant loss of neurogenesis in all groups, that retained over time (Fig. S3B). It can be appreciated that the higher dose group show a more severe loss of Ki67 + cells. Proliferation in non-neurogenic regions of the brain was induced in the cortex at 5 weeks and thalamus at 1 week in the 60 Gy group and was not affected in the lower dose groups (Fig. S3B).

Next, we assessed GFAP, a gene expressed by astrocytes that is upregulated during reactive gliosis. Upregulation of Gfap RNA was observed in the high dose group (60 Gy) at 5 weeks after treatment (Fig. S3C). GFAP IHC showed hypertrophy of astrocytes in the hippocampus, cortex and mPFC in the 60Gy 1 week group and the 30Gy 10-week groups (Fig. S3D). Astrocyte cell numbers (SOX9) did not increase upon radiotherapy (Fig. S4A). Aif1 (Iba1) is expressed by microglia in the brain and its expression is induced during inflammation/reactivity to disease.32,33 Aif1 RNA was only significantly upregulated the high dose group (60 Gy) at 5 weeks after treatment (Fig. S3C). Analysis of microglia using IBA1 IHC, labeling microglia and macrophages regardless of activation status, showed a decrease in IBA1 + cells in hippocampus and cortex in all groups except for 1 week after 12 Gy (cortex and hippocampus) and 5 weeks after 60 Gy (cortex), in the mPFC reduced cell numbers are found 4 and 8 weeks after 12Gy (Fig. 2A; Fig. S4B). We assessed the morphology of thalamic microglia using the mGlia tool developed by van Weering et al. (2023). Sholl analysis shows that the irradiated cells show more intersections, in particular at larger distances to the soma in the 12Gy 4 and 8 week groups and the 60Gy groups compared to controls (Fig. 2B). Cluster analysis shows increased presence of cells with higher complexity upon radiotherapy in all groups except for 12Gy 1 week (Fig. 2C). Assessment of various features extracted in the pipeline showed significant effects of irradiation on various branch parameters, cell size/spread parameters, nodes, sholl and branch area parameters (Fig. 2D). Moreover, it can be appreciated from the individual images that the microglia after RT have thicker branches and a hypertrophic appearance (Fig. 2E). Altogether, this shows that upon radiotherapy microglia show increased complexity and extended branching, suggesting their reactivity to radiotherapy.

PDGFRα is a gene expressed specifically by oligodendrocyte precursor cells and not in neuronal stem cells or oligodendrocytes. Pdgfra RNA expression was lower after high dose radiotherapy, albeit not reaching significance (Fig. S3C). PDGFRα IHC showed a loss of PDGFRα+ OPCs 1 week after RT irrespective of dose, but whereas the low dose animals showed a recovery in the number of OPCs, the high dose animals showed a persistent significant loss of OPCs (Fig. 2F; Fig. S4C). Analysis of the oligodendrocyte lineage using OLIG2 as a marker showed no changes in overall number of OLIG2 + cells, suggesting that the radiosensitivity is restricted to OPCs (Fig. 2G; Fig. S5A). MBP is expressed in mature myelinating oligodendrocytes specifically.34 We did not detect changes in expression of Mbp RNA, nor did we detect changes in MBP protein in the myelinated area of the cortex at the time points assessed (Fig. S5B).

Single Dose Radiotherapy Does Not Induce Cellular Senescence

To rule out that fractionation or age hampered the detection of cellular senescence in the brain, we subjected young mice (6 weeks of age) to single dose radiation (10Gy), fractionated radiotherapy (3 fractions of 10Gy) or sedation only (sham-controls) (Fig. 3A). We showed no changes in senescence markers SA-β-gal, Cdkn1a, Cdkn2a, Il6, Mmp3, Cxcl1, and Lmnb1 upon either single dose or fractionated dose radiotherapy in young animals (Fig. 3B and C, Fig. S5C, S6A, S6C) (Table 1). Only the pro-inflammatory chemokine Cxcl10 was induced upon radiotherapy, in particular after fractionated radiotherapy (Fig. S5C). As expected, increased yH2AX staining was found in the hippocampus (sign.) and thalamus (n.s.) after radiotherapy (Fig. S6B). The cortex of the sham-controls surprisingly contained γH2AX + staining. In line with the first experiment, there is no induction of cellular senescence upon radiotherapy, regardless of fractionation and dose.

In line with the previous experiment Aif1 (encoding IBA1), Gfap or Mbp RNA showed no changes between controls and the two radiotherapy groups (Fig. S7A). However, Pdgfra showed significantly reduced expression only in the fractionated radiotherapy group (Fig. S7A). Immunohistochemistry of P2RY12, a marker specifically labeling homeostatic microglia,35 showed significant reductions in P2RY12 + homeostatic microglia numbers in both radiotherapy groups in the thalamus and hippocampus, whereas in the cortex only the single dose group and in the mPFC only the fractionated dose group reached significance (Fig. 3D; Fig. S7B). PDGFRα+ cells were significantly decreased after both single dose and fractionated dose radiotherapy in the thalamus (Fig. S7C) and only after fractionated dose in the hippocampus (Fig. S7C). A stepwise decrease in cell numbers was observed in the cortex and mPFC with only significance upon fractionated RT (Fig. 3E; Fig. S7C). Importantly, these reductions in homeostatic microglia and OPCs were found only in the irradiated areas (Fig. S7B and C). Assessment of MBP IHC in cortex did not show changes (Fig. S7D). GFAP IHC showed increased presence of GFAP staining suggesting hypertrophy in the mPFC, cortex and hippocampus of the fractionated dose group (Fig. S7E).

To determine whether a high-resolution spatial transcriptomics approach could detect cellular senescence in the mouse brain, we analyzed brain slices from control and fractionated dose-treated mice using the CosMx SMI platform (Nanostring) (Fig. 4A). Spatial transcriptomics is a rapidly evolving field that enables in-depth, cell-type-specific analysis by mapping gene expression with regards to tissue architecture. CosMx SMI is an imaging-based technique that relies on accurate cell segmentation to assign transcripts. Inadequate segmentation could lead to transcripts being wrongfully assigned to cells, thereby increasing experimental noise and compromising data quality.36 After performing quality control and optimizing segmentation, we observed a significant upregulation of Gfap and B2m in the total dataset, suggestive of increased inflammation33,37,38 (Fig. 4A and B). In addition, there is an upregulation of Ttr and Pcp4, which are induced upon brain irradiation as described by others.39,40 Spatial transcriptomics enables in-depth analysis of cells in their spatial context, but in this study the 1000-gene panel and the limited detection limit, yielded count-based, 0-inflated data. In turn, this complicated cell-type identification and subsequent analysis on subsets of cell-types or spatial regions due to lack of statistical power. Subset analysis was only performed on the largest population in our dataset, which were astrocytes. Astrocytes showed a downregulation of Sparc and Nnat and upregulation of Gfap, Pcp4 and Slc17a7 (Fig. 4C). These changes suggest a potential effect on neuronal synaptic plasticity and an increase in inflammation.33,37,41–43

Unexpectedly, Pdgfra expression did not show an overall reduction. However, within the oligodendrocyte progenitor cell (OPC) population, Pdgfra expression was significantly lower in the irradiated group which was explained by the higher proportion of cells where no Pdgfra expression was detected (Fig. 4D). Additionally, cells were classified as OPCs with significantly greater certainty in the control group compared to the irradiated group (Wilcoxon W = 79106, P = .03).

We evaluated senescence-associated genes from the Nanostring gene panel and the Mayo Clinic’s senescence gene signature.44,45 Using single cell gene set enrichment analysis (ssgsea), the enrichment score of the two senescence gene sets was calculated per cell. There were no significant differences in scores between conditions. Bootstrapping analysis, using random gene sets of equal size, confirmed that the enrichment scores of the senescence lists did not exceed what would be expected by chance (Fig. 4E). This suggests again that cellular senescence is not enriched across the dataset.

To investigate potential alterations in the spatial distribution of cells in the brain post-radiotherapy, we conducted a distance analysis.46 The analysis of myeloid cells revealed that the overall distribution of cell proportions over distance was similar between the two groups, indicating that the cellular organization was largely preserved (Fig. 4F). Distance analysis across various cell types demonstrated that the overall cellular distribution was comparable between control and irradiated samples, suggesting that the cellular organization remains largely intact after radiotherapy (e.g. astrocytes and oligodendrocytes in Fig. 4G and H).

Assessment of Cellular Senescence and Glial Changes in Middle-Aged Mice Compared to Young-Adult Mice

We assessed brains from middle-aged to old female mice (77 weeks of age) that resemble early aging and compared them to young female mice (10- to 24-week-old) to compare the effects of early aging to those after radiotherapy. Markers of cellular senescence did not change between the age groups in any of the assessed brain regions (Fig. 5A and B; Fig. S8A–C), in line with previous studies showing that frailty and cellular senescence becomes evident past 24 months of age.47,48 As expected, neurogenesis in the SVZ, was reduced in the older mice (Fig. 5C), but less severe compared to that observed after RT (Fig. S3B). The cortex and hippocampus contained less Ki67 + cells in the older mice compared to the younger controls (Fig. S8D), which we did not see after RT (Fig. S3B). A significant reduction in OPCs (relative to total cells) was found in all regions examined (Fig. 5D; Fig. S8E) and a decrease in the number of microglia by either P2RY12 (marking homeostatic microglia) or IBA1 (marking all microglia/macrophages) in the cortex, but not in the thalamus and hippocampus was found (Fig. 5E; Fig. S8F and G). To assess changes in morphology we subjected IBA1 + microglia from the thalamus to the mGlia pipeline. Sholl analysis shows that microglia from 77-week-old animals, contain more intersections, in particular at larger distances from the soma compared to young controls, showing the same directionality as observed after RT (Fig. 5F and G). Significant effects of early aging were observed on various branch parameters, soma area, cell size/spread parameters, nodes, sholl and branch area parameters (Fig. 5H). Moreover, the cells from the older mice appear to contain thicker more pronounced ramifications (Fig. 5I).

Discussion

Assessment of various senescence markers did not indicate an increase in cellular senescence with radiotherapy at the doses, ages and time intervals tested (Table 1). An induction of P21 RNA, pP53 and P53 cells was detected only one week after radiotherapy, but this is likely the result of direct DNA damage due to the radiation, with these effects subsiding at later time points (Table 1). Moreover, yH2AX was observed after radiotherapy and appeared persistent up to 10 weeks after radiotherapy, in line with a previous report showing long-term activation of the DNA damage response in mice irradiated at early postnatal ages49 (Table 1). However, these (early) and transient changes in P21, P53 and yH2AX likely reflect the activation of the DNA damage machinery. This contrasts the typical sustained increase in P21 and/or P16 observed in senescence. Our radiotherapy findings challenge the previous studies that showed induction of cellular senescence, albeit with inconsistent cell types being found senescent. Differences between our study and previous ones are 1) our irradiation method is highly targeted, limiting exposure of surrounding tissues, 2) we used mainly fractionated radiotherapy at higher cumulative doses. However, the dose in the single dose group is similar to most previous studies. Others suggest that cellular senescence observed in vitro is different from changes in aging microglia in vivo.50,51 Moreover, the aging microglial transcriptome does not seem associated with any classical indicators of cellular senescence.6,52 We show no increase in traditional cellular senescence markers during early aging nor after radiotherapy supporting the notion that in vivo senescence in the brain differs from in vitro and tumor cellular senescence.

We observed a major reduction of cycling cells in the SVZ upon radiation and early aging, which is in line with previous studies.53,54 Here, we show that the loss of neurogenesis upon cranial radiotherapy is dose dependent in severity, but persistent over time regardless of the dose. The loss of OPC is dose-independent at 1 week after radiotherapy. However, at later time points, animals receiving lower doses showed recovery of OPC numbers, where higher dose radiotherapy (30Gy and 60Gy) showed a persistent loss of OPCs (measured at 5 and 10 weeks post RT, respectively). A previous report suggested that fractionation specifically reduces the OPC numbers,26 it seems that this effect is dose and time dependent, as the 12Gy animals receiving fractionated doses of 4Gy per day showed recovery over time in our dataset. The authors showed that OPCs undergo apoptosis very soon after radiotherapy (8 hours), suggesting that OPCs are highly sensitive to radiation and are lost through apoptosis rather than differentiation into oligodendrocytes.26 Importantly, the loss of OPCs upon cranial radiotherapy has been shown in the human brain as well.26 Early-aged animals presented with fewer OPCs, suggesting that OPC numbers decrease with age. In line with a recent report showing reduced OPC numbers upon aging in the mouse hippocampus.55 Whereas others showed stable OPC numbers upon aging in mice,56 rats57 and in human.58 Interestingly, ablation of OPCs in the mouse brain was able to induce spatial working memory changes,59 therefore the loss of OPCs upon radiotherapy might be an important factor in radiation-induced cognitive impairment.

Reduction in microglial numbers (IBA1) upon radiotherapy is region dependent, showing modest reductions in cortex and hippocampus but not thalamus, and this decrease is persistent over time. It has previously been shown that microglia undergo apoptosis at short term (6 hours) after radiotherapy, however apoptotic microglia were still found more frequently 30 days after radiation.27 This likely explains the reduced numbers of microglia observed after radiotherapy, also reported by others.27,60 Microglia in the irradiated brain presented with an altered morphology (higher branching complexity) compared to the controls, especially at higher doses and at later time points after the lower dose. Microglial morphological analysis showed increased branching area and complexity after radiotherapy and upon early aging. Hyper ramification of microglia has mainly been observed in mouse models of stress,61 but has been shown previously upon irradiation.16 Therefore, this hyper ramification likely reflects microglial reactivity to radiotherapy at doses and the time points assessed.

The morphological changes upon radiotherapy towards an ameboid phenotype were reported to be acute (6 hours and 24 hours) but returned to ramified in the majority of cells already at 7 days after treatment.27 This is in line with the (hyper)ramified morphology observed in our experiments. We noted that the morphology of microglia in our 60 Gy 5 weeks animals resemble by eye the morphology of microglia in rat brains reactive to radiotherapy.14 However, that report showed opposite changes in sholl analysis. These differences might be explained by the use of the mGlia tool on chromogen-based staining that might be sensitive to expression levels of IBA1 and effects of fixation. Importantly, in our IBA1 IHC of young and sham-controls microglial cell bodies are clearly defined, but the protrusions are stained very lightly and are difficult to trace. Whereas in the irradiated and the aged samples the microglial soma seems enlarged and protrusions are thicker (hypertrophy) and can therefore be traced more easily (Figs. 2E and 5I).

During early aging we found reduced microglial numbers (IBA1) only in the cortex and not in the thalamus and hippocampus and increased complexity of thalamic microglia. Others report increased microglia numbers and reduced complexity with age in rodents52,62–64 and stable microglia numbers with reduced complexity in humans.65 We observed an increase in soma area size upon aging using mGlia, indicative of perinuclear cytoplasm hypertrophy,66 which is in line with a previous report on aging mice67 and humans.68 A factor of influence might be the use of female mice in our early aging experiments. Sex differences were observed in microglial transcriptional changes upon aging where females show a stepwise change in gene expression whereas males show a sudden change in gene expression.52

Future Directions

Interestingly, a recent study showed that accelerated aging of microglia, induced by ablation and repopulation of microglia using an Csf1r inhibitor, results in cognitive impairment in mice.52 A large body of evidence points to a central role for microglia in neurodegenerative diseases,69 including adult-onset leukoencephalopathy with axonal spheroids and pigmented glia (ALSP) where the patients have reduced numbers of microglia.70,71 Some promising mouse studies show that manipulating microglial presence can protect animals from radiation-induced cognitive impairment.72–74 Therefore, it appears that microglia can have major effects on brain health and their reduced numbers and altered morphology found in our study could be implicated in radiotherapy-induced cognitive impairment.

Conclusion

Following cranial radiotherapy, cells in the healthy brain do not acquire a cellular senescence state, as we know from tumors and in vitro work. Neither the post mitotic nor the proliferating cells (e.g. neuroblasts, OPCs and microglia) in the brain seem to acquire a state of cellular senescence upon radiotherapy, but rather are highly RT-sensitive (neurogenic cells/OPCs) and/or show morphological changes in response to radiotherapy (microglia/astrocytes). However, accelerated aging as a process might still occur, as shown by a similar directionality in glial changes and loss of neurogenesis in early aging and radiotherapy-treated animals.
