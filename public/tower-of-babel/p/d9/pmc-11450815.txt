Trypsin Partially Cleaves Apolipoprotein A-I (ApoA-I) Precursor into Mature ApoA-I Hindering the Quantification of Naturally Occurring ApoA-I Proteoforms by Liquid Chromatography in Multiple Reaction Monitoring Mode Mass Spectrometry (LC-MRM-MS)
Authors: Carmen Llorens-Cebrià, Norberto Núñez-Seral, Yolanda Villena-Ortiz, Irene Martínez-Díaz, Maria José Soler, Roser Ferrer-Costa, Conxita Jacobs-Cachá, Joan López-Hellín
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Apolipoprotein A-I (ApoA-I), one of the most abundant proteins in plasma and the major protein component of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), is naturally found in several proteoforms; two of them are ProApoA-I and mature ApoA-I. These two proteoforms of ApoA-I coexist in biological samples and differ only in their N-terminal end. Virtually, the only way to differentiate them is by detecting the proteoform-specific N-terminal proteolytic peptides (RHFWQQDEPPQSPWDR and DEPPQSPWDR, respectively) using liquid chromatography in multiple reaction monitoring mode mass spectrometry (LC-MRM-MS). We have developed a bottom-up LC-MRM-MS method to simultaneously detect proApoA-I and mature ApoA-I. To test the specificity of the method, we digested with trypsin purified mature ApoA-I and recombinant proApoA-I. As expected, only the N-term peptide corresponding to the mature ApoA-I proteoform (DEPPQSPWDR) was detected when digesting mature ApoA-I. However, the digestion of the proApoA-I produced not only the N-terminal peptide corresponding to proApoA-I (RHFWQQDEPPQSPWDR) but also the N-terminal tryptic peptide corresponding to mature ApoA-I (DEPPQSPWDR). This effect was produced by standard and high-specificity trypsin as well as by the Arg-C enzyme in a self-limited manner (approximately 10% of the total). The synthetic proApo-I peptide is not cleaved by trypsin, suggesting that the here reported effect is dependent on protein conformation. The effect is not negligible, as it can be detected by LC-MRM-MS, and correction calculations should be applied to accurately quantify proApoA-I and mature ApoA-I in biological samples where these two proteoforms may coexist.

Introduction

The human proteome is estimated to consist of more than 1 million different proteoforms derived from the expression of approximately 20,000 genes.1 This complexity is explained by the coexistence of variants of the same protein generated from the expression of allelic variants, alternative splicing of the RNA transcripts, as well as from post-translational modifications (PTMs). These protein variants or proteoforms differentially participate in and/or regulate cellular and metabolic processes.2 Hence, the levels of a specific proteoform may increase or decrease in pathological conditions, which is why many of them are or may be used as biomarkers in clinical practice. A major example is blood hemoglobin; while the total levels of hemoglobin are used to assess anemia, the nonenzymatically glycated form (HbA1c) is a biomarker of glycemic control.3 Despite the existence of a wide range of assays developed to detect proteins (nephelometry, immunohistochemistry, Western blot, and chromatography, among others) not all are suitable for routine analysis in the clinical laboratory, and, further, some do not allow or are limited to distinguish specific proteoforms. In this sense, liquid chromatography in multiple reaction monitoring mode mass spectrometry (LC-MRM-MS) allows one to detect specific proteoforms because of its high molecular specificity.4

Apolipoprotein A-I (ApoA-I), one of the most abundant proteins in plasma and the major protein component of high-density lipoprotein (HDL), is naturally found in several proteoforms. ApoA-I is synthesized by the liver and in lesser extent by the intestine, and, once translated, it is released into the bloodstream as a proprotein (proApoA-I) after the cleavage of the signal peptide (aa 1 to 18).5,6 ProApoA-I represents approximately 5% of the plasmatic pool of ApoA-I. The N-Terminal propeptide sequence (aa 19 to 24) is immediately cleaved in blood by the bone morphogenic protein-1 (BMP-1) and the procollagen C-proteinase enhancer-2 (PCPE-2),7 giving rise to mature ApoA-I that represents the main form of circulating ApoA-I (95% approximately). Mature ApoA-I is immediately lipidated to form nascent HDLs (or preβ1-HDLs).6 Both proApoA-I and mature ApoA-1 may carry several PTMs (oxidations, glycations, acylations, and carbamylations, among others) and undergo N and C-terminal truncations related to proteolytic events.8−11 Some of these proteoforms have potential as biomarkers such as proApoA-I for lung cancer metastasis11 or an N-terminal misprocessed form of proApoA-1 for nephrotic syndrome recurrence.8 As mentioned before, ApoA-I precursor and mature ApoA-I only differ by the N-terminal propeptide sequence (aa 19 to 24) that is present in proApoA-I and not in mature ApoA-I. These naturally occurring proteoforms can be distinguished by 2D electrophoresis or by immunodetection using a specific antibody against the propeptide sequence,8 but the detection techniques are tedious and lack reproducibility. Therefore, LC-MRM-MS seems to be the best manner to analyze proApoA-I and differentiate it from mature ApoA-I by detecting their specific proteolytic N-terminal peptides (RHFWQQDEPPQSPWDR and DEPPQSPWDR, respectively).

We have developed a bottom-up LC-MRM-MS method to simultaneously detect and quantify proApoA-I and mature ApoA-I. As a proof of concept, we first tested pure commercially available recombinant proApoA-I and mature ApoA-I to ensure specificity. Here, we describe that the sample proteolysis process, required to measure proteins by MRM, partially cleaves the ApoA-I precursor into mature ApoA-I, an unexpected finding that needs to be considered when developing targeted mass spectrometry methods to detect naturally occurring ApoA-I proteoforms differing in the N-terminal end.

Methods

For all of the procedures, additional details can be found in the Supporting Information.

Coomassie Staining and Western Blotting

Purity of recombinant proApoA-I (AP1-H5225, Acro Biosystems) and mature ApoA-I (A0722, Sigma-Aldrich) was confirmed by coomassie staining and Western blot.

Intact Mass Measurement of proApoA-I

Intact mass of proApoA-I was determined using a quadrupole time-of-flight (Q-TOF) Premier mass spectrometer (Waters/Micromass) at the proteomics core facility (PCF) of the European Molecular Biology Laboratory (EMBL), Heidelberg, Germany.

Analysis of the Full MS/MS Spectra of the DEPPQSPWDR Peptide

To obtain the full MS/MS spectra of the DEPPQSPWDR peptide we digested proApoA-I or mature ApoA-I with trypsin and analyzed the tryptic peptides obtained using a Q Exactive plus hybrid quadrupole orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) at the PCF of EMBL.

LC-MRM-MS Analysis

One microgram of proApoA-I or mature ApoA-I was denatured, reduced, and alkylated before digestion with trypsin or Arg-C following the manufacturer’s protocols. To detect respective N-terminal tryptic peptides (RHFWQQDEPPQSPWDR and DEPPQSPWDR, respectively), the tryptic peptides were injected onto an ACQUITY UPLC Peptide BEH C18 Column 300 Å, 1.7 um, 2.1 × 100 mm column (186003686, Waters) coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer Xevo-TQ (Waters Associates, Milford, MA, USA). The ratio of the analyte peak to the IS peak or the relative response (RR) is used to express the results.

Results and Discussion

We have set up an LC-MRM-MS method to simultaneously quantify naturally occurring ApoA-I proteoforms (mature ApoA-I and proApoA-I) in biological samples that detect the corresponding N-terminal specific tryptic peptides (DEPPQSPWDR and RHFWQQDEPPQSPWDR, respectively). In order to determine the specificity of the method, we have digested purified mature ApoA-I and recombinant proApoA-I with trypsin expecting in both cases to only detect the specific N-terminal peptide for each proteoform.

First, we verified the purity and specificity of the proteins used. As depicted in Figure 1, a coomassie staining revealed that both mature ApoA-I and proApoA-I were free of contaminant proteins, and while a polyclonal antibody against ApoA-I recognized both forms of the protein, the custom-made antibody against proApoA-I only detected ApoA-I precursor.

Coomassie staining and Western blot to ascertain the purity and specificity of proApoA-I and mature ApoA-I proteins. One hundred nanograms of proApoA-I and ApoA-I were resolved in 12.5% SDS-PAGE gels that were either stained with colloidal coomassie or transferred to PVDF membranes and probed with rabbit anti-ApoA-I or custom-made rabbit anti-proApoA-I. Both recombinant proApoA-I and purified ApoA-I samples were pure as no other contaminant proteins were detected. Using the antibody against total ApoA-I, both proApoA-I and ApoA-I were detected while the antibody against proApoA-I only reacted with proApoA-I, confirming the identity of the proteins.

Once the purity of the samples was assessed, we performed a digestion time curve with trypsin (0.5 1, 4, and 18h) for each ApoA-I form to verify the efficiency of the proteolysis measuring the N-terminal peptide. As shown in Figure 2A, the mature ApoA-I digestion occurs fast at the beginning and stabilizes when it reaches 18h of digestion. Regarding proApoA-I, the digestion curve is very similar in terms of digestion kinetics, starting fast and stabilizing by 18h (Figure 2B). As expected, only the N-term peptide corresponding to the mature ApoA-I proteoform (DEPPQSPWDR) was detected upon digesting mature ApoA-I (Figure 2A). Curiously, during the digestion of proApoA-I, we detected not only the N-terminal peptide corresponding to proApoA-I (RHFWQQDEPPQSPWDR) but also the N-terminal tryptic peptide corresponding to mature ApoA-I (DEPPQSPWDR) (Figure 2B). The detection of the DEPPQSPWDR peptide when digesting proApoA-I was surprising as trypsin cleaves after lysine and arginine residues (except if followed by proline);12 thus, it is not expected to cleave after glutamine. The presence of the DEPPQSPWDR peptide corresponding to the N-term of mature ApoA-I represented about 10% of the total after digestion of proApoA-I with trypsin. Further, it was a self-limited effect as increasing the trypsin to protein ratio from 1:20 to 1:5 did not enhance further the reaction leading to the DEPPQSPWDR peptide (Figure 2B).

When digesting mature ApoA-I only the expected N-terminal peptide was detected (DEPPQSPWDR) (A) while during the digestion of the proApoA-I we detected not only the N-terminal peptide corresponding to proApoA-I (RHFWQQDEPPQSPWDR) but also the N-terminal tryptic peptide corresponding to mature ApoA-I (DEPPQSPWDR) (B). The experiments were done in triplicate, and data is represented as mean ± SD.

As no cross-contamination is present in our protein samples (Figure 1), this finding suggests that trypsin produces an erroneous cleavage at the N-terminal site of proApoA-I that involves cleaving at the carboxyl side of glutamine at position 6 thus aberrantly producing the N-terminal peptide of mature ApoA-I (DEPPQSPWDR) that we can detect with our LC-MRM-MS method. To verify the purity of the protein, we measured the intact mass of recombinant proApoA-I using a Q-TOF mass spectrometer. We obtained a predominant mass peak of 29797 Da (±13 Da mass error) (Figure S1) consistent with the proApoA-I plus a C-terminal His-Tag (HHHHHH) used. Some additional masses were identified. Those were less abundant, and none matched N-terminal truncations that could explain the presence of the DEPPQSPWDR peptide when digesting proApoA-I with trypsin (Figure S1). In addition, the full MS/MS spectrum of the DEPPQSPWDR peptide obtained from proApoA-I trypsin digestion was identical to the one obtained from digestion of mature ApoA-I (Figure S2), corroborating our finding.

In order to understand the nature of the event, we digested recombinant proApoA-I with both standard (Recombinant Trypsin, Sigma) and high-specificity trypsin (Trypsin Gold, Promega) for 18h, and the DEPPQSPWDR peptide appeared in both cases in a similar rate (Figure 3A) suggesting that the effect was not dependent on trypsin specificity. We also tested Arg-C protease, which is an endopeptidase that cleaves at the carboxyl side of arginine residues,12 thus producing virtually the same cleavage peptides as trypsin but by a different reaction mechanism. Again, after 18h of digestion of recombinant proApoA-I with Arg-C the DEPPQSPWDR peptide appeared despite the overall reaction being less efficient than when using trypsin (Figure 3B). We also ruled out autoproteolysis by performing the 18h long-reaction protocol without adding any proteolytic enzyme. In this case, as compared to performing the protocol with trypsin, we detected an approximately 500-fold less proApoA-I N-term peptide (RR = 1.97 ± 1.2 vs 878.2 ± 23.7) and 200-fold less mature ApoA-I N-term peptide (RR = 0.37 ± 0.6 vs 76.8 ± 6.8), which demonstrates that both the normal and the abnormal cleavages are enzymatically produced.

One microgram of ProApoA-I was digested with standard and high-specificity trypsin (A) and Arg-C (B) at an enzyme-to-protein ratio of 1:20 for 18h. The proteolytic peptides obtained were analyzed by LC-MRM-MS. Both standard and high-specificity trypsin (A) and Arg-C (B) produced the DEPPQSPWDR peptide beyond the expected N-term peptide RHFWQQDEPPQSPWDR. At this point, we were wondering if the event was dependent only on the AA sequence or if the protein conformation was involved. To this end, we digested 0.5 μg of the synthetic proApoA-I N-term peptide instead of the whole proApoA-I protein during 18h with trypsin. Most of the RHFWQQDEPPQSPWDR peptide was found undigested, and less than a 0.003% was found as the digestion product DEPPQSPWDR peptide. Because synthetic RHFWQQDEPPQSPWDR was not cleaved into DEPPQSPWDR by trypsin, it can be assumed that the aberrant cleavage reaction is dependent on the protein conformation.

As the effect happens not only when using trypsin but also when digesting with Arg-C (Figure 3A) one may think that the site between glutamine at position 6 and aspartic acid at position 7 of proApoA-I is somehow prone to incorrect proteolysis. In fact, it is the natural cleavage site of BMP-1/PCPE-2 to convert proApoA-I into mature ApoA-I (7); thus, the cleavage site might be transiently exposed. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time that this miscleavage produced by trypsin on proApoA-I is described. From a practical point of view, this unexpected finding is relevant because only the N-terminal proteolytic peptides can be used to discriminate mature ApoA-I from proApoA-I and, additionally, ApoA-I precursor is found in low concentration in human blood (approximately 5% of total ApoA-I). Thus, the effect has to be considered when detecting naturally occurring ApoA-I proteoforms that can coexist in human samples by LC-MRM-MS as it may interfere with the correct quantification of both proteoforms, potentially resulting in undetectable ApoA-I precursor. Fortunately, the effect is self-limited (up to a 10% of the total); thus, it would be plausible to apply correction calculations for an accurate quantification of mature ApoA-I and proApoA-I in biological samples.

Conclusion

Trypsin produces a self-limited aberrant cleavage on the proApoA-I precursor that produces the mature ApoA-I proteoform, which is estimated to occur in 10% of the total protein. This effect is not negligible, as it can be detected by LC-MRM-MS. Hence, this unexpected cleavage requires careful consideration when different ApoA-I proteoforms coexisting in biological samples are to be quantified by LC-MRM-MS for diagnostic purposes.
