Montelukast attenuates diclofenac sodium-induced cardiotoxicity in male rats via targeting Wnt/β-catenin pathway
Authors: Sary Alsanea, Shaykhah Albuhayri, Layla Alkharashi, Nemat Ali, Abdullah F AlAsmari, Maha Arafah, Iman Hasan, Rehab Ali, Hala Attia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiclofenac sodium (DCFN) is the most often prescribed NSAID, however its prolonged administration is linked to cardiotoxicity mediated by oxidative stress, inflammation, and apoptosis. The role of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in cardiac injury is well documented; however, it remains unclear whether this pathway is upregulated in the heart during DCFN treatment. This work aims to investigate the cardiac activity of this pathway in DCFN-intoxicated rats and to explore the potential cardioprotective effects of montelukast (MNTL), a leukotriene receptor antagonist. Four groups of male rats were treated as follows for 14 days: control, MNTL control (20 mg/kg, p.o), DCFN (10 mg/kg, i.m), and DCFN+MNTL. Cardiac injury was assessed by serum biomarkers (cardiac troponin-I, creatine kinase-MB, lactate dehydrogenase, and aspartate aminotransferase), oxidative stress markers (malondialdehyde, glutathione, superoxide dismutase), pro-inflammatory cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin-6), as well as apoptotic proteins (Bcl-2 and caspase-3). Protein expression of Wnt-1, active β-catenin, and Frizzled-1 (FZD1) was evaluated, and histological analysis of cardiac tissues was performed. DCFN treatment resulted in significant cardiac injury, characterized by elevated serum cardiac biomarkers, increased oxidative stress and inflammatory cytokines, upregulated apoptotic markers, and overexpression of Wnt-1/active β-catenin and FZD-1. Histological examination revealed myocardial degeneration, vascular compression, and marked inflammatory cell infiltration. Co-treatment with MNTL significantly mitigated these biochemical, molecular, and histopathological alterations. In conclusion, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is activated in the heart during DCFN treatment, suggesting its possible role in its cardiotoxicity. Introduction

Diclofenac sodium (DCFN) is the most often used nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) in the world for management of inflammatory conditions and pain1. It is also used off-label in conditions such as dental pain, myalgia, dysmenorrhea, fever, and migraine, due to its analgesic and antipyretic properties. DCFN mediates its anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting cellular cyclooxygenases, COX-1/COX-2, with a greater efficacy on COX-21. COX-1 and COX-2 catalyze arachidonic acid conversion to thromboxanes and prosta-glandins which are involved in gastric mucosa protection2, regulation of platelet aggregation and the maintenance of cardiac, renal, and hepatic functions1,3. Therefore, as a result of COX inhibition, DCFN use is linked to a number of negative side effects, such as gastrointestinal complications, hepatic, and renal injuries4.

In the last decade, attention has been focused on DCFN cardiac safety in patients with and without cardiac diseases. Clinical trials5 and preclinical studies1,6–8 indicated that long-term treatment with DCFN exerts serious cardiac injuries. This toxicity may be caused by oxidative stress resulted from overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS), inflammatory response, fibrosis as well as apoptosis1,3,6,8. Yet, the precise molecular mechanism that underlies the deleterious effects of DCFN on the heart is still unclear.

The Wingless/integrase-1 (Wnt)/β-catenin pathway is critical for cellular growth9. Wnt proteins consist of 19 glycoproteins that are responsible for the activation of many intracellular signaling10. There are two main pathways of Wnt signaling; one of them is the canonical pathway. This pathway depends on essential cofactor named; β-catenin, which contributes to nuclear signal transduction of Wnt-specific genes that regulate cell differentiation, growth, and migration11. In non-activated canonical Wnt pathway, β-catenin is typically phosphorylated and destructed via a multiprotein destruction complex, while, the pathway is activated when the pathway ligands, Wnt3a and Wnt1 bind to one of Frizzled receptors (FZD1 to FZD10) on the cell membrane leading to the activation of the receptors LRP5 and LRP6, members of the low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein family, which inhibits β-catenin complex. As a result, β-catenin becomes stabilized and translocated into nucleus, leading to the activation of the transcription factor TCF/LEF (T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor-1) to initiate Wnt-dependent gene transcription11.

It has been noted that, the activation of canonical Wnt pathway is associated with numerous cardiac injuries such as myocardial infarction, arrhythmic cardiomyopathy and cardiac fibrosis11. Activated pathway induces cardiac injury by elevating production of ROS, pro-inflammatory mediators and fibrotic proteins as well as, upregulating caspases levels12–16. Inhibiting Wnt/β-catenin pathway has shown promising cardioprotective effects in various preclinical models of cardiotoxicity via modulating the aforementioned mechanisms11–15. However, to date, it is unknown whether Wnt/ β-catenin is activated in the heart of DCFN-intoxicated rats. We hypothesize that DCFN-induced cardiotoxicity is associated with the activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and this activation may play a role in the cardiac oxidative stress, inflammation, fibrosis, and apoptosis induced by DCFN. Therefore, this pathway might be a novel protective target against such toxicity, and agents that suppress Wnt/β-catenin signaling may have promising cardioprotective effects against DCFN-induced injury.

Montelukast (MNTL, Singulair, Broncast) is a selective leukotriene receptor antagonist that is approved by FDA for prophylaxis and management of asthma, seasonal allergic rhinitis, and many inflammatory disorders. MNTL demonstrated beneficial effects against different models of cardiac damage17–19. In these studies, MNTL exerts its cardioprotective effects via various mechanisms including the attenuation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress, and cardiac cell apoptosis. Interestingly, MNTL has anti-inflammatory effects in arthritis induced in mice by collagen20 and sodium monourate21, which may benefit when used with DCFN indicated for rheumatic patients. Recently, MNTL has been reported to reduce the gastric damage associated with the use of NSAIDs and to potentiate the antiinflammatory effects induced by DCFN22. Additionally, early results showed that MNTL might protect against asthma via attenuating Wnt7b and β-catenin expression23. According to all previous beneficial effects, the present work was conducted to investigate the cardioprotective effect of MNTL in DCFN-induced cardiotoxicity and whether these effects are mediated through the suppression of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in rats.

Materials and methods

Animals and drugs

A total of 32 male Wistar albino rats (190–250 g) were obtained from College of Pharmacy Animal Care Center at the King Saud University campus in Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia.

Animals were housed as four rats per cage under standard laboratory conditions (temperature: 25 ± 1 °C; 12-h light/dark cycle). Rats had unrestricted access to food and tap water throughout the study period. All procedures involving animals were conducted in compliance with ethical standards set by the Research Ethics Committees. The study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org) and was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of King Saud University under approval number KSU-SE-23-11.

Diclofenac sodium (DCFN) vials were sourced from a retail pharmacy in Riyadh (Novartis Pharmaceuticals Co., London, UK). Montelukast (MNTL; Broncast, tablets 10 mg, Avalon Pharmaceuticals Co. Ltd., KSA) was obtained from the outpatient pharmacy of the University Hospital.

Experimental design

Following a week of acclimation, the rats were allocated to four experimental groups (eight rats per group) and treated once daily for 14 days as follows: Normal Control rats were injected intramuscularly with an equivalent volume of normal saline (0.9% NaCl; the vehicle of DCFN and MNTL); MNTL Control: rats were MNTL treated via oral gavage (20 mg/kg/day, dissolved in normal saline)17,19,22; DCFN model group: Intramuscularly injections were administered to the rats with DCFN (10 mg/kg/day, dissolved into normal saline)7,22 and DCFN+MNTL group: Rats were received MNTL concomitantly with DCFN at the same doses. The dose of MNTL was selected based on previous studies17–19 which demonstrate valuable cardioprotective effects of MNTL at this dose against doxorubicin-, arsenic trioxide-, and lipopolysaccharide -induced cardiotoxicity, respectively. Khodir et al.19 found that MNTL at 20 mg/kg was more effective than 10 mg/kg in ameliorating cardiac injury. The dose of DCFN and treatment duration were chosen based on the work of Dogra et al7. The doses of MNTL and DCFN were also selected based on a previous study22 in which co-administration of MNTL (20 mg/kg) with DCFN (10 mg/kg) attenuates the inflammatory insult and gastric damage induced by DCFN, and enhances the anti-inflammatory effects of DCFN on formalin-induced paw edema/inflammation.

Blood collection and preparation of sera and heart homogenate

At the end of the 14 days, all rats were weighed and anesthetized with CO2. The CO2 cylinder was connected to a chamber where the animals were placed. The CO2 displacement rate used is 10% of chamber volume per minute (cv/min) and gradually increased to 30%. These displacement rates are recommended to prevent pain resulting from exposure to high concentrations of CO2 (> 50%) before loss of consciousness. CO2 causes rapid loss of consciousness. Immediately after CO2 use, blood was obtained from the trunk by decapitation. Blood samples were then allowed to clot and centrifuged for 20 min at 3000 rpm at 4 °C to isolate sera for subsequent cardiac biomarker analyses. Hearts were promptly excised, washed with normal saline to clear residual blood, and then weighed. Each heart was sectioned into three parts: one portion was mechanically homogenized with ice-cold normal saline, with the resulting supernatants kept (at − 80 °C) for analysis of ROS-induced stress, inflammatory, and apoptotic markers; a second portion was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for western blot assays; and another portion was fixed in 10% formalin for histological evaluation.

Heart weight (HW)/body weight (BW) ratio calculation

The final body weight and excised heart weight of each rat were carefully measured. HW (milligrams) was divided by the corresponding BW (grams) to calculate the ratio. The HW/BW ratio serves as a physiological indicator of cardiac hypertrophy and was used to assess potential structural changes in response to the experimental treatments.

Assessment of serum-based cardiac injury biomarkers

Serum concentrations of cardiac-specific biomarkers, including cardiac troponin-I (cTn-I) and creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) were determined using sandwich ELISA kits (MyBiosource Inc., San Diego, US) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentrations were calculated based on a standard curve generated from known reference values. In addition, serum activities of AST (aspartate aminotransferase) and LDH (lactate dehydrogenase), which serve as non-specific indicators of cardiac injury, were evaluated using diagnostic kits supplied by Linear Chemicals (Barcelona, Spain) and Stanbio Laboratory (Boerne, TX, USA), respectively.

Assay of oxidative stress indices in cardiac tissue homogenate

Malondialdehyde (MDA), a lipid peroxidation byproduct, increases in response to oxidative stress and serves as a reliable marker for lipid membrane damage. Cardiac MDA levels were measured following the method described by Ohkawa et al.24, which involves the formation of a thiobarbituric acid (TBA)-reactive complex in acidic conditions at 95 °C. The resulting pink chromogen was quantified spectrophotometrically against a reagent blank at 535 nm. Reduced glutathione (GSH), a key non-enzymatic antioxidant, reflects intracellular redox balance. GSH content in heart homogenates was estimated using the protocol of Moron et al.25 in which Ellman’s reagent reacts with sulfhydryl groups to produce a yellow-colored compound measured at 412 nm. The activity of total superoxide dismutase (SOD) was determined by its ability to inhibit the autoxidation of pyrogallol at pH 8.026.

Determination of inflammatory cytokines and apoptotic markers in cardiac homogenate

Cardiac levels of the inflammatory mediators including interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), along with the cleaved caspase-3 and Bcl-2 (as markers of apoptosis) were quantified using ELISA kits purchased form MyBiosource Inc, (San Diego, US) in accordance with manufacturer guidelines.

Histopathological examination

Following euthanasia, cardiac tissue samples were promptly fixed in 10% formalin and then embedded in paraffin wax after dehydration in graded ethanol concentrations. Using a rotary microtome, 4 μm-thick sections were prepared. These sections were then deparaffinized in a 60 °C oven for one hour, cleared in xylene for 10 min, and rehydrated through a descending ethanol series. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess cardiac tissue architecture, and with Masson’s trichrome to detect collagen deposition and myocardial fibrosis. Histological features were evaluated under a light microscope.

Images of Masson trichrome-stained sections were analyzed using Fiji software to evaluate the percentage and optical density of the stain.

Immunoblotting assay

Proteins were extracted from frozen heart tissue with RIPA buffer, having both protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and total protein levels were measured via the BCA technique. Homogenized samples (40 mg tissue) were processed for western blot analysis to assess Wnt-1, active (non-phosphorylated) β-catenin, and FZD1 expression. After mixing 50 μg of protein with Laemmli buffer and denaturation, samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. Then the membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat dry milk, incubated with primary antibodies (1:1000; Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, and then treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000; CellSignaling). Protein bands were detected with ECL reagents and captured via imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ and normalized to β-actin, with results expressed as fold change relative to the control group.

Statistical analysis

All values are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). One-way ANOVA was used to evaluate group differences, followed by the Tukey–Kramer post-hoc test. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism V9 (San Diego, US), and significance was defined as P-values less than 0.05.

Results

Effect of MNTL on the serum cardiac biomarkers in DCFN-induced cardiotoxicity

Following two weeks of treatment, serum levels of cTn-I and CK-MB were assessed due to their high sensitivity and specificity as markers of myocardial injury. Additionally, serum activities of LDH and AST were evaluated to reflect the extent of cardiac damage. As shown in Fig. 1, DCFN exposure led to a significant rise in CK-MB and cTn-I levels (P < 0.001 and P < 0.01, respectively) when compared to the control group. Likewise, AST and LDH activities were markedly elevated in the DCFN-treated rats (P < 0.01 and P < 0.0001, respectively). Notably, co-administration of MNTL significantly mitigated these elevations, reducing CK-MB and cTn-I levels (P < 0.01 for both), as well as AST (P < 0.01) and LDH activities (P < 0.05).

Effect of MNTL on the histopathological changes in H&E-stained cardiac sections in DCFN-induced cardiotoxicity

Cardiac tissue sections from the normal control group (Fig. 2, panel a) and MNTL control group (panel b) displayed preserved myocardial architecture, characterized by intact muscle fibers (star), clearly defined nuclei (arrows), and normal blood vessels (bv). Effect of MNTL on the HW/BW ratio in DCFN-induced cardiotoxicity

The HW/BW ratio was used as an indicator of cardiac hypertrophy, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Discussion

Despite the efficiency of DCFN in the management of inflammatory conditions; its long-term use has been associated with many adverse effects on various organs including the heart. The Wnt/β-catenin pathway is essential for cardiomyocyte proliferation, homeostasis of myocardial tissue, and the cardiac development. In adult heart, Wnt/β-catenin signaling is adversely upregulated in cardiac injury and worsen the case which subsequently affects physiological cardiac responses12,14,15. In the current study, we found, for the first time, that the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is upregulated in the hearts of rats intoxicated with DCFN, and this upregulation was attenuated by concomitant MNTL treatment.

In this investigation, the cardiotoxicity induced by DCFN was manifested by the elevations of serum levels of CK-MB and cTn-I; the specific heart damage indicators as well as the increased serum activities of AST and LDH; non-specific markers. This elevation indicates cardiac cell damage and increased cell membrane permeability leading to the release of myocardial cell contents and enzymes27. This damage could arise from the overproduction of ROS, increased lipid peroxidation, and insufficient antioxidants resulting in oxidation of cell membrane phospholipids, cell membrane damage and cell injury. This cardiac injury may be also linked to the greater COX-2 inhibition and the imbalance in the products of COX-1/COX-2 pathways leading to an increased risk of cardiac side effects28. The DCFN-induced cardiac injury is confirmed by H&E staining which exhibited degenerated myocardial cells and mononuclear infiltrations. Our findings agree with those of Dogra et al.7, who found that administration of DCFN (10 mg/kg) for fifteen days significantly elevated the CK-MB, LDH and AST serum levels. Furthermore, elevation of CK-MB, cTn-1, LDH and AST after administration of DCFN were also observed in previous works29. In the present study, the increase in CK-MB, cTn-I, AST, and LDH were markedly suppressed in rats co-treated with MNTL, reflecting its cardioprotective effect, which was confirmed by the H&E staining where few degenerated myocardium cells and less edema and blood vessels compression were observed in MNTL-treated rats. These beneficial effects may be attributed to the antioxidant properties of MNTL, which results in the stabilization of cell membranes and restriction of membrane permeability. Cardioprotective effects of MNTL in the term of reducing serum enzymes were also proven in other models of cardiotoxicity17–19.

Myocardial cells degeneration and mononuclear cells infiltration are typical characteristics of DCFN-induced cardiotoxicity29 which is in agreement with our results. In our study, histological examination revealed degeneration of cardiomyocytes surrounded by mononuclear inflammatory cell infiltrates, accompanied by swollen muscle fibers in the heart tissue. This swelling may explain the high HW/BW ratio detected in DCFN-intoxicated rats. Co-treatment with MNTL markedly improved the myocardial degeneration and the mononuclear infiltration and reduced the muscle fiber swelling, which is accompanied with a significant reduction in HW/BW ratio which confirms its cardioprotective properties. Previous works showed advantageous cardioprotective role with MNTL administration in other models of cardiotoxicity, that is in the form of preserving tissue morphology and improvement of inflammatory cell infiltration in cardiac damage induced by LPS19, arsenic trioxide18, and doxorubicin17. This improvement was accompanied by a complete decrease in HW/BW ratio19. ICG001, an inhibitor of β-catenin-mediated gene expression, was seen to effectively diminished HW/BW ratio, and hypertrophy in the fibrotic mouse heart12, this finding suggests that the suppression of Wnt/β-catenin signaling, as seen in our study, may contribute to the reduced hypertrophy induced by MNTL.

It is approved that DCFN-induces cardiac damage through oxidative stress3,7,8,29 which is reflected in our work by the increase in MDA levels accompanying a decreased cardiac levels of GSH and activities of SOD. Consistent to our results, administration of DCFN (10mg/kg) for fifteen days significantly elevated MDA and reduced the GSH levels in the cardiac tissue7. In the current study, concomitant treatment with MNTL significantly attenuated the increased cardiac levels of MDA and corrected the cardiac antioxidants (GSH and SOD); suggesting that MNTL exerts its cardioprotective effects on DCFN-cardiotoxicity via preserving oxidant/antioxidant balance. This antioxidant properties of MNLT were also beneficial in protecting the heart against oxidative injury in other models17–19.

While DCFN suppresses inflammation, its chronic administration is accompanied by significant increase in pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as interferon-gamma, IL-6 and TNF-α7,8. These findings agree with our results where DCFN significantly augmented the myocardial expression of TNF-α and IL-6, suggesting its inflammatory response which has a critical role in the associated cardiotoxicity6. This cardiac inflammation is probably a subsequent to oxidative stress8, or due to the activation of immune system after the cell damage induced by DCFN2,3,8,13. The current study could add a novel mechanism of the DCFN-induced inflammatory response which is the activation of canonical Wnt pathway. This pathway could promote inflammatory cell recruitment and activate nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)12, both will stimulate high production of inflammatory cytokines. Treatment with MNTL concomitantly with DCFN markedly attenuated the cardiac levels of TNF-α and IL-6, reflecting its anti-inflammatory effects in this model, possibly via its antioxidant properties as observed in our study or due to its inhibitory effects on NF-κB as reported in other models of cardiac toxicity17–19. According to our work, the suppression of Wnt pathway may be another new mechanism of MNTL’s anti-inflammatory effects on the heart.

Myocardial fibrosis represents a critical component of adverse cardiac remodeling following inflammatory insults. In the present study, DCFN administration was associated with marked myocardial collagen deposition, as demonstrated by Masson’s trichrome staining, indicating fibrotic remodeling. These findings are consistent with previous reports showing that administration of DCFN promotes cardiac fibrosis secondary to sustained oxidative stress, inflammation, and cardiomyocyte injury8,29. Moreover, activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway may be implicated in cardiac fibrosis11,12. Importantly, co-treatment with MNTL significantly attenuated DCFN-induced myocardial fibrosis, suggesting a protective effect against extracellular matrix remodeling. This antifibrotic effect of MNTL may be related to the observed reduction in inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress. Attenuation of Wnt-1, active β-catenin, and FZD-1 expression observed in MNTL-treated rats may also be linked to the reduced fibrotic response.

The mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress induced by DCFN results in apoptosis in cardiac cells3. The mitochondrial-dependent apoptotic pathway mediates the activation of caspase-3, the executioner apoptotic caspase, and is tightly controlled by the Bcl-2 family, consisting of both survival-promoting (e.g., Bcl-2) and apoptosis-inducing (e.g., BAX) proteins. Pro/anti-apoptotic molecules interact and balance each other, so these effectors intensely influence the cells fate30. In the present research, injection of DCFN led to increased cardiac levels of caspase-3 along with reduced Bcl-2 levels which suggest its apoptotic role on the heart and support the previous works3,6,29. Importantly, β-catenin has been shown to exert pro-apoptotic effect in previous works31,32, thus, the high expression of β-catenin in the current work may contribute to the elevated caspase-3 and decreased Bcl-2 induced by DCFN injection. This abnormality in caspase-3 and Bcl-2 levels were significantly corrected by the co-treatment with MNTL, probably via preserving the oxidant/antioxidant system in the cardiac tissue as well as the suppression of Wnt/catenin pathway as revealed in our study.

In the next step, we investigated the cardiac levels of the components of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, which is a common pathogenic mediator of the heart following myocardial injury16 and it has been identified as one of the key mechanisms in heart failure pathogenesis12,15. Wnt-1 is the initial component of Wnt/β-catenin pathway expressed in the region of injury and is highly upregulated after cardiac injury33. β-catenin is a cytoplasmic secondary messenger of Wnt signaling pathway. The upregulation of β-catenin active form in cardiomyocytes can induce cardiac hypertrophic growth in vitro and in vivo12 which could explain the higher HW/BW ratio detected in the current study. The expression of FZD genes has been elevated during myoblast proliferation and migration after myocardial injury34. In addition, in infracted heart, the up-regulation of FZD receptors has been enhanced35. Wnt1, and β-catenin are highly expressed in the cardiac tissue in type 2 cardiorenal syndrome indicating their implication in cardiac injury16. In the current study, the protein levels of Wnt-1, active β-catenin, and FZD-1 were upregulated in the cardiac tissue of DCFN-intoxicated rats, suggesting a possible role for the canonical Wnt signaling in the cardiotoxic effects of DCFN. This upregulated expression could contribute to aggravated oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, hypertrophy, and fibrosis associated with DCFN cardiotoxicity. It has been reported that increased TNF-α concentrations upregulate more β-catenin and its downstream target genes and exaggerated cardiac injury16.

Downregulation of canonical Wnt pathway elicits cardioprotective effects against different models of cardiac injury, including isoproterenol-induced cardiotoxicity in rats14, huoxin induced- acute myocardial ischemia15, sepsis-induced cardiac dysfunction13 and heart failure resulting from transverse aortic banding12. The inhibition of the canonical Wnt pathway by SEN195, a small-molecular inhibitor of β-catenin/TCF-dependent gene transcription, alleviated the post-ischemic remodelling process, as demonstrated by the significant preservation of cardiac function, reduction in infarct scar size and the increase of peri-infarct arteriolar density compared to the untreated animals36. In our study, co-treatment with MNTL significantly alleviated the protein levels of Wnt-1, β-catenin, and FZD receptors, suggesting an association between the cardioprotective effects of MNTL and attenuation of Wnt/β-catenin pathway activation, along with reductions in oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and fibrosis.

It has been reported that inhibition of Wnt-1/β-catenin canonical pathway and the decrease in β-catenin minimizes the cardiac damage through reducing the monocytes recruitment, preventing the inflammatory cytokines release, and inhibiting the oxidative stress and the apoptotic cell death cascade12–14. Therefore, in the current study, the downregulation of Wnt1, β-catenin, and FZD1 may contribute to the beneficial effects exerted by MNTL involving minimizing mononuclear infiltration and cardiac injury, reducing lipid peroxidation, restoring antioxidant defense system and attenuating the apoptotic and inflammatory markers.

Finally, and from a translational perspective, MNTL is an orally administered leukotriene receptor antagonist with a well-established safety profile in humans and widespread clinical use for asthma and allergic rhinitis. MNTL exhibits high oral bioavailability, extensive plasma protein binding, and a relatively long elimination half-life (approximately 2.7–5.5 h), allowing once-daily dosing37. The standard approved human dose (10 mg/day) has been shown to achieve systemic concentrations sufficient to exert anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects beyond leukotriene receptor antagonism37. Importantly, several preclinical studies have reported pleiotropic cardiovascular and anti-inflammatory benefits of MNTL at therapeutically relevant doses17–19. However, direct extrapolation of the cardioprotective dose used in rats to humans is limited by interspecies differences in pharmacokinetics and drug metabolism. Therefore, while the present findings suggest a potential cardioprotective role for MNTL during DCFN therapy, future studies are required to define the optimal dose, exposure–response relationship, and clinical relevance of these effects in humans.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the current study demonstrates for the first time that activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is associated with DCFN-induced cardiotoxicity, and this activation may play a role in the cardiac injury induced by DCFN. Our study also provides the first evidence of the potential cardioprotective effect of MNTL against DCFN-induced cardiotoxicity in a rat model. Our findings also provide a mechanistic perspective to this protective effect through alleviation of the oxidative stress, inflammation, and pro-apoptotic effects, which may be mediated, at least in part, via downregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. Current observations provide a new guidance for the experimental and clinical management of cardiac toxicity following the prolonged use of DCFN.

Limitations and future directions

This study demonstrated the upregulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, suggesting its possible role in DCFN-induced cardiotoxicity. However, the specific role of this pathway should be validated using a selective Wnt inhibitor. Therefore, future investigations employing pharmacological inhibitors, such as pyrvinium, are warranted to confirm the mechanistic involvement and to explore potential therapeutic targets more precisely.

An important limitation of the present study is the exclusive use of male rats. Sex is recognized as a critical biological variable in cardiovascular research, as males and females differ in cardiac physiology, inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and susceptibility to drug-induced cardiotoxicity37,38. Estrogen, specifically, exerts cardioprotective effects through modulating oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis, and intracellular signaling pathways, including the Wnt/β-catenin pathway39,40. Therefore, the magnitude and mechanisms of DCFN-induced cardiotoxicity, as well as the cardioprotective efficacy of MNTL, may differ in female rats. The use of male rats in this study was intended to minimize hormonal variability and to allow a more controlled assessment of molecular mechanisms. However, these findings cannot be directly extrapolated to females. Future studies incorporating female rats and evaluating sex-specific responses are warranted to enhance the translational relevance of the current findings.

Another limitation of the present study is the lack of assessment of cardiac function, such as echocardiographic or hemodynamic measurements. While data on cardiac function would provide additional confirmation of cardiotoxicity/cardioprotection, the current work was designed to focus on biochemical, histopathological, and molecular endpoints that are well-established indicators of myocardial injury and cardioprotection. Future studies incorporating direct cardiac functional assessments are warranted to substantiate the biochemical/histological/molecular evidence of cardioprotection and to further validate the translational significance of these findings.

Furthermore, although myocardial fibrosis was quantitatively assessed in the present study, cardiomyocyte apoptosis was evaluated using biochemical markers without direct histological quantification. Future investigations incorporating cell-specific apoptosis assays, such as TUNEL staining would provide additional spatial and cellular resolution of apoptotic remodeling.
