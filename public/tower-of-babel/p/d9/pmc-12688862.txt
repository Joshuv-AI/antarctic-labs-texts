Unmasking benign intradural-extramedullary spinal tumors: a systematic review on treatment outcomes and radiological features
Authors: Wojciech Czyżewski, Michał Szymoniuk, Jakub Litak, Jan Sobstyl, Klaudia Kus-Budzynska, Barbara Buchalska, Bipin Chaurasia, Tomasz Mandat, Kamil Torres, Grzegorz Staśkiewicz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Benign intradural extramedullary (IDEM) spinal tumors constitute about 65% of primary spinal neoplasms. Certain primary malignant or metastatic intradural lesions, although infrequent, may resemble their primary counterparts. Moreover, despite significant advances in the operative techniques, morbidity following treatment of IDEM tumors remains a considerable problem. Therefore, the current review aimed to summarize the most important data about radiological features of IDEM tumors on MRI and discuss optimal treatment of these lesions.Materials and methods:A systematic search was conducted based on MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Clinical Trial.gov, CENTRAL, EBSCO, and Embase according to the PRISMA statement on 8 July 2024. Extracted data were synthesized only qualitatively since the obtained data were not suitable for conducting the meta-analysis.Results:A total of 1003 records were identified in the database search. After considering eligibility criteria, 33 articles were further analyzed, of which 5 discussed diagnostic aspects and 28 evaluated treatment outcomes.Conclusion:The multidisciplinary approach, combining advanced diagnostic imaging, surgical innovation, radiation therapy, and emerging technologies, provides a comprehensive framework for the effective management of intradural extramedullary tumors. Continued research and clinical trials are essential to refine these strategies, enhance diagnostic precision, and optimize treatment outcomes for patients with spinal tumors.

Introduction

Spinal tumors constitute around 15% of central nervous system (CNS) tumors[1]. These tumors can be categorized according to malignancy (benign or malignant), origin (primary or metastatic), anatomical localization with regard to the dura mater (intradural or extradural), and spinal cord (intramedullary or extramedullary). Primary spinal neoplasms exhibit a relatively low incidence rate, accounting for approximately 2–4% (CNS), with an estimated 65% manifesting as intradural-extramedullary (IDEM) lesions[2]. Most frequent IDEM tumors include meningiomas, schwannomas, and neurofibromas[3]. Among the less commonly encountered nonmalignant IDEM tumors are ependymomas, hemangiomas, lipomas, paragangliomas, vascular neoplasms, myxomas of nerve sheaths, and other exceptionally rare entities, including the seldom-described glomus tumors of the spinal canal[4].HIGHLIGHTSDespite significant advances in the operative techniques, morbidity following treatment of IDEM tumors remains a considerable problem. Therefore, the current review aimed to summarize the most important data about radiological features of IDEM tumors on MRI and discuss optimal treatment of these lesions.The multidisciplinary approach, combining advanced diagnostic imaging, surgical innovation, radiation therapy, and emerging technologies, provides a comprehensive framework for the effective management of intradural extramedullary tumors.Continued studies are essential to refine these strategies, enhance diagnostic precision, and optimize treatment outcomes for patients.

IDEM tumors may cause significant spinal cord compression, leading to pain, loss of bladder control, and neurological deficits in sensory and motor functions[5]. Although the clinical image of IDEM tumors may not invariably demonstrate pathognomonic traits, and their presentation most commonly is related to the affected spinal cord level, the utilization of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) frequently facilitates the recognition of essential characteristic features. Given the anatomical proximity to spinal nerves or the dura mater, schwannomas and meningiomas emerge as the predominant primary tumors of the spinal canal[6]. Certain malignant primary[7] or[8] intradural lesions, although infrequent, may resemble their primary counterparts. However, their initial management differs as it typically requires adjunct oncological treatment. Therefore, enhancing the diagnostic efficacy of MRI and improving knowledge about radiological features suggesting malignant lesions at the preoperative stage would be valuable for avoiding diagnostic mistakes, which aggravate treatment outcomes in consequence.

At present, total microsurgical resection of the tumor and decompression of the spinal canal constitute the gold standard for the management of IDEM tumors[9]. However, despite remarkable improvements in the operative techniques and neuroimaging of IDEM tumors observed in recent decades, morbidity following treatment remains a considerable problem[10]. As recent studies found, the use of intraoperative neuromonitoring, minimally invasive surgical techniques, and stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) as an additional component of treatment may decrease postoperative neurological deficits and improve treatment outcomes[11].

The current systematic review aimed to summarize the most important data about radiological features on MRI and the utility of MRI in the diagnosis of IDEM tumors. A critical aspect of this review is the potential for malignant spinal tumors to radiologically mimic benign ones, complicating diagnosis and treatment strategies. Furthermore, optimal treatment of these lesions was discussed. By synthesizing recent advancements and clinical trial data, we aim to provide a thorough understanding of the therapeutic landscape, emphasizing best practices and future directions in managing benign spinal tumors.

Materials and methods

Study design

The current systematic review complies with the standards of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) Statement (Figure 1) and AMSTAR (Assessing the Methodological Quality of Systematic Reviews) Guidelines (supplemental Digital Content Files 1, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/A967 and supplemental Digital Content 2, available at: http://links.lww.com/MS9/A968, respectively)Figure 1.A flowchart demonstrating the process of studies selection according to the PRISMA statement.

A flowchart demonstrating the process of studies selection according to the PRISMA statement.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria for the systematic review were as follows: (1) studies recruiting patients with benign, intradural-extramedullary spinal tumors; (2) studies focusing on radiological features or treatment outcomes of spinal tumors; (3) study design as the following: randomized controlled trial, single-arm study, case series, observational study; (4) article type as the following: conference proceeding and clinical protocol. Studies were excluded from the systematic review when fulfilled at least one of the following exclusion criteria: (1) sample size of <10 patients (2) articles written in a language other than English; (3) studies on pediatric populations; (4) studies on animal models; (5) preclinical studies; (6) article type as the following: review, systematic review, meta-analysis, case report, editorial, erratum, corrigendum.

Search strategy and article selection process

The study search was conducted on 8 July 2024 using MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Clinical Trial.gov, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), EBSCO (CINAHL Ultimate), and Embase databases. During the search, the following terms were used in combination with Boolean operators (AND, OR): “benign,” “intradural,” “extramedullary,” “spinal tumors,” “intradural extramedullary,” “spinal cord neoplasm,” “spinal cord tumor.” The search strategies were modified to suit each specific database. Obtained records were included or excluded according to the eligibility criteria by one of the authors (M.S.). Any uncertainties regarding article inclusion were consulted with other authors. No automation tools were used during the article selection process. Searches were supplemented by checking reference lists.

Data extraction

For qualified studies focusing on diagnostic aspects of IDEM tumors, extracted data included: (1) study and year of publication; (2) country of origin; (3) study design; (4) sample size; (5) main findings. In the case of eligible articles assessing treatment modalities and their outcomes, data extracted from each paper included: (1) study and year of publication; (2) country of origin; (3) study design; (4) sample size; (5) median length of follow-up; (6) intervention; (7) primary outcome; (8) results. No automation tools were used during the data extraction process.

Risk of bias assessment

The Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) checklist [https://www.acpjournals.org/doi/10.7326/0003-4819-155-8-201110180-00009] was used to assess the risk of bias of diagnostic studies. According to QUADAS-2, studies were classified as having a high, low, or unclear risk of bias across four key domains: patient selection, index test, reference standard, and flow and timing. A study was rated as low risk if all signaling questions were answered with “yes”; otherwise, it indicated potential bias. If there was not enough information to make a judgment, the risk was labeled as unclear.

For interventional studies, risk of bias was evaluated using the Methodological Index for Nonrandomized Studies (MINORS) developed by Slim et al[12] [https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12956787/]. This assessment tool consists of 12 items for comparative studies, with the first 8 items also applicable to noncomparative studies. Each item is rated on a scale of 0 (not reported), 1 (reported but inadequate), or 2 (reported and adequate), leading to a maximum possible score of 16 for noncomparative studies and 24 for comparative studies.

Data synthesis

The data extracted from each eligible study were qualitatively synthesized in tables using Microsoft Excel software and the main text of the article. Due to high variability between included studies in terms of study design, objectives, and reported outcomes, a meta-analysis was not conducted.

Funding of included studies

Information regarding funding sources for the studies included in this review was extracted whenever available. However, the majority of the analyzed articles did not clearly report their sources of financial support or potential sponsorship. As such, the influence of funding on study outcomes could not be comprehensively assessed, representing a limitation of the current review.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1003 records were identified in the database search. Following the removal of duplicates, titles, and abstracts of 407 records were screened, from which 72 were searched for full-text obtainment. A total of 66 full-text articles were retrieved. After the full-text screening, 29 records were qualified in the current systematic review and 37 records were excluded due to the following reasons: necessary data impossible to obtain from the article (11), ineligible aim of the study (8), ineligible article type (7), language other than English (5), too small sample size (5). The screening of reference lists of included articles revealed an additional four eligible records. Finally, 33 articles were included in the current systematic review, of which 5 discussed diagnostic aspects and 28 evaluated treatment outcomes.

Treatment of benign IDEM tumors

The characteristics of studies reporting on treatment outcomes of benign IDEM tumors qualified in the current systematic review are shown in Table 3. The total number of patients analyzed by eligible studies was 3444, including 2810 subjects with IDEM tumors. Five studies assessed the treatment effectiveness of SRS with or without surgical resection. In the remaining 23 studies, the authors treated the patients by surgery only, and six of these studies used additional intraoperative neuromonitoring. Mean follow-up in qualified studies ranged between 0.08 and 7.7 years. Twenty-three studies were conducted retrospectively, whereas five studies were prospective. Discussion

Radiological diagnosis of IDEM tumors

Radiological examination serves as the primary diagnostic approach for all intradural extramedullary tumors, with gadolinium-enhanced MRI being the imaging modality of choice. It also serves as a highly precise, nonintrusive, secure, and convenient imaging method as it proves invaluable for timely detection, strategizing treatment, and assisting in surgical biopsy procedures[13].

Meningiomas mainly develop in the thoracic spine and are located lateral to the spinal cord, and 60–70% present the dural tail sign[14]. Typically, present symptoms are associated with compression of the spinal cord or spinal nerve roots. MRI most commonly reveals T1: Typically isointense to slightly hypointense, potentially heterogeneous, T2: Generally isointense to slightly hyperintense, T1 with contrast: shows moderate homogeneous enhancement[15]. Calcified meningiomas may appear hypointense on T1 and T2, with minimal contrast enhancement[16].

Neurofibromas and schwannomas, though similar, have distinct features. Schwannomas frequently cause radicular pain and sensory issues and are often associated with adjacent bony remodeling[17]. They often exhibit vascular changes, hemorrhage, and fatty degeneration, which are rare in neurofibromas[18]. Signal characteristics include: T1: 75% isointense, 25% hypointense, T2: >95% hyperintense, often mixed, T1 C +: Virtually all enhance[19]. Neurofibromas typically manifest as compact tumors, often displaying a fusiform or spherical morphology and typically enveloping rather than displacing nerve roots[20]. On MRI, they appear isointense on T1-weighted images and markedly hyperintense on T2-weighted images, exhibiting pronounced gadolinium enhancement, typically homogeneously, although sometimes peripherally[21]. Plexiform neurofibromas typically present as substantial masses with a curved structure, resulting in a distinctive “bag of worms” appearance[13]. Melanotic schwannomas diverge and exhibit, in 64.7 and 70.6% of cases, respectively, hyperintensity on T1-weighted images and hypointensity on T2-weighted images[22].

Primary melanomas of the spine exhibit T1 hyperintensity on MRI due to paramagnetic free radicals[23]. Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNST) mimic schwannoma imaging but demonstrate a more aggressive demeanor. They exhibit characteristics such as size, peripheral enhancement, surrounding edema-like area, and internal cystic lesions. In cases linked to neurofibromatosis 1, heterogeneity on T1-weighted images is vital for distinguishing between neurofibroma and MPNST[24].

Spinal solitary fibrous tumors (SFTs) are rare, accounting for about 10% of CNS SFTs. They typically appear as oval lesions on MRI, with iso- to hypointensity on T1-weighted images and hyperintensity on T2-weighted images[25]. Malignant variants may have less-defined margins and heterogeneous enhancement on gadolinium. Large feeding arteries observed on angiography or as vascular voids on MR imaging can help narrow the diagnosis[26]. Within the cauda equina region, paraganglioma hemangioblastoma, spinal PNET, and myxopapillary ependymoma originate from the filum terminale, each displaying distinctive imaging characteristics[27].

Spinal metastases, predominantly vertebral and epidural, occur in about 20% of cancer patients, with intradural and extramedullary involvement being rare (5%)[8]. Lung (48%) and breast (16%) cancers are the most common sources. Differentiating primary spinal cord tumors from metastases via imaging alone is challenging; however, clinical factors like known primary cancer, rapid neurological decline, and concurrent systemic lesions can assist in diagnosis[28].

In a conventional MRI evaluation of a glomus tumor of the spine, discernible characteristics are evident. Specifically, T1-weighted images depict a well-defined hypointense nodule, whereas T2-weighted images illustrate hyperintensity[29]. Following intravenous administration of gadolinium contrast, pronounced enhancement is observed on T1-weighted images. This contrast examination additionally facilitates the identification of a sheath enveloping the majority of glomus tumors, delineated by a low signal intensity margin[30]. Of note is the resemblance observed in MRI characteristics between glomangioma and schwannoma. Nevertheless, a distinctive attribute is discernible with the presence of a signal-void fistular lesion within the tumor, a feature documented in MRI assessments of several glomus tumor cases but notably absent in schwannomas[31]. However, the limited number of documented patients precludes definitive categorization of this observation as a consistent hallmark.

Sathyanathan et al[32] investigated intradural spinal cord lesions using magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) and correlated the results with histo-pathological examination (HPE). While routine MRI alone detected 58% of cases, combining it with MRS increased detection to 84%. Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference (P = 0.007) between conventional MRI and MRS. However, the study specifically concentrated on intramedullary lesions. The authors concluded that MRS of the spinal cord enhances research and diagnosis by providing supplementary information to existing imaging techniques. Additionally, it offers valuable biomarker data for distinguishing between benign and malignant lesions, potentially reducing unnecessary procedures.

Data regarding the utility of MRI spectroscopy in the diagnostic process of glomangiomas are lacking in the literature. Diagnosing glomangiomas in deep anatomical locations may present challenges and can be readily conflated with neurilemmomas or paragangliomas based on imaging observations[33]. In such cases, it is recommended to proceed with a biopsy for diagnostic purposes. Clinical presentation of spinal glomus tumors is indistinguishable from analogic intradural and extradural spinal tumors and involves neurological deficits secondary to compression of the spinal cord or nerve roots, prompting diagnostic imaging investigations. In certain instances, it may present as an incidental, sporadic discovery[34].

In the recent study, Liu et al[35] proposed a multimodel weighted fusion framework based on MRI and age data for enhancing the effectiveness of benign and malignant spinal tumors diagnosis. The accuracy of the model was higher than the accuracy of one radiologist and two spine surgeons, regardless of the consideration of age information. Diagnostic accuracy was improved when age information was included in the model.

Diagnostic challenges: malignant spinal tumors mimicking benign lesions

Accurate diagnosis of spinal tumors is complicated by the potential for malignant lesions to mimic benign ones on imaging studies. MPNSTs, for instance, often resemble schwannomas or neurofibromas but exhibit more aggressive features such as larger size, peripheral enhancement, surrounding edema-like areas, and internal cystic lesions[36]. Similarly, primary spinal lymphomas can appear as homogeneously enhancing masses like meningiomas but may show more rapid growth and lack the characteristic dural tail sign[37]. Chordomas, typically located in the sacrococcygeal region, can mimic giant cell tumors or osteoblastomas with their well-defined, enhancing masses, but tend to be more destructive[38], [39]. There are also reports of chordomas of other regions mimicking schwannomas[40]. Metastatic tumors often resemble vertebral hemangiomas on MRI with varied intensity and significant postcontrast enhancement, but are typically multifocal with associated soft tissue masses[41–43]. Ependymomas, whether benign myxopapillary variants or malignant forms, can show enhancement and cystic components, though malignant types exhibit more irregular borders, hemorrhage, and necrosis[44,45]. Hemangiopericytomas can resemble meningiomas with well-circumscribed enhancing masses but tend to be more aggressive with extensive vascular involvement[46]. Chondrosarcomas, mimicking enchondromas, often exhibit cortical destruction and soft tissue extension, and Ewing sarcomas can present with mixed lytic and sclerotic lesions, periosteal reaction, and significant soft tissue masses, resembling an aggressive benign tumors[47,48]. Recognizing these malignant tumors requires careful assessment of imaging features such as lesion size, border characteristics, presence of necrosis, hemorrhage, and associated soft tissue components, along with clinical correlation and, often, histopathological confirmation. This complexity underscores the importance of comprehensive imaging evaluation and multidisciplinary collaboration in diagnosing and treating spinal tumors.

Treatment of IDEM tumors

Standard surgical approaches

Generally, the cornerstone of treatment for all intradural, extramedullary lesions remains radical surgical excision, which typically results in favorable long-term outcomes with minimal risk of recurrence[49]. However, approximately 10% of patients experience a recurrence after surgery due to incomplete removal[50]. In 2001, Saito et al[51] proposed a method for achieving complete resection of anterior-based lumbar spinal meningioma by selectively excising the involved inner layer of the dura while preserving the outer layer for closure. The primary objective of meningioma surgery is to attain a Simpson I total resection, thereby minimizing the risk of recurrence. However, complete resections, particularly for anteriorly situated spinal lesions, are frequently avoided due to the potential for postoperative cerebrospinal fluid leakage[51,52]. Some authors recommend prospective use of autologous fat grafting during intradural spinal tumor surgery, claiming it effectively prevents cerebrospinal fluid-related complications, which are reported in up to 18% of patients[32]. In instances where total resection is unattainable, observation may be considered for small, asymptomatic tumors. While the most commonly employed surgical technique involves traditional microscopic tumor excision, this classical method entails making an extended midline incision (typically two levels above and below the tumor), dissecting the paraspinal muscles subperiosteally, and performing multilevel laminectomies or facetectomies. This approach damages a significant portion of the structures within the posterior column, leading to decreased spinal stability and the potential for spinal deformity[53]. Due to that, minimally invasive endoscopic approaches are increasingly utilized in contemporary practice. According to some authors, panoramic illumination and visualization provided by endoscopy offer clear benefits compared to microscopy in the dissection of tumors from nearby neural and vascular structures. This necessitates even smaller bony and dural exposures. Thus, in conjunction with microscopy, endoscopy utilizes a multiangled endoscope to directly observe and excise the lesion through a small bone window that may not be visible or accessible under the microscope[54].

Minimally invasive and endoscopic techniques

Burtcher et al[55] were the first to employ an endoscope-assisted microneurosurgical technique for the removal of multilevel IDEM tumors in the lumbar region. Following this, Barami et al[56] documented the use of an endoscope-assisted posterior approach in two cases for resecting ventral IDEM tumors. Later, Caballero-García et al[57], in a retrospective cohort study, evaluated the efficacy of a minimally invasive full endoscopic approach for extra- and intradural spinal tumor removal, reporting excellent clinical outcome[57]. Similar observations have been noted by Şentürk et al[58] who used a translaminar endoscopic approach.

A novel endoscopic technique known as unilateral biportal endoscopy (UBE) in minimally invasive spinal surgery (MISS) permits the precise manipulation of instruments with an enhanced, magnified perspective. It has found extensive use in various spinal degenerative conditions and is now being more commonly used in tumor treatment in recent years. Compared to traditional surgeries and uniaxial endoscopic approaches, UBE offers greater flexibility, convenience, minimal tissue trauma, reduced blood loss, and decreased postoperative pain[59,60]. Peng et al[61] reported the first case when UBE was successfully utilized for intradural extramedullary tumor excision.

Role of intraoperative monitoring

Although intraoperative monitoring is commonly utilized during spine tumor surgeries, and no prospective trials have confirmed its effectiveness, Kujur et al[62] analyzed surgical outcomes for schwannoma and meningioma cases without employing intraoperative nerve monitoring (IONM). Despite this, their findings underscore the potential benefit of IONM in enhancing safety during surgery by providing real-time assessment of spinal cord function, especially in meticulous operations, thereby contributing to safer procedures and improved gross resection outcomes. Nonetheless, the absence of validated procedures to address intraoperative alerts underscores a crucial gap in knowledge. Future research efforts should concentrate on establishing treatment methodologies, validating procedural protocols, and consolidating clinical recommendations[63].

Robotic and hybrid surgical techniques

At present, robotic surgery is primarily utilized when there is suspicion of spinal instability and a requirement for precise screw placement[64]. Novel literature also provides instances of hybrid surgeries in cases involving extensive spinal tumors that extend into other body cavities, utilizing a combination of endoscopic and robotic surgery. Iga et al[65] employed a blend of minimally invasive techniques for a dumbbell-shaped tumor, utilizing spinal endoscopy in conjunction with robot-assisted intrathoracic surgery with the da Vinci robot.

Emerging technologies (AR/VR)

Recently, there has been a growing scientific interest in the utilization of emerging technologies such as augmented reality (AR), virtual reality (VR), and mixed reality (MR)[66]. Carl et al[67] employed AR assistance in microscopic operations on ten patients diagnosed with intradural spinal tumors. They utilized preoperative imaging data to segment the objects visualized by AR, and nonlinear image registration was employed to accommodate spine flexibility. They found this method to be a reliable tool for intradural spinal tumor surgery. The automatic registration of AR ensures exceptional accuracy and provides an intuitive representation of both the tumor’s extent and the surrounding structures. Similar conclusions were reached by Sommer et al[68].

Radiation therapy (SRS/SRT)

Radiation therapy is a viable option for managing spinal cord neoplasms, especially when facing unresectable lesions or residual disease postsurgery, or in cases where surgery is contraindicated due to medical comorbidities, performance status, lesion location, or rapidly recurring/progressive tumors[69]. Conventional radiation therapy for spinal cord neoplasms often yields modest outcomes due to limitations such as multiple tumors or proximity to organs-at-risk[70]. Hypofractionated approaches, with larger fraction sizes, offer potential benefits in tumor control and treatment duration reduction, particularly for metastatic disease in the bony spine[71]. However, it may impact hormonal balance in women, potentially affecting fertility and adverse pregnancy outcomes, while abdominopelvic radiation is linked to uterine dysfunction[72,73]. Conventional radiotherapy can induce neurocognitive impairment in cancer survivors by damaging neural progenitor populations responsible for adult hippocampal neurogenesis[74,75]. However, the application of stereotactic radiation therapy (SRT) for spinal cord tumors is less standardized, with challenges such as unique radiobiological responses and close proximity to the spinal cord[76]. While experiences from treating intracranial tumors guide some approaches, the efficacy and safety of SRT for spinal cord neoplasms require further exploration[77,78].

Several studies have explored the use of SRT or SRS for the treatment of intradural extramedullary (IDEM) tumors, with generally positive outcomes and few transient complications reported. For instance, Selch et al[79] treated 25 benign tumors, including intradural lesions, with SRS to 12 Gy in a single fraction and observed transient worsening of pretreatment neuropathies in two patients, which resolved with conservative treatment Similarly, Marchetti et al[80], while treating meningiomas and schwannomas (11 lesions treated 10–13 Gy in 1 frac; 10 lesions 18.5–25 Gy in 4–6 frac) reported acute worsening of sciatica in one patient posttreatment but found no late toxicity among 18 patients with stable or decreased lesions on radiological follow-up.

Shin et al[81] reviewed 66 spinal SRS procedures for 110 tumors in 58 patients, including schwannomas, neurofibromas, and MPNSTs, with additional analysis on MPNST patients’ survival. The median prescribed dose for benign neurogenic tumors was 13 Gy in a single session, but large tumors received hypofractionated SRS (median 25 Gy in 5 fractions). Malignant tumors were treated with a higher dose of 40 Gy in 5 fractions. After treatment, most patients saw stability or improvement in symptoms, with no notable radiation-related complications post-SRS. Among those with moderate-to-severe pain before treatment, significant improvement in VAS scores was observed (mean score decreased from 6.0 to 1.0 over 8.1 months). Patients with less severe pain also experienced stability or improvement posttreatment. Neurologically compromised patients who underwent surgery followed by SRS showed improvement. No significant radiation-induced complications were observed during the follow-up period post-SRS[81].

Gerszten et al[82] studied 73 benign intradural extramedullary (IDEM) tumors treated with CyberKnife SRS, including meningiomas, neurofibromas, and schwannomas. The study involved single treatment sessions for most patients and a median follow-up of 37 months, and a maximum radiation dose of 15–25 Gy (mean dose: 21.64 Gy). They reported 100% long-term tumor control and pain relief in 73% of patients, with few complications, including three cases of radiation-induced myelitis leading to Brown–Séquard syndrome on MRI. Other studies reported different complications, such as radiation-induced myelopathy, posterior column dysfunction[78].

Systemic therapies and chemotherapy

Adjuvant chemotherapy plays a very limited role in the treatment of benign spinal tumors treatment. Several studies evaluating various medical approaches aimed at inhibiting or stabilizing tumor growth were unable to show any clinical benefits for patients. Thus, adjuvant chemotherapy is not included in the standard therapy, regardless of the tumor’s location.

Chemotherapy drugs may be utilized in the management of advanced or unresectable tumors, specifically. In cases of recurrent SM, treatment with chemotherapy should be evaluated on a case-by-case basis in a multidisciplinary environment[83]. Youssef et al[84] utilized everolimus–octreotide combination for recurrent spinal meningiomas, demonstrated antitumor efficacy and was well-tolerated, with no significant long-term side effects observed. In a phase II clinical study, Kumthekar et al.[85] investigated the efficacy of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) monoclonal antibody – bevacizumab. In the studied patient cohort with various grades of malignancy (G1M, G2M, and G3M), bevacizumab demonstrated anti-tumor activity along with manageable adverse effects. Progression-free survival (PFS) at 6 months (PFS-6), median PFS, and median overall survival (mOS) reported as 87%, 22 months, and 35 months for Grade 1 meningiomas (G1M); 77%, 23 months, and 41 months for Grade 2 meningiomas (G2M); and 46%, 8 months, and 12 months for Grade 3 meningiomas (G3M). The use of bevacizumab in schwannomas is also well documented[86]. A phase II clinical trial assessing Sunitinib for recurrent anaplastic meningiomas revealed a mean PFS of 5.2 months across all patients. However, the limited number of participants in (only six infratentorial and spinal meningiomas) restricts its applicability and precludes drawing definitive conclusions[87]. Peptide radioreceptor therapy, known for its effectiveness in treating neuroendocrine tumors by targeting somatostatin receptors, has been considered for meningioma treatment and has shown promising activity, albeit solely within individual clinical trials[88].

Current literature lacks evidence supporting the use of chemotherapy for benign glomus tumors, and no malignant transformation or metastases have been reported in spinal glomangiomas. However, in rare cases of glomus tumors with uncertain malignant potential, such as those described by Li et al[91] and Nagata, adjuvant radiotherapy and reoperation were required due to tumor progression or recurrence, highlighting the complexity of managing these tumors[89,90].

Limitations

The current systematic review is burdened with some limitations. Broad research objectives and heterogeneous outcomes measured by the authors of particular studies precluded conducting quantitative analyses. Furthermore, the articles written in a language other than English were excluded, limiting the analyzed data.

Conclusion

The diagnosis and treatment of intradural extramedullary tumors, particularly spinal glomus tumors, present unique challenges and require a multidisciplinary approach. Radiological examination, with gadolinium-enhanced MRI as the preferred modality, remains essential for precise diagnosis, treatment planning, and surgical guidance. Despite MRI’s efficacy in identifying tumors such as glomangiomas, similarities with other lesions like schwannomas necessitate additional diagnostic tools, such as MRS, to improve detection and differentiation rates. Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for these tumors, often resulting in favorable outcomes. However, the rise of minimally invasive techniques, including endoscopic and robotic-assisted surgeries, offers promising alternatives with reduced morbidity and improved recovery times. The use of intraoperative nerve monitoring and novel imaging technologies such as AR further enhances surgical precision and safety. Radiation therapy, including SRS, provides an effective adjunct or alternative treatment, particularly for unresectable tumors or residual disease. Emerging technologies and refined techniques in radiation therapy show potential for better tumor control and reduced side effects, although further research is needed to establish standardized protocols. Chemotherapy plays a limited role in the treatment of benign spinal tumors but may be considered for malignant or recurrent cases. Current literature indicates a lack of evidence for the efficacy of chemotherapy in benign glomus tumors, highlighting the need for individualized treatment plans based on tumor behavior and patient-specific factors. Advancements in deep learning and weighted fusion frameworks on MRI for the benign and malignant diagnosis of spinal tumors promise to revolutionize diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning. As research progresses, the integration of these advanced technologies and techniques will likely improve patient outcomes and reduce the need for invasive procedures.

In conclusion, the multidisciplinary approach, combining advanced diagnostic imaging, surgical innovation, radiation therapy, and emerging technologies, provides a comprehensive framework for the effective management of intradural extramedullary tumors. Continued research and clinical trials are essential to refine these strategies, enhance diagnostic precision, and optimize treatment outcomes for patients with spinal tumors.
