HierarchicalNets for multi level hierarchical classification of yoga poses
Authors: Manav Barot, Priyank Thakkar, Vijay Ukani, Mayuri A. Mehta, Ketan Kotecha, Ajith Abraham, Dev Kapil, Laura Vanderbloemen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Human pose estimation has long been an actively studied subject in computer vision (CV), with substantial applications in healthcare. It assists in evaluation and monitoring, enabling personalized rehabilitation programs and injury prevention. When applied to yoga, human pose estimation provides accurate alignment guidance throughout practice, reducing the risk of injury and promoting correct posture. This combination enhances physical therapy outcomes, supports overall wellness, and encourages the maintenance of a healthy and efficient yoga practice for general health improvement. We present a unique approach that combines cutting-edge vision transformer technologies with fine-grained hierarchical pose classification. Specifically, we introduce four hierarchical vision transformer architectures designed to incorporate hierarchical class label information into classification models. The proposed method aims to produce more precise classification results by identifying subtle differences between similar poses, thereby improving classification accuracy for yoga postures. The current state-of-the-art (SoTA) Top-1 accuracy for Level 1, 2, and 3 classification tasks is 89.81%, 85.10%, and 79.35%, respectively. Our proposed models surpass these benchmarks, achieving Top-1 accuracy scores of 96.79%, 95.64%, and 93.07%, respectively.

Introduction

In conjunction with yoga, human pose estimation has significant healthcare benefits, including body language recognition and psychiatric symptom interpretation1–3, an instantaneous position monitoring system for identifying falls, benefiting elderly individuals4,5, clinical rehabilitation6,7, etc. Human pose estimation allows for precise patient movement analysis, which aids diagnosis, therapy scheduling, and progress monitoring in rehabilitation settings. Specifically, in gait analysis, pose estimation provides invaluable insights into the intricate biomechanics of walking patterns8–10. Individuals obtain immediate feedback on their alignment and posture by integrating pose estimation technology with yoga practice, minimizing the risk of injuries and increasing the effectiveness of their activities. This combination also allows for remote monitoring, enabling healthcare personnel to guide and modify patients’ routines outside clinical settings. Furthermore, yoga’s attentive features help reduce stress and promote mental well-being, complementing physical recuperation. Overall, integrating human pose estimation with yoga can enhance healthcare outcomes by promoting accurate movement, preventing injuries, and promoting overall wellness.

The process of estimating human pose involves applying computer vision algorithms to analyze visual data from images or videos to ascertain the posture and location of the human body. Determining the precise positions of joints within the human skeleton is the main objective of human posture estimation. For numerous applications, including virtual reality, sports analysis, and medical diagnostics, the location of joints offers information about the body’s orientation, position, and mobility. Manually estimating human postures is time-consuming and prone to mistakes, so trained experts are required. Also, it can only record a small number of postures at once. On the contrary, automatic human pose estimation systems can accurately predict various positions in real time, making it useful for professionals and laypeople. Mediapipe11 and Posenet12 offer a robust framework for real-time human pose estimation.

In the discipline of yoga, a physical, mental, and spiritual practice that comprises numerous positions or asanas, human pose estimation has become increasingly popular. These positions must be accurately estimated to give practitioners feedback, gauge their development, and create personalized yoga programs. Yoga posture estimation is still difficult despite its potential benefits because of a number of issues, including differences in body types, attire, and illumination. Nevertheless, computer vision (CV) and machine learning (ML) improvements have significantly accelerated the evolution of reliable and precise yoga posture estimation systems. Standing stances like warrior II and tree poses, seated poses like lotus and easy pose, and inversions like headstands and handstands are just a few of the many yoga poses with particular advantages. Figure 1 depicts some yoga poses. Estimating yoga poses necessitates using computer vision technologies to identify and track a person’s body movements throughout a yoga practice. This is difficult due to the inherent diversity and intricacy of yoga positions. The poses vary in body location, alignment, and muscle engagement, making it challenging for computer vision to track movements effectively. Related work

Human position estimation and classification is an interesting study area that many people have been researching. However, due to the unavailability of a good dataset, yoga pose classification is not as thoroughly researched. Nevertheless, interest has recently increased in classifying appropriate yoga positions utilizing powerful deep-learning algorithms. This section will examine previous efforts focusing on this subject. While some researchers have used convolutional neural networks, most have used more traditional machine learning methods, according to our research. To address the lack of an appropriate dataset, Verma et al.13 prepared a fresh dataset supporting fine-grained yoga pose classification. The dataset consisted of 82 yoga poses, with a multi-level class hierarchy based on the type of yoga posture. We have used the dataset made public by the authors in our work. The authors also used popular convolutional neural network (CNN) architectures like ResNet, MobileNet, MobileNetV2, ResNeXt, and several DenseNet variants for yoga pose classification. In addition to evaluating existing architectures, they proposed three modified versions of DenseNet 201 and achieved the highest accuracy of 79.35% across all 82 classes of yoga poses. DenseNet has properties such as feature reuse, parameter efficiency, better gradient flow, and feature fusion, which led to improvement.

To classify yoga poses, Agrawal et al.14 utilized various classical machine learning methods such as support vector machines, decision trees, naive bayes, logistic regression, and k-nearest neighbors. They also used tf-pose-estimation to extract the skeletal method to extract joint angles, which were then used as features in machine learning models. To develop and train their models, they curated their own dataset, known as Yogi Dataset. The dataset included 5500 images of 10 distinct yoga poses. They used an 80:20 train/test ratio and identified that the random forest was the most effective model, achieving an accuracy of 99.04%. Classical machine learning methods perform adequately with a limited number of data classes but struggle as the number of classes increases. Additionally, relying solely on features extracted from skeleton methods hinders the ability to capture other essential human body and background features, which are critical for complex yoga pose classification. Consequently, the use of classical methods with skeleton-based feature extraction has significant limitations.

Jose et al.15 proposed a deep learning (DL) strategy for yoga asana identification. The 700 images that made up the dataset for the study were evenly split across ten different yoga asanas, including poses like bridge, child, and downward dog etc. Resizing the photos to 100 x 100, applying a Gaussian filter to remove noise, and using histogram equalization to boost image contrast were the three steps the authors took to prepare images for the study. They used a pre-trained VGG16 model16 for classification. The suggested model correctly identified the ten distinct yoga asanas with an accuracy of 85%. VGG networks, characterized by their depth with 16 weight layers, are computationally expensive and memory-intensive. This substantial depth can lead to vanishing gradients during training, slowing down convergence. Unlike DenseNet or ResNet, VGG’s strict layer stacking does not explicitly promote feature reuse across layers. Although VGG performs well on various tasks, more modern architectures have surpassed it in both accuracy and computational efficiency. Long et al.17 developed a yoga pose training system with an interactive display and a transfer learning technique. They collected 1120 images of 14 different yoga poses from an RGB camera with the help of eight people, who each did each pose ten times. Data augmentation was used to oversample and prevent overfitting once the acquired data was partitioned into training, validation, and testing sets. The study used six different pre-trained models, including the TL-VGG16-DA, TL-VGG19-DA, TL-MobileNet-DA, TL-MobileNetV2-DA, TL-InceptionV3-DA, and TL-DenseNet201-DA18 to determine the best model for the yoga coaching system. Kumar et al.19 also addressed yoga pose classification. They used a dataset with 88 videos of 15 people doing six yoga poses. Each recording lasted between forty-five and sixty seconds. The authors used OpenPose27 to extract the keypoint attributes and combined long short-term memory (LSTM)28 and CNN models to forecast the poses in real-time. The training, validation, and testing accuracy were 99.92%, 99.87%, and 99.38%, respectively. Kothari et al.20 developed a method that employed CNN to classify yoga poses from photographs. The approach used a dataset of 1000 images segregated into six categories. The classification model achieved an accuracy of almost 85%. A deep learning model for detecting five distinct yoga postures using a small dataset of only 286 images was proposed in Yonet21. The suggested technique surpassed the accuracy of some of the most successful models for image classification, including ResNet29, InceptionNet30, InceptionResNet31, and Xception32. The authors explained that their architecture extracted spatial and depth data from images, which were then used for additional classification computations leading to performance improvement. They reported an accuracy of 94.91% in their studies, indicating the efficacy of their proposed methodology. Adho Mukha, Sukhasana, Tadasana, Virabhadrasana I, and Virabhadrasana II were the postures picked for classification. Yadav et al.22 presented YogNet, a deep spatiotemporal neural network architecture, for recognizing 20 various yoga asanas in multi-person scenarios. To test and train their suggested model, they created YAR database of 1206 videos captured with just one 2D web camera with the cooperation of sixteen volunteers. They used a deep spatiotemporal neural network with two streams. The pose stream identified the practitioner’s pose using keypoint detection and applied time-distributed CNNs to extract postural features. Regularized LSTM networks were used for temporal predictions. The RGB stream extracted spatiotemporal features from RGB videos using 3D-CNNs. The final predictions were achieved by fusing both streams scores using multiple fusion techniques. Using the posture stream, the RGB stream, and a combination of the two streams, YogNet’s accuracy was 77.29%, 89.29%, and 96.31%, respectively. They employed a two-stream network, extracting pose estimation key points from one stream and visual features from another. However, as pose complexity increases or when classes exhibit similar poses, relying solely on key points can limit performance due to insufficient information. For example, in complex yoga poses like Utthita Trikonasana A and Utthita Trikonasana B, which appear very similar with minor differences, the pose estimation system may struggle to identify the key points accurately. These poses share many commonalities, but subtle differences, such as arm positioning or body angle, are challenging for pose estimation methods to discern. Thus, relying solely on key points may not distinguish between these poses effectively. In contrast, the RGB stream provides richer features than key points alone. Consequently, the model might prioritize features from the RGB stream over key points. Therefore, when faced with a growing number of complex and similar poses, the network’s performance may deteriorate.

In their study, Mustafa et al.23 employed transfer learning on human pose estimation models for estimating Yogasanas. To gather the necessary data, they used Microsoft Kinect to record videos of 51 participants doing 20 yoga positions and a still pose in a controlled setting. Every video was between two and five minutes long. A random forest classifier was developed using the 136 key points that the authors collected from the body. The researchers used a large internal yoga video archive of 51 people recorded from four distinct camera angles to assess the generalizability of their classifier. They used a three-step evaluation method to put the classifier through its paces on test frames, test subjects, and test camera angles. The model achieved an impressive accuracy of 81.94% in their evaluation. The approach suggested by Wu et al.24 for accurately classifying and rating yoga poses consists of two benchmark datasets, Dataset A and Dataset B. Dataset A is a classification of the yoga posture image dataset that was taken from Kaggle33. This dataset comprises 1931 images of 45 yoga pose categories captured with various resolutions and diverse backgrounds. A 3000 triplet example for grading yoga poses is included in dataset B. It has three photographs of the same yoga posture category, each with a different resolution and background. The suggested method used the input yoga position image to extract important points from the human skeleton. It passed these coordinates to an encoder trained on contrastive triplet instances for pose feature extraction. The authors suggested a novel method that combined a coarse triplet and a fine triplet example to address the issue of creating contrastive instances in pose feature encoding. The coarse triplet example included an anchor, a positive sample from the same group, and a negative sample from a different group. The fine triplet example comprised positive and negative samples both from the same group. Their postures were different. The authors conducted rigorous testing with both datasets to show that their strategy performed better. The suggested methodology successfully classified and graded yoga postures using contrastive triplet examples and a pose feature encoder trained on key points in the human body skeleton with an accuracy of 83.21% on Dataset A and 63.58% on Dataset B. The approach has potential applications in the field of fitness and health monitoring.

Anand et al.25 suggested a DL-based methodology for yoga-posture assessment, which included the following steps: feature extraction, classification, and feedback generation. A public dataset of 70 videos featuring the six yoga poses, Cobra, Tree, Mountain, Lotus, Triangle, and Corpse, was used to assess the suggested methodology. To extract key points, the initial stage involved periodically removing frames from videos and sending them to multi-person posture recognition. They found all twelve angles between the x-axis and the joints using these critical points to compute twelve joint vectors. The second step used a classification model to categorize the pose among the six yoga postures. The 12 categorized pose angles array, which contained the average angles of the dataset’s 12 joints, was used to compare the angles. Making ideas for each angle and identifying the differences was part of the final step, feedback generation. Depending on the direction of the discrepancy, the feedback output indicated whether to rotate joints clockwise or anticlockwise. The proposed approach was evaluated on a dataset of 320 data instances for training and validation, and a separate dataset of 30 instances for testing. The results demonstrated that MLP achieved an exceptional accuracy of 99.58% while requiring fewer computations than several SoTA techniques.

Cao et al.26 suggested a model that combined Mediapipe and ML techniques, including SVM, Gaussian Naive Bayes, Random Forest, Gradient Boost, and K Neighbors to predict yoga postures. The “Yoga Poses Dataset”34, which comprises approximately 1500 photos of 5 yoga postures — Plank, Warrior 2, Downdog, Goddess, and Tree was used by the authors for training and holdout purposes. The dataset was also made publicly available. With the help of Mediapipe35, the authors were able to collect 33 significant features. The MediaPipe35 + SVM model had the best accuracy and F-score when the authors compared the results to other ML methods, and therefore, it was by the authors for the final work. The findings demonstrated that the suggested model could predict yoga poses with an accuracy of about 85%.The authors utilized pose estimation from the MediaPipe library alongside machine learning techniques for classification. However, the limitations of pose estimation became evident, especially with complex and similar yoga poses. For instance, distinguishing between variations of the Extended Triangle Pose proved challenging due to the system’s inability to accurately capture subtle differences in hand movements or gestures. Keypoints extracted from pose estimation often yielded similar results for poses with minor variations, hampering precise classification. Dataset

Yoga is a form of exercise that involves various postures, breathing techniques, and meditation. For centuries, it has been an ancient practice to promote physical, mental, and spiritual health. One of the challenges of studying yoga is the lack of comprehensive datasets containing various yoga poses. The Yoga 82 dataset13, however, is a noteworthy exception because it has various yoga positions that range in difficulty from simple to difficult. The publicly available version of this dataset has approximately 21,000 images and is available at https://sites.google.com/view/yoga-82/home. The complete dataset contains 28,478 images and is available upon request from the author of Yoga-8213. In our research, we used the full dataset, and the dataset split is shown in Table 2. The dataset includes pictures of one or more people doing each yoga stance, assembled from several online sources. Images from various camera angles and backgrounds, including forests, beaches, interior spaces, and gardens, are included in the dataset. The dataset includes some images that are exclusively vector or drawing images. Each of the 82 yoga postures in the dataset is more difficult than a standard human pose, making it special. There are different numbers of images in each class in the database, ranging from 64 to 1133 images per class, with an average of 347 images. Given that most publicly accessible yoga posture datasets have fewer classes, it is challenging to explore a broad range of poses. The dataset also includes hierarchical annotation, which is advantageous for part-based learning. With some layers of the deep network learning features based on body position, some on body position sub-classes, and others on the primary yoga posture, a deep neural network trained on this dataset may be able to recognize characteristics based on hierarchical classes. The dataset’s hierarchical classes are structured in a three-level hierarchy, with the name of the yoga posture making up the third and final level. Information on the body position is provided at the first level, and details about the body position’s subclass are provided at the second level. Standing, Sitting, Balancing, Inverted, Reclining, and Wheel are the first six stages of the hierarchical classifications. Standing refers to the position where a person is standing with both feet on the ground. Sitting is a position where a person sits on the ground with their legs crossed or extended. Balancing refers to the position where a person is balancing on one leg or arm. Proposed approaches

Verma et al.13 first adapted the Yoga-82 label hierarchy within a DenseNet201 architecture, achieving a Top-1 accuracy of 79.35%. Although this work demonstrated the potential of hierarchical supervision in yoga pose classification, it lacked the ability to capture long-range dependencies and input-adaptive feature weighting, two crucial aspects effectively handled by modern vision transformer architectures.

To address these limitations, we propose a method that integrates three key innovations: (1) fine-grained hierarchical supervision, (2) hybrid backbones combining convolution and self-attention, and (3) stage-wise auxiliary losses. First, the hierarchical label structure in Yoga-82 (6 coarse classes → 20 intermediate classes → 82 fine-grained classes) is explicitly embedded into our network by inserting intermediate classifiers at each semantic level. This coarse-to-fine strategy encourages the model to learn broad pose families before distinguishing subtle joint-angle differences. Second, we adopt hybrid backbones that fuse the strengths of convolution and self-attention. Convolutional layers offer efficient local feature extraction and translation equivariance, while self-attention captures global relationships across body joints and regions. Given these benefits, we select CoAtNet38 and MaxViT39 as the foundational backbones for our models. While both architectures share hybrid convolution-attention designs, they serve complementary roles in our framework. The CoAtNet-based models are designed to achieve higher accuracy through deeper architectures, making them suitable for high-performance environments. In contrast, the MaxViT-based models provide a more parameter-efficient alternative, enabling deployment in resource-constrained or real-time scenarios. This dual-architecture design allows us to evaluate the effectiveness of hierarchical learning across both high-capacity and lightweight models. Both architectures have demonstrated strong performance across various visual recognition tasks and naturally integrate convolutional and attention mechanisms. Third, we introduce stage-wise auxiliary losses at intermediate network depths to reinforce learning at multiple semantic levels. These auxiliary losses improve gradient flow during training and promote better generalization without requiring handcrafted features. Building on these components, our model begins by using convolutional layers to extract localized, discriminative features from the input yoga images. These layers automatically learn and refine spatial patterns relevant to pose structure. Next, transformer blocks equipped with self-attention modules capture long-range interactions and contextual dependencies, enabling the model to reason about subtle pose differences across the full image. Unlike prior methods, our framework avoids manual feature selection entirely. Instead, it relies on the network’s inherent capacity to learn rich representations, enhanced by the combination of hierarchical label supervision, hybrid backbone design, and auxiliary loss application.

This integrated framework is especially well-suited to the multi-level structure of the Yoga-82 dataset. Its effectiveness is supported by the experimental results detailed in Section "Results and discussions".

CoAtNet: The architecture of CoAtNet38 is shown in Fig. 3. In general, CoAtNet employs a mix of MBConv and Self-Attention modules in the network architecture. The Stem stage is the first in the network and comprises two convolutional layers with a 3 x 3 kernel. The stage decreases the input image’s dimensionality and enables global attention. Following the Stem stage, the CoAtNet38 design incorporates MBConv, which is based on the fundamental concept of Depthwise Separable Convolution. This convolution type separates the convolutional process into two steps: first, a deep convolution is applied, followed by a pointwise convolution. Furthermore, MBConv uses Inverted residuals, which enable lower layers of the network to access information from previous layers by utilizing skip connections. This concept was first presented in ResNet29 and has since demonstrated usefulness in improving deep network efficacy. Using MBConv with Inverted residuals in CoatNet38, feature representations at different scales can be efficiently learned while keeping the network size and computational cost low. The MBConv block serves as the foundation for the CoatNet network design. The first MBConv block in the network consists of 2 blocks with 128 channels. This block is followed by 6 more MBConv blocks, each with 256 channels. Utilizing multiple MBConv blocks with an increasing number of channels enables the network to capture features at various scales and resolutions. After convolution, the network uses Relative Self-Attention blocks to capture long-range dependencies, followed by a Feedforward Neural Network for non-linear transformations and feature extraction. To be precise, the network consists of 14 blocks of Relative Self-Attention and Feedforward Neural Networks with a hidden dimension of 512. The final block of Relative Self-Attention and Feedforward Neural Networks is repeated twice with a hidden dimension of 1024. The probability of output classes is then estimated using Global Pooling and a Fully Connected (FC) layer.

Hierarchical CoAtNet 1: Hierarchical CoAtNet 1 adapts CoatNet38 to utilize the hierarchical structure labels of Yoga82 dataset13 resulting in enhanced classification performance. The architecture of Hierarchical CoAtNet 1 is depicted in Fig. 4. We introduced hierarchical connections after 7 repetitions of the S3 and S4 blocks to address Level 1 (6 classes) and Level 2 (20 classes) classification, respectively. Each hierarchical connection incorporates Global Pooling, a dense layer, and a Softmax layer. Coarser classes are classified by the network’s middle layers, while finer classes are handled by its outer layers. The initial to middle layers focus on categorizing the first level, with the information from the input image subsequently passed on to the following layers for second-level classification. The third-level categorization, which has 82 classes, is the focus of the main branch. The Main Branch is for Class 3 Classification, which has a total of 82 Classes. The network layers up to S3 are shared by all three levels. These layers aid in learning the basic details, and additional layers build on this foundation to capture more nuanced information.

Hierarchical CoAtNet 2: The architecture of Hierarchical CoatNet 2 is shown in Fig. 5. It can be seen that it also has hierarchical connections after seven repetitions of S3 and S4 blocks for addressing Level 1 and Level 2 classification, respectively. However, instead of just using global pooling, dense, and softmax layers, this architecture uses repetitions of S3 and S4 blocks before these layers, as shown in Fig. 5. To be specific, we have added three repetitions of the Relative Self-Attention and Feedforward Neural Network (FNN) block to the hierarchical connection for level 1 and two repetitions of the same block to the hierarchical connection for level 2. The primary reason for this is that in the previous variant, the level 1 (6-class) classifier had access to features produced after S3 blocks. This representation may be insufficient, resulting in a level 1 classification accuracy decline. With the same perspective, we have also modified a hierarchical connection for level 2 classification.

MaxViT : Another vision transformer we adapted for hierarchical classification is MaxViT39. It has fewer parameters and thus a lower computational requirement than CoAtNet38. Figure 6 depicts the architecture of MaxViT. MaxViT’s design is made up of numerous critical components, starting with the S0 Stem block, which is comparable to CoAtNet’s S0 Stem stage. This stage uses two conv2d layers with a stride of 2 to initialize the network. Following the S0 Stem block, MaxViT uses the “MaxViT Block” in its S1 to S4 stages. This block leverages MBConv, Block Attention, and Grid Attention techniques to extract features. The MBConv component employs a DepthWise Convolution of 3 x 3 with a 1 x 1 convolution and SE Block. The Block Attention component includes Block SA and FFN, while the Grid Attention component features Grid SA and FFN. The MaXViT has many variants, and in the variant we have used, S1, S2, and S4 blocks are repeated twice with channel sizes of 64, 128, and 512, respectively. Additionally, the S3 MaxViT Block is repeated five times with a channel size of 256. Overall, this architecture offers a streamlined and effective means of extracting and utilizing features from our data. In our work, we propose two hierarchical variants of MaxViT, which can exploit hierarchical class information in the Yoga 82 dataset.

Hierarchical MaxViT 1 & Hierarchical MaxViT 2 : These architectures are depicted in Figs. 7 and 8. In Hierarchical MaxViT 1, we introduce hierarchical connections after S2 and S3 blocks for level 1 and level 2 classifications, respectively. These connections use a pooling layer, a dense layer, and the final softmax layer. On the other hand, in Hierarchical MaxViT 2, the hierarchical connection for level 1 classification also uses a MaxViT block before a pooling layer, a dense layer, and the final softmax layer. The rationale behind these modifications is the same as we have discussed for Hierarchical CoAtNets. To train all proposed models, we utilized the powerful PyTorch framework40. During training, we used the commonly used softmax-cross entropy loss function for all three class levels. Results and discussions

In our study, we conducted experiments on a high-end system equipped with an Nvidia A100 40GB GPU and 256GB RAM. We used the PyTorch library to train our models and employed no methods for augmenting input images. This is to maintain consistency in our experiments and focus solely on the performance of the suggested models. We resized images to 224 x 224 before training. The dataset used in experiments is the Yoga 82 dataset13, which consists of a total of 28,478 images of yoga poses divided into 82 categories. Out of these, we used 21,009 images for training and 7,469 images for holdout. By scaling the images to 224 x 224, we were able to feed them into the input layer of the Vision Transformer models, which requires fixed input dimensions. Overall, we built our experimental setup to ensure that we have enough computational resources and use the best available tools to evaluate the suggested models on the Yoga 82 dataset.

Table 5 and 6 compare the performance of several models, including proposed models and convolutional models, and highlight the outstanding performance of the proposed architectures. The upper half of the table depicts the performance of various CNNs. It is evident that DenseNet architectures were the best-performing convolutional models with Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy around 74.9% and 91.4%, respectively. Motivated by this, Verma et al.13 proposed hierarchical variants of DenseNet, which enabled these architectures to exploit hierarchical label information in the Yoga 82 dataset while classifying Yoga poses. These architectures improved the SoTA and provided Top-1 and Top-5 accuracy around 89.8% and 99.8%, respectively. These were certainly significant improvements. We were motivated by this work’s success, proposing to modify vision transformers to exploit hierarchical information in the Yoga 82 dataset and the Yoga pose classification problem. To be specific, we propose four architectures that are based on CoAtNet and MaxViT vision transformers. These architectures are Hierarchical CoaTNet 1, Hierarchical CoaTNet 2, Hierarchical MaxViT 1, and Hierarchical MaxViT 2.

As evident from Table 6, all proposed models outperformed the current state-of-the-art across all three classification levels. Hierarchical CoAtNet 1 achieved a notable accuracy score of 96.45% at Level 1 classification, indicating its ability to distinguish even minor differences in the data. Moving on to Level 2 classification, Hierarchical CoAtNet 1 excelled all previous and suggested algorithms with a 95.64% accuracy score, implying its extraordinary capacity to identify between nuanced variations in the data. Finally, at Level 3 classification, which requires the most detailed classification of data, Hierarchical CoAtNet 1 again stood out with an accuracy score of 93.07%, which is also the highest among all other approaches. These results demonstrate that the proposed ideas and adjustments to the existing network design have greatly improved its ability to accurately classify complex data. Hierarchical CoAtNet 2 fared well, achieving the best Top-1 accuracy for Level 1 classification and the best Top-5 accuracy for Level 1 and 2 classification tasks. Hierarchical MaxViT 1 and Hierarchical MaxViT 2 are vision transformers with approximately 1/3 parameters of the CoAtNet architectures. However, their performance is still almost similar to Hierarchical CoAtNets, as evident from the results in Table 5.

Figure 9 depicts the accuracy vs. epoch statistics during model training. The training accuracy almost saturates between 15 and 25 epochs in the case of Hierarchical CoAtNets. MaxViT variants, on the other hand, stay unstable for extended training sessions until stabilizing. This behavior could be attributed to the reality that the number of parameters in MaxViT is substantially lower, resulting in reduced representational power. Overall, all four proposals work well and contribute to advancing the SoTA for the problem of Yoga pose classification. Regarding the convergence properties of the proposed model, our analysis is as follows. In the early stages of training, vision transformers often exhibit slower convergence rates compared to traditional convolutional neural networks (CNNs). This issue arises from the transformer’s reliance on self-attention mechanisms, which may require additional iterations to capture spatial relationships in the input data effectively. However, vision transformers demonstrate superior generalization capabilities once converged, especially when dealing with complex, hierarchical data structures. This advantage stems from their ability to capture long-range dependencies and global context through self-attention, enabling them to learn more abstract representations of input data. Moreover, integrating auxiliary losses in our proposed approach accelerates the learning process by providing additional guidance for parameter adjustments, which is particularly beneficial for overcoming challenges such as vanishing gradients or slow progress in the main task. When tasks are closely related, as in our case, auxiliary losses can help learn even the most intricate details, enhancing the overall learning process and improving generalization. Vision transformers generally exhibit robust behavior regarding stability and reliability during classification processes. Their self-attention mechanism allows them to adaptively attend to relevant features within the input data, resulting in more stable predictions across variations and perturbations. The complete set of confusion matrices supporting these analyses is provided in the Supplementary Information, with Supplementary Figures S1 – S3 for Hierarchical CoAtNet-1, Supplementary Figures S4 – S6 for Hierarchical CoAtNet-2, Supplementary Figures S7 – S9 for Hierarchical MaxViT-1, and Supplementary Figures S10 – S12 for Hierarchical MaxViT-2, each covering results at Levels 1, 2, and 3 of the hierarchy. The detailed per-class precision, recall, specificity, and F1-scores corresponding to these models are provided separately in Supplementary Data 1 (Excel file).

Regarding trainable parameters, DenseNet Variant 3 has approximately 22.6 million, while the proposed Hierarchical MaxVIT has about 28.6 million. This increase in the number of parameters is modest, yet in terms of performance, Hierarchical MaxVIT achieves a Top-5 L3 accuracy of 98.55% compared to 92.66% for DenseNet Variant 3. Regarding time complexity, the inference time of the proposed model is approximately 16 to 17 milliseconds for a mini-batch of size 64 on 224 x 224 images using a GPU 4060 TI, which is suitable for real-time deployment. In contrast, DenseNet Variant 3 takes about 42 to 43 milliseconds under the same conditions. We attribute the improved classification accuracy of the proposed architecture to its combination of translation equivariance, input-adaptive weighting, global receptive field, and stage-wise auxiliary losses, which enhance feature engineering and error backpropagation. To validate this, we performed an additional experiment on the dataset from34, where the proposed model also achieved a Top-1 L3 accuracy of 98.1%.

Conclusion

This paper presented a novel approach for yoga pose classification by incorporating hierarchical class label information into vision transformer architectures. We proposed four modified models, two based on CoAtNet and two on MaxViT, specifically designed to align with the three-level label hierarchy of the Yoga-82 dataset. By integrating intermediate classifiers at multiple semantic levels, our models effectively captured both coarse and fine-grained distinctions among 82 yoga poses. Experimental results demonstrated the clear advantages of the proposed architectures. Hierarchical CoAtNet 1 achieved Top-1 accuracy scores of 96.45%, 95.64%, and 93.07% across Level 1, Level 2, and Level 3 classifications, respectively, significantly outperforming previous DenseNet-based baselines. Notably, even the lighter Hierarchical MaxViT variants, despite having nearly one-third the parameters of their CoAtNet counterparts, showed competitive accuracy, validating the efficiency and scalability of the proposed framework. Furthermore, the proposed models demonstrated strong suitability for real-time applications. In addition to batch-based inference (16–17 milliseconds per batch of 64 images at 224×224 resolution on an RTX 4060 Ti GPU), the model also achieved real-time processing speeds of approximately 65–70 frames per second (FPS) under streaming inference conditions on an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation GPU. These results highlight the robustness and efficiency of the proposed approach across different hardware settings and deployment scenarios. Overall, the findings confirm that leveraging hierarchical relationships among yoga poses enhances both classification accuracy and model interpretability. The architecture’s capability to operate efficiently in real-time environments makes it practical for deployment in applications such as yoga coaching systems, rehabilitation support platforms, and clinical movement monitoring. Additionally, the multi-level prediction outputs provide interpretable feedback that can assist healthcare professionals and yoga instructors in analyzing pose correctness and improving user guidance. In future work, we aim to introduce advanced data augmentation techniques and synthetic data generation tailored to the characteristics of yoga poses to further improve generalization under varying environments and viewpoints. We also plan to enhance model transparency by developing explainability modules that visualize critical body joints or regions contributing to each classification decision. These directions will help promote safe, personalized, and intelligent pose feedback systems that are scalable and accessible for broader wellness and healthcare applications.
