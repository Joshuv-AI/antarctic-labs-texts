Costs of 4 Months of Rifampin Versus 2 Months of Double-dose Rifampin for Tuberculosis Infection: Post-Hoc Analysis of a Phase 2b Randomized Trial
Authors: Kamila Romanowski, Yen Pham Ngoc, Leila Barss, Elias Jabbour, James C Johnston, Hansen Herman, Richard Long, Lika Apriani, Greg Fox, Dick Menzies, Jonathon R Campbell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCost is an important consideration when implementing tuberculosis preventive treatment regimens (TPT). We used data from a phase 2b randomized trial of TPT to estimate overall cost and key drivers of costs for two TPT regimens.MethodsWe did a post-hoc analysis of 915 participants aged ≥10 years who were randomized 1:1 to 2 rifampin-based regimens: a four-month daily regimen at 10 mg/kg (4R10) and a 2-month daily regimen at 20 mg/kg (2R20; 461 participants). We collected country-specific costs for medications, evaluations, and medical follow-ups from the three participating countries (Canada, Indonesia, and Viet Nam), and converted all costs to 2024 Canadian dollars. We report the overall costs of each regimen and cost drivers.ResultsOverall, 454 participants received 4R10 and 461 participants received 2R20. We found no difference in the cost of 2R20 versus 4R10, with a cost ratio of 0.93 (95% CI: .79–1.07); this was consistent in analyses limited to only those who completed treatment and stratified by country. Costs for medications and the baseline visit accounted for 68%, 49%, and 55% of all costs in Canada, Indonesia, and Viet Nam, respectively. Corresponding costs of routine follow-up visits accounted for approximately 26%, 45%, and 42% of all costs. In all countries, a minority of costs (<10%) were due to additional visits or evaluations not specified in the protocol.ConclusionsMost costs associated with TPT are due to medications and the baseline treatment initiation visit. TPT regimens requiring fewer follow-up visits may reduce overall cost, but the magnitude of this reduction varies by country.Keywords: global health, infectious disease, prevention, public health, tuberculosis

BACKGROUND

The search for short and safe tuberculosis preventive treatment (TPT) regimens—designed to prevent tuberculosis disease, which caused 1.25 million deaths globally in 2023 [1]—has been ongoing for decades. Until 2018, the global standard TPT regimen was 9 months of daily isoniazid [2]. However, its long duration and significant toxicity, affecting up to 1 in 20 people, led to underutilization and poor completion rates [3–5]. In the past 7 years, the World Health Organization (WHO) has recommended several shorter TPT regimens lasting 1–4 months [6]. Of these, only one, four months of daily 10 mg/kg rifampin (4R10), has been shown to be significantly safer than 9 months of isoniazid [7–10].

Yet, 4R10 is the longest of the WHO-recommended short-course TPT regimens, which may limit its widespread adoption. As the cost of rifapentine has declined, other shorter regimens—such as three months of once-weekly isoniazid and rifapentine (3HP)—have become more economically viable for tuberculosis programs [11]. The 2R2 trial compared 4R10 to a two-month daily regimen of rifampin given at double or triple the dose of the standard 4-month regimen, with primary outcomes of safety and completion [12]. The trial found rifampin given at double the dose (2R20) was just as safe as 4R10 but had lower completion rates. The trial investigators speculated this lower completion could be due to regimen intolerance or providers’ caution with a new regimen.

Given the reassuring safety profile of 2R20, further investigation is warranted. A critical consideration is the cost to the healthcare system and the factors driving that cost. To address this, we conducted a post-hoc analysis of the 2R2 trial. Our primary objective was to estimate the cost of 2R20 compared to 4R10 and identify key drivers of cost. Our secondary objective was to determine whether regimen intolerance was associated with increased healthcare utilization.

METHODS

Trial Description

This trial has been described in detail previously [12, 13]. In brief, the 2R2 trial (NCT03988933) was a non-inferiority, partially blinded, randomized phase 2b trial conducted at 7 centers in 3 countries: Canada (4 centers), Indonesia (1 center), and Viet Nam (2 centers). Between 1 September 2019, and 30 September 2022, participants were randomized to 1 of 3 regimens: 4 months of once-daily rifampin at 10 mg/kg, 2 months of once-daily rifampin at 20 mg/kg, and 2 months of once-daily rifampin at 30 mg/kg. Randomization was 1:1:1 using a computer-generated sequence in blocks of either 3, 6, or 9, and stratified by center for Canada and country for Indonesia and Viet Nam. Participants were not blinded to treatment duration, but if randomized to 1 of the 2-month arms, they were blinded to the dosage. Similarly, all other study personnel—except the study coordinator—were masked to dose (but not duration) until the primary trial analysis was complete. The trial was approved by the research ethics board of the McGill University Health Centre (Montreal, QC, Canada), Health Canada, and the ethics committees at all participating centers.

Inclusion criteria were age of at least 10 years and an indication to receive TPT according to national [14] or WHO guidelines [6]. Participants were excluded if they had tuberculosis disease, had a contraindication to rifampin (drug–drug interactions, allergy, hypersensitivity, pretreatment grade 3 hepatic or hematologic abnormality), were a contact of someone with rifampin-resistant tuberculosis disease, were less than 25 kg in weight, or were pregnant.

Dosing of each regimen was done according to weight, up to a maximum daily dose of 600 mg in the 4-month arm, 1200 mg in the 20 mg/kg 2-month arm, and 1800 mg in the 30 mg/kg 2-month arm. Blood was taken for hematologic and hepatic analysis at randomization and again after 4 weeks of treatment for all groups; in the 20 mg/kg and 30 mg/kg groups, the same blood tests were done at 2 weeks. All participants had three routine follow-up visits: at 4, 8, and 16 weeks in the four-month arm, and at 2, 4, and 8 weeks in the two-month arms. At each visit, participants were questioned about symptoms of medication intolerance (skin problems, gastrointestinal intolerance, neurological symptoms, or other) or tuberculosis-related symptoms, had their adherence assessed with pill counts, and were dispensed study medications. Additional tests and investigations were done as deemed necessary by treating physicians. As this trial was performed during the COVID-19 pandemic, remote follow-up visits by phone were permitted, although in-person visits were preferred.

Definitions and Outcomes

All study visits, tests, and evaluations, whether visits were in person or over the phone, as well as detailed management of adverse events, were recorded in study case report forms. Routine study visits were those occurring as close as possible to the protocol-specified follow-up time, while all other recorded study visits were classified as additional. If a participant answered positively to any symptom of medication intolerance, we defined them as having medication intolerance at that study follow-up visit. For additional visits, we classified them as “symptom-driven” if there was an additional visit that immediately followed a routine visit where symptoms of medication intolerance were reported; other additional visits were categorized as for “other reasons.” For this analysis, we classified end-of-treatment outcomes as completed treatment ever (80% of doses in any length of time), treatment discontinuation due to treatment-related adverse events (as adjudicated by a blinded, three-member expert panel), and treatment discontinuation for any other reason.

We collected costs for all visits, medications dispensed, tests, and evaluations performed in each country using standardized forms (see Supplementary Tables 1–3, for a detailed list of unit costs for each country). In Canada, costs for activities at all centers were estimated based on costs from the province of Quebec, using provincial reimbursement schedules. We used Quebec to represent Canadian costs, as costs for tuberculosis-related activities collected as part of a previous study in three Canadian provinces showed that costs on average were similar [15]. In Indonesia, costs for medical visits were estimated based on the average time of visits [16] and healthcare worker salary, costs for drugs were estimated from those incurred procuring medication during the trial, and costs for all tests and evaluations from fee schedules at the participating trial center (a university-affiliated hospital). In Viet Nam, costs for medical visits were estimated based on the average time of visits [16] and healthcare worker salary, costs for drugs were from a national distributor, and costs for all tests and evaluations were derived from the national fee schedule governing pricing of these activities covered by the national health insurance scheme [17]. All costs were collected in local currency, and for Indonesia and Viet Nam, converted to Canadian dollars (CAD) using exchange rates in June 2024 (0.000083 CAD per Indonesian Rupiah [IDR] and 0.000054 CAD per Vietnamese dong[VND]).

For adverse events, we estimated total costs associated with managing the adverse event based on the management described in the adverse event report. For medication costs, we considered the costs of all medications dispensed to the participant. For all other costs incurred at each visit for each participant, we multiplied the unit cost for each activity by the number of times each activity was performed and summed these values.

Statistical Analysis

Our analysis focused only on 4R10 and 2R20, as the triple dose arm was shown to be inferior in terms of safety and completion in the trial, and so is no longer being considered as a candidate TPT regimen.

In our primary analysis, for each participant in the trial, we summed costs across all visits and activities to arrive at a total cost per participant. We calculated the mean cost and standard deviation (SD) of 4R10 and 2R20 in the modified intention to treat (mITT) population overall, and among those who completed treatment in each arm. We next estimated the mean cost ratios (2R20 vs 4R10) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using the ratio of group means and standard errors, with an offset of 1e−6 added to prevent division by zero. We repeated this analysis stratified by country and by cost component: medication costs, baseline (pretreatment) visit, routine follow-up visit, additional symptom-driven visit, additional visit for other reasons, and costs related to adverse event management.

In our secondary analysis, we only included participants who had at least one routine follow-up visit. We evaluated if symptoms of medication intolerance reported at either the first or second routine follow-up visit were associated with additional visits and/or treatment discontinuation before the next planned routine follow-up. We descriptively compared country, regimen, and outcomes (additional visit and/or treatment discontinuation) between participants who reported symptoms at a routine follow-up visit and those who did not. To estimate the association between symptom reporting and subsequent additional visits or treatment discontinuation, we performed logistic regression using generalized linear mixed models. Adjusted odds ratios and 95% CIs were calculated. Models were adjusted for country and regimen, with a random intercept for participant. Effect modification by regimen was assessed through an interaction term between symptoms at a routine visits and regimen. All analyses were performed in R (V.4.4.2).

RESULTS

Overall, 915 participants were included in the mITT population, with 454 in the 4R10 arm and 461 in the 2R20 arm. Participant characteristics by treatment arm are summarized in Table 1 and by country in Supplementary Table 4. The median participant age was 41.0 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 28.5–53.0), and 536 (58.6%) were female. Among all participants, 861 (94.1%) attended at least one routine follow-up visit. Of these, 313 (36.4%) reported symptoms of medication intolerance during at least one routine follow-up visit, and 235 (27.3%) had at least one additional visit after a routine follow-up visit.

We found no significant difference in the cost of 4R10 and 2R20 regimens overall, or when stratified by country. In Canada, the mean cost of 4R10 was $663 (SD $118) and of 2R20 was $647 (SD $106). In Indonesia, the mean cost of 4R10 was $114 (SD $33) and of 2R20 was $118 (SD $40), while in Viet Nam, the mean cost was $58 (SD $16) for 4R10 and $54 (SD $19) for 2R20 (Supplementary Tables 5–7).

When pooled across all countries, the mean cost of 4R10 was $281 (SD $284) while the mean cost of 2R20 was $262 (SD $270), yielding an estimated cost ratio (2R20/4R10) of 0.93 (95% CI: .79, 1.07). Among those completing treatment, there was also no significant difference in the mean cost (cost ratio: 1.01, 95% CI: .87, 1.14) (Table 2).

Across all countries, the majority of costs for each regimen were attributed to medication (rifampin), the baseline visit, and routine follow-up visits (Figure 1). In Canada, medication and baseline visit costs accounted for 67.2% and 68.2% of total costs in the 4R10 and 2R20 arms, respectively. In Indonesia, these costs contributed 50.5% and 47.5%, while in Viet Nam, they made up 55.3% and 55.0% (Supplementary Table 8). The proportion of costs associated with routine follow-up visits varied by country and regimen, accounting for 25.4% and 27.4% in Canada, 43.1% and 48.0% in Indonesia, and 41.2% and 43.7% in Viet Nam. A total of 15 (3.3%) and 18 (3.9%) of participants in 4R10 and 2R20 arms discontinued treatment due to an adverse event; costs related to managing these adverse events, however, represented less 2% of total costs across all countries. Additional visit costs accounted for less than 10% of total expenditures, with the highest proportion observed in Canada.

DISCUSSION

In this post-hoc analysis of a phase 2b randomized trial, we found no significant difference in the cost of 4 months of daily 10 mg/kg rifampin and 2 months of daily 20 mg/kg rifampin. This finding was consistent across country-specific analyses. Symptoms of medication intolerance increased the odds of additional visits or premature treatment discontinuation but did not account for appreciable costs.

The similar costs between 4R10 and 2R20 are unsurprising given that both regimens involve the same total rifampin dosage and the same number of follow-up visits. At Canadian sites, we estimated the regimen costs to be $650−$670 CAD, comparable to prior estimates for another first-line TPT regimen, 3HP, which was estimated at $600 CAD (2024 dollars) [18]. However, this cost for 3HP did not consider adverse event management or additional visits, which may be higher with this regimen given its worse safety profile [10]. In Indonesia, a previous modeling study [19] suggested 3HP was less expensive than mono-rifampin regimens; however, this study did not consider the costs of blood tests, which are costly in Indonesia, and it is unclear if the costs of direct observation were included. Given the absence of empirical data directly comparing the health system costs of currently available short TPT regimens, it is difficult to draw firm conclusions on which regimen represents the best value for money.

A key finding of this study is that most costs incurred were due to protocol-required activities: medications, baseline visits, and routine follow-up visits. As the costs of medications and baseline medical visits cannot be avoided, it appears the only way to significantly reduce the cost of TPT regimens is to reduce the intensity of follow-up required. However, reducing the intensity of follow-up visits requires a careful evaluation of the consequences of such an action (eg, poorer completion, missed opportunities to detect adverse events early) in routine settings. In all settings, reducing the intensity of follow-up will only be possible with safe and well-tolerated regimens, highlighting the critical need to find not only shorter regimens, but safer ones [20].

This study has several strengths. We used country-specific cost data and leveraged standardized data collection from a randomized trial, ensuring detailed and reliable tracking of visits, evaluations, and medication dispensing. Additionally, our breakdown of cost contributors provides valuable insights for TPT program development and implementation. Despite these strengths, this study is also subject to limitations. First, while the use of randomized trial data ensures high-quality information, it may limit generalizability to routine practice. However, the schedule for follow-up was designed to mirror routine practice outside of the trial. Second, we did not account for costs incurred prior to the decision to provide TPT, such as those associated with tuberculosis infection testing, as this information was not available; though in a randomized study, we anticipate these costs to be similar between groups. Third, the trials only included participants at least 10 years of age, which limits the generalizability of our findings to children. Fourth, we did not include costs incurred by participants. However, given the similar number of follow-up visits between arms, this should not impact our conclusions. Finally, only activities recorded in case-report forms could be costed. Tests, visits, or evaluations performed but not recorded would therefore not be included in cost estimates.

In summary, we did not find a significant difference in cost between the 4R10 and 2R20 regimens. The largest cost drivers were protocol-required visits and medications, while additional visits contributed minimally to overall costs. Fewer follow-up visits during treatment would reduce the overall costs of TPT regimens, but this requires safe and well-tolerated regimens.
