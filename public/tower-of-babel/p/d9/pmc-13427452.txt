Biochemical and functional benchmarking of plant protein hydrolysates in serum-reduced adherent CHO-K1 cell culture
Authors: F Compeau, G Yalkin, P Mortamais, D Lutomski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Comprehensive biochemical profiling of six industrial plant protein hydrolysates.•Wheat (W106) and broad bean (F120) peptones producing the strongest effects, restoring CHO-K1 proliferation to levels comparable to—or in some conditions exceeding—those obtained with 10% FBS.•Optimal peptone supplementation markedly improving growth performance under low-serum conditions.•Proteomics-based mechanistic study underway to elucidate cellular responses to peptone supplementation.Keywords: Cho-k1 cells, Serum-reduced culture, Plant protein hydrolysates, Peptones, Cell proliferation

Introduction

In the field of biopharmaceutical production, Chinese Hamster Ovary (CHO) cells have emerged as the primary expression system for recombinant therapeutic proteins (RTPs), despite the presence of numerous alternative cell lines. CHO cells are widely recognized for their unique advantages, which include their ability to perform a variety of post-translational modifications closely resembling those found in human cells [1], a high degree of biosafety owing to their low risk of propagating human viral infections [2], and their adaptability to both adherent and suspension cultures in serum-free media [3]. Additionally, these cells exhibit the ability to achieve high peak cell densities under serum-free conditions [4]. The combination of these attributes has firmly established CHO cells as the gold standard in the bioindustry, contributing to the production of approximately 70% of all marketed RTPs [[5], [6], [7]].

Fetal bovine serum (FBS) is commonly utilized as a supplement in basal media for in vitro mammalian cell cultures. Its annual consumption is estimated to range from 600,000 to 800,000 liters, a quantity corresponding to the sacrifice of over 2 million bovine fetuses [8]. FBS is rich in essential macro- and micronutrients, including growth factors, amino acids, hormones, and vitamins, all of which are critical for proper cell function. The serum's composition, which includes key growth factors such as Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF) and Fibroblast Growth Factor (FGF), as well as serum proteins, transport proteins, and enzymes, plays an indispensable role in supporting cell culture. However, in addition to the ethical concerns associated with its use, the increasing demand, high cost, significant contamination risks (e.g., bacterial, viral, prions) [9], and batch-to-batch variability of FBS have prompted efforts to seek alternative supplements. To mitigate or eliminate FBS use, various plant protein hydrolysates have been explored as viable alternatives in culture media.

In the context of biopharmaceutical production, changes in culture medium composition may also influence the properties of recombinant proteins. Although this study focuses on cell growth, such aspects remain important considerations when developing alternative supplementation strategies.

Serum-free and chemically defined media are widely used in industrial CHO-based bioproduction because they improve reproducibility, reduce contamination risks, and facilitate process control. However, these optimized formulations are often proprietary, costly, and not always easily accessible or adaptable for research laboratories and early-stage development.

In this context, serum-reduced media supplemented with well-characterized plant protein hydrolysates represent a practical intermediate strategy. Such approaches allow partial reduction of animal-derived components while maintaining cell growth in simple and accessible basal media, without requiring fully optimized industrial formulations.

Research has demonstrated that serum-free media can significantly enhance both cell growth and antibody production in CHO cells, with monoclonal antibody production reaching levels exceeding 5 g/L [[10], [11], [12]]. The supplementation of serum-free media with various components, such as growth factors (e.g., insulin, insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1)), and lysophosphatidic acid (LPA), has been shown to further stimulate CHO cell proliferation. Additionally, the inclusion of non-ionic surfactants like Pluronic-F68 can protect suspension-cultured cells from shear stress, thereby improving overall cell growth. Essential components, including amino acids, vitamins, salts, lipids, and trace elements, are also critical to maintaining cell growth and viability in serum-free media [3,13].

Despite these advancements, the composition of serum-free media remains undefined for end-users, presenting a challenge to the widespread adoption of these media. To address this limitation, we have turned to the exploration of various plant protein hydrolysates as supplements to culture media.

In contrast to serum-free media, whose composition is often undisclosed, media with reduced serum levels supplemented with peptones offer distinct advantages by providing a more defined and predictable nutritional profile, which can help optimize cell culture conditions.

Vegetable peptones, which are partial hydrolysates derived from plant proteins, present a promising alternative due to their lower costs than FBS. Peptones consist of peptides, amino acids, vitamins, and trace elements and have been shown to enhance both cell growth and protein production. Notably, plant-derived peptones, such as those from soy and wheat, have demonstrated improvements in cell growth and recombinant protein production [3,14,15]. Moreover, studies have indicated that peptones, including rice hydrolysates and yeast-derived peptones, can significantly increase CHO cell density and protein production [16,17].

In conclusion, the incorporation of plant peptones into culture media has shown considerable promise in enhancing both cell growth and recombinant protein production. The primary objective of this study is to make «serum-reduced» media more accessible, given the significant cost disparity between serum-free and serum-supplemented media, with the former typically costing 1.5 to 5 times more than the latter. The objective of this study was not to compete with fully optimized industrial chemically defined platforms, but to evaluate whether compositionally characterized plant protein hydrolysates could support CHO-K1 proliferation under controlled serum-reduced conditions. By combining biochemical profiling and functional benchmarking of several industrial-grade hydrolysates, we aimed to identify practical and accessible supplementation strategies for early-stage development and research-scale applications. Our findings will contribute to the design of a more accessible serum-reduced supplementation strategy that incorporates basal media, plant hydrolysates, and selected additional substances.

Cell adaptation to low-serum condition

Adherent CHO-K1 cells were first inoculated in 75-cm² flasks at a viable cell density of 3.105 ¢/mL in normal medium condition (Ham’s F-12, RPMI, 1640 or DMEM supplemented with 1% of 10,000 U/mL Penicillin - Streptomycin and 10% FBS (Fetal Bovine Serum)). After reaching a density of 1.106 ¢/mL, the cells were subcultured at a density of 3.105 ¢/mL and were passaged every 3–4 days. Cells were progressively adapted to low-serum concentration (sequential adaptation). The process of the serum-reduction is indicated on both Fig. 1A, Fig. 1B and hereunder. Cells are passaged into mixtures of Ham’s F-12 + 10% FBS and Ham’s F-12 + 0% FBS until the optimal serum-reduction (from 10% to 1% FBS). The different thresholds are 10% - 7.5% - 5% - 2.5% - 1% FBS. For each FBS level, cells are subcultured 3 times in duplicate. Cells were considered fully adapted when the cell density attained 1.106 ¢/mL with at least 80% cell viability.

Cell cultivation

Adherent CHO-K1 cells were consistently cultivated in Ham’s F-12, RPMI, 1640 or DMEM supplemented with 1% of 10,000 U/mL Penicillin - Streptomycin containing 10% or 1% FBS (adapted cells). Once the cell number of each culture reached 1–2 × 106 ¢/mL (mid exponential phase) the cultures were inoculated at a cell concentration of 0.2.106 ¢/mL in 75-cm² cell culture treated flask. Both conditions were grown at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 85% humidity in a static incubator and were passaged every 3–4 days.

To study the effect of peptone (W106, S146B, P118, F120, R123, PR124), stock solutions have been realized (10%, w/v), filtered through membranes with a pore size of 0.22 µM (Merck Millipore) and stored at 4 °C. Different concentrations of peptones have been tested: 0 - 1 - 2 - 5 - 8% (w/v) and stored at 4 °C. For the seeding phase, once the cell number for each culture reached 1–2 × 106 ¢/mL (mid exponential phase) the cultures were inoculated at a cell concentration of 1.105 ¢/mL in 6-well plate (Corning™). Cells were grown at 37 °C, 5% CO2 and 85% humidity in a static incubator.

Experimental design and statistical analysis

For each peptone-screening experiment, CHO-K1 cells were cultured in triplicate for each condition. Peptone supplementation was applied at the time of seeding and maintained throughout the 4-day culture period without medium renewal.

Viable cell density and viability were measured using trypan blue exclusion with the Vi-Cell XR analyzer. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Statistical analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparisons tests. Comparisons were made against the relevant reference condition, either Ham’s F-12 + 1% FBS or Ham’s F-12 + 10% FBS, as indicated in the figure legends.

Given the screening nature of the study and the limited number of biological replicates, statistical analyses were primarily used to support the identification of reproducible biological trends rather than to establish definitive quantitative differences.

The growth-promoting effect observed for selected peptones, particularly W106, was further supported by independent repeat experiments, which reproduced the same overall trend.

Cell number and cell viability

Viable cell density and viability were measured using the Vi-Cell XR™ Cell Viability Analyzer (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA) through the Trypan blue dye exclusion method. Measurements were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions

Osmolality

Osmolality was measured using an Osmo1® Single-Sample Micro-Osmometer (Advanced Instruments, USA) and maintained at 300 ± 1 mOsm/kg H₂O. Measurements, based on the solution’s freezing point, were performed in duplicate using 70 μL of sample per injection.

Results

The following results present the effects of varying serum concentrations and peptone supplementation on the growth of CHO-K1 cells.

Adaptation of CHO-K1 cells to reduced serum conditions

First, we determined the lowest serum concentration to which CHO-K1 cells can adapt so that we could then test the contribution of plant peptones in terms of cell growth.

In this aim, CHO-K1 were gradually adapted to lower serum concentrations (7.5%, 5%, 2.5%, 1%, and 0%) using three different basal media: Ham’s F-12, RPMI, 1640 or DMEM. Fig. 1 presents the viable cell density (cells/mL) of CHO-K1 cells over time as they were progressively adapted to lower serum concentrations in the Ham’s F-12 medium. The same results are observed with the other media (not shown). The adaptation process spanned 57 days, during which cells were cultured sequentially at decreasing FBS concentrations to allow acclimation to reduced serum levels. Throughout the culture period, viable cell density is predominantly higher in the 10% FBS condition (black curve). In contrast, we observe a reduction in viable cell density when FBS concentration is 1% of FBS and collapses with 0% (red curve). After 39 days of adaptation when the serum is reduced to 0%, the cells lose their adhesion (not shown) and the viability decrease (dashed red curve) (Fig. 1B). After day 45 the cells progressively die (Fig. 1B). Despite a decline with a minimum of 1%, cells demonstrated recovery and stabilization of viable cell density over time as they adapted to the new serum conditions.

Although the doubling time progressively lengthened with each reduction in FBS concentration, we observed a stabilization upon reaching the 1% FBS. Furthermore, complete serum deprivation (0% FBS) yielded negative calculated doubling time values. This mathematical artifact is directly attributed to a complete arrest of cellular proliferation with a net decline in viable cell density. (Table 1)

Selection of optimal basal medium under 1% FBS

Secondly, we determined the best basal medium to use when CHO-K1 cells are cultured in 1% FBS in terms of cell growth. In this aim, a comparative study was made among the three previously selected basal media: Ham’s F-12, RPMI, 1640 or DMEM. Results are shown Fig. 2, Fig. 3. In the presence of high serum concentration (10% FBS), CHO-K1 cells displayed distinct growth profiles depending on the basal medium (Fig. 2). RPMI, 1640 supported the most robust proliferation, reaching >2.8 × 10⁶ viable cells/mL by day 4, significantly higher than Ham’s F-12 or DMEM, which both plateaued at ∼1.4 × 10⁶ cells/mL. Relative cell proliferation confirmed that RPMI, 1640 enhanced cell growth by 226% compared to Ham’s F-12, while DMEM provided only modest improvement (∼112%). However, when serum concentration was reduced to 1% FBS, overall proliferation decreased markedly across all basal media (Fig. 3). At this low concentration, the Ham’s F12 medium is most effective in term of cell growth compared to the other culture media. Therefore, the Ham’s F-12 medium was chosen as basal medium to test the contribution of plant peptones when CHO-K1 cells are cultured with 1% FBS.

Biochemical characterization of plant hydrolysates

After establishing the positive serum-reduced conditions (1%FBS, Ham’s F-12), several plant peptones were tested on the CHO-K1 cell line.

The composition and physicochemical properties of the peptones analyzed in this study, highlights their potential as nutrient sources for cell culture (Fig. 4). The amino acid composition of the peptones, shown in Panel A, reveals that Glutamate (Glu), Arginine (Arg), and Leucine (Leu) were the most abundant amino acids across all preparations, with some variability observed. These amino acids play essential roles in cellular metabolism, protein synthesis, and energy production, underscoring the nutritional relevance of these peptones.

The total free amino acid (FAA) content, presented in Table 2 and Fig. 4B, varied among the peptones, with C—CELL PR124, C—CELL F120 and C—CELL P118 exhibiting the highest concentrations. Elevated FAA levels may provide readily available precursors for biosynthetic processes, supporting enhanced cellular growth and productivity. However, this increased substrate availability does not always translate into a proportional enhancement of cell proliferation, as exemplified by the C—CELL PR124. The sugar composition of the peptones, depicted in Fig. 4C, shows that maltose and saccharose were the most abundant sugar, while glucose and lactose were present at lower levels. The high sucrose content in the peptones could serve as an energy source, sustaining metabolic activity during culture.

The mineral composition, displayed in Fig. 4D, reveals that Sodium (Na) and Potassium (K) were the most prevalent minerals, while Magnesium (Mg) and Calcium (Ca) were present in lower but essential concentrations. These minerals play crucial roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis and activating enzymatic processes.

The molecular weight distribution of peptides, analyzed in Fig. 4E, indicates that most peptides fell within the 500–3000 Da range, considered optimal for cellular uptake and metabolic assimilation. This distribution suggests that the peptones contain bioavailable peptides that can efficiently support cell growth. Additionally, the average molecular weight of the peptones, summarized in Fig. 4F, reflects variations in the hydrolysis process used during their production. These differences may influence the solubility, bioavailability, and metabolic impact of the peptones when incorporated into culture media.

So, our results highlight the diverse biochemical composition of the peptones, demonstrating their potential as a source of essential nutrients for cell culture applications. The variations in amino acid, sugar, mineral, and peptide profiles suggest that specific peptone preparations may be optimized to meet the unique metabolic needs of different cell lines or bioprocessing requirements.

As expected, CHO-K1 proliferation was reduced under 1% FBS compared with the 10% FBS control. Supplementation with several peptones improved viable cell density under serum-reduced conditions, although the magnitude of the effect varied depending on the peptone type and concentration. Overall, the response was nonlinear, with low concentrations generally being more favorable than higher concentrations.

For wheat- and pea-derived peptones, supplementation at 1–2 mg/mL generally improved CHO-K1 cell growth compared with the 1% FBS condition. In several experiments, viable cell densities approached those observed under the 10% FBS control, although statistical significance was not consistently reached across all conditions. For broad bean-derived peptone (Fig. 7), the strongest improvement was observed at 1 mg/mL, whereas higher concentrations were less favorable.

For soy- and rice-derived peptones, as well as for the chickpea–rice mixture (Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10), supplementation at low concentrations also improved cell growth compared with the 1% FBS condition, although viable cell densities remained below those observed under 10% FBS.

Based on these observations, W106, P118, and F120 appeared to provide the most consistent improvements under the tested serum-reduced conditions, particularly at low concentrations.

At higher concentrations, reduced cell growth was frequently observed, suggesting that excessive hydrolysate supplementation may negatively affect CHO-K1 proliferation.

These results indicate a nonlinear relationship between peptone concentration and cell growth.

This investigation was conducted to determine whether the growth inhibition observed at high peptone concentrations could be attributed to changes in osmolality. For all peptones tested, osmolality increased proportionally with peptone concentration as shown in Fig. 11 and detailed in Table 3. Discussion—Conclusion

The results of this study underscore the adaptability of CHO-K1 cells to reduced serum conditions and the potential of peptone supplementation as an alternative strategy to support cell growth and proliferation. By systematically reducing serum concentrations, we observed that CHO-K1 cells could acclimate to a 1% FBS environment over 57 days, although with an initial decline in viable cell density. This adaptability highlights the robustness of CHO-K1 cells and their suitability for serum-reduction strategies in biomanufacturing processes, aligning with previous studies [10,18], which demonstrated that CHO cell lines could adapt to serum-free media through gradual adaptation protocols.

The progressive reduction in serum concentration revealed distinct phases of adaptation. As expected, cell density remained consistently higher in the 10% FBS condition, serving as a control for optimal growth. However, under reduced serum conditions, viable cell density decreased initially, particularly at FBS concentrations below 1%. This decline is consistent with reports [19,20], which highlighted the critical role of serum components, such as growth factors and adhesion molecules [9], in supporting cell growth and attachment. Despite the initial decline, the ability of cells to recover and stabilize their growth under low-serum conditions suggests that intrinsic cellular mechanisms, including metabolic and transcriptomic adjustments, may compensate for the reduced availability of exogenous growth factors.

Our study demonstrates that peptone supplementation can mitigate the challenges of reduced serum availability, providing critical nutrients such as amino acids, sugars, and minerals. The biochemical characterization of peptones (Fig. 4) revealed their diverse composition, with amino acids such as Glutamate, Arginine, and Leucine playing pivotal roles in cellular metabolism and protein synthesis. The free amino acid content, particularly in C—CELL P118 and C—CELL F120 (Fig. 4B and Table 2), underscores their bioavailability and potential to support enhanced cellular growth. However, a high free amino acid content did not systematically correlate with increased proliferation, as observed for C—CELL PR124 (Fig. 10). The absence of proportional proliferative effects despite high free amino acid content may reflect imbalances in peptide composition, suboptimal molecular weight distribution, or the presence of inhibitory components. Previous studies have shown that not only amino acid abundance, but also peptide structure and transport kinetics, influence CHO metabolic responses and nutrient utilization efficiency. Similarly, the molecular weight distribution of peptides suggests that peptones are optimized for cellular uptake (Fig. 4E). This aligns with the findings of Duarte et al. [21] who demonstrated that CHO cells exhibit a strong metabolic dependency on key amino acids, notably asparagine and serine, whose rapid availability is critical to support cell growth, energy metabolism, and biosynthesis. These observations emphasize the importance of a finely tuned nutrient feeding strategy, facilitated by peptides linked to their molecular weight profile. This profile maximizes absorption and allows precise fulfillment of the cells metabolic demands by Duarte et al. Several studies have demonstrated that the presence of high molecular weight peptides within protein hydrolysates can enhance cellular metabolism and improve amino acid utilization efficiency [22,23].

Wheat peptones, evaluated over 4 days (Fig. 5), showed favorable effects on CHO-K1 proliferation under low-serum conditions. At 1 mg/mL, viable cell densities tended to approach those observed under the 10% FBS control, although this effect was not systematically statistically significant. These results suggest that wheat peptones may provide a favorable nutrient profile supporting cell growth under serum-reduced conditions. The enhanced performance of wheat peptones may also be attributed to their higher levels of bioavailable sugars and essential minerals, as suggested by Silva, J. S. et al. [24].

Soy peptones showed a concentration-dependent enhancement of CHO-K1 cell growth under 1% FBS conditions (Fig. 8). The highest viable cell density was observed at 2 mg/mL soy peptone supplementation, which provided sufficient nutrients to complement the minimal serum concentration. These observations suggest that peptone supplementation alone is not sufficient to fully compensate for the absence of serum-derived components required for optimal CHO-K1 proliferation, corroborating findings by Zhang et al. [25], who emphasized that specific growth factors and adhesion molecules present in media are indispensable for optimal CHO cell proliferation.

These results demonstrate that supplementation with plant-derived peptones can partially mitigate the growth impairment caused by serum reduction in CHO-K1 adherent cultures. Among the tested sources, wheat (Fig. 5) and broad bean (Fig. 7) peptones generally showed the strongest stimulatory effects, likely reflecting their favorable amino acid balance and peptide composition. Pea (Fig. 6) and soy peptones (Fig. 8) also contributed positively, albeit with more moderate effects, while rice peptone C—CELL R123 (Fig. 9) and a mix of chickpea and rice C—CELL PR124 (Fig. 10) showed only modest effects, indicating that not all plant sources are equally suitable for serum substitution.

Overall, the analyses suggest that selected peptones, especially wheat W106 and broad bean F120, may contribute to improved CHO-K1 proliferation under serum-reduced conditions. These observations support the hypothesis that specific hydrolysates provide nutrients and bioactive peptides capable of partially compensating for serum reduction.

Although statistical significance was not consistently observed across all tested conditions, the biological trends remained reproducible and coherent across independent experiments, particularly for selected hydrolysates such as W106.

Our experiments indicate that the osmolality of the supplemented cell culture media is not considerate as high osmolality > 450 mOsm/ kg H2O, (Fig. 11 and Table 3), which has been reported to impair cell proliferation and viability [4]. However, the inhibitory effects observed at higher peptone concentrations are likely mediated by complex mechanisms beyond osmolality alone. Further mechanistic investigations will be required to elucidate the cellular responses underlying the observed effects.

Protein hydrolysates not only provide a source of utilizable amino acids but also contain peptides—ranging from di- and tripeptides to higher molecular weight fractions—that can exert specific bioactivities, including mimicking growth factors or survival signals. Several studies have demonstrated that these peptides enhance cellular metabolism and improve amino acid utilization efficiency, thereby supporting cell proliferation and function [26].

Reducing serum dependency in CHO-K1 cell cultures may contribute to improved process robustness and cost management in research and early-stage development settings. Serum is a major cost driver and introduces variability and potential contamination risks in cell culture systems. By incorporating peptone supplementation, it is possible to achieve improved cell growth under serum-reduced conditions as demonstrated in this study. Wheat and broad bean peptones emerged as promising supplements for adherent CHO-K1 cultures.

While this study demonstrates the potential of peptone supplementation, several limitations should be addressed in future research. First, the inability of the peptones studied in this work to completely replace serum components underscores the need for further optimization in supplementation strategies, currently being addressed by our laboratory. Incorporating recombinant growth factors, adhesion molecules, or well-characterized defined supplements may provide a synergistic effect, enhancing cell growth under serum-free conditions. Additionally, long-term stability and productivity of CHO-K1 cells in reduced-serum media supplemented with peptones will require further investigation to ensure consistency and scalability in industrial settings.

Future studies should also explore the impact of peptone supplementation on recombinant protein production and quality, key metrics for evaluating the feasibility of bioprocessing strategies.

Furthermore, the mechanisms underlying the inhibitory effects of high concentrations of wheat – soy – pea – broad bean peptones call for detailed investigation to optimize their use. Advanced omics approaches, such as metabolomics and proteomics, may provide valuable insights into cellular responses to different peptone types and concentrations, facilitating the development of tailored supplementation strategies.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that CHO-K1 cells can adapt to progressively reduced serum environments, with peptone supplementation playing a critical role in supporting cell growth and proliferation. Among the peptones evaluated, wheat and broad bean peptones emerged as the most promising supplements under low-serum conditions, highlighting their potential for optimizing serum-reduced culture conditions in research and development contexts. The findings underscore the importance of selecting peptones based on their biochemical composition and compatibility with specific cell lines and culture conditions.

By reducing serum dependency, peptone supplementation offers a potentially cost-effective approach for improving cell growth under serum-reduced conditions, paving the way for more sustainable and efficient bioprocessing practices. Further research should aim to optimize these strategies by integrating additional defined and advanced analytical approaches. Overall, the data support the use of selected plant peptones as useful complements or serum-reduced CHO culture systems. Many questions remain unanswered, and further research is needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Importantly, the present study was conducted in adherent CHO-K1 cells and focused on cell proliferation rather than recombinant protein production or product quality attributes. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted within a research-scale and early-stage development context.
