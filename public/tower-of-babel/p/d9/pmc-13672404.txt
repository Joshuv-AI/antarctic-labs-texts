Preparation and Physicochemical Characterization of Chitosan-Coated Iron Oxide Nanoparticles Conjugated with Oxacillin: Antimicrobial, Antibiofilm, and Biocompatibility Activities against MRSA Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Zainab Hussein Jassim, Mais Emad Ahmed, Ali Z Al-Saffar, Saleh M Al-Maaqar, Hasan A Aal Owaif, Hakimi Kassim, Jameel R Al-Obaidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe growing threat of antimicrobial resistance and biofilm-associated infections has critically undermined the efficacy of conventional antibiotics. Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) represents a clinically significant multidrug-resistant pathogen renowned for its capacity to form protective biofilms that confer resistance to therapeutic agents. The present study investigated a nanocomposite system comprising chitosan-coated iron oxide nanoparticles conjugated with oxacillin (IONPs-CS-OXA) with the aim of enhancing antimicrobial and antibiofilm efficacy against MRSA. Iron oxide nanoparticles were synthesized via chemical co-precipitation, subsequently coated with chitosan, and conjugated with oxacillin. The nanocomposite was characterized by UV–Vis spectroscopy, FT–IR, XRD, zeta potential analysis, DLS, FE-SEM, and TEM. Multidrug-resistant MRSA isolates obtained from clinical specimens were subjected to antibiotic susceptibility testing, biofilm quantification, minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination, and assessment of antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity. Biocompatibility was evaluated via MTT assays using HdFn and WRL-68 cell lines. Physicochemical characterization confirmed the successful fabrication of spherical IONPs-CS-OXA nanoparticles with nanoscale dimensions and a stable surface charge. MRSA isolates demonstrated high resistance to multiple antibiotics, including oxacillin. The nanocomposite markedly enhanced antimicrobial efficacy, as evidenced by significantly lower MIC values relative to free oxacillin. Inhibition zones were notably enlarged against tested MRSA strains, while biofilm formation was reduced by 54–88%. Cytotoxicity assays confirmed minimal adverse effects on normal cells, with cell viability exceeding 80%. The synergistic interaction among chitosan, iron oxide nanoparticles, and oxacillin conferred enhanced antibacterial and antibiofilm performance alongside acceptable biocompatibility. The IONPs-CS-OXA nanocomposite constitutes a promising candidate platform that, subject to further validation, may augment antibiotic delivery and help combat multidrug-resistant MRSA infections.Keywords: MRSA, Iron oxide nanoparticles, Chitosan, Oxacillin, Antibiofilm, Biocompatibility

Introduction

The escalating public health crisis of bacterial resistance and microbial biofilm formation demands the development of innovative treatment strategies that surpass the limitations of traditional antibiotics [1]. Once considered the cornerstone of infection management, antibiotics are increasingly rendered ineffective against a growing number of pathogenic bacteria owing to acquired resistance mechanisms and the proliferation of multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens. MDR pathogens acquire resistance through genetic mutations and the horizontal transfer of mobile genetic elements, collectively diminishing the therapeutic effectiveness of conventional antibiotics [2]. These developments underscore the urgent necessity for the discovery and development of more potent antimicrobial agents. A landmark global estimate of antimicrobial resistance projected that nearly 39 million individuals could die as a consequence of antibiotic-resistant pathogens between the present day and 2050 [3].

Infections caused by Staphylococcus aureus are of considerable clinical significance and contribute substantially to the escalating burden of antimicrobial resistance and nosocomial infection [4]. S. aureus causes a broad spectrum of infections in humans and animals, ranging from mild skin infections to severe systemic infections like pneumonia and septicemia [5]. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is a significant emerging pathogen associated with antimicrobial resistance to different types of antibiotics, including β-lactam antibiotics [6, 7]. MRSA strains inactivate β-lactam antibiotics by modifying penicillin-binding proteins — notably PBP2a [8], and the production of β-lactam-degrading enzymes [9]. Beyond the biochemical intricacies of MDR, the ability of MRSA to form biofilms further complicates treatment [10]. These structured microbial communities confer protection against immune responses and therapeutic agents, rendering biofilm-associated infections particularly refractory to eradication. Nanotechnology has emerged as a powerful strategy for overcoming these challenges by enabling the manipulation of materials at the molecular scale, thereby enhancing antimicrobial efficacy while minimizing adverse effects [11]. The incorporation of antibiotics within nanocarriers has been shown to enhance drug efficacy by providing a protective barrier that limits drug inactivation by biofilm-associated enzymes and prevents premature drug degradation [12].

Biofilm-associated infections account for approximately 65–80% of all microbial infections encountered in clinical settings, with MRSA biofilms frequently implicated in chronic wound infections, endocarditis, osteomyelitis, and medical device-related infections [13]. The extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix — comprising polysaccharides, proteins, extracellular DNA, and lipids — functions as a physical and chemical barrier that reduces antibiotic penetration by up to 1000-fold compared to planktonic counterparts [14]. This structural complexity renders conventional antibiotic monotherapy largely ineffective against established biofilms, necessitating novel delivery strategies capable of disrupting biofilm architecture and delivering therapeutic payloads directly to embedded bacterial cells [15].

Chitosan, a naturally occurring polymer derived from deacetylated chitin, is particularly promising for its inherent bacteriostatic/bactericide properties, biocompatibility, and capacity for functionalization [16]. Chitosan nanoparticles exhibit remarkable biological efficacy, notably against MDR pathogens, attributed to their diminutive dimensions, quantum confinement effects, enhanced sorption capacity, and potent antibacterial properties [17]. When combined with metal nanoparticles — such as magnetic iron oxide — chitosan confers enhanced mechanical stability and antimicrobial activity. Antibiotic-loaded chitosan-coated magnetic nanoparticles exploit these advantages to enable targeted antibiotic delivery to infection sites, thereby circumventing the systemic toxicity and suboptimal efficacy associated with conventional drug delivery systems [18].

Iron oxide nanoparticles (IONPs) are favoured for their biocompatibility and easy synthesis [19], leading to their swift application in magnetic cell labelling, tracking, separation, catalysis, biomarker identification, drug delivery, and antimicrobial functions following surface functionalization [20]. Furthermore, nano treatment systems utilizing IONPs can diminish the therapeutic dosages of medications, enhance their efficacy, and facilitate targeted drug delivery to certain tissues or cells [21]. IONPs offer additional benefits through the potential to disrupt bacterial biofilms [22]. IONPs have the advantages of modification via interaction with different biomolecules, including antibiotics, peptides and proteins as a bio-nanosystem, thereby boosting the antimicrobial activity [23]. However, the surface of IONPs necessitates modification through coating with non-toxic, biodegradable polymeric carriers [24] to mitigate agglomeration arising from their high surface-to-volume ratio and susceptibility to oxidation. By integrating chitosan with IONPs, a dual-functional system is created that is capable of penetrating biofilms, delivering localized high concentrations of antibiotics, and minimizing the likelihood of resistance development [25].

The deliberate selection of oxacillin in this study, despite documented MRSA resistance, is strategically motivated. Oxacillin remains a clinically relevant β-lactam antibiotic whose efficacy against susceptible staphylococcal strains is well established; however, its utility against MRSA is curtailed by the expression of PBP2a, a low-affinity penicillin-binding protein encoded by mecA that sustains cell-wall synthesis in the presence of β-lactams [26]. Importantly, this resistance reflects reduced binding affinity and limited local drug availability rather than complete drug inactivity, and the majority of clinical MRSA exhibit a heterogeneous phenotype in which most cells display only low-level resistance [27]. These features provide a mechanistic rationale for delivery-based strategies: by concentrating oxacillin at the bacterial cell wall and protecting it from enzymatic degradation, nanoparticle carriers may raise the effective local drug concentration sufficiently to restore β-lactam activity. By encapsulating oxacillin within a nanocarrier system, it is hypothesized that the nanoformulation may partially restore its antimicrobial activity by bypassing conventional resistance mechanisms, enhancing membrane penetration, and delivering sustained local concentrations that exceed the resistance threshold [28].

In light of the above, the present study aimed to synthesize and characterize a chitosan-coated iron oxide nanoparticle system conjugated with oxacillin (IONPs-CS-OXA), and to evaluate its antimicrobial, antibiofilm, and cytotoxic properties against clinically isolated MRSA strains. The overarching objective was to establish proof-of-concept for a multi-functional nanoplatform capable of simultaneously addressing antibiotic resistance and biofilm-mediated treatment failure in MRSA infections.

Materials and Methods

Synthesis of IONPs-CS-OXA

IONPs were prepared based on the co-precipitation method employing the previously reported protocol [29]. An equal volume of FeCl2.4H2O (0.1 M) and FeCl3.6H2O (0.2 M) solutions (50 mL each) was mixed and stirred until a homogenous solution was formed. The mixture was heated at 60 °C for 20 min. After heating, an aliquot of 14 mL of NaOH solution (25%) was added with constant agitation. The change of mixture solution from yellow to brownish-black precipitate indicated the formation of metallic Fe3O4 nanostructure, signifying the reduction of FeCl2 and FeCl3. The mixture was subsequently subjected to centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 10 min, and the resulting precipitate was rinsed three times with D.W. The precipitate was collected and dried in an oven at 60 °C to obtain colloidal IONPs. Chitosan-coated IONPs were prepared by mixing 70 mg of prepared colloidal IONPs in 100 mL of newly prepared chitosan solution (20 mg chitosan in 75 mL D.W. and 6.3 mL acetic acid). The mixture was maintained overnight while stirring at approximately 25 °C. During the procedure, Chitosan molecules were immobilized on the surface of IONPs through adsorption interactions, leading to a noticeable, distinct color transition from black to brown. Following the alteration in color, the suspension was subjected to centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 30 min. The collected pellet was rinsed with D.W. to remove residual, unbound chitosan and acetic acid. The pellet was completely dried at 70 °C to obtain IONPs-CS. The fabrication of IONPs-CS-OXA complex was synthesized by direct solubilization of oxacillin and IONPs-CS (both at a concentration of 1 mg mL− 1) in D.W. The mixture was maintained under magnetic stirring (120 rpm) at ambient temperature for 1 h to obtain the IONPs-CS-OXA nanosystem.

Characterization of IONPs-CS-OXA

UV/Visible Spectroscopy

The spectral absorption pattern of oxacillin, chitosan, and colloidal IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA was obtained using a UV/Vis spectrophotometer UV-1280 (Shimadzu, Japan). The absorption spectrum for all examined materials was obtained within the region of 200 to 800 nm. The measurement was conducted 24 h post-preparation, and the wavelength of maximum absorption (λmax) was determined for each component individually. Deionized water was used as the blank reference for background correction.

Fourier Transform Infrared (FT-IR) Spectroscopy

FT-IR spectroscopy was conducted utilizing an FT-IR spectrometer (8400 S Shimadzu, Japan). FT-IR spectroscopy was conducted to identify the functional groups present in oxacillin, chitosan, colloidal IONPs, and IONPs-CS-OXA across the spectral region of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ using a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹. The solution was prepared by combining 0.1 mL of the sample with 100 mg of KBr and subsequently compressing it into a disk for analysis.

X-Ray Diffraction (XRD)

The crystallographic/amorphous structural nature of synthesized IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA was analyzed by XRD (Torontech, USA). The crystallographic analysis of IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA in diffraction patterns was recorded at the range of 2θ from 10° to 70° with a scan rate of 20° min−1 using Cu Kα (λ = 1.541 A°) and operated at an accelerating voltage of 40 kV and 30 mA current. Zeta-Potential and Particle Size Analysis

Zeta potential, particle size, surface charge, and polydispersity index (PDI) of IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA were determined using a ZetaPLUS particle size analyzer. The surface charge and electrophoretic mobility of the particles were evaluated based on electrophoretic light scattering (ELS), while particle size distribution and PDI were determined using dynamic light scattering (DLS). Prior to measurement, the samples underwent dilution in double-D.W. and were briefly sonicated for 5 min to minimize particle aggregation. Experiments were performed in triplicate at 25 °C with the scattering angle fixed at 90°.

Field Emission-Scanning Electron Microscope (FE-SEM)

FE-SEM was employed to analyze the morphology and dimensions of IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA. For FE-SEM analysis, samples were prepared by depositing drops of IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA solutions onto carbon-coated copper FE-SEM grids, which were subsequently allowed to air dry at ambient temperature. FE-SEM pictures were obtained utilizing a VEGA 3 (TESCAN, Czech Republic). The mean diameter, length, and area were quantified from FE-SEM images using ImageJ.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

TEM was used to estimate the features, shape, and particle size of IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA. A single drop of IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA dispersions was mounted on copper grids and air-dried before TEM observation. TEM imaging was conducted at 120 kV accelerating voltage, and particle size characteristics (diameter, length, and area) were quantified using ImageJ software.

Determination of Drug Loading and Encapsulation Efficiency

The drug loading capacity (LC) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) of oxacillin within the IONPs-CS-OXA nanosystem were determined indirectly by quantifying the unbound oxacillin remaining in the supernatant [31]. Following conjugation, the nanoconjugate suspension was centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was collected. The concentration of free oxacillin was determined by UV–Vis spectrophotometry at λmax (270 nm) against a standard calibration curve of oxacillin prepared in deionized water over the range of 5–200 µg mL⁻¹. Bacterial Strains and Culture Conditions

A total of 10 MRSA strains were kindly provided by the Microbiology Strain Bank, College of Biotechnology – Al-Nahrain University. The strains were previously isolated from nasal swabs (1), wounds (7), and burn (2) specimens and identified as MRSA following steps of cultural, microscopical, and biochemical identifications. The strains were further authenticated by the VITEK®2 Compact system (bioMerieux, France). MRSA strains were cultured in tryptic soy broth (TSB) under aerobic conditions with continuous agitation at 200 rpm and maintained at 37 °C. For each experiment, an overnight preculture was used as the inoculum, and the initial optical density at 578 nm was standardized to a range of 0.05–0.1.

Antibiotic Susceptibility Testing of MRSA Strains

The susceptibility of isolates to antibiotics was determined by the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion assay, following the protocol described by Hudzicki [32]. In brief, each MRSA strain was cultured in 5 mL of nutrient broth and incubated at 37 °C overnight. The resulting bacterial cultures were then centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 10 min. Cell pellets were resuspended in sterile normal saline, and each suspension was standardized to 0.5 McFarland turbidity (1.5 × 108 CFU mL− 1). MRSA inoculum was evenly distributed over the surface of dried Mueller-Hinton agar plates. Antibiotic disks, erythromycin (15 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), clindamycin (2 µg), oxacillin (30 µg), gentamycin (10 µg), cefoxitin (30 µg), imipenem (10 µg), and levofloxacin (5 µg), were aseptically placed onto the inoculated agar surface using sterile forceps. The plates were subsequently incubated at 37 °C overnight. Following incubation, the diameter of each inhibition zone, defined as the clear area surrounding the disc where no visible bacterial growth was observed, was measured in mm. The results were interpreted by comparing the measured zone diameters with the standard breakpoint values published by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) [33] to classify each isolate as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R) to the tested antibiotics.

Quantitative Biofilm Formation Assessment

The ability of MRSA strains to produce biofilm was evaluated based on the method reported by Singh et al. [34]. MRSA strains were inoculated in 5 mL of Brain Heart Infusion broth (BHI) enriched with 2% sucrose and subjected to incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, an aliquot of 20 µL of microbial suspension (equivalent to 1.5 × 108 CFU mL− 1) was used to inoculate 96-microliter wells containing 180 µL of BHI (2% sucrose) broth in each well plate and incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. Wells contained only BHI broth, considered as a negative control. Following incubation, the plate was washed three times with a normal saline solution. The biofilm-forming adherent cells were fixed with 200 µL of methanol (99%) for 15 min. The methanol was decanted, and the plate was placed at room temperature for 30 min to dry. Then, 200 µL of crystal violet (1%) was added to each well for 15 min. The staining step was stopped by washing the plate with D, W., and the attached dye was solubilized with ethanol (96%), and the optical density was measured in a micro-titer plate reader (Bio-Tek, USA) at 630 nm. The biofilm formation was evaluated after determining the cut-off optical density (ODc) (average OD of negative control + 3 times standard deviation) and strains were classified as non-biofilm former (OD ≤ ODc), weak biofilm former (2ODc > OD > ODc), moderate biofilm former (4 ODc > OD > 2 ODc), and strong biofilm former (OD > 4 ODc).

MIC of IONPs-CS-OXA

The MIC value of IONPs-CS-OXA against MRSA strains that showed significant biofilm formation and multiple antibiotic resistance was measured using the broth dilution technique [35]. Microbial inoculum was prepared by cultivating the test organism in 5 mL of nutrient broth at 37 °C for approximately 24 h. The resulting culture was then standardized to a turbidity equivalent to 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU mL− 1 using sterile saline solution. A range of treatment concentrations (0–512 µg mL− 1) was generated through serial dilution in nutrient broth, with each tube adjusted to a final volume of 3 mL. Each dilution was inoculated with 0.3 mL of the standardized microbial suspension. Following incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, microbial growth was assessed visually. The MIC was defined as the lowest concentration at which no visible turbidity was observed.

Antimicrobial Activity of IONPs-CS-OXA

Antimicrobial activity was examined using the agar well diffusion method to detect antimicrobial activity of IONPs-CS-OXA against MRSA strains at MIC and sub-MIC compared to oxacillin and IONPs [36]. Microbial suspension was adjusted to 1.5 × 108 CFU mL− 1 after overnight incubation. A suspension of bacteria adjusted to a defined concentration was uniformly applied onto Mueller–Hinton agar plates using a sterile swab and allowed to stand for approximately 10 min to facilitate absorption. Wells with a diameter of 5 mm were then carefully created in the agar. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of each treatment solution was dispensed into the respective wells using a micropipette. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, inhibition zone diameters were measured, with sterile normal saline used as the negative control.

Biocompatibility of IONPs-CS-OXA

This in vitro cytotoxicity method was performed to investigate the biocompatibility of IONPs-CS-OXA against the normal cell lines HdFn and WRL-68. HdFn and WRL-68 cells were cultured in complete DMEM medium (10% fetal bovine serum, benzyl penicillin 103 IU, streptomycin 103 IU) in a humidified atmosphere, supplemented with 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 24 h. At 80% confluency, cells were washed with PBS, trypsinized briefly (2–3 min), and detached for sub-culturing [37]. The cytotoxic effect of IONPs-CS-OXA was performed by using 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. Cells (2 × 104 cells mL− 1) were seeded into a 96-well tissue culture plate for 24 h. After incubation, the medium was removed and filter-sterilized IONPs-CS-OXA was added at concentrations ranging from 0 to 400 µg mL− 1 in DMEM and incubated at 37 °C with 95% humidity and 5% CO2 for 24 h. Post-incubation, 100 µL of medium was aspirated from each well, and 20 µL of freshly prepared MTT solution (5 mg mL− 1) was added, followed by a 4-hour incubation at 37 °C. Following removal of the medium, the formazan crystals were solubilized in 100 µL of DMSO per well. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using an ELISA reader. All concentrations were tested in triplicate, and IC50 values were calculated from the MTT assay results.

Statistical Analysis

Comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s post hoc test to evaluate differences among groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, while p < 0.01 indicated a higher level of significance. All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.0, GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results

Synthesis of IONPs-CS-OXA

The formation of IONPs from FeCl2.4H2O and FeCl3.6H2O was visually inspected by a brownish-black precipitate (Fig. 1A). Coating IONPs with chitosan resulted in a color change of the mixture into dark brown, indicating the formation IONPs-CS. This visible transition further supports successful surface modification, as chitosan adsorption typically alters the optical properties of iron oxide nanoparticles. The final step in synthesizing IONPs-CS-OXA was a direct mixing of chitosan-coated IONPs with oxacillin. As illustrated in Fig. 1B, the addition of oxacillin solution to IONPs-CS with continuous stirring resulted in a brownish precipitate, and the final product was designated as IONPs-CS-OXA. The absence of phase separation or precipitation instability suggests effective interaction between oxacillin and the chitosan-coated nanoparticle surface.

UV-Vis spectra of IONPs-CS-OXA and its individual components are shown in Fig. 2. The absorption profile of IONPs (Black Line) revealed a maximum absorption peak at 246 nm, while the maximum absorption peak for IONPs-CS-OXA exhibited a slight red shift in surface plasmon resonance at 261 nm. This shift in absorption peak may indicate successful conjugation and changes in the local electronic environment of the nanoparticles following coating and drug loading. FT-IR spectra of IONPs, chitosan, oxacillin, and IONPs-CS-OXA are presented in Fig. 3.

The shift in the absorption maximum from 246 nm (IONPs) to 261 nm (IONPs-CS-OXA) indicates successful surface coating and drug conjugation

FT-IR absorption spectra of IONPs (Black line), chitosan (Red line), Oxacillin (Blue line), and IONPs-CS-OXA (Green line). The experiment was performed at room temperature and a 4000–350 cm− 1 scanning range

In the chitosan spectrum, an absorption peak was detected at 1068 cm-1, which corresponds to C-O-C bond vibration, while in IONPs-CS-OXA, a peak shifting with increased intensity was recorded at 1051 cm-1. The stretching peak at 1670 cm-1 within the oxacillin spectrum was assigned to N-H amine bond vibration. The N-H bond vibration was demonstrated with slight shifting at 1618 cm-1. The characteristic vibration peak of chitosan at 1335 cm-1 associated with C-H bond stretching, shifted to 1412 cm-1 in IONPs-CS-OXA. Finally, all the spectra shared the peak at 3412 cm-1, which reflected the O-H bond stretching vibration. These spectral shifts collectively confirm the presence of intermolecular interactions among IONPs, chitosan, and oxacillin, indicating successful formation of the nanocomposite system. Quantitative analysis revealed that oxacillin was efficiently associated with the chitosan-coated nanoparticles, with an encapsulation efficiency of 87.38 ± 3.39% and a drug loading capacity of 31.92 ± 1.56%. These values confirm the successful conjugation inferred from the UV–Vis and FT–IR spectral shifts and indicate that the IONPs-CS carrier provides [an effective/a substantial] reservoir for oxacillin delivery. The X-ray diffraction pattern of IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA was represented in Fig. 4.

XRD pattern of (A) IONPs and (B) IONPs-CS-OXA. Peaks labelled with an arrow correspond to characteristic peaks of chitosan

IONPs exhibited six distinct peaks (2θ = 15°, 36°, 43°, 57°, 63° and 75°) corresponding to the typical face-centred cubic structure with crystallographic indices of (111), (311), (400), (511), (440), and (533), respectively. In general, the diffractogram of IONPs-CS-OXA matched the most predominant peaks of magnetite that were presented in the IONPs diffractogram (2θ = 36° and 63°). The fabrication of IONPs with chitosan and oxacillin broadened the peaks and caused a shift toward higher 2θ and a reduction in peak intensity. In addition, the XRD pattern of IONPs-CS-OXA showed the presence of chitosan as a very faint broad band at 2θ 20°–23°. The broadening and reduced intensity of peaks suggest partial amorphization due to polymer coating, which is commonly observed in polymer-functionalized nanomaterials. The average particle size of IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA was determined using Debye-Scherrer formula based on peaks presented in IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA diffractions. The calculated particle size for IONPs was 21.73 ± 3.73 nm, while that of IONPs-CS-OXA was 57.29 ± 6.64 nm. The increase in particle size further supports successful coating and conjugation processes. The surface charge and particle size of IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA were determined by analyzing the zeta potential and particle stability in colloidal solutions. ELS analysis showed that IONPs synthesized by the precipitation method upon NaOH reduction possessed a strongly negative surface charge, as indicated by a zeta potential of −76.75 mV.

The average particle size and PDI of IONPs detected by DLS were 34.05 ± 6.70 nm and 0.26, respectively (Fig. 5A). The negativity and surface charge of IONPs were reduced to −31.77 mV in IONPs-CS-OXA nano-conjugation accompanied by increased PDI and particle size, 0.54 and 94.39 ± 8.61 nm, respectively (Fig. 5B). The reduction in zeta potential magnitude indicates surface modification by chitosan and oxacillin, while the increased PDI suggests a broader particle size distribution due to aggregation or coating heterogeneity.

Morphological characteristics of IONPs and IONPs-CS-OXA were determined by FE-SEM. As shown in Fig. 6A, the synthesized IONPs were predominantly spherical in shape with an average particle size of 32.9 ± 8.6 nm in diameter. Images of IONPs-CS-OXA indicated that the complex maintained the spherical shape with increased agglomeration. The average particle size of IONPs-CS-OXA was 58.3 ± 9.1 nm in diameter (Fig. 6B).

Discussion

The incorporation of oxacillin into chitosan-coated IONPs yielded significant advances in combating MRSA. Findings from MIC determination and well-diffusion assays collectively demonstrated a synergistic interaction among the nanoformulation components in inhibiting bacterial growth. Notably, the reduction in MIC values relative to free oxacillin indicates that the nanoformulation enhances drug bioavailability at the bacterial interface, a factor of critical importance in overcoming resistance. Vancomycin conjugated with metal nanocomposites — including IONPs — has been reported to enhance antimicrobial activity against MRSA relative to free vancomycin. Similarly, nanoconjugated vancomycin and teicoplanin with IONPs were formulated as chemically stable constructs with a conserved antibacterial spectrum against pathogenic bacteria, including MRSA [38]. IONPs-CS-OXA may enhance antimicrobial activity through several proposed mechanisms.

The antimicrobial performance of IONPs-CS-OXA is consistent with, and in several respects superior to, analogous nanocomposite systems reported in recent literature. Most directly comparable is an oxacillin-loaded chitosan nanoparticle system co-delivering DNase I, which reduced S. aureus biofilm more effectively than drug-free nanoparticles and achieved up to 98% biofilm reduction after repeated treatment [39], closely paralleling the reduction obtained with a single sub-MIC exposure of IONPs-CS-OXA. The present platform is also structurally analogous to a chitosan-coated iron oxide system loaded with the antimicrobial peptide nisin (N-CS-IONP), which exhibited enhanced antibacterial and antibiofilm activity relative to bare IONPs and free drug [40]. More broadly, recent β-lactam–iron oxide combinations have been reported to restore efficacy against resistant organisms, and chitosan-based nanomaterials have achieved substantial antibiofilm activity against MRSA [41]. Collectively, these comparisons situate IONPs-CS-OXA within the current landscape of nanoparticle-mediated anti-MRSA strategies and underscore the advantage of combining a cationic chitosan coating, a magnetic iron oxide core, and a conjugated β-lactam within a single platform.

Chitosan coating improves biocompatibility and promotes cellular internalization. Furthermore, chitosan interacts electrostatically with the bacterial cell membrane owing to charge complementarity, facilitating improved antibiotic penetration [42]. This electrostatic interaction may also lead to partial destabilization of the bacterial membrane, thereby increasing permeability and allowing greater intracellular accumulation of oxacillin. IONPs function as efficient drug carriers owing to their elevated surface-to-volume ratio, superparamagnetic characteristics, and capacity for magnetic guidance to infection sites. Their nanoscale dimensions (generally 10–100 nm) facilitate enhanced penetration through bacterial biofilms in comparison to free antibiotics. The magnetic properties facilitate targeted delivery, which may decrease systemic exposure and related side effects [43]. Biofilm formation is well recognized to augment microbial resistance against both antimicrobials and environmental stressors [44]; accordingly, overcoming biofilm-associated barriers remains a central requirement for effective antimicrobial therapy, particularly in the context of chronic and device-associated infections.

Although IONPs alone exerted no direct inhibitory effect on bacterial growth, they may inhibit biofilm-embedded cells by degrading the biofilm matrix and disrupting non-specific physicochemical interactions that mediate bacterial surface attachment. This suggests that their role is predominantly supportive rather than directly bactericidal, reinforcing the rationale for combination platforms such as IONPs-CS-OXA. The chitosan component provides an initial antibiofilm effect through its polycationic nature, which disrupts bacterial adhesion to surfaces; the positive charge of chitosan interacts with negatively charged bacterial cell membranes, thereby interfering with early-stage attachment and biofilm development, and attenuating bacterial resistance to antibiotics. By virtue of its mucoadhesive properties, chitosan may enhance nanocarrier retention within target tissues while improving the pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of the encapsulated drug, thereby augmenting bacterial killing. The nanoscale dimensions of IONPs-CS-OXA enabled superior penetration through biofilm matrices, delivering oxacillin directly to the planktonic cells of MRSA — in contrast to free oxacillin, which is unable to penetrate mature biofilms owing to extracellular polymeric substances. IONPs as nanocarriers can navigate biofilm channels more effectively, thereby ensuring higher local antibiotic concentrations within the biofilm structure [45]. Moreover, IONPs have been reported to facilitate sustained drug release over an extended period [46], although release kinetics were not assessed in the present study. Such prolonged exposure, if confirmed, could contribute to consistent drug availability and inhibition of bacterial attachment and subsequent biofilm development. Should such sustained-release behavior be confirmed in future kinetic studies, it may further reduce dosing frequency, an advantage of considerable practical relevance in clinical settings where maintaining therapeutic concentrations poses a persistent challenge. The extensive resistance of S. aureus — and MRSA in particular — to a wide range of antibiotics poses a substantial threat to healthcare systems globally, severely restricting available therapeutic options. The observed resistance pattern can be attributed primarily to the mecA gene, which encodes an altered penicillin-binding protein with diminished affinity for β-lactam antibiotics, thereby permitting continued cell wall synthesis in their presence [47]. Notably, the enhanced efficacy of IONPs-CS-OXA against this resistance mechanism suggests that nanoparticle-mediated delivery systems may partially circumvent conventional resistance pathways. These findings align with a previous report demonstrating the sensitivity of S. aureus toward clindamycin (resistance observed in 7 of 43 isolates) [48]. The same study also showed that 51% of S. aureus isolates were susceptible to ciprofloxacin. On the contrary, research from Iran showed that MRSA strains isolated from hospitals in Tehran were resistant to clindamycin and ciprofloxacin at a rate of > 70% [49]. Biofilm formation represents a complex developmental process where bacteria transition from planktonic cells to surface-attached multicellular communities embedded within a matrix of extracellular polymeric substances [50]. IONPs-CS-OXA markedly reduced the formation of biofilms in MRSA, and such enhanced antibiofilm activity stems from multiple synergistic mechanisms related to nanoformulation design.

Despite an increase in particle size and a degree of particle aggregation following surface modification, IONPs-CS-OXA retained nanoscale characteristics after chitosan coating and oxacillin conjugation. Given that the chitosan layer thickness is directly proportional to individual particle dimensions, the particle size of IONPs-CS-OXA increased progressively throughout the coating process. Furthermore, the presence of biological macromolecules (chitosan and oxacillin) on the IONPs surface substantially promoted particle agglomeration [51] driven by polymer-mediated interparticle interactions, potentially involving bridging effects. Nevertheless, the resultant particle size range remains appropriate for biomedical applications, particularly with respect to biofilm penetration and cellular uptake.

The chitosan coating highly contributed to iron NPs biocompatibility, since chitosan is structurally similar to glycosaminoglycans, a component of the extracellular matrix, which reduces stress response and improves the proliferation of normal cells [52]. The toxicological profile of IONPs shows that IONPs have good biocompatibility, which primarily depends on size, surface coating, and concentration [53]. Many studies showed that at therapeutic doses, IONPs demonstrated absence or low adverse effects against normal cells. The cytotoxic activity of chitosan-IONPs prepared by Braim et al., against HEK-293 cells showed no obvious toxicity on cell viability [54]. Biocompatible magnetic IONPs conjugated with antibody J591, enhanced cellular uptake by prostate cancer cells without affecting cell viability, suggesting the improvement of cancer cell MRI diagnosis [55]. IONPs conjugated to macrophage membrane vesicles showed excellent biocompatibility for normal cells [56]. Jimenez et al., conducted disc diffusion studies to assess the susceptibility of S. aureus isolates to different antibiotics. The results indicated that all isolates were resistant to ampicillin and penicillin, while the remaining isolates exhibited varying degrees of resistance to the other antibiotics [57]. The fabricated IONPs-CS-OXA nanocomposite demonstrated potent antimicrobial and antibiofilm activities against clinical multidrug-resistant MRSA isolates examined in this study, with markedly reduced MIC values relative to free oxacillin. The nanosystem exhibited favorable biocompatibility toward normal human cell lines, thereby confirming its therapeutic safety profile. Collectively, the physicochemical characterization and biological evaluations support a synergistic antibacterial mechanism. On the basis of these findings, a multi-modal antibacterial mechanism is proposed (Fig. 13), involving electrostatic interaction mediated by chitosan, enhanced penetration through the EPS matrix, and localized oxacillin delivery to the peptidoglycan layer, culminating in membrane destabilization and biofilm disruption.

Proposed mechanistic illustration of the antimicrobial and antibiofilm activity of chitosan-coated iron oxide nanoparticles conjugated with oxacillin (IONPs-CS-OXA) against MRSA. The Fe₃O₄ core enables nanoscale delivery, while the cationic chitosan coating promotes electrostatic interaction with the negatively charged bacterial membrane. Surface-conjugated oxacillin enhances local antibiotic concentration at the cell wall, facilitating inhibition of penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs). The nanoscale size enables penetration through biofilm matrices and sustained drug release, collectively improving antibacterial efficacy and reducing biofilm formation. The illustrated mechanism is proposed on the basis of the nanocomposite’s physicochemical properties and supporting literature and has not been directly validated experimentally in this study

Several limitations of the present study merit acknowledgement. First, the study evaluation was based on a limited number of isolates: the ten MRSA strains examined represented the entire collection of confirmed MRSA isolates available in the institutional strain bank, of which four — selected for their extreme multidrug resistance and strong biofilm-forming capacity — were advanced to detailed antimicrobial and antibiofilm analysis. As the study was designed as a proof-of-concept evaluation of the IONPs-CS-OXA nanoplatform against the most resistant and biofilm-competent phenotypes rather than as an epidemiological or prevalence survey, the findings should be interpreted within this scope, and validation against a larger and more genetically diverse panel of clinical MRSA isolates across multiple centres is warranted to confirm their generalizability. Second, the biological evaluations were conducted exclusively under in vitro conditions, and the translation of these findings to in vivo models remains to be established. The long-term stability and release kinetics of IONPs-CS-OXA under physiological conditions warrant further investigation. Third, the potential immunogenic response to chitosan-coated nanoparticles in vivo, while generally considered low, should be systematically evaluated in future studies. Furthermore, although uncoated IONPs were included as a bare-carrier control, the present study did not include a dedicated parallel evaluation of chitosan alone and the drug-free IONPs-CS carrier within the same antimicrobial and antibiofilm assay series; a complete component-deconvolution series (chitosan, IONPs-CS, and IONPs-CS-OXA) in future work would allow formal quantification of the individual contribution of each constituent to the observed activity. Scaling up the synthesis process while maintaining nanoparticle uniformity and batch-to-batch reproducibility also represents a practical challenge that must be addressed prior to clinical translation. Future investigations should prioritize in vivo validation of IONPs-CS-OXA efficacy in murine MRSA infection models, optimization of surface functionalization for targeted tissue delivery, and assessment of pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic parameters. The modular design of this nanoplatform also lends itself to substitution or co-loading of alternative antibiotics, potentially broadening its therapeutic applicability to other MDR pathogens beyond MRSA.
