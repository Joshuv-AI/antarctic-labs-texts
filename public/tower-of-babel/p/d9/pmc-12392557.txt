It’s time to talk to emergency medical dispatchers: survey study on performance feedback and patient outcome follow-up to EMDs
Authors: Erik J. Wanberg, Greg Scott, Kristina M. Dostal, Aidan F. Mullan, Sarayna S. McGuire
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPerformance feedback and patient outcome follow-up are essential in emergency medical services. Prior literature has assessed the importance of feedback/follow-up on paramedics and emergency medical technicians, however there is a paucity of literature specific to emergency medical dispatchers (EMD). Our objective was to measure the frequency, satisfaction with, and importance of feedback/follow-up received by EMDs.MethodsAnonymous electronic survey study of United States (US)-based (approximately 60,000) members of the International Academies of Emergency Dispatch (IAED). Definitions of performance feedback and outcome follow-up were provided. Survey questions regarding frequency of feedback, satisfaction with feedback, and importance of feedback were scored on a 4-point Likert scale and were summarized with frequency counts and percentages.ResultsA total of 256 responses were received from 13,723 members who opened the email invitation, although not all are US-based. Respondents were predominantly female (n = 163, 63.7%), white (n = 224, 87.5%) and had been a dispatcher for ≥ 10 years (n = 158, 61.7%). Nearly half (n = 119, 46.5%) indicated receiving no performance feedback in the prior 30 days and 77.7% (n = 199) indicated no patient outcome follow-up in the same time period. EMDs were dissatisfied more with the quantity (n = 189, 73.8%) and quality (n = 174, 68.0%) of follow-up, compared to the quantity (n = 102, 39.8%) and quality (n = 102, 39.8%) of feedback they receive. Respondents indicated receiving feedback was important in terms of overall job satisfaction (n = 222, 86.7%), ongoing medical education (n = 222, 86.7%), future patient care (n = 215, 84.0%), and mental health and wellbeing (n = 200, 78.1%). Follow-up was important in terms of overall job satisfaction (n = 208, 81.3%), mental health and wellbeing (n = 201, 78.5%), ongoing medical education (n = 193, 75.4%), and future patient care (n = 187, 73.0%).ConclusionSurvey respondents indicated a paucity of both performance feedback and outcome follow-up and that there is more dissatisfaction with lack of the latter rather than the former. These findings suggest system-wide opportunities to improve communication to EMDs on performance and patient outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-025-01332-7.

Introduction

Emergency medical dispatchers (EMDs) serve as the first point of contact between the community and emergency medical services (EMS) and are the critical initial link in the out-of-hospital chain of survival [1, 2]. Upon receiving a 9-1-1 call from a patient, family member, or bystander, EMDs use structured call-taking protocols through the Medical Priority Dispatch System (MPDS), a standardized system used to assess whether the patient’s condition is life-threatening; triage case priority so appropriate emergency response and resources are sent; and provide caller instructions before trained first responders arrive, to help improve care outcomes. This hands-off assessment relies on obtaining accurate information from the caller on-scene who may be distressed, panicked, or incoherent, and generally has limited-to-no medical knowledge. In cases in which a patient is determined to be critically ill, EMDs will walk the caller through life-saving instructions, such as cardiopulmonary resuscitation, mouth-to-mouth ventilation, the Heimlich maneuver, emergency childbirth, and hemorrhage control. Given these job demands of rapid risk assessment with limited information and time-critical decision-making that impacts patient outcomes, EMDs understandably face significant stressors, including chronic exposure to visceral descriptions of traumatic calls, unappreciative callers, and role overload in under-resourced and high-pressured environments [3–5]. Referred to as “faceless heroes”, EMDs are located remotely, but exposed to repeated vicarious trauma, feelings of powerlessness, and lack of control over patient outcomes on-scene [4, 5]. These cumulative stressors contribute to a high-level of burnout and turnover within the EMD profession, with a recent survey indicating the average vacancy rate in 9-1-1 centers across America was approximately 25% between 2019 and 2022 [6].

Prior qualitative literature has identified a lack of control over, and knowledge about, patient outcomes as a significant source of stress and frustration among EMDs [4, 5, 7]. Previous research among other prehospital professions (e.g. paramedics and emergency medical technicians [EMT]) has revealed significant disparities in patient outcome follow-up and performance feedback provided to EMS personnel and that this serves as a major source of job dissatisfaction and impacts mental health; however, to-date, there is little-to-no literature attempting to quantify rates of both received by EMDs [9–12].

Performance feedback, defined as a ‘summary of clinical performance over a specified time period’ [13] and patient outcome follow-up, the process of determining a patient’s clinical outcome after initial evaluation and treatment [14], are essential tenets of medical education and clinical practice [15, 16]. Performance feedback aids medical professionals in identifying and improving weaknesses, encouraging existing strengths, and narrowing the gap between actual and desired performance [8, 16]. Outcome follow-up allows medical professionals to determine whether initial impressions were correct, interventions worked, and whether dispositions were appropriate [14, 17]. Equally as important, both feedback modalities serve a critical role in providing validation/reassurance, increased job satisfaction, improved mental health and closure, and decreased burnout.[9, 11, 12, 18, 19] The objective of this study was to describe the frequency, satisfaction with, and importance of feedback/follow-up received by EMDs within the United States (US). Additionally, we sought to compare demographic subgroups to assess for similarities or differences with existing literature on feedback/follow-up to other prehospital personnel (e.g. McGuire et al. found no disparities in feedback to paramedics/EMTs based on staff gender, but did find respondents with fewer years of experience were more satisfied with feedback they had received) [9].

Methods

Study design and setting

An anonymous, online survey was administered to US-based (approximately 60,000) emergency dispatch members of the International Academies of Emergency Dispatch (IAED), including those with EMD and concurrent IAED certifications (e.g. emergency police dispatchers (EPD), emergency fire dispatchers (EFD), etc.). An initial survey email was sent from the IAED to its entire membership base on March 21, 2024, with one reminder email sent prior to survey conclusion on April 12, 2024. Due to IAED being unable to send the survey specifically to US-based members, the survey was sent to the entire IAED membership distribution list (approximately 72,000 members with 64,000 successful deliveries) with instructions clarifying the survey was only open to dispatchers located in the US. The study was reviewed by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board (Rochester, Minnesota USA) and was granted an exemption under applicable federal regulations, indicating that a formal consent process was not required.

Survey

The survey (Supplement 1) included nine multiple-choice and twelve Likert-type questions and was modelled, in part, on a prior work by McGuire, et al. [9] and Cash, et al. [10] Definitions of performance feedback and outcome follow-up were provided to respondents. After subject matter expert review by two EMDs, we included protocol compliance via audit process in the definition of performance feedback given its practice standard within emergency medical dispatch [20] and expectation that a large amount of performance feedback provided to EMDs would result from this standardized process. Survey questions inquired about quantity and quality of performance feedback and patient outcome follow-up in the preceding 30 days; level of satisfaction/dissatisfaction with the quantity and quality of both; and level of importance/unimportance in receiving both in terms of overall job satisfaction, mental health and wellbeing, future patient care, and ongoing medical education. Demographic questions included specific emergency dispatcher role (air communication specialist, ground-based EMD, or both), active IAED certification(s), years’ experience as an EMD, US state of primary employment, gender identity, and race/ethnicity.

Data analysis

Survey questions regarding frequency of feedback, satisfaction with feedback, and importance of feedback were scored on a 4-point Likert scale and were summarized with frequency counts and percentages. These items were also coded ordinally and compared between demographic subgroups using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests. Since the Likert items only included 4 levels and the majority of responses to our survey items tended toward the scale extremes and suggested skew, we believe the use of Wilcoxon rank-sum tests to be appropriate [21, 22]. P-values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Characteristics of study subjects

A total of 256 responses were received from 13,723 members who opened the email invitation, although not all are US-based. Respondents were predominantly female (n = 163, 63.7%), white (n = 224, 87.5%), non-Hispanic or Latino (n = 238, 93.0%), and ground-based EMDs (n = 234, 91.4%; Table 1). Most respondents had been a dispatcher for > 10 years (n = 158, 61.7%).

Respondent perceptions on performance feedback and patient outcome follow-up

Summary of patient outcome follow-up

The majority of respondents (n = 199, 77.7%) indicated receiving no patient outcome follow-up in the prior 30 days (Table 2). Most EMDs were dissatisfied with the quantity (n = 189, 73.8%) and quality (n = 174, 68.0%) of patient outcome follow-up they receive. Outcome follow-up was important in terms of overall job satisfaction (n = 208, 81.3%), mental health and wellbeing (n = 201, 78.5%), ongoing medical education (n = 193, 75.4%), and future patient care (n = 187, 73.0%).

Feedback/follow-up trends by gender

Male and female respondents were equally likely to receive performance feedback (p = 0.35) and outcome follow-up (p = 0.31) in the prior 30 days (Table 3). Female respondents were more likely to be satisfied with the quantity (65.0% versus 50.6%, p = 0.031) and quality (66.9% versus 46.9%), p = 0.006) of performance feedback received. There was no significant difference in satisfaction/dissatisfaction between female and male respondents regarding quantity (28.2% versus 24.7%, p = 0.24) and quality (33.7% versus 30.9%, p = 0.49) of patient outcome follow-up received. There was no significant difference in reported level of importance in receiving performance feedback between genders in terms of overall job satisfaction (86.5% versus 86.4%, p = 0.82), mental health and wellbeing (79.1% versus 76.5%, p = 0.76), future patient care (81.6% versus 87.7%, p = 0.59), or ongoing medical education (84.7% versus 91.4%, p = 0.75). Male respondents indicated increased importance in receiving patient outcome follow-up in terms of overall job satisfaction compared to female respondents (88.9% males versus 77.3% females, p = 0.025). There was no significant difference in reported level of importance in receiving outcome follow-up between genders in terms of mental health and wellbeing (76.1% versus 84.0%, p = 0.095), future patient care (68.7% versus 80.2%, p = 0.14), or ongoing medical education (72.4% versus 82.7%, p = 0.25).

Feedback/follow-up trends by years of experience

Respondents with ≤ 10 years of experience and those with > 10 years of experience were equally likely to receive performance feedback (p = 0.097) and outcome follow-up (p = 0.33) in the prior 30 days (Table 4). There was no significant difference in satisfaction in quantity (66.3% versus 56.3%, p = 0.23) or quality (66.3% versus 56.3%, p = 0.32) of performance feedback received between those with ≤ 10 years of experience and those with > 10 years of experience. Similarly, there was no significant difference in the satisfaction in quantity (28.6% versus 24.7%, p = 0.44) or quality (33.7% versus 31.0%, p = 0.78) of outcome follow-up received between the two groups. Respondents with ≤ 10 years of experience indicated increased importance in receiving performance feedback in terms of overall job satisfaction (95.9% versus 81.0%, p = 0.003), mental health and wellbeing (85.7% versus 73.4%, p = 0.023), future patient care (91.8% versus 79.1%, p = 0.003), and ongoing medical education (91.8% versus 83.5%, p = 0.001). Those with ≤ 10 years of experience indicated increased importance in receiving patient outcome follow-up in terms of future patient care (79.6% versus 69.0%, p = 0.030).

Comparison of perceptions on performance feedback and patient outcome follow-up based on years of experience among emergency dispatchers

Discussion

In this survey study, we identify a paucity of performance feedback and outcome follow-up among US-based emergency medical dispatchers (EMDs), including particular dissatisfaction with the quality and quantity of outcome follow-up received. While similar findings have been reported in other prehospital professions [9–12], our survey uniquely identifies deficits in feedback and follow-up among EMDs, along with perceived effects on key aspects of their professional careers. These insights illustrate critical gaps in workplace feedback mechanisms that may impact EMD professional growth, job satisfaction, mental health and wellbeing, and clinical practice/medical education.

Nearly half (46.5%) of EMD respondents indicated they had received no performance feedback in the prior 30 days and the majority (77.7%) indicated no outcome follow-up in the same time period. This observed lack of feedback, particularly in regards to outcome follow-up, is greater than prior findings specific to EMTs and paramedics, with a survey study of regional Minnesota-based EMTs and paramedics revealing 50% had not received any type of feedback in the prior 30 days [9] and a survey study of nationally certified EMS clinicians indicating only 30% had not received any type of feedback in the same time period [10]. Longstanding barriers to EMS clinicians receiving feedback/follow-up have been well-described and include lack of linkage between prehospital and in-hospital electronic medical records, concern for patient confidentiality, and uncertainty on behalf of hospital staff regarding whether sharing of patient information with EMS is permissible under federal or state law or institutional policy [11, 26]. These barriers are further exacerbated in the emergency medical dispatch community, given the separation between call centers and the clinical care setting, lack of direct face-to-face patient care, and limited interactions and rapport with staff at receiving healthcare facilities. Further, while not well-reported or researched, there seems to be a general unwillingness in the EMS community to acknowledge the EMD as a critical member of the clinical continuum of patient care; perhaps best summarized in the debunked but still enduring EMS myth that ‘true patient care begins only when the trained EMS responders arrive at the patient’s side.’ Within the field of emergency dispatch is the unique practice of routine audits to ensure protocol compliance [20]. As such, we included protocol compliance via audit process in our survey’s definition of performance feedback and did not differentiate feedback received from this standardized process. Despite our inclusion of this established practice, nearly half of our respondents still indicated no performance feedback received in the preceding 30 days. Future research should attempt to distinguish how much respondent-perceived performance feedback involves protocol compliance versus clinical decision-making beyond structured guidelines. This distinction is necessary when evaluating performance feedback provided to EMS, overall, given EMS clinicians similarly practice under established patient care guidelines.

Our survey respondents were predominantly female (63.7%), consistent with a predominantly female US-based emergency dispatcher workforce [23–25]. Although male and female EMDs indicated a similar amount of performance feedback and outcome follow-up in the preceding 30 days, consistent with prior literature on feedback received by paramedics/ETs [9], we observed notable gender differences in how these experiences were perceived and valued. Female respondents indicated increased satisfaction with both the quality and quantity of performance feedback received, compared to male respondents. This may reflect prior findings that women tend to respond more positively to workplace feedback, possibly due to receiving more affirming comments or finding feedback more relevant or actionable [27, 28]. Male respondents indicated increased importance in receiving patient outcome follow-up in terms of overall job satisfaction, compared to female respondents. While the underlying reason is unclear, male EMDs may interpret outcome follow-up as a direct indicator of success or failure within a competitive framework, making it more central to job satisfaction by serving as a signal of status or impact in their professional role [29]. Importantly, these findings suggest that while standardized workplace protocols may equalize access to feedback, gendered differences in response to feedback warrant consideration when designing future interventions.

Respondents with ≤ 10 years of experience and those with > 10 years of experience were equally likely to receive performance feedback and outcome follow-up over the prior 30 days, with no significant difference in satisfaction between quantity and quality of feedback or follow-up received between the two groups. This finding differs from a prior survey study of regional Minnesota EMTs and paramedics that found those with fewer years of experience were more likely to receive feedback and more likely to be satisfied with the quality and quantity of feedback received, although in the study, the term “feedback” was not explicitly defined and was not differentiated from outcome follow-up [9]. Similarly, a survey study by Cash et al. found the odds of receiving feedback decreased with years of experience among nationally certified EMS patient care provider respondents [10]. Our study found that compared to their more experienced colleagues, respondents with ≤ 10 years of experience indicated increased importance in receiving performance feedback in terms of overall job satisfaction, mental health and wellbeing, future patient care, and ongoing medical education. Prior research has stressed the importance of regular, quality feedback in helping newer employees adjust to errors in their performance, while also increasing their overall commitment to their professional goals and positively influencing socialization and social integration at work [30, 31]. Encouraging information exchanges and social support may in turn influence overall job satisfaction, mental health and wellbeing, and job retention [31]. Respondents with ≤ 10 years of experience also indicated increased importance in receiving patient outcome follow-up in terms of future patient care. In contrast, nearly 1/3 of respondents with > 10 years of experience indicated it as unimportant. This may reflect the relatively recent emphasis on providing outcome follow-up in EMS systems [26, 32–34], with many experienced dispatchers having spent much of their careers without access to such information. As a result, they may be less accustomed to incorporating outcome follow-up into their practice or may view it as less useful, relying instead on prior experience to anticipate outcomes and inform future decision making. Overall, our findings indicate that interventions to provide feedback and/or outcome follow-up to EMDs may be better received by more novice dispatchers, with potentially more impact on crucial work-life domains. Alternatively, more experienced dispatchers may no longer perceive benefits/importance to feedback/follow-up after a career with limited opportunities to receive either.

Despite respondents indicating a lack of performance feedback and outcome follow-up received, the majority indicated that both are important in terms of overall job satisfaction, ongoing medical education, future patient care, and mental health and wellbeing. This sentiment is not unique to EMDs, with prior literature demonstrating the value of feedback and/or follow-up in terms of job satisfaction, medical education, and clinical performance among emergency medicine physicians [14, 35], physician trainees [17], and prehospital clinicians including paramedics and EMTs [9, 26]. Given stress on the job is listed as a top reason for EMDs leaving the profession [6], future research is needed to determine how much of this stress may be attributable to lack of performance feedback or outcome follow-up and whether providing either (or both) may help retain current EMDs, mitigate future job vacancies, or even recruit potential future candidates. Future research should also attempt to determine whether there is an association between EMD receipt of feedback/outcome follow-up and subsequent patient/call outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several important limitations. Although modelled from prior surveys by McGuire et al. [9] and Cash et al., [10] the survey was developed de novo for this study and therefore not validated. To remain consistent with this prior literature, the survey inquired about performance feedback and patient outcome follow-up in the preceding 30 days overall, which may have included variable days on-duty. The survey was distributed by IAED to its entire membership base, which includes non-US members, due to inability to distribute selectively to US-based members. Study authors who developed the survey were unaware that IAED membership included non-US members until after the survey was distributed to the membership base. For this reason, a location demographic question was unfortunately not included in the survey instrument. While IAED email metrics indicated 13,723 members opened the email invitation, not all of these members are US-based and therefore we are unable to reliably calculate a response rate among US-based members, who were the intended study population, as opposed to non-US members who were advised to disregard the email and survey if not practicing in the US. Respondent membership in a professional organization such as the IAED that requires continuing dispatch education for recertification may have influenced results and may limit generalizability to other EMDs. We recognize results only apply to those completing the survey (self-selection bias), however given a paucity of literature on this topic, we believe despite our study limitations, our results are important. This survey was intended for US-based EMDs and results may not be generalizable broadly to EMDs in other countries. Lastly, the study is subject to recall and reporting bias in terms of feedback and patient follow-up received over a 30-day time period.

Conclusions

Survey respondents indicated a paucity of both performance feedback and outcome follow-up and that there is more dissatisfaction with lack of the latter rather than the former. These findings suggest system-wide opportunities to improve communication to EMDs on performance and patient outcomes to benefit EMDs, patients and the EMS system, overall.
