Long-term safety and efficacy of upadacitinib in Japanese patients with rheumatoid arthritis and inadequate response to conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs: 5-year results from the SELECT-SUNRISE randomised controlled trial
Authors: Hideto Kameda, Tsutomu Takeuchi, Kunihiro Yamaoka, Motohiro Oribe, Mitsuhiro Kawano, Yuko Konishi, Sumi Chonan, Kimitoshi Ikeda, Heidi S Camp, Yoshiya Tanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the long-term safety and efficacy of upadacitinib over 5 years in Japanese patients with moderate-to-severe active rheumatoid arthritis and an inadequate response to stable doses of conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs-IR).MethodsJapanese patients received upadacitinib 7.5, 15 or 30 mg once per day or placebo for 12 weeks in a double-blind, randomised manner. Patients who completed the placebo-controlled phase entered a long-term extension phase during which they continued the same upadacitinib dose or were switched from placebo to upadacitinib 7.5, 15 or 30 mg once per day. Blinding was maintained until dose switching from upadacitinib 30 mg to 15 mg, prior to the regulatory approval of 15 mg; the earliest switch occurred at week 132. Safety and efficacy were evaluated through week 260.ResultsIn total, 121/187 (64.7%) patients who entered the long-term extension phase completed the study. The types and frequency of adverse events were comparable with those reported at week 84 and in other studies of upadacitinib. The incidences of any adverse events, serious adverse events, infection and serious infection were numerically higher on upadacitinib 15 and 30 mg than on 7.5 mg. Clinical outcomes were improved and maintained over 260 weeks; Clinical Disease Activity Index clinical remission rates after 5 years were 40.8%, 26.5% and 28.0% on upadacitinib 7.5, 15 and 30 mg, respectively (non-responder imputation).ConclusionsUpadacitinib showed sustained efficacy with no new safety signals identified through 5 years of treatment and is a long-term treatment option for Japanese patients with rheumatoid arthritis and csDMARDs-IR.Keywords: Antirheumatic Agents; Arthritis, Rheumatoid; Autoimmune Diseases

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a systemic chronic autoimmune disease that affects the joints and may require long-term continuous treatment to prevent functional loss, irreversible disability and decline in quality of life.1 The initial goal of RA treatment is to achieve sustained remission or at least low disease activity (LDA), and it is important to optimise the treatment until this goal is achieved.2–4 The first-line drugs are methotrexate (MTX) or other conventional synthetic disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (csDMARDs), but most patients have an inadequate response or intolerance to these drugs.5 For such patients, the next options are biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs) or targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs). However, the long-term clinical response has been found to be unsatisfactory in a proportion of patients even after treatment with bDMARDs or tsDMARDs,6 highlighting the need to expand available therapeutic options.

Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors are a class of orally administered small-molecule drugs that belong to the category of tsDMARDs and can be considered an alternative treatment option for patients who do not reach their treatment goal with MTX (or other csDMARDs) and/or bDMARDs.2–4 Furthermore, patient surveys have shown that patients with RA prefer oral treatments over injections.7 8 However, following ORAL Surveillance, concerns have been raised about the long-term safety of these therapies. ORAL Surveillance was a randomised, postmarketing study that identified a numerically higher incidence of major cardiovascular events and cancer associated with tofacitinib compared with TNF inhibitors in a population of patients with RA who had known cardiovascular risk factors.9 Therefore, there is a need to further investigate the long-term safety and efficacy of JAK inhibitors, especially in the Japanese population, given that ORAL Surveillance included only a very limited number of Asian patients.

Upadacitinib is a widely approved JAK inhibitor characterised by increased selectivity for JAK1 over JAK2, JAK3 and tyrosine kinase 2.10 Upadacitinib was demonstrated to have a positive risk–benefit profile at a dose of 15 mg once per day in the large-scale, global phase III (SELECT) programme, which included at least 4500 patients with RA who were either MTX-naïve or had an inadequate response to csDMARDs or bDMARDs.11–17 In Japan, the efficacy and safety of upadacitinib were examined in the phase IIb/III SELECT-SUNRISE study, which included adult Japanese patients with an inadequate response to csDMARDs. Based on the favourable efficacy and safety data confirmed at week 12 in the study,18 upadacitinib was approved in 2020 as an oral treatment for RA in Japan. The Japanese regulatory authority approved upadacitinib at two dose strengths: 15 mg once per day, which is the optimal dosage for adults, and 7.5 mg once per day, which may be acceptable, depending on the patient’s condition. The lower dose of 7.5 mg once per day was not evaluated in the global SELECT studies and is approved for use only in Japan. Therefore, it is important to show the long-term safety and efficacy of upadacitinib at both doses approved for use in the Japanese population. We previously reported that upadacitinib had sustained efficacy and acceptable tolerability over 84 weeks in the extension phase of SELECT-SUNRISE.19 In this report, we present the full 5-year efficacy and safety data for upadacitinib obtained in the SELECT-SUNRISE study.

Methods

Study design and patients

SELECT-SUNRISE (NCT02720523, ClinicalTrials.gov) was a multicentre phase IIb/III study that consisted of randomised double-blind treatment for 12 weeks (period 1) and a 260-week (5-year) blinded long-term extension phase (period 2) (online supplemental figure 1).18 19 The results of the interim analysis at 84 weeks have already been published.19

The eligibility criteria for period 1 have been described elsewhere.18 Briefly, the included patients were aged ≥18 years, had a diagnosis of RA for ≥3 months, fulfilled the 2010 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) and European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology classification criteria for RA,20 had moderate-to-severe RA (defined as ≥6 tender [of 68 assessed] and ≥6 swollen [of 66 assessed] joints at their screening and baseline visits, had a high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [CRP] concentration of ≥3 mg/L) and had received csDMARD therapy for ≥3 months and been on a stable dose for ≥4 weeks before the first dose of the study medication. Patients who had previous exposure to any JAK inhibitor or were considered to respond inadequately to bDMARDs were excluded. The recruited patients received upadacitinib 7.5, 15 or 30 mg once per day or placebo during period 1, up to week 12.

All patients who completed period 1 entered period 2. Patients who had received upadacitinib in period 1 continued to receive the same dose in period 2. Patients who had received placebo in period 1 were switched in period 2 to one of the three doses of upadacitinib according to prespecified randomisation assignment. During period 2, the treatment for patients assigned to upadacitinib 30 mg once per day was switched to 15 mg once per day at some time point after week 132, in accordance with a protocol amendment required before regulatory approval was secured in Japan; blinding was maintained until this dose switch. After week 24, patients were withdrawn from the study if their tender and swollen joint counts did not improve by ≥20% compared with baseline at two consecutive visits. The study sites are listed in online supplemental text 1.

Safety assessments

Assessment of safety was based on treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) per 100 patient-years and the clinical laboratory test results recorded up to week 260. Exposure-adjusted incidence rates were calculated as the number of patients with one or more events per 100 patient-years. For the patients who experienced an event, exposure time was defined as the time to the first event; for the patients who did not experience an event, exposure time was censored on the day of the patient’s last assessment or on the cut-off date of database lock, whichever occurred first. Exposure-adjusted event rates were also determined as the number of all events (including multiple events occurring in a single patient) per 100 patient-years. Exposure time for the event rate was the total study drug duration. Standardised incidence ratio (SIR) was calculated as the ratio of observed to expected cases. Expected numbers were derived by applying sex-specific crude all-cancer incidence rates for Japan (2019) to the total person-years of follow-up in each sex stratum.21 Selected adverse events (AEs) of special interest were also summarised for each dose group. The laboratory tests comprised haematology, chemistry and urinalysis, and the data were evaluated in accordance with the Outcome Measures in Rheumatology criteria at the central laboratory. Creatine phosphokinase (CPK) and serum creatinine were evaluated on the basis of the National Cancer Institute Common Toxicity Criteria. Patients were required to fast for at least 8 hours before the collection of blood samples for laboratory analysis.

Efficacy assessments

Efficacy assessments comprised the following: the proportions of patients achieving LDA, clinical remission (CR), Boolean remission and ACR 20/50/70 responses; the proportion of patients reporting a minimum clinically important improvement in the Health Assessment Questionnaire Disability Index (HAQ-DI) score (a ≥0.22 decrease from baseline); and the severity and duration of morning stiffness. LDA was defined by a Clinical Disease Activity Index (CDAI) value of ≤10 or a Simplified Disease Activity Index (SDAI) value of ≤11. CR was defined as CDAI≤2.8 or SDAI≤3.3. We also used protocol-defined LDA and CR based on the 28-Joint Disease Activity Score using CRP (DAS28(CRP)), with LDA defined as ≤3.2 and CR as <2.6. These measures were assessed at weeks 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20 and 24; every 12 weeks up to week 240 and at week 260.

Statistical analysis

The safety analysis set comprised all patients who received at least one dose of study medication. Patients who received placebo in period 1 and were then switched to upadacitinib for period 2 were included in the respective upadacitinib dose groups. The data for the upadacitinib 30 mg group were considered censored at the dose switch when it occurred at some time point after week 132, and the safety data for the switched group (from 30 mg to 15 mg) were analysed separately. The 95% CI for the SIR was estimated using the Poisson (Byar) approximation.22 23

Efficacy was analysed using the full analysis set, which comprised data for all randomised patients who received at least one dose of study medication. Efficacy data for patients in the upadacitinib 30 mg group were tabulated as those in the ‘30 mg group’ (original dose group) even after the dose switch to 15 mg. Non-responder imputation (NRI) was applied for binary endpoints, and as-observed data are shown for both binary and continuous endpoints. The sample size was determined as described previously.18 SIRs were calculated as the ratio of observed to expected cases. Expected numbers were derived by applying sex-specific crude all-cancer incidence rates for Japan (2019) to the total person-years of follow-up in each sex stratum.

Results

Patient disposition

As reported previously,18 187 (94.9%) of 197 randomised patients completed the 12 weeks of period 1 and entered the blinded extension phase (figure 1). Patient demographics and baseline disease characteristics have already been reported (online supplemental table 1).18 There were no noticeable differences among the groups. 121 patients (64.7%) completed the extension period up to week 260, with a higher completion rate in the 7.5 mg upadacitinib group (72.3%) than in the 15 (61.3%) and 30 mg (60.6%) groups (figure 1). The final database lock occurred on 24 June 2022. During the extension phase, 62% of the patients in the 30 mg upadacitinib group were switched to the 15 mg dose at some time point after week 132. The study was completed before the dose was switched for the remainder of the patients in the 30 mg group. More patients discontinued the study because of AEs in the upadacitinib 15 and 30 mg groups compared with the 7.5 mg group during the extension period.

Safety

Overall safety

The incidence of overall TEAEs, serious TEAEs and TEAEs leading to discontinuation tended to be numerically higher as the dose of upadacitinib increased (table 1). In particular, the incidences of serious TEAEs and TEAEs leading to discontinuation were numerically higher in the 15 and 30 mg groups compared with the 7.5 mg group. Two deaths in the 30 mg group were reported by week 84, as previously described19: one was a treatment-emergent death attributed to respiratory failure (adjudicated as a cardiovascular death) and the other was a non-treatment-emergent death caused by a ruptured aortic aneurysm. Thereafter, no deaths were reported through week 260 in any dose group. The frequency of overall infection also tended to be numerically greater with higher doses of upadacitinib. The most frequently reported TEAEs were, in descending order of incidence, nasopharyngitis, cystitis, herpes zoster, elevated blood CPK and constipation (table 2). The most common serious TEAEs were infections, including herpes zoster, Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia and pneumonia (table 3); P. jirovecii pneumonia was reported only in the 30 mg group. Among the AEs of special interest shown in table 1, the incidences of serious infection, herpes zoster, hepatic disorder, anaemia, neutropenia, lymphopenia and elevated CPK concentration tended to be numerically higher as the dose increased from 7.5 mg once per day to 30 mg once per day (see also online supplemental figure 2). The incidences of active tuberculosis, non-melanoma skin cancer, major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) and venous thromboembolic events were comparable between the dose groups, although the number of these events was small. The incidence of opportunistic infection in the 30 mg group was numerically higher than that in the other groups.

Infections

The proportions of patients who experienced herpes zoster events were 21.5% (14/65 patients) in the 7.5 mg group, 31.3% (20/64 patients) in the 15 mg group and 27.3% (18/66 patients) in the 30 mg group. One patient had a reported history of herpes zoster vaccination (live herpes zoster vaccine), and this patient did not experience a herpes zoster event. The majority of the herpes zoster events were non-serious, and the proportions that were serious were 23.5% (4/17 events) in the 7.5 mg group, 7.1% (2/28 events) in the 15 mg group and 19.2% (5/26 events) in the 30 mg group. Regarding the severity of herpes zoster, the majority of events were mild or moderate; the proportions that were severe were 23.5% (4/17 events) in the 7.5 mg group, 0.0% (0/28 events) in the 15 mg group and 15.4% (4/26 events) in the 30 mg group. No herpes zoster event was reported as having central nervous system involvement. Two patients in the 30 mg group had serious events that were diagnosed as having ophthalmic involvement. Eight patients had disseminated herpes zoster (one patient in the 7.5 mg group, three patients each in the 15 mg and 30 mg groups and one patient in the 15 mg switched from the 30 mg group). Of these eight patients, seven had disseminated cutaneous zoster, and one had disseminated non-cutaneous zoster in the oral cavity. The percentages of postherpetic neuralgia events were 11.8% (2/17 events) in the 7.5 mg group, 21.4% (6/28 events) in the 15 mg group and 26.9% (7/26 events) in the 30 mg group; all postherpetic neuralgia events were reported as non-serious AEs. Interruption of the study medication because of herpes zoster was reported in 52.9% of the events in the 7.5 mg group, 67.9% in the 15 mg group and 57.7% in the 30 mg group, while withdrawal of the study medication because of herpes zoster was reported in 17.6% of events in the 7.5 mg group, 3.6% in the 15 mg group and 15.4% in the 30 mg group (online supplemental table 2). Treatment-emergent COVID-19 was observed only in patients who switched from 30 mg to 15 mg once per day (three events, one of which was serious; incidence, 6.7 per 100 patient-years).

Opportunistic infections occurred in one patient each (incidence, both 0.4 per 100 patient-years) in the 7.5 mg group and 15 mg group and in seven patients (3.7 per 100 patient-years) in the 30 mg group. The opportunistic infection events in the 7.5 and 15 mg groups were non-serious; however, there were four cases of P. jirovecii pneumonia and one case of Legionella pneumonia in the 30 mg group, all of which were deemed to be serious (online supplemental table 3). Two patients had one or more of the previously reported risk factors of P. jirovecii pneumonia, such as elderly patients, pulmonary comorbidities or high doses of glucocorticoids.24 There were no reports of prophylactic administration in any of the cases. Also, two patients who developed P. jirovecii pneumonia had an abnormal lymphocyte count immediately before the infection was reported: one patient had a grade 4 decrease in lymphocyte count (this patient already had a grade 3 decrease at baseline) and the other had a decrease of grade 3.

Malignancy

Five malignancies were identified: one patient (incidence, 0.4 per 100 patient-years) in the 7.5 mg group, three patients (1.2 per 100 patient-years) in the 15 mg group and one patient (0.5 per 100 patient-years) in the 30 mg group. For the overall upadacitinib-exposed population, the SIR relative to the Japanese general population was 0.94 (95% CI 0.30 to 2.20), suggesting no clear excess risk. However, this estimate should be interpreted with caution given the small number of events, the resulting wide CIs and the lack of age-standardisation.

Two of these malignancies have been reported previously (one case each of acute lymphocytic leukaemia in the 15 mg group and Hodgkin’s disease in the 30 mg group). Since the previous report, three new events were observed up to week 260 (basal cell carcinoma on the tip of the nose in the 7.5 mg group; invasive ductal breast carcinoma and malignant melanoma on the left cheek in the 15 mg group) (online supplemental table 4).

MACE, venous thromboembolic events and hepatic AEs

Events adjudicated as treatment-emergent MACE were observed in four patients: two patients (incidence, 0.8 per 100 patient-years) in the 7.5 mg group, one patient (0.4 per 100 patient-years) in the 15 mg group and one patient (0.5 per 100 patient-years) in the 30 mg group. Two of these events were already reported in the analysis up to week 8419: one was a case of respiratory failure adjudicated to be cardiovascular death in the upadacitinib 30 mg group, and the other was a cerebral infarction adjudicated to be non-fatal stroke in the upadacitinib 7.5 mg group. The treatment-emergent MACEs newly reported after week 84 were one case of transient ischaemic attack in the upadacitinib 7.5 mg group and one case of acute myocardial infarction in the 15 mg group, both of which were non-fatal. An adjudicated treatment-emergent venous thromboembolic event was reported in one patient in the upadacitinib 30 mg group, who developed deep vein thrombosis in the right leg (online supplemental table 5).

None of the treatment-emergent hepatic disorders was serious. The most common hepatic disorders were abnormal hepatic function, hepatic steatosis, elevated liver function test results and an increase in alanine aminotransferase. Only one patient (in the upadacitinib 15 mg group) discontinued the study medication because of a hepatic disorder (hepatic steatosis).

Clinical laboratory tests

There was a single anaemia event (a case of anaemia of chronic disease) in the upadacitinib 15 mg group that was serious. For neutropenia, no serious events or events leading to discontinuation were reported. There was one serious lymphopenia event in the upadacitinib 30 mg group. A decrease in the lymphocyte count that led to discontinuation of the study medication was reported for one case in the 15 mg group and for two in the 30 mg group. The proportions of patients with a grade 3/4 decrease in haemoglobin and a grade 3/4 increase in CPK tended to be numerically higher as the dose of upadacitinib increased (table 4). No rhabdomyolysis events were reported in the patients with elevated CPK. Although the incidence of abnormal lymphocyte counts was high in all the upadacitinib-treated groups (table 4), it is important to note that, as previously reported,19 more than half of the patients already had a grade 2 or 3 lymphocyte count at baseline (some of these patients, for example, had a lymphocyte count below grade 3 at one visit, but the count returned to the normal grade at the next visit).

Efficacy

Overall, previously reported improvements in efficacy measures were maintained over 5 years (figure 2). Only one patient (in the upadacitinib 15 mg group) discontinued the study because of lack of response during period 2 (figure 1).

The proportion of patients who achieved an ACR20 response increased at week 12,18 and the proportion generally showed continued improvement or maintenance through week 8419; furthermore, the response generally continued to be maintained through week 260 (online supplemental figure 3). The ACR20 response rates for patients who received upadacitinib continuously (NRI) at week 260 were 67.3% in the 7.5 mg group, 57.1% in the 15 mg group and 46.0% in the 30 mg group, and the rates were similar in patients who switched from placebo. The ACR50/70 rates, which are more stringent, were also favourable, with similar trends of an increase after initiation of upadacitinib that was maintained through week 260. Specifically, the ACR50 response rates for patients who had received upadacitinib 7.5, 15 and 30 mg once per day continuously for 260 weeks were 59.2%, 55.1% and 44.0%, respectively, and the ACR70 response rates were 42.9%, 46.9% and 34.0%, respectively (NRI). Although the ACR 20/50/70 rates at week 260 tended to be numerically higher in the 7.5 mg group compared with the higher-dose groups because of the higher dropout rates in the higher-dose groups. The initial improvement in the ACR70 rate was more rapid and numerically higher in the upadacitinib 15 and 30 mg groups compared with the 7.5 mg group. As-observed data showed similar results.

The proportion of patients achieving an LDA/CR, defined by CDAI, SDAI and DAS28(CRP), increased at week 12, was maintained through week 84, as previously reported,18 19 and was generally further maintained at the same level through week 260 by NRI (figure 2). The Boolean remission rate also increased after initiation of upadacitinib and was maintained at the same level through week 260 (figure 2). The initial LDA/CR and Boolean remission responses were more rapid and numerically higher in the upadacitinib 15 and 30 mg groups compared with the 7.5 mg group. The trends were similar in the as-observed analysis (online supplemental figure 4).

Sustained reduction in the scores of several disease activity scales was observed through week 260; these scales comprised the tender joint count (in 68 joints), swollen joint count (in 66 joints), patient’s/physician’s global assessment of disease activity, patient’s assessment of pain, HAQ-DI score and the high-sensitivity CRP concentration (online supplemental figure 5). The change from baseline in some of these scales, such as the patient’s global assessment and HAQ-DI scores, was relatively small in patients who switched from placebo to upadacitinib 7.5 mg compared with the other groups. This gap was especially prominent for the HAQ-DI score and was already observed at week 84 and remained for over 260 weeks. The increased proportion of patients reporting a minimum clinically important improvement (ie, a ≥0.22 decrease in the HAQ-DI) was also maintained through week 260; the proportions at week 260 (NRI) were 57.9% in the 7.5 mg group, 48.8% in the 15 mg group, 43.2% in the 30 mg group, 60.0% in the 7.5 mg switched (from placebo) group, 40.0% in the 15 mg switched group and 69.2% in the 30 mg switched group. The reduction in severity and duration of morning stiffness was also sustained up to week 260, but the change from baseline tended to be smaller in patients who switched from placebo to upadacitinib 7.5 mg compared with the other groups (online supplemental Figure 6).

The numbers of patients who discontinued concomitant glucocorticoid use at week 260 were five (25.0%), five (33.3%) and four (44.4%) for patients receiving upadacitinib 7.5, 15 and 30 mg in period 1, respectively, and one (16.7%), three (50.0%) and one (25.0%) for patients switched from the placebo group and receiving 7.5, 15 and 30 mg, respectively.

Discussion

The SELECT-SUNRISE study assessed the risk–benefit profile of upadacitinib 7.5, 15 and 30 mg once per day in Japanese patients with moderate-to-severe active RA and an inadequate response to csDMARD therapy. In the present analysis of data up to 5 years (week 260), no new safety signals were observed, in accordance with those that had been reported by week 84 of this study and those in other global studies.25 26 Although not statistically validated because of the small sample size, the incidence of several TEAEs—including infections (serious infections, opportunistic infections and herpes zoster), hepatic disorders and anaemia—tended to occur more frequently at higher doses. These observations align with the clinical and regulatory perspective that the 15 mg dose offers the most favourable overall risk–benefit profile for long-term treatment of RA and was therefore selected as the optimal dose approved in Japan, whereas 7.5 mg may be used in selected patients. Although the dose groups were similar in size in this trial, cumulative exposure to the 7.5 mg dose has been substantially lower across the global upadacitinib programme14 17; therefore, the lower incidence of some AEs with 7.5 mg observed here should not be overinterpreted as evidence of a consistently safer profile. The overall 5-year completion rate was relatively high at 64.7% for the entire cohort, led by the 7.5 mg group with the highest retention rate. The improvement in efficacy measures observed at earlier timepoints, including the LDA/CR rate and patient-reported outcomes, was generally sustained through 5 years across dose strengths. These findings suggest that upadacitinib is well-tolerated and a favourable option for the long-term treatment of Japanese patients with RA who do not respond adequately to csDMARDs.

Among the TEAEs observed in the patients treated with JAK inhibitors, the most commonly reported serious infectious disease was herpes zoster, which is more common in Asian patients compared with patients from other geographical regions.25 27 28 Indeed, the incidence of herpes zoster in this study was numerically higher than that in the global SELECT trials.27 Although most herpes zoster events reported in this study were non-serious and non-severe, serious cases were observed for all dose groups. Meanwhile, the incidence of herpes zoster tended to increase with higher doses of upadacitinib (table 1). Cross-trial comparisons of herpes zoster incidence or dose–response are not appropriate because studies differ in design, enrolled populations, background therapies, observation windows and vaccination uptake. Comparability is further limited by non-uniform rate calculations (eg, exposure-adjusted incidence per 100 patient-years vs cumulative incidence; first event vs all events; on-treatment vs intent-to-treat risk windows; case definitions and adjudication). We therefore limit our interpretation to within-trial findings; head-to-head or harmonised analyses would be required to make molecule-specific inferences. Regardless, there is a need for continued awareness of herpes zoster as an AE in Japanese patients who receive JAK inhibitors, including upadacitinib, for RA, and particular attention should be paid to herpes zoster in patients with risks identified in previous reports.27 28 The use of an adjuvanted herpes zoster recombinant subunit (HZ/su) vaccine may be considered to prevent the occurrence of herpes zoster events.29–31 P. jirovecii pneumonia was found only in the 30 mg group in the present study but should be noted because it is a common infectious event observed in Japanese patients with RA.32 In patients with multiple risk factors (eg, advanced age, pulmonary complications, glucocorticoid use, diabetes mellitus and peripheral blood lymphopenia), prophylactic administration of trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole combination or other antibiotics should be considered, and careful monitoring is essential.32

Malignancy and MACE are now the subjects of increasing attention as potential AEs associated with JAK inhibitors. During this 5-year study, there were five reports of the development of malignancy and four of MACE, and none of these TEAEs showed clear evidence of any dose-related increase in incidence (table 1 and online supplemental figure 2). Although not directly comparable and limited by the relatively small sample size and rarity of the events, the incidence of malignancy (0.4–1.2 events/100 patient-years) and that of MACE (0.4–0.8 events/100 patient-years) observed in each dose group were similar to those in RA patient populations in global studies.25 26 Long-term use of upadacitinib for up to 5 years did not increase the incidence of these TEAEs in Japanese patients with RA compared with the global patient population.

In terms of efficacy, upadacitinib at a dose of 7.5 or 15 mg once per day improved and maintained the LDA/CR rates (defined by CDAI, SDAI and DAS28(CRP)), the Boolean remission rate, the ACR 20/50/70 response and the HAQ-DI value over 5 years in Japanese patients with RA who had not responded adequately to csDMARDs. The 15 mg group showed a more rapid and numerically higher initial response compared with the 7.5 mg group in several stringent disease activity measures (eg, ACR70, LDA/CR rates and Boolean remission), as described previously,19 but the subsequent long-term efficacy was generally comparable among the three dose groups. According to the results of the NRI analysis, LDA/CR rates (defined by CDAI, SDAI and DAS28(CRP)) and the ACR20 rate at 5 years in the 7.5 mg group were higher than those in the 15 and 30 mg groups. This was likely owing to the more favourable safety profile and lower dropout rates in the lower-dose groups in which fewer patients were imputed as non-responders. However, improvements in the HAQ-DI and patients’ global assessment scores were smaller in patients who switched from placebo to upadacitinib 7.5 mg compared with the other groups, which presumably reflects a smaller effect on pain (online supplemental Figure 5). This finding suggests that a dose of 7.5 mg upadacitinib once per day may not be sufficiently effective as an induction treatment.

One of the unique aspects of this study is that it assessed the long-term safety and efficacy of upadacitinib 7.5 mg once per day. This dose strength was also evaluated in Japanese patients with RA who participated in the SELECT-EARLY trial,33 but the long-term results have not yet been published. The incidences of some TEAEs were numerically lower in the 7.5 mg group than in the 15 and 30 mg groups, including those of any TEAEs, serious TEAEs, TEAEs leading to discontinuation, infection, serious infection, herpes zoster, hepatic disorders, anaemia, neutropenia, lymphopenia, CPK elevation and several laboratory test abnormalities (tables 1 and 4). Although not statistically validated, these findings may justify the regulatory approval of upadacitinib 7.5 mg once per day in Japan, depending on a patient’s condition, possibly for long-term maintenance therapy. The Japanese clinical guidelines state that tapering of a JAK inhibitor may be considered in patients who maintain remission or LDA4; however, the present results do not necessarily support considering tapering to 7.5 mg, as some AEs were still observed at 7.5 mg, and the tapering from the approved 15 mg to 7.5 mg was not directly assessed. Further clinical trials and real-world evidence are needed to draw conclusions regarding the appropriateness of this tapering strategy.

This study has some limitations. First, the efficacy data should be interpreted with caution because there was no placebo control group after week 12, and the efficacy of upadacitinib could be overestimated. Changes in the background RA treatment were allowed after week 24, and treatment adjustment may be required more frequently in the lower-dose group, although such a tendency was not clearly observed for the proportion of patients who discontinued concomitant glucocorticoid use by the end of the study. In addition, it should be noted that DAS28(CRP) thresholds used in this study were prespecified in the protocol and may overestimate LDA and CR compared with other definitions, although we also reported results based on CDAI, SDAI, as well as Boolean, which provide more stringent criteria.34–36 Second, the sample size in this study was relatively small, and no statistical comparison among the dose groups was performed. Therefore, it is unclear whether there was a meaningful difference in efficacy measures between groups or whether the risk of specific TEAEs could be reduced by lowering the dose from 15 mg to 7.5 mg. Third, the effect of upadacitinib on joint destruction was not investigated. However, in the subgroup analysis of Japanese MTX-naïve patients enrolled in the multinational SELECT-EARLY trial, the change in modified total Sharp score at week 24 with upadacitinib 15 and 30 mg once per day was significantly lower than with MTX monotherapy, but this was not significant with 7.5 mg once per day.33 Despite these limitations, this study provides important insights into the risk–benefit profile of upadacitinib during long-term use in the Japanese population with RA.

In summary, the safety profile of upadacitinib 7.5, 15 and 30 mg once per day over 5 years in Japanese patients with RA who do not respond adequately to csDMARDs was comparable to that reported previously.18 19 25 26 The data reported here do not suggest any new safety findings. The efficacy of upadacitinib when assessed by the LDA/CR rate, ACR 20/50/70 response rate and other patient-reported outcomes was generally maintained over 5 years. Overall, the results of the SELECT-SUNRISE study indicate that upadacitinib has a favourable risk–benefit profile when used in the long-term treatment of Japanese patients with RA who do not respond adequately to csDMARD therapy.
