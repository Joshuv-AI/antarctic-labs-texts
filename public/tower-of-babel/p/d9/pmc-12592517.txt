The association of the rumen virome with methane emissions in dairy cattle
Authors: Carlos Navarro Marcos, Mónica Gutiérrez-Rivas, Idoia Goiri, Aser García-Rodríguez, Óscar González-Recio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnteric methane production in ruminants is a major environmental concern, yet its association with the ruminal virome remains largely unexplored. Here, we conduct a bioinformatic analysis on previously published ruminal metagenomes from 448 Holstein cows to investigate the virome and its association with methane production. We identify 8933 viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs), including bacteriophages, archaeophages, megaviruses, and virophages. Differences between high- and low-emitting cows are observed. Low emitters show greater abundance (mean log-FC = 0.72, Padj ≤ 0.049) of some vOTUs infecting bacteria like Prevotella, whereas greater abundance (mean log-FC = 0.70, Padj ≤ 0.047) of archaeophages and megaviruses infecting Methanobrevibacter, ciliates, and fungi, all microorganisms linked to methane production, are observed in high emitters. Associations between viruses and microorganisms might suggest viruses influence methane emissions by modulating key microbial populations. Although mechanisms remain unclear, rumen viruses could serve as biomarkers for selecting low-emission animals or developing microbial interventions.Subject terms: Agriculture, Metagenomics

Introduction

Ruminants possess a complex digestive system capable of fermenting non-human-edible food and agro-industrial by products to obtain nutrients, transforming these into high-quality products like meat or milk. They significantly contribute to food security1,2. A complex microbial community in the rumen is responsible for this feed fermentation. Ruminal fermentation is an intricate process in which certain products, such as volatile fatty acids, provide energy to the animal, while others, such as H2 must be neutralized to ensure proper ruminal function. Methanogenic archaea utilise this H2 to produce methane3 which is not absorbed by the host animal and is then exhaled into the atmosphere, mainly through eructation events. This methane is a greenhouse gas (GHG) with a global warming potential 82.5 times greater than that of CO2 over a 20-year period4. Indeed, methane is the main GHG contributor from the livestock sector5, and that produced by ruminants accounts for up to 16% of global GHG emissions and 30–32% of anthropogenic methane emissions4. Reducing methane emissions from livestock is essential to ensure an environmentally sustainable production of secure and high-quality animal products for a growing global population. Efforts to reduce methane emissions in the dairy cattle sector have been ongoing for over 20 years6, but substantial advancements are still needed7. Some fermentation processes and the microorganisms involved are well known, but there is still a lack of comprehensive understanding of all processes undergoing in the rumen as well as the interrelationship between microbial groups. Among these microbial groups, the viral domain remains one of the least understood. Bacteriophages have long been speculated to play a role in modulating methane emissions in ruminants3,8,9. Identifying bacteriophages or other viruses associated with methane emissions either directly or through their microbial hosts could help develop methane mitigation strategies, such as targeting methane-producing microorganisms, regulating fermentative pathways, or using viral markers as proxies in genetic selection7. Nonetheless, research regarding these strategies has been scarce possibly due to a lack of understanding and information regarding ruminal viruses10. Recent advancements in bioinformatic tools specifically designed for viral analysis, as well as the increasingly available sequencing technologies, have facilitated the identification of viruses and provided new insights into their roles within the ruminal ecosystem10–12. However, no studies have specifically examined association between the rumen virome and methane emissions.

López-García et al.13 previously assessed the association of bacteria, archaea and eukaryotes with methane emissions in dairy cattle using a large cohort sequenced with Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT). The data from ONT provides unbiased and comprehensive insights into microorganisms across all domains of life, including bacteria, eukaryotes, archaea, and viruses, with a single analysis13–15. Additionally, long reads provided by ONT might facilitate de novo identification of microorganisms. For these reasons, ONT offers a valuable opportunity for virus identification10,11 and study the association between ruminal viruses and methane emissions, as well as the interactions with other rumen microorganisms and the co-occurring host12,16. Gaining new insights into ruminal viruses could help develop effective strategies to mitigate methane emissions in the dairy sector.

Hence, the objective of this study was to perform a bioinformatic analysis of previously published ruminal metagenomes generated with ONT to identify rumen viruses associated with methane emissions in dairy cattle under commercial conditions and to investigate their interactions with other rumen microorganisms.

Our study reveals a significant link between the ruminal virome and microorganisms involved in methane production, suggesting that some viruses may influence methane emissions, while others are associated to microorganisms involved in methanogenesis. This includes some Caudoviricetes class viruses infecting bacteria and archaea, as well as megaviruses targeting ciliates and fungi. While the exact mechanisms remain unknown, the results reveal complex interactions between viruses and microbial communities in the rumen. Oxford Nanopore Technologies offers potential for studying the ruminal virome and its interactions with other microorganisms. Bioinformatic tools and available viral reference dataset need to be enlarged in the future to overcome the limitations of environmental virome studies. Some viruses may serve as a proxy of methane emissions. The abundance of these viruses could be estimated through quantitative amplification approaches, serving as a phenotype in breeding programs. These findings provide a foundation for future studies to explore the virome’s role in methane production and its potential for mitigating methane emissions.

Results

Rumen virome composition

A total of 8933 viral operational taxonomic units (vOTUs) were identified with an average length of 25.2 ± 30.2 kb (mean ± SD), ranging from 500 to 375,213 bp (Supplementary Data 1). We obtained 782 high-quality, 1350 medium-quality, 5727 low-quality vOTUs, while the quality of 1073 vOTUs could not be assessed. Among them, only one resulted in a complete genome. CheckV classified up to 0.73% of vOTUs as proviruses. When vOTUs were mapped against the RVD genomes as described in methods, only 1.10% (98 vOTUs) showed alignment, suggesting a vastly different ruminal virome. It is important to note that the bioinformatic tools used in this study may increase the false discovery rate when applied to bulk metagenomes17.

Out of the 8933 vOTUs, geNomad classified 8679 as viruses (Supplementary Data 1). Among these, 8670, 425 and 85 vOTUs (97.1, 4.76 and 0.95% of the total vOTUs identified) were assigned to existing class, order and family levels, respectively, based on the latest ICTV taxonomy. Previous studies have struggled to accurately determine the taxonomy of most ruminal viruses as well10,11. The vast majority of vOTUs were classified within the Duplodnaviria realm (96.7% of the total vOTUs identified), with all belonging to the dsDNA Caudoviricetes class (Fig. 1a). Within this group, the Crassvirales order, and Schitoviridae and Herelleviridae families were the most abundant (3.81, 0.29, and 0.11% of the total vOTUs identified, respectively). Viruses from the Ackermannviridae, Autographiviridae, Kyanoviridae, Straboviridae, and Vilmaviridae families were also identified (Fig. 1b). Only 32 vOTUs (0.36% of the total vOTUs identified; Fig. 1c) were classified within other realms. We identified viruses from the Varidnaviria realm, including NCLDVs (Nucleocytoplasmic large DNA viruses; 24 vOTUs) which constitute the Nucleocytoviricota phylum, and one virophage vOTU from the Lavidaviridae family (Fig. 1c). The Nucleocytoviricota phylum (24 vOTUs) included viruses of the Megaviricetes (16 vOTUs) and Pokkesviricetes (8 vOTUs) classes, and Phycodnaviridae (2 vOTUs), Mimiviridae (13 vOTUs), Ascoviridae (1 vOTU), and Poxviridae (8 vOTUs) families. Four vOTUs were classified within the ssDNA Monodnaviria realm. These vOTUs were assigned to the Inoviridae, Microviridae, and Geminiviridae families. Additionally, two vOTUs were classified as RNA viruses within the Riboviria realm, including one vOTU from the Rhabdoviridae family. One vOTU was identified from the dsDNA Bicaudaviridae realm.

Identification of rumen viral hosts

The viral host was identified for 32.6% of the vOTUs (Supplementary Data 1), with most hosts being bacteria (98.6%), and a small proportion archaea (1.38%). Among bacterial hosts, 53.2% belonged to the Bacteroidota phylum, 33.6% to the Bacillota phylum, 11.5% to other phyla, and 1.67% to unclassified bacteria. Hosts from the Bacteroidota phyla were mainly classified in the Bacteroidales order (99.6%: Fig. 2a), with Prevotella being the most abundant genus (67.8%). Although many hosts could not be classified to the genus level, other notable genera included Sodaliphilus and Bacteroides (2.74 and 4.12%, respectively). For bacterial hosts in the Bacillota phylum, 73.0% were classified within the Clostridia order (Fig. 2b), 21.8% within the Bacilli order, and 5.17% within the Negativicutes order. The most abundant genera were UBA3789 (12.5%), Ruminococcus_E (11.0%), CAG-791 (5.27%), and Butyrivibrio (3.72%). Other bacterial hosts phyla included Pseudomonadota (4.63%; Fig. 2c), mainly the UBA2810 genera (3.26%), as well as Fibrobacterota (1.06%), Cyanobacteria (1.51%), Actinomycetota (2.09%) and Spirochaetota (1.10%).

Most archaeal hosts were from the Methanobacteriota phylum (Fig. 2d; 85.0%), with the Methanobrevibacter genus being the most abundant (80.0%). The genera Methanosphaera was identified as a potential host as well. Notably, no eukaryotes were predicted as hosts for any vOTU. Although the existence of viruses infecting protozoa and fungi in the rumen has been discussed10,18,19, there is probably not a comprehensive information about eukaryote hosts in the databases.

The Caudoviricetes class was the most abundant viral class in the rumen (96.9% relative abundance; Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Data 2). Megaviricetes and Pokkesviricetes were the next most abundant classes although with a substantial difference (1.46 and 0.36%, respectively). The vOTU with the largest mean relative abundance was vOTU_26887, classified within the Caudoviricetes class (7.78%; Supplementary Fig. 2). This was followed by vOTU_9 and vOTU_1704, belonging to the Schitoviridae family (3.21%) and Caudoviricetes class (3.12%), respectively. There were no vOTUs present across all samples in our study; however, thirteen vOTUs were present in 90% of the cows, including the vOTU with the largest relative abundance. Nonetheless, all animals used in this study were the same breed, sourced from a similar geographic region, fed a similar diet (but different crops) and housed under comparable management practices. This small number of viruses may constitute part of the core ruminal virome. All vOTUs in this core group belonged to the Caudoviricetes class, whose host were from the Prevotella genus (83%), and from the UBA2810 genus (17%). It is not surprising that the core virome would be associated with the most abundant bacterial genera in the rumen.

The ruminal virome is associated with cattle methane emissions

The relative abundances of viral classes and vOTUs in cows with LOW and HIGH methane emissions are shown in Fig. 3. Both farm and lactation stage significantly influenced the overall ruminal virome based on vOTUs (PERMANOVA; p < 0.001 and 0.019, respectively), and differences between LOW and HIGH emission cows were also significant (p < 0.001). Significant differences in Shannon and Simpson diversity indexes (p = 0.037 and 0.039, respectively) were observed between LOW and HIGH emission cows. Similar results were found using the RVD abundance matrix.

Seventeen vOTUs from the Caudoviricetes class were overabundant in LOW cows (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Data 3). Among these, the host could be identified for ten vOTUs, corresponding to the genera Prevotella (7), Ruminococcus_E (1), Alistipes (1), and Erwinia (1). The most abundant vOTU (vOTU_26887) in our virome was also significant, although its host was not identified. Three vOTUs from the Caudoviricetes class were overabundant in the rumen of cows with higher methane emission levels, with Prevotella and Ruminococcus_E being two known host microbes. The fourth vOTU associated to high methane emission belonged to the Mimiviridae family which exhibit a broad host range, including eukaryotes. None of the differentially abundant vOTUs were assigned as proviruses.

Similar results were observed when using the RVD abundance matrix. Thirty-three viruses were overabundant in LOW cows, including members of the Myoviridae (1) and Schitoviridae (1) families, while the remaining 31 viruses were from unassigned families although these were probably Caudovirales (Supplementary Data 3). In contrast, only three viruses were overabundant in HIGH cows (Fig. 4b). Differences in our results could be attributed to recent changes in viral taxonomy by the ICTV. For the differentially abundant viruses obtained when using the transformed RVD abundance, the host could be assigned only to ten of them. Within overabundant viruses in LOW cows, the host was predominantly from the genus Prevotella (5), although hosts from the Mitsuokella (1) and UBA2810 (2) genera were also identified. For viruses overabundant in HIGH cows, the hosts included one bacterium from the genus Ruminococcus_E and one Methanobrevibacter_A archaea.

Differences in the abundance of other microorganisms were also observed. Up to 31 bacterial taxonomical features (TF), mainly from Bacillota phylum, were overabundant in LOW cows, along with bacteria from other phyla, such as Bacteroidota and Pseudomonadota, and three archaeal TFs from the Methanomicrobia class, two of which were from the Methanocorpusculaceae family. On the contrary, 4 fungal and 4 protozoan TFs were overabundant in HIGH cows (Fig. 4c; Supplementary Data 3). Archaea from the Methanocorpusculaceae family have been associated with low methane emissions4, while other results are consistent with previous findings by Wallace et al.1 and Martínez-Álvaro et al.20.

Viral co-occurrence networks reveal structures associated to methane emissions

The co-occurrence analysis revealed a complex community structure composed of five distinct clusters (Fig. 5). Detailed information regarding nodes and edges is available in Supplementary Data 4. Cluster I contained vOTUs from the Varidnaviria and Monodnaviria realms, along with two vOTUs overabundant in HIGH cows. Cluster II was primarily composed of Duplodnaviria vOTUs, although one Varidnaviria vOTU was also present. Cluster III consisted exclusively of Duplodnaviria vOTUs with Bacteroidota as their hosts. Both clusters II and III contained vOTUs associated with low methane emissions, but Cluster III also included a single vOTU linked to high methane emissions. Cluster IV was the largest and mainly included Duplodnaviria vOTUs that were overabundant in LOW cows. Finally, Cluster V contained vOTUs from the Duplodnaviria, Varidnaviria and Riboviria realms, with distinct hosts. This cluster also included one vOTU linked to high methane emissions. Negative correlations, represented by red lines or edges and indicating mutual exclusion (i.e., an increase in the abundance of one vOTU, or node, corresponds to a decrease in the abundance of a connected node), were scarce between clusters but noticeable among vOTUs within Cluster IV. In contrast, positive correlations, represented by green edges and indicative of co-occurrence, were found within all clusters except Cluster V. Additionally, Clusters II and IV were interconnected through positive correlations between their nodes.

We also investigated associations between viruses and other microorganisms (bacteria, archaea, fungi, and protozoa). Host predictions obtained using iPHoP were validated with this co-occurrence network. The analysis revealed that most vOTUs were positively correlated with their predicted host or with taxonomically related organisms, suggesting a higher abundance of vOTUs in association with their hosts. This network analysis grouped vOTUs into four distinct clusters (Fig. 6). Likewise, detailed information about nodes and edges is available in Supplementary Data 4. Cluster α consisted of Duplodnaviria vOTUs and included two vOTUs overabundant in HIGH cows. vOTUs within this cluster were negatively correlated with bacteria overabundant in LOW cows, particularly those from the Bacillota and Bacteroidota phyla. Additionally, some vOTUs were negatively correlated with bacteria from the Actinomycetota phylum, and some archaea, fungi, and protozoa, including the Stentor genus. Cluster β included vOTUs from the Duplodnaviria, Varidnaviria, and Monodnaviria realms, and most of these were positively associated with fungi, primarily from the Neocallimastigales order, and protozoa from the Ciliophora phylum, which are associated with higher methane emissions as well. Positive correlations were also observed with other fungi, protozoa, and bacteria. Cluster γ contained all the vOTUs overabundant in LOW cows, and these were positively correlated with other microorganisms also overabundant in LOW cows. On the other hand, some vOTUs within this cluster showed negative correlations with the Dialister genus (associated with LOW cows). In addition, vOTUs in Cluster γ showed negative correlations with other bacteria from the Bacillota and Pseudomonadota phyla, and fibre-associated bacteria from the Elusimicrobia, Fibrobacterota, Kiritimatiellaeota, Planctomycetota, Riflebacteria, Synergistota, Tenericutes and Verrucomicrobiota phyla21, as well as with methanogenic archaea (e.g., the Methanobrevibacter genus), fungi, and protozoa. Cluster δ included all vOTUs identified as Crassvirales and other Caudoviricetes, and most of the vOTUs in this cluster were negatively correlated with the protozoa genera Polyplastron, Epidinium and Ophryoscolex.

Discussion

This study provides insights into the association between the ruminal virome and methane emissions in dairy cattle under commercial production settings. By identifying a diverse array of rumen viruses, including some bacteriophages and megaviruses that infect ciliates and fungi, we demonstrate that viral populations are intricately associated with the ruminal microorganisms responsible for methane production. A large number of dsDNA viruses and their predicted hosts, along with the smaller fractions of ssDNA and eukaryotic-infecting viruses were identified, in agreement with previous studies10,11,19,22. The high abundance of Caudoviricetes in the rumen and the small core of viruses also align with earlier research. However, the viruses we identified somewhat differed from those reported by Yan et al.10. Several factors, including diet, geographic location, management, species, and breed may influence the ruminal virome10,11. It should be noted that, although the diets were similar, subtle differences in their exact composition may have influenced the results reported in this study. This study describes the rumen viral composition and the host microbes, providing evidence of the association of certain vOTUs with enteric methane production, likely through correlations with the relative abundance of their hosts involved in hydrogen metabolism and methanogenesis. The presence of RNA viruses in the rumen has likely gone unreported due to the inherent challenges of detecting RNA viruses using traditional DNA-based approaches19. Reverse-transcribing RNA viruses within the Riboviria realm may replicate by converting their genomes into DNA and integrating into the host23, which could partially explain our findings. Members of the Rhabdoviridae family are negative sense single-stranded RNA viruses that infect a wide range of hosts24, and their replication cycle relies exclusively on RNA23. Perhaps the taxonomic classification of this vOTU was incorrect. Nonetheless, ONT could assist to the study of certain RNA viruses, which might contribute to a better understanding of ruminal ecosystems19. Additionally, we reported the presence of virophages from the Lavidaviridae family, and these depend on a coinfecting Mimiviridae virus for propagation25. Their presence within the ruminal ecosystem is not unexpected, and it could suggest that interactions among ruminal microorganisms are far more complex than previously recognized.

Although iPHoP was unable to predict the hosts for most vOTU, potential hosts can be found in the literature, offering valuable insights into ruminal ecosystems. Members of the Phycodnaviridae family are known to infect Cryptophyta and Stramenopila, but they have also been predicted to infect Alveolata, which includes ruminal ciliates16. Similarly, Mimiviridae viruses can infect amoebae and other unicellular eukaryotes26. Viruses in the Bicaudaviridae family infect archaea19, while smaller viruses, such as those from the Inoviridae and Microviridae families, can infect bacteria or archaea19,27. Previous research on virus-host associations may come handy to identify potential viruses concomitantly ingested with feed, introduced by small insects present in the feed, or associated with animal viruses that do not typically infect ruminal microorganisms19. For example, viruses from the Ascoviridae and Poxviridae are known to infect animals16, while viruses from the Geminiviridae family are well-documented crop viruses28. Nonetheless, we cannot entirely rule out the possibility that these viruses are either transient entities with a specific role in the ruminal ecosystem or actual members of the ruminal microbiome.

In this study, we found that ruminal viruses are significantly associated to methane emissions in dairy cattle, possibly through virus-host interactions. Similarly, Yan and Zu12 reported differences in the ruminal virome of sheep with varying methane emissions, though their sample was much smaller, and their results were less notorious. Our results also suggest that ruminal viruses (bacteriophages and megaviruses) associated with methane emissions clustered based on their hosts and role in methane production and thus, microbial networks are similar to those for other ruminal microorganisms13, although the interactions in this context may be more complex.

The most abundant hosts for bacteriophages associated with low methane emissions were Prevotella, Ruminococcus, and members of the Gammaproteobacteria class. Prevotella is linked to propionate production, while a higher abundance of Gammaproteobacteria has previously been observed in animals with lower methane emissions13. This may help explain the greater abundance of these vOTUs in cows with low methane emissions. Hosts for viruses that were overabundant in high-emission cows included Prevotella, Ruminococcus, and Methanobrevibacter, all of them associated with methane emissions either through fibre degradation11 or via direct involvement in methanogenesis. Mechanistically, it has been proposed that archaeophages or bacteriophages, or their derived enzymes, may target microorganisms associated with methane production thereby reducing emissions3,8,9. Interpreting viral abundances and its link to methane emissions can be challenging. Viruses can regulate host abundance through lysis, a process often referred to as ‘killing the winner’29. Lytic phages primarily target fast-growing microorganisms, preventing the exclusion of slow-growing microorganisms and maintaining microbial diversity and balance30,31. However, fast-growing microorganisms can be associated with lower methane emissions32. The lysis of these bacteria might lead to increased methane emissions. An alternative virus-host lysogenic dynamic, in which viruses co-exist with their host, known as ‘piggyback the winner’, may be also prevalent in the rumen11,12,19,33. Sato et al.11 reported that lysogenic signatures are frequent in the rumen suggesting ‘piggy-back the winner’ dynamics are more plausible. Bacterial lysis may also be influenced by the capacity of bacteriophages to alternate between lytic and lysogenic cycles18,34,35. Furthermore, bacteriophages might have multiple hosts with diverse dynamics, and differ between rumen regions19,29,35. In this study, we observed that viruses were associated with microorganisms linked to both high and low methane emissions, and the interpretation of these associations may depend on the underlying microbial dynamics. It is important to note that this analysis was conducted at the genus level; species- or strain-level resolution could provide further insight. Additionally, it is possible that database limitations and bioinformatic tools used here influenced the results. These complex mechanisms may pose challenges for developing strategies targeting viral hosts associated to methane emissions. Despite the limitations, the findings are encouraging and underscore the need for continued investigation in this area. Nevertheless, the abundance of certain viruses could still serve as an effective proxy of methane emissions, potentially aiding in the selection of animals with lower emissions for breeding programs.

The association between ruminal ciliates and fungi, and methane emissions is generally well understood. During ruminal fermentation, certain microorganisms produce H₂, which must be eliminated to prevent any negative effects on fibre fermentation. One major H₂ sink is methane production36. Ruminal ciliates and fungi are often associated with methanogenic archaea, as they provide archaea with H2 for methane production37–39. Therefore, lowering the abundance of ciliates or fungi might promote lower methane emissions, which has been the objective of certain breeding programs32. In this study, we identified a viral operational taxonomic unit (vOTU_19349) belonging to the Mimiviridae family that was significantly overabundant in high-emission cows and positively correlated with the ciliate genus Paramecium, which was also overabundant in these animals. This correlation suggests that Mimiviridae viruses might influence ciliates population within the rumen and thus could be used to modulate these populations and reduce methane production. Harnessing viruses to selectively modulate protozoal communities with known roles in methanogenesis represents an appealing strategy to mitigate methane emissions, although virus-host dynamics must be considered and further research is needed.

Although not differentially abundant, viruses within Cluster β were associated with ruminal ciliates and fungi linked to larger methane emissions, warranting further investigation. Annotation provided by geNomad was limited (Supplementary Data 5), but these viruses displayed a greater abundance of leucine-rich repeat proteins which have been identified in many Nucleocytoviricota, including Mimiviridae viruses40,41. Using Host Taxon Predictor42 (HTP; https://github.com/wojciech-galan/viruses_classifier) to determine whether these viruses infect eukaryotes or are bacteriophages, we realized that most of these viruses were indeed eukaryote-infecting viruses, as consistently shown by the various classifiers available in the tool (Supplementary Table 1). Given the limited reliability of current bioinformatic tools to assign viral taxonomy, some of these viruses might be misclassified taxonomically. These viruses within Cluster β might well be Mimiviridae infecting ruminal eukaryotes associated with methane emissions, either with lytic or lysogenic dynamics. Despite their relatively low abundance in the rumen compared to bacteriophages, Mimiviridae viruses may play a significant role in regulating methane emissions. While these findings remain speculative, they may guide future research into the role of megaviruses within the rumen and the underlying mechanisms through which they exert their influence. Co-occurrence networks and associative thinking can be valuable tools for studying the ruminal virome, but their results should be interpreted with caution; observing burrows in a field might lead us to speculate about bunnies living there, but direct confirmation is needed. Conclusions regarding these ‘bunny-like viruses’ might be speculative, yet they can be useful for understanding or proposing hypothesis regarding virus-microbe relationships in future studies.

The results in this study proved association between viruses and methane emissions occurring through their hosts, but viruses could also have a direct influence on methane emissions, although our current understanding is limited18. For instances, viruses interact with microbial biofilms, a crucial step in fibre degradation18. Additionally, lysogenic viruses can confer new metabolic capabilities to their hosts29. Giant viruses may influence or enhance complex metabolic processes in their hosts. They could modulate fermentation capacity and provide advantages to Tetraselmis virus hosts in nutrient-deficient environments16. Perhaps, lysogenic Megaviricetes infecting fungi influence their capacity to ferment fibre in the rumen. Viruses can contribute to nutrient cycling and may serve as a nutrient source for microorganisms associated with methane production. Recently, De Long et al.43 demonstrated that small protists, including ciliates such as Halteria sp., can proliferate consuming only chloroviruses and viral particles. In our study, we observed numerous negative correlations between ciliates, particularly Polyplastron, and viruses, which might reflect such trophic interactions, although this is highly speculative. The ruminal virome remains a largely unexplored and fascinating area of study with numerous unknowns. Further investigation into this topic could be crucial for developing strategies to mitigate methane emissions.

It must be pointed out that current bioinformatic tools and publicly available datasets pose some limitations, mainly due to the limited existing knowledge on rumen virus10. In addition, DNA extraction protocol used in this study was not specific for the viral fraction, which may bias or mask true representation of viruses in the rumen11. Nevertheless, ONT proved effective in identifying viruses directly from ruminal metagenomes. ONT long-read sequencing may facilitate the identification of viruses with smaller genomes and RNA viruses, and its integration with other methodologies could enhance viral identification11. Our findings differed significantly from those of previous studies10, particularly in the number of viruses identified. Differences may be attributed to the use of long-read sequencing compared to short-read sequencing. Assembling more contigs from a larger number of short reads is possible, although these contigs would be shorter and had lower quality. Indeed, we observed a higher (24%) proportion of viral contigs with completed, high- and medium-quality compared to the 11% reported by Yan et al. 10. Finally, in terms of read mapping, our pipeline outperformed SqueezeMeta (SQM) using curated viral genomes database, with 600,981 mapped reads. Additionally, the taxonomic resolution was superior as well; 27.9% of mapped reads via SQM were classified generically as virus, and 27.6% were assigned to higher taxonomic levels, such as Uroviricota or Caudoviricetes, with limited further classification.

Results from this study suggest that classification of high and low emitting cows can be performed by identifying a small amount of the viral fraction associated with microorganism known to be involved in methane production. This could be done using an amplification of viral contigs previously selected based on their association with methane production and host. Then, a quantitative PCR, or identification by sequencing could differentiate high from low emitters.

Methods

Rumen metagenomes and data processing

This study utilised available data from López-García et al.13. The data consisted of ruminal metagenomes from 448 dairy Holstein cows within 14 commercial farms located in the north region of Spain and collected between May 2018 and June 2019. Detailed information on the generation of methane phenotypes is provided in López-Paredes et al.44, whereas information on sampling and sequencing is provided in López-García et al.13 and Saborío-Montero et al.45. In summary, animals were fed a total mixed ration (TMR) based on a high-cereal concentrate plus maize and grass silage. The forage-to-concentrate ratio of the TMR for all animals throughout the entire lactation period was between 35:65 and 40:60, in accordance with Spanish guidelines for dairy cattle. Farm conditions, as well as nutritional and animal management practices, were similar across all farms according to the Spanish guidelines for dairy cattle, ensuring a homogeneous group of animals. Metadata for each cow, including their methane concentration and their classification as either HIGH or LOW emitters, can be seen in Supplementary Data 6. Original fast5 files were re-basecalled and demultiplexed using Dorado software (v0.7.0., Oxford Nanopore Technologies) in sup-accuracy mode. Reads with a quality score below 12 or lengths under 150 bp were excluded from analysis. A total of 96,094,947 raw reads from 448 ruminal metagenomes were sequenced, with an average N50 value of 4241 bp and an average read length of 2399 bp.

Assembly, identification and characterization of vOTUs

Assembly, identification, and characterization of vOTUs obtained from ruminal metagenomes was conducted using specialized bioinformatic tools as follows: To identify potential viral sequences from raw reads, VirSorter246 (v2.2.4.; https://github.com/jiarong/VirSorter2) and CheckV47 (v1.0.3.; https://bitbucket.org/berkeleylab/checkv/src/master) were employed. VirSorter2 was initially executed (options: virsorter run --keep-original-seq --include-groups ssDNA,dsDNAphage,NCLDV,RNA,lavidaviridae --min-length 3000 --min-score 0.5 --provirus-off --max-orf-per-seq 20) with a loose cut-off score of 0.5 to maximize sensitivity, including all available viral groups. Host regions trimming was deferred to more specialized software. To hasten processing, the maximum number of ORFs tested per sequence was restricted to 20. The minimum sequence length was set to 3 kb, even though a 5 kb minimum might be recommended to reduce the number of false positives10. This relaxed threshold was used at this stage to enhance the discovery of viral sequences from long raw reads. CheckV (options: checkv end_to_end -d checkv-db-v1.5) was used to assess the quality of potential viral contigs obtained from VirSorter2 and to trim residual host regions. Sequences were retained if they met one of the following criteria: contained >0 viral genes, contained no viral genes but had no host genes and either a score ≥0.95 or >2 hallmark genes, following the VirSorter2 SOP (10.17504/protocols.io.bwm5pc86). This process identified approximately 2,473,147 viral sequences. The viral sequences were assembled into contigs using metaFlye48 (v2.9.5.; https://github.com/mikolmogorov/Flye) and the -nano-hq mode optimized for ONT data (options: flye --nano-hq --meta). This process yielded 24,522 potential viral contigs. Contigs with a coverage <10 were subsequently removed, resulting in 12,235 contigs. To confirm the viral nature of potential viral contigs, and determine their taxonomy, the ‘Viruses and Plasmids’ workflow from geNomad49 (v1.8.0.; https://github.com/apcamargo/genomad) with default parameters was used. Plasmids identified were not included in this study. Viral contigs with a virus_score >0.7 were retained, resulting in 8933 vOTUs (long-reads-assembled vOTUs; LRAV genomes). Additionally, the quality and completeness of vOTUs were assessed with CheckV. The bioinformatic tool iPHoP50 (options: iphop predict -d Aug_2023_pub_rw; v1.3.3.; https://bitbucket.org/srouxjgi/iphop/src/main) was used to predict the host of vOTUs. The taxonomy provided by iPHoP was consistently used throughout the entire study. Multiple predictions can be made for a single vOTU. While it is recommended to select the prediction with the highest score, we instead opted for a last common ancestor approach to choose the prediction. Finally, binning was performed on the viral contigs using vRhyme51 (v1.1.0.; https://github.com/AnantharamanLab/vRhyme), but no binning results were reported. To assess similarities between vOTUs and viruses previously reported in the literature, we mapped vOTUs to the largest and most recent ruminal viral database (Yan et al.10; RVD genomes, available at https://github.com/ yan1365/RVD). For that purpose, we used the contig function of the CoverM tool (v0.7.0.; https://github.com/wwood/CoverM) and the trimmed mean method as described by Yan et al.10, although we used minimap2-ont as the underlying mapping software (options: coverm contig --single -p minimap2-ont -m trimmed_mean --min-read-percent-identity 0.95 --min- read-aligned-percent 0.75 --min-covered-fraction 0.7).

Identification of other ruminal microorganisms

To identify other ruminal microorganisms, including bacteria, archaea, protozoa, and fungi, within the ruminal metagenomes, we used the SQM v1.6.5. pipeline for long reads and the --euk option (options: sqm_longreads.pl --euk) to improve eukaryotic annotation52. This pipeline executes Diamond Blastx against the GenBank nr taxonomic database and identifies and annotates open reading frames using the LCA method. All unclassified sequences or those mapped to viruses, animals, plants, or algae were removed. A total of 50,202,400 sequences from 445 ruminal metagenomes were classified into 3296 TF at the genus level. The taxonomical features also included microorganisms that could not be classified to the genus level, which were labelled as ‘unclassified’ along the TF identified at the latest taxonomic level.

Determining the abundance of ruminal microorganisms

To estimate the abundance of ruminal microorganisms and study its association with methane emissions, we first mapped the whole set of raw reads against the LRAV genomes using the contig function of the CoverM tool and the count method (options: coverm contig --single -p minimap2-ont -m count --min-read-percent-identity 0.95 --min-read-aligned-percent 0.75) to obtain a counts matrix. We removed cows with no methane concentration record and those with zero values in > 90% of vOTUs. Afterwards, vOTUs with a prevalence <25% were filtered out. Classification of cows as LOW or HIGH emitter was conducted through a quartile-based criteria, and thus we considered this relaxed prevalence could facilitate the identification of vOTUs associated to methane emissions. The final LRAV counts matrix contained 856,117 mapped reads from 888 vOTUs within 431 cows. Relative abundances were calculated using this counts matrix. Following the same methodology, we also generated a filtered counts matrix from a reduced version of the RVD genomes, which included only genomes of medium quality or higher, as well as those with available information on taxonomy or host. We decided to use a reduced version to diminish the number of tests conducted, thereby retaining the maximum number of genomes when adjusting the p-value, while minimizing false positives. This counts matrix contained 3,570,383 mapped reads from 3262 genomes. The SQM pipeline generates a counts matrix, which, after being filtered as described earlier, contained 48,571,226 reads from 575 TFs in 428 cows.

Co-occurrence analysis

Counts matrices were used to construct co-occurrence networks to assess potential relationships among ruminal viruses, as well as between ruminal viruses and other microorganisms. For that purpose, we employed the CoNet tool (v1.1.1.) in Cytoscape (v.3.9.1.) following the workflow outlined in Faust and Raes53. Kamada–Kawai (edge-weighted spring embedded layout) algorithm54 was utilised for graph visualization, using the q-value as the force parameter. In this layout, smaller distances between connected nodes (representing pairwise correlated microorganisms) indicate stronger positive correlations, while larger distances indicate stronger negative correlations.

Associations between ruminal microorganisms and methane emissions were investigated using only cows classified as LOW (n = 109) and HIGH (n = 108). Differentially abundant vOTUs or TF between these groups were analysed on the CLR-transformed data using the limma R package60. p-values were adjusted using the Benjamin-Hochberg method, and a threshold of padj > 0.05 and |log-FC| ≥ 0.5 was applied to identify differentially abundant microorganisms.

Statistics and reproducibility

The statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.3.1.), following the methods outlined in earlier sections. Since no new experiments were conducted in this study, no statistical approach was applied to predefine the sample size. The data utilised originated from prior research13.
