Immunomodulating melatonin-decorated silica nanoparticles suppress bacterial wilt (Ralstonia solanacearum) in tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) through fine-tuning of oxidative signaling and rhizosphere bacterial community
Authors: Munazza Ijaz, Luqiong Lv, Temoor Ahmed, Muhammad Noman, Abdul Manan, Rafia Ijaz, Rahila Hafeez, Muhammad Shafiq Shahid, Daoze Wang, Gabrijel Ondrasek, Bin Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) production is severely threatened by bacterial wilt, caused by the phytopathogenic bacterium Ralstonia solanacearum. Recently, nano-enabled strategies have shown tremendous potential in crop disease management.ObjectivesThis study investigates the efficacy of biogenic nanoformulations (BNFs), comprising biogenic silica nanoparticles (SiNPs) and melatonin (MT), in controlling bacterial wilt in tomato.MethodsSiNPs were synthesized using Zizania latifolia leaves extract. Further, MT containing BNFs were synthesized through the one-pot approach. Nanomaterials were characterized using standard characterization techniques. Greenhouse disease assays were conducted to assess the impact of SiNPs and BNFs on tomato plant immunity and resistance to bacterial wilt.ResultsThe SiNPs and BNFs exhibited a spherical morphology, with particle sizes ranging from 13.02 nm to 22.33 nm for the SiNPs and 17.63 nm to 21.79 nm for the BNFs, indicating a relatively uniform size distribution and consistent shape across both materials. Greenhouse experiments revealed that soil application of BNFs outperformed SiNPs, significantly enhancing plant immunity and reducing bacterial wilt incidence by 78.29% in tomato plants by maintaining oxidative stress homeostasis via increasing the activities of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (31.81%), peroxidase (32.9%), catalase (32.65%), and ascorbate peroxidase (47.37%) compared to untreated infected plants. Additionally, BNFs induced disease resistance by enhancing the production of salicylic acid and activating defense-related genes (e.g., SlPAL1, SlICS1, SlNPR1, SlEDS, SlPD4, and SlSARD1) involved in phytohormones signaling in infected tomato plants. High-throughput 16 S rRNA sequencing revealed that BNFs promoted growth of beneficial rhizosphere bacteria (Gemmatimonadaceae, Ramlibacter, Microscillaceae, Anaerolineaceae, Chloroplast and Phormidium) in both healthy and diseased plants, while suppressing R. solanacearum abundance in infected plants.ConclusionOverall, these findings suggest that BNFs offer a more promising and sustainable approach for managing bacterial wilt disease in tomato plants.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-024-02910-w.

Introduction

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is a widely consumed and highly nutritious crop worldwide. In the last few decades, tomato plants have been frequently infected by bacterial wilt, a disease caused by the soil-borne phytopathogen (Ralstonia solanacearum), which severely compromises fruit quality and yield (up to 20–50%), resulting in economic losses of around $0.88 billion [1–3]. Traditional breeding and chemical disease management strategies have not been fully successful in controlling bacterial wilt and may pose serious risks to human health and the environment [4–6]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find an efficient and environmentally friendly method to control R. solanacearum infection and mitigate its significant threat to tomato fruit production.

Biogenic nanomaterials are gaining significant attention as a promising tool for enhancing yield and global food security due to their small size, large surface area, targeted nutrient delivery, reduced input requirements, greater biocompatibility and lower eco-toxicity over conventional products and approaches [7, 8]. Different types of nanomaterials serve different purposes in agriculture, such as acting as antimicrobial agents, plant defense response elicitors against pathogens, and functioning as carriers for active ingredients including pesticides, micronutrients, and elicitors [9–12]. Moreover, biogenic nanomaterials particularly silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs) have been suggested as good elicitors for initiating innate plant immunity [13, 14]. For example, SiNPs enhanced disease resistance in Zingiber officinale rhizomes by inducing antioxidant enzymes and phenylpropanoid pathway-related genes [15]. Moreover, SiNPs enhanced tomato tolerance to branched broomrape (Orobanche ramosa) infection by activating plant defense mechanisms [16]. In a study, El- Shetehy et al. [17] highlighted the capacity of SiNPs to trigger systemic disease resistance against the bacterial pathogen Pseudomonas syringae pv. syringae in Arabidopsis thaliana by inducing the production of stress-related hormone, including salicylic acid (SA). Moreover, Si-based NPs has also been reported to reshape soil microbial communities to suppress diseases, for example, foliar application of SiO2-NPs positively affected beneficial microorganisms in the soil, but led to decreases in the relative abundance of bacterial genera causing plant diseases including Litorilinea, Rhodoplanes, Caldilineaceae, and Bdellovibrionaceae in Brassica chinensis [18].

Additionally, innate defense mechanisms such as systemic acquired resistance (SAR) can also be initiated through the application of phytohormones, such as MT [19–21], that has been documented to augment plant growth and suppress diseases in plants [22, 23]. For instance, MT treatment significantly enhanced tomato fruit resistance to Botrytis cinerea by modulating defense-related enzyme activities, reducing hydrogen peroxide content, and up-regulating the expression of key genes involved in the stress signaling pathway [24]. MT can also enhance antioxidant systems like superoxide dismutase (SOD), peroxidase (POD), catalase (CAT), and ascorbate peroxidase (APX) to scavenge reactive oxygen species (ROS) induced by infestation of phytopathogens [25, 26]. Moreover, exogenous application of MT has improved the growth of Malus domestica by enhancing tolerance to apple replant disease (caused by a complex of different pathogens including Phytophthora cactorum, Cylindrocarpon, Pythium species and Rhizoctonia solani) through up-regulating antioxidant enzyme-related genes, promoting soil enzyme activity and bacterial richness, and decreasing harmful fungi abundance in the rhizosphere soil [27].

Thus, the utilization of MT-coated SiNPs, developing BNFs, presents a promising tool for enhancing growth, antioxidant activity, inducing stress signaling and reshaping soil microbiome to combat biotic stresses. Previously, it has been reported that SA-coated iron NPs suppressed Fusarium wilt in watermelon by activating SA-responsive defense mechanisms [28]; however, there is a lack of understanding regarding the functional benefits and underlying disease-suppressive mechanisms of synergistic application of MT and SiNPs in crops. The current study hypothesizes that BNFs can more effectively enhance tomato resistance against bacterial wilt disease as compared to SiNPs by stimulating plant immunity through multiple mechanisms. Specifically, this study proposes that BNFs contribute to disease resistance by reducing ROS levels through increased antioxidant production, inducing SAR in tomato plants to combat bacterial pathogens, and reshaping the rhizosphere microbial community to suppress R. solanacearum. This multifaceted approach highlights the potential of BNFs to offer superior protection against bacterial wilt and promote sustainable crop management practices.

Materials and methods

Green synthesis of SiNPs

The SiNPs were synthesized by using silicon dioxide (SiO2; Boer technology Co. Ltd, China) and Zizania latifolia leaves extract, following the protocol outlined by Rahimzadeh et al. [29]. Briefly, fresh leaves of Zizania latifolia were obtained from Zijingang Campus, Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China (coordinates 120°08′ E and 30°30′ N). The leaves were thoroughly cleaned by washing multiple times with tap water, followed by rinsing with distilled water and ethanol to remove soil particles. The 10 g fresh leaves were finely blended with 100 ml of double distilled water (ddH2O). This mixture was then gently heated with magnetic stirring for 40 min at 65 °C. Afterwards, the obtained solution was filtered using Whatman filter paper No.1, and this filtrate was used to dissolve 0.01 M SiO2 at 60 °C while continuously stirring. The resulting solution, aimed at synthesizing SiNPs, was left for stirring at 180 rpm overnight for allowing the synthesis of SiNPs and then solution was centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 x g, the supernatant was discarded and obtained pellet was washed with ethanol followed by washing with ddH2O to purify SiNPs. The SiNPs were then subjected to drying using an Alpha1-2 LDplus Freeze Dryer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Engineering of BNFs

The synthesis of BNFs was carried out using a one-pot approach by following the protocol devised by Toledano-Osorio et al. [30]. Initially, MT solution was prepared by dissolving 0.25 g of MT powder (Sigma Aldrich Co., Ltd. China) in 100 mL of methanol. Following that 0.5 g of SiNPs was mixed into the 100 ml of freshly prepared MT solution. The mixture was then subjected to continuous stirring at 180 rpm and kept at 27 ± 2 °C for 48 h approximately. To activate the reaction medium, a 0.2% (w/v) glutaraldehyde solution was added. The resulting BNFs were isolated from the reaction mixture through centrifugation for 15 min at 12,000×g. The collected BNFs were washed with ddH2O thrice to ensure the removal of any residual reactants or impurities. The purified BNFs were subjected to freeze drying using the aforementioned method, resulting in the formation of dried BNFs. To ensure reproducibility, the synthesis of SiNPs and BNFs were conducted in five batches, with yield consistency and percentage variation among batches carefully evaluated.

Characterization of SiNPs and BNFs

The biogenically synthesized SiNPs and BNFs were subjected for characterization. The functional groups present in SiNPs and BNFs were analyzed by FTIR (NICOLET iS50FT-IR instrument, Thermo-Fisher Scientific, USA), covering the spectrum of 4000 to 400 cm− 1. The XRD (XPert Pro diffractometer, Holland) was used to determine crystalline nature of SiNPs and BNFs, and the measurements were recorded at the 2θ angle spanning from 20 to 80° [31]. Observations regarding the size, shape, and morphological characteristics of the SiNPs and BNFs were made through SEM (TM1000, Hitachi Japan), as well as an attached EDS module was used to characterize the elemental composition. The TEM (1230 JEOL, Akishima, Japan) was performed, to further examine the fine morphological details of the particles [32].

Construction of R. Solanacearum strain containing pBBR1MCS2-luxCDABE plasmid

The wild-type R. solanacearum strain was obtained from the “State Key Laboratory of Rice Biology and Breeding at Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China”. The strain containing the pBBR1MCS2-luxCDABE plasmid was constructed by adopting the methodology developed by Xu et al. [33]. Briefly, R. solanacearum was cultured in 5 mL of casamino acid-peptone-glucose medium and kept at 28 °C for 16 h, while continuously shaking (200 rpm) to obtain liquid culture. Afterwards, 1 mL aliquots of the culture were centrifuged for 5 min at 3500×g in a temperature-controlled centrifuge machine to maintain 4 °C while centrifugation. The obtained pellet was re-suspended in the sucrose solution of 300 mM sucrose. The plasmid containing pBBR1MCS2-luxCDABE operon was introduced into the competent R. solanacearum cells by using electroporation method.

Experimental design and SAR assays

Tomato plants were cultivated in soil obtained from the Zijingang Campus of Zhejiang University, Hangzhou, China (coordinates 120°08′ E and 30°30′ N). Prior to sowing, tomato seeds underwent thorough washing with deionized water and ethanol, followed by germination in a moist dish at 37 °C for 7 days before transplantation into the soil. The greenhouse conditions maintained a relative humidity of 70%, a 14-hour photoperiod, and a temperature of 28 °C. Firstly, optimization experiment for determining the suitable concentrations of SiNPs and BNFs to enhance tomato (var. zheza 809; susceptible variety) growth was measured by their soil application (via soil drenching method) at various concentrations (0, 50, 100, 250, and 500 µg mg− 1 soil) to plants [34]. Another experiment was performed to assess the effectiveness of BNFs and SiNPs against R. solanacearum infection, comparing them to precursor materials, including SiO2 and MT. Control groups included untreated healthy plants and those solely infected with R. solanacearum. In this experiment, SiO2, MT, SiNPs, and BNFs were applied in soil at a consistent concentration of 250 µg mg− 1 soil. Additionally, a final pot experiment was implemented with six treatments, including healthy and infected control groups, with SiNPs and BNFs applied at a concentration of 250 µg mg− 1 in healthy and diseased plants [35].

Upon reaching 45 days of age, the freshly prepared culture of R. solanacearum (1 × 108 CFU mL− 1) were inoculated to the roots of tomato plants [36]. Treatments, including SiNPs and BNFs, were applied in soil 4 days prior to R. solanacearum inoculation, with plant samples collected for analysis at 3 days post inoculation (dpi). The disease index was determined by using a 5-point scale rating system. This system categorized disease severity as follows: 0 represented no disease, 1 indicated slight disease, 2 denoted slight to moderate disease, 3 identified moderate disease, and 4 designated the highest severity of disease, based on morphological observations of leaves and stems [32, 35]. Moreover, in planta relative bacterial biomass was determined by extracting total DNA from infected plants and represented as the ratios of genomic DNA of the gene encoding flagella subunit (RsFlic) in R. solanacearum [37] to the housekeeping gene (SlActin) of tomato. The relative bacterial biomass was determined by the 2−ΔΔCt method [35]. Experimental setup followed a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replicates per treatment.

Assessment of plant growth and in planta Si content

The leaves of tomato plants were sampled at 3 dpi to assess various physical and biochemical parameters. The plant length (PL), as well as the fresh weight (FW) and dry weight (DW), were measured. Total chlorophyll content and soil plant analysis development (SPAD) value to measure the greenness of leaves was determined by following the method by Ahmed et al. [38]. The in-planta Si content was measured by using oven dried leaves that were acid-digested with HNO3 for 48 h, followed by the addition of ddH2O to make up a final volume of 15 mL. The ICP-MS (Optima8000DV, Pekin Elmer, Waltham, MA, USA) was used to determine the Si content in leaf tissues.

Estimation of antioxidant enzymes, and reactive oxygen species

The 0.1 g of leaf tissues were properly mixed in a 50 mM phosphate buffer at pH 7.0 and centrifuged for 10 min at 12,000 ×g to evaluate antioxidant enzyme activity. After that, a UV-vis spectrophotometer (U-2910, Hitachi, Japan) was utilized to measure the activity of CAT at 240 nm [39], POD at 470 nm [40], SOD at 560 nm [41], and APX at 290 nm [42]. Moreover, the levels of ROS were determined by homogenizing 0.1 g leaf tissues in 0.1% trichloroacetic acid followed by centrifugation for 12 min at 10,000 ×g. The resulting supernatant was then treated with 0.5% thiobarbituric acid and heated for 30 min at 95 °C in a water bath [43]. After cooling it down to room temperature, the concentration of O2•−, H2O2 and MDA were quantified at 530 nm, 430 nm and 532 nm, respectively. In addition to this, the presence of O2•− and H2O2 was visualized in tomato leaves by staining them with nitro-blue tetrazolium (NBT) dye and 3, 3-diaminobenzidine (DAB) dye, respectively.

Bioluminescence and minimum inhibitory concentration assay

R. solanacearum was cultured by streaking it onto petri dishes containing Casamino Acids-Peptone-Glucose-agar (CPG-agar) medium and incubating the plates for 36–48 h, following standard microbiological techniques. To evaluate the effect of SiNPs and BNFs, the well diffusion method was employed. A solution of SiNPs and BNFs, at a concentration of 250 µg mL− 1, was carefully added into wells created in the CPG-agar plates containing R. solanacearum. Additionally, for assessing bioluminescence signals in tomato plants the entire plant was used for both infected and non-infected plants, but only the stems were photographed [33]. The luminescence signals were monitored via photon camera (Photek Limited, UK). Images and data analysis were conducted by using Image32 software, which is specifically designed to work with the photon camera. The binary slice and a 10% neutral density (ND) filter were used to take pictures by photon camera. Photon counts were captured and integrated over 1-minute and 5-minute duration for bacterial petri dishes and plant stems, respectively that provides a comprehensive view of the bioluminescent activity. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay was conducted to evaluate the effects of SiNPs and BNFs. The R. solanacearum bacterial culture (1 × 10⁸ CFU mL⁻¹) was exposed to 250 µg mL− 1 of SiNPs and BNFs for 24 h, after which the bacterial optical density (OD) was measured.

Determination of MT and SA levels

The MT and SA content was measured by adopting the methodology devised by Ghanem et al. [44] with slight modifications. In short, fresh leaves were first grinded into a fine powder using liquid nitrogen and 100 mg sample of this powder was then placed into 1 ml of 80% methanol solution having internal standards at a concentration of 10 ng µL− 1. The mixture was left to incubate for 2 h before being dried under nitrogen gas. These samples were then resuspended in 400 µL of acetic acid ethyl ester and centrifuged for 15 min at 12,000 × g to separate the organic layer which was dried using nitrogen gas. The resulting extract was diluted by the addition of 250 µL of 50% methanol and homogenized by vortexing it for 5 min. This mixture was then centrifuged for 5 min at 12,000 × g to separate the aqueous phase. The aqueous phase was employed for phytohormone estimation via chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LCMS; AB SCIEX, Framingham, MA, USA).

Plant RNA extraction and RT-qPCR analysis

The total RNA was extracted from fresh tomato leaves by the RNA isolation kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The purity and concentration of the RNA were assessed via NanoDrop-1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo-Fisher scientific, USA). The complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the reverse transcriptase PCR kit (Thermo-Fisher scientific, USA). The qRT-PCR reaction was performed by using qRT-PCR Master Mix (SYBR premix, Takara Bio, Dalian, China) for phenylalanine ammonia lyase 1 (SlPAL1), isochorismate synthase 1 (SlICS1), non-expressor of pathogenesis related genes 1 (SlNPR1), enhance disease susceptibility (SlEDS), phytoalexin deficient 4 (SlPD4), systemic acquired resistance deficient 1 (SlSARD1), catalase 1 (SlCAT1), peroxidase 3 (Slpod3), ascorbate peroxidase (SlAPX2), and superoxide dismutase (SlSOD) tomato genes. The SlActin genes served as housekeeping controls to normalize the expression data. The NCBI tool primer blast was used to design primers from mRNA sequence of the above-mentioned genes as mentioned in supplementary Table 1. The relative expressions of targeted genes were assessed by the 2−ΔΔCt method [45].

16 S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

After completing 3 dpi, soil samples were collected in triplicate from the rhizosphere of tomato plants. Genomic DNA was extracted from these soil samples utilizing the DNA isolation kit (Omega Bio-tek, Norcross, GA, USA). The concentration and quality of the extracted DNA were evaluated using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ND-1000 Thermo Fisher Scientific Waltham, MA, USA). The bacterial 16S rRNA gene (V4-V5 region) was amplified using sequence-specific primers (515F 5’-barcode- GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGG)-3’ and 907R 5’-CCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT-3’) in a PCR machine. The barcode used is an eight-nucleotide unique sequence for each sample. The PCR products were purified and then quantified using Qubit®3.0 (Life Invitrogen). Every 24 amplicons were pooled together based on distinct barcodes before being utilized to construct an Illumina Pair-End library (2 × 250), which was used for sequencing on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Shanghai BIOZERON Co., Ltd).

The 16 S rRNA gene sequences were rigorously analyzed by using the Q11ME2 and R packages [46]. Initially, the dataset went through quality trimming to remove low-quality reads, subsequent denoising was applied for efficient sequencing error correction, and finally, the high-quality sequence reads were aggregated utilizing the “DADA2 plugin” [47]. The alpha (α) diversity, which measures within-sample diversity, was analysed by popular metrics including abundance-based coverage estimators (Ace), Chao1, and Shannon indices were calculated using the ASV table within the Q11ME2 platform, while beta (β) diversity, indicating differences in microbial community structures among samples, was estimated by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Jacard metrics. Furthermore, heat maps were generated based on the top 20 microbial phyla and genera using R packages (ver. 3.2.0) to visualize the abundance patterns. Statistical analyses including permutational multivariate analyses of variance (PERMANOVA) and analysis of similarity (ANOSIM) were conducted to ascertain significant differences in microbial community composition among various treatments. Moreover, the linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) was used to identify taxa that were differentially abundant among different treatments by using default parameters [48].

Statistical analysis

The experimental data, comprising three replicates, was statistically analyzed using the data processing system (ver. 20.05) alongside statistix packages (ver. 8.1). The significance of the treatments was assessed via a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). To determine significant differences (P ≤ 0.05), Fisher’s least significant difference (LSD) test was utilized. GraphPad Prism 8.3 was employed for constructing all graphs, while heatmaps were generated using TBtools software.

Results and discussion

Biosynthesis and characterization of SiNPs and BNFs

In this study, SiNPs was extracellularly biosynthesized using plant extract of Z. latifolia as it is a good reducing agent as described by Li et al. [49] and has been used to synthesize biogenic nanoparticles (NPs) as reported by Satheesh et al. [50]. The successful synthesis of biogenic SiNPs by extracts of different plants (including, Oryza sativa) has been reported in previous studies [51]. However, according to the best of our knowledge, we have used extract of Z. latifolia for the first time for the synthesis of SiNPs. The BNFs were synthesized by coating SiNPs with MT, introducing a novel approach, while role of MT in enhancing plant growth and defense is well-established in previous studies [52–55], therefore we have used MT to coat SiNPs. The average yields of SiNPs and BNFs were 196.2 mg and 600.2 mg per 100 mL of reaction volume, respectively. These yields showed variations of ± 6.63% for SiNPs and ± 2.99% for BNFs across five different batches, as detailed in supplementary Table 2. The consistent yields of SiNPs and BNFs, along with the low batch-to-batch variations, make the process well-suited for large-scale synthesis. Additionally, investigating optimization strategies, such as adjusting reaction conditions and scaling up the synthesis process, could further enhance the yield of SiNPs and BNFs in future.

The synthesis, crystalline nature, morphology, size and functional groups of SiNPs and BNFs was confirmed using FTIR, XRD, TEM, and SEM techniques. Similar methods were employed by Ahmed et al. [34] to characterize ferric oxide nanoparticles and bio-nanocomposites, identifying their functional groups, crystalline structure, morphology, and size. The coated nanomaterials have been reported as more stable nanomaterials due to the activity of functional groups and strong capping present in them [56, 57]. The surface characteristics of BNFs were investigated by FTIR in the current study. The biogenic SiNPs have shown absorption peaks at 3432, 1637, 1105, 802 and 471 cm− 1 that represent O-H stretching, C = O stretching, C-O stretching, C-H bending, and C-Cl stretching, respectively as shown in Fig. 1(a). In the case of BNFs, multiple absorbance peaks at 3309, 1630, 1587, 1560, 1442, 1101, 798 and 472 cm− 1 that demonstrate O-H stretching, C = O stretching, C = C stretching, C-N stretching, C-H2 bending, C-O stretching, C-H bending, and C-Cl stretching, respectively as represented in Fig. 1(b). The presence of the above-mentioned functional groups clearly indicates the presence of different biomolecules and enzymes around the surface of SiNPs and BNFs which were serving as predominant capping biomolecules. Moreover, these functional groups were identified and are associated with the surface chemistry of the SiNPs and BNFs. Thus, it can be stated that the alcoholic compounds and proteins present in plant extract might be involved in the production of SiNPs [58] and presence of C = C stretching, C-N stretching and C-H2 bending in BNFs might prove the MT bonding with SiNPs [59]. The presence of these functional groups indicates not just the formation of biogenic SiNPs and BNFs, but also their potential functional role in improving interaction with biological systems, particularly in enhancing plant growth and defense mechanisms [60]. The XRD spectra of SiNPs has shown single diffraction peak at 2θ degree = 28.42º that represent 111 plane as shown in Fig. 1(c) that is in line with Kabaa et al. [61]. The XRD patterns of SiNPs synthesized in this study do not exhibit two additional diffraction peaks corresponding to the (220) and (311) crystallographic planes, as observed in previous studies [62, 63]. The single diffraction peak suggests that the SiNPs are primarily oriented along the (111) crystallographic plane. The absence of other two diffraction peaks could be due to various factors, such as the use of biogenic methods, reaction conditions (e.g., temperature and precursor materials), and synthesis duration, which may have influenced the crystallinity and preferential growth of the SiNPs, resulting in the lack of these peaks. Additionally, smaller particle sizes or amorphous characteristics of the SiNPs may reduce the intensity or visibility of these secondary peaks [64, 65]. The XRD spectra of BNFs (Fig. 1(d)) was representing 4 additional diffraction peaks at 2θ degree (viz., 20.07° (112), 25.66° (100) and 35.03° (102)) other than 111 peak, that is might be due to crystallization of melatonin in the BNFs [66]. The TEM and SEM images have revealed that SiNPs and BNFs have spherical morphology (Fig. 1(e-h)) which are found to be readily aggregated. The SiNPs exhibited a size distribution ranging from 13.02 nm to 22.33 nm, as illustrated in Fig. 1(g), whereas the size range of BNFs was between 17.63 nm and 21.79 nm, as depicted in Fig. 1(h). These results align closely with a previous study that has characterized spherical aggregates of silver NPs synthesized using plant extract [67]. The aggregation of SiNPs and BNFs synthesized from extract of Z. latifolia is primarily driven by the presence of organic biomolecules, such as proteins and polysaccharides, which can lead to particle agglomeration through electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and van der Waals forces [68]. The EDS spectrum and elemental analysis demonstrate that biogenic SiNPs contain Si (31.68%), oxygen (53.27%), carbon (14.65%), and nitrogen (0.40%), whereas the major element present in BNFs were Si (30.78%), oxygen (43.80%), carbon (22.44%), and nitrogen (2.98%) as illustrated in Fig. 1(i-l). The change in EDS spectrum of SiNPs and BNFs might be due to formation of new bonds of SiNPs with MT and might indicate presence of a new compound in BNFs.

SiNPs and BNFs improved tomato plant growth in a concentration-specific manner

The dose of biogenic SiNPs and BNFs for improving plant growth was optimized by applying different concentrations (0, 50, 100, 250, 500 µg mg− 1) to tomato plants as shown in supplementary Fig. 1 (a and e). The FW, DW and PL were also measured, and an obvious improvement in growth parameters was observed in all treatments when compared to control (supplementary Fig. 1 (b-d and f-h)). But the maximum plant growth was observed at 250 µg mg− 1 concentration, where FW (85.87% and 90.83%), DW (87.61% and 90.21%) and PL (29.25% and 30.84%) were increased with the application of SiNPs and BNFs, respectively, as compared to control. These results were consistent with Ijaz et al. [69], who reported the increase in PL, FW and DW of rice at the concentration of 250 mg kg− 1 of SiNPs prepared from plant extract. Moreover, several past studies have identified that concentrations of various nanoparticles in the range of 200–250 mg kg− 1 (equivalent to 250 µg mg− 1) are most effective for directly or indirectly controlling pathogens in various crops [32, 35, 38].

BNFs vs. precursor materials for suppressing bacterial wilt in tomato plants

Although the disease-suppressive potential of biogenic NPs, including chitosan, iron, and manganese, has been reported in different crops such as watermelon and rice [32, 35, 38], the precise roles of SiNPs and Si-based nanocomposites in controlling plant diseases are still elusive. Repeated disease assays were conducted to assess the effects of precursor materials (SiO2, MT, and SiNPs) and BNFs on plants infected with R. solanacearum to determine whether BNFs offer advantages over their precursors. The significant difference was observed between the plants treated with SiNPs and BNFs, as well as the plants treated with precursors materials have shown almost equal growth to healthy control, while plants infected with R. solanacearum without any treatments were severely infected after 3 dpi as shown in Fig. 2(a). The FW, DW and PL were enhanced to 34.1%, 40.36% and 12.63%, respectively, in plants treated with SiNPs under diseased condition, whereas the plants treated with BNFs have shown maximum increase in FW (55.53%), DW (59.63%) and PL (21.9%). The FW, DW and PL of R. solanacearum infected plants without any treatment was decreased to 32.95%, 34.40% and 19.44%, respectively, in comparison with control as described in Fig. 2(b-d). These findings are in line with Noman et al. [35], who described the impact of bulk manganese and bio-functionalized manganese NPs on FW, DW and PL under Fusarium wilt disease in watermelon. Moreover, in planta Si content was measured to testify that increase in growth parameters is due to Si content as the BNFs used in this study are majorly constituted on Si. The Si content was decreased upto 51.32% in diseased plants without any treatment and the plants treated with SiO2 had the higher Si content when compared to control and MT treatment, while the plants treated with SiNPs and BNFs have shown the maximum content of Si under R. solanacearum infection (Fig. 2(e)). Furthermore, relative bacterial biomass in plants was also significantly reduced with the application of SiNPs and it was found to be minimal in infected plants treated with BNFs (Fig. 2(f)). The disease score was also calculated by using a 5-point disease scale rating; these points were given by analyzing the morphology of plants as illustrated in Fig. 2(g). The disease score was also found to be minimum in plants infected with R. Solanacearum but treated with BNFs. Similar findings have been found in a study by Hafeez et al. [32], who reported chitosan NPs can increase the plant nutrient contents, improve plant growth and decrease the fungal biomass in plants. Conclusively, it could be stated that BNFs have improved the plant health and overall plant growth, as well as both treatments have helped the plants to fight with R. solanacearum infection. Therefore, further experiments about SiNPs and BNFs have been conducted to elucidate their mechanism of action in plants.

Real-time monitoring of R. solanacearum in tomato plants

To create a bioluminescence R. solanacearum strain, pBBR1MCS2-luxCDABE plasmid was introduced in the R. solanacearum strain and successful transformation was confirmed by analyzing the bacterial colonies under a photo camera in comparison with bacterial colonies without pBBR1MCS2-luxCDABE plasmid as shown in Fig. 3(a-b), same plates were also captured by digital camera as shown in supplementary Fig. 2 (a-b). Clear bioluminescent signals were observed in the modified bacterial strain under photon camera that have confirmed the successful transfer of pBBR1MCS2-luxCDABE plasmid into R. solanacearum strain. The use of luxCDABE operon to create bioluminescent bacterial strains has been well documented in the literature [70–73]. Moreover, an interesting phenomenon was observed during the in vitro antibacterial activity assessment (well-diffusion method) of SiNPs and BNFs: despite their significant reduction in the pathogenicity of R. solanacearum in tomato plants, SiNPs and BNFs showed no direct antibacterial activity in vitro, as illustrated in Fig. 3(c-d) and supplementary Fig. 2 (c-d). Furthermore, the MIC assay revealed no significant changes in the optical density (OD) of R. solanacearum after the application of SiNPs and BNFs, as shown in supplementary Fig. 2 (e-f). These findings suggest that SiNPs and BNFs do not kill the bacteria directly in vitro, but rather reduce pathogenicity in plants by increasing ROS scavenging activities, inducing SAR and activating phytohormone signaling. Similar findings have been also reported in the past, where Si-based nanomaterials have helped the plants to fight with pathogens, without killing the pathogens directly. For example, Du et al. [74] have suggested that silica based nanomaterials have helped the plants to reduce the pathogenicity of Magnaporthe oryzae, by inducing the salicylic acid signaling that can promote the systemic acquired resistance in plants. In another study, the SiO2 NPs have been found to confer SAR in Arabidopsis plants when infected with a bacterial pathogen [17]. Furthermore, the real-time monitoring of R. solanacearum in tomato plants at 3 dpi also represented a significant decrease in the bioluminescence signal after the application of BNFs in comparison with control and SiNPs as shown in Fig. 3(e-f). As R. solanacearum infection majorly impacts the shoots of the plants, bioluminescence signals have been only monitored in the stem of the tomato plants. These findings are similar with Xu et al. [33], who described the fluorescence signals become intensive as R. solanacearum infection become adverse in the tomato plants. These findings clearly suggest the decrease in R. solanacearum infection in the plants treated with BNFs. Therefore, to elucidate the mechanism of action of BNFs to reduce the pathogenicity of R. solanacearum, antioxidant activity and components involved in the SAR, as well as impacts of these amendments on soil microbial diversity have been investigated in the following sections.

SiNPs and BNFs induced SAR defense in tomato plants to suppress bacterial wilt

The SAR is a plant defense mechanism that provides broad-spectrum protection against a variety of pathogens. Upon applying chemical elicitors such as phytohormones and metabolites, SAR is triggered, inducing resistance not only in the infected tissue but also in the surrounding healthy tissue [75]. In the final experiment, the BNFs were used at the concentration of 250 µg mg− 1 as optimized in the pre-experiments. The SiNPs were also used in the final experiments as it has shown the best performance out of all precursors used in the biogenic synthesis of BNFs. The biogenic nanomaterials were applied 4 days before inoculation of the pathogen and phenotype of tomato plants after 3 dpi with R. solanacearum was observed under both healthy and diseased conditions as shown in Fig. 4(a). The FW (57.24%), DW (61.01%) and PL (25.51%) were increased significantly in healthy plants treated with BNFs respectively as compared to control and SiNPs, while in diseased plants treated with BNFs these parameters were found approximately equal to the control in contrast with the diseased plants without any treatment, where FW, DW and PL were significantly decreased (Fig. 4(b-d)). The increase in FW, DW, and PL with the application of silica based biogenic nanomaterials and phytohormones, such as MT have been widely reported in the literature [76–78]. Total chlorophyll content and greenness of the plants was also significantly increased with the application of BNFs in comparison with control and SiNPs as shown in supplementary Fig. 3 (a-b). Moreover, the Si content was measured in both healthy and diseased plants treated with BNFs, and it was found almost equal in all plants treated with biogenic nanomaterials, irrespective of the fact that plants are healthy or diseased, but it was significantly increased as compared to control. The silicon content increased in healthy and diseased plants treated with BNFs, while it significantly decreased in diseased plants without any treatment compared to the control group, but Si content was found maximum in plants treated with SiNPs (Fig. 4(e)). In previous studies, there has been extensive analysis about the enhanced levels of materials (comprising nanomaterials) in plants [31, 35, 79]. Furthermore, R. solanacearum has been reported to block the vascular system of plants, leading to wilting, yellowing of leaves, and eventually causing plant death. The R. solanacearum infection starts from the roots and severely impact the shoot of the plants because R. solanacearum preferentially colonize itself in the shoots [79]. Therefore, determination of in planta biomass of R. solanacearum is important to measure the severity of infection. The relative biomass of R. solanacearum was decreased up to 78.29% in plants treated with BNFs in comparison with the diseased plants with no treatment at 3 dpi as depicted in Fig. 4(f). These results are similar with the findings of Albalawi et al. [80], who reported the silica based nanoparticles can increase the disease resistance in eggplant against Alternaria solani responsible for causing early blight disease. Moreover, disease severity was phenotypically analyzed according to 5-scale rating and found to be reduced in plants treated with BNFs as shown in Fig. 4(g). An interesting observation has been made in the context of the tested biogenic nanomaterials and their influence on the pathogenicity of R. solanacearum to tomato plants. As discussed earlier, the in vitro application of BNFs at 250 µg mg− 1 concentration did not suppress the growth of R. solanacearum strain. Moreover, the lack of inhibition observed in this case seems to contradict the reduction in plant biomass and the level of disease severity observed after application of BNFs to infected plants. It is likely that, as a result of applied materials, Ralstonia’s pathogenic activity may have been affected by either enhanced antioxidant activity or systemic acquired resistance mechanisms via inducing SA signaling pathway within the plants, as well as biogenic nanomaterials can also reshape rhizospheric bacterial communities by enhancing the beneficial bacteria for plants that can leads to a more adverse competition for nutrients necessary pathogens to survive in soil environment. More detailed explanations regarding these effects are discussed in the following sections of the article.

SiNPs and BNFs reduced oxidative damage in tomato plants

Plants produce ROS molecules that contain oxygen and have a bit of a reactive nature under normal homeostasis, but if these molecules start accumulating in the plant’s cells, it can cause oxidative damage. This oxidative stress can lead to disruption of important processes like growth, development, and even the plant’s ability to defend itself against pests and diseases [81]. However, application of biogenic nanomaterials can help plants to combat with oxidative stress [82]. In the current study, the maximum decrease in O2•− (36.73% and 34.69%), H2O2 (40.20% and 34.02%) and MDA (67.53% and 58.44%) was observed in healthy and diseased plants, respectively at 250 µg mg− 1 BNFs as compared to non-treated controls (Fig. 5(c-d)). Similarly, Khan et al. [3] reported that Trichoderma coated SiNPs can reduce ROS accumulation induced by bacterial wilt disease and Cd stress in tomato plants. In another study, silica NPs have been reported to induce resistance to bacterial wilt disease in peanuts by modulating the ROS production [83]. Additionally, histochemical staining demonstrates the reduced level of ROS (O2•− and H2O2) in the leaves infected with R. solanacearum and treated with BNFs as compared to the infected control and infected plants treated with SiNPs (Fig. 5(a-b)). In response to oxidative stress, plants produce antioxidants (enzymatic and non-enzymatic compounds) that help plants to fight with damage caused by ROS and maintain the normal homeostasis of plants [84]. However, in case of pathogenic stress plants natural immune system is compromised and plant’s ability to produce antioxidants in response to oxidative stress which results in halted growth and eventually death of the plants [85, 86]. But, the application of nanomaterials can help plants to strengthen their immune mechanism against pathogen infestation [87–89]. In the current study, application of BNFs have significantly increased the content of SOD (31.81%), POD (32.9%), APX (47.37%) and CAT (32.65%) under diseased conditions in comparison with healthy control as shown in Fig. 6(b-e). The level of antioxidant was also increased in healthy and infected plants treated with SiNPs but, it was observed significantly less as compared to healthy and infected plants treated with BNFs. The increase in antioxidant levels in diseased plants after application of BNFs is due to higher oxidative stress and indicates that plant immune response has been strengthened. The relative expression of the genes (SlSOD, SlPOD3, SlCAT1 and SlAPX2) responsible to produce antioxidants was also significantly up-regulated after the application of BNFs as illustrated in the Fig. 6(a). Similarly, application of zinc oxide NPs was previously reported to increase the antioxidant profile in tomato plants [90]. The increase of antioxidant production decreases the ROS accumulation because of the ROS scavenging activity of antioxidants after application of BNFs during R. solanacearum infection in tomato plants; and that’s how plants maintain their regular homeostasis.

SiNPs and BNFs triggered phytohormone signaling and SAR-related genes

The Si-based biogenic nanomaterials have been widely reported to stimulate MT and SA signaling which is directly linked to enhance SAR in plants [91–94]. After the application of BNFs a significant increase in the accumulation of MT and SA was observed in the present study as shown in Fig. 6 (f-g). The MT and SA content was enhanced up to 31.28% and 18.28%, respectively, in plants infected with R. solanacearum and treated with BNFs as compared to healthy control. Furthermore, the relative expression of SAR-responsive genes was also enhanced with the application of BNFs in healthy and diseased plants in comparison with control and SiNPs as shown in Fig. 6(a). The RT-qPCR reaction was performed for SlPAL1, SlICS1, SlNPR1, SlEDS, SlPD4, and SlSARD1 tomato genes as these genes are key regulators of SAR in plants. These genes work together in tomato plants to develop resistance against pathogens, for example, SlPAL1 induce the synthesis of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) that is an enzyme involved in the first step of the phenylpropanoid pathway, which leads to the synthesis SA. PAL catalyzes the conversion of phenylalanine to trans-cinnamic acid, which is a precursor of defensive-responsive phytohormone, SA [95, 96]. The SlICS1 primarily plays a role in the biosynthesis of SA in plants [97], there is no direct evidence to suggest SlICS1 involvement in MT signaling, but it is important to note that MT and SA signaling pathways may interact with each other under certain conditions [98]. When MT and SA levels increase in response to pathogen attack, SlNPR1 is activated and produces non-expressor of pathogenesis-related proteins which are then translocated to the nucleus, where it induces the expression of defense-related genes. The SlNPR1 acts as a central player in coordinating MT and SA mediated defense responses [97, 99, 100]. The SlEDS induces the enhanced disease susceptibility pathway that interacts with phytohormone signaling in plant defense responses by producing ethylene which is known to interact with MT and SA in regulating plant defense. Ethylene often acts synergistically with other phytohormones to enhance defense responses against pathogens [101]. Pathogenesis-related protein 4 is a class of proteins induced by SA and involved in plant defense against pathogens, subsequently, SlPD4 likely plays a role in the downstream defense responses triggered by SA signaling [102]. The SlPD4 is also involved in the conversion of lycopene to β-carotene, which is a precursor in the melatonin biosynthetic pathway. The SlSARD1 regulates the expression of MT and SA responsive genes by binding to their promoters and is involved in modulating plant defense responses mediated by phytohormone signaling [103, 104]; possibly influencing how the plant responds to pathogenic threats. These genes collectively form a network of MT and SA signaling, allowing for the coordinated activation of defense responses against pathogens in tomato plants. Similarly, Ahmed et al. [34] have reported that the expression of SAR-related genes was enhanced by the application of nanocomposites in rice under leaf blight disease. Therefore, determining the expression of these genes is important to elucidate the role of BNFs in promoting SA signaling and triggering SAR during R. solanacearum infection in tomato plants.

SiNPs and BNFs reshaped bacterial diversity

The data from 16 S rRNA sequences from 18 tomato rhizosphere samples (i.e., 3 biological replicates for all treatments), producing 678,882 high-quality sequence reads for determining the impacts of BNFs on the spatial distribution of bacterial communities in the rhizosphere of tomato plants with or without R. solanacearum inoculation. It was investigated by analyzing the change in the relative abundance at both phylum and genus levels. At phylum, Proteobacteria (32.73%), Chloroflexi (13.67%) and Bacteroidota (8.34%) have been found to be the most dominant members in rhizosphere of healthy control. The R. solanacearum, pathogen used in this study also belongs to phylum Proteobacteria that’s why the relative abundance of phylum Proteobacteria (49.81%) was found at maximum in the rhizosphere of plants infected with R. solanacearum without any treatment. However, in the rhizosphere of tomato plants infected with R. solanacearum and treated with BNFs the relative abundance of the Proteobacteria was decreased as shown in Fig. 7(a), that might depict application of biogenic nanomaterials provides a feasible environment for the plant growth promoting rhizobacteria which leads to a pressure on pathogenic bacteria and halt their replication process. Moreover, heatmap analysis for the top 20 Phyla revealed that the relative abundance of Chloroflexi, Bacteroidota, Nitrospirota, Myxococcota, Planctomycetota, Gemmatimonadota, Acidobacteriota, Desulfobacterota, Elusimicrobiota, Bdellovibrionota and Armatimonadota have been increased in the rhizosphere of plants treated with BNFs as shown in supplementary Fig. 4 (a). These results are in-line with Liu et al. [23], who provided a novel evidence about bacterium Stenotrophomonas rhizophila that acts as an early warning system for plant defense, enhancing wheat growth and inducing disease resistance against Fusarium pseudograminearum by modulating the wheat-associated microbiome. At genus level, the analysis of relative bacterial abundance has clearly shown that R. solanacearum is dominant in diseased plants and its abundance was decreased with the application of BNFs (supplementary Fig. 4 (b)) showed that Flavobacterium, Lysobacter and Ralstonia were decreased, while Gemmatimonadaceae, Ramlibacter, Microscillaceae, S0134 terrestrial group, SBR1031, Anaerolineaceae, Chloroplast and Phormidium were increased in the rhizosphere of infected tomato plants treated with BNFs. Moreover, it has been widely reported in the literature that by modulating the plant growth promoting rhizobacteria can cause a serious threat to soil-borne pathogens [105–108]. Furthermore, the potential of biogenic nanomaterials in reshaping the soil microbial community have been also extensively discussed in the literature [32, 35, 38]. Conclusively, it can be stated that the application of BNFs in tomato plants has reshaped the rhizospheric bacterial community and helped the plant by reducing the R. solanacearum abundance in the rhizosphere of tomato plants.

Impact of SiNPs and BNFs on α and β diversity

The impacts of BNFs on bacterial community diversity in rhizospheric soil were evaluated by examining α-diversity through Ace, Chao1 and Shannon indices, and β-diversity by using PCoA analysis (Fig. 8). The Ace and Chao1 metrics analysis have revealed a significant difference in the all treatments and maximum α-diversity was identified in the rhizosphere of plants treated with BNFs and minimum α-diversity was found in the rhizosphere of plants infected with R. solanacearum without any treatment. The Shannon index have also revealed almost the same results, but it showed the no difference in the rhizospheric α-diversity of plants treated with SiNPs and BNFs irrespective of the fact that they are diseased or healthy. All three matrices analysis have clearly showed that the application of BNFs improves the α-diversity in rhizosphere of healthy and diseased plants, while the infected plants with no treatments of BNFs have shown the minimum α-diversity in comparison with the control (Fig. 8(a-c)). These findings are consistent with those of Hao et al. [109], who found that carbon-based nanomaterials change the structure of the microbial community inside rice plants. This is important because having a greater variety and number of beneficial bacteria around the plants can help in fighting with diseases [110]. Additionally, when the β-diversity of bacterial communities in the tomato rhizosphere using a method called unconstrained PCoA of Jacard metric were analyzed, it was found that there were distinct groupings of bacterial communities from the six different treatments. This suggests that BNFs plays an important role in shaping the bacterial communities of the tomato rhizosphere. The first two coordinate axes (PCoA1 and PCoA2) explained 52.9% and 14% of the total variation, respectively (Fig. 8(d)). The R2 value (0.6689) was calculated using PERMANOVA with the Jaccard metric, indicating that 66.89% of the variation in microbial community structure is found by the BNF treatments; the effect size was found to be large (Cohen’s f2 = 2.02), with a 95% confidence interval. Overall, these results indicate that soils treated with silicon BNFs showed a higher level of variation in bacterial communities compared to rhizospheric soils of control and SiNPs. The BNFs treatment improved the richness of specific bacterial communities in the rhizosphere of both healthy and diseased plants when compared to their respective untreated controls and SiNPs. These findings are in-line with the research conducted by Xiong et al. [111], who noted that changes in the diversity and abundance of bacterial communities play a significant role in fighting Fusarium wilt disease. These analyses of α-diversity and β-diversity indicate that the structure of beneficial bacterial communities in diseased tomato plants was significantly altered by BNFs as compared to control and SiNPs. Therefore, the suppression of bacterial wilt disease by application of biogenic nanomaterials leads to changes in the abundance and diversity of bacteria in diseased tomato plants. This change might help to maintain endophytic microbial populations similar to those found in healthy tomato plants that aren’t under stress of R. solanacearum.

SiNPs and BNFs modulated the taxonomic abundance

To identify the most common microbial taxa that could potentially act as biomarkers causing variations in the tomato rhizosphere among different treatments, LEfSe analysis was conducted for focusing on treatments with linear discriminant analysis (LDA) scores greater than 4.0 (Fig. 9). This analysis revealed significant differences in the microbial composition of the rhizosphere in healthy untreated tomato plants compared to those stressed by Ralstonia solanacearum infection. In healthy untreated tomato rhizosphere, the class Alphaproteobacteria (LDA 4.63), phylum Actinobacteria (LDA 4.53), order Rhizobiales (LDA 4.37), and 12 other bacterial taxa were significantly enriched. Conversely, in the rhizosphere of R. solanacearum-stressed plants, there was a higher abundance of the phylum Proteobacteria (LDA 5.09), class Gammaproteobacteria (LDA 5.05), genus Ralstonia (LDA 4.48), and seven other bacterial taxa. Additionally, in the rhizosphere of healthy tomato plants supplemented with SiNPs, class Anaerolineae (LDA 4.59), order SBR1031 (LDA 4.37), class S0134 terrestrial group (LDA 4.01), order Cytophagales (LDA 4.00), and two other unclassified taxa were found. However, in the rhizosphere of R. solanacearum-infected plants treated with SiNPs, only two taxa were abundant, including genus Ramlibacter (LDA 4.22) and phylum Firmicutes (LDA 4.00). Similarly, in the rhizosphere of healthy plants treated with BNFs, only three taxa were abundant, including genus Piscinibacter (LDA 4.15), family Comamonadaceae (LDA 4.69), and order Burkholderiales (LDA 4.83). In contrast, in the rhizosphere of R. solanacearum-infected plants treated with BNFs, four taxa were abundant, including phylum Myxococcota (LDA 4.077), family Pseudomonadaceae (LDA 4.58), genus Pseudomonas (LDA 4.58), and order Pseudomonadales (LDA 4.62). The results are consistent with the findings of Sinong et al. [112], who conducted a similar study but in rice plant for comparing the root microbial community using Lefse analysis. In their study, diseased rice plants treated with nanomaterials showed an increased abundance of Rhodocyclaceae, Bacterium_Ellin5237, Deltaproteobacteria, and Candidatus Accumulibacter. Conversely, healthy rice plants treated with nanomaterials exhibited an enrichment of Massilia. Similarly, Wang et al. [113] observed changes in the soil microbial community composition due to the planting of transgenic rice using Lefse analysis. Conclusively, the LEfSe analysis revealed significant changes in the microbial community of healthy and diseased tomato rhizospheres with or without treatments of BNFs, suggesting that BNFs amendments maintain the bacterial community composition of the tomato rhizosphere under pathogen attack, thereby increasing the abundance of specific microbial species. In the present study, we did not directly assess the impact of BNFs on the composition of the bacterial community. However, we hypothesize that application of BNFs triggered physiological, biochemical, and genetic changes in both healthy and diseased tomato plants. These changes may have promoted the proliferation of phyto-beneficial bacteria, particularly those involved in disease suppression, while concurrently reducing the abundance of neutral or harmful bacteria. Thus, further research is needed to investigate these processes and microbial interactions, such as quorum sensing, to fully elucidate the mechanisms by which BNFs modulate the rhizosphere community.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the multifaceted benefits of MT-coated BNFs in mitigating bacterial wilt disease caused by R. solanacearum in tomato plants. Soil application of BNFs at 250 µg mg− 1 significantly improved plant growth parameters, increased silicon uptake, reduced bacterial biomass, and mitigated disease severity compared to untreated infected plants. Moreover, exposure to BNFs induced SAR and stimulated plant immunity by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity, increasing SA accumulation, and upregulating defense-related genes. High-throughput sequencing revealed notable changes in the rhizosphere microbiome composition, which not only suppressed R. solanacearum abundance but also enriched beneficial bacterial communities. These findings underscore the potential of BNFs as multifunctional eco-friendly agent; capable of boosting plant defenses through diverse mechanisms. The considerable variation observed in soil microbial communities provides a foundation for further research into the mining and utilization of beneficial microbial strains. Importantly, the metagenomics analysis highlighted the significant alterations in microbial community structure and function resulting from BNFs treatment, suggesting enhanced interactions with plant growth-promoting bacteria that could improve agricultural outcomes. This novel approach holds significant implications for sustainable agriculture, offering innovative strategies for disease management and ensuring the safe production of tomato plants. Future research should explore the broader applications of these BNFs against other crop diseases and pathogens, as well as investigate their long-term effects on soil health and microbial dynamics.
