Comparative microbial and metabolite profiles of air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky under identical natural drying conditions
Authors: Jiachong Zhang, Jindi Wu, Yanan Xia, Xujin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobial communities are central to flavor development in air-dried meats; however, whether similar microbial assemblages produce comparable metabolic outputs across different raw substrates under identical natural conditions remains unresolved. We hypothesized that beef and mutton matrices would differentially reorganize microbial–metabolite interaction networks despite environmental consistency. Air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky produced under identical natural conditions in Xilingol League, Inner Mongolia, were analyzed using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing combined with derivatization-based GC–MS metabolomics. Both products exhibited highly similar community structures dominated by Lactobacillus (17–19%) and Halomonas (13–16%), yet substrate-associated enrichment was observed, with Staphylococcus prevailing in air-dried beef jerky and Ruminococcus and Enterobacter enriched in air-dried mutton jerky. Metabolomic profiling demonstrated clear interspecies separation, with 404 of 838 shared metabolites differing significantly (VIP > 1, P < 0.05), mainly involving amino acids, organic acids, and lipid-related compounds. Correlation analysis based on centered log-ratio transformation and Spearman's rank tests with FDR correction revealed structured positive–negative complementary association patterns among dominant genera. Genera such as Halomonas and Kocuria were predominantly positively associated with core metabolites, whereas Ruminococcus, Serratia, and Clostridia_UCG-014 exhibited opposing negative associations, forming coordinated metabolic complementarity networks. The specific metabolites involved differed between Air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky, indicating substrate-dependent reorganization of microbial–metabolic linkages. These findings demonstrate that raw material composition reshapes metabolic outcomes despite similar microbial frameworks, providing mechanistic insight into species-specific flavor formation in traditional air-dried meats.Keywords: Substrate-driven microbial assembly, Substrate-dependent metabolism, Microbial–metabolite interactions, Flavor metabolism, 16S rRNA gene sequencing

Introduction

Air-dried fermented meat products are widely consumed in regions such as Inner Mongolia, South America, and Europe, where they are valued for their extended shelf life and distinctive sensory properties formed through natural drying and spontaneous fermentation (Niu et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2024c; Zhang et al., 2017). In Western countries, traditional products such as Italian bresaola and other dry-salted meats have also been studied for their nutritional composition, aroma characteristics, and processing-related biochemical changes (Lopez et al., 2024; Rocchetti et al., 2022). Studies on dry fermented sausages demonstrate how lipolysis and proteolysis contribute to volatile compound development, highlighting the combined roles of substrate composition and microbial or chemical processes in flavor formation (Almeida et al., 2024; Bogdanović et al., 2023). During this process, complex microbial consortia colonize the meat matrix and drive a series of biochemical transformations, generating diverse volatile and non-volatile compounds that directly influence product flavor and quality (Hu et al., 2022) Consequently, increasing attention has been directed toward understanding how microbial communities and substrate composition jointly influence flavor formation in fermented meat systems (Zhou et al., 2022; Rocchetti et al., 2022)). Fermented sausages from pork, beef, and mutton exhibit distinct volatile profiles and sensory characteristics, reflecting differences in raw material composition and substrate-dependent metabolic conversions during fermentation (Xie et al., 2025).

The microbial ecosystem of air-dried meats is dynamic and compositionally heterogeneous, shaped by intrinsic factors such as meat type and physicochemical properties, and extrinsic factors including environmental conditions and artisanal practices (Zuo et al., 2023). Metagenomic analyses have revealed diverse bacterial and fungal communities in cured meat systems, with Proteobacteria—reaching approximately 78% relative abundance in certain bovine-derived products—frequently reported as dominant members (Sun et al., 2022b; Zuo et al., 2023). Metabolic networks play a central role in substrate transformation, as proteolytic and lipolytic activities release amino acids and free fatty acids, providing precursors for downstream flavor synthesis (Liu et al., 2023a). Because protein and lipid composition differs among species, substrate availability may vary, potentially leading to distinct microbial metabolic outputs and flavor characteristics (Mu et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024c).

Distinguishing the influence of meat species from environmental and processing factors remains challenging (Liu et al., 2022b). Muscle from different species exhibits inherent physicochemical differences, including lipid composition, fatty acid distribution, free amino acid content, and oxidative stability, which may influence microbial metabolism and volatile compound formation. Recent multi-omics studies (e.g., Wang et al., 2021b; Jiang et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2024b) characterized microbial communities and flavor-related compounds, but often focused on single meat types or geographically distinct production systems. In many cases, beef, mutton, or yak-derived products originated from independent production units with heterogeneous salt usage, drying regimes, storage conditions, and microclimatic environments. Such variability makes it difficult to disentangle the respective contributions of animal species, processing practices, and environmental factors. Consequently, the specific effect of raw material composition per se on microbial–metabolic differentiation remains insufficiently resolved.

Comparative analyses across animal species have therefore emerged as an effective strategy for elucidating both conserved and differential microbial–metabolic mechanisms underlying flavor formation in fermented meat systems. Previous studies have reported that fermented meat products from different animal species may exhibit distinct volatile profiles and characteristic biomarkers (Liu et al., 2022b; Man et al., 2023). Few studies have implemented a paired sampling framework, where beef and mutton are processed concurrently within the same production units under identical natural drying conditions.

Accordingly, this study selected air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky produced under identical natural drying conditions in Xilingol, Inner Mongolia, as research subjects (Fig. 1). The objective was to compare bacterial community composition and flavor-related metabolite profiles using 16S rRNA gene sequencing combined with derivatization-based flavor metabolomics analysis. By integrating microbial community analysis with metabolite profiling under a paired production design, this study aims to clarify associations between dominant bacterial taxa and characteristic flavor-related metabolites, elucidating the microbial basis of species-dependent flavor differentiation in air-dried fermented meats.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and study design

A paired sampling design was adopted in this study, with the production household defined as the biological replicate (n = 3). Each household produced both air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky and was therefore treated as the experimental unit. Samples were obtained from three independent artisanal households located in Sunit Left Banner, Xilingol League, Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region. These households follow traditional local practices for producing air-dried meat products under natural ambient conditions. Meat strips were hung in naturally ventilated environments and dried without artificial heating, smoking, starter culture inoculation, or humidity control. Within each household, one air-dried beef jerky sample (Sunit Yellow Cattle, slaughter age 18–24 months) and one air-dried mutton jerky sample (Sunit Sheep, slaughter age 6–12 months) were collected aseptically, forming paired air-dried beef jerky–air-dried mutton jerky sample sets.

According to field investigation and communication with producers, air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky within each household were processed using comparable traditional procedures, including similar salt usage levels, cutting size, drying duration, and storage conditions. Therefore, animal species was considered the main factor examined in this study.

Sampling was conducted on two closely spaced occasions under consistent production practices to reduce variation associated with a single batch. Two independent production batches from each household were therefore collected. Each sampling occasion yielded three paired sample sets, resulting in twelve physical samples in total (six air-dried beef jerky and six air-dried mutton jerky samples). Samples from the same household were regarded as temporal repeats, while biological replication was defined at the household level (n = 3). For microbial community analysis, the two batches from each household and meat type were homogenized prior to DNA extraction to obtain a representative microbial community profile for that production unit. In contrast, flavoromics analyses were conducted on individual batch samples to retain sample-level variability in metabolite profiling. For statistical analysis, measurements were averaged at the household level so that the effective biological replication corresponded to the three independent production households.

To ensure traceability, a structured coding system was applied. Beef samples were labeled JB (jerky beef) and mutton samples JM (jerky mutton). The first digit denotes the sampling occasion (1 or 2), and the second digit represents the producer (1–3), yielding air-dried beef jerky samples JB1–1, JB1–2, JB1–3, JB2–1, JB2–2, JB2–3 and corresponding air-dried mutton jerky samples JM1–1, JM1–2, JM1–3, JM2–1, JM2–2, JM2–3.

All sampling procedures were performed under aseptic conditions. The outer surface of each product was trimmed to remove potential contamination, and approximately 50 g of internal tissue was excised, transferred into sterile sampling bags, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen on-site, and stored at −80 °C until analysis.

Microbial community profiling

Genomic DNA was extracted from homogenized meat samples using the Mag-Bind Soil DNA Kit (Omega Bio-tek, Inc., Norcross, GA, USA). Approximately 0.25 g of each homogenized sample was used for DNA extraction according to the manufacturer's protocol. DNA concentration was initially quantified using a Nanodrop NC2000 UV–Vis Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and further quantified using a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). DNA purity and integrity were assessed by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis (Agarose, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Nucleic Acid Marker DL15000, Takara, Kusatsu, Japan; TAE buffer (AM9870), Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) using a DYY-6C electrophoresis system (Beijing Liuyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) and visualized with a BG-gdsAUTO (130) gel imaging system (Beijing Baygene Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Extraction negative controls and PCR blank controls were included to monitor potential contamination during DNA extraction and amplification.

PCR amplification was performed using diluted genomic DNA as template with barcode-tagged primers targeting the V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene (341F/806R). Each 30 μL reaction contained 15 μL Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (2×, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), 3 μL primer (2 μM), 10 μL genomic DNA (1 ng/μL), and 2 μL sterile water. Thermal cycling conditions were 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, 50 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. PCR was conducted on a Bio-Rad T100 thermal cycler (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA). Amplicons (400–450 bp) were verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis (Agarose, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA; Nucleic Acid Marker DL15000, Takara, Kusatsu, Japan; TAE buffer (AM9870), Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), excised, and purified using the Qiagen Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). Libraries were constructed using the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), quantified with Qubit®, and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (PE250) (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA).

Raw reads were demultiplexed according to barcode sequences and saved in FASTQ format. Adapter and primer sequences were first trimmed using cutadapt, and paired-end reads were then merged using FLASH v1.2.11. Quality filtering, denoising, chimera removal, and error correction were performed using the DADA2 algorithm implemented in QIIME2 version 2023.9. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated using the DADA2 v1.28.0 algorithm implemented in QIIME2 version 2023.9 (https://qiime2.org/). Sequences with extremely low abundance (total abundance <10 across all samples) were removed to reduce potential noise. Taxonomic annotation was performed using the QIIME2 classify-sklearn plugin with a pre-trained Naive Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA 138.1 SSURef NR99 database (https://www.arb-silva.de/), with a confidence threshold of 0.7. Sequencing depth ranged from 53,000 to 72,000 reads per sample after quality control. Based on rarefaction curve analysis, samples were rarefied to 10,000 reads prior to downstream diversity analyses.

Alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Observed ASVs, Shannon, and Simpson) were calculated, and rarefaction curves were generated. Taxonomic composition at the phylum and genus levels was visualized using bar plots. Beta diversity was analyzed using PCoA and UPGMA clustering based on Bray–Curtis distances, and group differences were assessed using ANOSIM. Shared and unique ASVs were displayed using Venn diagrams. Differential taxa were identified using LEfSe v1.0 (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/lefse/) (Wang et al., 2024a). Potential contaminant sequences were evaluated by comparing negative control samples during the quality control process, and no dominant contaminant taxa were detected.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis

Flavor-related metabolites were analyzed using solvent extraction followed by methoximation and silylation derivatization coupled with gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Approximately 50 ± 1 mg of homogenized meat was transferred into a 2 mL microcentrifuge tube, and 500 μL of pre-cooled extraction solvent (methanol/chloroform, 3:1, v/v) containing adonitol (≥99%, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) as an internal standard was added. Methanol (HPLC grade, CNW Technologies GmbH, Düsseldorf, Germany) and chloroform (HPLC grade, Adamas Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were used as extraction solvents. Samples were vortexed for 30 s, followed by bead-assisted homogenization using a JXFSTPRP-24 tissue grinder (Shanghai Jingxin Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 40 Hz for 4 min. Ultrasonic extraction was then performed for 5 min in an ice-water bath using an YM-080S ultrasonic cleaner (Shenzhen Fangao Microelectronics Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). The homogenization and ultrasonic extraction steps were repeated three times to ensure efficient metabolite extraction.

Extracts were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm (13,800 ×g) for 15 min at 4 °C using a Heraeus Fresco17 centrifuge (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). Subsequently, 200 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a new tube and evaporated to dryness using a vacuum concentrator (LNG-T98, Taicang Huamei Biochemical Instrument Factory, Taicang, China). For quality control, 100 μL aliquots from each extract were pooled to generate a composite QC sample.

Derivatization was performed by adding 60 μL methoxyamine hydrochloride solution (20 mg mL−1 in pyridine) and incubating at 80 °C for 30 min. Methoxyamine hydrochloride (AR grade, TCI Chemicals (Tokyo) Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and pyridine (HPLC grade, Adamas Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) were used for the methoximation reaction. Subsequently, 80 μL BSTFA reagent (with 1% TMCS, v/v, REGIS Technologies, Inc., Morton Grove, IL, USA) was added and samples were incubated at 70 °C for 1.5 h for silylation. After cooling, 5 μL of a fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) mixture (Dr. Ehrenstorfer GmbH, Augsburg, Germany) dissolved in chloroform was added to the QC sample for retention index calibration.

GC–MS analysis was carried out using a GC-2030 gas chromatograph coupled with a QP2020 NX mass spectrometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 250 μm × 0.25 μm, Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). A 1 μL aliquot of derivatized sample was injected in splitless mode with the injector temperature set to 280 °C. Helium was used as the carrier gas at 1 mL min−1, with a septum purge flow of 3 mL min−1. The oven temperature program was 50 °C for 1 min, increased to 310 °C at 8 °C min−1, and held for 11.5 min. Transfer line and ion source temperatures were 280 °C and 200 °C, respectively. Electron ionization was performed at 70 eV, and spectra were acquired in full-scan mode (m/z 50–500) at 12.5 spectra s−1 with a 7.2 min solvent delay. Selected samples were analyzed in technical duplicate to evaluate analytical repeatability.

Pooled QC samples were injected periodically (approximately every 4–5 samples) and at both the beginning and end of the analytical sequence to monitor instrument stability. Features showing RSD > 30% in QC samples or detected in fewer than 50% of QC injections were removed prior to statistical analysis. Blank samples were also analyzed to evaluate potential carry-over (Dunn et al., 2011).

Raw data were processed using ChromaTOF software (version 4.3×, LECO Corporation, St. Joseph, MI, USA) for peak extraction, baseline correction, deconvolution, alignment, and integration (Kind et al., 2009). Metabolite annotation was performed by matching mass spectra and retention indices against the LECO-Fiehn Rtx5 database, with retention indices calculated using a C8–C30 FAME standard series.

Remaining missing values were imputed using half of the minimum detected value, and the dataset was normalized to the internal standard and log-transformed prior to multivariate statistical analysis. Differential metabolites between groups were subsequently screened using orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), and compounds satisfying VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. To control for multiple testing, p-values were further adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method to obtain Q-values.

The method for analyzing the correlation between dominant bacterial genera and core metabolites

Key bacterial genera were identified using LEfSe analyses as described in Section 2.2, and core flavor metabolites were selected based on GC–MS results and the screening criteria described in Section 2.3 (VIP > 1, p < 0.05). Because microbial relative abundance data are compositional, centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation was applied prior to correlation analysis. Normality of the variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Because most variables did not follow a normal distribution, Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to evaluate associations between bacterial genera and metabolites. P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method, and FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Correlation analysis and heatmap visualization were performed using the correlation module of Wekemo Bioincloud (version 2024; Gao et al., 2024). Correlations were calculated separately within each group (air-dried beef jerky or air-dried mutton jerky) using household-level averaged values to maintain consistency with the paired sampling design.

Statistical analysis of data

Experimental data were processed using Microsoft Excel, SPSS 27, and Origin 2018. Figures were generated using Origin 2018. Microsoft Excel was used for data organization and preprocessing of 16S rRNA gene sequencing and derivatized flavor metabolomics datasets, including data screening, normalization, and calculation of household-level averaged values. Production households were treated as the biological replicates for statistical analysis. For metabolomic data, measurements from the two batches within each household were averaged prior to statistical testing. SPSS 27 was used for inferential statistical analyses. Given the paired sampling design, household-level averages of air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky were compared using paired-sample t-tests, with production household treated as the pairing factor. Differences between air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05. All results are presented as mean ± standard error (SE), calculated from independent production households.

Results and discussion

Bacterial community composition in air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky

High-throughput sequencing of six air-dried meat specimens through the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (paired-end 250 bp, PE250) generated 646,829 quality-filtered sequences (average: 107,804 ± 5943 sequences/sample; read length: 227 bp). Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were generated using QIIME2 based on the DADA2 algorithm, and a total of 12,588 ASVs were obtained following rarefaction to the minimum sequencing depth. Alpha diversity analysis of bacterial communities across samples is presented in Fig. S1. The observed ASVs curve showed pronounced fluctuations at low sequencing depths, followed by gradual stabilization as sequencing depth increased. Although the observed ASVs continued to increase gradually with sequencing depth, indicating that rare taxa were still being detected, the Shannon diversity curve reached a plateau at approximately 10,000 reads, suggesting that further sequencing depth mainly improved abundance estimation rather than revealing additional novel taxa. Accordingly, 10,000 reads were selected as the rarefaction depth, as this threshold provided stable diversity estimates while retaining all samples for downstream comparative analyses. Goods_coverage values exceeded 0.996 for all samples, indicating high sequencing completeness. This sequencing coverage was considered sufficient for comparative ecological analyses focusing on dominant and moderately abundant taxa without substantially affecting ecological interpretation.

Alpha diversity served as a key metric for assessing microbial richness and diversity. As shown in Table S1, Goods_coverage values exceeding 0.9965 were recorded for both samples, confirming adequate sequencing depth for accurate microbial community representation. Higher Observed Species, ACE, and Chao1 indices were noted in the JB group compared to the JM group, though without statistical significance (P > 0.05), indicating comparable species richness. Similarly, marginally elevated Shannon and Simpson indices were observed for the JB group (P > 0.05), demonstrating equivalent species evenness and diversity. In summary, while the JB group exhibited marginally superior richness and diversity metrics, these differences were not statistically significant. No significant differences in alpha diversity indices were observed between JB and JM groups (P > 0.05). These metrics describe the overall community diversity in the two products.

At the phylum level (Fig. 2A), Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota, and Actinobacteriota collectively accounted for 97.32 ± 0.28% of the total microbial community in air-dried beef jerky (JB). Similarly, in air-dried mutton jerky (JM), Firmicutes, Proteobacteria, Bacteroidota, Actinobacteriota, and Verrucomicrobiota together represented 98.03 ± 0.12% of the microbial community. In both products, Firmicutes and Proteobacteria were the predominant phyla. These results are largely consistent with the review by Wang et al. (2021e), who reported Firmicutes and Proteobacteria as the dominant phyla commonly detected in dried and fermented meat products. However, unlike the samples summarized by Wang et al. (2021a), which were mainly derived from cured and ham products in southern China, the samples in the present study originated from naturally air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky produced in the Xilingol region of Inner Mongolia, where both climatic conditions and raw materials differ substantially. Therefore, the present findings are consistent with previous observations of Firmicutes and Proteobacteria predominance and provide additional data for air-dried meats produced under northern arid climatic conditions, although broader generalization beyond the sampled households should be made cautiously.

At the genus level (Fig. 2B), Lactobacillus, Halomonas, Psychrobacter, and Kocuria were identified as the top five dominant genera in air-dried beef jerky (JB), together accounting for 60.69 ± 0.11% of the total microbial community. Similarly, these genera also constituted the five most abundant genera in air-dried mutton jerky (JM), representing 59.61 ± 0.08% of the microbial community. In both products, Lactobacillus was the most abundant genus, with relative abundances of 19.18 ± 0.03% in JB and 19.36 ± 0.03% in JM. These observations are consistent with previous metagenomic evidence (Wang et al., 2021c; Bogdanović et al., 2023)), which demonstrated the ecological dominance of Lactobacillus in fermented meat ecosystems. This dominance is commonly attributed to ecological traits such as acid tolerance and competitive persistence reported in fermented meat systems, although such traits were not experimentally verified in the present study.

Sample-specific profiling (Fig. 2C-D) demonstrated phylum-level conservation across all specimens, with Firmicutes (0.41–0.44) and Proteobacteria (0.36–0.39) exhibiting narrow abundance ranges, suggesting relatively stable representation across the sampled households. Interspecies comparison at the household level showed that JM samples exhibited approximately 21.7% higher relative abundance of Firmicutes and 34.2% lower Actinobacteriota compared with JB (P < 0.05, Welch's t-test with BH correction). Although statistically significant, these differences should be interpreted cautiously given the limited number of independent households (n = 3). Notably, JM1–3 exhibited a relatively higher Verrucomicrobiota abundance (6.8 ± 0.4%) compared with other samples. Because statistical inference was performed at the household level (n = 3), this observation represents sample-level variation rather than a generalized interspecies difference, which may be related to differences in raw material characteristics and processing-related variability among samples, such as moisture distribution or salt diffusion, factors that can influence microbial growth during air-drying (Tang et al., 2020). Similar sample-dependent variations are commonly observed in spontaneously fermented meat products (Barbier et al., 2021). These observations align with metagenomic surveys by Zuo et al. (2023), who identified Proteobacteria and Firmicutes as ubiquitous core phyla in fermented meat matrices. However, the present results describe compositional patterns only, and no functional capacity was directly assessed.

The JM group showed a 39.4% reduction in Kocuria relative abundance compared with JB (P < 0.05, Welch's t-test with BH correction). This difference may reflect substrate-associated microbial selection rather than environmental effects. Given that both air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky were collected from the same production site and processed under identical air-drying conditions, large-scale environmental variation is less likely to account for this difference. Instead, the reduced abundance of Kocuria in JM is more plausibly explained by substrate-driven microbial selection associated with intrinsic differences in raw material composition between beef and mutton, including protein structure, collagen relative level, and lipid profiles. These compositional differences may alter nutrient availability and interspecies competition within the microbial community, thereby constraining the ecological niche of Kocuria in the ovine matrix while favoring other genera, such as Ruminococcus and Serratia, which were concurrently enriched in JM samples. Within the present dataset, this pattern supports a substrate-associated explanation, although confirmation in larger and more controlled studies is required.

In contrast, Serratia and Ruminococcus were enriched in JM, with relative abundances approximately 1.1-fold higher than in JB (P < 0.05, Welch's t-test with Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction), indicating substrate-associated compositional variation. At the genus level, Lactobacillus remained the most abundant taxon across all specimens (relative abundance: 0.17–0.21), followed by Halomonas (0.13–0.16) and Psychrobacter (0.13–0.15). This finding aligns with previous investigations of dry-cured and air-dried meats, where lactic acid bacteria were shown to stabilize the microbial ecosystem and enhance flavor profiles (Cheng et al., 2024). Consistent with group-level trends, JM specimens demonstrated 39.4% reduction in Kocuria abundance (P < 0.05, Welch's t-test with BH correction) alongside 1.08−/1.12-fold up-regulation of Serratia/Ruminococcus (P < 0.05, Welch's t-test with BH correction) relative to JB controls. Notably, JM1–3 displayed exceptional suppression of Rikenella (0.02 ± 0.01% vs 0.15–0.23% in other samples, P < 0.05), suggesting localized microenvironmental constraints – that may be influenced by nutrient competition or metabolite accumulation, although these mechanisms were not directly assessed. The phylogenetic plasticity observed between JB/JM systems suggests that raw material composition may contribute to differences in microbial succession during air-drying fermentation, but broader ecological generalization should be made with caution given the limited sample size.

Comparison of bacterial communities and differential genera between air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky

To further evaluate differences in overall community structure between air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky samples beyond individual genus comparisons, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was conducted based on the Bray–Curtis distance matrix derived from the bacterial composition data. The PCoA results (Fig. 3A) showed a non-significant separation trend between bovine (JB) and ovine (JM) samples, consistent with the ANOSIM results (R = 0, P = 0.6). The first two principal coordinates explained 53.12% of the total variation (PC1: 27.75%, PC2: 25.37%). JB samples were primarily distributed in Quadrant I, whereas JM samples were mainly located in Quadrant IV, with partial overlap between groups. For example, JB1–2 was positioned close to JM samples, while JM1–3 showed a relatively distinct distribution pattern.

Hierarchical clustering analysis based on Bray–Curtis distances (Fig. 3B) revealed that JM1–1 and JM1–2 clustered closely, with JB1–2 positioned nearby. JB1–1 and JB1–3 formed another cluster, while JM1–3 appeared as a relatively separate branch, indicating certain compositional variation among samples. However, ANOSIM results (Fig. 3C) indicated no significant differences between groups (R = 0, P = 0.6), suggesting comparable levels of within- and between-group variation.Overall, the ordination and clustering analyses indicate that while minor distribution patterns were observed, there was no clear group-level separation between air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky in terms of overall bacterial community structure.

Comparative Venn analysis (Fig. 3D) identified 354 shared genera between bovine (JB) and ovine (JM) systems, with 123 JB-unique and 102 JM-specific taxa, suggesting slightly higher unique richness in JB. Substrate-associated differences were observed: JB communities harbored uncultured Bacteroidales (28%), Dactylosporangium (28%), and SWB02 (17%), whereas JM ecosystems favored Shuttleworthia (26%), Arthrobacter (25%), and uncultured Clostridiales (21%). Core microbiome analysis highlighted Lactobacillus (19%), Halomonas (15%), and Psychrobacter (15%) as universal functional hubs across both substrates. Compared with findings in French commercial dry-aged beef reported by Coton et al. (2024), where Pseudomonas spp. predominated and Lactobacillus accounted for approximately 2%, the present samples exhibited a markedly higher relative abundance of Lactobacillus, possibly reflecting differences in climatic conditions and processing practices.

Differential abundance profiling (Fig. 3E) suggested several genera with relatively different abundance patterns between groups, including higher relative abundance of Ruminococcus, Leuconostoc, and Marinilactibacillus in JM, whereas JB showed relatively higher representation of Kocuria, CHKCI002, and Escherichia/Shigella. Differential abundance analysis was conducted using LEfSe and STAMP (Welch's t-test with Benjamini–Hochberg correction) as described in Section 2. However, considering the small sample size (n = 3 per group) and the compositional nature of 16S rRNA sequencing data, the statistical power of these approaches may be limited, and the potential risk of false positives cannot be excluded. Accordingly, the reported differential genera should be regarded as exploratory indicators of potential substrate-associated trends rather than statistically definitive biomarkers. These observations converged with global fermented meat studies – aligning with Lactobacillus dominance (91.0%) in aged beef (Zheng et al., 2023) yet diverging from irradiation-free shredded beef dominated by Enterobacter (25.94%) and Staphylococcus (17.28%) (Xu et al., 2024). Notably, JM's apparent enrichment of Serratia and Colidextribacter contrasted sharply with Pseudomonas/Arcobacter prevalence in Tibetan yak jerky (Zhang et al., 2024a) and Haemophilus dominance in mutton sausages (Niu et al., 2024), collectively demonstrating the profound modulation of microbial consortia by substrate biochemistry, geoclimactic parameters, processing protocols, and cultural traditions.

The observed interspecies differences—particularly the relatively higher Ruminococcus abundance and lower Kocuria proportion in JM—may be associated with substrate-specific nutrient composition, including differences in protein structure, collagen content, and lipid profile between beef and mutton (Glover et al., 2022). Collagenase (EC 3.4.24.3) was also detected in this study, suggesting potential microbial involvement in collagen degradation. Such substrate-related metabolic differences may influence microbial succession during air-drying. Overall, these findings indicate that raw material characteristics may contribute to shaping microbial community structure in traditional air-dried meats.

Comparative analysis of untargeted metabolites in air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky

A comprehensive GC–MS analysis was performed on twelve air-dried meat samples. After peak deconvolution and annotation, 838 metabolic features were obtained. In air-dried beef jerky (JB), a total of 309 volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were identified and classified into 11 chemical categories (Fig. 4A). Among these, organic acids and their derivatives constituted the predominant class (24.92%, 77 compounds), followed by lipids and lipid-like molecules (20.39%, 63 compounds), other metabolites (19.09%, 59 compounds), organic oxygen-containing compounds (17.48%, 54 compounds), organic heterocyclic compounds (7.44%, 23 compounds), benzenoid compounds (3.88%, 12 compounds), and nucleosides/nucleotides and their analogues (3.88%, 12 compounds). In air-dried mutton jerky (JM), 269 volatile organic compounds were identified and likewise categorized into 11 chemical classes (Fig. 4B). Organic acids and their derivatives also represented the largest group (26.02%, 70 compounds), followed by lipids and lipid-like molecules (21.93%, 59 compounds), other metabolites (19.33%, 52 compounds), organic oxygen-containing compounds (15.61%, 42 compounds), organic heterocyclic compounds (5.95%, 16 compounds), benzenoid compounds (4.09%, 11 compounds), and nucleosides/nucleotides and their analogues (4.09%, 11 compounds). The relatively high proportions of organic acids and their derivatives and lipids and lipid-like molecules observed in both air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky may be associated with microbial metabolism and lipid oxidative degradation during the air-drying process; however, given the limited biological replication (n = 3 at the household level), these interpretations should be considered preliminary and interpreted with caution.

Specifically, the accumulation of organic acids may reflect microbial fermentation of carbohydrates, whereas lipids and lipid-like molecules may be related to lipid hydrolysis and oxidative reactions (Li et al., 2025). Collectively, these compounds are likely involved in flavor development in air-dried meats, and differences in their relative levels may partially contribute to the distinct flavor profiles observed between air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky, although broader validation is required. Previous studies analyzing volatile compounds in fresh beef or raw muscle tissues using HS-SPME-GC–MS approaches have typically identified dozens of VOCs, with lipid-derived volatiles frequently reported as dominant components (Sun et al., 2022a; Bhadury et al., 2021). These studies provide a general reference for volatile detection in meat matrices. However, the present study focuses on naturally air-dried meat products, and no direct comparison in volatile complexity between fresh and air-dried meats is intended.

As shown in Fig.4C–D, the predominant metabolites in air-dried beef jerky (JB) included 2-deoxy-D-arabitol, leucine, oxoproline, hypoxanthine 1, valine, D-talose 1, isoleucine, fructose 1, succinic acid, and fructose 2. These compounds encompass organic acids and derivatives, amino acids and their derivatives, organic oxygen-containing compounds, and nucleosides/nucleotides and their analogues. Collectively, these metabolites represent major detected components and may be associated with flavor development and quality attributes in air-dried beef jerky. Functionally, these metabolites serve both as critical flavor precursors and as direct contributors to sensory characteristics. The branched-chain amino acids (leucine, valine, and isoleucine) were present at relatively high levels, indicating substantial protein degradation during the air-drying process. This observation suggests active proteolysis during processing, although quantitative validation in larger sample sets would be necessary to confirm this trend. These compounds not only influence taste perception but also act as key precursors for volatile flavor compounds, including branched-chain aldehydes, alcohols, and acids, which closely correspond with the enrichment patterns of lipids and oxygen-containing volatiles in JB (Chen et al., 2024).

Sugars such as fructose and 2-deoxy-D-arabitol provide substrates for non-enzymatic browning reactions. Although these compounds exhibit low intrinsic volatility, they can participate indirectly in the generation of sweet and roasted aroma notes via Maillard and oxidative reactions during air-drying and subsequent processing (Liu et al., 2022a). Nevertheless, their specific contributions in the present study were inferred based on metabolic profiles rather than direct sensory validation. Succinic acid likely originates from microbial metabolism and the tricarboxylic acid cycle, and its moderate accumulation may contribute to flavor layering while synergizing with amino acid-derived compounds to establish the foundational flavor profile of air-dried beef jerky. The enrichment of hypoxanthine reflects the progressive nucleotide degradation, which may serve as an indicator of maturation and umami development, although further targeted quantification is warranted. In air-dried mutton jerky (JM), the metabolites with relatively high levels included oxoproline, 2-deoxy-D-arabitol, stearic acid, taurine, succinic acid, inosine, hypoxanthine 1, leucine, estriol, and valine. These compounds span organic acids and derivatives, lipids and lipid-like molecules, organic oxygen-containing compounds, nucleosides/nucleotides and their analogues, and other metabolites, and may be associated with flavor formation and quality evolution in air-dried mutton jerky.

Functionally, these metabolites act both as essential flavor precursors and as direct contributors to sensory properties. Branched-chain amino acids (leucine and valine) perform functions analogous to those in JB, potentially synergizing with the lipid and lipid-like molecule profiles in JM to generate the characteristic meaty aroma and mature flavor of air-dried mutton jerky. Sugar alcohols such as 2-deoxy-D-arabitol and organic acids such as succinic acid similarly contribute to the formation of the basic flavor framework, paralleling their roles in air-dried beef jerky. Furthermore, the accumulation of inosine and hypoxanthine reflects deep nucleotide degradation, which may be related to umami formation and quality evolution during mutton maturation. Fatty acids such as stearic acid provide the substrate basis for lipid oxidation, potentially contributing to the development of characteristic flavors in air-dried mutton jerky.

Comparative analysis of untargeted metabolites in air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) revealed distinct clustering between bovine (JB) and ovine (JM) specimens, with PC1 (48.1%) and PC2 (10.9%) collectively explaining 59% of total variance (Fig. 5A). Intragroup clustering and intergroup separation suggest metabolomic differentiation between species. Orthogonal Projections to Latent Structures Discriminant Analysis (OPLS-DA) enhanced group discrimination by incorporating a priori class information, yielding predictive component (47.3%) and orthogonal component (8.86%) variance (Fig. 5B). Tight clustering of biological replicates and clear intergroup separation suggested significant metabolomic differentiation (P < 0.01, permutation testing). It should be noted that discrimination performance was evaluated based on household-level data (n = 3 per group), and therefore model stability should be interpreted cautiously due to limited biological replication, which may restrict the generalizability of the model. OPLS-DA analysis showed clear separation between JB and JM samples, but further studies with expanded cohorts are required for confirmation. As shown in Figs. 5C-D, the OPLS-DA model demonstrated excellent goodness-of-fit (R2Y = 0.999) and strong predictive ability (Q2 = 0.972), both exceeding the 0.9 threshold, indicating robust and reasonable model parameters. However, despite these favorable statistical indicators, the relatively small sample size necessitates cautious interpretation of model robustness. Permutation testing with 200-fold external cross-validation yielded intercepts of R2 = 0.81 and Q2 = −0.98. Although the R2 intercept was relatively high (0.81), the negative Q2 intercept (−0.98) and steep regression slope collectively indicate absence of overfitting, yet independent validation with larger sample sets would strengthen confidence in these findings.

To further elucidate species-dependent differences in volatile composition, differential metabolite analysis was conducted. A Venn diagram analysis (Fig. 5E) revealed that 254 volatile flavor compounds were shared between air-dried beef jerky (JB) and air-dried mutton jerky (JM) systems. In addition, 55 compounds were unique to air-dried beef jerky, whereas 15 were specific to air-dried mutton jerky, which may indicate comparatively greater detected chemical diversity in air-dried beef jerky under the present analytical conditions; however, given the limited sample size, this observation should be interpreted cautiously. Air-dried beef jerky-specific metabolites could be broadly categorized into amino acids and their derivatives, fatty acids and lipid-derived metabolites, indole compounds and tryptophan-related metabolites, carbohydrates and organic acid derivatives, and nucleosides and purine-related metabolites. Among these, 5-Aminovaleric acid lactam, an amino acid derivative associated with lysine metabolism, commonly occurs in air-dried meat products. Its relatively higher abundance in air-dried beef jerky may reflect progressive protein degradation under low-moisture and extended drying conditions. Previous studies indicate that such compounds are closely linked to the characteristic meaty and matured aroma of fermented meats, serving as key metabolic markers for flavor development (Zhang et al., 2021). Nevertheless, in the present study, its functional contribution was inferred from metabolomic patterns rather than direct sensory validation.

Within the indole and tryptophan-related metabolite class, tryptophol originates from tryptophan metabolism and is a common aromatic metabolite in fermented foods. It may contribute to flavor complexity and persistence in air-dried products, potentially enhancing overall flavor depth, although this requires further confirmation (Alessandro et al., 2019). Fatty acids and lipid-derived metabolites, such as palmitic acid, are abundant in beef. During drying, decreasing moisture facilitates mild oxidative reactions of these lipids, which may promote the formation of fatty aroma-related compounds and influence flavor persistence and balance. (Domínguez et al., 2019).

Air-dried mutton jerky-specific metabolites were grouped into amino acids and their derivatives, aromatic organic acids and derivatives, fatty acids and steroid-related metabolites, carbohydrates and glycosides, and nucleosides. Among the detected metabolites, several showed relatively higher abundance in JM (VIP >1, P < 0.05), including phytanic acid, which has been reported in ruminant-derived products and may participate in lipid transformation during drying. Studies have highlighted that phytanic acid and related metabolites contribute to the unique fatty aroma and species-specific odor of mutton, forming an essential chemical basis for distinguishing air-dried mutton jerky from other meat products (Zhang et al., 2024b). In the present study, these associations should be considered indicative rather than definitive due to the limited biological replication. Aromatic organic acids such as 3-phenyllactic acid are reported to provide subtle aromatic notes and moderate undesirable off-flavors, thereby improving overall flavor harmony (Sun et al., 2025).

The amino acid derivative putrescine is associated with flavor depth and aftertaste development, and may be related to the matured flavor profile of air-dried mutton jerky. Shared metabolites between the two meat types mainly fall into amino acids and derivatives, organic acids and derivatives, fatty acids and lipid-related compounds, carbohydrates and sugar-acid derivatives, and nucleosides/purine/pyrimidine-related metabolites. Lactic acid, a typical glycolysis product, was present in both beef and mutton and plays a dual role in modulating acidity and influencing protein structure and microbial metabolism, thereby affecting the balance and stability of overall flavor (Wang et al., 2024a). However, its precise contribution in the current samples was not directly quantified at the sensory level. Leucine, a branched-chain amino acid, serves as a common flavor precursor, undergoing further conversion to branched aldehydes, alcohols, and acids, contributing to the formation of volatile flavor compounds (Chen et al., 2024). The long-chain alcohol phytol originates from lipid-associated structures or their degradation products and is thought to enhance the fatty aroma background, potentially improving flavor roundness and persistence (Carmen et al., 2022).

Comparative metabolomic analysis identified 838 shared metabolites between bovine (JB) and ovine (JM) specimens, with 404 exhibiting significant differences in relative levels (VIP >1, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5F). Differential metabolites spanning 11 chemical classes included 260 compounds with relatively higher levels in JB (e.g., L-methionine, D-glucosamine-1-phosphate, and fructose-6-phosphate; 1.8- to 3.2-fold differences), which are functionally associated with protein biosynthesis and glycolytic pathways. Conversely, 144 metabolites with relatively lower levels in JB (0.3- to 0.6-fold lower relative levels), including phytanic acid, citric acid, and taurine, showed strong linkages to β-oxidation pathways and mitochondrial energy metabolism (Fig. 5G). These pathway associations are based on database annotation and should be interpreted as putative functional implications. In comparison with other meat-type metabolomics studies, which often report fewer detected metabolites and differential compounds mainly enriched in central carbon and amino acid metabolism (Sun et al., 2022a; Fu et al., 2022; Anwar et al., 2025), our dataset (838 shared metabolites; 404 significant, VIP > 1, P < 0.05) revealed a comparatively broad spectrum of metabolites spanning glycolysis/protein biosynthesis (e.g., fructose-6-phosphate, L-methionine) and β-oxidation/mitochondrial-associated pathways (e.g., citric acid, taurine). This expanded coverage may reflect the metabolic diversity associated with natural air-drying processes in bovine and ovine meats from the Xilingol region of Inner Mongolia. Nevertheless, differences in analytical platforms, sample preparation strategies, and annotation pipelines across studies may also influence metabolite numbers and pathway distributions. Therefore, while our findings are broadly consistent with previous reports highlighting amino acid- and lipid-centered differentiation, cross-study comparisons should be interpreted with caution, particularly considering the limited biological replication in the present study.

L-Methionine (2.7-fold upregulated in JB), an essential sulfur-containing amino acid, L-methionine is known to participate in sulfur metabolism and redox regulation, and its relative abundance may influence oxidative reactions within meat matrices. Notably, its oxidative susceptibility in fermented meat matrices was shown to modulate protein functionality via disulfide bond formation and surface hydrophobicity alterations (Li et al., 2024). Taurine (0.4-fold downregulated), a sulfonic acid derivative, demonstrated cardioprotective effects through cholesterol modulation and ROS scavenging, particularly preserving neural integrity against oxidative insults (Aydın et al., 2016). While these physiological functions are well documented, their direct relevance to flavor formation in the present system remains speculative.

Overall, the observed patterns are broadly consistent with previous reports highlighting amino acid- and lipid-related differentiation in fermented meats; nevertheless, validation with larger cohorts is required (Liu et al., 2023b; Li et al., 2023). Notably, Wang et al.'s identification of 1782 flavor-associated metabolites (including 23.1% lipids and 18.7% organic acids) in artisanal jerky products reinforced our observed chemical stratification (Wang et al., 2024c). The recurrent detection of organic acid derivatives across studies supports their potential involvement in pH regulation and maturation processes, although quantitative comparability is limited.

Untargeted metabolomic profiling based on internal standard normalization revealed notable compositional differences between bovine (JB) and ovine (JM) products, with JM exhibiting 4.7-fold higher relative levels of D-talose (P < 0.001) alongside significant increases in relative levels of isoleucine (3.2×), glycine isomers (2.8–3.1×), and proline (2.5×) relative to JB (Fig. 5H). Concomitant decreases in relative levels of myristic acid (0.4×) and carnitine (0.3×) suggested possible substrate-related lipid remodeling, while enhanced amino acid flux was broadly consistent with previous studies reporting fermentation-associated enrichment of branched-chain amino acids; however, given the limited sample size (n = 3 per group at the household level), these trends should be interpreted as preliminary observations rather than definitive species-specific signatures.

Correlation analysis between dominant genera and core metabolites

Correlation analysis suggested several statistically supported association patterns between dominant bacterial genera and metabolites with relatively high levels in air-dried beef jerky (JB) and air-dried mutton jerky (JM) (Fig. 6A–B). Correlation coefficients were calculated at the household level within each meat type (n = 3 independent households per meat type) using CLR-transformed relative abundance data and Spearman's rank test with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction, as described in Section 2.4. Given the limited number of biological replicates, only correlations that remained significant after FDR adjustment were interpreted, and these associations should be considered exploratory. Correlations were calculated separately for the beef and mutton groups (n = 3 for each group) using the average values from each household. In air-dried beef jerky (JB), Lactobacillus exhibited a significant positive correlation with isoleucine. This association is consistent with the reported proteolytic capacity of Lactobacillus in fermented meat systems, where protein degradation may contribute to the release of amino acids such as isoleucine (Liu et al., 2021). However, given the correlation-based nature of the present analysis, this relationship should be interpreted as a statistical association rather than direct functional confirmation. Halomonas showed significant positive correlations with 2-deoxyerythritol and succinic acid. Among these, 2-deoxyerythritol contributes to maintaining cellular osmotic balance, which is closely associated with the halotolerant characteristics of Halomonas, while succinic acid enhances umami attributes. These functional interpretations are inferred from literature reports and should not be considered direct evidence of metabolic causation in the present system. Meanwhile, Halomonas was significantly negatively correlated with hydroxyproline, fructose 1, and fructose 2, suggesting that this genus may preferentially utilize fructose as an energy source during air-dried beef jerky processing.

It is also possible that such interactions indirectly influence the activity of other microorganisms, thereby modulating texture–flavor balance; however, this remains speculative. In contrast, Rikenella exhibited significant positive correlations with hydroxyproline, fructose 1, and fructose 2, indicating that this genus may participate in collagen degradation by secreting collagenases, thus improving product tenderness. Nevertheless, enzyme activity was not directly measured in this study, and this inference is based solely on correlation patterns. At the same time, its relatively low efficiency in fructose utilization leads to the accumulation of fructose within the system. Conversely, Rikenella showed significant negative correlations with 2-deoxyerythritol and succinic acid, suggesting that its metabolic activity is more oriented toward protein degradation, while its carbohydrate fermentation capacity is comparatively weak. Notably, Rikenella displayed correlation patterns opposite to those of Halomonas and Lactobacillus, indicating contrasting association trends among these genera under the processing conditions of air-dried beef jerky. In addition, Kocuria, Psychrobacter, Clostridia_UCG-014, and Serratia act as synergistic genera, exhibiting similar patterns of metabolic complementarity. These coordinated correlation patterns may reflect structured co-occurrence patterns in the high-salt, low-temperature, and low-water-activity environment characteristic of air-dried beef jerky, although causal relationships cannot be established.

In air-dried mutton jerky (JM), Lactobacillus appeared as a relatively central node within the correlation network and showed significant positive correlations with inosine and 2-hydroxypyridine, while exhibiting a significant negative correlation with estriol. Lactobacillus has been reported to possess nucleolytic activity in fermented meat systems, which may contribute to inosine formation. Inosine serves as a key flavor precursor, contributing to umami perception and enriching the overall flavor complexity of the product (Wang et al., 2023). The positive association between Lactobacillus and 2-hydroxypyridine is presumed to be related to the formation of pyrimidine-derived metabolites during microbial metabolism. However, this presumption is based on metabolic annotation and requires targeted validation. In contrast, the negative correlation with estriol may result from consumption of precursor compounds or indirect metabolic interactions. However, the present findings are based on correlation analysis and do not establish direct metabolic causality.

As a psychrotolerant genus, Psychrobacter exhibited a correlation pattern fully consistent with that of Lactobacillus. This similarity may indicate similar association structures in nucleic acid–related metabolic processes, although direct cooperative mechanisms were not experimentally verified. Moreover, the cold-adaptation capability of Psychrobacter complements the metabolic activity of Lactobacillus, enabling stable metabolic regulation under low-temperature processing conditions (Tian et al., 2022). In contrast, Clostridia_UCG-014 displayed correlation trends opposite to those of Lactobacillus, indicating a clear metabolic division of labor among microbial taxa. It is conceivable that Lactobacillus and Clostridia_UCG-014 occupy distinct metabolic niches; however, substrate fluxes were not directly traced in this study. Halomonas showed significant positive correlations with succinic acid, stearic acid, and myristic acid.

Succinic acid is known to enhance umami characteristics, whereas the accumulation of fatty acids such as stearic and myristic acids is likely associated with the involvement of Halomonas in lipid metabolism. These associations suggest statistical co-occurrence with lipid-related metabolites, although lipid degradation pathways were not directly quantified. Kocuria exhibited correlation patterns highly consistent with those of Halomonas, while Ruminococcus and Serratia showed completely opposite trends. These contrasting relationships suggest functional complementarity among the four genera in lipid metabolism, umami compound formation, and salt-tolerance adaptation. Collectively, these correlation networks may reflect coordinated microbial–metabolite interactions shaping product characteristics, but mechanistic confirmation is required. Overall, Lactobacillus appears as a central node within the correlation network and, together with genera such as Halomonas, Kocuria, and Psychrobacter, forms a statistically interconnected association network. Given the exploratory nature of the dataset (n = 3 per group), this network should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating framework rather than definitive evidence of microbial regulatory hierarchy.

Across both air-dried meat products, Lactobacillus was mainly characterized by correlations of moderate strength, indicating a certain degree of conservatism in its regulatory role on core metabolites in air-dried meat systems. However, its specific association patterns differed between air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky. In air-dried beef jerky, Lactobacillus showed a significant positive correlation with isoleucine, whereas this association was not observed in air-dried mutton jerky. Instead, in air-dried mutton jerky, Lactobacillus exhibited significant positive correlations with inosine and 2-hydroxypyridine, along with a significant negative correlation with estriol. Moreover, in air-dried beef jerky, Lactobacillus displayed significant negative correlations with multiple metabolites. These differences may be related to variations in raw material matrices, including differences in protein and lipid composition and their relative proportions, as well as to the substrate-specific generation of metabolites during processing.

Despite these product-specific differences, clear patterns of positive–negative metabolic complementarity among multiple bacterial genera were consistently observed in both products. In both air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky, Halomonas and Kocuria functioned predominantly as positively associated genera, whereas Rikenella, Clostridia_UCG-014, Serratia, and Ruminococcus acted as negatively associated genera. Meanwhile, Lactobacillus and uncultured microorganisms served as supplementary taxa with moderate correlation strength. Such structured association patterns may indicate a relatively stable co-occurrence structure under air-drying conditions, although ecological stability was not longitudinally assessed.

Notably, the metabolite categories involved in these interactions were largely conserved, mainly encompassing amino acids, organic acids, and sugar-derived compounds, which are widely recognized contributors to flavor development. Similar association patterns between microbial taxa and metabolites have been reported previously. For example, Yang, Zhang, Li and Dong (2023). demonstrated through integrated metagenomic and metabolomic analyses of fermented meat products that lactic acid bacteria and cocci were significantly associated with amino acids, fatty acids, and volatile compounds, suggesting synergistic metabolic interactions. Furthermore, Xing et al. (2023) highlighted that microbial communities in meat products interact via protein, lipid, and carbohydrate pathways, and that complementarity between dominant and subdominant genera contributes to flavor formation and quality stability. Taken together, the present correlation-based findings are broadly consistent with prior reports describing microbial–metabolite association structures in fermented meats. However, due to the limited biological replication and the observational design, these results should be regarded as preliminary evidence supporting potential microbial metabolic interactions in air-dried meat systems, warranting further validation through expanded sampling and functional assays.

Discussion

The observed differences in microbial composition and metabolite profiles between air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky may reflect intrinsic variation in raw material composition and substrate availability between the two matrices. Intrinsic differences in protein composition, lipid profiles, fatty acid distribution, and endogenous enzyme activity between beef and mutton may influence microbial colonization and metabolic processes, thereby shaping matrix-specific microbial–metabolic linkage patterns. In the present study, associations between dominant genera and metabolites differed between air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky, suggesting substrate-dependent interaction networks rather than universally conserved metabolic relationships. Previous investigations of traditional fermented meats from Xinjiang have reported functional dominance of Staphylococcus, Micrococcus, and Lactobacillus species, with Lactobacillus contributing through dual metabolic roles: proteolytic activity that facilitates bioactive peptide formation and carbohydrate metabolism generating organic acids such as acetic, formic, and succinic acids that serve as precursors for downstream flavor compounds (Wang et al., 2021d; Barcenilla et al., 2022）). Although Lactobacillus was also identified as a predominant genus in both air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky in the present study, the genus-level associations differed between matrices, suggesting that microbial functionality may be influenced by substrate context.

Nevertheless, comparisons between air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky in artisanal production systems may still be affected by residual processing-related variation. Although paired sampling within each household was implemented to reduce inter-producer variability, production parameters were not experimentally standardized. Subtle differences in factors such as salt penetration, cutting geometry, drying microenvironment, airflow conditions, and hygienic handling may influence microbial assembly and metabolic activity. Therefore, while the paired household-level design helps control for producer effects, the observed differences between air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky should not be interpreted as arising solely from animal species under fully standardized processing conditions. Importantly, air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky were produced concurrently within the same households using comparable traditional procedures, making systematic producer-specific bias unlikely to fully explain the consistent separation observed in metabolomic profiles. The reproducible differentiation across three independent production units suggests that raw material composition likely contributes substantially, although not exclusively, to the observed microbial–metabolic patterns. Thus, animal species in this study should be interpreted mainly as a comparative framework rather than a completely isolated biological variable.

The number of independent production households was limited (n = 3 per meat type), which restricts statistical power and limits the broader generalization of the findings. Accordingly, correlation-based analyses should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. In particular, correlation analyses conducted within each product group were based on a very small number of biological replicates, which may lead to unstable correlation coefficients and limited statistical robustness, even after CLR transformation and FDR correction. Because correlation analyses were performed separately within each product group, the resulting associations should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as preliminary observations requiring validation in larger datasets. Differential taxonomic features were identified using LEfSe analysis, which combines nonparametric statistical testing with effect-size estimation. However, when biological replication is limited, the robustness of LEfSe-based differential taxa identification may be reduced, and the detected discriminatory genera should be interpreted as indicative rather than definitive biomarkers. Alpha diversity indices reported in this study were used only to describe overall microbial community structure and were not employed to infer functional differences or metabolic capacities between air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky samples. Analyses of microbe–metabolite associations were conducted independently of diversity metrics, and the interpretation of microbial–metabolic linkages was therefore not based on alpha diversity comparisons. Several physicochemical parameters closely related to flavor development, including water activity (Aw), lipid oxidation (TBARS), and protein oxidation, were not measured. These factors may interact with microbial metabolism and influence the formation of key flavor precursors, which limits deeper mechanistic interpretation of the observed microbial–metabolic associations. In addition, detailed quantitative metadata describing processing conditions—such as exact salt concentration, drying duration, and microclimatic temperature or humidity—were not instrumentally recorded.

For microbial community analysis, the two production batches collected from each household were homogenized prior to DNA extraction in order to obtain a representative microbial community profile for each production unit. While this approach helps reduce stochastic variation between closely spaced artisanal batches and avoids pseudo-replication, it also limits evaluation of short-term temporal variability within the same household. In contrast, metabolomic measurements were conducted on independent samples from each batch in order to preserve potential sample-level variability, after which batch measurements were averaged at the household level for statistical comparison.

In addition, mock community standards were not included in the sequencing run. Although negative controls were applied during DNA extraction and amplification, the absence of a defined mock community limits the direct evaluation of sequencing accuracy and potential methodological bias.

Future studies incorporating larger sample sizes, integrated physicochemical measurements, and time-resolved multi-omics analyses would help clarify microbial succession and metabolite transformation pathways during air-dried meat production. Controlled validation experiments, including targeted functional assays or model fermentation systems, would further help determine the specific contributions of genera such as Kocuria and Ruminococcus to flavor formation. Such approaches would strengthen causal inference and provide a more comprehensive understanding of substrate-driven microbial assembly and metabolic output in traditional air-dried meat systems.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky exhibit distinct microbial and metabolite profiles despite sharing several dominant bacterial genera. In both products, Lactobacillus accounted for approximately 17–21% of the community and Halomonas for 13–16%, indicating their predominance under the investigated air-drying conditions. At the genus level, Kocuria was more abundant in air-dried beef jerky, whereas Ruminococcus and Serratia were enriched in air-dried mutton jerky. These compositional differences were accompanied by variation in metabolite profiles, particularly among amino acids, organic acids, and lipid-related compounds, resulting in separation of air-dried beef jerky and air-dried mutton jerky samples in multivariate analyses. Correlation analysis further revealed matrix-specific association patterns between dominant bacterial genera and selected metabolites; however, these relationships were based on statistical associations and do not establish metabolic causality. All statistical analyses were conducted within a paired hierarchical framework with production household treated as a random blocking factor (n = 3 per meat type). Given the limited biological replication, the findings should be interpreted cautiously and generalized conservatively.
