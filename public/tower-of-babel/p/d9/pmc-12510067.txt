Prospecting technology and economic potential to produce bio-succinic acid: a review

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highligths•Biotechnological routes are increasingly replacing petrochemical methods for succinic acid production.•Escherichia coli and Actinobacillus succinogenes demonstrate high potential for efficient biosuccinic acid production.•The biosuccinic acid market is projected to grow at an annual rate of up to 6.7 % through 2032, offering a sustainable alternative to maleic anhydride.Keywords: Bio-succinic acid, Escherichia coli, Actinobacillus succinogenes, Biosuccinic acid market, CAGR

Introduction

The growth of social, environmental, and economic demands drives the production of chemicals from renewable raw materials, preferably through biological routes, especially as substitutes for fossil-based ones. Additionally, the bioeconomy is driven by innovations in utilizing renewable natural resources. It is interesting to consider replacing products obtained through the petrochemical route due to fluctuations in oil prices on the international market and significant environmental pressures [1,2]. Several high-value molecules can be obtained by fermenting sugars from biomass and their waste products. Bio-succinic acid, also known as 1,4-butanedioic acid, is a platform molecule obtained from various microorganisms, typically through anaerobic fermentation, and has gained prominence in recent years [3]

However, the choice of microbial strain is a key factor in BioSA synthesis [4,5], as succinic acid can be produced by wild-type bacteria, fungi, or genetically engineered organisms. Among the various microorganisms studied for BioSA production from carbon-based sources, Actinobacillus succinogenes and E. coli are among the most prominent. Actinobacillus succinogenes offers notable advantages in succinic acid production, with a high capacity for large-scale production, efficiency in using various carbon sources, resistance to stress conditions, low nutritional requirements, and the ability to generate valuable by-products. Furthermore, this strain can be genetically modified to enhance its performance. E. coli also shows great promise in BioSA production, with advantages such as performing two simultaneous saccharification and fermentation phases under anaerobic conditions, rapid cell growth, low complexity, and low-cost nutrient requirements, as well as the possibility of genetic modifications to increase productivity and fermentation efficiency [[4], [5], [6], [7]]E. coli also arouses great interest in BioSA production, presenting advantages such as performing two simultaneous saccharification and fermentation phases under anaerobic conditions, its rapid cellular growth, and demand for low-complexity and low-cost nutrients, as well as the possibility of genetic modifications to increase productivity and fermentation efficiency [3,8].

The chemical structure of succinic acid makes it a potential building block for commodity chemicals with applications in various industries, allowing the manufacture of pharmaceuticals, resins, varnishes, paints, surfactants for treating metal surfaces, plasticizers, polyesters, and other polymers. Considering these characteristics, succinic acid is also a potential raw material for the production of 1,4-butanediol (BDO), tetrahydrofuran, γ-butyrolactone, "green solvents," and biodegradable polymers like polybutylene succinate (PBS) and polyamides [9]

Until recently, large-scale production of succinic acid was limited to petrochemical routes using hydrocarbons as feedstock, which posed environmental concerns and made production costs vulnerable to fluctuations in oil prices. However, this scenario began to shift with the rise of the bioprocessing industry. In 2009, the first commercial-scale BioSA production facility was launched through a joint venture between USDNP Green Technology and ARD (Agroindustrie Recherches et Développements), utilizing glucose derived from wheat as a renewable source of energy. According to Maximize Market Research (2024) [10], the global succinic acid market reached a value of USD 181.91 million in 2024 and is projected to grow at a compound annual growth rate (CAGR) of 6.7 % from 2025 to 2032, potentially reaching USD 305.62 million by the end of the forecast period. Despite this growth, BioSA applications have remained somewhat limited due to high raw material and production costs, with primary use in the food and pharmaceutical sectors, which offer higher added value. However, as the market evolves, industrial applications—particularly as intermediates in the production of paints, solvents, and varnishes—have gained significant market share in 2024, highlighting the strong potential of new routes and uses for BioSA [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. The market remains highly competitive, with major multinational companies pursuing strategies such as partnerships and capacity expansions. Despite the impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic, the gradual reopening of industrial sectors is expected to drive demand for succinic acid further. This review aims to analyze the biotechnological pathways for producing succinic acid from renewable sources. We will explore the main microorganisms used (E. coli and A. succinogenes), optimal cultivation conditions, and the applications of succinic acid produced through biotechnological routes. Furthermore, we will discuss the challenges associated with succinic acid production through biotechnological routes, market opportunities, and growth projections, including the Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) for this technology.

Development of technologies for the synthesis of BioSA

The main factors for developing commercially attractive technologies using biological routes to produce BioSA on an industrial scale are low-cost raw materials, high-yield fermentation technologies, and efficient recovery and purification processes. Research has used many microorganisms to produce BioSA from carbon-based sources [15]. Metabolic engineering strategies employed in the production of BioSA enhance productivity and selectivity in biological conversion. Another issue of great importance is that microorganisms undergo two distinct phases simultaneously: growth and fermentation. The conditions that meet the requirements of one phase are not always suitable for the other [16]

In fermentation, succinic acid is an intermediary in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle of much of living organisms' production. Consequently, many microorganisms have been utilized in research to produce BioSA from carbon-based sources, with E. coli and A. succinogenes standing out. These microorganisms proliferate in environments with high concentrations of carbon dioxide (CO2) (capnophilic growth), which plays a preponderant role in the yield and productivity of bio-succinic acid fermentation. The theoretical yield from the stoichiometric equation is approximately 1.71 mol of succinate produced per mol of glucose, based on the number of available electrons (i.e., 24 electrons in glucose divided by 14 electrons in succinate)(18), according to Eq. Considering the molar masses of the components involved, the maximum theoretical yield for the conversion of glucose to succinic acid is approximately 1.31 g of succinate per gram of glucose. Therefore, reports of yields exceeding this theoretical maximum in wild-type strains suggest inconsistencies that may arise from experimental, calculation, or reporting errors [17,18].

Several factors can contribute to these inflated yield values. Common causes include underestimation of glucose consumption due to unaccounted carbon sources such as yeast extract, glycerol, or CO₂ fixation [19]; overestimation of succinic acid concentration caused by impurities interfering with analytical methods; loss of glucose through evaporation or sampling that is not included in yield calculations, resulting in artificially high values; data normalization errors, particularly when not all of the added glucose is consumed, leading to inaccuracies in mass balance calculations.

In contrast, engineered strains can achieve higher yields through targeted metabolic modifications. A rational strategy involves optimizing the supply of Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Hydrogen (NADH) via the pentose phosphate (PP) pathway and enhancing carbon flux toward succinate production [18], allowing yields to approach the theoretical maximum more reliably. Production of BioSA by Escherichia coli

The microbial production of BioSA by E. coli has been investigated using a genetic engineering approach, primarily due to its rapid growth rate, the wide range of genetic tools available, and the ease of cultivation [[19], [20], [21]]. E. coli is a bacterium with a low ability to produce succinate compared to other microorganisms. It also produces acetic, ethyl, and formic acid, which reduces the potential for BioSA synthesis and process profitability. On the other hand, it can produce SA from multiple metabolic routes, highlighting improvements in substrate transportation, optimization of succinate synthesis pathways, and decreased side reactions that cause the deposition of by-products [22]. The metabolic versatility of E. coli may justify it as one of the main microorganisms studied in BioSA production.

The type of fermentation employed in succinate production through E. coli plays an essential role in the feasibility of this material production. In the case of the E. coli wild strain, the anaerobic fermentation traditionally used in other strains utilizes only about 5 % of the total carbon flux for succinate production, with the majority being transformed into lactates, formates, and acetates [23]. Genetic variations of E. coli that suppress the enzymes responsible for synthesizing these by-products are widely used to increase productivity in succinate formation. However, anaerobic fermentation inhibits cell growth due to the inability to produce high levels of acetyl-CoA (Acetyl coenzyme A) and intracellular imbalance, with the accumulation of pyruvate and NADH. To overcome its limitations, preliminary stages of anaerobic production, such as cellular growth under aerobic conditions, have been used [[28], [29], [30]] In the anaerobic phase, PEP (Phosphoenolpyruvate) and pyruvate are converted to oxaloacetate using PEP carboxylase and PEP carboxykinase enzymes in the carboxylation process. Several techniques have been investigated for supplying NADH during the fermentative process. Zheng et al. [32] evaluated the effect of implementing a microbiological electrolytic cell to increase succinate synthesis.

With the application of this process, an increase of 29.1 % in yield was achieved, obtaining 0.71 gg-1 of glucose and a 36.2 % increase in glucose consumption.

The best-reported results obtained by treating excessive osmotic stress and formation of byproducts that can compromise cell performance were Gao et al. [19], which adopted a strategy of using a strain resistant to osmotic pressure (FMME-N-2), dynamically regulating the flow of oxygen, achieving concentration of 119 g l-1, productivity of 1.65 g l-1 h-1 and a yield of 1.10 gg-1 in a pilot-scale reactor of 30 liters.

Another important path for using the E. coli strain, genetic engineering, was used by Gutiérrez-García et al., [96] where WDHA (succinate producer) or WDHFP (ethanol producer) E. coli strains, with suppression of the genes d-lactate dehydrogenase (ldhA); fumarate reductase (frdD); and phosphate acetyltransferase (pta), were worked in an environment defined as auto display adhesin involved in diffuse adherence (AIDA), which would be a device where protein decoding and the entire cell growth process occur. These processes yielded hydrogen, ethanol, and succinic acid, with the main goal being to promote a versatile cell, achieving a conversion of 0.69 g of succinic acid per gram of sugar. Zhang et al. [33] evaluated the resistance of E. coli to salt stress, which presents a major obstacle to succinic acid production. For simulation, a reaction medium with characteristics like seawater was used. The genetic modification of E. coli with the expression of a global regulator IrrE was used, and no significant differences were observed in both cellular growth and BioSA production. BioSA yields of 24.5 g l-1 were obtained with a yield of 0.88 gg-1 of glucose. Li et al. [102] investigated the transformation of glycerol, which theoretically has greater carbon availability and yield, with the MBL strain, maintaining the suppression of the malate dehydrogenase genes, maleic enzyme, phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase, and carboxinase in two distinct fermentation growth stages. Despite the good yield of 0.93 mol of BioSA/mol glycerol, glycerol consumption was very slow, impacting the low succinate concentration.

In addition to glucose, other feedstocks, such as red Palmaria algae [108] and green coconut water [109], were studied as alternatives for BioSA production through E. coli. The KLPPP strain, with lactate dehydrogenase, pyruvate formatoliase, acetate phosphotransacetylase, and pyruvate oxidase enzyme suppression and overexpression of the phosphoenolpyruvate carboxinase gene, presented higher yields for the transformation of glucose and galactose sugars, obtaining 1.13 mol mol-1and a concentration of 22.4 g l-1. In the study with coconut water as raw material, a new strain, M6PM, was used with the same overexpression strategy of the phosphoenolpyruvate carboxinase gene. These materials contain high glucose, fructose, and sucrose levels, with BioSA concentration after fermentation of 11.8 g l-1 and a yield of 1.23 mol mol-1.

Due to the versatility of E. coli to work in aerobic and anaerobic environments, two-stage fermentation was used by Bai et al. [30] to optimize the cell growth (aerobic) and fermentation (anaerobic) phases. In this work, the BS002 strain was modified by suppressing the genes encoding the enzymes lactate dehydrogenase (ldhA) and pyruvate-formate lyase (pflB) to avoid the formation of undesirable byproducts. As this suppression impacts anaerobic cell growth due to the inability to synthesize biochemical intermediates such as acetyl-CoA, leading to the accumulation of pyruvates and NADH, the solution of using two phases in the growth/fermentation process was the ideal solution, obtaining a BioSA concentration of 17.4 g l-1 and a yield of 1.24 mol mol-1 of sugar. Other strategies can be used, such as increasing productivity and yield through mutation of the succinate dehydrogenase gene, suppression of acetate formation through modification of the genes responsible for acetate kinase production, acetyl phosphate transferase, and pyruvate oxidase, or even other genetic manipulations to reconnect metabolisms towards succinate, such as activating the glyoxylate bypass or blocking the pentose phosphate pathway by deleting repressor genes of isocitrate lyase or gluconate dehydrogenase. Liang et al. [103] used the two-stage technique for the NZN111 strain. The focus was the suppression of lactate dehydrogenase and pyruvate format lyase genes using glucose in two-phase aerobic and anaerobic processes. This divided process into stages increased fermentation productivity, reducing the NADH/NAD+ ratio from 0.64 to 0.26 and forming byproducts. The yield was 0.72 gg-1 of glucose and 1.01 g l-1 h productivity. Li et al. [90] used the YL106/pSCsfcAstrain to produce 85.3 g l-1 and yield 0.91 mol mol-1 sugar. These results were obtained by performing the fermentation process in three stages: aerobic, microaerobic, and anaerobic. The insertion of the microaerobic stage, where oxygen is allowed at very low concentrations, accelerates the fermentation process, even during the cell growth phase, thereby improving the yield.

Production of BioSA by Actinobacillus succinogenes

Actinobacillus succinogenes is considered one of the best bacteria for BioSA production. Its high efficiency results from the rumen's capacity for synthesizing succinic acid [38] It is also versatile in terms of carbon sources, being used in anaerobic conditions, based on a wide range of C5 and C6 sugars, as well as various disaccharides and other carbon sources such as glucose, xylose, arabinose, mannose, galactose, fructose, sucrose, lactose, cellobiose, mannitol, maltose, and glycerol [15]. Several studies have been performed on diverse raw materials, such as sulfite liquor, corn cob, straw, and glucose.

The fermentation process using Actinobacillus succinogenes is illustrated in Fig. 2. Glucose is transported into the cell where phosphorylation occurs, not only through the PEP-dependent phosphodesidrogenase system but also through glucosidase activity, represented by pathway [1] in Fig. 2. Thus, glycerate-3-phosphate is synthesized through the consumption of (Adenosine Triphosphate) ATP. NADH is formed from NAD+ through phosphorohydrogenase (degradation of glycerate-3-phosphate into PEP), continuing the fermentation cycle, still with energy consumption of ATP.

Biochemical synthesis of succinate via succinogenes and succiniciproducens from glucose.

Glyoxylate is not present in the fermentative metabolism of A. succinogenes. The four key components in the fermentation process are phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), oxaloacetate (OAA), malate, and pyruvate [16]. The main route for succinic acid flows throughout the metabolic flow, from phosphoenolpyruvate to oxaloacetate, malate, fumarate, and finally succinate catalyzed by PEP carboxykinase, malate dehydrogenase, fumarase, and fumarate reductase, respectively (Fig. 2). PEP can be converted into formate, acetate, and ethanol by the C3 route (pyruvate) and succinate by the C4 route (oxaloacetate), with malic enzyme and oxaloacetate decarboxylase maintaining a potential role for conversion to pyruvate, thus forming reversible pathways between C3 and C4 [38].

PEP carboxykinase is considered the main enzyme for succinic acid production with subsequent ATP formation, as it can catalyze the conversion of PEP to OAA through the consumption of CO2. In addition, malate dehydrogenase catalyzes the conversion of oxaloacetate to malate with NADH as a catalyst, while fumarate is generated from malate using the fumarase enzyme. Fumarate reductase is an NADH-dependent enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of fumarate to succinate, generating 2/3 of ATP. Pyruvate kinase occurs in the first reaction of the C3 pathway, converting PEP to pyruvate, producing one molecule of ATP. Pyruvate can be converted to formate, acetate, and ethanol. Pyruvate-format-lyase uses CoA as a cofactor to convert pyruvate to acetyl-CoA and formate. The A. succinogenes synthesizes BioSA through the anaerobic fermentation process, with flexibility for different carbon sources, such as lignocellulosic biomass, industrial and agricultural waste, and directly from sugars. This microorganism ferments sugars in high concentrations, which represents a differential concerning most microorganisms studied in the production of BioSA. Despite these advantages, the rates of BioSA production in batch reactors are relatively low, and continuous or semi-continuous reactors are recommended [37]. Enzyme immobilization has been employed to increase the effectiveness and productivity of the fermentation process [35].

Genetic modifications of the strain have led to production at economically viable levels [41,42]. It is important to emphasize that A. succinogenes requires complex nutrients for proper growth, such as amino acids, vitamins, and abundant nitrogen sources, which can impact the final production cost [50]. Supplementary feeding is required to raise CO2 levels, which function as an electron acceptor, and alter the flux of phosphoenolpyruvate (PEP), inducing the pyruvate and lactate metabolism into succinate [102]

BioSA yield values ranged from 0.31 to 1.23 g of BioSA per gram of fermentable sugars, depending on the type of raw material used, the fermentation strategy employed, and specific process conditions. Most studies that use A. succinogenes use agricultural residues that are underutilized or discarded as waste, generating a negative environmental impact. The most common are sugar cane residues, such as bagasse, straw, and molasses [[35], [36], [44], [45], [46], [47]]. Cheese residues [48,49], palm oil [[40], [50], [51]], fruit and citrus residues [52], and corn residues [[41], [42], [53], [54]] have also been used. In addition, some studies highlight glycerol [34,102], a by-product of biodiesel production. Using these raw materials has the advantage of mitigating the environmental impact their disposal would generate in nature. Costs should consider supply logistics, suggesting that the fermentation plant should be near agro-industrial units.

The challenge of using residues with high lignin content, such as sugarcane, is optimizing the pre-treatment stage and achieving high conversion rates in the hydrolysis of lignocellulose to obtain glucose- and xylose-concentrated solutions. Chen et al. [44] evaluated three sugar cane bagasse pre-treatment types: thermal with steam, ethanol, and sodium hydroxide. In this study, the alkali-treated material led to the largest lignin removal, increasing sugar availability and subsequent fermentation yield. Furthermore, this treatment prevents the formation of fermentation inhibitors, such as furans and phenolics. The yields achieved were 0.32 gg-1 and 0.30 g l-1 h-1 productivity. Chen et al. [45] compared acid pre-treatment with H2SO4 to alkaline treatment with NaOH. They found that the alkaline medium performs better in separating lignin from cellulose, removing approximately 90 % compared to 66 % in the acidic medium. The mechanism of alkaline hydrolysis can occur by saponification of intermolecular ester bonds, rupture of the lignin structure, swelling, and separation of the structural bonds between lignin and carbohydrates. The yields achieved were 0.81 gg-1 and a productivity of 1.65 g l-1 h-1. Wang et al. [47] obtained better results with sugar cane residue, adding polyacrylamide to the alkaline treatment and applying a flocculation and sedimentation treatment to remove impurities from the sugar cane bagasse, increasing the process yield to 0.93 gg-1 and productivity to 1.70 g l-1 h-1.However, it is necessary to evaluate the impact of generating a flocculant and precipitate for the purification stage of succinic acid. Oil palm frond (OPF) bagasse is one of the most affordable reported biomass sources. When hydrolyzed, it can be used as a raw material for the production of fermentative succinic acid. Results indicated that glucose and xylose were released as the main carbon source after alkaline pretreatment, followed by enzymatic hydrolysis. The widespread availability of this material in tropical countries such as Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand, and Brazil, combined with its low cost compared to other waste, generates significant interest in its use as a biomass source. Luthfi et al. [40] evaluated the impact of alkaline pre-treatment using self-hydrolyzed NaOH (adding steam under pressure). The self-hydrolysis presented good results, with equivalent structural carbohydrate levels of 78.3 % of the total weight of the initial biomass. This corresponded to an increase of around 20 % compared to its original base of 65.5 %. This type of pre-treatment was competitive, as it did not show any apparent inhibitory effect during the fermentation stage. Akhtar and Idris [51] obtained better results, with a yield of 0.71 gg-1 and productivity of 0.61 g l-1 h-1, as well as productivity of 0.69 g l-1 h-1, also using alkaline pre-treatment, where NaOH was subjected to microwave radiation, which can penetrate the lignocellulosic structure and directly oscillating the molecules, facilitating the transformation of lignin. Bukhari et al. [50] opted for the study of acid pre-treatment through different concentrations of oxalic acid, obtaining a recovery of only 60.3 %. In this case, the result of the fermentation was quite modest, with a yield of 0.37 gg-1 and productivity of 0.26 g l-1 h-1.

The residues from whey and lactose production found in dairy effluents were used as raw material for fermentation in BioSA [48,49] Using this material, in addition to allowing a relevant reduction in the operational cost of the process, can significantly reduce inhibition phenomena, allowing higher substrate concentrations. In the case of cheese whey, lactose is transformed into glucose and galactose, which are the true inhibitors. Because these are reaction intermediates, their concentrations tend to be very low, minimizing inhibition. The ideal succinic acid production was obtained containing around 25 g.L-1 of lactose and 35 g l-1 of whey, with yields and productivities of 65 % and 0.9 g l-1 h-1 for lactose and 62.1 %, 0.81 g l-1 h-1 for whey [118]. The feasibility of this material as raw material for bioprocesses depends on an economic evaluation and logistical availability, as well as the need for the destination of this type of waste with the creation of an ecosystem between the dairy industry and biorefineries since other materials, such as sugarcane residues have achieved better results in terms of yield and productivity aspects.

Fruits constitute a category of food that generates the most waste, with a global production of around 25 million tons per year, consisting mainly of peels and seeds, which represent up to 50 % of the weight. The citrus peel (CPW - citrus peel waste) was subjected to biological and physicochemical treatment to make its use as raw material in the fermentation and production of BioSA possible. This material, composed of 0.43 % essential oils and 30.53 % pectin as extractable products, when subjected to acid hydrolysis and the addition of cell growth-promoting vitamins, was fermented using Actinobacillus succinogenes [117]. It was found that the simultaneous saccharification and fermentation (SSF) process in batch reactors did not contribute to an increase in BioSA concentration and yield. A concentration of 22.4 g l-1 and a yield of 0.73 gg-1 were obtained through semi-continuous batch reactors, with the removal of the fermentation product throughout the reaction, bringing an increase of about 27 % in productivity.

Some consider glycerol as a residue from biodiesel production due to its low added value, which has led researchers to evaluate its use as raw material in the production of BioSA. The yields of its fermentation are significant, reaching 0.61 to 1.15 gg-1 [34]. Lin et al. [15] obtained a yield of 0.94 gg-1 in a batch process, which used seawater as a source of nutrients for the growth of A. succinogenes, reducing the cost of the process with a productivity of 1.12 g l-1 h-1. Ercole et al. [58] used the batch process with immobilized cells, achieving a yield of 1.15 gg-1, justifying this value due to the increase in the mass transfer rate in the process, which benefits fermentation. It was also found that high sugar concentrations did not interfere with the microorganism's performance. When using glycerol, a much larger proportion of succinic acid, compared to acetic acid, can be obtained than when glucose is used as a substrate, for example. Succinic acid production from glycerol fermentation by A. succinogenes was more efficient than the culture using glucose as a sole carbon source. In fact, due to its greater degree of reduction, glycerol generates more reducing equivalents (NADH) to be oxidized, and therefore, reduced compounds, such as succinate, ethanol, xylitol, propionate, hydrogen, butanol, and propanediol, should achieve higher yields when derived from glycerol compared to glucose [34,59]

Cell immobilization is a strategy that can be used to increase production in fermentation processes [43,58,35,45,48,53] In this process, plastic or organic materials (such as alginate, agar, or cotton tissue) can be used as immobilizing agents for microorganism, aiming to increase cell density. Immobilization provides a suitable environment for cells to produce BioSA, enhancing nutrient diffusion with increased mass transfer rates and, primarily, the possibility of reusing the strain in repeated fermentation cycles. The process with immobilized microorganisms can be used in large-scale SA production in different bioreactors, Continuous Stirred Tank Reactor (CSTR) Packed Bed Reactor (PBR) and Fluidized Bed Reactor (FBR)(51)

Chen et al. [45] used alginate granules to immobilize Actinobacillus succinogenes with high cell retention and efficient nutrient diffusion, resulting in high concentrations of succinic acid compared to free cell cultures. Starting from sugarcane bagasse, results of 0.85 gg-1 conversion and 1.65 gL-1.h-1 productivity were obtained. Immobilized Actinobacillus succinogenes cultures in alginate were also studied by Ercole et al. [58] to improve the performance of this microorganism, obtaining a production of 1.15 g of BioSA per gram of substrate (pure glucose) and a concentration of 69.0 g l-1 of BioSA. In the same study, continuous fermentation was conducted in an FBR, allowing for the combination of high BioSA productivity (25.6 g l-1 h-1) with a high concentration (31 g l-1), resulting in a conversion rate of 76.4 % of the glucose substrates into BioSA.

E. coli and A. succinogenes have been successfully engineered to enhance succinic acid production. E. coli benefits from extensive genetic engineering tools and consistently achieves high yields under optimized conditions. In contrast, A. succinogenes, with its innate capacity for SA production and superior pH tolerance, represents a strong candidate for industrial-scale processes, particularly when complemented by ongoing genetic engineering innovations [60].

The market for biosuccinic acid

The technological evolution of biosuccinic acid production

Many microorganisms have been extensively researched as promising sources for the commercial production of bio-succinic acid. These organisms encompass bacteria, yeasts, and fungi, and they can thrive under carefully controlled and optimized conditions to enhance acid yields. Furthermore, microorganisms are amenable to genetic modifications to boost bio-succinic acid output or enhance their proficiency in converting renewable feedstocks into acid. These advancements enable a more efficient and cost-effective production of bio-succinic acid.

Until recently, large-scale succinic acid production relied solely on hydrocarbons as feedstock, and the market was susceptible to fluctuations in the price of oil and its derivatives. The scenario began to change in 2009 when the first BioSA production factory started operating through a joint venture between the United States DNP Green Technology and ARD (Agro Industrie Recherches et Développements), which produces renewable succinic acid from glucose derived from wheat [61] However, succinic acid derived from fossil sources still maintains its market dominance [62]

A significant increase in filed patents was evident when assessing succinic acid technology development progress from 2012 to 2022. A search using keywords such as "succinic acid" OR "biosuccinic acid," and "biosuccinic acid" AND "biotechnological routes" yielded over 1000 patents. With the evolution of the market, a gradual bio-SA price decline was achieved and is associated with new industrial applications from chemical intermediates to produce paints, solvents, and varnishes, capturing 57.1 % market share by 2021, with more than [55].

Current challenges focus on reducing the total fermentation cost to $0.55/kg and utilizing lignocellulosic residues as a feedstock, which could enhance the competitiveness of bioprocesses. Market reports, growth projections, and CAGR for succinic acid.

Turning to historical trajectories, from 2011 onward, the succinic acid market began a large growth phase, and according to Weastra, production was $68.2 M in 2011, of which only 3 % was bio-based [74]. With the various growth prospects in the sector, production expanded to 226 thousand tons by 2014, representing a 78 % CAGR, and reached 638 thousand tons in 2020 (36 % CAGR), despite market consolidationE4tech confirmed this growth, reporting a 2013 turnover of $208 M—4 % above earlier projections. This expansion was underpinned by industrial partnerships (Succinity, Reverdia) and microbial process innovations [71]. This early bio-based share underscored both the nascent stage of bioprocess adoption and the opportunity for expansion through optimized strains and feedstock diversification.

The strong growth during this period is due to the establishment of joint ventures between Basf and Purac in Spain, giving rise to Succinity, and between DMS and Roquette in Italy, with the Reverdia unit. In addition, there was significant progress in developing new technologies for succinic acid production through some microorganisms.

In 2013, two studies were collected, one from Grand View Research, which indicated a turnover for the succinic acid market of $137 million and pointed out a 9.20 % CAGR for the period from 2015 to 2020, and the E4tech Strategic Thinking in Sustainable Energy report, which separately presented a turnover for bio-succinic acid of $108 million with the global movement of 38,000 tons at an average price of $2.86/ton, and for succinic acid, sales of $100 million, movement of 40,000 tons, with an average price of $2.86/ton, making the total turnover $208 million [71,73]. The E4tech study, which the European Energy Commission used through its final report on platforms for producing biofuels and biochemicals from sugar, proved more reliable, alongside breakthroughs in microbial fermentation and vacuum‐distillation recovery.

In 2015, the outlook became more coherent: the ICR World Research estimated $48.7 million in revenue and a 6.3 % CAGR for 2016–2021 [72], while E4tech updated its forecast to $302 million in total sales (bio + petro routes) with a strong prediction 13.1 % CAGR from 2015 to 2020. These shifts reflected not just price variations but also advances in lignocellulosic pretreatment—integrating continuous fermentation steps—and purification improvements that drove raw‐material costs down to about $1.0/kg [71].

In 2018, Markets and Markets cited a total revenue of $131.7 million and projected a 7.0 % CAGR for 2019–2027 [70], but this market experienced a 43.6 % decrease over three years (CAGR of −14.5 %), largely due to collapsing oil prices and intensified competition from petrochemical derivatives.

By 2021, the global succinic acid market was targeting process intensification, including staged-cell bioreactors, membrane crystallization, and integrated saccharification with pilot-scale vacuum distillation. These improvements reversed the downturn from 2018 to 2021, anchoring the market at $222.9 million [75] and underpinning a 6–9 % CAGR forecast through 2030 [68,69]. Despite the COVID-19 pandemic impacting demand, sector reopenings were anticipated to revive succinic acid consumption [70].

Among the 2023 reports, Vision Research Reports and Technavio presented optimistic forecasts, projecting revenues of $513.4 million and $491.3 million by 2032, respectively. They attributed this growth to the applications of bio-succinic acid in biodegradable plastics and green materials. Conversely, Globe News Wire and Fact.Mr forecasted lower estimates, around $360 million and $301.4 million, respectively, reflecting continued dependence on petrochemical feedstocks for stable yields [75,[56], [57], [65], [66], [67], [76]]. This divergence highlights the tension between innovation-driven demand and established supply chain preferences, suggesting that improvements in downstream processing and feedstock flexibility will be crucial to capturing the higher end of market potential.

Production of high-value-added derivatives of BioSA

One of the most attractive aspects of BioSA production is the potential to generate high-value-added derivatives with a broad range of applications in the automotive, plastics, resins, and paints industries. Companies such as Bioamber and Succinity are producing derivatives such as 1,4 butanediol (BDO) and polybutylene succinate (PBS). However, production cost challenges still hinder increasing the volume traded for these products.

Furthermore, it is known that the largest market volume is in the application of BioSA as an intermediate for petrochemical and fine industries. Succinic acid has the potential to replace maleic anhydride (MAN), whose global market in 2021 was 2.8 $ billion [77]. It was found that 1.2 tons of succinic acid are needed to replace 1 ton of MAN. Succinic acid: current applications and potential applications.

Another product derived from BioSA is Poly Butylene Succinate (PBS), a potentially interesting polyester for compounds made from biopolymers. Its applications range from foam production to plastic and textile containers [82]. PBS synthesis is performed through the polycondensation of succinic acid and 1,4-butanediol. However, it was exclusively produced by the petrochemical route. The added cost of the production chain for BioSA still limits its market expansion. The melting point above 100 °C makes this polymer a versatile choice for applications that require a significantly wide range of temperatures. In conventional melt processing techniques, PBS can find applications in electronics, food, and agriculture containers, where biodegradability is important mainly due to compossibility.

Certainly, 1,4-butanediol (BDO) plays an important role as a commodity chemical in polymer synthesis, contributing significantly to the annual production of over 2.5 million tons of polymers like polyesters, plastics, and spandex fibers. Historically, BDO has been derived from petroleum-based substrates such as acetylene, butane, propylene, and butadiene, utilizing established industrial processes. However, with the growing emphasis on sustainable practices and the increasing demand for eco-friendly alternatives, there's a notable interest in exploring alternative pathways for BDO production [83].

Numerous studies have examined the economic and environmental benefits of producing BioSA from waste. Hafyan et al. 2024 [84] analyze a biorefinery that simultaneously produces bioethanol and succinic acid (SA) from biomass waste, specifically utilizing bread waste. Their findings emphasize both the economic and environmental advantages, showing that this bioprocess is more sustainable and profitable than traditional petrochemical methods. Notably, this process achieves the highest Net Present Value (NPV) of $163 million and the lowest Global Warming Potential (GWP) of −0.344 kg CO₂-eq/kg of bread waste (BW), primarily due to CO₂ capture and the production of SA. While the environmental advantages of BioSA production are widely acknowledged, recent studies provide concrete data to support its sustainability claims. Life cycle assessment (LCA) analyses demonstrate that biotechnological routes can significantly reduce greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions compared to petrochemical pathways. For example, Khoshnevisan et al. (2020), Ladakis et al. (2022), and Kumar et al. (2024) [[85], [86], [87]]report reductions of up to 90 % in GHG emissions when lignocellulosic biomass or food waste is used as feedstock. Furthermore, Melitos et al. (2022) [60]estimated that the carbon footprint of BioSA produced from renewable sources ranges from 0.5 to 1.2 kg CO₂-eq/kg, markedly lower than the 3.5 to 4.5 kg CO₂-eq/kg associated with fossil-based succinic acid production. These findings reinforce the environmental appeal of BioSA and its alignment with global decarbonization goals, thereby strengthening its potential as a sustainable alternative to petrochemical intermediates, such as maleic anhydride [88].

Conclusion

Biosuccinic acid (BioSA) emerges as a promising molecule with the potential to add significant value to second-generation ethanol biorefineries, especially when derived from renewable biomass. Its versatility as a chemical platform enables the production of high-value derivatives such as 1,4-butanediol (BDO) and polybutylene succinate (PBS), which are increasingly relevant in the context of sustainable materials and green chemistry.

However, despite its potential, the scalability and competitiveness of BioSA compared to petrochemical routes still face several technical and economic bottlenecks. Key challenges include low productivity in batch processes, the formation of undesirable by-products, high energy demand (particularly for NADH regeneration), and the costs associated with purification and complex nutrient requirements for some microbial strains. Economically, the high cost of raw materials and the final price of BioSA (currently around \$2.94/kg) still place it at a disadvantage compared to fossil-derived succinic acid.

To overcome these barriers, advances in metabolic engineering have been essential. Genetic modifications in E. coli and Actinobacillus succinogenes have enabled higher yields, reduced by-product formation, and the use of alternative substrates such as glycerol and agro-industrial residues. Process optimization strategies—such as two-stage aerobic/anaerobic fermentation, cell immobilization, and the use of fluidized bed reactors (FBRs)—have also contributed to increased productivity and cost reduction.

Furthermore, the valorization of low-cost residues (such as whey, sugarcane bagasse, and palm oil waste) and integration with biorefineries are promising pathways to improve the economic feasibility of BioSA production. Continued investment in research and development is essential to enhance existing technologies, reduce energy and water consumption, and make BioSA a truly competitive and sustainable alternative to petrochemical routes.
