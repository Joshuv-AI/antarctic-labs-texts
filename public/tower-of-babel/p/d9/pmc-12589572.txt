Catalytic ester hydroboration promoted by actinide complexes featuring six-membered N-heterocyclic imine ligands and actinide-alkoxides activation
Authors: Konstantin Makarov, Natalia Fridman, Moris S. Eisen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In the last decade, organoactinide complexes have been active catalysts for the hydroboration of carbonyl moieties, including aldehydes, ketones, amides, isocyanides, and carbonates. The decoration of the metal center by 5, 6, or 7-N-heterocyclic imines increases the electron density at the metal, allowing for remarkable catalytic performances. We report herein the synthesis of a family of thorium and uranium complexes featuring an analogous set of N-heterocyclic iminato ligands built upon a six-membered core ligand framework. The catalytic ability of these complexes was tested in the ester hydroboration reaction, aromatic, aliphatic, and polymeric esters. The reaction was thoroughly investigated, exhibiting an induction period, which was overcome by manipulating the spectator ligands around the metal. Kinetic and thermodynamic studies were conducted to establish the rate law for the reaction and derive the corresponding activation parameters. Deuterium labeling and stoichiometric reactions were performed, and plausible mechanisms were proposed based on the observations.

Introduction

Reduction reactions are fundamental to chemical transformation, synthesis, and recycling, and are critical processes in industrial, agricultural, and energy applications1–3. Traditionally, hydrogenation reactions have dominated these fields. However, hydroboration has introduced a transformative approach due to its inherent advantages, including low toxicity, high reactivity, and excellent selectivity. Hydroboration has emerged as a sophisticated alternative to conventional hydrogenation, with mild boron reagents such as pinacolborane (HBpin) and catecholborane (HBcat) replacing the more reactive 9-BBN in the reduction of unsaturated bonds4–6.

Among recent advancements, the hydroboration of esters has attracted significant attention due to its industrial importance7–9. The conversion of esters into alcohols is a pivotal transformation with wide-ranging applications, particularly in the pharmaceutical industry. This highlights the need for efficient and sustainable methodologies10–12.

In the past decade, significant efforts have been devoted to developing catalysts based on earth-abundant metals for the hydroboration of unsaturated bonds. Studies have demonstrated the use of boron reagents in conjunction with s-block, p-block, transition, and lanthanide metals to facilitate the hydroboration of ester carbonyl groups13–30. Actinide metals, however, stand out due to their unique characteristics, including large ionic radii and the presence of 5f orbitals, which enable distinctive regio- and chemo-selective catalytic behavior31–37. These properties make actinides unique in catalysis, differentiating them from transition, main-group, and lanthanide metal catalysts38–40.

Over the last few years, our group has comprehensively researched the hydroboration of C=X (X = heteroatom) groups using actinide-based catalysts; in this respect, the hydroboration of oxygen-containing substrates (X=O) has been tackled41–46. The idea that we can form an actinide-hydride active catalyst from An–N or An–O (An=Th, U) bonds and consequently regenerate it as a part of a catalytic cycle was thoroughly investigated (equations 1 and 2). Moreover, in a recent report, we disclosed the catalytic activity of organoactinides in the hydroboration of the carbonate group, demonstrating that organoactinides with N-heterocyclic iminato ligands possess an excellent ability to tune the catalytic properties of actinide complexes48. This arises from the ligand’s capability to contribute up to six electrons in a 2σ, 4π manner to the highly electrophilic metals49,50. Consequently, they can stabilize high oxidation states, facilitating the formation of actinide metal-based complexes. While the majority of the N-heterocyclic iminato ligands are based on five-membered-ring systems, such as imidazolin-2-iminato and benzimidazolin-2-iminato systems51–55, a recent report also showed that expanding the ring size from five to six and seven-membered ring ligands substituted with the mesityl group in Th(IV) complexes containing the N-heterocyclic iminato ligands significantly affects their catalytic reactivity (Scheme 1b)48.

Thus, we intended to compare the structure and reactivity of a series of six-membered ligands by removing the unsaturation from the perimidine (iPr) ligand backbone, thereby concentrating electron donation from the six-membered core into the exocyclic nitrogen (Scheme 1c)56. In many cases, the observed reactivity of actinide N-heterocyclic imine complexes exhibits somewhat better reactivity as the coordination sphere becomes more open57. However, a delicate balance must be maintained between the opening of the coordination sphere around the metal center for substrate coordination and the ligand’s steric ability to protect the metal from oversaturation and catalytic activity. Hence, we aimed to synthesize six-membered ligands analogous to the known six-membered perimidine-based ligands, featuring smaller substituents, such as the isopropyl group, and direct the study toward the hydroboration of esters.

Results and discussion

Ligand synthesis

The perimidine ligand with the isopropyl moiety (iPr) was synthesized according to previously reported procedures56. The synthesis of the neutral ligand L1-H begins by following the literature methods to obtain 2-(isopropylamino)benzaldehyde58,59. Condensation of the aldehyde with isopropyl amine in ethanol yields the Schiff base (imine 1, Scheme 2) in high yields as a yellowish solid that precipitates from the reaction mixture. Further, reducing the imine bond with sodium borohydride allows the formation of the diamine 1 compound in 72% yield after its purification on a neutral alumina column. Treatment of the diamine with cyanogen bromide in boiling toluene for 24 h under nitrogen results in the precipitation of the cyclized product as the hydrobromide salt (L1-HBr), which was neutralized with a basic potassium hydroxide solution. The free ligand is extracted with diethyl ether, and after evaporating the solution, the L1-H ligand is obtained (Scheme 2).Scheme 2Synthesis of 6-membered N-heterocyclic ligands. The X-ray structural analysis of the complexes revealed that the central An(IV) is in a distorted tetrahedral geometry consisting of one N-heterocyclic iminato ligand and three N(SiMe3)2 groups. The ligand LH binds with the metal in a monodentate fashion, and the remaining three positions are occupied by amido ligands, completing the tetrahedral environment. The An−NC=N bond distances are on average 0.2 Å shorter than the An−N(amido) bonds in the same complexes, with values of 2.184(4), 2.213(2), 2.142(4) Å for the thorium complex 1, 3, and 5 and 2.130(4), 2.146(2) and 2.083(4) Å for the uranium complexes 2, 4, and 6, respectively. The uranium An−N(1) bond distances are on average 0.05 Å shorter than those of the thorium complexes, which corresponds to the 0.05 Å difference in the ionic radius between the two metals. Further, the resulting An−N(1)−C(1) angles are slightly distorted from linearity, with values of 167.5(3), 157.7(2), and 169.6(4)°, for the thorium complex 1, 3, and 5 and 169.1(4), 159.41(19), and 168.7(4)°, for the uranium complexes 2, 4, and 6, respectively. The close to linear angle and the shorter An−NC=N bond distances indicate that the six-membered N-heterocyclic iminato ligands are attached to the metal as a metallacumulene, with a substantial π donation from the ligand to the metal, generating a higher bond order between the metal and the ligand imine nitrogen.

All six complexes display similar steric properties, with cone angle values of 122°, 123°, 131°, 133°, 125°, and 127° for complexes 1–6, respectively. These values are closely related to those of the corresponding six-membered perimidine actinide complexes, which have cone angles of 122–125° and 123–127° for the corresponding thorium and uranium complexes, respectively.

Interestingly, we could not observe a substantial structural effect by removing the unsaturation from the backbone, as no difference in the C–N or N–An bond lengths was observed. For instance, the thorium and uranium complexes L3-An[N(SiMe3)2]3 with the new saturated backbone ligand has the An−N(1) bond distances of 2.142(1) Å (5) and 2.083(4) Å (6), respectively, which are similar to those reported for the (perimidineiPrN)An[N(SiMe3)2]3 analogs with the extended aromatic system on the backbone with values of 2.190(5) Å and 2.128(4) Å, for thorium and uranium, respectively.

It is important to clarify the influence of the aromatic ring on the backbone of six-membered ring ligands. The presence of the aromatic ring affects only the structural arrangement of the six-membered core structure. For example, permidine-based ligands exhibit distortion out of the plane of the exocyclic nitrogen, whereas complex 1–4, with one aromatic ring removed from the backbone, displays distortion of the six-membered ring through the benzylic position. Furthermore, the fully saturated ligands show an in-plane arrangement of the three nitrogen units, while the backbone is distorted to an envelope conformation in complexes 5–6. These structural issues are expected to play a significant role in the reactivity of these complexes.

4-chlorobenzyl benzoate was completely hydroborated in 8 h with the thorium catalyst 5, exhibiting a shorter reaction time than that of methyl benzoate (entries 2g and 2a, Table 2). However, for the uranium catalyst 6, no significant change in yields was observed for either substrate, yielding 82% after 24 h. Furthermore, complexes 5 and 6 showed complete selectivity toward the ester group functionality in the cases of methyl cinnamate and propargyl benzoate (entries 2h and 2i, Table 2), leaving the double and triple C–C unsaturated bonds intact.

Ethyl acetate and ethyl 4-benzyl acetate were hydroborated fast in less than 3 h using the thorium catalyst 5, while the uranium catalyst 6 reached 67% and 99% after 24 h, respectively (entries 2j, k, Table 2). Increasing the bulkiness of the ester’s R substituent (entry 2l, Table 2) caused a longer reaction time to reach complete conversion, with 12 h for the thorium catalyst 5, and only a conversion of 47% for the uranium catalyst 6 after 24 h. When comparing the steric bulk from both sides of the ester (R vs OR′), the substitution of the OR′ with tert butyl as in the case of tert butyl acetate, caused both catalysts to sluggishly operate reaching 38 and 29% percent after 24 h of the reaction for the complexes 5 and 6, respectively (entry 2m, Table 2). Thus, by comparing the effect of steric bulkiness from both sides of the ester (R vs OR′), a substitution of the OR′ moiety with a bulkier group reduces the reactivity of the actinide complexes, mainly affecting the most reactive complex 5.

Surprisingly, benzyl acetate was hydroborated quantitatively (>99%) with both catalysts (5 and 6) in 2 h (entry 2n, Table 2). Moving from benzyl acetate to phenyl acetate caused both catalysts to show reduced yields, 46% and 62% in 24 h for complexes 5 and 6, respectively (entry 2o, Table 2), suggesting that the better leaving group on the ester causes a slower rate during the catalytic cycle, as it forms a less reactive metal-alkoxo moiety An–OR′, which reacts slowly in a sigma bond fashion with an incoming HBpin molecule (vide infra).

Interestingly, increasing the electrophilic character of the carbonyl carbon of phenyl acetate by placing a strong EWG group, –CF3, adjacent to it resulted in complete conversion (>99%) after 24 h using complex 5, and 16 h using complex 6 (entry 2p, Table 2). These results suggest that increasing the electrophilicity of the carbonyl promotes more efficient hydride transfer to facilitate the reaction. In addition, cyclic esters were also tested, showing complete conversion in short reaction times, producing the borylated open-ring alcohol precursors, in 1 and 2 h for catalysts 5 and 6, respectively (entries 2q and 2r, Table 2). The catalytic reaction of the cyclic diester rac-lactide (2 s) produced the boronated diol in 2 and 4 h with complexes 5 or 6, respectively, whereas for the linear diester dimethyl adipate (2t), quantitative yields were observed for complex 5 in only 3 h and for complex 6 in 24 h. In the case of dimethyl terephthalate and tri-esters such as glyceryl triacetate and glyceryl tribenzoate, 24 h were needed to reach complete conversion for both complexes (entries 2u–2w, Table 2).

Mechanistic studies

The evolution of the product with time from the hydroboration of 4MePhCOOMe with the catalysts 5 displays a sigmoidal curve (Fig. 2a)63–65.Fig. 2Product formation as a function of time.Reaction profile in the hydroboration of 4MePhCOOMe and ethyl p-tolylacetate with HBpin catalyzed by 2.0 mol % of complex 5.

The product formation in the first hour of the reaction is negligible (less than 10%) and rises sharply in the next five hours, reaching an 80% conversion. The product then gradually increases toward a quantitative conversion. Interestingly, the induction period, during which the product appears, varies between different types of esters. For instance, for substituted methyl aryl benzoates (Fig. S92). For methyl p-toluate the induction period takes 22 min, while for ethyl p-tolylacetate, it takes almost 50 min until the product is observed (Fig. 2). This induction period suggests that the initial substrate binding to the catalyst induces changes in the complex structure, which are maintained until the active species of the catalyst is formed gradually, accompanied by an increase in the rate of product formation, resulting in the sigmoidal curve.

We investigated the catalyst effect on the induction time by increasing the catalyst loading from 2.0 to 4.0 mol% on the hydroboration of the 4MePhCOOMe reaction profile (Fig. 3a). Increasing the catalyst loading resulted in an overall increase in the rate of product formation while shortening the induction time. This indicates that the active catalyst is transformed into the reactive species to a larger extent, thereby boosting the reaction rate.Fig. 3Reaction Profile.Effect of increasing concentration of a catalyst 5, b 4MePhCOOMe, ester, c HBpin, on the reaction profile in the hydroboration of 4MePhCOOMe.

It was also interesting to investigate the concentration effect of the substrate and the HBpin on the induction time. We observed that doubling the amount of ester reduces the induction time, speeding up the transformation of the catalyst into the active species (Fig. 3b). In contrast, doubling the amount of HBpin resulted in only a slight retardation in the formation of the product and a light elongation in the induction time (Fig. 3c).

To gain more insight into the reaction mechanism, a set of stoichiometric reactions was carried out using the catalyst 5 with HBpin and the 4MePhCOOMe ester. The stoichiometric reaction of the thorium catalyst 5 with three equivalents of HBpin does not produce any change in the catalyst at room temperature, as observed by 1H NMR. Heating the same reaction mixture for 8 h at 70 °C results in the formation of some new signals that arise from the decomposition of the catalyst. A small septet is observed at 4.93 ppm with a doublet at 0.95 ppm for the isopropyl group of the ligand. This small fraction of the ligand is coordinated to the Bpin moiety, with the pinacol methyl portion found as a singlet at 1.24 ppm. Furthermore, multiple small signals arise in the region between 0.0 and 0.6 ppm, which may suggest decomposition of the Th-N(SiMe3)2 moiety, with a signal at 0.08 ppm for (SiMe3)2N−H. However, most of the catalysts maintained their integrity (Fig. S94). The addition of one equivalent of the ester and heating for 5 h at 70 °C produced the hydroborated alcohols (Fig. S95), suggesting that the remaining catalyst is still active. The 11B NMR confirms the formation of a typical N-Bpin signal at 25.2 ppm, which arises from the boronation of the ligand, L-Bpin, an unreacted HBpin signal at 28.50 ppm, and the RO-Bpin at 22 ppm, which belongs to the hydroboration of the ester product. In addition, a new small signal was observed at 33.7 ppm, in the alkyl-Bpin region, which arises from the Th-N(SiMe3)2 moiety decomposition with HBpin and the formation of Me-Bpin (Fig. S96). Treating catalyst 5 with one equivalent of ester (4MePhCOOMe) for one hour at 70 °C does not produce any change in the catalyst as well (Fig. S97). The addition of HBpin to this mixture and heating at 70 °C for up to 5 h, results in the gradual formation of the boronated alcohol products, 4MePhCH2O-Bpin and MeO-Bpin, with typical signals at δ 7.20 (d, J = 7.9 Hz, 2H), 6.93 (d, J = 7.9 Hz, 2H), 4.90 (s, 2H), 3.48 (s, 3H, MeO-B), 2.09 (s, 3H), 1.04 (s, 12H), 1.03 (s, 12H). L-Bpin and (SiMe3)2N−H formation was also observed (Fig. S98). The stacked 11B spectra were recorded at different stages of the reaction for 5 h and support the gradual formation of the RO-Bpin products, with a signal at 22.5 ppm, which rises with time, while only in a late stage of the reaction, the L-Bpin signal at 25 ppm could be observed (Fig. S99).

The small fraction of formed L-Bpin suggests the formation of [(SiMe3)2N]3Th-H species, which could operate as a catalytically active species in this hydroboration process. Furthermore, it can explain the induction period observed in the reaction profile. In addition, the formation of the L-Bpin moiety is accompanied by the appearance of other signals in the region between 0 and 1 ppm, which may suggest that the Th-hydride is very active, and side reactions take place, which tamper with our attempts to trap the actual operative catalyst. Th-hydride formation from iminato thorium complex with HBpin was previously suggested as the main active species during catalytic hydroboration reactions (Scheme 7)48. However, the typical signal for (SiMe3)2N-Bpin (two singlets at 1.04 and 0.36 ppm), which is the product of a sigma bond metathesis reaction of the thorium iminato complexes with HBpin, was not observed in this study. Interestingly, we did not observe a hemiacetal moiety as observed by Marks and coworkers in their research with La(N(SiMe3)2)3, showing that the latter can bind to an ester molecule, avoiding the direct formation of La-H; however, it could not be excluded66. Similarly, Zhu et al. presented a similar case with a series of lanthanide triariloxide complexes, which were shown to be active in ketones and aldehydes through coordination to the metal center, thereby avoiding the formation of Ln–H bonds68. Considering the higher oxidation state of our catalyst (+4), we can assume a stronger interaction between the actinide metal and the carbonyl group of the ester during the formation of the active species. This interaction may persist throughout the reaction; however, it cannot account for the induction period.

For the complex L-An(N(SiMe3)2)3, we suggest that the mechanism starts from the formation of the active species, an actinide-hydride complex A, from the reaction of the starting complex with HBpin, producing L-Bpin. As observed in the stoichiometric reactions44,48. The reaction is slow, accounting for the sigmoidal kinetics. The hydride complex A reacts with the incoming ester to form an actinide-hemiacetal intermediate B, which was previously suggested as a plausible intermediate in the ester hydroboration process using actinide catalysts47. Cleavage of the ester C−O bond takes place with an incoming HBpin molecule, resulting in the formation of the intermediate An−OCH2R (C) and RO-Bpin moiety as the rate-limiting step. Conclusions

This work aimed to introduce a new six-membered iminato ligand by removing unsaturation from the ligand’s backbone, and subsequently led to successful complexation with actinide metals, forming actinide-iminato complexes. The catalytic properties of the resulting complexes were tested in the hydroboration of esters and compared to previous work performed in our group with the actinide metallacycles (a and b). We found that the new catalysts showed an induction time, which wasn’t encountered in other studies related to any hydroboration using actinide complexes. We overcame the induction period by starting with the corresponding alkoxo complex, which increased the reaction rate.

Typical NMR-scale reaction procedure for the catalytic hydroboration of esters to the hydroborated alcohols

Into a J. Young Teflon-sealed NMR tube, ester (0.2 mmol, 1.0 equiv) and HBpin (0.46 mmol, 2.3 equiv) were added, followed by the addition of a catalyst from a freshly prepared stock solution (4.0 μmol, 2,0 mol% or 10.0 μmol, 5.0%) in C6D6. The reaction mixture was diluted to 550 μL with C6D6, sealed, and placed into an oil bath preheated to 70 °C. The crude reaction mixtures were analyzed using 1H, 13C{1H}, and 11B NMR; the values of known compounds were compared to those previously reported in the literature. The yield was calculated from the ratio of hydroboration product to starting material from the crude ¹H NMR spectra, or by comparison with the signal of an internal standard (1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene).
