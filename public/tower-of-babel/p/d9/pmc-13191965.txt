Women’s reported experience of respectful maternity care measures worldwide: a systematic review
Authors: Ephrem Yohannes, Gonfa Moti, Eshetu E Chaka, Laura Gabriel, Debra K Creedy, Carolyn Hastie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeasuring women’s experiences of Respectful Maternity Care (RMC) requires valid and reliable tools. Although several RMC tools exist, their methodological quality has not been thoroughly evaluated.AimTo evaluate the quality of women’s-reported experience of RMC measures worldwide.MethodsA comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and CINAHL databases was conducted. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (reference number CRD42024585853). The review followed the PRISMA guideline and employed the PICO framework. Peer-reviewed English-language studies published between 2014 and 2025 were included. The search targeted articles on Women Reported Experience Measures (WREMs) of RMC, with at least one psychometric property. Data were presented using Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments’ (COSMIN) method. Tools were evaluated for methodological bias, item development, study quality, measurement properties, evidence synthesis, and certainty grading using the COSMIN.ResultsThis review included 47 articles on the creation and psychometric assessment of 22 measures. Evidence indicating the scale development of each tool was inadequate. The risk of bias evaluation revealed inadequate methodological quality. Internal consistency and test-retest reliability were insufficient or not undertaken. Many measures were deemed inadequate in terms of criterion, cross-cultural, and structural validity. Responsiveness and measurement errors were not reported. Currently, there is no level A recommended scale for measuring RMC.ConclusionThis review provided a comprehensive evaluation of the available tools to measure RMC. The quality of measurement properties of WREMs of RMC was inadequate. Future studies should develop and validate a standardised tool to measure women-reported experiences of RMC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27157-y.Keywords: Reliability, Respectful maternity care, Women-reported experience measure, Scales, Tools and Validity

Introduction

Women’s experiences of maternity care are complex and varied. Evidence shows that women who give birth in healthcare facilities in low and middle income countries frequently report mistreatment, including verbal and physical abuse, neglect, discrimination, non-consented care, and breaches of privacy [1, 2]. Such practices represent violations of human rights and discourage women from seeking facility-based care during future pregnancies [3]. In addition, mistreatment has serious health implications, contributing to maternal complications, psychological trauma, postnatal depression, and adverse fetal and neonatal outcomes [2, 3].

Growing evidence of disrespect and abuse in health care facilities has brought global attention to the need for RMC [4]. A study conducted in Ethiopia, Kenya, Nigeria, and Tanzania by Sando et al. revealed varying prevalence rates of disrespect and abuse ranging from 15% to 98% [5]. These divergent results suggest that methodological differences and systematic errors related to operational definitions, measures used, participant selection, data collection mode, timing, and setting may have contributed to discrepancies [5]. Researchers have highlighted that involving women in developing and validating instruments was essential for meaningful, value-based measurement in maternity care [6].

The work of Bowser and Hill [7] was pivotal in advancing the visibility of maltreatment of women during childbirth into policy. Subsequently, the RMC Charter [8, 9] and the WHO’s statement on the “Prevention and Elimination of Disrespect and Abuse during Facility-based Childbirth (2014) [10] were developed. A study on mistreatment in the context of facility-based care by Bohren et al. [11] expanded the typology of Bowser and Hill [7] and shed light on health system deficits. Other studies [12–15] have employed the mistreatment terms proposed by Bohren et al. [7] which include physical, sexual, and verbal abuse; stigma and discrimination; failure to meet professional standards of care; poor rapport between women and providers; and health system conditions and constraints [11].

Measuring RMC has significant potential for enhancing maternity care providers’ knowledge, skills, attitudes, and practices and ultimately improving women’s experiences and outcomes [16]. Women Reported Experience Measures (WREMs) are valuable tools for capturing women’s perspectives on maternity care [17]. WREMs capture the service user’s perspective, enhance women-centred care, and drive improvements in healthcare delivery [18]. WREMs assess both the content and experience of care [19], offering insights into whether services are respectful and aligned with women’s preferences, hopes, and expectations [17]. To be effective, WREMs must be carefully developed and adapted to the specific context in which they are use [17].

Researchers have attempted to develop reliable tools to capture women’s experiences of care received during childbirth. In a systematic review by Cantor et al., twenty-four studies (three observational and twenty-one cross-sectional designs) evaluated 12 tools measuring RMC [20]. However, only two of the 12 tools were validated for measuring women’s childbirth experiences in low-resource countries and exhibited fair psychometric properties [20]. These scales are the RMC Women’s Perception Scale validated in Ethiopia [21] and the Person-Centred Maternity Care (PCMC) scale validated in Ghana, India, and Kenya [22–24]. These tools provide valuable indicators for evaluating interventions to improve women’s childbirth experience [22–24]. A systematic review by Dhakal et al. identified six tools designed to measure RMC in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [25]. These authors revealed that existing tools were of poor quality, not specific to the LMICs context, and none had been assessed for measurement error, cross-cultural validity, or responsiveness [25].

Studies developing standards for RMC are currently underway, and a variety of validation studies, programs, and approaches to measure RMC are emerging [8, 20, 26–29]. Nevertheless, these tools face challenges in terms of fully capturing respectful and dignified care [28–30]. They have been criticised for their lack of consensus on best practices for measurement and for using various definitions of RMC [11, 29, 31]. Good-quality tools are essential for developing context-appropriate care guidelines for respectful care [4], as differences in healthcare systems and geographical variations have implications for measuring RMC [20, 31, 32]. Recognising the themes, domains, and key principles of RMC can help researchers and service providers to be aware of target metrics for assessment, as there is no single standardised tool for identifying or measuring RMC [28]. There are also concerns that the use of poor data collection methods may lead to underestimations of the true prevalence of disrespectful care [5, 33]. The recent updates of the core principles of RMC highlight the need to develop or validate standardised scales that comprehensively capture these key principles [16, 34].The questions guiding this review are: (1) What is the quality of existing tools used to measure women’s experiences of RMC? (2) Which tool(s) can be recommended to measure women’s experiences of RMC?

Methods

This review followed the International Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [35] (details are presented in Supplementary Data 1). The protocol was registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024585853). Consensus-based standards for the selection of health measurement instruments, namely the COSMIN framework quality appraisal method [36], was employed in this review. Although this approach was initially designed to assess women’s outcome measures, it has been used to evaluate women’s experience measures in multiple studies [17, 20]. The guideline was deemed suitable and enhanced precision in the current review.

Search strategy

The literature search was conducted using electronic databases such as CINAHL, Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, Embase, and Google Scholar. The combination of key search terms such as “respectful-maternity care” OR “disrespectful-maternity care” AND “WREMs” OR “scale” OR “measurement” OR “tool” OR “questionnaire development” OR “measure” OR “survey items” OR “questionnaire” AND “measurement properties” OR “reliability” OR “internal consistency” OR “test-retest reliability” OR “validity” OR “psychometric properties” OR “content validity” OR “structural validity” OR “construct validity” OR “convergent validity” OR “discriminant validity” OR “divergent validity” OR “criterion validity” OR “hypothesis testing” OR “measurement error” OR “cross-cultural validity” OR “responsiveness” were used to search for articles. Medical subject headings (MeSH), texts, and keywords were also employed to identify relevant articles. The Boolean operators “AND” and “OR” were used to combine the search terms. A detailed search strategy for the PubMed database is presented in Supplementary Data 2. Keywords were coupled with a verified search filter to determine the psychometric qualities of the scale. A manual search of reference records was performed to identify other articles.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This review examined studies that investigated women’s experiences of RMC during perinatal period and reported at least one psychometric property of the tool or scale. The inclusion of tools with documented psychometric properties ensures attempts by those authors to establish reliability and validity. This methodology facilitates the identification of instruments that are both conceptually significant and empirically robust in assessing women’s experiences with RMC. Studies that did not address the scale development process, reliability, validity, or evaluation of other psychometric properties of the WREMs were excluded. Articles were required to be original research published between January 1, 2014, and November 13, 2025, in a peer-reviewed English-language journal.

This systematic review used the PICO framework for eligibility criteria [37]. The interventions were measures of women-reported RMC experiences. The outcome was the quality of a tool used to assess women’s experience of RCM during their perinatal care. Conference abstracts, protocols, editorial comments, modelling studies, systematic reviews, umbrella reviews, scoping reviews, interrogative reviews, review of reviews, and narrative reviews were excluded. Although review articles were excluded, their reference lists were examined to identify additional relevant studies. WREMs initially created in a context other than RMC and not self-reported by women, and those that measured satisfaction or expectations were also excluded.

Study selection

Articles were imported to Endnote and then uploaded into the data screening software tool, Covidence [38], where duplicates and irrelevant articles were eliminated. Two independent assessors screened the titles and abstracts of the studies using inclusion criteria. In cases where they had opposing perspectives, a third reviewer was consulted to achieve a consensus. Two team members examined the complete texts of potential papers for inclusion. Any differences were addressed by consensus among the researchers.

Data extraction

Two researchers independently extracted the findings from the studies via a structured format on a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. The data summary consisted of study details (principal investigator name, publication year, study design, sample size, eligibility criteria, study setting, and country) as well as a scale description (name of the measure, domains/constructs/subscales, item number, response options, score range, language, recall time, and measurement properties). A summary of the extracted data is provided in Supplementary Data 3. The modified Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used to assess the risk of bias (i.e., methodological quality of studies) [39]. All extracted data were double-checked by a team member.

Methodological risk of bias assessment

Study quality was evaluated using the COSMIN Risk of Bias checklist and the updated criteria for measurement properties [6, 40]. This checklist comprises 10 items for the development of women-reported experience measures (WREMs) and several items for psychometric properties of validity (content, construct, cross-cultural validity or measurement invariance, criterion validity, hypothesis testing), reliability (internal consistency, test-retest reliability, measurement error), and responsiveness. Individual items within each psychometric property were rated using a 4-point grading method (very good, adequate, doubtful, and inadequate) based on the items listed in the COSMIN manual [6]. In accordance with the COSMIN guidelines, the “worst score counts” assumption was employed (i.e., if a construct containing eight criteria results “very good” for seven of them but “inadequate” for one), the overall construct rate would be deemed “inadequate”). Psychometric properties were evaluated based on measurement tests performed and reported during the development and validation of each tool. Studies were classified as “low,” “moderate” or “high” high risk of bias. Articles with a distinguished risk of bias were not automatically disqualified, but were deemed to be less dependable than those with a low or moderate risk of bias when the results were combined [6, 40]. Details are presented in Supplementary Data 3.

Quality appraisal of content validity and WREM development

Content validity is an essential psychometric element of WREM. This is the degree to which a tool accurately represents the desired phenomenon from the user’s perspective [41]. Content validity emphasises the importance of respecting centricity in the creation and testing of WREMs. Included studies were assessed for relevance (five items), comprehensiveness (one item), and comprehension (four items) [41]. Relevance pertained to the significance of respect for centricity in WREM development and content validation. Comprehensiveness was assessed to ensure that all the key concepts were included. Comprehensibility was assessed by inquiring whether childbearing women could comprehend the instrument’s instructions, items, and response options as intended. The review team examined whether the items were appropriately written and whether response options matched the questions. Scores could range from ‘sufficient (+) to insufficient (−), inconsistent (±), or indeterminate evidence (?). Results were then compiled to formulate the total content validity grade for each WREM. A detailed explanation of the scoring process is provided in Supplementary Data 3.

Evaluating the psychometric properties of WREMs

The COSMIN-modified criteria for quality psychometric measurements were used to assess the validity and reliability of each tool [36]. The assessment included examining validity (content, construct, cross-cultural or measurement invariance, criterion validity, hypothesis testing), reliability (internal consistency, test-retest reliability, measurement error), and responsiveness against specific criteria outlined in the COSMIN manual. The criteria were then scored as adequate evidence (+), inadequate evidence (−), or indeterminate evidence (?).

Operational definitions

ReliabilityExtent to which scores remain consistent for respondents who have not changed. It includes internal consistency (different items from the same WREM), test–retest reliability (over time), inter-rater reliability (different raters on the same occasion), and intra-rater reliability (the same rater on different occasions) [40].Hypotheses testingAssesses whether a WREM’s scores align with expected relationships to other measures or group differences, assuming it accurately reflects the intended construct [40].Cross-cultural validityExtent to which a translated or culturally adapted WREM reflects the performance of items in the original version [36].Measurement errorSystematic and random error in a person’s score that is not due to true changes in the construct being measured [36].Structural validityStructural validity is the extent to which a WREM’s scores accurately reflect the dimensionality of the construct being measured [40].ResponsivenessAbility of a WREM to detect changes over time in the construct being measured [36].Criterion validityExtent to which a scale’s scores accurately reflect a recognised gold standard [40].

Summary and grading of the quality of evidence

The COSMIN manual outlines the process of synthesising and rating the evidence quality using the GRADE system [36]. We assessed the quality of evidence using the COSMIN GRADE approach, which examines four key factors: (1) risk of bias, reflecting the methodological quality of the included studies; (2) inconsistency, indicating unexplained differences in results across studies; (3) imprecision, relating to the total sample size not considered in the risk of bias assessment; and (4) indirectness, referring to evidence drawn from populations that differ from those targeted in the review [6, 40]. Although publication bias is considered the fifth criterion, the COSMIN manual notes that it is difficult to assess in studies of measurement properties due to the absence of registries for such studies. Consequently, this factor was not included in our assessment methodology [6, 40]. A high level of evidence signifies a high level of certainty in the results, whereas “moderate” indicates a modest level of confidence, “low” indicates inadequate confidence, and “very low” indicates negligible assurance [36]. The scores are shown in Supplementary Data 3.

Formulating recommendations for WREMs

The final stage involved recommending the most effective WREM(s) for maternity care practice, monitoring RMCs, and conducting further research. WREMs classified as “A” are recommended because of their adequate content validity, internal consistency scores and other psychometric properties [36]. WREMs categorised as “B” are likely to be suggested but require further evaluation because they do not meet the criteria for “A” [36]. WREMs graded “C” are not recommended for use, because evidence suggests at least two unsatisfactory measurement qualities [36].

Results

Selected articles

A total of 10,047 records were identified (99,77 from electronic databases and 70 from websites and citation searches). After removing duplicates (n = 6,761) and excluding studies (n = 3,101) that did not explicitly describe RMC measurements, 185 articles remained. Among the 185 full-text articles reviewed, 138 were excluded for reasons such as incorrect study design (n = 29), setting (n = 27), year of publication (n = 22), population (n = 26) and outcome (n = 34). Finally 47 studies were included in this review. Details are shown in Fig. 1.

Validated tools utilised to measure RMC

Across 47 studies, researchers validated 22 tools designed specifically to measure respectful maternity care. There is only one tool developed and validated in a low-resource setting [21]. This 15-item scale has four domains: friendly care, abuse-free care, timely care, and discrimination-free care, with each component rated on a 5-point Likert scale. Cronbach’s alpha for the total scale was 0.86 in Ethiopia and Iran [21, 42]. Other tools were developed and validated in the US [43], Iran [44], Australia [45], Canada [46], Netherlands [47, 48], India [23, 24], Kenya [22, 24], Ghana [49], Palestine [50], Spain and etc [51, 52].

Certain tools, such as the intrapartum version of the “Mother’s Autonomy in Decision-Making” (MADM) and “Mothers On Respect Index” (MORi), RMC perception of women scale [21, 42], as well as the “Childbirth Options, Information, and Respectful Maternity Care scale” (CHOICES), have exhibited moderate overall validity and are applicable to low-resource countries. The person-centered maternity care scale (PCMC) has shown moderate overall validity in evaluating childbirth women’s experiences and encompasses some RMC components [22]. Risk of methodological bias

The risk of bias ratings are illustrated in Supplementary Data 3. Scores varied from “inadequate” to “very good.” No study obtained “adequate” or “very good” evidence of low risk of bias across all measurement properties. No WREM assessed measurement error and responsiveness. The methodological risk of bias ratings for most WREM creation and content validity were “inadequate” (lowest rate). A few studies scored “doubtful” (second to lowest score) [22, 49, 68], demonstrating inadequate quality and a high risk of bias. Most studies scored very well on structural validity and internal consistency assessments [21, 69, 79]. The risk of bias grade for all psychometric features of included instruments indicated weak quality and a significant risk of bias.

Synthesis of psychometric properties results

Content validity

A summary of respect-centricity or content validity evidence for each WREMs was presented in Table 2. The Childbirth Abuse and Respect Evaluation-Maternal Questionnaire (CARE-MP) [52], CHOICES [43] and ReproQ-Dutch [73, 74, 84] demonstrated moderate overall quality of content validity, indicating that women reported the content to be significant, complete, and clear. Five WREMs did not report content validity [23, 24, 47–49], and six tools [42, 50, 51, 54, 59, 66, 72] reported insufficient content validity. Professionals usually assessed the relevance of a scale [21, 22, 43–46, 51, 56, 59–61]. Similarly, communication experts assessed comprehensibility for readability and language clarity [43, 44, 46, 48, 51, 59, 60]. Comprehensiveness was assessed by asking women [43, 45, 60], professionals [21, 22, 49, 59], or both [42, 56].

Structural or construct validity

Several studies used only ‘Exploratory Factor Analysis’ (EFA) [21, 22, 46, 56, 60, 61], other tools utlised both EFA and ‘Confirmatory Factor Analysis’ approaches (CFA) [23, 24, 42, 44, 51, 52], and other WREMs assesed CFA alone [59, 71]. There was no evidence of structural validity in the RMCS-Hindi [54], MORI and MADM-Dutch [48], MADM, MORi and CEQ 2.0-Dutch [47], MORi and MADM-English [45], MLWC [72] and Mother-Centered Prenatal Care (MCPC) [69] scales. The construct validity quality of most tools was insufficient.

Criterion validity

The main scale of the PCMC-30 [22] and subscales (PCMC-13 scale) were associated with global measures of women’s satisfaction with maternity care scale, implying moderate criterion validity [24]. The CHOICE, MORi, and MADM scales showed significant positive correlations, confirming moderate criterion-related validity [43]. The RMC scale was associated with the Global Satisfaction Measures Scale [21]. The PCMC-United States scale, MORi, and MADM scale scores all showed significant correlations with a PCMC subscale with MADM having a coefficient of 0.69 and MORi having a coefficient of 0.63 [61]. An Australian study revealed a significant positive correlation between MORi and MADM scale scores [45]. Except for the PCMC scale [24, 61], all tools lacked sufficient criterion validity quality due to no available gold-standard comparison scale.

Reliability

Internal consistency

Most studies, MORi and MADM-English [45], RMCS-Hindi [54], MLWC [72], Kh-PCMC-1.1 [66] and MCPC survey [69] did not assess internal consistency. Cronbach’s alpha of many tools exceeded 0.90 [43, 44, 46, 48, 49, 52, 61]. Several tools reported Cronbach’s alpha values greater than or equal to 0.70 for all subscales [24, 51, 59, 60]. Despite an overall Cronbach’s alpha value of ≥ 0.70 in some studies, none of the subscales met this criterion [21–23, 42, 45, 50, 56]. Overall most tools had an adequate quality of evidence.

Test-retest reliability

Test-retest reliability was reported in studies such as CARE-MP [52], RMC-Persian [42], RMC-Farsi [53], Respectful Maternity Care Questionnaire (RMCQ) [59], Women’s Perception of Respectful Maternity Care (WP-RMC) [60], DAQ-Faris [44] and ReproQ-Dutch [73, 74, 84]. However, these tools used inappropriate statistical approaches, and the quality of evidence was very low.

Hypothesis testing for construct validity

Several studies tested a hypothesis for convergent validity [21, 23, 24, 43, 48]. One study hypothesised that PCMC-27 was linked to future intention to give birth at the same health facility [23]. The differential item functioning (DIF) analysis of CHOICE revealed no significant difference in the measure across racial, age, and parity groups [43]. The RMC scale in Ghana showed that respondents without formal education rated their experiences of RMC positively compared to those with some formal education [49]. Other studies showed convergent validity was confirmed by analysing the relationship between specific variables and the scale score for known-group validation [48, 51, 52].

Cross-cultural validity

Cross-cultural validity was assessed in WREMs like CHOICES [43], PCMC-Chinese [62], Kh-PCMC-1.1 [66] and Kh-PCMC-1.0 [67]. However, no WREM assessed measurement error and responsiveness.

Table 3 presents a comprehensive summary of the psychometric properties of each WREM, along with their recommended level of use. The most frequently assessed properties were content validity, structural validity, internal consistency, and criterion validity. Thirty-nine WREMs received a level “B” recommendation due to suboptimal validity and reliability, and five were rated “C” for not meeting recommended usage criteria. Based on the COSMIN assessment, no tools achieved a level “A” rating or were considered fully recommended for use.

Discussion

It is critical to measure women’s experiences of RMC based on what they value. This review identified 22 WREMs of RMCs, assessed in 47 studies [21–24, 42–84]. Only one tool was developed and validated in a low-resource country [21]. These tools generally incorporated themes such as abuse-free care, privacy, dignity, respect, autonomy, communication, and shared decision making. However, these tools did not address issues of culturally appropriate care, inclusiveness, collaborative care, or safety while providing RMC. Supporting this finding, a systematic review conducted by Cantor et al. revealed that most WREMs lack depth for measuring RMC [20].

Most studies in the present review reported content validity in terms of relevance, comprehensibility, and comprehensiveness. Content sufficiency is essential if items assess what they are supposed to evaluate [85]. Furthermore, content validity stipulates significance and representation, suggesting that the items reflect the relevant experience of the target audience under evaluation [86]. The quality of content validity of the tools included in this review was inadequate because of the lack of detailed assessment and inadequate explanation of how women were involved in scale development. Notably, several women-reported measure reviews reported a lack of content validity [17, 87]. The exclusion of women in the content validation processes in WREMs may lead to inaccurate conclusions and hinder the implementation of value-based maternity care [87]. The absence of content validity in measurement properties can contribute to decreased internal consistency reliability, inaccurate factor structures, and diminished instrument interpretability [41]. Tools that include unrelated or inappropriate items can lead to biased answers and poor response rates [41]. The ability of a WREM to track women’s health status over time may be hampered by a lack of content validity [88]. Addressing these issues requires women to be invited to identify what is essential content, the extent to which items are comprehensive, and the easiest approaches for measurement.

Most studies evaluating the structural validity of WREMs yielded insufficient quality. Structural validity is the degree to which a tool examines the concept and is linked to evidence evaluating other concepts in the category as well as particular practical criteria [89]. The findings of the present review are consistent with those of Dhakal et al. who reported that findings obtained from factor extraction, rotation, and analysis processes were not consistent [25]. The weak structural and content validity of WREMs may raise questions about a tool’s ability to accurately measure the full concept of the subject (RMC) [36]. Poor-quality structural validity can impact the internal consistency and reliability of tools and their scoring methods for meaningful practice and performance information [36]. Future RMC WREMs development should prioritise and document rigorous structural validity assessment, clearly illustrating the instrument’s dimensionality.

Several studies in this review reported on the reliability of the tool to accurately measure RMC [42, 44, 52, 53, 59, 60, 73, 74, 84]. Reliability is the level of stability demonstrated when an assessment is repeated over time under the same conditions [90]. Most studies in this review employed the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) for ordinal response data, whereas the ideal technique is the use of weighted kappa [36]. Weighted kappa is a measure of agreement for ordinal data that accounts for the degree of disagreement between raters. Values range from − 1 (less than chance) to 1 (perfect agreement) [91]. Weighted kappa is the preferred statistic for assessing reliability of ordinal scores, and no superior alternative has been identified [92]. Thus, future studies should use weighted kappa rather than ICC to assess the reliability of ordinal data, as it more accurately accounts for disagreement between raters.

Studies reporting on test-retest reliability of WREMs presented significant methodological and statistical flaws [42, 44, 52, 59]. Test‒retest reliability measures participants’ performance repeatability, demonstrating stability in the measured construct, and conferring WREM reliability by comparing scores at different points [89]. Although a limited number of studies [42, 44, 52, 59] attempted to evaluate test-retest reliability, further research should be conducted to assess this aspect, as reliability is a critical factor for accuracy and reproducibility of results. Therefore, enhancing the rigor of WREM reliability evaluation is essential in the future.

Approximately half of the tools in this review employed hypothesis testing to determine convergent validity. Convergent validity is the extent to which the overall score of a tool correlates with measures of other constructs that are theoretically predicted to be comparable [89]. Most studies received uncertain quality ratings because the authors failed to define the pattern and magnitude of the relationship between WREM and identified groups or other tools that capture relevant dimensions. The current finding is supported by another review by Bull et al. (2019), which highlighted a similar finding [19]. Dhakal et al. (2021) proposed the need for a comprehensive RMC scale with superior measurement quality as the gold standard [25].

Most studies reported criterion validity, but there was insufficient evidence to substantiate the “gold standard tool” used as a comparison group in their reports. Criterion validity is defined as the relationship between the result of a test and achievement on another relevant measure (the gold standard) [89]. There are two types of criterion validity: predictive and concurrent assessed in this review. Predictive validity was established by assessing the link between scores on the scales and the criterion before the event [93]. Concurrent criterion validity refers to the correlation between test scores and criteria measurements obtained during or immediately after administration [89]. However, none of the included tools met the criterion validity criteria.

No study assessed measurement error and only one study assessed the responsiveness of the tool. Systematic reviews by Cantor et al. (2019) and Dhakal et al. (2024) also indicated that measurement errors and responsiveness were not assessed in different validation studies [20, 25]. Responsiveness is an instrument’s capacity to detect significant changes in a measured construct over time, often linked to health status measures, where significant changes can impact clinical practice [94]. Women’s experiences can change over time, impacting maternity practices, but their recall can also fluctuate due to intrinsic and external factors such as self-reflection and comparisons with family and friends [95]. This review revealed that data on responsiveness in maternity care experiences are insufficient to establish measurement accuracy of a maternity WREM [17].

Many included instruments were translated into different languages, but only three were examined for cross-cultural validity. Cross-cultural validity is the degree to which the performance of items on a translated or culturally adapted instrument matches that of the original version [36]. Researchers can assess cross-cultural validity via “measurement invariance” or “non-differential item functioning” (non-DIF), which evaluates psychometric equivalence between groups to ensure that the construct holds the same meaning [96]. Future research should strive to demonstrate this quality in translated scales.

Strengths and limitations

Through a comprehensive search strategy, manual reference-list checks, and screening methods, we successfully identified significant articles that met the eligibility criteria. This systematic review thoroughly analysed the risk of bias, content validity, and measurement aspects of the included papers according to the COSMIN guidelines. This enabled us to provide specific suggestions for maternity care practice, performance monitoring, and future research involving respectful maternity care utilising women’s reported experience measures.

Previous reviews such as those by Dhakal et al. and Cantor et al. encompassed only a limited selection of scales [20, 25]. Cantor et al. primarily concentrated on frameworks and implementation rather than a rigorous assessment of tool quality [20]. Moreover, the review was broad in scope, addressing various aspects of RMC rather than specifically evaluating the quality of RMC measurement instruments [20]. Neither Dhakal et al., nor Cantor et al. thoroughly evaluated the psychometric properties of each instrument. By contrast, our review systematically adhered to the COSMIN guidelines [40], incorporated several pertinent studies, and evaluated the reliability, validity, and contextual applicability of RMC tools, thereby providing guidance for research and practice.

One of the limitations of the current study was the use of the “worst score counts” standard to determine the potential for bias [40]. While COSMIN recommends this method to simplify evaluation and reduce researcher bias, it inadvertently results in disproportionately negative scores for the risk of bias and evidence quality. As a result, even tools with several strong measurement properties may be rated as low or very low quality due to a single weak domain. This strict criterion may therefore underestimate the overall utility of existing RMC measurement tools, and the interpretation of tool quality should be considered in that context. Despite this issue, the COSMIN manual remains a reasonable resource for evaluating the quality of women-reported experience measures. Given this limitation, future studies should prioritise this issue.

Conclusion

This systematic review found the quality of women-reported RMC experiences measures were generally low and subject to high risk of bias. Consequently, it is challenging to recommend an optimal instrument to measure RMC during perinatal period. This review did not identify any level “A” instruments for assessing RMC. Level “B” WREMs may be used with caution due to validation limitations. Future validation studies should follow COSMIN guidelines and use appropriate statistical methods and transparent reporting. We also recommend future research should focus on developing and rigorously validating a standardised RMC scale, enabling more reliable and comparable assessments across diverse settings.
