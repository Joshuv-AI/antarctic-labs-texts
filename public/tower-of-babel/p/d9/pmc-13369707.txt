SIRT5-mediated HSDL2 desuccinylation promotes diabetic-associated cognitive dysfunction via disrupting mitochondrial TRAP1
Authors: Yinmeng Qu, Renjie Liu, Xuan Chen, Peifan Wu, Yuhao Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diabetic-associated cognitive dysfunction (DACD) is a severe complication of diabetes characterized by mitochondrial failure in hippocampal neurons, yet its precise molecular drivers remain poorly understood. Here, hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase-like 2 (HSDL2) was identified as a key mitochondrial-related protein significantly upregulated in DACD models. Knockdown of HSDL2 alleviated cognitive impairment, synaptic loss, and neuronal apoptosis in DACD mice. Mechanistic investigations revealed that HSDL2 promoted mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis by downregulating TNF receptor-associated protein 1 (TRAP1). Furthermore, HSDL2 was identified as a novel substrate of the desuccinylase sirtuin 5 (SIRT5). SIRT5 mediated HSDL2 desuccinylation at the K42 site to stabilize its protein, leading to its upregulation. Importantly, SIRT5 was upregulated in DACD, and its knockdown mitigated hippocampal damage and cognitive dysfunction. This study unveiled a novel SIRT5/HSDL2/TRAP1 axis in DACD pathogenesis, wherein SIRT5 upregulation drove HSDL2 desuccinylation and stabilization, ultimately promoting mitochondrial dysfunction and cognitive decline via downregulation of TRAP1.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10565-026-10210-2.

Introduction

With the increasing global prevalence of diabetes, its severe neurological complications have become increasingly prominent (Bellia, Lombardo, Meloni, et al., 2022; Crawford and Laiteerapong 2024). Among them, DACD seriously threatens patientsʼ quality of life and self-management capabilities (Biessels and Despa 2018). DACD is characterized by progressive learning and memory deficits, while its underlying molecular pathogenesis remains incompletely elucidated, limiting the development of effective therapeutic strategies (Ehtewish et al. 2022; He et al. 2023). Growing evidence suggests that mitochondrial dysfunction in hippocampal neurons is a core event in DACD, leading to synaptic impairment, oxidative stress, and neuronal apoptosis (Chang et al. 2023; Tang et al. 2022; Tang et al. 2023). Therefore, identifying key mitochondrial-related molecules and their regulatory mechanisms in DACD is crucial.

Bioinformatic analysis of the public dataset (GSE261887) revealed that HSDL2, a mitochondrial-associated protein (Yang et al. 2024), was a significantly upregulated differentially expressed gene (DEG) in DACD models. HSDL2 is localized to mitochondria, and its knockout has been confirmed to impair mitochondrial respiration, fatty acid oxidation, and tricarboxylic acid cycle activity in hepatocytes (Samson et al. 2024). Furthermore, studies suggest that HSDL2 can serve as a biomarker for diabetes-related heart failure and regulates cardiomyocyte apoptosis (Chen et al. 2024), and is associated with susceptibility to Alzheimerʼs disease (Ma et al. 2021). Nevertheless, its precise role and regulatory mechanisms in DACD are entirely unknown.

To deeply analyze the mechanism of HSDL2 action, predictions using the GeneMANIA and STRING databases indicated a potential interaction between HSDL2 and TRAP1. TRAP1 is a mitochondrial chaperone essential for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis (Cannino et al. 2022). In models of diabetic cardiomyopathy, TRAP1 has been demonstrated to alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction and cardiomyocyte apoptosis (Zhang et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2025). In neurodegenerative diseases, TRAP1 overexpression can protect motor neurons from mitochondrial dysfunction-induced death (Clarke et al. 2022). Additionally, TRAP1 can influence key mitochondrial dynamics proteins, alter mitochondrial structure and function in primary hippocampal neurons, and exert neuroprotective effects in hypoxia-induced acute brain injury (Liu et al. 2024). These findings strongly suggest that HSDL2 might interact with TRAP1, together forming a critical link in mitochondrial dysfunction in DACD.

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) have emerged as key regulatory mechanisms for mitochondrial function and neuronal survival (Grel et al. 2023; Junqueira et al. 2019). Among them, the regulatory role of lysine succinylation in the development of central nervous system diseases has gained increasing attention in recent years (Wang et al. 2024). This modification is regulated by desuccinylases, such as SIRT5 in mitochondria, and can profoundly alter protein stability and function, thereby impacting mitochondrial activity (Park et al. 2013; Rardin et al. 2013). Predictions using databases like Uniprot and PhosphoSitePlus indicated that HSDL2 possesses potential succinylation sites. This prompted us to investigate whether HSDL2 is regulated by succinylation and to identify the desuccinylase responsible for this process.

Utilizing both in vivo and in vitro experimental models, this study investigated the impact of HSDL2 upregulation on mitochondrial function in neuronal cells, as well as hippocampal damage and cognitive impairment in DACD mice. The results revealed that the promotion of HSDL2 on mitochondrial dysfunction in neuronal cells was dependent on its downregulation of TRAP1. Furthermore, this research elucidated that the desuccinylation modification of HSDL2 in DACD was responsible for its upregulated expression and was regulated by SIRT5. From the novel perspective of succinylation modification, this study systematically clarified the role of the SIRT5-HSDL2-TRAP1 signaling axis in DACD pathogenesis, providing a theoretical basis for revealing new mechanisms of this disease and identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatic analysis

The dataset GSE261887 was downloaded from the GEO database. Six samples from the high-fat fructose diet group and six samples from the control group were selected for subsequent analysis. Principal component analysis (PCA) and correlation heatmap were performed to evaluate the reproducibility and intra-group consistency among the samples. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the R package limma. Genes with an absolute log2 fold change greater than 1 and an adjusted p-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant DEGs. A volcano plot was generated to visualize the overall distribution of the DEGs. To identify mitochondrial-related DEGs, the list of DEGs was intersected with a predefined set of mitochondrial function-related genes, and a Venn diagram was used to display the overlap. From the resulting intersection genes, the target gene of interest was selected, and its expression levels across the different sample groups were specifically visualized.

Animals

Four-week-old male C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Sipeifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) animal laboratory under controlled conditions (temperature: 21–26 °C, humidity: 30%–70%) with a 12-h light/dark cycle. After one week of acclimatization with free access to food and water, the experiments were initiated. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Hospital of Jilin University.

Establishment of the DACD model and treatments

A diabetic mouse model was established to study DACD (Chang et al. 2023). C57BL/6 J mice were first fed a high-fat diet for 4 weeks. Subsequently, diabetes was induced by intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, #S110910, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) at a dose of 75 mg/kg body weight for 3 consecutive days. The STZ was freshly prepared in citrate buffer (pH 4.5). Control mice received equivalent volumes of citrate buffer only. One week after the final STZ injection, non-fasting blood glucose levels and body weight were measured. Mice with sustained blood glucose levels > 16.7 mmol/L were considered diabetic and included in the study as the DACD model.

For intracranial viral delivery, diabetic mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of Avertin (0.02 mL/g, #MA0478, Meilun Biotechnology, Dalian, Liaoning, China) and securely positioned in a stereotaxic apparatus. Following craniotomy, recombinant adeno-associated virus (AAV) carrying short hairpin RNA (shRNA) targeting HSDL2 (AAV-sh-HSDL2) or SIRT5 (AAV-sh-SIRT5), along with their respective scrambled control viruses (AAV-sh-NC), were bilaterally injected into the hippocampal CA1 region (coordinates: AP −1.94 mm, ML ±1.2 mm, DV −1.6 mm from bregma) using an automated microinjection system with a Hamilton syringe. The virus was infused at a rate of 0.1 μL/min, and the needle was left in place for an additional 10 min post-injection to ensure proper diffusion before slow withdrawal. Behavioral tests were conducted 8 weeks after viral injection to assess cognitive function.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

Recognition memory was evaluated using the NOR test. The testing apparatus consisted of an open-field arena. In the first trial (training phase), two identical objects were placed symmetrically within the arena, and the mouse was allowed to explore freely for 10 min. After 1 h inter-trial interval, one of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel object for the second trial (test phase), during which the mouse again explored for 10 min. All sessions were recorded by a video tracking system and analyzed using KEMaze animal behavior analysis software (Nanjing Kalwen Biotechnology, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). A recognition index was calculated as [time exploring novel object/(time exploring novel object + time exploring familiar object)] × 100%.

Morris water maze test

Spatial learning and memory were assessed using the Morris water maze test. The experiment was conducted using the KW-MWM Morris water maze system (Nanjing Kalwen Biotechnology) and analyzed with KEMaze animal behavior analysis software. During the adaptation phase, mice were allowed to swim freely in the pool for 60 s to acclimate to the water maze environment. In the subsequent acquisition phase, mice underwent training trials to locate a hidden platform over 5 consecutive days, with 4 trials per day. For each trial, mice were released from one of four predetermined starting points (one from each quadrant) in a random order and allowed a maximum of 60 s to find the platform. The escape latency and swimming path were recorded for each trial. The probe trial was performed on day 6, 24 h after the last learning trial, by removing the platform and allowing the mice to swim freely for 60 s. The time spent in the target quadrant where the platform had been previously located and the number of platform crossings were recorded and analyzed to evaluate spatial memory retention.

Golgi staining and dendritic spine density analysis

Dendritic spine density in the hippocampal CA1 region was assessed using Golgi staining. Following deep anesthesia, brain tissues were quickly harvested and immediately immersed in Golgi-Cox fixation solution (#G1069, Servicebio, Wuhan, Hubei, China) for 14 days at room temperature in the dark, with the solution changed according to the manufacturerʼs protocol. The tissues were then transferred to a tissue processing solution for 3 days at 4 °C. Subsequently, 60 μm thick coronal sections were prepared using a vibrating microtome (#VT1000S, Leica, Wetzlar, Hesse, Germany). The sections were developed using a developing solution (#G1069-2, Servicebio), rinsed, and mounted with glycerin gelatin mounting medium (#G1402, Servicebio). Finally, the stained sections were scanned using a slide scanner, and the density of dendritic spines in the CA1 region was quantified and analyzed.

Nissl staining

Neuronal density in the hippocampal CA1 region was assessed using Nissl staining. Paraffin-embedded tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a graded series of ethanol. The sections were then stained with Nissl staining solution (#G1036, Servicebio) for 5 min, followed by brief differentiation in 0.1% acetic acid. After rinsing with distilled water, the sections were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam (#10,004,160, Sinopharm Chemical Reagent, Shanghai, China). The stained sections were examined under a light microscope, and the number of intact neurons with clearly visible Nissl bodies in the CA1 region was quantified.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Hippocampal tissue morphology was evaluated using H&E staining. Briefly, tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (#G1101, Servicebio), embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Following deparaffinization and hydration, the sections were stained with hematoxylin (#G1004, Servicebio) for 5 min and eosin (#G1001, Servicebio) for 5 min according to standard protocols. After dehydration and clearing, the sections were mounted with mounting medium (#WG10004160, Servicebio). Stained sections were examined under a light microscope (#DM500, Leica).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The protein expression levels of SIRT5, HSDL2, and TRAP1 in mouse hippocampal tissues were detected by IHC assay. Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval using EDTA antigen retrieval buffer (#P0085, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After blocking endogenous peroxidase activity, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-HSDL2 (#15,631–1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, Hubei, China), anti-TRAP1 (#10,325–1-AP, Proteintech), or anti-SIRT5 (#M02395-1, Boster, Wuhan, Hubei, China) (diluted at 1:100). Subsequently, the sections were incubated with a secondary antibody (#PV-6000, Zhongshan Golden Bridge, Beijing, China) and visualized using a DAB kit (#G1212-200 T, Servicebio). Finally, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Stained sections were observed and imaged under a light microscope.

Cell culture and treatment

The human neuroblastoma cell line (SH-SY5Y) was obtained from Immocell Biotechnology (Xiamen, Fujian, China) and maintained in Dulbeccoʼs Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. To establish the high glucose (HG) model, cells were cultured in serum-free DMEM containing 25 mM D-glucose (#ST1228, Beyotime) for 48 h, while the normal glucose (NG) control group was maintained in standard serum-free DMEM. For lentiviral transduction, SH-SY5Y cells were seeded in 24-well plates. When cells reached approximately 70% confluence, they were transduced with recombinant lentiviruses (sh-NC, sh-HSDL2, OE-NC, OE-HSDL2, sh-HSDL2 + OE-NC, or sh-HSDL2 + OE-TRAP1) at a multiplicity of infection of 2 μL virus per well (1 × 10⁹ PFU/mL) for 8–16 h. Subsequently, the virus-containing medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and cells were cultured for an additional 24–72 h to allow for transgene expression before subsequent experiments.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 kit (#40203ES60, Yeasen, Shanghai, China). Following the respective treatments, SH-SY5Y cells were seeded in 96-well plates. After 48 h of incubation under HG conditions, 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. The absorbance at 450 nm was then measured using a microplate reader (#Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis

Mitochondrial ultrastructure was examined by TEM analysis. SH-SY5Y cells were collected and fixed with electron microscope fixative (#G1102, Servicebio) at 4 °C overnight. The samples were then post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydrated, and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections were prepared using an ultramicrotome, stained with 3% uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and observed under a transmission electron microscope. Mitochondrial morphology in the captured images was analyzed.

Flow cytometry analysis

Mitochondrial calcium levels and superoxide production were measured by flow cytometry. For mitochondrial Ca2⁺ detection, cells were collected and loaded with the Rhod-2 AM fluorescent probe (#S1062S, Beyotime) at a 1 × working concentration (diluted from 500 × stock solution) according to the manufacturerʼs instructions. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min and washing, the fluorescence intensity was immediately analyzed using a flow cytometer (#RMNNC-3000, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). For mitochondrial superoxide measurement, cells were stained with the MitoSOX Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator (#B8808, Houston, TX, USA) at a final concentration of 500 nM following the standard protocol. Briefly, after treatment, cells were incubated with MitoSOX Red working solution at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. The cells were then washed, resuspended in PBS, and analyzed by flow cytometry. The obtained data were processed and the mean fluorescence intensity was used for quantitative analysis.

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP)

The MMP was assessed using the JC-1 assay kit (#C2006, Beyotime). Following the respective treatments, SH-SY5Y cells were collected and incubated with JC-1 working solution at 37 °C for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with JC-1 assay buffer. The fluorescence was then immediately analyzed. The MMP was indicated by the ratio of red fluorescence (aggregates, indicating high MMP) to green fluorescence (monomers, indicating low MMP), which was quantified using fluorescence microscopy.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

After treatment, SH-SY5Y cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and blocked with normal goat serum. For examining HSDL2, TRAP1, and SIRT5 expression using single staining, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against HSDL2 (#15,631–1-AP, 1:300; Proteintech), TRAP1 (#10,325–1-AP, 1:300; Proteintech), or SIRT5 (#M02395-1, 1:300; Boster). After washing, cells were incubated with corresponding fluorescent secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (#A0516; Beyotime) was used for HSDL2 and TRAP1, while FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (#A0568; Beyotime) was utilized for SIRT5. For IF double staining to examine co-localization, cells were simultaneously incubated with rabbit anti-HSDL2 and mouse anti-SIRT5 primary antibodies, followed by incubation with a mixture of Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG and FITC-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies. All samples were counterstained with DAPI to visualize nuclei, and fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (BZ-X800, Keyence).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay

Apoptosis was detected in both mouse hippocampal tissue sections and cultured SH-SY5Y cells using TUNEL assays. For tissue analysis, sections were processed according to the manufacturerʼs instructions of the one-step TUNEL apoptosis detection kit (#G1501, Servicebio). Briefly, after deparaffinization and rehydration, sections were treated with Proteinase K for antigen retrieval. The sections were then incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (#G1012, Servicebio), and the sections were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium (#G1401, Servicebio). For cell culture analysis, SH-SY5Y cells were grown to 60%–80% confluence in 96-well plates. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. Apoptosis was detected using the TUNEL cell apoptosis detection kit (#C1086, Beyotime) following the manufacturerʼs protocol. Cells were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture at 37 °C for 60 min in the dark, followed by DAPI counterstaining. All samples were examined under a fluorescence microscope (#BZ-X800, Keyence).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from tissues and cultured cells using RNA extraction reagent (#G3013, Servicebio) according to the manufacturerʼs instructions. RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (#RR037Q, Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). qPCR was performed with TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ II (#RR820Q, Takara) on a real-time PCR system (#SLAN-96S, Hongshi, Foshan, Guangdong, China). The reaction conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 20 s. The expression levels of HSDL2, TRAP1, p300/CBP, a-KGDHC, KAT2A, CPT1A, HAT1, SIRT3, SIRT7, and SIRT5 were normalized to β-tubulin and calculated using the 2–ΔΔCt method. Western blot analysis

Cells or hippocampal tissues were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Protein concentrations were quantified using a BCA protein assay kit (#BL521A, Bio sharp, Beijing, China). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (#IPVH00010, Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany). After blocking with 5% skim milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-HSDL2 (#A11317-1, 1:1000; Boster), anti-SYP (#67,864–1-Ig, 1:1000; Proteintech), anti-PSD95 (#A02128, 1:1000; Boster), anti-BAX (#CY5059, 1:1000; Abways, Shanghai, China), anti-Bcl-2 (#381,702, 1:1000; Zenbio, Chengdu, Sichuan, China), anti-TRAP1 (#67,693–1-Ig, 1:1000; Proteintech), anti-SIRT5 (#67,257–1-Ig, 1:1000; Proteintech), anti-pan-Ksucc (#PA5-120,740, 1:1000; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and anti-β-tubulin (#AB0039, 1:1000; Abways). The membranes were then incubated with corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (#AB0101 for rabbit primary antibodies, #AB0102 for mouse primary antibodies, 1:5000; Abways) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized and detected by a gel imaging system (#ChampChemi 910, SINSAGE, Guangzhou, Guangdong). β-tubulin was used as a loading control.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and succinylation analysis

Protein–protein interactions and succinylation modifications were analyzed by Co-IP assays. Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. For each Co-IP assay, total protein was incubated with specific antibodies conjugated to Protein A/G magnetic beads (#P2179S, Beyotime) at 4 °C overnight. To investigate HSDL2 interactions, samples were incubated with anti-HSDL2 antibody (#A11317-1, Boster). The immunoprecipitated complexes were then analyzed by western blot using anti-TRAP1 (#67,693–1-Ig, Proteintech) or anti-SIRT5 (#67,257–1-Ig, Proteintech) antibodies to detect respective interactions. Normal IgG (#ab172730, Abcam, Waltham, MA, USA) served as negative control. For succinylation analysis, HSDL2 was immunoprecipitated from cell lysates using anti-HSDL2 antibody. The precipitates were then immunoblotted with anti-pan-Ksucc antibody (#PA5-120,740, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to assess total HSDL2 succinylation levels. To specifically examine K42 site succinylation of HSDL2, the samples were analyzed by western blot using a custom anti-Succ-K42 antibody (#4408-M-R1, Youke Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) to detect site-specific succinylation after HSDL2 immunoprecipitation. For all western blot analyses following Co-IP, proteins were separated and detected as above described. Input samples were included as loading controls.

Dot blot analysis

The specificity of the custom anti-Succ-K42 antibody was validated by dot blot assay. Synthetic peptides (unmodified and succinylated) were denatured and spotted directly onto a nitrocellulose membrane. The membrane was cross-linked using a UV crosslinker (#UCL-3200, Luyang Instruments, Shanghai, China), followed by blocking with 5% skim milk. The membrane was then incubated with the primary anti-Succ-K42 antibody (#4408-M-R1, Youke Biotechnology) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (#AB0101, Abways) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein spots were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (#WBKLS0100, Millipore). For normalization, a parallel membrane was stained with 0.1% methylene blue (#G1300, Solarbio, Beijing, China) to confirm equal peptide loading.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α in hippocampal tissues were quantified using commercial ELISA kits (#E-EL-M0037 for IL-1β and #E-EL-M3063 for TNF-α, Elabscience, Wuhan, Hubei, China) according to the manufacturerʼs instructions. Briefly, hippocampal tissues were homogenized in cold PBS, and the supernatants were collected after centrifugation. The samples and standards were added to the pre-coated plates and incubated at 37 °C for 90 min. After washing, biotinylated detection antibodies were added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated avidin for 30 min. The reaction was developed with TMB substrate, stopped with stop solution, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (#Multiskan FC, Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Statistical analysis

Each assay was performed for 3 times. Data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism 8.0 (La Jolla, CA, USA) and expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Two-tailed Studentʼs t test were used for comparing two variables. One-way ANOVA test was used for multiple variable comparison. p < 0.05 was considered as a significant difference.

Results

HSDL2 was upregulated in DACD

To identify key mitochondrial-related genes involved in DACD, the public dataset GSE261887 was analyzed. Heatmap and PCA plot demonstrated excellent reproducibility among biological replicates within the control and DACD groups (Fig. 1A). Volcano plot analysis of this dataset confirmed HSDL2 as one of the significantly upregulated genes in DACD group compared to control (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, Venn diagram analysis identified HSDL2 as a mitochondrial-associated DEG by intersecting DEGs from the dataset GSE261887 with mitochondrial function-related genes (Fig. 1C). Consistently, violin plot analysis revealed a significant increase in HSDL2 mRNA level in DACD group (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, a STZ-induced DACD mouse model was established. qRT-PCR, Western blot and IHC analysis confirmed that both the mRNA and protein level of HSDL2 was significantly elevated in the hippocampus of DACD mice compared to the Control group (Fig. 1E-G). Similarly, compared to the NG group, a significant increase in HSDL2 mRNA and protein expression was observed in an in vitro model using SH-SY5Y cells exposed to HG (Fig. 1H-J). HSDL2 knockdown ameliorated cognitive deficits and hippocampal damage in DACD mice

HSDL2 was knocked down in the hippocampal CA1 region of DACD mice using AAV-sh-HSDL2. Fluorescence imaging confirmed successful sh-HSDL2 viral infection (Fig. 2A). The knockdown efficiency of HSDL2 was validated by qRT-PCR and Western blot (Fig. S1A-B). In behavioral tests, DACD model mice exhibited severe cognitive impairments, as evidenced by a reduced preference for novel objects in the NOR test (Fig. 2B) and deficits in spatial learning and memory in the Morris Water Maze, including prolonged escape latency, fewer platform crossings, and less time spent in the target quadrant (Fig. 2C-F). Notably, knockdown of HSDL2 significantly improved these cognitive deficits. Morphologically, Golgi staining revealed a significant decrease in dendritic spine density in the hippocampal CA1 region of DACD mice, which was rescued by HSDL2 knockdown (Fig. 2G-H). Nissl staining showed reduced number of neurons in the DACD mice, which was attenuated by HSDL2 knockdown (Fig. 2I-J). H&E staining indicated that DACD mice suffered from severe neuronal damage, including neuronal shrinkage, disorganized and loose arrangement, nuclear pyknosis, and cytoplasmic loss (Fig. 2K). In contrast, HSDL2 knockdown markedly alleviated these pathological changes. At the molecular level, Western blot analysis showed that the expression of synaptic proteins SYP and PSD95 was decreased in the hippocampus of DACD mice, which was reversed by HSDL2 knockdown (Fig. 2L). ELISA assays revealed elevated levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IL-1β in the hippocampus of DACD mice, which were reduced following HSDL2 knockdown (Fig. 2M). HSDL2 induced mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis in neuronal cells in vitro

HSDL2 expression was modulated in SH-SY5Y cells under HG conditions. HSDL2 knockdown and overexpression efficiencies were validated (Fig. S2A-B). TEM analysis revealed severe mitochondrial damage in HG-treated SH-SY5Y cells, characterized by mitochondrial shortening, swelling, and disorganized or lost cristae (Fig. 3A). HSDL2 knockdown alleviated these morphological abnormalities, while HSDL2 overexpression exacerbated them. Functionally, flow cytometry and IF assays showed that HG conditions led to mitochondrial calcium overload (Fig. 3B-C), elevated mitochondrial superoxide production (Fig. 3D-E), and decreased MMP (Fig. 3F-G). These indicators of mitochondrial dysfunction were significantly mitigated by HSDL2 knockdown but were aggravated by HSDL2 overexpression. Moreover, HSDL2 knockdown relieved HG treatment-induced reduction in cell viability and increase in apoptosis, while HSDL2 overexpression exerted the opposite effects (Fig. 3H-I, S2C-D). To exclude the possibility that HG-induced mitochondrial dysfunction was caused by hyperosmotic stress rather than HSDL2 upregulation, osmotic control experiments using osmitrol were performed. No significant differences in mitochondrial superoxide levels, apoptosis, or BAX/Bcl-2 expression was observed among NG, NG + PBS, and NG + osmitrol groups. In contrast, OE-HSDL2 significantly increased mitochondrial superoxide production and apoptosis under NG conditions compared to the vector control (Fig. S2E-I). SIRT5 mediated desuccinylation of HSDL2 at K42 site

In vitro assays confirmed that HSDL2 could be succinylated in a succinyl-CoA concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 6A). Treatment with the pan-sirtuin inhibitor NAM increased HSDL2 succinylation level in HEK 293 T cells overexpressing HSDL2-Flag and in SH-SY5Y cells at the endogenous level (Fig. 6B-C). Database analysis predicted four potential succinylation sites (K32, K42, K141, K318) on HSDL2 (Fig. 6D-E, S4A-C). Mutagenesis studies identified K42 as the major succinylation site of HSDL2, as the K42R mutation most drastically reduced HSDL2 succinylation level (Fig. 6F). Then, a site-specific antibody against Succ-K42 was generated. Dot blot assay revealed that the anti-Succ-K42 antibody preferentially recognized the K42-succinylated peptide but not the unsuccinylated control peptide (Fig. 6G). Western blot assay using an anti-Succ-K42 antibody confirmed the presence of HSDL2 K42 succinylation signal in wild-type HSDL2, but not in the K42R and K42E HSDL2 mutants (Fig. 6H). Moreover, NAM treatment increased HSDL2 K42 succinylation level in SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 6I). Importantly, the K42 succinylation signal was detectable in both HEK293T and SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 6J-K), suggesting that K42 succinylation was a physiological PTM of HSDL2. SIRT5 knockdown increased HSDL2 K42 succinylation level (Fig. 6L), confirming SIRT5 as the major desuccinylase for K42 site of HSDL2. Crucially, a decrease in HSDL2 K42 succinylation and an increase in SIRT5 expression were observed in the hippocampus of DACD mice compared to controls (Fig. 6M). Discussion

The pathogenesis of DACD is complex, with mitochondrial dysfunction in the hippocampus considered a central event (Chang et al. 2023; Tang et al. 2022, 2023). Therefore, elucidating the specific molecular drivers linking hyperglycemia to hippocampal mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal loss is crucial for developing effective DACD treatments. This study revealed a novel regulatory axis centered on the PTM of HSDL2 and its impact on mitochondrial function. Our results systematically demonstrated that SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation of HSDL2 promoted its protein stabilization and upregulation, which subsequently induced mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis by downregulating TRAP1, ultimately driving hippocampal damage and cognitive impairment.

Through bioinformatic analysis and experimental validation, this study was the first to identify significant upregulation of HSDL2 in the hippocampus of DACD models. This finding is significant because previous research has primarily focused on the role of HSDL2 in liver metabolism (Samson et al. 2024) and heart disease (Chen et al. 2024), while its function in neurological disorders was largely unknown. Notably, our data revealed that HSDL2 promoted hippocampal damage and cognitive dysfunction in DACD mice. These findings are consistent with genetic studies by Smith et al. linking HSDL2 to Alzheimerʼs disease susceptibility (Ma et al. 2021), suggesting HSDL2 may play a role in various cognitive disorders. Furthermore, although the regulation of HSDL2 on cancer cell apoptosis has been confirmed in multiple cancer models (Liu et al. 2022; Shi et al. 2020; Zeng et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2018), its impact on mitochondrial dysfunction has rarely been reported. This study confirmed that HSDL2 promoted HG-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis, providing new mechanistic insights into how HSDL2 regulated cell death. Importantly, consistent validation from both in vivo and in vitro experiments that HSDL2 knockdown alleviated DACD phenotypes and mitochondrial dysfunction provided experimental support for its potential as a therapeutic target in DACD.

This study also provided the first evidence of an interaction between HSDL2 and TRAP1, offering a novel mechanistic explanation for mitochondrial dysfunction in DACD. The protective role of TRAP1, a mitochondrial chaperone, has been demonstrated in various disease models (Gutierrez et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2020). In diabetic retinopathy and nephropathy, TRAP1 alleviates high glucose-induced mitochondrial damage (Li et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2020). Butler et al. have reported that TRAP1 prevents mitochondrial dysfunction caused by α-synuclein overexpression in human SH-SY5Y cells (Butler et al. 2012). Similarly, the present study confirmed the mitochondrial protective role of TRAP1 in HG-induced SH-SY5Y cells. Notably, our discovery was the first to reveal that TRAP1ʼs mitochondrial protection mechanism was negatively regulated by HSDL2. The results demonstrated that TRAP1 was a key downstream effector through which HSDL2 induced mitochondrial dysfunction in neuronal cells. This finding not only refined the molecular pathway promoting DACD progression but also suggested that targeting the HSDL2-TRAP1 interaction could have therapeutic potential.

Although the regulatory role of succinylation modification in central nervous system diseases has been recognized (Wang et al. 2024), its impact on DACD progression had not been investigated. This study provided the first confirmation of the pathological significance of protein desuccinylation in DACD and was the first to establish that succinylation modulated HSDL2 protein stability and function. Furthermore, through multi-site validation, K42 was identified as the primary succinylation site on HSDL2, and SIRT5 was determined to be the key enzyme responsible for its desuccinylation. These findings were significant because previous research on SIRT5-regulated protein succinylation has primarily focused on metabolism-related diseases (Wu et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2024), with limited studies in neurological disorders. In contrast to the protective role of SIRT5-catalyzed RAB7A desuccinylation reported by Deng et al. in Alzheimerʼs disease (Deng et al. 2024), our study found that SIRT5 upregulation in DACD exerted detrimental effects on hippocampal neurons by stabilizing HSDL2 protein via desuccinylation. This discrepancy might reflect the context and substrate specificity of SIRT5 function, highlighting the importance of comprehensively analyzing the desuccinylation network across different disease models.

The alleviation of hippocampal damage and cognitive dysfunction in DACD mice following in vivo SIRT5 knockdown underscored the translational significance of our work. The discovery of the SIRT5/HSDL2/TRAP1 axis not only provided a new perspective for understanding the molecular mechanisms of DACD but also offered potential targets for new therapeutic strategies. Specifically, the identification of the HSDL2 K42 site laid a theoretical foundation for developing specific inhibitors. However, this study has several limitations. First, the specific molecular mechanism by which HSDL2 K42 succinylation affects its protein stability requires further investigation. Second, the precise regulatory mechanism underlying SIRT5 upregulation in diabetes remains unclear. Additionally, the role of the SIRT5/HSDL2/TRAP1 axis may vary across different brain regions and cell types, which warrants further exploration in future studies.

In summary, this study systematically elucidated a novel molecular regulatory axis in DACD. Under diabetic conditions, upregulated SIRT5 expression stabilized the HSDL2 protein by enhancing its desuccinylation at the K42 site. The increased HSDL2 protein subsequently led to mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis via downregulation of TRAP1, ultimately resulting in cognitive impairment. This discovery not only deepens the understanding of DACD pathogenesis but also provides a theoretical basis and potential targets for developing new therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study established HSDL2 as a critical pathogenic driver in DACD. We delineated a novel molecular pathway wherein SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation stabilized HSDL2 protein. The upregulated HSDL2 subsequently interacted with and downregulated TRAP1, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and neuronal apoptosis. These findings unveiled the SIRT5/HSDL2/TRAP1 axis as a central mechanism contributing to cognitive decline in diabetes. Therefore, targeting this pathway represents a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating DACD.
