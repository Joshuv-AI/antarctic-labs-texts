Initiatives to support nursing workforce sustainability: A rapid umbrella review
Authors: Gail Tomblin Murphy, Tara Sampalli, Annette Elliott Rose, Ruth Martin-Misener, Meaghan Sim, Alyssa Indar, Jennifer Murdoch, Kristy Hancock, Sophia Salmaniw, Kaylee Murphy-Boyle, Kianna Benson, Adrian MacKenzie, Caroline Chamberland-Rowe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDespite the global shortage of nurses, there is limited literature that comprehensively maps interventions to support, stabilize, and enhance the nursing workforce.PurposeThis umbrella review aimed to identify structures, interventions, and health system arrangements that healthcare systems have put in place to stabilize, support, and sustain the nursing workforce.MethodsThe review is guided by the Effective Practice and Organization of Care (EPOC) taxonomy and aimed to synthesize review-level evidence relevant to nursing workforce planning and policy development within a policy-relevant timeframe. Reviews that evaluated one or more interventions to stabilize, support, or enhance the nursing workforce within healthcare systems were considered for inclusion. We conducted academic literature searches in MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, and Scopus on March 11, 2024, and conducted grey literature searches in Google from April 11–16, 2024. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and keywords related to nurses, recruitment and retention, and review study designs. Searches were limited to sources published between January 2014 and March 11, 2024, and to English and French. Two reviewers independently screened and extracted each identified article.We used the JBI critical appraisal checklist for systematic reviews and research synthesis to appraise the included reviews, applying it pragmatically across the heterogeneous review designs in the sample. This approach was selected to provide a single, transparent review-level appraisal process across the included sample; however, it was used as a pragmatic indicator of methodological and reporting completeness.FindingsWe categorized the included interventions using the EPOC taxonomy. Following screening, we selected 69 studies for inclusion, the majority of which addressed implementation strategies (n = 60) or delivery arrangements (n = 8). The findings directly addressed the primary objective of identifying interventions used to support nursing workforce sustainability and highlighted important evidence gaps related to rural and remote settings, diversity and representation, population needs, equity, and governance and financial arrangements.Discussion/ConclusionThe findings of this umbrella review suggest that there is no one-size-fits-all approach for supporting the nursing workforce; rather, a multi-level, multi-pronged approach may be more appropriate to collectively have the most impactful outcomes.Limitations of this review include the heterogeneity of the included review designs and the pragmatic use of a single appraisal approach across those designs, which limits direct cross review quality comparisons. Future studies should incorporate rigorous implementation and evaluation plans, focusing on the long-term impacts of strategies.The targeted grey literature search was designed to identify review-level synthesis.

Background

Nurses have a critical role in the functioning of healthcare systems around the world. The stabilization, support, and enhancement of the nursing workforce is therefore a key priority globally to meet the healthcare needs of populations. This workforce must be well-trained, adequately supported, and appropriately valued (Chamanga et al., 2020; Clements et al., 2016).

Despite their importance, there is currently a global shortage of over four million nurses, with challenges more acute in some sectors than others. A study comprising five European countries, for example, found that >20% of critical care nurses planned to leave their positions (Llaurado-Serra et al., 2025), while nurse turnover in some US emergency departments exceeds 40% (Lee, 2024). During the 2022–2023 fiscal year in Canada, there were 120,140 healthcare job vacancies, on average. This is twice the number reported in 2019–2020 (before the COVID-19 pandemic) and four times higher than in 2015–2016 (Canadian Institute for Health Information, 2024). Globally, healthcare workforce vacancies are projected to reach 18 million by 2030 (Bayliss‐Pratt et al., 2020). In addition to the impact on the public, insufficient staffing levels can negatively impact health professionals’ well-being, leading to increased absenteeism and burnout due to overtime and disproportionate workloads (Bayliss‐Pratt et al., 2020). The consequences of these shortages underscore the need for system-level reforms that improve the nursing workforce environment and long-term workforce engagement (Tomblin Murphy et al., 2022).

The COVID-19 pandemic, alongside changes in technology, leadership, and skill mix, has exacerbated existing obstacles to nursing retention and workforce capacity. The rising demand for nurses within constrained environments has contributed to widespread physical, psychological, and social health challenges across the healthcare system. These changes have long-term impacts on mental health, recruitment, and retention in the nursing sector, primarily through their implications on work, study, and well-being (Lopez et al., 2022). A substantial amount of evidence supports the need for a multi-pronged approach to create supportive work environments for nurses at every stage of their careers and promote a more sustainable workforce (Acorn and Adynski, 2023; Afriyie, 2024; Farghaly Abdelaliem et al., 2024; Johansen and Cadmus, 2016).

To support stabilization of the nursing workforce, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends focusing on the following key areas of action: (a) facilitating workforce entry, (b) investing in an active workforce, and (c) improving support and managing attrition from the workforce (World Health Organization, 2006). Numerous peer-reviewed systematic reviews have examined these focus areas, as well as additional factors related to nurse recruitment and retention. For example, research has explored nurse recruitment and retention within specific services or geographic regions (Marufu et al., 2021), strategies that support nurses’ transition from pre-licensure education to practice (Vázquez-Calatayud and Eseverri-Azcoiti, 2023), and initiatives that facilitate the acculturation of internationally trained nurses. A consistent finding across multiple reviews is that the factors most important to nurse recruitment and retention vary substantially across the career stages of nurses (Buckley et al., 2025; Ejebu et al., 2024; Pressley and Garside, 2023).

Recently, Ben-Ahmed and Bourgeault (2022) conducted a rapid review of peer-reviewed and policy literature, alongside stakeholder dialogues and nurse surveys, to identify priority nursing workforce stabilization interventions for Canadian settings. Their work identified strategies to retain, return, integrate, and recruit nurses to the workforce. However, a broader umbrella review synthesizing the structures, interventions, and health system arrangements used internationally to support nursing recruitment and retention remains lacking, limiting the evidence available for integrated workforce planning and policy development.

To address this gap, we conducted a rapid umbrella review (review of reviews) to identify policy and other structural options, interventions, and health system arrangements relevant to nursing workforce sustainability through recruitment and retention. Guided by the EPOC taxonomy, this review forms one component of policy research informing the development of a comprehensive nursing workforce strategy for Nova Scotia, Atlantic Canada’s most populous province (Government of Nova Scotia, 2016). The EPOC taxonomy was used as an organizing framework for classifying the intervention and health system arrangement types identified in the review, rather than as a definitional framework for the concept of “structures” in workforce policy.

While the findings will inform a provincial strategy, they are also relevant to a global audience, where health system decision-makers and governments are challenged to address similar nursing and health workforce constraints while supporting the current and future needs of the population and health services and delivery systems.

Review questions

This review was guided by the following question: What incentives have healthcare systems put in place to stabilize, support, and sustain the nursing workforce? Within this review, the term structures were interpreted using the EPOC taxonomy of delivery arrangements, financial arrangements, governance arrangements, and implementation strategies. More specifically, rather than defining “structures” through EPOC, we used the taxonomy to organize the identified intervention and arrangement types across its four domains. Accordingly, EPOC was applied as an analytic classification framework for organizing review findings, not as a proxy definition of “structures.”

In addition to the primary question, the following sub questions were of interest:•What feasible, appropriate, meaningful, and/or effective structures (delivery arrangements, financial arrangements, governance arrangements, and implementation strategies) have been implemented to stabilize the nursing workforce in rural and remote settings?•Are certain directions more feasible, effective, meaningful, and/or appropriate in certain practice settings (i.e., acute care vs. long term care)?•What supports the sustainability of these initiatives/interventions over time?•How do these initiatives/interventions consider the needs of the population?•How do these initiatives/interventions consider diversity and representation in the nursing workforce?•How do these initiatives/interventions support equitable care? and•How are these initiatives/interventions distributed across the health system?

Methods

This umbrella review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for umbrella reviews, as outlined in Chapter 9 of the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis (Aromataris et al., 2024). We integrated pragmatic rapid review methods appropriate to a review-of-reviews design (Tricco et al., 2017). Owing to the complexity of the research question, similar umbrella review methodological approaches in the health services and policy research field also informed the review (Amri et al., 2022).

Although the research questions included exploratory and mapping-oriented elements, we selected a rapid umbrella review design rather than a rapid scoping review because the objective was to synthesize and interpret existing review-level evidence relevant to nursing workforce sustainability within a policy-relevant timeframe. The field already contained a substantial and methodologically diverse body of review literature, making a review-of-reviews approach appropriate for identifying higher-order patterns across interventions, structures, and health system arrangements. The umbrella review design also aligned with the study’s applied policy purpose by supporting a pragmatic synthesis of evidence across the EPOC domains while enabling consideration of implementation-related concepts reflected in the FAME-informed sub-questions.

We adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement (Page et al., 2021) for the reporting of this review. We received exemption through the Nova Scotia Health Research Ethics Board (REB FILE#:1029,525). The research team included patient partners in an advisory capacity.

This review follows the published protocol, “Initiatives to support nursing work for sustainability: a rapid umbrella review protocol” (Tomblin Murphy, Sampalli, Elliott-Rose, et al., 2025). Consistent with the published protocol and the rapid umbrella review design, selected review parameters were applied pragmatically to support a timely, policy-relevant synthesis, including limiting included reviews to those published between January 2014 and March 11, 2024. The review procedures included defining eligibility criteria; searching bibliographic databases and grey literature; removing duplicates; conducting title/abstract and full-text screening in duplicate; extracting data using a piloted template; critically appraising included reviews; and synthesizing findings narratively using the EPOC taxonomy.

This study employs an integrated knowledge translation (iKT) approach through active engagement of research team members representing the health system (delivery of health services), academia (pre-licensure education of nurses), government (policy development) (Canadian Institutes of Health Research, 2015) and patient advisors. Several members of the research team also provide guidance for the implementation of Nova Scotia’s Nursing Strategy. Nova Scotia’s Provincial Nursing Network (PNN) is another audience that has reflected the findings of this study. The PNN is a multi-sectoral network comprised of nurse representatives from post-secondary institutions, regulatory bodies, unions, and professional associations advising the provincial government on the Nursing Strategy and broader issues affecting the profession of nursing in the province.

These mechanisms help ensure that both knowledge users and the research team refine Nova Scotia’s Nursing Strategy to be both pertinent to nursing practice and grounded in evidence.

Eligibility criteria (PICO-informed framework and eligible study designs)

Inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented using a PICO-informed framework (Population, Intervention/interest, Context, and Outcomes), followed by the eligible review study designs.

Population

The population of interest was the nursing health workforce practicing within a healthcare setting. We further defined it as regulated nurses (i.e., registered nurses, registered practical nurses, licensed practical nurses), nurse-midwives, and advanced practice nurses (i.e., nurse practitioners, clinical nurse specialists, and advanced practice registered nurses). We included those within the defined population not providing direct patient care (e.g., nurse managers, administrators, and instructors/educators working in a post-licensure environment) if they worked within a healthcare environment. We excluded direct entry midwives, pre-licensure nurses not in clinical placements (i.e., students), nursing faculty working in a pre-licensure or post-secondary environment, and regulated non-nursing health professions (e.g., physicians). Articles that included a mix of nursing groups (e.g., newly qualified nurses and nursing students) were included.

Intervention/interest

Consistent with the protocol, this review focused on structures, interventions, and health system arrangements intended to stabilize, support, and sustain the nursing workforce through recruitment and retention. For the purposes of this review, these were understood to include policy and other structural options, organizational strategies, programs, planning approaches, delivery models, governance mechanisms, financial arrangements, and related implementation supports relevant to nursing workforce sustainability. We excluded reviews focused only on factors associated with recruitment and retention (e.g., job satisfaction or organizational commitment) when they did not describe or evaluate a responsive intervention.

Context

Eligible reviews were those examining these structures, interventions, and health system arrangements within health systems and health service delivery environments. We used the WHO definition of health systems and considered evidence from any country. We excluded reviews focused on recruitment or retention interventions outside the health system context, such as pre licensure education settings or other environments where service or care delivery was not the primary mandate.

Consistent with the protocol and the rapid review design, we limited inclusion to review-level sources published between January 2014 and March 11, 2024. This 10-year boundary was selected pragmatically to support timely completion of the review while maintaining contemporary evidence base relevant to current nursing workforce policy and practice. Due to language restrictions within the team and other resource limitations, we limited inclusion to sources written in English or French. This boundary was therefore framed as a pragmatic parameter of the rapid review design rather than a methodological claim that older evidence was unimportant; where relevant, older primary studies could still be represented within the included review-level literature.

Outcomes

We included reviews reporting any outcomes relevant to stabilizing, supporting, or enhancing the nursing workforce, including recruitment, retention, turnover, intention to stay or leave, job satisfaction, well-being, competence, patient care, and implementation-related findings such as barriers, enablers, feasibility, appropriateness, meaningfulness, and sustainability.

Eligible study designs

Because this was an umbrella review, we included review-level evidence only. Eligible designs were systematic reviews, scoping reviews, integrative reviews, and rapid reviews. This broader set of eligible review types reflects the pragmatic scope of the review and the methodologically diverse health services and policy literature relevant to nursing workforce sustainability.

Search strategy

Owing to the nature of our research question and the field of inquiry, we expanded the eligible review types to include those beyond traditional JBI umbrella reviews (limited to quantitative, qualitative, and mixed methods systematic reviews) and included other types of reviews, such as scoping reviews, integrative reviews, and rapid reviews. An information specialist developed the database search strategy. The search combined controlled vocabulary and keywords related to nurses, recruitment and retention, and review study designs. The initial database search was developed in MEDLINE All (Ovid) and peer reviewed by another information specialist (LB) using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) checklist (McGowan et al., 2016). The Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health (CADTH) systematic review search filter (Canada’s Drug Agency Search Filters, 2026) was modified and applied to identify eligible review types. The MEDLINE search was adapted for Embase (Elsevier), CINAHL Full Text (EBSCO), and Scopus (Elsevier). MEDLINE and Embase records were removed from the Scopus search results prior to export. All database searches were executed on March 11, 2024. Appendix A presents the full searches in each database.

A targeted grey literature search from April 11–16, 2024, was completed. Because this was an umbrella review, the grey literature search was designed to identify review type documents available on the websites of nursing associations and ministries of health rather than the full range of conventional grey literature outputs. We searched selected websites of international nursing associations and ministries of health across jurisdictions using Google’s site searching function. The grey literature search yielded an initial pool of 41,955 records through Google site searching. These records underwent a multistage screening process, including rapid title and snippet screening within the search interface to remove clearly irrelevant web content, before 50 sources were retained for full text review in Covidence. Because this umbrella review was restricted to review level evidence, grey literature sources that took the form of policy documents, organizational reports, or guidance materials were not eligible for inclusion. This targeted approach intentionally privileged review-type documents and therefore was not designed to capture the broader universe of conventional grey literature, such as standalone policy documents, workforce reports, or implementation guidance. Because review-type terminology was embedded in the search approach, many potentially relevant non-review grey literature sources would have been screened out as ineligible by design.

We uploaded all citations identified through both the database and the grey literature search to Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2022) and removed duplicates.

Two of ten reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of all citations (title and abstract screening stage, N = 2585), as well as full text sources (full text screening stage, N = 140), for inclusion. Members of the research team addressed conflicts through consensus. We recorded reasons for exclusion at the full text level (see Fig. 1). For grey literature, we also documented the predominant reason for exclusion at full text, as most screened sources were policy, organizational, or informational documents rather than eligible review level evidence. Data extraction and analysis

Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, 2022) and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for umbrella reviews, as described in Chapter 9 of the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis (Aromataris et al., 2024), informed the development of the data extraction template. Independent reviewers piloted the extraction template on two relevant studies and modified it to improve inter-rater clarity and consistency of extraction. Information extracted included reviews comprised review characteristics, purpose, methods and analyses, intervention or structure type, context, findings relevant to the review question, implementation considerations (including barriers, enablers, gaps, and opportunities), reported impacts or evaluation findings, and author recommendations. Two reviewers independently completed extractions, and members of the research team facilitated consensus. We appraised the included reviews using the JBI critical appraisal checklist for systematic reviews and research syntheses. Because the included evidence comprised systematic, scoping, integrative, and rapid reviews, we applied this checklist pragmatically as a common review-level appraisal framework rather than as a design-specific measure of methodological rigor. Appraisal results were used to support cautious interpretation of methodological and reporting rigor and were not used to exclude reviews or to generate directly comparable appraisal rankings across review designs. For non-systematic review types, checklist scores should therefore be interpreted as approximate indicators of methodological and reporting completeness.

We organized and synthesized the review findings within the four EPOC domains; Implementation Strategies, Delivery Arrangements, Governance Arrangements, and Financial Arrangements. Consistent with comparable umbrella-review approaches in health services and policy research, we then undertook a review-level narrative comparison within and across domains to identify recurrent analytic patterns, common implementation conditions, areas of convergence or inconsistency, and major evidence gaps. We summarized barriers, enablers, and lessons learned, and used research-team consensus to refine the final analytic interpretation rather than reanalyzing primary studies. We selected a single structured appraisal approach to support consistency across the review process while recognizing that full methodological equivalence among the included review designs could not be assumed.

Because the included reviews differed in design, scope, and analytic purpose, we did not treat them as directly comparable summaries of intervention effects. Consistent with the umbrella review design, we synthesized the included evidence narratively at the review level rather than retrieving or reanalyzing primary studies. Reviews were grouped by intervention category using the EPOC taxonomy, and their findings were interpreted in relation to review type, review purpose, and methodological rigor. This approach allowed us to integrate evidence on intervention types, reported outcomes, and implementation considerations while avoiding inappropriate pooling across fundamentally different review designs. We did not formally assess overlap of primary studies across included reviews; therefore, we did not aggregate primary study counts or treat repeated findings across reviews as independent confirmations, and all findings were interpreted cautiously at the review level.

We used guiding questions from the FAME framework for sources that did not formally evaluate structures (The Joanna Briggs Institute Levels of Evidence and Grades of Recommendation Working Party, 2014). This allowed us to reflect on the described structures and factors that would have to be considered for real-world practical application.

Patient and public involvement statement

(P42) We engaged two patient family advisors with the analysis phase (post-source extraction). Advisors shared documents for their review, included in team meetings, and invited to participate in relevant workshops. Specifically, they were engaged during the analysis phase following article extraction to provide a patient and family perspective on the interpretation of the findings. Their involvement was intended to support the practical relevance of the review; however, no protocol-specified changes to the review methods, eligibility criteria, or extraction processes were attributed to patient partner input. Their perspectives helped to shape the final reporting of findings.

Results

Characteristics of included sources

For the grey literature search, we identified 41,955 records using search strings developed by an information specialist. After title and abstract screening, 50 sources were retained for full text review. Following full text assessment, no grey literature sources met the eligibility criteria for inclusion. The predominant reason for exclusion was that these sources were not systematic, scoping, integrative, or rapid reviews; instead, they were primarily policy documents, organizational reports, guidance materials, or other non-review grey literature outputs. This exclusion pathway is shown in Fig. 1.

We extracted 69 peer reviewed reviews that met the eligibility criteria for inclusion. The included reviews were methodologically heterogeneous: 35 were systematic, 16 were integrative, 15 were scoping, and three were rapid reviews. Consistent with the umbrella review design, findings were synthesized narratively at the review level rather than combined quantitatively or reanalyzed at the primary study level. Interpretations were made in relation to review type, review purpose, and methodological and reporting rigor. Most reviews described the population of nurses broadly or included multiple types of nurses (n = 40). More specific populations included in some sources included newly graduated nurses (n = 32); licensed practical nurses (LPNs, n = 4); internationally educated nurses (IENs, n = 4); and advanced practice nurses (n = 3). A summary of included sources is available in Appendix C or through Mendeley Data (Tomblin Murphy, Sampalli, Elliott Rose, et al., 2025).

We noted substantial variation across the included reviews in terms of design, implementation focus, and reporting. Using the JBI checklist as a pragmatic review-level appraisal approach, many reviews met a mid-range number of checklist items: 41 sources scored between 5 and 7, 13 scored 8, six scored 9 or 10, and nine scored between 2 and 4 (Fig. 2). Because the included review types were methodologically heterogeneous and the appraisal tool was not designed specifically for all eligible review designs, these scores are reported descriptively as indicators of methodological and reporting completeness. Interventions to support nurse recruitment and retention

Most articles included reviews that mapped to Implementation Strategies (n = 60) or Delivery Arrangements (n = 8), whereas relatively few addressed Governance Arrangements or Financial Arrangements relevant to nursing workforce sustainability through recruitment and retention (n = 6). The identified interventions were heterogeneous in content, scope, and evaluation approach, and some reviews mapped to more than one EPOC category.

Across the included reviews, several analytic patterns were evident. First, interventions were more likely to report favourable workforce outcomes when they were multi-component rather than single component, particularly when they combined organizational support, relational leadership, and workforce development elements. Second, evidence was concentrated in transition-to-practice and acute care contexts, with relatively limited review-level evidence for non-acute settings, experienced non-RN groups, and long-term system-level policy levers. Third, although many interventions were associated with positive outcomes, the strength and comparability of this evidence were limited by methodological heterogeneity, inconsistent outcome measurement, and variable implementation detail across reviews.

Implementation Strategies focused on change at the level of organizations or related to the behaviors and practices of nurses. We identified seven synthesized implementation strategy groups: transition-to-practice and residency programs; preceptorship and clinical supervision; graduate transition and orientation programs; leadership and organizational culture interventions; mental health and resilience supports; team building and professional development initiatives; and targeted policy and implementation supports for recruitment and retention.

We included eight sources in the delivery arrangements subcategory. Delivery arrangement interventions synthesized in the evidence comprised four main groups: standardized and collaborative staffing models; self-scheduling and flexible work arrangements; rural and underserved preceptorship models; and retention-focused workplace support models for experienced nurses.

Delivery arrangements

Themes in delivery arrangements encompassed both intervention strategies (e.g., staffing models, scheduling) and their intended outcomes (e.g., patient safety, job satisfaction).

Standardized and collaborative nurse staffing models

Sources reported that standardized nurse staffing models leveraging metrics such as full-time equivalents (FTEs) and patient-to-nurse ratios significantly optimized workload distribution and improved organizational outcomes, including patient care and nurse retention (Adynski et al., 2022; Butler et al., 2019). These complementary strategies enabled more equitable task distribution, enhanced role clarity, and improved alignment between staffing levels and patient acuity. Notably, standardized staffing ratios alone were not consistently associated with improved retention, emphasizing the importance of integrating complementary strategies—such as workload-based matching, primary nursing assignments, and advanced practice roles—to enhance workforce outcomes and organizational performance. Methodological inconsistencies and variable reporting standards limited the ability to draw definitive conclusions, underscoring the need for context-sensitive, multifaceted staffing models supported by leadership and standardized evaluation frameworks (Adynski et al., 2022; Butler et al., 2019).

Self-Scheduling programs

Self-scheduling programs, where nurses have autonomy over their shifts, were consistently associated with improved morale, work-life balance, and job satisfaction. These initiatives, alongside broader policy-based work schedule strategies such as limiting overtime and enforcing staffing ratios (Bae, 2024), led to decreased absenteeism and turnover, suggesting greater organizational commitment among nurses (Koning, 2014). Success depended on structured implementation, leadership support, and shared decision-making. In this context, structured implementation refers to the intentional and coordinated rollout of self-scheduling programs—characterized by defined processes, supportive leadership engagement, and mechanisms for collaborative planning with staff. Successful adoption requires clear policies, designated support roles, and an incremental approach to roll-out to avoid unintended consequences across units.

Rural nursing preceptorships

Preceptorships tailored for rural and underserved regions supported recruitment and retention by fostering community connection, interprofessional collaboration, and a shared sense of purpose among students and staff. Key enablers included clear role definitions, ongoing faculty support, and rural-specific orientation sessions to mitigate challenges such as isolation and limited resources. A noted barrier was that preceptors often felt unprepared to provide students with evaluation feedback (Oosterbroek et al., 2017).

Leadership and recognition programs

Programs like shared governance and recognition initiatives (e.g., DAISY Awards) significantly empowered nurses, contributing to job satisfaction and stronger retention, especially among experienced staff. These interventions were relatively cost-effective and enhanced the workplace environment when paired with flexible scheduling and professional development opportunities (Williamson et al., 2022).

Retention strategies for experienced nurses

Evidence-based approaches for retaining experienced RNs included professional practice models, transformational leadership, flexible scheduling, teamwork strategies, and workplace safety enhancements (Montayre et al., 2023). These multi-faceted strategies consistently demonstrated reductions in turnover and improvements in retention (Lartey et al., 2014). However, methodological variability and lack of standardization in measuring outcomes limited cross-study comparisons, underscoring a need for robust and consistent evaluation frameworks.

Despite promising strategies, the delivery domain is characterized by inconsistent methodologies and underrepresentation of non-acute care, rural, and LPN roles, suggesting the need for broader and more inclusive research.

Implementation strategies

This domain featured a range of strategies within the sources supporting mental health, professional development, and transition to practice especially among students, newly graduated nurses (NGNs), and early-career professionals.

Transition-to-practice and residency programs

A wide range of strategies have been implemented to support newly qualified nurses (NQNs) as they transition into clinical practice, with a strong emphasis on structured programs, mentorship, and supportive work environments. Transition-to-practice models encompassing nurse residency programs (NRPs), internships, orientations, and fellowships—proved effective in enhancing nurse satisfaction, clinical competence, and retention (Asber, 2019; Bakon et al., 2018; Chant and Westendorf, 2019; Cochran, 2017; Eckerson, 2018; Edwards et al., 2015; Mohamed and Al-Hmaimat, 2024). Programs exceeding 12 months, guided by theoretical frameworks like Benner’s novice-to-expert model (Benner, 1982), showed retention rates over 90% (Chant and Westendorf, 2019). Findings indicate that comprehensive strategies combining mentorship, preceptorship, didactic training, immersive clinical placements, and clinical supervision supported by protected preceptor time, flexible scheduling, and structured mentor training significantly enhance nurse satisfaction, confidence, competence, and retention (Brook et al., 2019; Calleja et al., 2019; Chen and Lou, 2014; Moss and Jackson, 2019; Reebals et al., 2022; Van Camp and Chappy, 2017; Vidal and Olley, 2021; Weller-Newton et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2016). Tailored residency and transition to practice programs further promote resilience, professional growth, and a sense of belonging, with models such as UHC/AACN and Versant demonstrating particular effectiveness (Van Camp and Chappy, 2017). Similarly, structured strategies for internationally educated nurses, including ethical recruitment, bridging programs, and supports for the establishment of inclusive workplace environments, are essential for successful integration and long-term retention (Zulfiqar et al., 2023).

Preceptorship and clinical supervision

Preceptorship and clinical supervision programs significantly improved job satisfaction, perceived competence, and retention among newly qualified nurses (Aparício and Nicholson, 2020; Ke et al., 2017; Quek and Shorey, 2018; Zonneveld et al., 2024). High-impact models emphasized role clarity, protected time, and manageable workloads, with evidence supporting comprehensive preceptor training, low preceptor to preceptee ratios, validated selection tools, and an online platform to enhance engagement (Wray et al. 2021). Both formal and informal strategies were found to increase strength, confidence, and organizational commitment (Wray et al., 2021). Formal strategies included preceptorships, mentorship programs, and internships, while informal strategies encompassed peer recognition, supportive workplace culture, and opportunities for professional development (Wray et al., 2021). Technology-enhanced support platforms also contributed to confidence, job satisfaction, and long-term retention (Webster et al., 2020). Preceptor-preceptee mismatches and unclear expectations were common challenges, highlighting the importance of institutional support and structured frameworks.

Graduate transition and orientation programs

Graduate transition programs varied in length and content but were most effective when combining theoretical and clinical learning. Programs incorporating Quality and Safety Education for Nurses (QSEN) competencies or the Association of Critical-Care Nurses (AACN) Synergy Model showed enhanced job satisfaction, competence, and patient care (Bakon et al., 2018; Laflamme and Hyrkas, 2020; Missen et al., 2014; Peltokoski et al., 2016). For IENs, tailored clinical bridging programs combined with strong organizational support through recognition, inclusive environments, and structured mentorship or preceptorship were identified as effective strategies to build practical competencies and support workplace integration (Njie-Mokonya et al., 2023). Together, these approaches highlight the value of structured, evidence-based programs that address both professional development and contextual support to optimize transition and retention across diverse nursing populations.

Leadership and organizational culture

Transformational, authentic, and servant leadership approaches that foster engagement, cohesion, as well as resilience, and psychologically safe environments, emerged as pivotal to staff retention (Conroy et al., 2023; Cummings et al., 2018; De Vries et al., 2023; Halter et al., 2017; Horvath and Carter, 2022; LeJeune, 2023; Long, 2020). Organizational alignment with strategic goals, shared governance and recognition, as well as enhanced organizational supports including rewards, stress management, and supervisor assistance, further enhance retention by reducing turnover intentions (Galanis et al., 2024). Supportive supervisory relationships, marked by effective communication and collaborative decision making, improve job satisfaction and care outcomes in long-term care settings (McGilton et al., 2016). Strategies that support workforce diversity, including inclusive policies, cultural competence and communication training, orientation, mentorship, and career advancement pathways, also promote integration and stability (Kamau et al., 2022). Complementary interventions, such as educational programs addressing incivility and Clinical Ladder Programs for professional growth and leadership development, further strengthen organizational culture, though evidence on cost-effectiveness and direct patient outcomes remains limited (Olsen et al., 2020; Slagle et al., 2023).

Mental health and resilience support

Programs supporting mental health and resilience, particularly during transitions, reduce burnout and dropout intentions (Bakker et al., 2020; Concilio et al., 2019; Stewart et al., 2024; Westermann et al., 2014). Effective strategies combine person-directed interventions, such as stress management, mindfulness, communication training, and relaxation techniques, with organizational approaches. Organizational approaches include resilience-focused residency models, supportive preceptors, peer support, and wellness-focused environments like 24/7 restorative or serenity rooms (Mileski et al., 2024). These combined strategies sustain long-term well-being and foster a culture that protects nurses’ mental health. Workplace incivility, verbal aggression, and lack of support remain key barriers to resilience and workforce retention.

Team building and professional development

Creating supportive and cohesive work environments through team-building and professional development is a critical strategy for enhancing communication, collaboration, and job satisfaction among nurses (Concilio et al., 2019; Niskala et al., 2020). Tools such as e-portfolios support reflective learning and ongoing skill development (Connelly et al., 2023). Educational programs, mentorship, and professional development initiatives enable autonomous practice, provide career progression pathways, and offer financial or training incentives, particularly in rural or underserved settings, thereby supporting recruitment and retention of nurse practitioners (de Bruin et al., 2022; Kueakomoldej et al., 2022). Specialty nurse certification validates advanced clinical expertise, strengthens organizational commitment, and can improve patient care, though variations in certification practices limit conclusions about overall effectiveness (Whitehead et al., 2019). Continuing professional development initiatives that combine formal and experiential learning, aligned with nurses’ career stages and supported by organizational investment, further enhance competence, confidence, and job satisfaction (Vázquez-Calatayud and Eseverri-Azcoiti, 2023).

Policy interventions and targeted incentives

Policy interventions such as wage increases, nurse residency programs, and targeted incentives—both financial and non-financial—are associated with improved recruitment, retention, and full-time employment opportunities, particularly in underserved or rural areas (Park and Yu, 2019).

Governance arrangements

Governance-related strategies focused on accreditation, ethical recruitment, and oversight mechanisms that support workforce integration and retention.

Accreditation and magnet recognition

Magnet accreditation represents a formal governance model structured around transformational leadership, professional empowerment, innovation, and outcome-based practices (Petit Dit Dariel and Regnaux, 2015). It has been associated with improved workplace culture, higher job satisfaction, and lower staff turnover by aligning leadership and organizational processes with staff engagement and collaboration (Márquez-Hernández et al., 2020; Petit Dit Dariel and Regnaux, 2015). While evidence suggests potential improvements in healthcare outcomes, findings are mixed, and high costs along with limited international applicability pose challenges to broader scalability.

Ethical talent management and recruitment of IENs

For IENs, governance frameworks emphasizing ethical recruitment, onboarding, mentorship, and career development were critical (Njie-Mokonya et al., 2023; Zulfiqar et al., 2023). Success depended on diversity training, transparent role communication, and equitable pay. Barriers included discrimination, lack of orientation, and career stagnation, particularly affecting nurses from low-income countries.

Governance oversight of bridging programs

Clinical bridging programs helped IENs develop foundational competencies and integrate into acute care settings (Njie-Mokonya et al., 2023). Effective governance included culturally sensitive program design, structured preceptor support, and institutional involvement. Persistent challenges included ambiguity in the preceptor role, inadequate training, and a lack of standardization in implementation.

Financial arrangements

Compensation-related strategies played a key role in nurse retention and recruitment, particularly for IENs and in regions facing shortages.

Financial incentives for IENs

Enhanced financial packages—including competitive salaries and bonuses—supported retention of IENs by addressing disparities in compensation and career progression (Zulfiqar et al., 2023). Financial incentives were most effective when embedded within broader professional development and mentoring frameworks. These approaches also mitigate the negative effects of undesirable shift assignments and workplace dissatisfaction. However, their long-term cost-effectiveness remains underexplored.

Salary increases to address nursing shortages

In countries facing acute shortages, salary increases and financial rewards were used as part of a broader approach to support workforce stability (Shamsi and Peyravi, 2020). These were typically implemented alongside strategies such as increasing nursing school admissions and public outreach to elevate the profession’s image.

Recognition as a cost-effective incentive

Recognition initiatives, such as DAISY Awards and formal appreciation programs, were inexpensive yet powerful tools for retention (Williamson et al., 2022). When combined with flexible scheduling and shared governance, they fostered morale, gratitude, and engagement. Their success hinged on strong leadership support and a culture of appreciation. The need for longitudinal studies remains to establish causal links with workforce outcomes.

Discussion

In this umbrella review, we synthesized review-level evidence on structures, interventions, and health system arrangements intended to stabilize, support, and sustain the nursing workforce. This extends earlier literature in several ways. First, whereas many prior reviews have concentrated on factors, facilitators, or barriers associated with nurse recruitment and retention, this review foregrounded review-level evidence on responsive structures and system-level options. Second, compared with prior rapid work undertaken in Canadian settings, such as Ben-Ahmed and Bourgeault (2023), it offers a broader international synthesis. Third, by organizing the findings across implementation strategies, delivery arrangements, governance arrangements, and financial arrangements, it clarifies both where the review-level evidence is concentrated and where major gaps remain.

Taken together, the findings support the view that nursing workforce sustainability is unlikely to be achieved through any single structure or intervention. Rather, the review-level evidence points toward combinations of organizational, practice, governance, and policy responses that fit local workforce realities. This is consistent with broader nursing workforce literature emphasizing complexity and supportive work environments, but the present review adds a more explicit EPOC-guided map of the structures currently represented in the review literature. In particular, the evidence base remains concentrated in implementation and delivery approaches, while governance and financial arrangements remain comparatively underexamined despite their likely importance in shaping workforce conditions over time.

Several cross-cutting themes emerged from the review-level synthesis. Structures were more likely to be associated with favorable reported outcomes when they were multi-component, context-sensitive, and supported by leadership. The synthesis also suggests that promising approaches were not only relational or cultural in nature; they were often linked to delivery conditions such as staffing fit, scheduling flexibility, protected support for transition, and inclusive mechanisms for integrating internationally educated nurses. At the same time, the review highlights persistent weaknesses in the literature, including limited long-term evaluation, inconsistent outcome measurement, and underrepresentation of rural, outpatient, and non-RN contexts.

Comparison across the four EPOC domains suggests that the most developed review-level evidence concerns structures operating close to nurses’ day-to-day work, particularly transition supports, leadership, mentorship, staffing design, and scheduling flexibility. This pattern matters because it suggests that the literature has framed workforce sustainability more often as an organizational and practice-level challenge than as a governance or financing issue. By contrast, governance and financial arrangements were less frequently represented, even though higher-level policy decisions are likely to shape the conditions under which other workforce structures succeed.

A key contribution of this review to contemporary nursing practice is that it shifts the conversation from why nurses leave to which clusters of structures appear most actionable for health systems and nursing leaders. For practice, the findings suggest that workforce sustainability is most likely to be supported through coordinated approaches that combine transition support, flexible and collaborative staffing arrangements, supportive leadership, recognition, mental health supports, and inclusive integration pathways for internationally educated nurses. For decision-makers, the review further indicates that practice supports, organizational arrangements, and broader policy structures need to be considered together rather than as isolated retention initiatives.

These findings are also relevant to contemporary policy development. For example, coordinated system-level tools such as the national nursing retention toolkit developed by Health Canada reflect growing interest in multi-pronged retention and recruitment responses and illustrate the type of policy and practice integration to which this review speaks.

These findings point to several priorities for future review and primary research. Studies should move beyond descriptive reporting of promising strategies and examine how bundles of structures perform across settings, career stages, and workforce groups over time. In particular, more evidence is needed on governance and financial arrangements, rural and underserved contexts, non-RN groups, and the ways in which diversity, representation, and equity are incorporated into workforce structures and their evaluation.

The paucity, complexity, and varying rigor of the available review-level evidence preclude definitive identification of best practices for stabilizing, supporting, and sustaining the nursing workforce. This interpretation should be made cautiously because the included reviews were methodologically heterogeneous and a single appraisal tool was applied pragmatically across different review designs. Accordingly, the appraisal findings are better understood as broad indicators of methodological and reporting rigor than as precise or directly comparable measures of overall evidence strength across all included review types. Additional limitations include minimal evidence in care settings outside acute care, limited rigorous evaluation and implementation reporting, a lack of long-term standardized outcome data, and limited consideration of diversity and representation. Future strategies must incorporate rigorous implementation and evaluation plans, including focusing on long-term impacts of such strategies, and better disseminate learnings. Urgent attention must be given to the development of improved data systems to enable better tracking and evaluation of the nursing workforce (Nagle and White, 2025).

Ultimately, this study contributes a structured review-level synthesis of heterogeneous review evidence on nursing workforce sustainability. By mapping identified structures across the EPOC domains and highlighting where evidence is concentrated or sparse, it helps bridge high-level policy priorities and frontline practice considerations. This contribution is relevant for decision-makers who must respond to urgent workforce pressures while selecting strategies that are context-sensitive, equitable, and feasible within evolving health system conditions.

Limitations

The broader policy context informing this review specifically its alignment with work supporting a provincial Canadian nursing strategy (Nova Scotia) may have influenced aspects of the review design, including the framing of research questions, prioritization of interventions, inclusion decisions, and interpretation of findings. While this context strengthened the policy relevance and applicability of the review within Canadian settings, it may have introduced a degree of contextual bias toward health system structures, workforce models, and policy priorities characteristic of high-income, publicly funded systems. As a result, certain approaches or innovations from other jurisdictions that are less aligned with the Canadian context may have been underemphasized, which should be considered when interpreting the transferability of findings to different health system and policy environments.

We adhered to the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology for umbrella reviews, as described in Chapter 9 of the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis (Aromataris et al., 2024); however, we introduced modifications necessitated by the significant breadth and varying methodological and reporting rigor of the literature. For example, the diverse nature of nursing workforce interventions and their associated characteristics led to team discussions that supported the literature refinement of inclusion/exclusion criteria. Although helpful, these iterative discussions limited our ability to follow the JBI umbrella review methodology in a linear manner.

A further limitation relates to critical appraisal. Because the included reviews comprised systematic, scoping, integrative, and rapid reviews, no single appraisal tool was ideally suited to all review designs. We therefore applied the JBI critical appraisal checklist for systematic reviews and research syntheses pragmatically across the included sample to support a consistent review-level appraisal process; however, this checklist is better aligned with some review types than others. Consequently, appraisal scores should be interpreted cautiously, particularly when comparing across review designs, and should not be taken as definitive indicators of overall strength of evidence.

The choice of a rapid umbrella review design reflected a pragmatic methodological selection given the scope of the work. While several review questions were exploratory and mapping-oriented and may also align with scoping review methodologies, the umbrella review approach was selected to synthesize existing review-level evidence and generate higher-order policy-relevant findings within constrained timelines. This approach may nevertheless have limited inclusion of emerging primary studies and broader contextual evidence that could have been captured through a scoping review design.

A further limitation of this umbrella review is the potential overlap of primary studies across the included reviews. Because umbrella reviews synthesize evidence from multiple reviews, the same underlying primary studies may be represented more than once, which can inflate the apparent weight of particular findings. As 69 reviews were included, such overlap may have influenced the overall synthesis. This overlap was not formally assessed and should therefore be considered when interpreting the findings.

Although the review sought evidence relevant to health systems internationally, the targeted grey literature search had limited global representativeness. It was conducted to support a broader program of work informing Nova Scotia’s Nursing Strategy, was restricted to English- and French-language sources, and relied on selected ministry and nursing association websites identified through Google site searching. As a result, grey literature from jurisdictions using other dominant languages, different web-hosting practices, or less searchable web indexing may have been underrepresented.

In addition, because the grey literature search was oriented toward review-level evidence to align with the umbrella review design, it was not intended to function as a comprehensive search for conventional grey literature such as policy documents, implementation reports, guidance documents, or workforce strategies. This design choice should be considered when interpreting the absence of included grey literature sources.

The restriction to English- and French-language sources across both the peer-reviewed and grey literature searches introduces a risk of language bias and limits the global representativeness of the findings. Evidence from non-Anglophone and non-Francophone jurisdictions, particularly from regions where innovative workforce models and policy approaches are emerging may not have been captured. This may skew the synthesis toward high-income, English-dominant contexts and constrain the transferability of findings to diverse health system settings, including low- and middle-income countries and Indigenous or multilingual contexts.

Finally, although the grey literature search was international in intent, its practical orientation reflected the broader Canadian policy context of the program of work, particularly Nova Scotia. This may have influenced the selection of websites searched and the interpretation of relevance.

As reflected in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1), the grey literature search yielded no included sources because most screened full text records were not eligible review types, which should be considered when interpreting the contribution of grey literature to this review. This pattern should therefore be interpreted as reflecting both the review-level eligibility criteria and the targeted design of the grey literature search, rather than an absence of all relevant grey literature on the topic.

The heterogeneity of included review types (systematic, scoping, integrative, and rapid reviews) introduces challenges for synthesis consistency and should be considered when interpreting the comparability and overall coherence of findings across the evidence base.

The application of a single critical appraisal checklist across methodologically diverse review types may not have fully captured differences in methodological rigor and reporting completeness and should be considered when interpreting appraisal results.

The choice of a rapid umbrella review design, rather than a scoping review, reflects a methodological and pragmatic trade-off. While the research question is exploratory and mapping-oriented, and may align with scoping review methodologies (e.g., Tricco et al., 2017), the decision to focus on review-level evidence supported synthesis of higher-order findings within constrained timelines. This approach may have limited the inclusion of emerging primary studies and broader contextual evidence typically captured in scoping reviews and should be considered when interpreting the scope and comprehensiveness of findings.

Conclusions

We identified 15 synthesized intervention groups, with transition-to-practice programs and flexible work arrangements among the most frequently represented in the included review literature. This EPOC-organized synthesis offers health system decision-makers a review-level map of evidence-informed options to inform strategic planning, while also signalling where important evidence gaps remain.
