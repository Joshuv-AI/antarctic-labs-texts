Is ambulatory flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy safe and feasible? evidence from a propensity score–matched cohort
Authors: Cong Tian, Jie Gu, Lianming Wang, Liulin Xiong, Bo Yang, Hao Hu, Tao Xu, Junjun Xu, Jun Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the safety, efficacy, and health economic value of flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy (fURS) for upper urinary tract stones in ambulatory surgery compared to traditional inpatient management.MethodsA retrospective cohort study analyzed 174 fURS cases performed by a single surgeon between January and December 2024. Patients were divided into ambulatory and inpatient groups. Propensity score matching (PSM, 1:1 ratio) was applied to balance baseline differences. Demographic data, perioperative parameters, and outcomes including stone-free rate (SFR, assessment via abdominal X-ray within 24 h postoperatively, with residual fragments > 2 mm considered significant), complication rates (Clavien-Dindo classification), hospitalization duration, and costs were analyzed.ResultsA total of 174 patients with upper urinary tract stones were included. Pre-matching analysis revealed that the inpatient group had significantly older age (58.55 ± 12.52 vs. 50.14 ± 12.79 years, P < 0.001), larger cumulative stone diameter (1.67 ± 0.74 vs. 1.34 ± 0.57 cm, P = 0.001), and longer operative time (53.09 ± 26.64 vs. 41.30 ± 21.45 min, P = 0.002). No significant differences were observed in postoperative complications (2.33% vs. 1.14%, P = 0.491) or SFR (80.23% vs. 82.95%, P = 0.789). The ambulatory group demonstrated a 74.6% reduction in hospitalization duration (1.09 ± 0.29 vs. 4.29 ± 1.14 days, P < 0.001) and lower unadjusted total costs (27,075.41 ± 4,112.93 vs. 29,240.94 ± 3,856.12 RMB, P < 0.001). Post-PSM analysis confirmed comparable baseline characteristics, with the ambulatory group maintaining shorter hospitalization (1.12 ± 0.33 vs. 4.18 ± 1.09 days, P < 0.001), with no statistically significant difference in total costs between the two groups (27,378.33 ± 3768.93 vs. 28,639.67 ± 4,033.38 RMB, P = 0.106).ConclusionAmbulatory fURS represents a safe and effective treatment option for selected patients with upper urinary tract stones. This model substantially reduces hospitalization duration without compromising surgical outcomes or patient safety in carefully selected patients treated by experienced surgeons.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02129-x.

Introduction

Upper urinary tract stones (renal and ureteral stones) are highly prevalent worldwide, with an increasing incidence in recent years [1]. These stones not only cause severe pain, hematuria, and urinary tract obstruction but also lead to renal dysfunction, significantly compromising patients’ quality of life. With advancements in minimally invasive techniques, flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy (fURS) has emerged as a mainstream treatment for upper urinary tract stones due to its minimal invasiveness and rapid recovery [2, 3]. However, the high medical resource utilization associated with traditional inpatient fURS limits its clinical efficiency.

The ambulatory surgery model, characterized by “same-day admission, surgery, and discharge within 24 hours,” was first introduced in 2001 and has been widely applied in procedures such as laparoscopic cholecystectomy, inguinal hernia repair, and varicose vein. This model significantly reduces hospitalization duration and optimizes healthcare resource allocation. Previous studies have reported the feasibility and safety of urological stone-related procedures in ambulatory setting [4]. Nevertheless, the widespread adoption of this model faces systemic challenges. For fURS, key concerns include preoperative control of urinary tract infections, intraoperative management of elevated renal pelvic pressure, early identification and management of postoperative complications (particularly infections), feasibility of post-discharge pain management, and the risk of short-term readmission [3, 5].

While previous studies have validated the feasibility and safety of ambulatory ureteroscopic lithotripsy for upper urinary tract stones, gaps remain in the evidence base for this model in the Chinese clinical setting, particularly regarding analyses of perioperative outcomes and resource utilization [6]. This study retrospectively analyzed clinical data from patients undergoing fURS for upper urinary tract stones in ambulatory surgery setting, aiming to evaluate stone-free rates, postoperative complication rates, hospitalization costs, and other outcomes. Propensity score matching (PSM) analysis was employed to balance baseline characteristics between groups. The findings will provide evidence-based support for standardizing the ambulatory surgery model in urological practice while offering theoretical insights into optimizing healthcare resource allocation and improving patient treatment experiences.

Methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study included patients who fURS for upper ureteral or renal stones performed by a single senior urologist (surgical experience > 10 years) between January 2024 and December 2024. The allocation of patients to the ambulatory or inpatient group was determined based on a comprehensive assessment of patients’ systemic comorbidities and preoperative anesthetic evaluation results, in conjunction with the patients’ own preferences for the surgical management mode. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Age 18–75 years; (2) Preoperative CT-confirmed renal or upper ureteral stones; (3) Complete follow-up data. The exclusion criteria included the following: (1)Severe urinary tract infection, coagulation disorders, or pregnancy; (2)Bilateral stones requiring simultaneous surgery; (3) Conversion to percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL). The follow-up period began at admission and ended at 1 month postoperatively, with follow-up conducted via outpatient visits or telephone interviews.

Data collection

Perioperative data included: Demographics and baseline characteristics: Age, sex, Body Mass Index (BMI), American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification System (ASA) classification, and medical history (hypertension, diabetes, coronary artery disease, etc.);Surgical details: Cumulative stone diameter (defined as the sum of the maximum diameters of all stones in coronal/axial/sagittal planes), stone type, stone location, preoperative nephrostomy/stent placement, and operative time; Outcome measures: Postoperative vital signs, fever, blood transfusion, special medication use, reoperation, ICU admission, complications, hospital stay duration, hospitalization costs, and stone-free rate (assessed via abdominal X-ray within 24 h postoperatively; residual fragments > 2 mm were considered significant).Sepsis was diagnosed using the Sepsis-3.0 criteria [7]. Postoperative complications were classified into 5 grades according to the modified Clavien-Dindo grading system [8,9].

Perioperative management

Preoperative preparation

All patients underwent urinalysis and urine culture during outpatient visits. Those with positive cultures received culture-directed antibiotics until two consecutive negative results were achieved. A single dose of prophylactic antibiotics (second-generation cephalosporin/levofloxacin/etimicin) was administered intravenously 30 min preoperatively.

Surgical procedure

Under laryngeal mask general anesthesia: Cystoscopy was performed, and a hydrophilic guidewire (Brad®) was placed into the affected ureteric orifice. For upper ureteral stones, pneumatic lithotripsy was used to retropulse stones into the renal pelvis; for renal stones, the guidewire was advanced directly into the renal pelvis. A ureteral access sheath (Flexor® 12/14F, Cook Medical) was placed over the guidewire. Stones were fragmented using a flexible ureteroscope with holmium laser (Power Suite 60 W Plus, Lumenis; settings: 0.8–1.2 J, 10–20 Hz). Larger fragments were retrieved with a nitinol basket (NGage®, Cook Medical). A 6Fr double-J stent (Bard®) was routinely placed. Postoperative care: Inpatients: Urinary catheter retained postoperatively; removed after normal KUB findings on postoperative day 1. Ambulatory patients: Single catheterization postoperatively; discharged after confirming normal KUB and blood tests. The double-J stent was removed via outpatient cystoscopy at 14 days.

Discharge criteria

Ambulatory surgery group

Normal voiding function post-catheter removal (no dysuria or gross hematuria); Stable vital signs (afebrile, no chills); Normal mobility (independent ambulation); Normal neurological status (no headache/dizziness); Normal digestive function (no nausea/vomiting). Normal postoperative KUB.

Patients can be discharged 2 h postoperatively if they meet the above clinical criteria and have none of the special circumstances listed below: (1) presence of comorbidities (e.g., uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes mellitus) that required close monitoring of vital signs postoperatively; (2) intraoperative findings (e.g., excessive bleeding, ureteral edema, suspected intraoperative infection) suggesting a need for short-term observation; (3) occurrence of special postoperative conditions such as severe pain, severe hematuria, and significant leukopenia; (4) social factors (e.g., lack of a caregiver at home) that made same-day discharge unsafe.

Inpatient group

Standard discharge criteria applied, with additional emphasis on Daily fluid intake (2000–2500 mL); Self-monitoring (urinary output, temperature, pain score).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.4.2. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD or median [Interquartile Range]; categorical variables as frequencies (%). Intergroup comparisons used independent t-tests/Mann-Whitney U tests (continuous) or chi-square/Fisher’s exact tests (categorical). Propensity score matching (PSM, 1:1 ratio, caliper = 0.1) balanced baseline differences. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 174 patients with upper urinary tract stones were included, comprising 88 in the ambulatory surgery group and 86 in the inpatient group. Key demographic and clinical data are summarized in Table 1. Inpatients were significantly older than ambulatory patients (58.55 ± 12.52 vs. 50.14 ± 12.79 years, P < 0.001), with no sex disparity (P = 0.923). ASA classification (P = 0.108) and BMI (26.16 ± 3.57 vs. 25.54 ± 3.73, P = 0.267) were comparable between groups. Inpatients had larger cumulative stone diameters (1.67 ± 0.74 vs. 1.34 ± 0.57 cm, P = 0.001) and longer operative times (53.09 ± 26.64 vs. 41.30 ± 21.45 min, P = 0.002). No differences were observed in preoperative nephrostomy (P = 0.254) or double-J stent placement rates (P = 0.632). Hospitalization costs were higher for inpatients (¥29,240.94 ± 3,856.12 vs. ¥27,075.41 ± 4,112.93, P < 0.001). Ambulatory patients had significantly shorter hospital stays (1.09 ± 0.29 vs. 4.29 ± 1.14 days, P < 0.001). Stone-free rates (82.95% vs. 80.23%, P = 0.789) and complication rates (2.33% vs. 1.14%, P = 0.491) were similar, with all complications being Clavien-Dindo grade I. No difference was found in 30-day readmission rates (P = 0.506). One ambulatory patient (an elderly male with benign prostatic hyperplasia) was readmitted for febrile urinary retention and discharged after antibiotic therapy. Discussion

This retrospective propensity score–matched study systematically compared the safety, effectiveness, and health system impact of ambulatory versus inpatient flexible ureteroscopic lithotripsy (fURS) for upper urinary tract stones. Our findings demonstrate that, with experienced surgeons, intraoperative use of a negative pressure sheath, and optimal perioperative management, ambulatory fURS enables rapid and efficient 2-hour postoperative discharge in carefully selected patients, while achieving comparable stone-free rates and postoperative safety outcomes to conventional inpatient management and significantly reducing hospitalization duration. These results support the feasibility of expanding ambulatory fURS within modern urological practice.

After adjustment for baseline differences using propensity score matching, no significant differences were observed between groups in terms of postoperative complications, stone-free rates, or 30-day readmission rates. Importantly, all recorded complications were minor (Clavien–Dindo grade I), and no severe infectious or surgical adverse events occurred. These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that fURS, when performed under standardized protocols, is associated with low morbidity. However, compared with earlier studies reporting complication rates ranging from 0.3% to over 30% [10], the notably low complication rate in our cohort highlights the importance of strict perioperative management and patient selection.

Several factors may explain the favorable safety profile observed in the ambulatory group. First, rigorous preoperative infection control was implemented, including routine urine culture screening and culture-directed antibiotic therapy until confirmed negativity. Second, standardized intraoperative techniques—such as routine use of ureteral access sheaths (include flexible and navigable ureteral access sheath [11]), and controlled laser settings—likely reduced the risk of bacterial translocation and postoperative infection. Third, clearly defined discharge criteria and routine placement of double-J stents provided additional postoperative safety, enabling early discharge without increasing complication or readmission rates. However, the notably low complication rate observed in this study should be interpreted with caution and has several important limitations that restrict its generalizability. This study was a single-center retrospective analysis with procedures performed exclusively by a high-volume, experienced urologist with over 10 years of fURS practice, which ensured strict adherence to standardized perioperative and surgical protocols that may not be universally replicable in low-volume centers or by less experienced surgeons. Additionally, the study cohort was carefully selected based on strict inclusion criteria (e.g., exclusion of severe urinary tract infection, bilateral stones), which may have created a lower-risk patient population and thus contributed to the low complication rate, rather than the ambulatory surgery model alone.

Although the overall outcomes were comparable, subgroup analysis within the ambulatory cohort revealed that a small proportion of patients required overnight observation. These patients were generally older, predominantly female, and had higher ASA classifications, suggesting a greater burden of comorbidities. This finding underscores the critical role of patient selection in ambulatory fURS. While most patients can be safely discharged within 24 h, elderly individuals and those with multiple comorbid conditions may benefit from extended postoperative monitoring, even when managed within an ambulatory framework. Such risk stratification may help optimize safety while maintaining efficiency.

From a health services perspective, ambulatory fURS was associated with a significantly shorter hospitalization duration both before and after propensity score matching. While unadjusted total hospitalization costs appeared lower in the ambulatory group, this difference was not statistically significant following PSM adjustment. Thus, the primary demonstrated benefit of the ambulatory fURS model is the reduction in hospital stay duration, with no definitive evidence of a direct reduction in total costs after accounting for baseline differences between groups. Shorter inpatient stays may potentially contribute to more efficient healthcare resource allocation by enhancing bed turnover efficiency and reducing inpatient occupancy, a practical consideration for high-volume tertiary medical centers.

This study has several limitations. A retrospective single-center design, even with propensity score matching, may introduce selection bias. All procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon, limiting generalizability to low-volume or less-experienced centers. Stone-free rate (SFR) was assessed by 24-hour postoperative KUB, which is less sensitive than non-contrast CT for detecting small residual fragments (> 2 mm) and may overestimate actual SFR. Long-term outcomes (e.g., stone recurrence) and patient-reported measures (e.g., postoperative discomfort, satisfaction) were not evaluated, and indirect costs and socioeconomic impacts were excluded from cost analysis. Future multicenter prospective studies or RCTs are warranted to validate these findings, optimize patient selection, and adopt CT as the gold standard for SFR assessment to obtain more accurate results.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ambulatory fURS represents a safe and effective treatment option for selected patients with upper urinary tract stones. This model substantially reduces hospitalization duration without compromising surgical outcomes or patient safety in carefully selected patients treated by experienced surgeons, which constitutes the primary clinical benefit observed in the present study.
