Prevalence, antibiotic susceptibility and associated risk factors for extended spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Enterobacteriaceae among patients suspected with urinary tract infection at Nigist Eleni Mohammed Memorial Hospital, Ethiopia
Authors: Hadbacho Tsegaye Erancho, Duressa Shafi Ahmed, Sintayehu Fekadu, Deneke Wolde, Habtamu Teshale Shinato, Abdulhakim Mussema, Tsegaye Alemayehu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNowadays, increasing extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing Enterobacteriaceae, which were a common cause of urinary tract infection, have become a global concern because they induce resistance to most of the antimicrobial classes and make treatment difficult.ObjectiveThis study aimed to determine the prevalence of extended spectrum beta-lactamase producing Enterobacteriaceae, antibiotic susceptibility and its associated factors among patients suspected with urinary tract infection at Wachemo University Nigist Eleni Mohammed memorial Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Hosanna, Ethiopia.MethodsA hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted from May 10 to July 31, 2024, involving 287 patients suspected of having urinary tract infections. Socio-demographic information, clinical data, and potential risk factors were collected using the Kobo Toolbox software. Urine samples were obtained from each participant, mixed thoroughly, and aseptically inoculated onto MacConkey agar and CLED agar using a standard calibrated loop. Identification of Enterobacteriaceae isolates was performed using standard bacteriological methods. Antibiotic susceptibility testing was carried out using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion technique. Extended spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) production was confirmed through the double-disk synergy test and other standard phenotypic methods. Data were entered and analyzed using SPSS version 29. Both univariate and multivariable analyses were conducted to assess associations between ESBL production and the collected variables.ResultsThe proportion of urinary tract infections caused by Enterobacteriaceae was 33% (95% CI: 27.7–38.9). A total of 95 Enterobacteriaceae isolates were identified, with Escherichia coli predominating (62.1%), followed by Klebsiella spp. (23.2%). The overall prevalence of extended spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae was 26.3% (95% CI: 17.8–36.4), with E. coli accounting for 64% of cases and Klebsiella spp. for 24%. ESBL-producing isolates demonstrated complete resistance (100%) to cefotaxime and ceftriaxone. Resistance rates to other antibiotics were as follows: norfloxacin (80%), nalidixic acid (68%), ciprofloxacin (60%), amoxicillin-clavulanic acid (48%), gentamicin (36%), and amikacin (28%). In contrast, lower resistance was observed to meropenem (8%) and nitrofurantoin (20%). Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified three independent risk factors for ESBL production: a previous history of urinary tract infection (AOR = 8.002), prior urinary catheterization (AOR = 3.385), and history of urinary tract surgery (AOR = 4.535).ConclusionESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae pose a major challenge in urinary tract infections due to their high prevalence and resistance to commonly prescribed antibiotics. Prior urinary tract infections, catheterization, and urinary tract surgery were identified as independent risk factors, underscoring the importance of preventive strategies. These findings highlight the urgent need for region-specific antimicrobial stewardship programs, improved diagnostic capacity, and continuous surveillance to curb multidrug resistance and ensure effective patient outcomes.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-12969-5.

Introduction

Background

Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs) are enzymes that cleave the beta-lactam ring of antibiotics grouped under the beta-lactam families [1], confer resistance to a variety of antibiotics, including penicillin, cephalosporins, and aztreonam [2]. The exposure to various beta-lactams, such as penicillin, ampicillin, amoxicillin, cefazolin, cefoxitin, and imipenem, can induce the production of these beta-lactamases [3].

Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases are widely distributed across the globe and can be found in most Enterobacteriaceae family. They are considered to be one of the most concerning groups of beta-lactamases in the field of clinical practice. ESBLs play a significant role in causing both hospital-acquired and community-acquired infections [4]. In regions where ESBL-producing bacteria are common, the transmission of antibiotic-resistance genes can occur through human exposure to antibiotic residues, antibiotic-resistant bacteria, and residence, particularly through the consumption of animal and plant products [5].

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is infectious syndrome that affects the urinary tract anywhere from the urethra to the kidneys [6], and is identified as the presence of microbial pathogens within the urinary tract, accompanied by associated symptoms [7]. There are two main categories of urinary tract infections: uncomplicated urinary tract infections and complicated urinary tract infections. Uncomplicated urinary tract infections are the most common and occur without any blockage in the urinary tract. On the other hand, complicated urinary tract infections indicate the presence of abnormalities and obstructions within the urinary tract [8].

Urinary tract infections are recognized as a significant infectious condition affecting individuals of all genders and age ranges, including infants, children, adults, and the elderly [9]. Females are more susceptible to UTI due to their shorter urethra [10]. At least once in their lifetime a significant majority of women, exceeding 50%, visit healthcare facilities as a result of UTI. Subsequent to receiving antibiotic treatment, the infection is prone to recurs in around 20 to 30% of women within a span of 3 to 4 months following the initial infection [11].

Infections of the urinary tract can be caused by a broad spectrum of organisms, including viruses, bacteria, fungi, and parasites [12]. UTIs are frequently caused by Gram-negative bacteria, particularly Enterobacteriaceae [13]. Enterobacteriaceae that inhabit the rectum are the main cause of upper urinary tract infections (UUTI), suggesting that the infection spreads through the fecal-perianal-urethral route [14].

Enterobacteriaceae resistant to beta-lactams, extended spectrum beta-lactams, and third-generation cephalosporins are frequently classified as ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae [15]. Among the Enterobacteriaceae family, ESBL-producing strains, particularly E. coli and K. pneumonia, are significant antimicrobial-resistant organisms in the human population. These organisms are frequently responsible for urinary tract infections and bloodstream infections, presenting a notable challenge in both developed and developing nations [16]. In previous times, ESBL-positive isolates were mainly found in UTIs acquired within hospital settings. Nevertheless, there has been a notable rise in ESBL positivity among community isolates in recent times [17].

Early identification of patients at risk for infection with ESBL-producing bacteria is therefore essential for directing empirical treatment and implementing preventative measures to limit the spread of illness due to ESBL producing Enterobacteriaceae [18]. Thus, the purpose of this study was to assess the prevalence of ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae, antibiotic susceptibility and its associated risk factors among patients suspected of having a urinary tract infection at Wachemo University Nigist Eleni Mohammed Memorial Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Central Ethiopia, Ethiopia, 2024.

Hospital based cross-sectional study was conducted from 10 May − 31 July 2024. The study was conducted in Wachemo University Nigist Eleni Mohammed Memorial Comprehensive Specialized Hospital. This hospital was established in 1984 in Hadiya zone which is one of the zones in Central Ethiopia. The hospital serves up to 2.5 million populations in the catchment, nearby zones and districts.

Data collection

Socio-demographic characteristics, personal habits and clinical information were collected using Kobo toolbox which is a suited software tools for field data collection for use in challenging environments. We developed a structured questionnaire specifically for this study. An English version is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Urine specimen collection

For outpatients a freshly voided mid-stream urine samples (10–20 ml) was collected in a clean wide mouthed urine cup with a screw cap by the patients [19]. From patients exhibiting symptoms of catheter associated UTI, urine sample (10–20 ml) was collected by disconnecting the bag from the catheter port and transferring it into a clean, wide-mouthed urine cup with a screw cap [20]. Finally, The samples were transported in a cold box to the Microbiology Laboratory of Wachemo University.

Inoculations

Semi-quantitative culture technique was used to inoculate urine sample. Urine specimen was mixed well and aseptically inoculated on MacConkey agar (Hi media, Mumbai, India) and CLED agar (Hi media, Mumbai, India) using a standard 1-µl calibrated loop. The culture plates were incubated aerobically at 37 °C for 24 h. On a plate agar from the urine sample with a colony count of ≥ 105 CFU/ml was considered as significant bacteriuria [21].

Identification of bacteria

Uropathogens were identified based on standard laboratory procedures including; culture characteristics, morphological characteristics, Gram’s stain, and biochemical tests (citrate utilization, indole test, motility test, carbohydrate fermentation) [22].

Antibiotic susceptibility tests

Criteria for antibiotic disk selection

The antibiotics tested were chosen based on their common use in empirical treatment of urinary tract infections in Ethiopia, CLSI recommendations for Enterobacteriaceae, and their relevance in detecting resistance patterns, particularly ESBL production.

The antibiotic sensitivity test was performed on Mueller-Hinton agar media by Kirby Bauer’s disc diffusion method as described in CLSI guidelines [22]. Susceptibility to ciprofloxacin (5 µg), norfloxacillin (10 µg), naldixic acid (30 µg), nitrofurantoin (300 µg), and gentamicin (10 µg), amikacin (30 µg), clavulanic acid (10 µg) and meropenem (10 µg) (Bio Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France) was tested by the disk diffusion method on Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA; Bio Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France) using the modified Kirby Bauer’s disc diffusion method as described in the guidelines of the clinical laboratory standard institute [22].

Pure isolate of Enterobacteriaceae species was suspended in normal saline and compared to the McFarland 0.5 standard. Finally, the isolates were inoculated onto MHA, and after minutes of inoculation, the antibiotics used for disc diffusion test were dispensed and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The zones of inhibition measured using a caliper, compared with the standard, and interpreted as susceptible (S), intermediate (I), or resistant (R) [22].

Quality control

To ensure reliability of results, standard quality control strains (E. coli ATCC 25922 and K. pneumoniae ATCC 700603) were used to check disk potency and validate procedures, in accordance with CLSI guidelines.

Initial screening test for ESBL

The isolates were screened for ESBL production by disk diffusion method as described by CLSI. The isolates with reduced susceptibility to cefotaxime (zone diameter of ≤ 27 mm), ceftazidime (zone diameter of ≤ 22 mm), and/or ceftriaxone (zone diameter of ≤ 25 mm) around the disks were selected for confirmation for ESBL production [22].

Phenotypic confirmatory test for ESBL

Isolates with reduced susceptibility to cefotaxime (zone diameter of ≤ 27 mm), ceftazidime (zone diameter of ≤ 22 mm), and/or ceftriaxone (zone diameter of ≤ 25 mm) were tested for the presence of ESBL by double disc synergy test. Double-disc synergy described as disc diffusion test in which discs containing cephalosporin (cefotaxime, ceftriaxone or ceftazidime) were applied next to a disc with clavulanic acid and synergy between the disks and clavulanic acid was observed [23].

Loop full of bacteria cells was collected from a pure culture and put into a tube with 4–5 ml of sterile normal saline before being gently stirred until it produced a uniform solution. The inoculum quantity was then calibrated by adjusting the suspension’s turbidity to a density of McFarland 0.5 standards. Test organism was inoculated into prepared Mueller-Hinton agar to give a semi-confluent growth and was detected by placing disk of cefotaxime, ceftriaxone or ceftazidime were applied next to a disc with clavulanic acid on an inoculated agar plate. After 24 h of incubation clear extension of the edge of the cefotaxime, ceftazidime, or ceftriaxone inhibition zone toward the disc containing clavulanic acid (keyhole) was interpreted as synergy, indicating the presence of an ESBL [24].

A positive double-disk synergy test (DDST) for extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) synthesis is indicated by a distinct enhancement or “keyhole” shape in the zone of inhibition around a third-generation cephalosporin disc when placed near a clavulanate-containing disc [25].

Data analysis

From Kobo toolbox all the data was entered into excel sheet. After checking the completeness of data, the data was exported to statistical package for social science (SPSS) version 29 for analysis. The descriptive results were summarized using frequencies, mean, median, standard deviation and presented using figures and tables. Binary logistic regression was used to determine the association between dependent and independent variables. In this case variables having p-value ≤ 0.25 were candidate for multivariable logistic regression [26]. Multivariable logistic regression was used to control confounding factors (identify independent risk factors). In all cases, a p-value of ≤ 0.05 was assumed statistically significant and 95% confidence intervals along with the exact p-values was presented [27].

Operational definitions

Suspected urinary tract infection

local symptoms such as dysuria, frequency, urgency, pain, and/or bladder tenderness are commonly observed in cases of cystitis (lower UTI). On the other hand, pyelonephritis (upper UTI) which is associated with flank pain and signs of a systemic infection, including fever, nausea, and vomiting. It may also present with costeovertebral angle tenderness, with or without the presence of cystitis symptoms [28].

Community acquired urinary tract infection (CA-UTI)

a UTI that develops in the community or within 48 h of being admitted to the hospital, and was not already present upon admission [29].

Catheter-associated urinary tract infections (CAUTI)

refers to UTIs that develop in individuals who have had a catheter inserted into the urinary bladder, either currently or within the last 48 h [30].

Multiple drug resistant (MDR)

organisms are defined as those that demonstrate in vitro resistance to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial categories [31].

Semi-quantitative culture

refers to a method that estimates the amount of bacteria present in a urine sample, but doesn’t provide precise numerical count [32].

Delayed urination

a prolonged time between the urge to urinate and initiation of urine flow.

Prevalence of UTI and ESBL-PE

Out of the 287 processed urine samples, 110 (38.3%) exhibited significant bacteriuria. From these culture-positive samples, 95(86.4%) Enterobacteriaceae were recovered, including 92 lactose fermenters and 3 non-lactose fermenters. The predominant uropathogen was E. coli, accounting for 59(62.1%), followed by Klebsiella spp. 22(23.2%), Citrobacter spp. 7(7.4%), Enterobacter spp. 4(4.2%), Morganella morganii 2(2.1%), and Providencia spp. 1(1.0%) (Table 3).

The overall prevalence of ESBL-PE was 26.3% (25/95). Among these ESBL-producing isolates, E. coli was the most common, accounting for 16(64%), followed by Klebsiella spp. 6(24%), Citrobacter spp. 2(8%), and Enterobacter spp. 1(4%) (Fig. 1).

Antibiotic resistance profile of Enterobacteriaceae

Among ESBL-producing isolates, resistance was universal to cefotaxime (100%) and ceftriaxone (100%), with a high rate also observed for ceftazidime (72%). Lower resistance was seen with nitrofurantoin (20%) and meropenem (8%) (Fig. 2). In contrast, non–ESBL-producing isolates exhibited substantially lower resistance rates. Resistance to cefotaxime and ceftriaxone was 27% and 28.6%, respectively, while ceftazidime resistance was 23%. Lower resistance was also observed to amikacin (7.1%), amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (11.4%) and gentamicin (10%). Notably, no resistance was detected to nitrofurantoin or meropenem among non–ESBL producers (Fig. 3).

Associated risk factors for ESBL positive UTI

Binary logistic regression analysis identified several variables as candidates for multivariable analysis. A previous history of urinary tract infection was strongly associated with ESBL-PE (COR = 8.104, p < 0.05, 95% CI: 2.704–24.289). Similarly, a history of urinary tract catheterization (COR = 5.019, p < 0.05, 95% CI: 2.151–11.687) and urinary tract surgery (COR = 6.404, p < 0.05, 95% CI: 2.317–17.702) were significant predictors. The presence of chronic disease showed a weaker, non-significant association (COR = 1.829, p = 0.162, 95% CI: 0.785–4.260) (Table 4).

In the multivariable logistic regression model, three independent risk factors were confirmed. A previous history of UTI (recurrent UTI) remained highly significant (AOR = 8.002, p = 0.001, 95% CI: 2.298–27.868). Prior urinary tract catheterization was also independently associated (AOR = 3.385, p = 0.018, 95% CI: 1.231–9.307), as was a history of urinary tract surgery (AOR = 4.535, p = 0.036, 95% CI: 1.101–18.678). These findings indicate that recurrent UTI, catheterization, and urinary tract surgery are independent predictors of infection with ESBL-PE (Table 4).

Discussion

Urinary tract infections remain among the most common bacterial infections worldwide, affecting millions of individuals each year. Although oral antibiotics such as trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, fluoroquinolones, and certain cephalosporins have historically been prescribed for empirical treatment, recent surveillance studies indicate that resistance to these agents is increasing. Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Proteus mirabilis continue to be the predominant uropathogens, yet their rising resistance to commonly used oral antibiotics has complicated therapeutic decision-making [33].

The epidemiology of UTIs caused by extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae provides critical insights into both prevalence and resistance trends. ESBL-producing strains are resistant to most beta-lactam antibiotics, including penicillin and cephalosporins, thereby limiting treatment options and often necessitating the use of carbapenems or other reserve agents. This growing challenge mirrors global patterns of antimicrobial resistance and underscores the importance of continuous monitoring at national and regional levels to guide empirical therapy and stewardship efforts [34].

Our study found a UTI prevalence of 38.32%, which is consistent with reports from India (38.50%) [35] and Iraq (42.75%) [36]. In contrast, lower prevalence rates have been documented in Saudi Arabia (25.3%) [37], Sri Lanka (21.5%) [38], and Pakistan (17.8%) [39]. These differences likely reflect variations in antibiotic use practices, healthcare access, diagnostic methods, and socio-demographic factors [40]. The similarity between our findings and those from India and Iraq may be explained by comparable healthcare-seeking behaviors, limited antibiotic stewardship, and socio-cultural conditions that predispose populations to higher UTI rates. Conversely, the relatively lower prevalence in Saudi Arabia, Sri Lanka, and Pakistan may be linked to stronger healthcare infrastructure, more effective infection control programs, and differences in hygiene practices and climate.

Clinically, these findings highlight the importance of region-specific strategies for UTI prevention and management. In high-prevalence settings, interventions should prioritize improving diagnostic capacity, rational antibiotic use, and public awareness of hygiene practices. In lower-prevalence regions, sustaining effective healthcare delivery and stewardship programs is essential to prevent future increases.

Out of the 110 bacterial isolates identified in our study, 95 (86.36%) belonged to the Enterobacteriaceae family, which are frequently implicated in urinary tract infections due to their ability to colonize and contaminate the urinary tract, particularly in women, as they are part of the normal intestinal flora [41]. Within Enterobacteriaceae, E. coli was the most prevalent species (59 isolates, 62.11%), followed by Klebsiella spp. (22 isolates, 23.2%), Citrobacter spp. (7 isolates, 7.37%), Enterobacter spp. (4 isolates, 4.21%), Morganella morganii (2 isolates, 2.11%), and Providencia spp. (1 isolate, 1%).

Comparable findings have been reported in other regions. Studies from Pakistan [39], Mexico [42], and Egypt [43] consistently identified E. coli as the dominant uropathogen, followed by Klebsiella spp. and other Enterobacteriaceae. However, the relative proportions of non-E. coli isolates varied. For instance, higher rates of Klebsiella spp. were observed in Pakistan [39], while Citrobacter and Enterobacter spp. were more frequently reported in Mexico [42]. In Egypt [43], E. coli remained predominant, but Klebsiella spp. also contributed significantly to the burden of infection.

These similarities reinforce the global predominance of E. coli in UTIs, which can be explained by its virulence factors (adhesins, toxins, biofilm formation), ability to colonize the periurethral area, and resistance mechanisms such as extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production. The observed differences in the distribution of other Enterobacteriaceae may be attributed to regional variations in antibiotic prescribing practices, hospital versus community-acquired infections, and local epidemiological trends.

From a clinical and public health perspective, the predominance of Enterobacteriaceae, particularly E. coli and Klebsiella spp., underscores the importance of routine antimicrobial susceptibility testing and region-specific antibiotic stewardship programs. Strengthening infection control measures and patient education on hygiene practices are essential to reduce the burden of UTIs and limit the spread of multidrug-resistant strains.

The overall prevalence of ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae observed in this study was 25(26.3%) of the total. This prevalence is higher than reports from studies conducted in Benin and Somalia, where prevalence rates were 10.2% and 10.2%, respectively [41, 44]. In contrast, markedly higher prevalence has been reported in Senegal (41%) [45] and Pakistan [39]. These findings demonstrate considerable variation in ESBL occurrence across different regions.

The high rate of resistance observed in our study may be explained by several contributing factors, including poor adherence to prescribed antimicrobial regimens, widespread self-medication due to easy access to antibiotics, and the frequent use of empirical treatment in medical settings [46]. Differences in ESBL positivity across studies may also be influenced by the number of isolates examined, institutional infection control practices, local prescribing behaviors, and broader geographic and socioeconomic conditions. For instance, countries with stricter antimicrobial stewardship programs often report lower prevalence, whereas regions with limited regulation of antibiotic use tend to show higher rates. Institutional differences, such as laboratory capacity, diagnostic protocols, and patient populations, further contribute to variability.

In terms of bacterial species distribution, ESBL production in this study was predominantly observed in E. coli (16 isolates, 64%), followed by Klebsiella spp. (6 isolates, 24%), Citrobacter spp. (2 isolates, 8%), and Enterobacter spp. (1 isolate, 4%). These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in Pakistan, Palestine, and Ghana [39, 47, 48], which also identified E. coli as the leading ESBL-producing uropathogen, followed by Klebsiella spp.

The antibiotic resistance profile of ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae in this study showed a very high level of resistance to third-generation cephalosporins, with 100% resistance to cefotaxime and ceftriaxone, and 72% resistance to ceftazidime. Fluoroquinolone resistance was also substantial, with 68% to nalidixic acid, 60% to ciprofloxacin, and 80% to norfloxacin. In contrast, lower resistance rates were observed for aminoglycosides and β-lactamase inhibitor combinations, ranging between 28 and 40% for amikacin, clavulanic acid, and gentamicin. The isolates demonstrated comparatively higher susceptibility to nitrofurantoin (20% resistance) and meropenem (8% resistance), suggesting that these agents remain effective therapeutic options against ESBL-producing strains. These findings are consistent with reports from other regions [48, 49], where ESBL-producing isolates typically show complete resistance to cephalosporins and high resistance to fluoroquinolones, while carbapenems and nitrofurantoin retain activity. The observed resistance pattern reflects the selective pressure caused by widespread and often empirical use of cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones in clinical practice, which accelerates resistance development. Conversely, carbapenems and nitrofurantoin are less frequently prescribed, which may explain their preserved effectiveness.

Variation in resistance rates across different studies can be explained by differences in antibiotic usage patterns, institutional prescribing practices, and geographic factors. In regions where antibiotics are easily accessible without prescription, self-medication contributes significantly to resistance [50]. Hospitals with strong antimicrobial stewardship programs often report lower resistance rates compared to those with limited regulation [51]. Socioeconomic conditions, diagnostic capacity, and local treatment guidelines also play a role in shaping resistance trends. Overall, the resistance profile observed here aligns with global patterns and underscores the urgent need for stricter antibiotic stewardship, surveillance, and rational prescribing to mitigate the spread of multidrug-resistant ESBL-producing pathogens [52].

Beyond conventional agents, recent evidence highlights the potential of novel therapies against multidrug-resistant Enterobacterales. Aztreonam-avibactam (ATM-AVI) and cefiderocol have shown effectiveness against blaNDM-producing E. coli, offering options where carbapenems fail [53]. Similarly, fosfomycin has demonstrated activity against blaNDM-5-producing E. coli, supporting its role as a salvage therapy in resistant urinary tract infections [54]. The ARGONAUT-III and -V trials reported potent activity of cefepime-taniborbactam against carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae and multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas aeruginosa, underscoring its promise for Enterobacterales infections [55]. These emerging agents expand the therapeutic arsenal and should be considered in future stewardship frameworks, particularly in regions facing high ESBL prevalence.

This study identified several independent risk factors associated with urinary tract infections caused by ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae. A previous history of urinary tract infection was strongly associated with ESBL positivity, with an adjusted odds ratio (AOR) of 8.104 (p = 0.001, 95% CI: 2.298–27.868). The presence of an indwelling urinary catheter was also a significant risk factor, with an AOR of 5.019 (p = 0.018, 95% CI: 1.231–9.307). In addition, a history of urinary tract surgery was linked to increased risk, with an AOR of 6.405 (p < 0.05, 95% CI: 1.101–18.678). These findings are consistent with results reported in other studies [56–58], which similarly highlight prior urinary tract infections, catheterization, and surgical interventions as important contributors to the development of ESBL-producing infections.

The associations observed in this study are biologically plausible and clinically significant. Recurrent urinary tract infections often lead to repeated antibiotic exposure, which increases selective pressure for resistant strains. Indwelling catheters provide a surface for biofilm formation, facilitating bacterial persistence and resistance development [59, 60], while also bypassing natural host defenses. Surgical interventions may disrupt normal urinary tract anatomy and physiology, creating opportunities for colonization by resistant organisms. These mechanisms explain why patients with such histories are at greater risk of ESBL-producing infections.

Limitations of the study

This study, while valuable in forecasting the prevalence of ESBL among patients suspected of having urinary tract infections, has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design makes it difficult to establish cause-and-effect relationships, as data are collected at a single point in time and therefore cannot capture long-term trends. Second, the detection of ESBL was based on phenotypic methods, which, although cost-effective and widely used, may be influenced by factors such as inoculum size, incubation conditions, and the technical expertise of laboratory personnel. These methods also offer lower sensitivity and specificity compared to molecular approaches, which could affect the accuracy of the findings. Consequently, while the results provide important insights, they should be interpreted with caution and complemented by further studies employing longitudinal designs and molecular diagnostic techniques to strengthen the evidence base.

Conclusion

This study highlights the significant burden posed by ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae in urinary tract infections, with notable prevalence, high resistance to commonly prescribed antibiotics, and clear associations with specific clinical risk factors. The predominance of E. coli and Klebsiella spp. as leading uropathogens, coupled with their extensive resistance to cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones, underscores the growing challenge of antimicrobial resistance and the need for careful selection of therapeutic agents. The identification of prior urinary tract infections, catheterization, and urinary tract surgery as independent risk factors further emphasizes the importance of preventive strategies, including minimizing unnecessary catheter use, strengthening infection control measures, and promoting rational antibiotic prescribing. Taken together, these findings reinforce the urgent need for region-specific antimicrobial stewardship programs, improved diagnostic capacity, and continuous surveillance to mitigate the spread of multidrug-resistant pathogens and ensure effective management of urinary tract infections.

Recommendation

Effective strategies for combating antimicrobial resistance should prioritize infection prevention while ensuring the judicious use of antibiotics and the promotion of vaccination. Good hygiene practices play a crucial role in reducing transmission, and timely, accurate detection of resistant strains is essential for implementing effective therapeutic interventions. Regular analysis of resistance patterns, with findings communicated to healthcare providers, helps refine treatment strategies and prevent antibiotic misuse. Additionally, efforts must be directed toward reducing risk factors to minimize the overall burden of resistance.
