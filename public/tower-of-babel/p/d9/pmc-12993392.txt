Patient-derived lung tumoroids reveal determinants of CAR T cell efficacy in solid tumors
Authors: Martí Farrera-Sal, Lukas Ehlen, Michael Schmueck-Henneresse
Published: 2026

Main text

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell therapy has transformed the treatment of hematologic malignancies, yet its efficacy in solid tumors remains limited. Lung cancer, the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, exemplifies this challenge.1 CAR T cell failure in solid tumors has been attributed to heterogeneous antigen expression, immunosuppressive tumor microenvironments, and tumor-intrinsic resistance mechanisms.2 A key missing component has been a preclinical platform that preserves individual tumor biology while enabling functional testing of CAR T cell activity in a patient-specific manner. In our recent study in Nature Biomedical Engineering,3 we established patient-derived lung tumoroids (TOs), alongside matched healthy lung organoids (HOs), as a precision platform for evaluating both standard therapies and CAR T cell responses in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (Figure 1). These three-dimensional cultures, generated directly from surgical specimens, preserve the molecular and phenotypic identity of their parental tissues and enable longitudinal, reproducible functional testing. Establishment of lung TOs and preservation of tumor identity

Robust and reproducible long-term culture of lung cancer organoids has historically limited their translational utility.4 By optimizing tissue handling and culture conditions, we established patient-derived lung TOs across diverse NSCLC subtypes, including advanced-stage disease, with substantially improved success rates. Notably, gentle mechanical dissociation and short tissue freezing times emerged as key determinants of successful TO generation, underscoring the importance of clinically compatible tissue pipelines for functional precision testing. To ensure biological relevance, lung TOs were comprehensively profiled and shown to retain the molecular identity of their parental tumors. Genomic, epigenomic, proteomic, and histological analyses consistently demonstrated preservation of tumor-specific alterations and lineage features, while clearly distinguishing tumor-derived cultures from matched HOs. Together, these data establish lung TOs as faithful patient avatars, providing a robust foundation for functional precision testing.

Modeling patient-specific responses to standard therapies

To establish functional and clinical relevance, lung TOs were exposed to the same chemotherapies and targeted agents administered to the corresponding patients. In vitro responses closely mirrored clinical outcomes, with TOs derived from resistant tumors exhibiting reduced drug sensitivity, while those from responsive tumors retained susceptibility. Proteomic profiling of resistant TOs revealed enrichment of established resistance-associated proteins and pathways, providing mechanistic context for these functional phenotypes. Together, these findings demonstrate that lung TOs capture patient-specific therapy resistance programs and support their use as a functional platform to interrogate treatment responses.

A pipeline for individualized CAR T cell testing

Building on this foundation, we developed an integrated workflow to evaluate CAR T cell therapy in a patient-specific context. Tumor tissues, healthy tissues, and matched organoids were screened for clinically relevant tumor-associated antigens (TAAs). Candidate TAAs showing differential expression between TOs and HOs were selected as CAR targets. Modular CAR T cells were generated using a non-viral, genome-editing-based engineering approach, enabling rapid exchange of antigen-binding domains and standardized CAR architectures. TO-HO cocultures combined with live-cell imaging and cytokine profiling provided quantitative readouts of CAR T cell activation, cytotoxicity, and potential on-target off-tumor toxicity. HER2 and PD-L1 emerged as targetable antigens in distinct patient subsets. CAR T cells targeting these antigens showed robust activation and cytokine production upon coculture with organoids. Notably, activation levels were comparable across patient samples despite variable antigen expression, indicating that activation alone does not predict cytotoxic efficacy. This observation set the stage for dissecting additional tumor-intrinsic factors shaping CAR T cell performance.

Patient-specific determinants of CAR T cell cytotoxicity

Quantitative killing assays performed at physiologically relevant effector-to-target ratios revealed striking inter-patient heterogeneity in CAR T cell efficacy. While high effector ratios induced killing in both tumor organoids and HOs, physiologically relevant effector-to-target ratios uncovered patient-specific differential susceptibility. One TO line displayed particularly strong HER2-CAR-mediated killing, correlating with high HER2 expression, low basal IL-8 secretion, minimal PD-L1 upregulation upon inflammatory stimulation, and reduced expression of autophagy- and immune-evasion-associated proteins. In contrast, another TO showed unexpectedly efficient killing despite low HER2 expression; this sample uniquely secreted IL-6, a cytokine known to enhance CAR T cell function, suggesting a compensatory pro-inflammatory milieu. Across the cohort, HER2 expression accounted for approximately 60% of variance in maximal killing, confirming antigen density as an important but incomplete predictor. Several TOs deviated markedly from predictions based solely on antigen levels, demonstrating that tumor-intrinsic resistance programs and cytokine environments critically shape CAR T cell efficacy. Parallel experiments with PD-L1-targeting CARs showed similarly heterogeneous, patient-specific killing patterns, further underscoring the contributions of antigen expression and checkpoint regulation to the cytotoxic response. These findings provide direct functional evidence that CAR T cell activation and antigen recognition are necessary but not sufficient for effective tumor elimination in solid cancers. Instead, CAR T cell efficacy is shaped by antigen density, tumor-intrinsic resistance pathways, and local cytokine signaling, all of which are captured in patient-derived TOs but are difficult to model in conventional preclinical systems.

Clinical feasibility and translational outlook

We demonstrate that the entire pipeline, from tumor resection through TO establishment, molecular profiling, CAR T cell generation, and functional testing, can be completed within approximately 3 months, representing a clinically actionable time frame for patients with resectable disease. Importantly, the parallel generation of matched HOs enables direct assessment of potential on-target off-tumor toxicity, partially addressing a key challenge in CAR T cell development. Beyond lung cancer, this framework is readily adaptable to other solid tumors, enabling systematic benchmarking of CAR designs and rational combination strategies to overcome resistance. Looking forward, incorporation of stromal and immune components and microfluidic culture systems will further refine modeling of CAR T cell trafficking, persistence, and microenvironmental interactions. Coupled with single-cell multi-omics, such platforms may enable deeper dissection of spatial and temporal tumor-immune dynamics. Ultimately, prospective clinical studies will be required to determine whether TO-guided CAR T cell selection improves patient outcomes.

Conclusion

Patient-derived lung TOs constitute a functional precision platform that captures individualized determinants of both standard therapy response and CAR T cell efficacy in solid tumors. By demonstrating that CAR T cell activation and antigen density alone fail to predict cytotoxic outcome, and that tumor-intrinsic resistance programs, checkpoint regulation, and cytokine signaling critically shape therapeutic performance, this approach provides a framework for rational patient stratification and informed CAR T cell design in lung cancer and beyond.5
