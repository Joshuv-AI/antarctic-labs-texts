An extraconal orbital cavernous venous malformation: A case report
Authors: Anas Orgi, Afafe El Attar, Omar Amriss, Aicha Merzem, Hasnaa Belgadir, Nadia Moussali, Naima El Benna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cavernous venous malformation, previously termed orbital cavernous hemangioma, and recently classified as a venous malformation in the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) classification, represents one of the most common benign tumors of the orbit. It’s a slow-growing lesion with mainly venous flow. Clinically, this lesion typically presents with a gradual onset of symptoms such as proptosis, vision loss, or diplopia, or it may be clinically silent and discovered incidentally on imaging performed for another reason. The reported cases of this lesion are typically located in the intraconal space. We present here an unusual case of a 40-year-old male with an extraconal cavernous venous malformation.

Introduction

Cavernous venous malformation (CVM), formerly known as orbital cavernous hemangioma, is classified as a venous malformation by the International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) [1], and is the most common primary benign orbital lesion in adults, accounting for 5%-9% of orbital lesions in most studies. It is more common in females, with over two-thirds occurring in young to middle-aged women [2].

This lesion may be clinically silent and found incidentally during imaging performed for other reasons, or it may be symptomatic due to exerted mass effect. Imaging techniques such as CT or MRI are very helpful in accurately diagnosing this lesion, as they can determine the exact location, the mass effect, the extent of the lesion, and help exclude other differential diagnoses that require urgent or more extensive treatment. Most of these tumors are located in the intraconal space.

Case report

A 40-year-old man with no significant past medical or surgical history presented to the ophthalmology consultation for a slowly progressive, nonaxial, nonpulsatile protrusion of the right eye that had been developing over the preceding 12 months. He reported no antecedent trauma, no periocular infection, and no previous ophthalmologic diagnoses. Family history was unremarkable for thyroid disease, connective tissue disorders, or orbital tumors.

The protrusion was initially subtle and painless, noticed incidentally by family members. Over time, it gradually worsened but remained asymptomatic. The patient denied eye pain, discomfort with eye movement, diplopia, photophobia, or changes in visual acuity. He also reported no headache, weight changes, palpitations, or focal neurological deficit.

Physical examination

On presentation, the patient was fully conscious, oriented, and in no acute distress. Vital signs were within normal limits: blood pressure 120/81 mmHg, heart rate 71 beats per minute, respiratory rate 14 breaths per minute, and oxygen saturation of 98% on room air.

The ophthalmologic assessment revealed a unilateral right eye exophthalmos with increased scleral visibility, no periorbital edema, full extraocular movements in all directions, with no diplopia or ophthalmoplegia, a visual acuity of 20/20, no relative afferent pupillary defect, and normal optic discs bilaterally with no venous congestion at the fundoscopy.

The clinical exam included thyroid palpation, which revealed no goiter, nodules, or tenderness, and a general examination, both of which were strictly normal.

A comprehensive blood test panel, including complete blood count with all parameters within normal limits, thyroid function with Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) of 1.8 mIU/L (normal reference range of 0.5-5.0 mIU/L), Free T4 of 1.2 ng/dL (Normal reference range of 0.7-1.9 ng/dL), antithyroglobulin antibody negative, anti-thyroperoxidase antibody negative, TSH receptor antibody < 1.0 IU/L (< 1.5 IU/L), and C-reactive protein (CRP) of 2 mg/L (< 5 mg/L).

All laboratory results were within normal limits, effectively ruling out thyroid-associated orbitopathy, inflammatory causes, and autoimmune conditions.

The clinical conclusion was that of an adult man with unilateral eye protrusion without any associated symptoms, with normal blood tests, and given the absence of red flags (pain, vision loss, diplopia, and systemic symptoms) no immediate medical therapy was initiated. The patient was reassured and advised monitoring of any changes in vision or onset of diplopia.

Imaging findings

To further evaluate the etiology of unilateral proptosis, a cranio-orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was requested.

Findings

A well-defined, ovoid mass was identified in the extraconal space of the right orbit, specifically located superiorly between the orbital roof and the levator palpebrae superioris/superior rectus complex. The lesion measured 26 mm in anteroposterior dimension, 25 mm in transverse dimension, and 15 mm in craniocaudal dimension.

On T2-weighted images, the mass appeared hyperintense and mildly heterogeneous. On T1-weighted images, it was hypointense relative to orbital fat. Based on the characteristic imaging features—namely, extraconal location, well-defined margins, hypointensity on T1 with hyperintensity on T2, progressive centripetal contrast enhancement, and absence of restricted diffusion—a diagnosis of cavernous venous malformation was made. No biopsy was performed, as the imaging features were considered pathognomonic, and the lesion was asymptomatic. Histologic confirmation was therefore deemed unnecessary and potentially risky given the location. He was advised to seek further consultation if any new symptoms develop.

Discussion

Cavernous venous malformation (CVM), also referred to as orbital cavernous hemangioma, is the most common benign vascular lesion of the orbit, accounting for 6%-9% of all orbital lesions [3,4]. It occurs most frequently in women (60%-70%), typically presents between the ages of 18 and 72 years (mean age 43-48), and gradually enlarges over time without spontaneous involution [5].

It has been confirmed as a subtype of vascular malformation rather than a true neoplasm, as reflected in the current International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) classification [3].

They have a characteristic morphological architecture with large channels and vascular spaces filled with blood [2]. Histology demonstrates encapsulated lesions, surrounded by moderately thick fibrous tissue, with perforating venous channels derived from the surrounding vasculature.

Orbital CVMs present a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations. They may be completely asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during imaging for unrelated indications. Alternatively, when symptomatic, the most common finding is slowly progressive proptosis, followed by orbital pain and, less frequently, reduced visual acuity. Since these are slow-growing lesions, symptoms typically develop gradually and are subtle. Occasionally, they can cause mild visual impairment, retinal striae, papilledema, or blurred vision [2,6].

More than 80% of orbital CVMs are found in the intraconal space and typically lie lateral to the optic nerve [7]. Extraconal location—as in the present case—is less common but well recognized.

Ultrasound is often the initial imaging modality but has limited ability to evaluate the posterior orbit and is frequently normal in small or deep lesions.

Magnetic resonance imaging is especially useful for investigating this pathology because it identifies the precise location of the lesion, its margins, signal characteristics, enhancement pattern, and the mass effect on adjacent orbital structures.

On MRI, CVMs appear as a well-defined, noninfiltrative mass, usually found in the intraconal space but rarely in the extraconal space, as in this case. They are hypointense on T1-weighted images and hyperintense on T2-weighted images with variable internal heterogeneity. Contrast injection typically reveals progressive, centripetal enhancement, with homogenization in the venous or delayed phase, which is characteristic of the CVM. This enhancement helps differentiate it from other well-defined orbital masses [3].

Multiparametric MRI can be useful and provides additional diagnostic information that will allow a more accurate diagnosis and differentiation from benign and malignant orbital masses, such as diffusion-weighted imaging, with CVM appearing relatively bright on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) with apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values ranging from 1.23 × 10⁻3 mm2/s to 1.39 × 10⁻3 mm2/s. In the present case, the measured ADC was 1.39 × 10⁻3 mm2/s, which falls within the reported range for CVMs. This contrasts sharply with ocular adnexal lymphoma, which shows true restricted diffusion (a less bright lesion on DWI and low ADC values between 0.44 and 0.92 × 10⁻3 mm2/s) [3].

The computed tomography (CT) scan, though also limited, is mainly useful for visualizing osseous changes such as smooth bony remodeling or thinning adjacent to the lesion. However, it can detect the intra-orbital mass and determine whether it's intra or extraconal, but it remains a screening tool when MRI is unavailable.

Differential diagnosis (extraconal location)

For an extraconal orbital mass, the differential diagnosis includes:

Schwannoma: fusiform shape, possible cystic degeneration, variable enhancement.

Solitary fibrous tumor: heterogeneous hypointense T2 foci, avid enhancement.

Lymphoma: restricted diffusion (low ADC), homogeneous signal, infiltrative margins.

Orbital venous varix: dynamic changes with Valsalva, phleboliths on CT.

Dermoid/epidermoid cyst: fat signal or restricted diffusion with rim enhancement.

Lacrimal gland lesion: location in lacrimal fossa, possible infiltrative features.

In this case, progressive centripetal enhancement and elevated ADC values strongly favored CVM over these alternatives.

Management of orbital CVM depends on clinical presentation, symptom severity, imaging findings, tumor size, and location [3].

Conservative management of asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic patients—such as the individual described in this case—consists of watchful waiting approach with regular clinical and radiological surveillance. This includes serial ophthalmologic examinations every 6-12 months and interval MRI every 1-2 years to document stability. If unchanged after 5 years, no further imaging needed [8].

Indications for surgery include progressive visual compromise, intractable pain, compressive optic neuropathy, diplopia, significant cosmetic deformity, or patient preference after counseling. Surgical approaches are tailored to lesion location and include anterior orbitotomy, lateral orbitotomy, transcranial orbitotomy, and endoscopic endonasal approaches.

Recently, techniques such as the endoscopic endonasal approach (which does not require orbitotomy) and preoperative embolization have gained traction as strategies to reduce risk. Other alternative therapies are also considered for tumors that are inaccessible to surgery, such as radiotherapy [9,10].

The final treatment plan is typically determined in a multidisciplinary meeting involving ophthalmologists, neurosurgeons, and interventional radiologists.

Conclusion

Orbital masses include a wide range of pathologies, among which cavernous venous malformation is the most common benign vascular lesion. Although most orbital CVMs arise within the intraconal space, extraconal location—as presented in this case—is a well-recognized but less frequent variant. Recognition of this atypical location is important to avoid diagnostic confusion and to guide appropriate imaging interpretation and management. This case underscores the value of characteristic MRI features—particularly progressive centripetal enhancement and elevated ADC values—in establishing a noninvasive diagnosis of CVM, even when the lesion occurs in an unusual extraconal location.
