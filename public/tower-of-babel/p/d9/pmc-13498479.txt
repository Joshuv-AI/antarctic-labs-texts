The Cost Effectiveness of Treatment Strategies for Depression in Ethiopia: A Multiple Cohort Markov Model Analysis
Authors: Yared Belete Belay, Cathrine Mihalopoulos, Lidia Engel, Yong Yi Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate the cost effectiveness of delivering treatment interventions for major depression among Ethiopian adults aged 18–64 years.MethodsA multiple cohort Markov model was developed to simulate how population cohorts move between three health states over time: healthy, depression and dead. Three drug interventions (tricyclic antidepressants [TCAs], selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [SSRIs] and serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors [SNRIs]), one non-pharmacological therapy (psychotherapy) and one combination therapy (drug plus psychotherapy) were compared to a common comparator, a partial null scenario. We modelled interventions for Ethiopians aged 18–64 years with major depression in 2021 (n = 2,431,898). The study employed a cost-utility analysis framework to estimate the incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), expressed as a cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY). The model was run over a 10-year period, adopted a health sector perspective to estimate population-level costs and benefits with a 3% annual discount rate. Uncertainty analysis was conducted using a Monte Carlo simulation with 3000 iterations.ResultsPsychotherapy was associated with incremental cost of US$17million (M) (95% CI US$9M–US$26M) and QALY gains of 11,857 (95% CI 125–35,227). In comparison, antidepressants, such as SNRIs, had a higher cost of US$236M (95% confidence interval [CI] US$132M–US$353M) with QALY gains of 15,369 (95% CI − 304 to 50,059). Combination therapy was associated with the highest health benefit (40,755 QALYs [95% CI − 210 to 124,928]) and incurred an incremental cost of US$162M (95% CI US$89M–US$244M). The ICER for psychotherapy was US$1,419 per QALY gained (95% CI US$344–US$22,433/QALY), suggesting cost effectiveness when adopting a one-times GDP per capita per QALY threshold. In contrast, combination therapy had an ICER of US$3,973/QALY (95% CI dominated to $63,677/QALY) and may be an appropriate option for individuals requiring both pharmacotherapy and psychotherapy. Conversely, drug therapies did not appear to be cost effective.ConclusionsPsychotherapy appears to be a cost-effective intervention in Ethiopia, while combination therapy may be an alternative cost-effective option. However, access to psychotherapy and combination therapy in Ethiopia is currently restricted to hospitals and the private sector, largely due to a shortage of trained professionals such as clinical psychologists. To address this gap, policymakers should explore cost-effective strategies to expand the availability of psychotherapy services.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40258-026-01055-1.

Key Points for Decision Makers

This study provides evidence to guide the selection of cost-effective depression treatment interventions in Ethiopia, supporting evidence-informed decision making.At a one-times GDP-per-capita per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) threshold, psychotherapy appears to be cost effective (ICER: US$1,419/QALY) and represents the most efficient first-line strategy, although its current availability is largely limited to hospital settings. Expanding delivery to primary care may improve access and cost effectiveness.Combination therapy generated the greatest health gains but at higher cost (ICER: US$3,973/QALY). While less cost effective than psychotherapy alone, it may be appropriate for individuals requiring both drug therapy and psychotherapy.

Introduction

Major depressive disorder, hereafter major depression, is a common mental illness characterised by sustained periods of sadness and diminished interest in daily activities, lasting for more than 2 weeks [1]. In 2021, major depression accounted for 1.9% of the global disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [2], with a rising burden in low-income regions like Sub-Saharan Africa [3]. Major depression is also associated with substantial economic costs that can impact individuals, families and countries [4].

To date, various treatment options have been developed for major depression, including drug therapies (i.e., usually antidepressants) and non-pharmacological (i.e., most commonly psychotherapy) approaches. Commonly prescribed classes of antidepressants include the following: tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) [5, 6]. However, there are many other classes that are less frequently prescribed, such as monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs) and norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibitors (NDRIs) [5, 7]. Meanwhile, various non-pharmacological interventions have been developed for managing major depression. Among these, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) [8], interpersonal psychotherapies [9] and problem-solving therapies [10] are the most widely used and evaluated approaches. Given the availability of both antidepressants and non-pharmacological treatment options for depression, the Ethiopian treatment guidelines recommend SSRIs (e.g., fluoxetine) and TCAs (e.g., amitriptyline) as drug options for the management of major depression. Alternatively, psychotherapy is recommended as an adjunct to pharmacotherapy, or as a sole treatment for individuals with mild to moderate depression [11, 12]. However, provision of psychotherapy requires the availability of a trained workforce [6]. Despite the recommendations outlined in the national guidelines, many individuals continue to rely on traditional medicine and religious healers [13, 14].

Evidence indicates that, despite the availability of treatment options for depression [15], there are disparities in treatment rates, with 48.3% of individuals living in higher-income countries and 16.8% in lower-income countries (there are no Ethiopian-specific estimates). The low treatment rate in low-income settings may be attributable to shortage of healthcare resources, limited awareness of available mental health treatments, fear of stigma and social rejection, and a shortage of trained healthcare providers capable of delivering evidence-based care [16]. Ethiopia’s lack of a strong and well-established health technology assessment system means that healthcare decisions are not made based on open and accountable criteria like effectiveness, cost effectiveness and efficiency [17]. Selecting an appropriate treatment requires careful consideration of several factors, including the clinical severity of the conditions, the availability of interventions, consumer preferences and the cost effectiveness of the options [18].

Different economic models have been built to conduct economic evaluations of major depression interventions, such as a decision tree model for evaluating the cost effectiveness of various antidepressants [19–21], a Markov cohort model for evaluating the cost effectiveness of psychotherapy [22] and a microsimulation model for evaluating antidepressants and cognitive behavioural therapy [23, 24]. It was unclear why these different model structures were chosen for each study. However, the evidence regarding the cost effectiveness of various major depression treatment options in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) is mixed, making it unclear whether drug therapy, nonpharmacologic therapy, or combination therapy is cost effective [25]. Because healthcare financing and resource allocation decisions are made at the national level, results from other countries cannot be directly transferred to Ethiopia. Ethiopia currently lacks locally relevant economic evaluation evidence to inform such decisions. This highlights the need for context-specific cost-effectiveness data for Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aims to develop a decision-analytic model to evaluate the cost effectiveness of treatment strategies for major depression in adults within the Ethiopian context.

Methods

Analytic Approach

This study employed a cost-utility analysis framework. Five interventions—three drug therapies (TCAs, SSRIs, and SNRIs), one non-pharmacological therapy (psychotherapy) and one combination therapy (drug plus psychotherapy)—were compared to a common comparator, which was a partial null scenario (i.e., receiving non-evidence-based treatment or no treatment at all). This scenario assumes that in-scope individuals who are not receiving evidence-based treatment also experience a null treatment effect size. Evidence-based treatment in this context refers to interventions delivered within the healthcare system that have demonstrated effectiveness and safety through rigorous scientific evaluation (e.g., randomized controlled trials) and are recommended in clinical guidelines. The comparison was based on costs (measured in 2021 US dollars [US$]) and benefits (measured in quality-adjusted life year [QALYs]). Costs reported in Ethiopian Birr were converted to US dollars using the CCEMG–EPPI centre cost converter, which applies GDP deflators and purchasing power parity adjustments [26]. The analysis adopted a health-sector perspective. The baseline year for the analysis was set at 2021 to align with the epidemiological parameters derived from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study. All costs and QALY gains were discounted at an annual rate of 3%, commonly applied in global health, with a 5% rate used for sensitivity analysis [27]. Costs from various sources were adjusted to 2021 values using the Ethiopian consumer price index (CPI) for health [28]. Model parameter inputs were derived from local, country-specific data sources where available; in the absence of such data, evidence from international sources was used (see Table 1). This study follows the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards 2022 (CHEERS 2022) [29] (see Supplementary File 1).

Intervention Description

This study estimates the cost effectiveness of interventions targeted exclusively at individuals with major depression, who currently seek care [30], but receive either non–evidence-based treatment or no treatment at all [16]. This simplifying assumption was necessitated by the complex and heterogenous nature of the existing clinical care pathways for major depression care in Ethiopia, which are not well characterised because of limited availability of data—particularly among those with major depression who do not seek treatment. The interventions were identified through a targeted review of the literature and national treatment guidelines, supplemented by consultation with clinical experts. This rapid review ensured that the selected interventions reflect those most commonly used in practice and recommended within the Ethiopian clinical context. Ethiopian mental health experts were contacted via personal communications to inform assumptions and validate model inputs. Information derived from personal communication referred to clarification and contextual validation of model assumptions obtained through a phone call with a local clinical expert (five experts were involved, all working in clinical settings, including psychiatrists and psychiatric nurses). These informal discussions were used solely to confirm the plausibility and contextual relevance of selected inputs.

This study compared different treatment options for major depression, which included the following:

Treatment with TCAs: Initial treatment with amitriptyline, starting with a single daily dose for the first week to monitor adverse events, followed by two doses per day for 2 months, and a 9-month continuation phase with a single daily dose. This regimen schedule was informed by treatment guidelines adopted in Ethiopia [11, 12] and validated by experts working in the mental health sector in Ethiopia. This regimen recurs annually in the base case analysis.

Effectiveness Data

Comprehensive evidence mapping, involving an extensive literature search, was conducted to identify data on the effectiveness and cost effectiveness of major depression treatments in LMICs (see Supplementary File 2). The meta-analysis by Cipriani et al. [5], and an update from Cipriani et al. [31], compared 21 antidepressants with placebo and reported differences using odds ratios (ORs) for each antidepressant. The reported effect sizes were aggregated into antidepressant drug classes. For instance, the effect sizes for fluoxetine, citalopram, sertraline, and similar medications were aggregated to generate the effect size of SSRIs. Aggregate effect sizes were computed for TCAs, SSRIs, and SNRIs. The inverse variance heterogeneity (IVhet) method was used to estimate the pooled mean OR effect size. The IVhet method calculates an estimator under the fixed effect model assumption with a quasi-likelihood based variance structure [32]. The pooled ORs were then converted to corresponding risk ratios (RRs) using the Cochrane conversion method [33] (see Supplementary File 2).

A meta-analysis by Cuijpers et al. [34] published in 2018 and updated by Tong et al. [35], assessed the effectiveness of psychotherapy. The initial review included 32 studies from LMICs, while the updated review encompassed 105 trials (117 comparisons). The overall effect size in LMICs was a Hedges’ g of 1.10 (95% CI 0.90–1.31). The findings indicated that studies with a high risk of bias showed a greater effect size, whereas studies with a low risk of bias demonstrated a smaller effect size. For more reliable and unbiased predictions in our economic model, we used an adjusted effect size (g = 0.60, 95% CI 0.34–0.87), which accounted for publication bias. The reported effect size (g) was converted to a RR effect size using the Cochrane conversion method [33] (see Supplementary File 2).

In the base-case analysis, the psychotherapy intervention was assumed to be delivered in the first year. The treatment effect size for psychotherapy was applied only in the first year, followed by a 50% annual decay over the subsequent 10 years. In the absence of local longitudinal data, this 50% annual decay assumption was adopted, consistent with a previous study conducted in Australia [36]. For drug therapy, it was assumed that the intervention would be delivered every year throughout the entire time horizon, with a consistent effect size throughout the cohort follow-up. No difference in effectiveness was assumed between drug therapy and psychotherapy across baseline severities or in the RR of mortality [37].

Comparator

The comparator involved a ‘partial null scenario’ where members of the eligible population received non–evidence-based treatments for major depression that incur a cost but led to a null effect size. This approach corresponds to the ‘partial null scenario’ defined by WHO-CHOICE researchers, where the eligible population receives no health care treatment [38]. Therefore, the comparator was not modelled as a zero-cost condition. Differences in costs between groups arose from reductions in disease prevalence attributable to the intervention, which consequently reduced the number of individuals incurring these background costs (i.e., cost offsets). This approach is particularly suitable when current non-evidence-based practices are highly inefficient and where individuals are liable to spend money on interventions that are no more effective than spontaneous remission. No measurable health gain was modelled for the comparator condition due to lack of robust evidence supporting effectiveness comparable to evidence-based depression treatments. This assumption is consistent with previous economic evaluations in which non-evidence-based care was used as the comparator and treatment benefits were attributed exclusively to interventions supported by evidence of clinical effectiveness [39]. In sub-Saharan countries, including Ethiopia, traditional medicine and religious practices are the most widely used non-evidence-based treatments among individuals seeking care [13, 14]. The cost per case for the ‘no intervention’ scenario was estimated using the treatment costs of traditional and/or religious healers [40]. Given the high treatment gap and low uptake of evidence-based treatment for major depression in Ethiopia, this partial null comparator reflects the prevailing care context, influencing incremental costs but not incremental health outcomes.

Eligible Population

We modelled interventions for Ethiopians aged 18 to 64 years with major depression in 2021 (n = 2,431,898) [41]. We extracted population data from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021) [41] (see Supplementary File 2). The care-seeking proportion was estimated from a published systematic review, which reported a 42% prevalence of help-seeking behaviour among individuals with depression [30]. Accordingly, of the total target population, an estimated 1,021,397 individuals (42% of 2,431,898) sought care. To estimate the proportion of the population not receiving evidence-based treatment, we imputed the treatment rate based on Ethiopia’s Gross National Income (GNI), which suggests that only 8% of individuals with depression receive adequate treatment [16]. Consequently, 939,140 did not receive evidence-based treatment (see Fig. 1).

Epidemiological Data

Input epidemiologic parameters, including age-specific prevalence and incidence data on major depression, were based on data from the GBD 2021 [41]. All-cause mortality data was also obtained from GBD 2021 [41]. The case fatality rate (CFR) for major depression was derived using the RR of mortality associated with major depression [42] and age-specific all-cause mortality data. A meta-analysis, which included studies with baseline major depression and follow-up all-cause mortality, estimated the RR of major depression at 1.52 (95% CI 1.45–1.59) [42]. The RR of excess mortality in depressed participants compared to non-depressed was used to calculate the age-specific CFR of major depression. Depression is an episodic disorder, with each episode having a variable duration. This study assumed that the duration for a 12-month prevalent case of major depression was 37.7 weeks [43]. This duration, along with the CFR, was used to estimate the remission rate of untreated depression (see Supplementary File 2).

Health Benefit Modelling

A multiple cohort Markov model was constructed in Excel when adopting the DISMOD-II model [44]. DisMod II is a deterministic compartmental model designed to enforce internal consistency among key epidemiological parameters: prevalence, incidence, remission, case-fatality, and mortality. The core analytical principle is that if any three of these parameters are known, the model can estimate the others based on a steady-state assumption. The model is implemented through a set of differential equations representing the balance between inflows (e.g., incidence) and outflows (e.g., remission, death) in a defined population cohort. DisMod II is useful in contexts where epidemiological data are incomplete, helping to generate consistent parameter estimates needed for further modelling or burden of disease analysis [45]. DisMod II applies the life-table method to estimate state membership as the average of the population at the start and end of each cycle, thereby implicitly accounting for half-cycle timing within the model.

The integration of DisMod II and Markov models leverages the strengths of both approaches: the epidemiological consistency of DisMod II and the economic evaluation capabilities of Markov models. DisMod II-derived parameters (e.g., incidence, remission) are transformed into transition probabilities for the Markov model, ensuring that the health economic model is grounded in consistent epidemiological data. This method is particularly advantageous in settings with limited data, where DisMod II can fill critical gaps and improve the reliability of subsequent cost-effectiveness analyses using Markov modelling.

This model simulates the disease dynamics within a population cohort, stratified by age and sex, as it transitions among three health states—healthy, depression, and dead—over time, with or without intervention. These transitions were estimated using epidemiological parameters such as incidence, prevalence, remission, and mortality rates [44]. This model structure has been used in other cost-effectiveness studies on depression [36, 46]. This was deemed to be the most appropriate model structure because it integrates data on epidemiological parameters to estimate disease progression over time, which accommodates the long-term nature of chronic conditions and handles incomplete or inconsistent data effectively [42]. Model cohorts start at 18 years of age, with each cohort stratified by 1-year age groups and sex. The model simulates the annual transitions among health states for each age-sex cohort over a 10-year time horizon following the delivery of an intervention – which is sufficient to capture the long-term costs and benefits [46]. A 10-year time horizon was selected to provide sufficient duration to capture the medium- to long-term population health and cost impacts of the interventions. This timeframe allows the model to reflect sustained treatment effects, relapse patterns, and downstream health consequences beyond the first year of intervention. A 1-year cycle length was used to estimate health outcomes and costs associated with delivering the interventions. The model accounted for the prevalence of major depression (i.e., the proportion in the ‘depression’ health state) that was observed in the population at baseline. In modelling the intervention benefit, effect sizes were applied to the remission parameter by multiplying the remission rate (observed without active treatment) by the RR effect size. In the absence of data, an assumption was made to keep in line with other studies [39, 47], which also accounted for functional changes in depressive symptoms. Therefore, the same RR effect size was also applied to the health state utility values (HSUVs)—used to calculate QALYs—to capture improvement in function due to the intervention. Final health benefits were measured using QALY gains resulting from increase in remission and functional changes in depressive symptoms.

The QALY gains were derived by accounting for HSUVs (i.e., total life years × HSUVs). The ‘healthy’ health state was assigned to the background morbidity rate and the HSUVs of the general public in Ethiopia using the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L) questionnaire [48]. Those in the ‘depression’ health state were assigned the HSUVs associated with depression after accounting for background morbidity, derived based on data involving all-cause prevalent years lived with disability [45]. Those in the dead state were assigned a HSUV of zero [49]. Disutility weights for adverse events were applied to those receiving drug and combination therapy, but not to those receiving psychotherapy interventions [50]. Common side effects of antidepressants include headaches, nausea, agitation, and sedation, which were captured by applying a disutility weight for the duration individuals received drug therapy [50, 51]. Tricyclic antidepressants are well known for their side effects, including cardiotoxic effects. Therefore, when modelling the health benefits of TCAs, we applied the disutility weight of mild heart disease [52] with an assumption that this side effect would occur in the same rate with other side effects (see Supplementary File 2). While these disutilities were not derived using the EQ-5D-5L, there were no other available weights that could be used. Furthermore, while the overall HSUVs may account for disutility associated with adverse events, it is unclear if indeed they do—therefore, we decided to include separate weights for adverse events.

The state transition diagram in Supplementary File 2 illustrates the movement of population cohorts between health states. For this model-based analysis, the interventions were assumed to operate at their maximum efficacy potential, meaning that the RR effect sizes observed in the previous meta-analysis were expected to be replicated in real-world practice [39]. Costing Interventions

The unit costs for drug interventions were derived from a published study that reported the median price ratio of antidepressants in Ethiopia [56]. The unit cost was then estimated using the median price ratio and international price of medicines reported by Management Sciences for Health in Ethiopia (MSH) [57] (see Supplementary File 2). To calculate the annual cost of drugs, the unit cost of medicine was multiplied by the total annual units required per person, based on expert recommendations and Ethiopian treatment guidelines [12]. Direct medical costs include medication costs, expenses for monitoring outcomes and costs for adverse event management. Index drug used for TCAs, SNRIs and SSRIs were amitriptyline, fluoxetine and venlafaxine, respectively, as these are commonly prescribed medicines. The price estimate of public sector was considered for the base-case analysis since 88% of consumers procure medicines from the public sector [58]. The cost of psychotherapy includes fees for therapy sessions, which include the number of sessions (minutes per session), the provider involved and the settings (e.g., primary care or hospital). Additionally, intervention costs incorporate health service expenses, such as outpatient costs [59]. The annual drug treatment cost was applied for the full year and assumed to continue over the 10-year time horizon, unless individuals transitioned out of the relevant health state (e.g., remission or death). In contrast, psychotherapy costs were applied only for the specified number of sessions assumed in the model, rather than for the entire year.

Cost offsets (i.e., the savings generated by an intervention compared to non-evidence-based treatments), were included in the base-case analysis. The cost of non-evidence-based treatment was estimated based on the cost of traditional or faith-based services, as reported in a previous study, with a mean of US$3.45 (SD = 25.71) per year. This study assumed that the intervention was functioning in a steady state, with a trained workforce and necessary infrastructure in place to support its delivery. Therefore, training, operating, and overhead costs were excluded from the analysis. The steady-state assumption is limited in a resource-constrained setting like Ethiopia where there are low ratios of psychiatrists, psychologists and other trained health care workers. Despite this, the mental health workforce staffing gap is estimated to provide context for policymakers and readers of this research (see Supplementary File 2). The average cost per case for each of the interventions was calculated by summing the costs for (a) the intervention/drugs, (b) outpatient consultation costs, and (c) adverse events management costs. The costs for psychotherapy were calculated using the salaries of the professionals delivering these services, with the assumption that health professionals allocated 70% of their time to clinical duties. The following key assumptions were made in the model: mild cases are treated in health centres and primary hospitals, while moderate-to-severe cases are managed in general and specialist hospitals (this assumption is based on personal communication with experts in the area). Psychotherapy requires a minimum of 8 sessions, each lasting 20 min [38]. A 40-min session duration was considered based on the literature; however, clinicians advised that, in routine Ethiopian practice, professionals typically see many patients and contact time is usually around 20 min. Therefore, 20 min was used in the base case. A 40-min duration was examined in a scenario analysis. Pharmacotherapy involves all-year treatment including four professional visits for ongoing monitoring and review [60]. For combination therapy, eight psychotherapy sessions and all-year treatment of drugs are assumed. Recruitment, diagnosis, and screening costs are assumed to be the same across interventions; therefore, only intervention, follow up and adverse event management costs were considered.

Cost-Effectiveness Outcomes

The main outcome of this model included incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs) for each intervention in the base-case analysis and sensitivity analyses. Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios were calculated as incremental net costs divided by incremental health benefits (defined as QALYs gained). As Ethiopia lacks a conventional willingness-to-pay threshold, the cost effectiveness of an intervention was defined by comparing the resulting ICERs with one- to three-times the GDP per capita per QALY, which are the thresholds formerly recommended by the World Health Organization [41, 61]. Cost-effectiveness outcomes were presented on the cost-effectiveness plane, while cost-effectiveness acceptability curves were used to show the probability of each intervention being cost effective [62], with respect to various willingness-to-pay thresholds.

Uncertainty and Sensitivity Analyses

To address parameter uncertainty (i.e., sampling error) and its impact on model outputs, we conducted uncertainty analyses using Monte Carlo simulations with 3000 iterations (a sufficient number to achieve convergence of model results). We used Ersatz (version 1.31, Sunrise Beach, Australia; available at: http://www.epigear.com/), an Excel add-on to perform the simulations. This method allowed us to estimate incremental costs, QALYs, and ICERs, along with their 95% uncertainty intervals. Sensitivity analyses were performed, considering the effects of varying model parameters and assumptions. Five alternative scenarios were examined within these analyses, providing a comprehensive assessment of their potential influence on the results.

Base-Case Analysis

The cost-effectiveness analysis for major depression treatment strategies is summarised in Table 2. Psychotherapy incurred an incremental cost of $17 million (95% CI 9M–26M) and achieved an incremental QALY gain of 11,857 (95% CI 125–35,227) when applied to adults aged 18–64 years who sought care but did not receive evidence-based treatment (n = 939,140). In contrast, SNRIs were associated with a higher incremental cost of $236 million (95% CI 132M–353M), resulting in an incremental QALY gain of 15,369 (95% CI − 304 to 50,059). Combination therapy provided the highest benefit among all options, with an incremental cost of $162 million (95% CI 89M–244M) and incremental QALY gains of 40,755 (95% CI − 210 to 124,928). Despite their lower implementation cost, TCAs achieved a higher incremental QALY gain compared to other drug treatments.

In Sensitivity Analysis 1, disability weights for depression were used instead of HSUVs, reducing mean ICERs across all interventions. Tricyclic antidepressants were found to be cost effective at three-times GDP per capita, a result not identified in the base-case analysis. In Sensitivity Analysis 2, disability weights were applied alongside intervention costs sourced from the OneHealth Tool. The base-case results were found to be sensitive to this assumption, with all interventions found to be cost effective. Conversely, assuming 100% decay in psychotherapy effectiveness after the first year (Sensitivity Analysis 3) or increasing psychotherapy session time from 20 to 40 min (Sensitivity Analysis 4) resulted in an ICER indicating that psychotherapy was not cost effective at one-times GDP per capita but remained cost effective at three-times GDP per capita. Lastly, applying a 5% discount rate, assuming 70% adherence of interventions and adopting cost offsets for only 50% of the population did not alter the conclusions of the base-case analysis. A threshold analysis was conducted to determine the intervention cost at which drug therapy would become cost effective. Drug therapy would require substantial cost reductions of approximately 74% for TCA, 84% for SSRI, and 88% for SNRI to become cost effective (see Supplementary File 2).

Discussion

The base-case analysis evaluating the cost effectiveness of major depression treatments in Ethiopia found that psychotherapy is a cost-effective intervention when evaluated against a willingness-to-pay threshold equivalent to one-times GDP per capita. When assessed using a willingness-to-pay threshold of three-times the GDP per capita, combination therapy was also found to be cost effective. In contrast to earlier studies from low-income countries [64, 65], this analysis shows that drug interventions for major depression treatment were not cost effective. Previous economic analysis in Ethiopia found that TCAs and SNRIs were associated with lower ICERs, while psychotherapy had relatively higher ICERs compared to drugs [55]. In contrast, the current analysis shows that drug therapies were associated with higher ICERs than psychotherapy. Factors such as rising inflation in Ethiopia, which drives up drug prices, might have contributed to the increased total costs of drug interventions. Furthermore, the higher HSUVs used in this analysis [49, 50] and the cost parameter sourced from published literature [55–59] could contribute to the differences in the conclusions. The higher HSUVs used in this analysis indicate a relatively better baseline health-related quality of life among individuals with depression, which reduces the absolute QALY gains achievable from any intervention. Because ICER estimates are sensitive to incremental health gains, smaller improvements translate into higher ICERs overall.

To test the robustness of the analysis, alternative scenarios were explored. In Sensitivity Analysis 1, the inverted disability weight was used to calculate QALY instead of HSUV [49]. This method resulted in higher QALY gains, as the inverted disability weight was lower than the HSUV reported by individuals with major depression in Ethiopia. Under this sensitivity analysis, TCA drug intervention and combination therapies, as well as psychotherapy, were found to be cost effective at three-times the GDP per capita threshold. In Sensitivity Analysis 2, where the total cost of drug interventions per case estimates in the OneHealth Tool, the tool developed by UN experts that aids national health planning in LMICs, were used. In this scenario, all interventions were found to be cost effective at one-times the GDP per capita. The base-case results are sensitive to the assumptions used in this analysis.

The assumed long-term benefit of psychotherapy contributed to a lower ICER. Sensitivity analysis showed that if the effectiveness of psychotherapy completely decays after the first year, the ICER increases to US$4,069 per/QALY gained (95% CI $1,160–$56,990/QALY gained), making it not cost effective at a one-times GDP per capita threshold. The base-case results are also influenced by the time required for psychotherapy sessions. Therefore, context-specific evidence on the long-term benefit, number of sessions, and session duration should be further explored. Despite this, psychotherapy interventions remain cost effective under three-times GDP per capita, even with the assumption that their effectiveness wanes after the first year. Similarly, combination therapy was consistently cost effective under three-times GDP per capita across various alternative scenarios explored. This could be attributed to the higher effectiveness (i.e., RR of effect size) associated with combination interventions. However, the psychotherapy and combination therapy services are currently limited to hospitals and the private sector in Ethiopia due to a shortage of trained health professionals, such as clinical psychologists, who can provide psychotherapy services. However, several implementation-related elements were not incorporated into the analysis, including costs associated with workforce expansion and broader practical challenges related to scaling up service delivery. These additional requirements may influence the feasibility and economic outcomes of implementation and should be considered in future evaluations.

The current analysis evaluated psychotherapy and combination therapy within hospital settings. Expanding these services to primary care may not necessarily reduce total costs, as improved access could increase utilisation and overall expenditure. However, delivery in primary care could reduce the cost per treated patient through lower personnel costs, task-sharing, and reduced hospital overheads, although these potential savings may be offset by increased uptake and coverage and therefore require further investigation.

Implementation will also depend on affordability and budget impact (potentially in other sectors, such as education if more health professionals need to be trained). Expanding service availability would require additional investment and may increase total expenditure as coverage improves. These financial implications, including opportunity costs associated with reallocating resources from other services or sectors, were not assessed in this study and warrant future budget impact analysis.

To highlight the staffing gap in Ethiopia, we calculated the required number of mental health professionals based on two benchmarks: the Ethiopian national mental health strategy target (i.e., 10,660 staff required) [66] and the recommended target for low-income settings (i.e., 8,024 staff required) [67]. Currently, the total mental health workforce in Ethiopia is around 763, far below any recommendations (see Supplementary File 2) [68]. This indicates that the Ethiopian government needs more commitment to training mental health professionals. A review that summarised evidence from low-income setting showed that implementing a task-shifting approach (also known as task sharing with lay health workers or non-specialist health care providers), has been found to be cost effective in several settings [29]. Such an approach could potentially address the shortage of adequately trained mental health staff in Ethiopia. For example, the Friendship Bench programme in Zimbabwe trains village grandmothers to provide basic psychosocial support to community members, resulting in improved mental health outcomes [69]. Such innovative programmes offer promising solutions to expand access to mental health care, including psychotherapy, through lay health workers embedded within local communities. However, it remains to be seen whether the efficacy of these community-based interventions is equivalent to those interventions evaluated in our analysis.

Ethiopia has begun integrating mental health services into primary care using the WHO’s mhGAP intervention guide [70]. The WHO’s mhGAP initiatives integrate mental health into primary care or community-based settings, enabling non-specialists with training and support to deliver evidence-based interventions [71]. The mhGAP intervention guide is aiding the rollout of task-shared care to non-specialist health care workers and community lay providers [6]. This approach has been evaluated in a trial, which found no significant difference in effectiveness. However, the intervention demonstrated improved technical efficiency—by optimizing the providers and locations of care delivery to maximise access—and reduced intervention costs, making it cost effective in the Ethiopian context [42].

The cost-effectiveness analysis in this study employs GDP per capita thresholds formerly recommended by the WHO [41]. The use of GDP-based thresholds has been criticised for lacking a theoretical basis, as it is not rigorously derived and relies on outdated research [72]. A context-specific cost-effectiveness threshold is needed to evaluate whether an intervention provides a good value for money. Input parameters, including HSUVs and intervention costs, were derived from published data from institutions in the capital city, limiting their national representativeness. Future research should incorporate data from regional institutions to enhance generalisability. Furthermore, the evaluated interventions in the current economic analysis do not address the impact of conflict and trauma on major depression, particularly in regions like Tigray and Amhara, where more specialised treatment packages are likely required. The interpretation and generalisability of the findings reported in this analysis should take these limitations into account.

Furthermore, depression frequently co-occurs with anxiety disorders [73], and several interventions evaluated in this study (e.g., antidepressant medications and psychotherapy) are also effective for anxiety symptoms [74]. However, the model considered outcomes only for major depression because epidemiological, and effectiveness data were more readily available for this condition, whereas comparable data for comorbid anxiety were limited. Consequently, potential additional health gains arising from improvements in co-occurring anxiety were not captured. The estimated QALY gains may therefore be conservative, and the cost effectiveness of the interventions may be underestimated. Future analyses incorporating comorbidity would provide a more comprehensive assessment of benefits. Also, the model did not explicitly distinguish relapse from new onset of depression among individuals in remission. The incidence parameter used in the model represents population-level risk and therefore implicitly includes both first episodes and recurrent episodes. However, modelling relapse separately could influence long-term outcomes if recurrence risks differ by treatment history. Due to the lack of evidence to reliably parameterise relapse probabilities, a distinct relapse transition was not included. This may affect long-term projections of health outcomes, although the direction of bias is uncertain.

Although local expert input was used to improve contextual relevance and support interpretation of selected assumptions, these communications were informal and did not follow a formal expert elicitation or qualitative methodology. While expert input strengthened local applicability in the absence of complete published evidence, it may not fully capture variation in clinical practice and introduces potential subjectivity into selected assumptions.

Conclusions

Treating major depression in adults aged 18–64 years with psychotherapy or combination therapy appears to be cost effective in Ethiopia, within a willingness-to-pay threshold of one- to three-times GDP per capita. In contrast, drug treatment alone does not appear to be cost effective, although this result is sensitive to changes in model parameters. Ethiopian policymakers should prioritise non-drug interventions for major depression that offer better value for money. However, psychotherapy services are currently limited to hospitals and require additional human resources. Implementing innovative approaches, such as task sharing (e.g., the Friendship Bench programme in Zimbabwe), should be explored as a potential approach to expand these services to primary care or community-based settings. However, these approaches need to be robustly evaluated to ensure that they are effective and cost effective.
