Electrokinetic nanoparticle transport in an interconnected porous environment: Decoupling cavity escape and directional bias
Authors: Anni Shi, Siamak Mirfendereski, Ankur Gupta, Daniel K. Schwartz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceWhile electrokinetic transport underlies bioengineering and separation technologies, the roles of electrophoresis (EP) and electroosmosis in complex porous environments remain poorly understood. Here, we report that these phenomena control the transport of charged nanoparticles via different pathways. While EP drives directional migration at high field strengths, it can cooperate with electroosmotic flow recirculation at weak fields to enhance transport efficiency by positioning particles near boundaries, facilitating rapid cavity escape without directional preference. This mechanistic decoupling challenges empirical models in applications that treat transport efficiency and directionality as unified. Our findings demonstrate that the combined effect dramatically improves transport efficiency independent of electrophoretic directionality, suggesting strategies for optimizing separation and bioengineering protocols by selectively leveraging the unique contributions of each mechanism.

External force fields are widely employed to control mass transport in bioengineering applications at multiple scales, ranging from bioinspired nanopore systems (1–3) and microfluidic diagnostic devices (4, 5) to industrial separation processes (6). In gel electrophoresis (EP), for example, direct current (DC) electric fields are routinely employed to separate biomolecules based on differential electrophoretic velocities that depend on molecular mass and charge, as well as internal network geometry. Despite their wide applications, these processes often rely on optimization with empirical parameters (7–9), rather than mechanistic understanding. In particular, a dependence on oversimplified models (7, 10, 11) fails to account for complex boundary effects on the intricate dynamics of charged colloids and macromolecules in heterogeneous and interconnected networks during electrokinetic transport.

Mass transport within highly interconnected pore spaces is complex and challenging to study and describe. In previous work, we investigated transport of Brownian and self-propelled nanoparticles in interconnected porous materials employing highly multiplexed superresolution localization microscopy. We proposed a universal model describing transport in the context of successive cavity exploration and escape (12, 13), revealing a direct relationship between single-cavity parameters (escape time and geometry) and microscopic diffusion, elucidating the ways in which intracavity dynamics contribute to microscopic transport. Our research also demonstrated that the cavity escape of charged Brownian particles depends not only on electrostatic barriers (14), but also responds to hydrodynamic forces from external stimuli, such as vicinal self-propelled nanomotors (15).

Here, we aim to extend the successive-escape paradigm to transport driven by external fields, specifically the electrokinetic transport where both nanoparticles and the interfaces of porous materials carry charges under the influence of electric fields. Currently, there is no comprehensive theory describing the electrokinetic motion of (nano)particles/macromolecules in complex interconnected interface-rich environments. While the electrophoretic drag aligns with the electric field, the electroosmosis of solvents and electrolytes can form recirculating flow structures due to the internal geometry of void spaces (7, 11, 16–20). Together with electrostatics and steric hindrance (14, 15, 21–23), the picture of charged colloid transport becomes more complicated in realistic porous environments. At a macroscopic level, one obviously expects that nanoparticles will migrate in a direction defined by an applied electric field, with a drift velocity defined by field strength. However, a microscopic picture of these effects is lacking. For example, it is not known whether the expected overall increase in drift velocity with field strength within complex pore-space networks is due to more rapid escape from local cavities, greater directional bias of cavity escape, or a subtle combination of both effects.

To understand the relationship between intracavity dynamics of charged particles and macroscopic drift velocity, we have deconvoluted electrokinetic transport into nanoscale elementary processes by experimentally and computationally examining the effects of electrophoretic forces and electroosmotic (EO) flow on charged particles confined by charged boundaries in interconnected networks. Using superresolution three-dimensional (3D) single-nanoparticle imaging (Fig. 1A), we directly observed both cavity-level single-particle dynamics and overall migration within materials with well-defined interconnected pore spaces. Based on the examination of cavity escape time (tescape), as a parameter for transport efficiency, and escape bias (Δx), representing the nanoscale migration direction preference, we hypothesize that the macroscopic drift of field-driven nanoparticles, in interconnected porous void spaces, can be described using coupled distributions; these include a temporal distribution (i.e., of cavity escape times) and a spatial distribution (i.e., of escape bias displacements); these distributions provide insights into the elementary mechanisms underlying porous electrokinetic transport. Our results revealed a surprising decoupling between the electric field dependence of how fast the particles escape vs. the location of escape. In particular, a significant decrease in escape time was observed in the presence of weak electric fields, while directional bias became pronounced only in the presence of much stronger fields, suggesting that these effects were associated with more complicated mechanisms (Fig. 1B) rather than dominated simply by EP. Overall, these findings connect classic theoretical descriptions of confined electrokinetic transport to practical application scenarios involving complex interconnected tortuous void spaces.

Results

Visualization of Electrokinetic Transport in Porous Environments.

We visualized electrokinetic transport phenomena using 3D superresolution imaging of COOH-capped fluorescent nanoparticles in a model porous medium, silica inverse opal (24). Inverse opals comprise a hexagonally close-packed array of spherical cavities with 12 holes connecting each cavity to neighboring cavities, allowing confined tracer particles to explore individual cavity structures and to translocate between adjacent cavities (Fig. 1A). Nanoparticles were dispersed at low concentration in a refractive index matched solution (pH = 7). 3D trajectories of charged nanoparticles, including spatiotemporal coordinates, revealed both overall migration and nanoscale cavity exploration processes within the inverse opal (Fig. 2); we interpreted intracavity dynamics using two parameters, escape time (tescape) and escape bias (Δx). The probability for a particle revisiting the same cavity was approximately 20% without the external field, and decreased modestly with increasing field strength (SI Appendix).

3D trajectories of charged particles demonstrated the influence of DC fields on transport in the porous void space. Without an applied field bias (Fig. 2A), the confined Brownian particles systematically explored a full 3D cavity volume before escaping to a random neighboring cavity with no directional bias; the dense clustering of spatiotemporal data points visually depicts the internal pore space geometry (25). In the presence of weak electric fields (e.g., 2 V/cm, Fig. 2B), cavity residence times (tescape) decreased, with sparser nanoparticle observations in a given cavity, leading to more rapid exploration of larger pore space volumes. Despite this enhanced exploration efficiency in the presence of weak fields, cavity escape was random without apparent directional bias. At higher field strengths (10 V/cm, Fig. 2C) particles escaped from cavities even more rapidly (evident from the sparse clusters), and a clear directional drift emerged, with negatively charged particles exhibiting migration against the field direction.

As a statistical analysis of the trajectories, the ensemble- and time-averaged mean squared displacement (EA-MSD) (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) was calculated and used to determine the time-dependent apparent diffusion coefficients [D(t) = MSD/2t] in the parallel (D||) and perpendicular (D⊥) directions relative to the DC field, in order to isolate field effects and the indirect impacts of the local microenvironment (Fig. 2 D and E). The behavior of D|| and D⊥ for short lag times (<300 ms) provided insights into intracavity dynamics (Fig. 2F), which were calculated from the initial linear slope of EA-MSD plots (t < 200 ms). Both D||,short and D⊥,short were enhanced modestly in the presence of weak fields, suggesting that boundary-confined electroosmosis may assist the random exploration of nanoparticles within the porous environment, with no directional bias. At higher field strength, D||,short was enhanced more strongly than D⊥,short, suggesting that EP, aligning with the field dimension, provided an additional contribution in this regime. The behavior of D(t) for intermediate lag times provided information about trapping within cavities. In the presence of a weak field (or no field), D|| and D⊥ decreased with lag time for intermediate lag times. This subdiffusive regime, consistent with so-called “caging” effects associated with trapping within cavities, weakened and ultimately disappeared in the presence of stronger fields. Finally, the behavior of D(t) for long lag times provided information about long-time macroscopic transport. Notably, the presence of stronger electric fields (above 5 V/cm) resulted in a dramatic enhancement of D|| and a modest enhancement of D⊥. In the presence of strong fields (20 V/cm), D⊥ remained Fickian (constant with lag time), but D|| exhibited superdiffusive behavior (increasing with lag time), demonstrating a strong electrophoretic driving force and the emergence of directional migration.

The discrepancy between the significant enhancements in D|| at higher field strengths vs. the gradual alleviation of caging effects at low applied biases suggested more complex mechanisms underlying the electrokinetic transport in these interconnect networks, motivating a deep investigation of the escape efficiency and directional migration mechanisms at the individual cavity level.

Enhanced Transport Efficiency at Low Field Strength.

By analyzing the time-dependent position fluctuations of nanoparticles (14, 25) (see details in SI Appendix), we identified individual escape events (and determined associated escape times, tescape) as a function of applied field strength (SI Appendix, Fig. S5). These state-change escape events became more frequent with increasing electric field strength. To determine the characteristic escape time, we fitted the complementary cumulative distributions (SI Appendix, Fig. S6) with a double decaying exponential function P(t)=A exp(−t/T1)+(1−A) exp(−t/T2) to obtain accurate mean values while minimizing the influence of data noise (<tescape >= AT1 + (1-A)T2). We note that, given the scatter and experimental uncertainties, other fitting functions could be equally effective in performing this “smoothing” procedure. As shown in Fig. 2G, <tescape> decreased with increasing potential, demonstrating the enhancement of transport efficiency. Interestingly, we observed a dramatic reduction of cavity escape time even for very weak applied electric fields. For example, the mean escape time in the presence of a biasing field of only 2 V/cm (1.7 ± 0.3 s) was less than half of the zero-field escape time (4.4 ± 0.5 s). The escape time continued to decrease more gradually with further increases in electric field strength. We hypothesized that the dramatically improved escape efficiency for weak fields was due to an increase of the effective short-time diffusion (Fig. 2F) as well as more subtle effects associated with persistent long-range motion in interconnected and insulating environments (15, 23). For example, hydrodynamic effects of the recirculating EO flow within the interconnected local microenvironments may enhance transport efficiency even at lower field strengths where electrophoretic forces are relatively weak compared to thermodynamic (Brownian) motion.

Directional Bias Emerges in the Presence of Stronger Fields.

To complement the above analysis of cavity escape time, we analyzed the intracavity escape bias (Δx) associated with individual trapping events, which was calculated as the projection of the vector connecting entry and exit locations onto the electric field direction. Escape bias distributions were compiled and fitted to Gaussian functions (SI Appendix, Fig. S7) to determine the mean escape bias (<Δx>) for each electric field strength (Fig. 2G). Interestingly, although the negatively charged nanoparticles exhibited noticeable migration preference against the electric field direction at high field strengths (≥7 V/cm) as expected, very little directional preference was observed at lower field strengths (< 5 V/cm). Thus, while cavity escape efficiency improved dramatically in the presence of a very weak biasing field (Fig. 2G), very little directional bias was observed until the strength of the applied field was significantly greater. This decoupling of cavity escape time and directional bias as a function of field strength is a major finding of this manuscript. We hypothesized that the absence of directional escape bias for nanoparticles at low field strength was related to the strong random motions of the nanoparticles. Indeed, experiments using twofold larger particles and cavity features exhibited noticeable directional motion at lower field strengths, e.g., 3 V/cm (SI Appendix, Fig. S8).

We believed that the microscale drift velocity of nanoparticles could be connected to nanoscale (i.e., cavity-level) phenomena by combining the measurements of escape time and directional escape bias. In particular, we calculated an effective cavity escape velocity in the field direction for each escape event, uescape,x = Δx/tescape, and calculated the mean, uescape,x, by fitting cavity escape velocity distributions to Gaussian functions (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). Notably, the changes in uescape,x as a function of applied field strength were quantitatively consistent with the microscale electrophoretic drift velocities (<ux>) (Fig. 2H), verifying the connection between intracavity nanoscale first-passage dynamics and microscale phenomena. Thus, to fully comprehend the mechanisms underlying electrokinetic transport in complex pore spaces, one must understand the competing effects of thermal fluctuations, electroosmosis, and electrophoresis on both cavity escape efficiency, and directional cavity escape bias.

The complex behavior observed in the analysis of cavity-level escape events reflects a delicate balance between Brownian motion and multiple electrokinetic effects. Because random thermal motion must be overcome by sufficiently strong external fields to achieve directional drift, it is intuitively reasonable that a field strength threshold would be required for manipulation of the migration direction within interconnected environments. However, the transport efficiency (i.e., cavity escape time) is not so simply connected to the competition between Brownian motion and directional forces in our observation of particles with small Péclet (Pe) number (Pe ≈ 0.01). We hypothesized that the decoupling of the response to field strength of directional bias and escape time at low Pe numbers was connected to the coordination of EO recirculating flows and weak EP, which may enhance particle transport through mechanisms independent of electrophoretic directionality. To test this hypothesis and elucidate the underlying physical mechanisms, we performed computational simulations that isolated and quantified the contributions of EP and EO in interconnected porous geometries.

Computational Prediction for Enhanced Transport and Directional Preference.

We performed numerical simulations to analyze nanoparticle transport within a spherical cavity, focusing on enhanced cavity escape and migration bias under the effects of confined EP and EO microflow. Fig. 1B demonstrates our computational model used for the current system. We separately account for three different mechanisms that contribute to particle transport, namely EP, electroosmosis, and Brownian motion. The local electric field remains spatially uniform and electroneutrality is valid under the thin Debye layer limit (i.e., λD≪a, where λD is the Debye length and a is the particle radius) and weak field approximation. This assumption breaks down in the limit of overlapping double layers and large surface charges, when the fully coupled nonlinear PNP equations and modified Stokes equations would need to be solved. EP drives charged particles with velocity proportional to field magnitude and particle mobility, while recirculating EO flow creates complex fluid patterns that exert hydrodynamic forces on suspended particles (14, 15, 22, 23, 25, 26). For comparison with the experiments, we performed a statistical analysis of the time each particle remained inside the domain before escaping and its displacement during this period. Fig. 1 B, III illustrates the key outputs targeted by our simulation. We ran simulations for 100,000 particles randomly placed in the domain and calculated the time at which each particle exited the cavity—referred to as the escape time (see “Material and Methods” for more details). We also computed the displacement component along the electric field direction relative to the total displacement for each particle before exiting the domain, a quantity we define as the escape bias. These figures represent an example of the probability distribution of the escape time and the escape bias over many particles, from which we calculate the mean escape time and mean escape bias. The simulations demonstrate reasonable agreement with the experiments, both qualitatively and quantitatively, in predicting escape bias (Fig. 3A) and particle escape time (Fig. 3B) over the experimentally accessible range of Pe and beyond. Moreover, the simulation results reflect key experimental observations, specifically the decoupling between the transport efficiency and the directional motion. In particular, at low Pe values—where the impact of the electric field on transport is weak relative to thermal fluctuations—directional bias remains insignificant. However, this is not the case for the escape time, which decreases notably even in the low Pe regime. Some quantitative differences in measuring escape time are reasonable between simulations and experiments. These differences arise from the simplified nature of the model, which does not account for several potential experimental factors such as dielectrophoretic trapping (10, 27, 28) and electrostatic barriers (14) at the cavity exits. Additionally, the definition of escape time slightly differs between simulation and experiment: In simulations, escape time is defined as the time required for a randomly initialized particle to exit the cavity, whereas in experiments it is measured from the time a particle enters until it exits the cavity. Therefore, we do not consider the possibility of escape time reaching zero to be realistic.

Distinctive Roles of Electrophoretics and EO in Transport.

The simulations further confirm that the increase of the particle escape efficiency (i.e., escape time) does not depend strongly on directional migration from EP. Therefore, we separately examined the impacts of EP and EO flow, as presented in Fig. 4. In the experimentally accessible Pe regime (Pe < 0.1) when Brownian motion is dominant, directional drift (Fig. 4A) arises primarily from electrophoretic transport, as EP alone results in nearly the same escape bias as when both EP and EO are active. Moreover, when only the EO effect is present, no significant directional bias emerges. However, in the same Pe regime, we observe a continuous decrease of escape time with increasing Pe in simulations that include EO only. In other words, while the EO recirculation flow enhances transport efficiency, it does not induce a notable directional preference. More surprisingly, in the low Pe regime, either EO or EP alone lead to a reduction of mean escape times that is comparable to the reduction observed when both EO and EP are simultaneously active (Fig. 4B); this implies a cooperative relationship between EO and EP, rather than competition in transport efficiency enhancement. It is worth noticing that the trapping time scaling as ∼Pe-1/4 remains the same with varied confinement factor (rcavity/rnp) in the simulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S14). We speculated that the stochastic (as opposed to deterministic) nature of escape at low electric field strengths, may explain the weak dependence. However, the origin of the ∼1/4 exponent remains unclear and motivates future investigation (29, 30).

To connect the transport efficiency enhancement to intracavity dynamics, we analyzed the distance between particles and boundaries by calculating the probability of the wall distance dw of a single particle over many simulation instances at a specified timeframe. Fig. 4C shows the effect of field strength (i.e., Pe) on the probability distribution of the normalized particle-wall surface distance (dw/a-1) for simulations where only EO, only EP, or both are active. Both EO and EP effectively push particles toward the wall boundaries on average, increasing the probability distribution of particles near the boundary. The analysis of experimental intracavity particle position distributions (SI Appendix, Fig. S9) demonstrated a qualitatively similar shift toward boundary proximity with increasing Pe [quantitative differences between experimental and computational positional distributions are likely due to effects omitted from simulations, such as dielectrophoresis (8)]. We speculate that in the low Pe regime, electrokinetic effects promote long-range particle transport by increasing the probability that particles approach the wall, thereby facilitating translocation through cavity exits (15, 31). The simulation results extend the field strength to the high Pe regime (i.e., Pe>0.1). In this experimentally inaccessible regime, strong electrophoretic effects become the dominant transport mechanism, and show a more direct correlation to the transport efficiency (see SI Appendix, Fig. S13 and discussion in SI Appendix).

Discussion

Previous theoretical research has shown that boundary effects have significant impacts on electrokinetics (7, 11, 16–20), where parameters, such as the particle-radius to Debye-length ratio (κa), exhibit distinctive correlations to migration in spherical cavities (16, 32) compared to cylindrical geometries (33–35), illustrating the importance of local geometry and connectivity on recirculating EO flow (36, 37). Our experimental and computational analysis of electrokinetic transport at the single-cavity level further revealed a complex interplay between EP, EO flow, and thermal fluctuations in more realistic heterogeneous, interconnected environments. For weak electric fields, where random thermal motion was dominant, cooperative electrokinetic effects (both EP and EO recirculation flow), drive particles toward cavity boundaries and enhance their apparent diffusivity, resulting in rapid escape of charged particles from cavities, but without a notable directional preference. When the electrophoretic force strengthens at higher field strength (i.e., larger Pe), the dominance of electrokinetic motion begins to determine the preferred direction of cavity escape and further enhances the transport efficiency. It is worth noting that electrokinetic effects enhance nanoparticle transport through mechanisms that differ from those of pressure-driven flow. Pressure-driven flow generates directional microhydrodynamic pathways within the cavity due to the externally applied pressure gradient, providing a more direct and biased route for particle escape. In contrast, EO flow—induced by electrically driven slip velocities at charged walls—produces recirculating flow structures that lack a direct directional bias toward the exits. Similarly, while electrophoretic transport is aligned with the electric field, it does not necessarily create a direct pathway for escape.

Our findings underscore the effects of confinement on controlled migration and transport processes. Colloids/molecules, driven by magnetic, electric, or pressure forces, can create complicated flow patterns through interactions with interfaces. Under a strong driving force yielding transport with high Pe numbers, controlled dynamics, such as trapping/release and acceleration, can be achieved by a careful balance between advection and dispersion (38, 39) through thoughtful design of interfacial geometries (40, 41) and dynamic channels (23, 42) in nanopores and nanofluidics. Our results suggest potential applications in mobilizing and sorting charged nanoparticles or macromolecules based on their directional transport as a function of differential Pe numbers. In the low Pe regime, rapid transport can be achieved without significant directional migration under weak applied fields, providing fundamental insights relevant to electrokinetic separation technologies. Additionally, given that intracellular transport of nucleic acids and proteins influences cell fate (43), the elucidation of the coupling between electrokinetics and boundary effects in complex environments may explain how weak electric fields influence biomolecular dynamics within the intricate cytoplasmic environment.

Materials and Methods

Imaging Experiments.

A Nikon Ti-Eclipse microscope with a through-the-objective TIRF unit and a 100× oil immersion objective (NA 1.49, Nikon APO TIRF 100×) was mounted on a perfect focus module. The incident angle of the excitation laser was adjusted for pseudo-TIRF imaging. 3D tracking employed double-helix point spread function imaging using a SPINDLE module (Double Helix Optics, Inc.)(44), where the axial position was encoded into the shape of the engineered point spread function (44). Silica inverse opal films were prepared with uniform spherical cavities (radius, rcavity = 250 or 400 nm) with 12 pores (rpore = 55 or 100 nm) connecting each cavity to neighboring cavities (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The inverse opal film was integrated between copper tape electrodes spaced 1 cm apart, using a hydrophobic pen to prevent direct contact between the liquid and the electrodes (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Polystyrene nanoparticles (rnp = 20, and 50 nm) with COOH-functionalized surface were used as charged fluorescent tracers. These nanoparticles were suspended in a refractive index matching solution (70% glycerol/water solution, w/w) with 10 mM NaCl. To enable particle localization, the nanoparticle dispersions were extremely dilute, ranging from 10−4 to 10−5 mg/mL. After adding the nanoparticle solution to the inverse opal film, we allowed up to 3 h for its complete permeation before imaging under applied voltages ranging from 0 to 20 V. To ensure statistical reliability, we analyzed approximately 40 trajectories longer than 30 s at each electric field strength.

A computational model (Fig. 1B) was developed to simulate the transport of a charged spherical nanoparticle within a uniformly charged spherical cavity under an applied electric field, E∞=E∞z^.

The particle’s motion is governed by three transport mechanisms: Brownian motion, EO microflow, and EP. In the presence of an electric field, the charged cavity boundary influences particle transport primarily through electroosmosis (26). Initially, each particle is randomly placed within the cavity. The particle position is updated using the second-order Adams–Bashforth scheme, with the Euler scheme applied. It should be noted that neglecting inflow and outflow through the porous cavity wall when calculating the flow field is justified by the wall’s low porosity and the relatively small pore size compared to the overall cavity dimensions. Although not considered in the present study, introducing heterogeneities in surface charge or incorporating active surfaces capable of generating self-sustained electrokinetic flows (47) could significantly alter flow structures and vorticity patterns. Investigating such effects would be a valuable direction for future work.
