The use of TikTok as a source of medical information regarding tubal ligation: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Emily Bundy, Pranathi Darbha, Ashleigh Post, Dawn M Kopp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTikTok is a key source of contraceptive information, including female permanent contraception (FPC), for young adults. Therefore, this cross-sectional study seeks to assess the quality of health information regarding FPC as presented in top-viewed TikTok videos.MethodsTwo independent reviewers analyzed 101 most viewed videos with the hashtags #tubal, #tuballigation, and #tubestied for creator demographics, tonality, and content. Statistical analysis using the two sample Wilcoxen rank-sum (Mann-Whitney) test was performed to compare DISCERN and Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool (PEMAT) scores between medical professionals and laypeople.ResultsMany videos portray personal experiences and have a negative tone, highlighting distrust, side effects, and tubal failure resulting in pregnancy. Educational content has a significantly higher average medical accuracy (p = 0.003) and content accessibility (p = 0.02) scores when created by medical professionals as compared to laypeople.ConclusionsAs TikTok promotes engagement over shared concerns, many videos portray a narrative of dissatisfaction and complication following permanent contraception procedures. Healthcare professionals may find value in utilizing the platform to intentionally share credible content and counter misinformation regarding permanent contraceptive methods.Keywords: TikTok, Tubal ligation, Mobile social media, Consumer health information, Contraception

Background

Female permanent contraception (FPC), which includes sterilization procedures such as tubal ligation and salpingectomy, remains the most commonly utilized method of pregnancy prevention in the United States, with use increasing by age as 2.4% of women aged 20–29, 11.9% of women aged 30–39, and 26.4% of women aged 40–49 rely on it [1]. Following the 2022 Dobbs v. Jackson decision, there was a notable increase in both FPC and vasectomy procedures among individuals under the age of 26, especially in states with limited access to contraception [2]. During a similar period, TikTok has emerged as a major source of health information for young women, becoming particularly influential in reproductive health and permanent contraception. While social media use has grown rapidly between 2019 and 2025 for all age groups, TikTok viewership is particularly prominent among individuals aged 18–34 [3]. Recent surveys found that 65.5% of young women actively use TikTok for health information and prefer content from healthcare providers [4]. While other studies assessing the quality of medical information on TikTok have found it to be generally poor, including surgical fields such as plastics and bariatrics, it is unknown what content is being created regarding FPC [5–6].

With the increasing use of FPC among younger patients, who are more likely to engage with social media, there is potential for both benefit and harm, as platforms such as TikTok may facilitate health education and engagement while also exposing users to inaccurate or misinformed health information. Therefore, this analysis seeks to assess the quality and accessibility of health information regarding FPC as presented in top-viewed TikTok videos.

Methods

Apify, a web-scraping software, was used to collect information on TikTok videos related to FPC on January 16, 2025. The most viewed videos for each of the hashtags #tubal, #tuballigation, and #tubestied were compiled and then sorted by view counts. The hashtags were selected based on commonly used terminology in the field of FPC, as well as what we anticipated content creators may use when referring to the procedure. Videos were excluded if they featured creators under 18 years old, used a non-English language, duplicated other videos in the sample, or did not reference FPC. Videos were reviewed until a pre-specified sample of the 100 most-viewed videos was reached.

Two reviewers (EB and PD) independently assessed the videos, with a third reviewer (AP) mediating discrepancies. Data collected included creator demographics, content characteristics, and medical information. Content characteristics included length of video, country of video origin, type of video, tone of video, negative experience, side effects discussion, tubal failure, implied distrust in healthcare worker, used compelling factors, and revealed a conflict of interest. Videos were classified as containing medical information if they explicitly referenced medical information regarding FPC, discussed a factual element of the surgical procedure, a recognized side effect, or discussed the logistics of the procedure or its anatomy. Those videos containing medical information then had their quality rated using the validated DISCERN and PEMAT tools [7–8]. The DISCERN tool assesses the quality and reliability of health information using a 5 point rating scale (1 = low quality to 5 = high quality) across factors including clarity of aims, relevance, source of information, balance and bias, and details about risks and benefits of treatments [7]. The PEMAT (Patient Education Materials Assessment Tool) evaluates how understandable and actionable patient education materials are using percentage scores (0-100%) based on criteria including word choice, organization, layout, and use of visuals [8]. Accuracy of medical information regarding FPC was evaluated against the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) Practice Bulletin Number 208 for the benefits and risks of sterilization [9]. We performed statistical analysis using Stata Version 13.0 (StataCorp, 2013, College Station, TX), utilizing the two sample Wilcoxen rank-sum (Mann-Whitney) test to compare DISCERN and PEMAT scores between medical professionals and laypeople. The Washington State University IRB determined this study to be non-human subjects research on December 12, 2024 (IRB #20888-001).

Results

After exclusion criteria were applied, a pre-specified sample of the 100 most-viewed videos was analyzed. One video was re-evaluated for inclusion, resulting in a final sample of 101 videos (Fig. 1). Of the reviewed videos, 45% came from #tuballigation, 36% from #tubestied, and 17% #tubal. Many of the videos (Table 1) were from the US (88.1%) with others from the UK (4.9%), Kenya (3%), Australia, Mexico, and the Philippines. Most of the videos featured a female creator (90.1%) and were produced by individuals over the age of 21 (96%). Only a minority were created by healthcare professionals (24.8%), and among those, most (80%) were board-certified obstetrician gynecologists. Therefore, a majority of the videos were created by laypeople sharing personal testimonies of their experiences with FPC.

There were many different types of videos identified including surgical-personal experience (28.7%), educational (25.7%), and humorous (16.8%). Videos were evenly split between positive (32.7%), negative (31.7%), and neutral (27.7%) tonality regarding FPC. Of the 101 videos, 23 (22.8%) discussed pain, and an equal number addressed side effects. Sixteen videos (15.8%) focused specifically on tubal failure. Additionally, 25.7% expressed distrust toward either their personal healthcare provider or the broader healthcare system due to their tubal ligation experience. Many videos incorporated compelling music or sound (36.6%) and others utilized strong emotion (4%) or imagery (4%) to enhance engagement.

Of the reviewed videos (n = 101), those sharing medical information (n = 38) were assessed and assigned a DISCERN/PEMAT score. The median DISCERN score for all videos containing medical information was 3. DISCERN scores were significantly higher for healthcare professional creators compared to laypeople (median score 5 vs. 3, respectively, p = 0.003). Similarly, the median PEMAT score for all videos containing medical information was 90%. PEMAT scores were significantly higher for healthcare professional creators compared to laypeople (median score 100% vs. 80%, respectively, p = 0.02).

Conclusions

The most-viewed TikTok videos on FPC were primarily created by women in the U.S. over age 21, with only a quarter produced by healthcare professionals. Content ranged from personal experiences to educational and humorous posts, with mixed tones and frequent discussion of pain, side effects, distrust in healthcare, and tubal failure. Videos created by medical professionals demonstrated higher accuracy and understandability of medical information.Similar to our findings, a study of 700 unique videos regarding contraception on TikTok found that a minority (19.3%) of the most-viewed contraception-related TikTok videos were created by healthcare professionals [10].

Although video tonality was evenly split, negative content regarding side effects and tubal failure may have a more lasting psychological impact, reflecting negativity bias and the amplification of failure narratives on TikTok [11]. While our results suggest that 15.8% of videos discussed tubal failure, the CREST study found the overall 1-year failure rate of all methods of FPC to be 0.6% and the 10-year cumulative failure rate to be 1.9% [12]. Therefore, while the rate of tubal failure is very uncommon in reality, it has become sensationalized on TikTok, demonstrating how creators take to making negative content on the platform, skewing the risks of FPC to appear more common. Comparably, a #IUD (intrauterine device) study found a predominance of TikTok videos highlighting pain and insufficient informed consent [13]. Furthermore, in our sample only one video explicitly addressed regret following FPC, despite literature, including ACOG recommendations, recognizing regret, particularly among younger patients, as a key counseling consideration [9]. Therefore, while regret does appear in FPC content, our findings suggest a disproportionate emphasis on narratives of tubal failure and reported side effects.

Post-tubal ligation syndrome is not a recognized medical diagnosis and is largely unsupported by available evidence, however is a widely utilized colloquial term on TikTok to describe perceived changes in pain and menstruation following FPC, including among creators in our sample. However, a large prospective study, following 9514 women for 5 years following tubal ligation procedures found no increased risk in menstrual abnormalities, suggesting that post-sterilization menstrual changes are likely coincidential or related to discontinuation of hormonal contraception rather than the procedure itself [14].

While TikTok offers a space to connect over shared concerns, it also poses the risk of misinformation. Health-related misinformation is widespread and can have serious consequences, as inaccurate advice may lead individuals to delay seeking appropriate medical care or adopt practices that may ultimately worsen health outcomes [15]. A recent study regarding oxytocin use in labor induction found an average DISCERN score of only 0.9, highlighting extensive shortcomings in the TikTok content regarding certain reproductive health topics [16]. In contrast, our study found somewhat higher scores, reflecting higher quality medical information regarding FPC specifically. However, if individuals considering FPC were to view a series of TikToks under the selected hashtags, they would be predominantly exposed to content emphasizing failure, side effects, and negative experiences, which may skew their perceptions and influence their willingness to pursue this contraceptive option.

Study strengths include dual-reviewer analysis with mediation and standardized content collection utilizing DISCERN. Limitations include single-category classification complicating coding of overlapping content, restriction to English-language videos, and reliance on algorithm-driven selection, which may limit generalizability. Only including particular hashtags may have limited which videos were included in the sample. Since 2013, the Society of Gynecologic Oncology has recommended salpingectomy for FPC, yet national uptake of the procedure has remained generally low [17]. It is therefore unclear how including the hashtag #salpingectomy may have influenced the content and quality of the videos, as it may have captured additional content from healthcare professionals more familiar with the terminology and thus more likely to share higher-quality medical information.

As patients increasingly turn to TikTok as a primary source for medical information, healthcare professionals may find value in utilizing the platform more intentionally to share credible content, guide informed medical decision-making, and counter misinformation regarding permanent contraceptive methods. Videos created on FPC can prioritize clear, focused messaging that breaks down complex information into simple, digestible points, while remaining grounded in evidence-based guidance. Effective content is approachable, non-judgmental, and non-identifiable, avoiding references to specific patient cases or personal clinical experiences. Instead, videos can focus on explanations supported by visuals to aid understanding and directly address common misconceptions and patient concerns including effectiveness, recovery, and procedural logistics to ensure the content is both accurate and genuinely useful for informed decision-making.
