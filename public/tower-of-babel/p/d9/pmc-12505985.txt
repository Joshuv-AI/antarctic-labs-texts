Development and validation of a machine learning-based prediction model for prolonged length of stay after laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery: a secondary analysis of the FDP-PONV trial
Authors: Jiankun Shi, Yabin Huang, Jiaxin Han, Shimin Zhang, Xingshan Cheng, Tongfeng Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProlonged postoperative length of stay (PLOS) is associated with several clinical risks and increased medical costs. This study aimed to develop a prediction model for PLOS based on clinical features throughout pre-, intra-, and post-operative periods in patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery.MethodsThis secondary analysis included patients who underwent laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery in the FDP-PONV randomized controlled trial. This study defined PLOS as a postoperative length of stay longer than 7 days. All clinical features prospectively collected in the FDP-PONV trial were used to generate the models. This study employed six machine learning algorithms including logistic regression, K-nearest neighbor, gradient boosting machine, random forest, support vector machine, and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). The model performance was evaluated by numerous metrics including area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and interpreted using shapley additive explanations (SHAP).ResultsCases on or before 2021–12-18 formed the training cohort (802 patients) and those after that date formed the validation cohort (339 patients). Among the models evaluated, the XGBoost model demonstrated the best performance in the validation cohort, achieving an AUC of 0.756. After five times repeated tenfold cross-validation, the XGBoost model demonstrated a mean AUC of 0.816. SHAP plots were generated based on the predictors: education level; preoperative hypoalbuminemia and insufficient sleep; surgery type and duration; blood loss; postoperative hypotension; postoperative albumin infusion; highest pain score during 73–120 h after surgery; time to first flatus and defecation; postoperative infection; and postoperative acute kidney injury.ConclusionsThis study developed a prediction model using machine learning to identify patients at high risk of PLOS after laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery.Trial registrationThe FDP-PONV trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04853147) on 2021–04-27.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04330-y.Keywords: Length of hospital stay, Gastrointestinal surgery, Prediction model

Introduction

Laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery is frequently performed to remove lesions and improve survival in patients with gastrointestinal cancer or benign disease. Furthermore, the laparoscopic approach was associated with a decreased risk for all adverse outcomes [1, 2]. The length of hospital stay is an important quality metric for surgery, particularly in the era of enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS). Multiple studies have demonstrated that implementation of ERAS protocol can reduce the median postoperative hospital stay to 5–6 days following laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery [3–5]. Prolonged postoperative length of stay (PLOS) is associated with multiple adverse outcomes, including increased risk of hospital-acquired infections, higher short-term mortality rates, and disruption of optimal patient flow through healthcare systems [6]. Moreover, PLOS also increases healthcare costs. According to the Statistical Bulletin on China’s Health Development 2023 released by the National Health Commission of China, the target patient population has an average cost of approximately 1172.0 Chinese yuan (CNY) per day [7].

Currently, machine learning is commonly used for complex problem-solving, cost–benefit evaluations, and risk assessment in healthcare and it may help address challenges unique to the field of global health [8–10]. Numerous studies have applied machine learning methods for the prediction of PLOS, and predictive factors, such as age, sex, comorbidity, preoperative patient-reported outcomes, and laboratory test results (such as albumin and hemoglobin) have been reported [11–14]. However, the factors influencing length of stay varied from surgery and population and should be investigated in specific situations. Additionally, the clinical definition of PLOS remains inconsistently specified in surgical literature, with significant variation in diagnostic thresholds across institutions and study populations [11]. Therefore, this study aimed to develop and validate a machine learning-based prediction model for PLOS after laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery in patients, using comprehensive clinical features during the pre-, intra-, and postoperative periods.

Methods

Study population

This study was a secondary analysis of the FDP-PONV trial. The original FDP-PONV trial was a single-center, randomized, double-blind study assessing the efficacy and safety of fosaprepitant combined with dexamethasone and palonosetron in preventing PONV after laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery [15]. The original trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04853147) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before their participation. The inclusion criteria of the original trial were age 18–75 years, scheduled for laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery, and three or four Apfel risk factors [16]. Exclusion criteria included an American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status of 4–6, severe hepatic dysfunction; contraindications to fosaprepitant, 5-hydroxytryptamine type-3 receptor antagonist, or dexamethasone; preoperative use of medications with known antiemetic properties; mental disorders or inability to communicate; and pregnant women or nursing mothers. General anesthesia was induced and maintained with total intravenous anesthesia using effect-site target-controlled infusion. Propofol (2–6 µg/mL, Schnider model) and remifentanil (2–6 ng/mL, Minto model) concentrations were adjusted based on blood pressure and bispectral index. Intraoperative data—including drug dosage, blood pressure, pneumoperitoneum and surgery duration, procedure type, fluid infusion, and blood loss—were recorded. Postoperative hydromorphone was delivered via a patient-controlled analgesia pump. In the ward, trained blinded investigators collected postoperative data (blood pressure, temperature and adverse events), recorded by participants or medical staff every 24 h for 120 h after surgery. There was no significant difference in postoperative length of stay between the randomized groups.

Patients who underwent laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery in the FDP-PONV trial were included in this study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University (2024ZSLYEC-160) and reported in accordance with the TRIPOD statement [17].

Outcome parameters

The primary outcome was the presence of PLOS. Given a lack of standardized definition for PLOS, we defined it as a postoperative length of stay greater than 7 days based on the cut-off values used in previous studies regarding gastrointestinal surgery [18–20]. Postoperative length of stay was defined as the duration from date of surgery to date of discharge. The criteria for hospital discharge included [21–23]: 1) tolerance of diet with no need for intravenous nutrition; 2) analgesic-free status, defined as a visual analog scale score ≤ 3 without intravenous analgesic drugs; 3) adequate mobility; and 4) afebrile status without major infectious complications.

Predictive variables

All variables, including baseline characteristics, intraoperative information, postoperative outcomes, and adverse events prospectively collected in the original FDP-PONV trial were considered potential predictors of PLOS, which was consistent with previous studies [11, 24]. To address model complexity and improve generalizability, we implemented a two-step feature selection approach. First, we applied absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) [25] regression to identify clinically meaningful variables by tuning the regularization parameter (lambda). Subsequently, we used recursive feature elimination with cross-validation (RFECV) [26] based on the extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) classifier, to further refine the feature set. RFECV was performed by selecting features associated with the highest average area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) by tenfold cross-validation. These steps ensured a balanced trade-off between model interpretability and predictive accuracy, in accordance with guidelines for clinical predictive modeling [9].

Sample size

To ensure accurate estimates and reduce the risk of overfitting, we adopted a methodology for calculating the minimum sample size for developing a multivariable prediction model [27]. The minimum required total sample size was estimated to be 1113 for 16 candidate predictors, assuming an event fraction of 0.49, a C-index of 0.70, and a uniform shrinkage factor of 0.90. This study is a secondary analysis of the FDP-PONV trial, in which a total of 1141 patients were ultimately enrolled and met eligibility criteria. Since larger sample size generally improves model stability, reduces variance, and enhances generalizability, we included all eligible participants. The final sample size of 1141 patients was sufficient to support the development of a model with no more than 16 predictors.

Missing data handling

This study excluded the variables with a missing proportion greater than 20%. We used Little’s Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) test [28] for multivariate data with missing values. If the missing data were missing completely at random, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) [29] was employed to handle missing data. MICE constructed a prediction matrix with the outcome variable and subject identifier excluded from imputation. Multiple imputation was performed on the training set using five imputations with five iterations each. The resulting imputation model was then applied to the testing set. The first imputed dataset was used for the primary analysis, and the remaining imputations were reserved for sensitivity analyses.

Model development

Using a time-based split, the dataset was divided into a training set comprising 70% of the patients and a validation set comprising the remaining 30%. We selected six well-established algorithms—logistic regression, K-nearest neighbor (KNN), gradient boosting machine (GBM), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and XGBoost—to span key paradigms in supervised learning, ensuring a diverse and informative benchmark.

Logistic regression [30] served as the interpretable baseline and the gold standard in clinical prediction, offering transparent odds‑ratio outputs that reveal whether more complex models truly add value. KNN [31], a non‑parametric instance‑based learner, provided a distance‑based benchmark that, despite higher computational cost and limited interpretability, excels at detecting local patterns. SVM [32] was selected for its kernel‑based ability to model non‑linear decision boundaries and to generalize well in high‑dimensional spaces with small‑to‑moderate sample sizes. RF [33] delivered strong accuracy, resistance to noise and outliers, and built‑in feature‑importance estimates with minimal hyperparameter tuning. GBM [34] applied sequential ensemble learning, iteratively adding weak decision trees to correct residual errors and capture complex data structures. XGBoost [35] extended GBM with second‑order optimization, column sampling, regularization, and sparsity awareness, achieving exceptional computational efficiency and state‑of‑the‑art performance on structured medical datasets where feature relationships are non‑linear or hierarchical.

In model training, we applied cost-sensitive learning [36] to address class imbalance by incorporating class-specific weights into the loss function. We computed the weights from the training cohort only, setting the negative-class weight to 1 and the positive-class weight to the ratio of negative to positive cases. We employed randomized search with cross-validation (RandomizedSearchCV) [37] to optimize hyperparameters for the machine learning algorithms. Fifty random parameter combinations from a predefined space were evaluated via repeated tenfold cross-validation to maximize AUC and the best hyperparameters were used to build the final model.

We evaluated the performance of these models in both training and validation cohorts, employing metrics with 95% confidence interval (CI) such as AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1 score and Brier score [9]. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) [38] and decision curve analysis (DCA) [39] curves were used to ascertain the discrimination and the net benefit of machine learning-based models, respectively. Calibration curve analysis and Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test were applied to assess the calibration performance of the prediction models. Five times repeated tenfold cross-validation [40] was employed to evaluate the performance of the machine learning model. This approach involved partitioning the data into 10 subsets, iteratively training on 9 subsets and validating on the remaining fold, and repeating the entire process five times with different random splits. The AUCs of the machine learning models were compared using the DeLong test with Bonferroni corrections [41]. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses were performed to evaluate the impact of missing data handling methods and variations across different types of surgery. The final model was implemented as a web-based calculator at https://streamlit.io/.

Variable importance

To interpret our prediction model, we used the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) library [42], which provides a unified framework for quantifying the contribution of each feature to model output. SHAP assigns every variable a Shapley value—derived from cooperative game theory—that reflects its importance in a given prediction.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range], and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons were performed using Student’s t-test for normally distributed data with equal variances, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for non-normally distributed or heteroscedastic data. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson’s chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Statistical significance was established at a two-sided p-value of less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using R Statistical language (version 4.4.3) and Python (version 3.10.7). The imputation process was conducted using the ‘mice’ package (version 3.18.0). Models were developed using Python with scikit-learn (version 1.6.1) and evaluated using R Statistical language with reportROC package (version 3.6). ROC and DCA curves was plotted using the pROC package (version 1.18.5) and the dcurves package (version 0.5.0), respectively. SHAP plots were generated using the shap library (version 0.48.0).

Cases on or before 2021–12-18 formed the training cohort for model development (802 patients) and those after that date formed the validation cohort (339 patients). Missing data

As shown in Additional File 2, the variable with the highest proportion of missing data was postoperative procalcitonin (50.78%) and it was excluded in this study. The missing data were considered to be missing completely at random (MCAR test, p = 0.083), so the MICE method was used to address missing data and the first imputed dataset was extracted for subsequent analysis.

Model development

First, LASSO was used to select features after choosing an optimal lambda value of 0.0381852. Features with a coefficient of zero were considered redundant and removed, leaving nine features for downstream selection. RFECV based on XGBoost classifier was applied to finalize variable selection for model inclusion. Finally, 13 features were retained for inclusion in the models, including education level, preoperative hypoalbuminemia, preoperative insufficient sleep, type of surgery, duration of surgery, blood loss, postoperative hypotension, postoperative albumin infusion, highest pain score during 73–120 h after surgery, time to first flatus, time to first defaecation, postoperative infection and acute kidney injury (Table 3). As shown in Additional File 3, the interaction analysis between perioperative hypoalbuminemia and postoperative albumin infusion indicated that postoperative albumin infusion was associated with a higher likelihood of PLOS regardless of the presence of perioperative hypoalbuminemia. Details of feature selection using LASSO regression and RFECV were provided in Additional File 4, while hyperparameter settings of machine learning-based models by RandomizedSearchCV were summarized in Additional File 5.

As shown in Fig. 2, the AUCs for the six models ranged from 0.710 to 0.756 in the validation cohort. As shown in Table 4, the XGBoost model performed best, achieving an AUC of 0.756 (95% CI, 0.705–0.807), with 0.693 (95% CI, 0.692–0.694) accuracy, 0.659 (95% CI, 0.587–0.731) sensitivity, 0.727 (95% CI, 0.660–0.793) specificity, 0.701 (95% CI, 0.629–0.772) PPV, 0.687 (95% CI, 0.619–0.754) NPV, 0.679 (95% CI, 0.619–0.737) F1 score and 0.202 (95% CI, 0.126–0.391) Brier score in the validation cohort. The XGBoost model showed superior calibration in the validation cohort, as evidenced by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test (p = 0.670, Table 4) and the calibration curve (Fig. 2), compared with logistic regression which demonstrated poor fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow test p = 0.001, Table 4). As shown in Fig. 2 and Additional file 6, the DCA curve of the XGBoost model in the validation cohort indicated that using the model to predict PLOS offered a greater net clinical benefit compared to both the treat-all and treat-none strategies within a threshold probability range of 20% to 95%, with the highest net benefit of 0.367 observed at a threshold probability of 20%. This corresponded to a net gain of 36.7 true-positive equivalents per 100 patients versus a treat-none strategy after accounting for the harm of unnecessary interventions, which further translated to 3.8 fewer unnecessary interventions per 100 patients compared with treating all at a 20% threshold.

SHAP plot for the XGBoost model

Owing to the robust performance of the XGBoost model, SHAP values were used to visually demonstrate the influence of specific features on the prediction of PLOS. As illustrated in the SHAP summary plots (Fig. 4), duration of surgery exhibited the strongest predictive value, followed by the highest pain score during 73–120 h after surgery, time to first flatus, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative infection, time to first defaecation, postoperative albumin infusion, postoperative hypotension, preoperative insufficient sleep, college or higher education, colon resection, preoperative hypoalbuminemia, postoperative acute kidney injury, high school education and rectum resection. Preoperative insufficient sleep and hypoalbuminemia, longer surgery duration, greater blood loss, higher postoperative pain, postoperative albumin infusion, and postoperative complications—including delayed gastrointestinal recovery, hypotension, infection, and acute kidney injury—were all positively associated with an increased risk of PLOS. In contrast, college or higher education level and colon resection appeared to be protective factors against PLOS after laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery. Discussion

In this study, we utilized perioperative data from the FDP-PONV trial, comprising 1141 patients underwent laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery, to develop and validate six machine learning models for predicting PLOS. Among the six models, the XGBoost model exhibited good fit by calibration curve analysis, good discriminative ability performed well with an AUC of 0.816 by five times repeated tenfold cross-validation. The XGBoost model provided the highest net clinical benefit of 0.367 with a threshold probability of 20%, which indicated clinical utility across a broad spectrum of decision-maker preferences while identifying 20% as the most favorable trade-off point for implementation. Furthermore, SHAP analysis was employed to visualize the contribution of seven predictors to PLOS, providing novel insights into its risk stratification. We also implemented the XGBoost model as a web-based calculator at https://nmuq6dxcdbnvcqyjthhemn.streamlit.app/ with our institution’s clinical informatics team.

To translate our PLOS prediction into routine care, we will embed the model within the electronic health record. If a patient’s risk exceeds 20%, they’ll be auto-flagged for a multidisciplinary review and placed on enhanced recovery measures, including extra nursing checks, early mobilization, tailored nutrition and a multimodal approach to prevent postoperative ileus. Using the 2023 national average bed-day cost of 1172 CNY [7], an implementation cost of approximately 400 CNY per alerted case (Additional file 12), and a conservative length of stay reduction of 1.0 day among flagged patients, the net saving per flagged patient is 772 CNY. We will validate the XGBoost model in independent cohorts from Foresea Life Insurance Guangzhou General Hospital. The external validation will follow a predefined timeline: (1) January 1-March 31, 2026 — institutional ethics committee approval; (2) April 1-June 30, 2026 — data extraction and harmonization; (3) July 1-September 30, 2026 — locked analysis in accordance with the statistical analysis plan; and (4) October 1-December 31, 2026 — report preparation and manuscript drafting. The protocol will be registered at ClinicalTrials.gov upon approvals.

Predictive factors associated with PLOS are complex and may exist in every link during hospitalization. This study utilized the variables prospectively collected from the FDP-PONV trial, covering the preoperative, intraoperative, and postoperative periods, to provide comprehensive data coverage. This approach differs from previous PLOS prediction models, which primarily focused on preoperative and intraoperative factors or relied on retrospective analyses of clinical medical records [12, 14, 24, 43]. Prediction models were established based on 13 perioperative factors. Among these, nine classic factors, including preoperative hypoalbuminemia, procedure type, duration of surgery, blood loss, postoperative pain score, postoperative acute kidney injury, postoperative infection, time to first flatus and defecation were reported to be associated with PLOS in previous prediction models [18, 44–47]. Additionally, four interesting factors were identified: education level, preoperative insufficient sleep, postoperative hypotension and postoperative albumin infusion.

Compared to the low level of education, high level of education were negatively related to the occurrence of PLOS in this study. Preoperative education is a crucial component of ERAS care as it can reduce length of stay [43, 48]. With the promotion of ERAS, the level of education is increasingly important in recovery since it affects the patients’ comprehension of the strategies for rehabilitation and their compliance [49]. Early and repeated education in the perioperative period by nurse specialists is essential for the patients with low level of education.

Sleep is vital for maintaining healthy mental, physical, and metabolic states [50]. Insufficient sleep duration (< 4 h) could elevate the risk of cognitive disorders [51], and higher preoperative sleep quality induced a shorter stay after total joint arthroplasty [52]. Based on previous studies and the collected data, this study offered a cut-off value of sleep duration on the night before surgery and found that a sleep duration of < 4 h was a risk factor for PLOS.

Most studies have focused on the relationship between intraoperative hypotension and adverse events [53, 54]. This study fully investigated blood pressure events, including intraoperative hypotension and hypertension, postoperative hypotension, and hypertension; only postoperative hypotension was selected. This finding was consistent with that of a cohort study showing that only postoperative hypotension, and not intraoperative hypotension, was associated with myocardial injury in 1710 patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery [55], suggesting that postoperative hypotension should also be paid more attention to.

Although albumin infusion is usually considered to reduce tissue edema, this study found that postoperative albumin infusion was associated with a higher likelihood of PLOS, regardless of perioperative hypoalbuminemia status. Albumin infusion likely reflects a combination of underlying disease severity and treatment intensity, and no causal relationship between postoperative albumin infusion and PLOS is implied. However, previous studies also found that perioperative fluid resuscitation with albumin was associated with an increased risk of acute renal failure, bleeding, PLOS and most other complications [56–58]. Moreover, early oral diet resumption within ERAS pathways is considered safe for patients and reduce length of hospital stay following lower gastrointestinal surgery [43, 59]. These results suggest that after surgery, albumin infusion should be guided by clinical necessity and administered as minimally as possible.

Our study has several limitations. This study is limited by the characteristics of the original FDP-PONV trial population, which predominantly included participants with high risk of PONV and excluded patients with severe comorbidities (e.g., ASA class IV), potentially restricting the generalizability of our findings to more diverse or medically complex surgical populations. Second, this was a single-center study conducted at one institution in China and we used five times repeated tenfold cross-validation to access the performance of prediction models. However, an external validation cohort would better reflect real-world prediction scenarios. Third, this work represents a proof-of-concept in a well-characterized, high-risk group and a future prospective pilot study should focus on external validation in multi-center, heterogeneous cohorts to assess the model’s performance across different healthcare systems, surgical procedures, and demographic groups.

In conclusion, we developed a machine‑learning model to predict PLOS in patients undergoing laparoscopic gastrointestinal surgery. Our web application could enable clinicians to calculate the individualized risk of PLOS and implement targeted interventions to shorten hospitalization time.
