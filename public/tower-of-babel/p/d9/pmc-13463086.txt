Bonding character and octahedral geometry as key determinants of solute miscibility in Ir-based oxides
Authors: Ki Hyun Park, Hyojin Kim, Dongho Kim, Daehyeon Wang, Juneseo Ahn, Chang Hyun Park, Jun Seop Kim, Hyung Bin Bae, Sung-Yoon Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The miscibility between two oxides can be estimated using the Hume-Rothery criteria, which consider crystal structure, ionic radius, and cation valence. However, rutile-based IrO2−TiO2 and IrO2−SnO2 systems show unexpectedly limited miscibility despite fully satisfying these criteria. This puzzling phenomenon has not been explained since their phase diagrams were first reported in 1967. Here we find that d0 and d10 M cations (such as 3d0 Ti4+ and 4d10 Sn4+) form primarily ionic M–O bonds due to the lack of overlap between their d states and O 2p states, while Ir–O bonds in IrO2 exhibit highly covalent bonding characteristics. Consequently, these two distinct bonding types at a shared oxygen site in the edge-sharing rutile structure lead to geometrically frustrated electron density localization, resulting in the immiscibility of M in IrO2. However, this frustration is significantly alleviated when the bonds meet in a corner-sharing octahedral geometry with a nearly linear Ir–O–M configuration. Complete solid solutions across the entire composition range can thus be achieved in cubic-perovskite-based Ir oxides with exclusively corner-sharing octahedral geometry. Our findings highlight the crucial role of bonding characteristics and octahedral geometries in determining oxide miscibility.

Introduction

Forming crystalline solid solutions and determining the solubility limits of solute cations are fundamental yet critical challenges in the synthesis of single-phase oxide materials with multiple compositions. Although the semi-empirical rules Hume-Rothery proposed in 19361 and later extended with Darken and Gurry in 19532 were originally developed to explain miscibility in metallic alloys, they have also been widely applied to oxide systems as general guidelines. As pointed out in a recent work3, the Hume-Rothery criteria have limited predictive power, once surface effects and multicomponent interactions become important, particularly in high-entropy nanoscale solid solutions. Nevertheless, for binary systems with comparable ionic radii and identical crystal structure, the qualitative Hume-Rothery criteria still provide a reasonably consistent guideline for miscibility, especially when evaluated within a single oxidation state series.

According to these rules, the miscibility between two crystalline oxides is restricted when (1) the ionic radii of the cations differ by more than 15%, (2) the two oxides possess different crystal structures, or (3) the cations are aliovalent. For example, three NiO-based binary systems, NiO−MgO, NiO−CaO, and NiO−SiO2, provide representative demonstrations of the applicability of the Hume-Rothery rules to various oxides4. NiO–MgO forms a complete solid solution across the entire composition range (see Supplementary Fig. 1a), as both oxides adopt a rocksalt structure, the cations (Ni2+ and Mg2+) are divalent, and their ionic radii are similar (69 pm and 72 pm, respectively). In contrast, miscibility in NiO–CaO is significantly restricted (see Supplementary Fig. 1b) due to the large difference in ionic radii (69 pm vs. 100 pm for Ca2+), despite their identical valence and crystal structure. For NiO–SiO₂, solid solubility is further suppressed (see Supplementary Fig. 1c) owing to both the pronounced mismatch in cation size (69 pm vs. 40 pm for Si4+) and the structural incompatibility between rocksalt NiO and quartz SiO2.

While the miscibility in many binary oxide systems is reliably predicted by the Hume-Rothery rules, rutile-type IrO2 has long posed a notable anomaly that is not accounted for by the conventional criteria. As shown in Fig. 1a, a complete solid solution forms between rutile IrO2 (cation radius, r(Ir4+) = 63 pm) and rutile RuO2 (r(Ru4+) = 62 pm), in good agreement with the rules5. In stark contrast, IrO2 and TiO2 exhibit markedly limited miscibility (see Fig. 1b), even though all three major criteria are fully satisfied: identical rutile structures, a less than 15% difference in ionic radii (r(Ir4+) = 63 pm; r(Ti4+) = 61 pm), and the same tetravalence. Furthermore, IrO2 shows nearly immiscible behavior with rutile SnO2 (r(Sn4+) = 69 pm), as evident in the phase diagram in Fig. 1b (see Supplementary Fig. 2 for experimentally verified powder diffraction results as well). To unravel the long-standing puzzle presented by rutile IrO2, this work focuses on the distinct electronic structures of metal cations and their bonding characteristics, beyond the conventional Hume-Rothery criteria. The partially filled Ir 5 d states in IrO2 significantly overlap with the O 2p states near the Fermi level (EF) and give rise to highly covalent Ir−O bonds. In contrast, completely empty d0 and fully occupied d10 cations, such as Ti4+ ([Ar] 3d0) and Sn4+ ([Kr] 4d10), show a predominantly ionic bonding character due to their lack of overlap with O 2p states. Consequently, it becomes energetically unfavorable for oxygen to simultaneously accommodate both covalent and ionic bonding interactions in M−O−Ir linkages (where M is d0 or d10 cations) within the rutile lattice consisting of edge-sharing oxygen octahedra.

More importantly, we demonstrate that this intrinsic miscibility limitation in the rutile structure is resolved in AxIryOz-type complex iridium oxides (A = Ca, Sr, or Ba) with a framework of corner-sharing octahedra. In such structures, the two distinct bonds in the M–O–Ir linkage linearly align in opposite directions, effectively mitigating the instability arising from bonding heterogeneity. As a result, the structural feature of ordered occupancy of M in hexagonal-perovskite Ba(MxIr1−x)O3 can be reasonably understood. Furthermore, complete solid solutions across the entire composition range between Ir and Sn, as well as between Ir and Ti, are attainable in cubic-perovskite-derived CaIrO3 and Sr2IrO4, both of which are composed exclusively of corner-sharing octahedra. Our combined density-functional theory (DFT) calculations and experimental results not only resolve a half-century-old scientific puzzle regarding the unexpected immiscibility in rutile IrO2, but also reveal that bonding character and octahedral geometry are critical factors affecting solute miscibility in oxides.

Results and Discussion

Metal−oxygen bonding character and solute miscibility in rutile IrO2

Despite sharing the same rutile structure, having very similar cationic radii, and presenting the same tetravalent oxidation state, IrO2, RuO2, TiO2, and SnO2 show distinct differences in the electronic states of their metal d orbitals and their degree of overlap with O 2p states. As indicated by arrows in the plots of density of states (DOS) in Fig. 1c, the partially filled Ir 5 d and Ru 4 d states, along with O 2p states, are significantly distributed across EF in both IrO2 and RuO2, resulting in strong overlap between metal d and O 2p orbitals. In contrast, as the Ti4+ cations in TiO2 have empty 3 d0 orbitals, the majority of the Ti 3d states are located above EF in the conduction band, as illustrated in the DOS plot in Fig. 1d. For Sn4+, with the electronic configuration [Kr] 4d10 5s0 5p0, the completely filled 4d10 states lie far below −8 eV (not depicted here), while the empty 5s0 5p0 states are located above +4 eV. Consequently, the absence of electronic state overlapping in metal d and O 2p states, along with the presence of a bandgap, is evident in the DOS plots, as indicated by white arrows in Fig. 1d.

To visualize changes in the electron distribution resulting from bond formation in the lattice, we acquired electron density difference fields, Δρe, through DFT calculations (see the Methods for Δρe)7–10. As observed in the isosurface contours of the difference field in Fig. 1e, the electron orbitals around both Ir/Ru cations and O anions are distinctly oriented toward each other in IrO2 and RuO2. This directional distribution reflects the highly covalent nature of the Ir–O and Ru–O bonds, in good agreement with the DOS plots in Fig. 1c, where a strong overlap between the Ir/Ru d states and O 2p states is evident. For comparison, the isosurfaces in TiO2 and SnO2 are presented in Fig. 1f. A readily discernible feature is the absence of electron density around Ti and Sn, with much less directional contours appearing exclusively around O anions. This demonstrates that the Ti–O and Sn–O bonds are predominantly ionic in nature, driven primarily by electrostatic interaction between the metal cations and oxygen anions.

Our DFT calculations also quantitatively verified the lattice instability of IrO2 when doped with Ti4+ and Sn4+. The substitutional energy (ΔEs) was calculated using the equation ΔEs = E(Ir3MO8) – 3E(IrO2) – E(MO2), where M represents Ru, Ti, or Sn (see Methods). While ΔEs for Ru4+ was negative (–380 meV), indicating favorable substitution, the values for Ti4+ and Sn4+ were positive (+110 and +185 meV, respectively), consistently supporting immiscible behavior. This instability, which leads to immiscibility, appears to originate from frustration caused by an asymmetric electron-density distribution associated with dissimilar bonding characteristics. As magnified in Fig. 2a, the isosurface shows a three-fold symmetric electron density around O sites that are coordinated by three covalent Ir–O bonds within the edge-sharing octahedral framework of the rutile structure. However, when ionic d0 Ti4+ or d10 Sn4+ substitutes for Ir4+, the symmetric field of the electron density is inevitably frustrated by the presence of Ti−O or Sn−O ionic bonds. As illustrated in Fig. 3a, b, the foreign cations in the IrO2 matrix can be classified into three groups: (Group I) ionic d0 cations with empty d states (Al3+, Sc3+, Ti4+, Zr4+, Nb5+, Hf4+, Ta5+, and W6+) located far above EF; (Group II) ionic d10 cations with fully filled d states (Zn2+, Ga3+, Ge4+, In3+, Sn4+, and Sb5+) located far below EF; and (Group III) dn cations (where n = 1−9; V4+, Cr4+, Mn4+, Fe3+, Co2+, Ni2+, Cu2+, Mo4+, and Ru4+, including Ir4+) whose d states overlap with O 2p states near EF. Typical DOS plots for three representative cations are exemplified for each of the groups in Fig. 3c. For Group I d0 cations, the DOS plots of Ti, Zr, and Ta in Fig. 3c consistently show that their metal d states reside largely above EF in the conduction band, leaving the valence band region exclusively dominated by O 2p character with no meaningful orbital overlap. To validate that the solubility is dependent on the bonding character, we carried out X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses of polycrystalline IrO2 samples containing 30% of foreign cations (composition: Ir0.7M0.3O2, where M represents each of the 21 cations). All 21 XRD patterns are provided in Supplementary Fig. 7. As observed in the patterns, many samples consist of two-phase mixtures comprising IrO2 and the corresponding counter oxides. More importantly, although TiO2, GeO2, SnO2, VO2, CrO2, MoO2, and RuO2 all adopt a rutile structure, the electronic structures of their cations and their bonding behaviors with O (ionic vs. covalent) critically determine the degree of miscibility. To demonstrate that the bonding-character-based miscibility framework is not unique to IrO2 but represents a more general principle, we extended the same systematic investigation to RuO2, which similarly exhibits strong Ru 4d–O 2p orbital overlap and highly covalent Ru–O bonding. As shown in the XRD patterns of Ru0.7M0.3O2 (Supplementary Fig. 8), three distinct miscibility behaviors are observed as well, perfectly mirroring the trends in IrO2: foreign cations with crystal structures different from rutile are largely insoluble; ionic-bonding d0 and d10 cations (Ti4+, Ge4+, and Sn4+) remain immiscible despite sharing the rutile structure; and covalently bonding dn cations (V4+, Cr4+, Mo4+, and Ir4+) readily form single-phase solid solutions. The consistent reproduction of this bonding-character-dependent miscibility trend across two independent rutile host systems with distinct d-orbital configurations (Ir 5d5 vs. Ru 4d4) strongly supports the generality of the proposed framework.

Solute miscibility in hexagonal-perovskite iridates

As shown in Fig. 2a, the close proximity of distinct Ir−O and M−O bonds converging at an O site with a narrow angle induces geometric frustration in the electron density within the edge-sharing oxygen octahedral lattice of the rutile structure. We found that this instability is avoidable in complex iridates, such as hexagonal-perovskite BaIrO3, when some of the octahedra are arranged in a corner-sharing configuration. The original hexagonal-perovskite polytype, 9 R, of pristine BaIrO3 transforms into the 6H and 12 R polytypes if other metal cations are incorporated (see Supplementary Figs. 9−11 for the structures and XRD results). Solute miscibility in iridates with corner-sharing octahedra

The results of this study clearly indicate that Ir−O−M bonding geometry plays a pivotal role in determining solute miscibility in Ir-based oxides. As shown above, the linear geometry based on the corner-sharing octahedra is notably favorable for accommodating the uneven electron-density localization within oxygen atoms that bridge covalent Ir−O and ionic M−O bonds. In this context, CaIrO3 with a cubic perovskite-derived structure, in which all [IrO6] octahedra are corner-connected38 (see the crystal structure and isosurface contours in Fig. 6a), is expected to exhibit high solute miscibility. This is indeed confirmed by the XRD patterns of Ca(M″xIr1−x)O3 (M″ = Ru, Ti, Zr, and Sn) in Fig. 6b. In all four systems, continuous solid solutions are successfully achieved across the entire composition range. Figure 6c presents EDS elemental maps of Ca(M″0.5Ir0.5)O3 that provide direct evidence of the distribution of Ir and solutes within single particles (see Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15 for an additional set of EDS maps). Although single-phase solid solutions were formed, nanoscale compositional clustering, characterized by Ir-rich and M″-rich regions, was observed within individual particles for the Ti-, Zr-, and Sn-substituted samples (see Supplementary Fig. 16 for an additional EDS map). This clustering behavior indicates that the instability induced by the asymmetric electron-density distribution at shared O sites is not entirely eliminated, even with the linear Ir−O−M″ geometry.

The clustering induces locally varying strain fields due to differences in lattice parameters across regions. To examine the strain distribution, we carried out a four-dimensional (4D) STEM analysis39 using a converged electron probe to raster-scan individual particles while recording diffraction patterns at each scan position (see Supplementary Fig. 17 for details of 4D-STEM). Given that the lattice parameters of CaSnO3 are larger than those of CaIrO3, the tensile (+εxx) and compressive (−εxx) regions observed in the strain map in Fig. 6d correlate well with the Sn-rich and Ir-rich nanodomains identified in the EDS map, respectively (see Supplementary Fig. 18 for additional consistent 4D-STEM results).

Another important iridate material with a corner-sharing geometry is Sr2IrO4, which has a Ruddlesden−Popper phase structure, An+1BnX3n+1 (n = 1)40. As illustrated in Fig. 7a, Sr2IrO4 features a layered structure consisting of [IrO6] octahedra that are corner-shared in a two-dimensional lateral arrangement. Extension to Ru-based oxides

To further establish the generality of the proposed framework, we extended the same investigation to Ru-based complex oxides, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 19. The bonding-character and geometry-dependent miscibility behavior is consistently reproduced across all three structural families examined. Incorporation of ionic d0 Ta5+ and Ti4+ into BaRuO3 induces identical structural transformations to the 12 R and 6H polytypes observed in the corresponding Ir-based systems, with ionic cations selectively occupying corner-sharing octahedral sites in both cases. Furthermore, complete solid solutions across the entire composition range are achieved in Ca(TixRu1−x)O3, directly mirroring the behavior of Ca(TixIr1−x)O3. The perfect one-to-one correspondence between the Ir-based and Ru-based systems across all three structural families provides compelling evidence that the bonding-character and geometry-dependent miscibility framework established in this work is a general principle governing solute miscibility in covalent transition metal oxides.

Implications on Ir-based oxides

This study provides several key insights into the structural stability and design principles of Ir-based oxides. First, as schematically illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 6, 5 d orbitals are more spatially extended than 3 d and 4 d orbitals. Due to their greater radial reach, they overlap more effectively with O 2p orbitals, resulting in much stronger covalent bonding41–43. Consequently, it is energetically favorable for 3 d Fe3+, Co2+, and Ni2+, which exhibit a relatively lower degree of covalency, to occupy the corner-sharing octahedral sites, rather than substitute the highly covalent 5 d Ir4+ ions in the face-sharing dimers in 6H Ba(M0.33Ir0.67)O3 (see Supplementary Fig. 20 for direct visualization by atomic-scale EDS mapping).

Second, as clearly demonstrated above, the formation of rutile-type IrO2 solid solutions with targeted foreign elements is highly challenging, while several studies have allegedly reported the synthesis of Ir−M oxides44–49. Thus, it is essential to precisely determine the atomic structure and identify the resulting phases following the synthesis of Ir-based oxides before considering their applications. In addition, although further follow-up studies are needed, our findings on the successful synthesis of Ir-based solid-solution oxides may contribute to the design of a new family of multi-component oxides with significantly reduced Ir content, while still preserving electrocatalytic activity and electrochemical stability for acidic water electrolysis.

Finally, the corner-sharing octahedral framework identified in this work not only enables the formation of complete solid solutions with otherwise immiscible ionic cations but also has direct functional implications for acidic oxygen evolution reaction (OER) catalysis. As presented in Supplementary Figs. 21–23, we systematically measured the OER activity of 38 Ir-based oxide compositions (11 barium iridates, 16 calcium iridates, and 11 strontium iridates) in acidic media (see Supplementary Figs. 24–26 for their particle morphologies). As summarized in Fig. 8, numerous solid-solution compositions exhibit OER activity at 1.53 VRHE that meets or exceeds that of pristine IrO2 (Sigma-Aldrich). As expected, increasing the content of electrochemically inactive but chemically stable ionic cations (Ti, Sn, Nb, and Ta) inevitably leads to a gradual decrease in OER activity at higher substitution levels. Nevertheless, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 27, compositions with 20 to 40 at% Ir content maintain activities competitive with pristine IrO2, highlighting a practically relevant activity-stability trade-off window in which meaningful Ir reduction38,50 can be achieved without unacceptable sacrifice of catalytic performance. Our DFT calculations reveal that d0 and d10 M cations (e.g., Ti4+ and Sn4+), whose d states do not overlap with O 2p states near EF, form largely ionic M−O bonding characteristics. This behavior contrasts with that of IrO2, where substantial overlap between Ir4+ 5 d and O 2p states leads to highly covalent Ir−O bonding. When two such distinct bonding types converge at a common O site in rutile IrO2, a geometrically frustrated electron density is induced at the O site. Because this electron localization is energetically unfavorable, the miscibility of ionic d0 and d10 solute cations is significantly limited, despite meeting all of the Hume-Rothery criteria.

We also found that this frustration can be effectively mitigated when the two distinct bonds meet at a shared O site within a corner-sharing octahedral framework to result in a linear Ir−O−M geometry. Accordingly, although the d0 and d10 cations show low solubility limits in edge-sharing IrO2, they preferentially occupy the corner-sharing octahedral sites in hexagonal-perovskite BaIrO3 polytypes. Furthermore, these cations are fully miscible and form continuous solid solutions in cubic perovskite CaIrO3 and Ruddlesden−Popper phase Sr2IrO4, both of which consist of corner-sharing oxygen octahedra. In addition to clarifying the origin of the immiscibility of Ti and Sn in IrO2, this study emphasizes that bonding character and geometry are critical factors to consider when evaluating the solute miscibility in Ir-based oxides.

Methods

DFT calculations

Ab initio DFT calculations were performed to obtain the DOS and the isosurfaces of electron density difference fields of rutile-type IrO2, RuO2, TiO2, SnO2, and complex iridates under the spin-polarized generalized-gradient approximation (GGA) along with the PBEsol functional. The ultrasoft pseudopotentials were utilized for ionic cores, as implemented in the CASTEP code (Biovia Inc., Materials Studio 2019). The PBEsol functional was selected as it is optimized for densely packed solids. As our bonding-character analysis relies on the distribution of occupied M d and O 2p states within the valence band rather than on the precise magnitude of the band gap, the conclusions are not affected by the well-known band-gap underestimation of GGA-level functionals. In all cases, low-spin (t2g5)(eg0) for the Ir4+ 5d5 states and (t2g6)(eg0) for the Ir3+ 5d6 states were assumed. For the substituted foreign cations in IrO2, the following electronic states were taken with (1 × 1 × 3) supercells with a (5 × 5 × 3) k-point mesh: (t2g1)(eg0) for V4+; high-spin (t2g2)(eg0) for Cr4+; high-spin (t2g3)(eg0) for Mn4+; low-spin (t2g5)(eg0) for Fe3+; low-spin (t2g6)(eg1) for Co2+; high-spin (t2g6)(eg2) for Ni2+; (t2g6)(eg3) for Cu2+; high-spin (t2g2)(eg0) for Mo4+; and low-spin (t2g4)(eg0) for Ru4+. The plane-wave basis set for the kinetic energy cutoff was set to 500 eV. Relaxation of the internal coordinates in the unit cell was performed using the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) algorithm with convergence tolerances of 0.1 eV Å−1 for the maximum ionic force, 5 × 10−5 eV/atom for the total energy, and 0.005 Å for the maximum ionic displacement.

Synthesis of rutile-type Ir oxides and Ru oxides with foreign elements

All of the rutile-type Ir oxides and Ru oxides in this work were synthesized via sol-gel and hydrothermal methods. Iridium (IV) chloride hydrate (≥99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), ruthenium (III) chloride hydrate (99.98%, Sigma-Aldrich), aluminum chloride hexahydrate (99%, Sigma-Aldrich), scandium (III) chloride hydrate (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), titanium (III) chloride solution (Sigma-Aldrich), vanadium (III) chloride (99%, Alfa Aesar), chromium (III) nitrate nonahydrate (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), manganese (II) chloride tetrahydrate (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), iron (III) nitrate nonahydrate (≥99.95%, Sigma-Aldrich), cobalt (II) nitrate hexahydrate (99.999%, Alfa Aesar), nickel (II) chloride hexahydrate (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), copper (II) nitrate hemi(pentahydrate) (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), zinc nitrate hexahydrate (99.998%, Alfa Aesar), gallium (III) nitrate hydrate (99.9%, Sigma-Aldrich), germanium (IV) oxide (99.999%, Alfa Aesar), zirconium oxynitrate hydrate (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), ammonium niobate (V) oxalate hydrate (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), ammonium molybdate (para) tetrahydrate (99%, Thermo Scientific), indium (III) nitrate hydrate (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), tin (IV) chloride pentahydrate (98%, Sigma-Aldrich), hafnium (IV) oxychloride hydrate (99.99%, Sigma-Aldrich), tantalum (V) ethoxide (99.98%, Sigma-Aldrich), were used as precursors. Citric acid (99.5%, Sigma-Aldrich) and nitric acid (60.0%, Samchun) were used as chelating and oxidizing agents, respectively.

Pristine IrO2 and foreign-element-added IrO2 were prepared via a sol−gel process. Iridium (IV) chloride hydrate and dopant precursors, as listed above, were prepared according to stoichiometric ratios, with a total metal content of 0.5 mmol. Subsequently, 0.5 mmol of citric acid, 20 mL of nitric acid, and 80 mL of distilled water were added. In particular, for the incorporation of Ta, ethyl alcohol-based stock solutions of the dopants were used, while maintaining the total solution volume at 100 mL. The mixture was then heated to 90 °C and stirred continuously for 6 h until the solvent was fully evaporated. The gel was formed by heating at 170 °C overnight, and finally annealed at 900 °C−950 °C for 5−24 h in a tube-type furnace for crystallization. To synthesize V- and Mo-doped IrO2, 0.23 mL of nitric acid and 99.77 mL of distilled water were used instead, while all steps remained unchanged.

Pristine RuO2 and foreign-element-added RuO2 were prepared via the same sol−gel process, with specific modifications to the solution chemistry and thermal treatment. Unlike the IrO2-based material synthesis, 0.115 mL of nitric acid, and 99.885 mL of distilled water were used. Furthermore, RuO2-based materials were annealed at 700 °C−800 °C for 10 − 60 h in a tube-type furnace for crystallization. To synthesize V-, Cr-, Mo-, and Ir-doped RuO2, 1.5 mmol of ethylene glycol was additionally introduced into the above total solution, while maintaining the rest of the procedure consistently.

For ‘hydrothermal IrO2’ used as a reference for the S-number comparison, 0.5 mmol of iridium (IV) chloride hydrate was dissolved in 5 mL of distilled water. To obtain a homogeneous aqueous solution, the mixture was stirred vigorously at 95 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, the solution was mixed with an additional 40 ml of distilled water and transferred to a PTFE-lined autoclave, followed by hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C for 1.5 h. After the reaction, the resulting black precipitate was collected, washed several times with deionized water and ethanol, dried, and finally annealed at 500 °C for 3 h.

BET, XRD, and Bragg-reflection simulations

The specific surface areas of the synthesized particles were measured using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method with a gas adsorption analyzer (Tristar II, Micromeritics). For the phase identification, X-ray diffraction (XRD) using diffractometry (SmartLab, Rigaku) with Cu-Kα radiation was utilized. XRD pattern simulations were also conducted using CrystalDiffract (CrystalMaker Software Ltd, ver. 6.9.3) in conjunction with CrystalMaker (CrystalMaker Software Ltd, ver. 10.7.2) for Bragg-reflection verification.

SEM, STEM, EDS, and 4D-STEM

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, JSM-IT800, JEOL, and Magellan 400, Thermo Fisher Scientific) imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 5–15 kV to observe the polycrystalline structures and particle sizes of the samples. For STEM analyses, each powder sample was ultrasonically dispersed in high-purity ethyl alcohol for 15 min, and several drops of the suspension were placed onto lacey carbon film Cu or Au grids and then dried. Atomic-column-resolved STEM imaging was carried out using a transmission electron microscope (Spectra Ultra, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in high-angle annular dark-field (HAADF) and bright-field (BF) modes, at an operating voltage of 200 kV with spherical aberration corrections of the electron probe. The collection semiangles for the STEM detectors were set to be 63−200 mrad (HAADF) and 0 − 25 mrad (BF), respectively. Elemental mapping by atomic EDS maps was carried out by six integrated silicon-drift EDS detectors with a wide solid angle (4.04 srad). In addition, EDS mapping for bulk composition analysis was performed using a transmission electron microscope (Talos F200X, Thermo Fisher Scientific) operating at 200 kV. The 4D-STEM technique was employed in this study to analyze lattice parameter deviations caused by the inhomogeneous B-site distribution in Ca(SnₓIr1 − x)O₃. The same procedure as for STEM imaging was used to prepare the specimen. The 4D-STEM data were acquired using a Spectra Ultra at 200 kV, equipped with an electron microscopy pixel array detector (EMPAD, Thermo Fisher Scientific). 4D-STEM produces a four-dimensional dataset, with two dimensions representing the real-space scan positions of the electron probe on the sample, and two dimensions representing the corresponding diffraction pattern (reciprocal space) at each position (see Supplementary Fig. 17a). Both real-space and reciprocal-space data were acquired with a resolution of 128 × 128 pixels. An acquisition time of 1 ms per pixel resulted in a total electron dose of 5.12 × 109 electrons per dataset. A convergence semiangle of 0.7 mrad was used. Lattice parameter deviation maps were generated from the 4D-STEM data by analyzing the electron diffraction patterns at each scan position using the py4DSTEM package (v0.14.8).

Electrochemical measurements

All electrochemical tests were carried out in 0.1 M HClO4 (pH ≈ 1) electrolyte, prepared by diluting 70% HClO4 (99.999%, Sigma-Aldrich) with Milli-Q water (18.2 MΩ·cm). A Pt wire and an Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl) electrode were used as the counter and reference electrode, respectively. All recorded potentials (EAg/AgCl) were converted to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) scale (ERHE) using the following equation. ERHE = EAg/AgCl + 0.05922 ×× pH + E0Ag/AgCl, where E0Ag/AgCl is the standard potential of Ag/AgCl (3 M NaCl) at 25 °C, i.e., 0.21 V. To fabricate the working electrode, catalyst ink was prepared by mixing 17.6 mg of catalyst powder, 1.76 mg of acetylene black, 0.176 mL of K+-exchanged 5-wt% Nafion solution (D521, Dupont), and 2.324 mL of high-purity ethyl alcohol. The mixture was thoroughly ultrasonicated to ensure homogeneity. Subsequently, a 5 µL of the ink was drop-cast onto a glassy carbon ring-disk electrode (RRDE-3A, ALS, 3 mm in diameter) and then dried at 80 °C in a drying oven for 2 h. The catalyst loading was 0.5 mg/cm2, and all measurements were conducted using a potentiostat (SP-300, Biologic).
