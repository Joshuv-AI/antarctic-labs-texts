A physics-informed alternative to Richardson-Lucy deconvolution across SNR regimes without iteration cutoffs
Authors: Zachary H. Hendrix, Peter T. Brown, Rory Kruithoff, Tim Flanagan, Douglas P. Shepherd, Ayush Saurabh, Steve Pressé
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Richardson-Lucy deconvolution is widely used to restore imaged objects blurred by a point spread function and corrupted by noise and is known to readily overfit noise, leading to high-frequency artifacts. Practical use therefore relies on hand-tuned stopping criteria or ad hoc regularization with limited physical justification. To resolve this problem, we present DeBayes: a rigorous Bayesian deconvolution framework that builds upon a physically accurate image formation model. DeBayes performs deconvolution in the spatial domain, jointly models accurate noise sources, and infers full posterior distributions over the underlying object. It avoids assumptions of sparsity or continuity, yields strictly positive reconstructions, and converges stably without user-tuned regularization parameters or iteration cutoffs. Our method is unsupervised and designed for fast, parallelizable computation, providing a principled alternative to Richardson-Lucy for robust, physics-informed image reconstruction. We demonstrate DeBayes’ stable convergence and minimal noise amplification on simulated and experimental images of mitochondria networks in HeLa cells.

Introduction

Deconvolution improves contrast and recovers small, or equivalently, high spatial frequency features in images obtained by diffraction-limited optical equipment such as telescopes and microscopes. However, diffraction of light ultimately limits spatial frequencies supported by the optics, leading to attenuation and loss of high-frequency features1. All information from spatial frequencies above the microscope bandpass is lost in the image formation process, making the inverse problem of recovering the underlying object ill-posed. The ill-posedness is exacerbated by the Poisson noise originating from the quantum nature of photons, the detector noise, inherent data pixelization, as well as the 3D distribution of light sources in realistic samples not captured by 2D images. In light of the stochasticity introduced by the image formation model, the full inverse problem is naturally re-pitched probabilistically, where high-frequency features outside the bandpass set by the Fourier transform of the point spread function (PSF) cannot be resolved with certainty.

In the 1970s, William H. Richardson and Leon B. Lucy independently proposed a computationally inexpensive algorithm to restore diffraction-degraded images through an iterative process maximizing the observation likelihood, assuming Poisson noise only. The resulting algorithm is now known as Richardson–Lucy (RL) deconvolution2,3. While Poissonian noise is a reasonable approximation in the higher signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) regime, with large photon counts, the true noise model deviates considerably when the detector noise is significant4. Furthermore, maximum likelihood methods are prone to overfitting data5. Indeed, the RL algorithm prefers sparse solutions and exhibits overfitting by amplifying noise, causing artifacts to appear in the deconvolved image. A contemporaneous alternative, maximum entropy deconvolution6, was later combined with RL to help identify an iteration stop criterion7.

To keep the noise model simple and avoid extra parameters irrelevant to high SNR data, many modern approaches still only consider Poissonian noise and only improve upon the likelihood maximization problem by introducing regularization8–14. Regularizers are added to the likelihood, and a two-term convex optimization problem is then solved for a single point object estimate. While these approaches have been successful in many contexts, they often require hand-crafted regularizers with carefully tuned hyperparameters8,9. Most commonly used regularizers can be classified according to the types of structures they are designed to recover to help reduce ill-posedness: smooth or sparse. Regularizers such as total variation (TV)10, Hessian11, and Wiener12,13 encourage object smoothness, while others such as compressed sensing encourage sparsity14.

The TV regularizer is among the most popular regularization techniques and involves minimizing the total variation in the deconvolved image, which encourages smoothness while preserving sharp edges. However, TV-based implementations often result in restored images manifesting staircase artifacts, whereby deconvolved images exhibit piecewise constant structures and loss of fine details (e.g., textures)10,15, visually adjusted via the regularization parameter.

Hessian matrices are yet another regularizer encouraging smoothness, but avoid oversharpening of boundaries between regions of significantly different intensities11. Here, unlike the TV penalty, where the integral over the first-order derivatives or pixel-to-pixel variation is minimized, integration is performed over second-order derivatives, enforcing differentiability of the object at every point. However, while differentiability encourages smoothness of transitions between regions, it may result in loss of resolution.

Wiener regularizers, contrary to TV and Hessian, work in the Fourier domain. These regularizers suppress high-frequency noise based on SNR at each frequency. However, SNR is difficult to characterize at each frequency and requires ad hoc estimates to balance artifacts and deblurring.

Compressed sensing14 is another optimization technique that allows deconvolving or denoising images where the data is sparse in some basis (for instance, the Fourier basis or a wavelet basis16). A combination of sparsity and continuity assumptions have also been used in image restoration17. Such strategies require advanced knowledge of the underlying object of interest, which may not always be available.

More recently, plug-and-play (PnP) regularization has emerged as an alternative to classical variational regularization in image deconvolution. In PnP methods, the explicit regularization functional is replaced by a powerful image denoiser–such as BM3D or a learned neural denoiser–which is embedded within an iterative optimization framework, typically based on ADMM or related operator-splitting schemes18. The denoiser implicitly encodes complex image statistics otherwise difficult to capture using handcrafted priors such as TV or Hessian penalties, often resulting in improved texture preservation and perceptual quality. However, as the denoiser does not generally correspond to the proximal operator of a known regularization functional, PnP methods typically lack a well-defined underlying objective function or explicit Bayesian interpretation, and convergence guarantees are limited to restrictive assumptions on the denoiser. As a consequence, principled uncertainty quantification and parameter interpretability remain challenging.

Separately, neural networks have allowed fast deconvolutions without requiring any knowledge of the noise sources. On one hand, prior knowledge of diffraction or blurring physics can be incorporated implicitly by parameterizing the unblurred image using a specific network architecture. For example, using either convolutional neural networks (CNNs) as in the deep image prior (DIP) approach19 or coordinate-based neural radiance fields as implicit neural representations20 have been successful in various contexts. However, these networks are typically optimized iteratively using the input raw images only and have a tendency to overfit data without a suitable stopping criterion19,20. On the other hand, many fully supervised methods21–25 have been developed, demanding large amounts of input network training data before deconvolution can be performed and raising concerns as to their generalizability in deconvolving different structures.

Lastly, an automatic stopping criterion has been introduced recently26 that involves Poisson thinning to first distribute photons into two noisier images with lower photon counts followed by application of RL algorithm to each image, where consensus among RL gradients at each iteration is checked27 until a set threshold is reached. Such an approach is inherently dependent on SNR, made worse by Poisson thinning and resulting in unreliable stopping criteria, especially at low SNR. On the one hand, overfitting can occur due to noisy gradient directions, where it takes too long to arrive at consensus. On the other hand, loss of resolution may occur from underfitting where, due to the loss of high-frequency features by Poisson thinning, RL updates in both images are dominated by shared noise patterns after initially recovering the strongly informed low- frequency features, inducing premature convergence.

In light of these issues, we take a different approach and avoid hand-crafted regularizers and stopping criteria, or neural networks. Instead, we retain a physical image formation model as shown in Fig. 1 and present an unsupervised, tuning-free, and parallelizable Bayesian image deconvolution framework, DeBayes, incorporating an accurate noise model that now generates a full probability distribution (and uncertainty maps) over the deconvolved object. Such Bayesian reconstruction frameworks have been attempted in other microscopy domains, such as cryo-EM28 to study structural heterogeneity, highlighting the utility of Bayesian approaches for high-dimensional inverse problems, but not for optical microscopy techniques where the image formation process differs. Our framework intrinsically treats sparse and continuous samples on the same footing, and incorporates the physics of the optical setup. We mitigate the effect of overfitting from high spatial frequencies beyond the optical bandpass by placing a prior on spatial frequencies in accordance with those allowed by the optical transfer function (OTF) and by using the mean of the Monte Carlo samples to recover a smoothed object. Results

Our Bayesian image deconvolution tool DeBayes builds a posterior probability distribution describing the imaged object ρ given the data. As detailed in Methods, this posterior collects the data likelihood with prior knowledge codified into a prior probability distribution satisfying our desiderata mentioned earlier in our abstract. This posterior, however, does not admit an analytic form. Accordingly, we employ Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling29,30, to estimate the posterior over ρ as informed by the provided raw image.

Unlike RL deconvolution, where the likelihood—a product of pixel-wise Poisson distributions—is iteratively maximized, we consider instead a likelihood integrating all known noise sources corrupting the data. Our method naturally avoids the runaway overfitting by RL not by imposing ad hoc regularization tuning or iteration cutoffs, but rather by first informing the inference method of the optical setup’s bandpass via the prior; and second, by using the lack of information in the data itself beyond the bandpass. More concretely, we first devise a prior distribution that takes the OTF itself as the input and probabilistically suppresses frequencies beyond the bandpass in the samples themselves, as later described in Methods. Second, we note that the posterior itself is naturally degenerate for high-frequency modes beyond the bandpass. In other words, any one posterior sample including the maximum a posteriori (MAP) may exhibit high-frequency artifacts. However, the mean of the collected Monte Carlo samples smooths over these high-frequency modes while keeping low-frequency modes that are well-informed by the data intact. Consequently, the mean reflects the final deconvolved object. Finally, to achieve high performance, we use a parallelized MCMC inspired by ref. 31 to generate sufficient samples to draw from the posterior.

In the following sections, we apply our strategy to simulated and experimental data, demonstrating artifact-free recovery of the underlying objects, with no adjustable parameter. In contrast, RL degrades images by amplifying high-frequency noise with a rate determined by SNR – the higher the noise in the image, the fewer iterations it takes for the noise amplification to occur. Furthermore, attempts to control such amplification using regularizers produce inconsistent results.

In the second row of Fig. 2, we see progressive overfitting by RL deconvolution algorithm after 100 iterations; high-frequency, edge, striping, or ringing artifacts dominate over deconvolution, ironically visually degrading the restored image, though Fig. 2d suggests that the best peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and root mean square error (RMSE) are achieved early on at about ten iterations. On the contrary, the mean image produced by DeBayes in the third row eventually converges to the diffraction-limited ground truth object without giving rise to high-frequency artifacts. Moreover, the ground truth object in Fig. 2 contains step discontinuities. As the optics are diffraction-limited, discontinuities in ground truth objects are broadened in any physically consistent reconstruction. However, overshoot/ringing is not a generic consequence of band limitation alone; it depends on the effective transfer function near the diffraction limit cutoff. Finally, stable convergence is evident from the increasing PSNR and shrinking RMSE in Fig. 2e as we increase the number of samples used for averaging. As also shown in our conceptual Fig. 1, noise decreases outside the bandpass as more samples are collected. This progressive decrease in noise amplification in the mean is what we interpret to be approaching a stable solution. Such converging noise reduction implies that the user is free to stop at any point once the improvements are visually imperceptible, which is distinct from the case of RL deconvolution, where the user must choose, in an ad hoc manner, an optimal number of iterations beyond which the noise is further amplified.

Once more, as shown in Fig. 4, RL quickly degrades the image rather than restoring it, resulting in the generation and preservation of conspicuous artifacts. Such an iterative deterioration of the deconvolved image’s quality beyond ~20 iterations would typically require us to invoke at this stage a criterion for stopping iterations and whether to promote smoothness or sparsity of the final image using a regularizer based on visual appeal. For instance, as shown in the Supplementary Fig. S2, different choices of TV regularization parameter and RL iteration counts result in visually different results with artifacts. Our physics-informed Bayesian deconvolution tool, DeBayes, on the other hand, does not amplify noise and converges to a single high-contrast reconstruction by taking pixel-to-pixel camera calibration maps into account. Furthermore, we also compare these results with those produced by other deconvolution methods: gradient consensus, PetaKit’s OTF masked Wiener, and deep-learning based Zero-shot, as shown in Fig. 4. We note that Zero-shot reconstructions depend on the chosen network training and test-adaptation schedule, including the number of optimization iterations used22; therefore, the Zero-shot panel in Fig. 4 should be interpreted as the result obtained under the default iteration settings used here rather than as an exhaustive optimization over Zero-shot training schedules.

As evident from the PSNR plots in Fig. 4d, we find the highest similarity between the DeBayes deconvolved image and RL deconvolved images in the iteration range 10–25 beyond which RL appears to amplify noise significantly. While other deconvolution methods are able to minimize noise amplification, they do not seem to deconvolve/resolve small features optimally. For example, in the image deconvolved by gradient consensus, most features visible in the Bayesian deconvolved image are also present but are blurrier, indicating resolution loss. Such resolution loss is also present in images deconvolved by other methods. We can attribute the better performance of our Bayesian method to accurate noise modeling that incorporates detector calibration maps. We also note that given the perceptibly different levels of resolution in the deconvolved images, the computed PSNR numerical values do not seem to provide a reliable metric for comparisons. Finally, as a cautionary note, we note that visual comparisons are naturally challenging in Fig. 4 because of the absence of known ground truth. Therefore, uncertainty estimates, not provided by other methods, become critical. In other words, while the mean of posterior samples is what we use as a representation of the deconvolved object, it should be complemented with the uncertainty map to avoid visually interpreting possible noise amplification driven by low photon counts.

Uncertainty propagation from measurement to deconvolved object

Posterior probability distributions over a deconvolved object are estimated using MCMC sampling of the posterior, allowing us to compute not only point estimates for the restored object but also full distributions over candidate objects (and their respective probabilities) supported by the data and, thus, by construction, uncertainty estimates as well. For example, we can use well-separated (decorrelated) MCMC samples drawn from the posterior to compute standard deviations over density estimates and other measures, such as confidence intervals. As an example, in Fig. 5, we show the coefficient of variation (CV)—the ratio of standard deviation to the mean—for the DeBayes deconvolved image in Fig. 2. As expected, the relative uncertainty represented by the CV is lowest where large numbers of photons are collected. On the other hand, the solution’s degeneracy (the posterior’s flatness) becomes large in the darker outer regions of the image, resulting in much higher relative uncertainty. The ability to report uncertainty stands in sharp contrast to RL, which only reports point estimates. We also note here that the peak PSNR for the RL deconvolved object is similar to the PSNR that DeBayes converges to, as previously shown in Fig. 2. However, iterative deconvolution methods with stopping rules (e.g., RL or gradient-consensus) do not yield a uniquely defined estimator, as the reconstruction depends on SNR and algorithmic choices such as iteration count or consensus thresholds. Consequently, such reconstructions are not directly comparable to posterior uncertainty bands, which reflect marginalization over poorly constrained modes, namely spatial frequency components that are weakly informed or entirely unconstrained by the data (e.g., beyond the optical bandpass). Our package

Our package for Bayesian deconvolution is available at ref. 35. This Julia code takes the raw image as input, along with microscope and camera parameters. The package then runs a Monte Carlo sampler to generate hundreds of candidate deconvolved images along with their mean. Furthermore, these samples can then be used to compute uncertainty estimates of choice, such as quantiles—the range of potential error in a predicted, specific percentile of a data distribution—and variance.

Discussion

Deconvolution is the process of removing the blurring effect of the PSF introduced by the optical setup. Numerical instabilities inherent to the convolution operator’s inversion owing to noise generate high-frequency artifacts that have stymied rigorous, parameter-free, numerical deconvolution. While a fully probabilistic approach is ideal, families of tools that exist for deconvolution ultimately treat the removal of these high-frequency artifacts in a user-dependent fashion8–11,13 or rely on expensive training of neural networks to avoid high computational cost21–25. Here, we have successfully demonstrated parallelized artifact-free deconvolution of images with physically accurate noise modeling. Our method, DeBayes, does not have a preference for sparse or continuous solutions, unlike regularized maximum likelihood estimates. Only the data dictates whether a sparse or continuous solution is preferred. Furthermore, we imposed a prior informed by the imaging system’s optics, improving contrast for spatial frequencies within the diffraction limit while penalizing frequencies outside the optical setup’s bandpass to improve sampling efficiency. This physics-informed, OTF-aware prior imposes no constraints on the imaged object itself—only on the spatial frequencies allowed by the instrument. Though more computationally demanding, our method eliminates the tunable ad hoc number of RL iterations or regularization parameters used in image restoration, while yielding a posterior-mean reconstruction whose denoising arises naturally from averaging decorrelated Monte Carlo samples. This posterior averaging attenuates poorly informed high-frequency fluctuations while preserving structures supported by the data. The reported deconvolved object is therefore best interpreted as the posterior mean over many physically consistent candidate reconstructions, as opposed to a single heavily regularized point estimate.

Our framework can be generalized and improved in a number of ways. Our current implementation uses CPUs for parallelization; however, a GPU implementation can significantly reduce computation wall time. For the 1024 × 1024 pixel image in Fig. 3, the initial estimate obtained at 1000 iterations required 10−15 min of computation time on a desktop with 24-core i9 processor, whereas the final Bayesian result with uncertainty maps is computed after running the code for 50,000 iterations requiring a few hours for completion. Alternative camera models, such as EMCCDs36, can be easily implemented into the current framework as well, with the introduction of additional intermediate random variables to represent the electron multiplication stage. Finally, the physics-informed approach used to mitigate the amplification of high-frequency features immediately applies to other computational reconstruction approaches that rely on the PSF of a limited size for image formation. For instance, we have successfully demonstrated a similar approach for structured illumination microscopy31 where illumination patterns multiply with the underlying fluorescent object and the parallelization of the algorithm follows the same principles as presented here. Fourier ptychography is yet another computational imaging tool where reconstructions could benefit from a fully physics-informed inverse approach that provides full probability distributions over reconstructions37. This extends to other 3D imaging approaches that could equally benefit from a generalization of what we have provided herein.

Sampling strategy

Naively, one could apply a basic MCMC approach in which samples of the object ρ at each pixel are generated iteratively and sequentially using the classic Gibbs sampling algorithm29,30, where the object at mth pixel ρm is updated by treating values at all other pixels as constants. However, because the marginalized likelihood in Equation (9) involves an intractable summation over the unobserved photon emissions ϕn, posterior sampling must be augmented with an additional step. Specifically, we decompose sampling from the posterior into two steps by introducing Monte Carlo sampling of the latent variables ϕn to approximate the effect of this summation, as follows:

Computational implementation

Initial optimization phase

We empirically discovered that if we set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha ({\rho }_{m}^{{{\rm{prop}}}},{\rho }_{m}^{{{\rm{old}}}})$$\end{document}α(ρmprop,ρmold) in Equation (13) to 1 for approximately the first 1000 MCMC iterations or the warm-up/burn-in period beyond which we do not perceive significant changes in the structures, we recover a deconvolved object that closely resembles the mean image obtained from hundreds of uncorrelated samples. Consequently, this initial optimization phase provides a relatively inexpensive estimate of the deconvolved underlying object. However, to compute a full uncertainty map, a few hundred uncorrelated samples should be collected, which we accomplish by running the MCMC sampler for about 50,000 iterations, collecting samples every 400 iterations.

Collecting uncorrelated samples

Next, after the burn-in, DeBayes switches to full Bayesian inference by sampling from Equation (9) using our parallelized MCMC scheme in order to estimate the full posterior and uncertainty maps. Each subsequent iteration performs Metropolis-within-Gibbs updates of (i) the object ρ and (ii) the latent photon-counts ϕ, now evaluating proposals using the complete log-posterior (log-likelihood plus the OTF-informed Dirichlet prior). In contrast to the burn-in, these updates use a proper Metropolis–Hastings accept/reject step (with stochastic acceptance) so that samples are distributed according to the target posterior upon convergence. To reduce sample-to-sample correlations and encourage mixing, we use a cyclic tempering/annealing schedule (periodically reheating and cooling the sampler)43 and only compute posterior summaries from uncorrelated samples. Repeatedly applying simulated annealing43 artificially widens the shape of the posterior distribution at regular intervals and perturb the MCMC chain of samples by accepting improbable samples and allows the sampler to quickly escape local maxima. Specifically, we thin the MCMC chain by accumulating the running posterior mean only from non-successive reconstructions (saved at a fixed stride and only after the high-temperature portion of each annealing cycle). We run the chain for a total of 50,000 iterations, which is far greater than needed for convergence across the images tested. We consider the sampler converged once the monitored log-posterior stops climbing and only weakly fluctuates about a stationary value; continued sampling beyond this point does not materially change the posterior-mean reconstruction, indicating a stable solution.

Parallelization technique

To make probabilistic estimates computationally inexpensive, we do not make our noise model simple or make approximations. A naive approach for parallelizing our computations would be to run many independent full-field reconstructions (independent MCMC chains) in parallel and then average their outputs. However, the initial burn-in phase and annealing costs would be paid many times over, and memory and computation time would be prohibitively expensive when deconvolving large objects. Instead, we parallelize the generation of samples of a single global reconstruction by splitting the field of view into spatial chunks that exchange boundary information at each iteration.

Our parallelization technique is founded in the limited spread of light by diffraction. Due to the limited size of the PSF, the light entering a camera pixel originates only from a small neighborhood of the object surrounding that pixel. Conversely, a point on the object is only informed by a small set of camera pixels in the neighborhood of that point. Consequently, the probability distribution for a point on the object can be estimated from its immediate neighborhood. Such localization of the probability distributions allows for independent treatment of the different regions of a raw image and parallelization of computation. Parallelization is therefore achieved by breaking the raw image into smaller equally sized chunks padded with data in the neighborhood.

An important caveat to be considered, however, is that probability distribution for a point on the object implicitly depends on knowing the contribution of the rest of the object to the light entering the camera pixels in its neighborhood. From an optimization algorithm point of view, this conditioning implies that the points on the object are not independent and can only be estimated in a sequential manner once per iteration in the spirit of Gibbs sampling. Consequently, the image chunks must be updated sequentially as well. Parallelization of this sequential sampling is however much more challenging. A solution we have demonstrated to work successfully is wavefront parallelization, as shown in Fig. 6. Here, the MCMC sampling starts in a corner chunk first. The updated object in this corner chunk is then shared (only the padding) with the neighboring chunks that start updating themselves, and at the same time, the corner chunk immediately moves on to the next MCMC iteration. At this stage, the original corner chunk is at iteration 2 while its neighbors are at iteration 1, being updated simultaneously in parallel. These neighbors then share their updates with their neighboring chunks, moving themselves to the next MCMC iteration immediately. This process is repeated until the diagonally opposite corner chunk is reached and updated. Finally, all the chunks are now being updated simultaneously, achieving parallelized Gibbs sampling, but are at different MCMC iterations at a given time. Visually, as shown in Fig. 6, we see that chunks lying along a given “wavefront” transverse to the diagonal connecting the two corner chunks are always at the same MCMC iteration. HeLa cells (Kyoto strain) were grown in 60-mm glass Petri dishes on Poly-D-lysine coated 40 mm #1.5 coverslips (Bioptechs, 40-1313-03192) for a minimum of 48 h in DMEM media (ATCC 30-2002) supplemented with 10% FBS (ATCC 30-2020) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin solution (ATCC, 30-2300) at 37 ∘C and 5% CO2. Cells were live-stained with 200-nm Mitotracker Deep Red (Thermo Fisher, M22426) in DMEM medium for 15 min in the same incubation environment. The staining solution was then aspirated off, and fresh DMEM medium was added for 5 min to rinse. The sample coverslip was then transferred to an open-top Bioptechs FCS2 chamber and imaged in prewarmed (37 ∘C) DMEM culture medium.

HeLa cells with labeled mitochondria were imaged using a previously described custom multimodal microscope44,45 in the pseudo-widefield mode for 635 nm excitation light derived from a Laser diode (LSR635-500). The camera integration time was fixed at 100 ms, and the signal level was varied by changing the illumination time using the DMD as a fast shutter. Illumination times were 100, 10, and 1 ms.

Richardson–Lucy deconvolution

We used a Julia package, DeconvOptim.jl46, and a Python package, scikit-image47, to test all Richardson–Lucy deconvolutions (including TV regularization).
