An island of receptor-like genes at the Rrs13 locus on barley chromosome 6HS co-locate with three novel sources of scald resistance
Authors: Peter E. Eckstein, Lindsay J. Griffith, Xiang M. Zhang, T. Kelly Turkington, Mark G. Colin, Samuel Holden, Sean Walkowiak, Gurcharn S. Brar, Aaron D. Beattie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Three Hordeum spontaneum-derived resistances (referred to as 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5) have demonstrated long-term effectiveness against barley scald, caused by Rhynchosporium commune, in western Canada. Genetic mapping of these resistances in three populations, and the use of five barley genome assemblies, revealed they co-located to a narrowly defined 0.58–1.2 Mbp region of chromosome 6HS containing the Rrs13 scald resistance gene. Differential disease reactions among the three resistances and a Rrs13 carrier (AB6) to a panel of 24 scald isolates indicated that the four resistances were unique from one another. A marker created to target the 6HS scald locus was screened across a panel of barley germplasm that included H. vulgare, H. spontaneum and H. bulbosum lines. The marker showed specificity to H. vulgare lines known to carry the 6HS scald resistances and to two H. spontaneum lines that trace their origins to Jordan. Within the 0.58–1.2 Mbp region were 2–7 tandemly repeated leucine-rich repeat receptor-like proteins (LRR-RLP) and one lectin receptor-like kinase (Lec-RLK) genes with abundant sequence variation between them. The well-defined role that RLP and RLK genes play in plant defense responses make them logical candidate resistance genes, with one possible hypothesis being that each unique scald resistance may be encoded by a different RLP that interacts with a common RLK. It is suggested the three scald resistances be temporarily named Rrs13145L2, Rrs1341T1 and Rrs1340Y5 to recognize their co-location to the Rrs13 locus until it is determined whether these resistances represent unique genes or alleles of the same gene.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00122-024-04746-2.

Introduction

Scald (caused by Rhynchosporium commune) (formerly R. secalis; Zaffarano et al. 2011) is an important disease of barley (Hordeum vulgare L.) that prefers cool, semi-humid conditions (Wallwork 2000). While epidemics are rare, the disease is persistent and of worldwide concern. Rhynchosporium commune is pathogenic at both the seedling and adult plant stages and can impact both yield and malting quality once infection levels are sufficiently high, particularly on the flag leaf (Brown et al. 1996; Khan and Crosbie 1988). Yield reduction due to scald varies widely. Kavak (2004) observed losses from 3 to 16% in Turkey, Karjalainen and Jokinen (1993) noted losses from 11 to 21% in Finland, while in Australia yield loss as high as 45% has been described (Brown 1985). In western Canada, scald is a high priority disease and is considered during cultivar registration. Losses up to 19% have been documented in Canada (Xi et al. 2000) and scald was the most prevalent and severe leaf spot disease in 2019 with > 70% of all inspected fields infected (Rauhala and Turkington 2020). Malt quality is also adversely affected by scald via lower seed weights and thinner grain, both of which can increase grain protein content and decrease malt extract (McLean and Hollaway 2018; Paulitz and Steffenson 2011).

Management of scald involves an integrated management strategy that uses on-farm management of inoculum, fungicides, growing disease resistant cultivars, or a combination thereof. Initial infections arise from inoculum produced from infected crop residue or seed (Ayesu-Offei and Carter 1971; Reed 1957), and thus practices such as crop rotation, residue management and sowing good quality seed can aid in managing the disease (Mayfield and Clare 1984; Turkington et al. 2011). In western Canada, seed-borne inoculum plays a minor role as a disease source, especially when rotational intervals between crops are more than two years, while infected barley residues are more important (Turkington et al. 2006, 2011). Scald severity is affected by seeding date (Juskiw and Helm 2003) and can be reduced by delayed seeding (Kavak 2004; Murray and Brennan 2010; Xi et al. 2008). Scald can also be managed along with other leaf diseases through foliar fungicide applications (Turkington et al. 2015; Xi et al. 2000), although variable effectiveness of fungicides under different environmental conditions (Kendall et al. 1993) and the development of resistance to commonly applied fungicides (Locke and Phillips 1995) are potential issues. In addition, there are several other disadvantages regarding fungicide use, such as concerns regarding pesticides in the environment, prohibition of pesticides by regulatory bodies (Cope et al. 2021), fuel costs and soil compaction associated with fungicide application. As such, the deployment of cultivars resistant to disease is a more efficient and cost-effective disease management strategy (Turkington et al. 2015; Xi et al. 2000). However, few resistant cultivars are in widespread use in Canada, especially among malting barley cultivars, thus limiting disease management options available for farmers.

Pathogen diversity creates a significant challenge for developing cultivars with durable disease resistance. Rhynchosporium commune is a genetically diverse pathogen (Zhang et al. 2020) with significant gene flow occurring between R. commune populations on a regional scale (Salamanti et al. 2000). Brunner et al. (2007) assessed 161 isolates from across the globe and identified 37 distinct haplotypes, while Ali et al. (1976) assessed 203 isolates from Australia and identified 35 haplotypes. Xi et al. (2002) isolated 52 pathotypes from fields in Alberta and observed wide variation in population diversity and virulence between fields. Zaffarano et al. (2006) outlined how variability in the R. commune population may arise through processes that include mutation and migration (Goodwin et al. 1994) or asexual recombination (Forgan et al. 2007; Newman and Owen 1985; Newton 1989). Interestingly, R. commune has not been confirmed to have a sexual state, but the potential occurrence of a cryptic sexual stage has been reported (Linde et al. 2003; McDonald et al. 1999; Salamati et al. 2000). Recently, Holtz (2020) assessed scald isolates in central Alberta and demonstrated the presence of two mating types, suggesting the potential occurrence of cryptic sexual reproduction; however, a “skewed mating type ratio” was observed which indicated the predominance of asexual reproduction in the R. commune population. Whatever process is occurring, the shifting population structure in a given area allows the pathogen to rapidly circumvent resistance genes, often before they can be deployed in commercially available cultivars. Thus, the identification and characterization of disease resistance genes are important in order for resistance genes to be more efficiently combined and stacked to provide more durable disease resistance in new cultivars.

Ultimately, understanding the genomic location, allelic relationship, and pathogen virulence spectrum for the various scald resistance genes will allow a more effective implementation of gene pyramiding. Pyramiding of several unique resistance loci was proposed for scald in the 1970s (Ali et al. 1976) and continues to gain traction (Wang et al. 2014; Zhang et al. 2020; Hautsalo et al. 2021). Early attempts (Brown et al. 1988, 1996) to pyramid scald resistance genes into a single cultivar showed promise as the resulting lines demonstrated increased resistance in comparison to lines with single genes.

Approximately 50 resistance loci have been discovered on all seven barley chromosomes, with a prevalence to chromosomes 3H and 7H (as comprehensively reviewed by Zhang et al. 2020). Sources of resistance have not only originated from H. vulgare (Looseley et al. 2012; Schweizer et al. 1995), but also from wild relatives such as H. spontaneum and H. bulbosum (Clare et al. 2023). For example, Rrs12 on chromosome 7H (Abbott et al. 1992), Rrs14 on 1H (Garvin et al. 1997) and Rrs15 on 7HL (Genger et al. 2005) all originated from H. spontaneum, while Rrs16Hb on 4HS originated from H. bulbosum (Pickering et al. 2006). Although there are new reports of scald resistance, most recently on chromosomes 2H (Cope et al. 2021), 5H (Büttner et al. 2020; Cope et al. 2021; Coulter et al. 2019; Zantinge et al. 2019) and 6H (Coulter et al. 2019; Ijaz et al. 2024); in some cases, the resistance has been mapped to previously reported loci and it is unclear whether the ‘new’ resistance is an allele of a currently known gene or represents a closely linked gene. However, with the availability of an increasing number of genome assemblies (Jayakodi et al. 2020, 2024; Mascher et al. 2017, 2021) characterization of the various scald loci should be possible and allow a better understanding of the resistance located in these regions. To date, no single resistance locus has been cloned or functionally characterized, although several groups have targeted specific loci (Cope et al. 2021 (5H); Coulter et al. 2019 (Rrs18 on 6HS); Looseley et al. 2020 (Rrs1Rh4 on 3HS); Marzin et al. 2016 (Rrs2 on 7H); Wang et al. 2020 (QSc.VR4 on 4H).

The scald resistance locus on chromosome 6HS, first reported and designated as the Rrs13 locus by Abbott et al. (1995), was based on resistance derived from a H. spontaneum accession collected from Bar Giyyora, Israel, and backcrossed into the Australian cultivar Clipper (designated BC-line 30; Abbott et al. 1991, 1992). This locus is important to western Canadian barley production as it harbors several potentially novel resistance genes (or alleles) which have retained their effectiveness since 2000 (Singh 2003a, 2003b). Like the Rrs13 locus which was introgressed from H. spontaneum, several other sources of scald resistance (provided by Dr. R. Pickering), including 4176n/10/n/3/2/6, 145L2, RP40Y5 and RP41T1, were derived either from H. spontaneum or H. bulbosum. Initial work indicated that the resistance in these lines consisted of major genes located on the short arm of chromosome 6H (R. Pickering, unpublished data). The likelihood that these genes resided at the Rrs13 locus was confirmed over the years as the locus was studied and tagged with molecular markers (Eckstein et al. 2011, 2017).

Due to the importance of the Rrs13 (6HS) scald resistance locus, this study characterized three sources of scald resistance that co-located to this region. The specific objectives of this study were to: 1) determine whether the resistances are unique from each other and the Rrs13 gene using a panel of scald isolates, 2) determine the genetic inheritance of the three sources of resistance, 3) confirm and delineate the 6HS region associated with the resistance loci via QTL analysis, 4) develop markers linked to this locus to aid in breeding for resistance, and 5) identify candidate resistance genes at the locus. We report on the development of near-perfect molecular (SNP) markers for the Rrs13 locus, along with the presence of a cluster of receptor-like (RL) genes within a tightly delineated region on chromosome 6HS.

All sources of resistance were obtained from Dr. R. Pickering (Plant and Food Research, NZ). The resistance in 145L2 (“unknown”/Vada/908–11/907–122/Valetta) was originally believed to originate from H. bulbosum but is now thought to originate from an unknown accession of H. spontaneum (Singh et al. 2003a, 2003b). The pedigrees of RP41T1 and RP40Y5 are unclear, although they are also believed to originate from H. spontaneum introgressions generated by Pickering et al. (1987). The scald susceptible parent CDC Austenson (TR258/94Ab12271) is a feed barley cultivar bred by the Crop Development Centre (University of Saskatchewan, Saskatoon, SK, Canada), while the scald susceptible parents CDC Meredith (SM98427/SM98787) and TR08116 (SM01408/SM00393) are malting barley cultivars or breeding lines developed at the Crop Development Centre. Previous research indicated that the resistance carried by 145L2 (Singh et al. 2003a, 2003b), RP41T1 and RP40Y5 (data not shown) was monogenic, and previous molecular marker analysis (Eckstein et al. 2011; 2017) confirmed that the resistances in all three sources co-located to chromosome 6HS.

The feed cultivar Laurel (Triumph/Patty) was used as the scald resistant check when assessing scald reaction in both the scald field nursery and growth chamber. Laurel is a non-Rrs13 locus carrier with resistance located on chromosome 3H near Rrs1 (unpublished data). The malting cultivars Harrington (Klages/3/Gazelle/Betzes//Centennial) and CDC Copeland (WM861-5/TR118) were used as the scald susceptible checks in the scald field nursery and growth chamber, respectively. The Australian breeding line AB6 (H. spontaneum accession/3*Clipper; provided by Dr. Hugh Wallwork) carries Rrs13 locus scald resistance that was introduced from an unnamed H. spontaneum accession collected from Bar Giyyora, Israel (Abbott et al. 1992). AB6 was used to understand whether the resistance carried in this line was the same or different from the Rrs13 locus resistance carried in the 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5 populations.

A panel of 96 genotypes consisting of barley cultivars and lines carrying scald resistance genes from several genomic locations (including the Rrs13 locus), scald susceptible cultivars and lines, and several H. spontaneum and H. bulbosum accessions was used to assess the specificity of the molecular marker (RhNZ6SNP7) developed to target the Rrs13 locus (Supplementary Table S1).

CDC Copper (TR08116/SB050739), which carries the 145L2-derived resistance from SB050739, was used for sequencing and genome assembly of the chromosome 6HS scald resistance region. CDC Copper is the only malting cultivar registered in Canada to have moderate resistance to scald. Harrington was also sequenced for comparative analysis.

AAC Synergy (TR02267/Newdale), Harrington, Morex and WBDC_237 were used to assess their disease reactions to the three isolates of R. commune utilized to genetically map the three unique scald resistances and for alignment of the chromosome 6HS scald resistance genomic region.

Disease reaction evaluation

Field

All plant materials were evaluated for scald reaction in the field nursery located on the Agriculture and Agri-Food Canada (AAFC)-Lacombe Research and Development Centre (LRDC) station at Lacombe, Alberta, Canada. The 145L2 population was assessed as a single replication in 2010 and as two replications in 2012 and 2014. RP41T1 and RP40Y5 populations were assessed as one replication in 2008 and as two replications in 2012 and 2014. In addition to the three populations, the susceptible check Harrington and the resistant check Laurel were grown in the nurseries with the same amount of replication as was used for each population in each year. Lines were planted as hill plots (8–10 seeds) on 0.5 m centers in a randomized complete block design. Infected straw from the prior season was spread at the late tillering stage (Zadok stage 28). Additionally, fresh inoculum was prepared from 2- to 3-week-old cultures grown on lima bean agar, adjusted to 0.01 g/liter (1 × 105 spores/mL), and misted onto the plants using a compressed air sprayer during the evening when the plants were at the early stem elongation stage (Zadoks stage 30). Both sources of inoculum were mixed endemic, non-characterized pathotypes. Hills were irrigated in the morning and evening (if needed) for five to seven days post-inoculation to ensure disease establishment. All plant materials were evaluated in late July/early August at the late milk-early dough stage (Zadoks stage 80). Ratings were based on a 0–9 scale (Couture 1980; Saari and Prescott 1975), where a rating of 0 indicated no disease in the lower, middle and upper canopies, and 9 indicated > 50% diseased leaf area in the lower, middle and upper canopies. Lines were classified as resistant (ratings of 0–4) or susceptible (ratings of 5–9) for the purpose of Chi-square analyses.

Growth chamber

The resistant and susceptible parents of the three RIL populations, as well as the resistant check Laurel, the Rrs13 carrier AB6 (H. spontaneum CPI71283/Clipper*3) and the susceptible check CDC Copeland (WM861-5/TR118), were screened for reaction to scald under controlled environmental conditions in growth chambers at the University of Saskatchewan with 24 (single-spore) scald isolates (Table 2). All isolates were collected from Lacombe, an area known to harbor a large diversity of virulent pathotypes (Xi et al. 2002). The purpose of the isolates was to help determine if the Rrs13 locus scald resistance carried by the three resistant parents (SB050739, 145L2 and RP40Y5) and AB6 were the same or different from one another (which could be resolved by their disease reactions to the 24 isolates). Six seeds per line and four lines per pot were seeded in one-liter pots containing a soilless medium (Sunshine Mix #3, Sun Gro Horticulture Canada Ltd., Seba Beach, AB). Four replicates of each line were grown in a completely randomized design within the growth chamber with all replicates being conducted simultaneously for all lines. The resistant and susceptible parents of the three RIL populations, along with the lines comprising the RIL populations, the susceptible check CDC Copeland the resistant check Laurel, were also screened for reaction to scald in the same manner as described above except that only a single isolate was used to assess each population. Isolates were selected solely on their ability to produce highly differential reactions against the resistant and susceptible parents in order to create a second set of disease reaction data (under more controlled conditions) that could be compared against the field nursery data. Scald isolates AB15, AB9 and AB13 were used to screen the 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5 populations, respectively. All replicates for each population were conducted simultaneously. In addition, AAC Synergy, Harrington, Morex and WBDC_237 were evaluated for their reaction to the same three isolates.

Single-spore isolates were cultured on lima bean agar 18 days prior to plant inoculation. Lima bean agar was prepared by soaking 62.5 g of lima beans in 1 L of tap water over night. The mixture was then boiled for 45 min, strained through two layers of cheesecloth to remove the lima beans, 15 g of agar added and then the solution was autoclaved before pouring into Petri plates. Five Petri plates were prepared per isolate, plates were maintained at 18 °C, 80% relative humidity and 12-h days. Spores were harvested by applying 15 mL of sterile water to each plate, releasing the spores from the mycelia using a soft toothbrush, and filtering through two layers of cheesecloth. Spore concentrations were determined via hemocytometer and the inoculum adjusted to a concentration of 0.01 g/liter (1 × 105 spores/mL). Barley plants were inoculated at the three-leaf stage (approximately 14 days post-seeding) using small spray bottles to evenly distribute the spore solution at a rate of 10 mL per pot. Plants were placed in a humidified chamber (100% RH) at 18 °C and no light for 48 h, followed by a four-hour period of light and a further 24-h period of darkness. Plants were then returned to their pre-inoculation growth conditions.

Disease severity was assessed 15 days post-inoculation on the second and third leaves of each line, based on a modified Jørgensen and Smedegaard-Petersen (1995) scale. A rating of 0 or 1 was considered resistant with 0 indicating no visible symptoms and a score of 1 indicating that small lesions covered less than 10% of the leaf. Scores of 2 or 3 were considered susceptible, with 2 indicating that larger lesions covered 10–40% of the leaf and a score of 3 indicating that more than 40% of the leaf was covered with lesions, often with the occurrence of partial or total collapse of the leaf. Lines were classified as resistant (ratings of 0–1) or susceptible (ratings of 2–3) for the purpose of Chi-square analyses.

The 24 scald isolates were grouped into pathotypes based on their differential reactions among a set of six barley genotypes that included the resistant parents of the three RIL populations (SB050739, RP41T1, RP40Y5), the resistant check Laurel (non-Rrs13 carrier), the Rrs13 carrier AB6 and the susceptible check CDC Copeland. Isolates were ordered into nine pathotype groups based on virulence against the most (Group A) to least (Group I) number of barley genotypes. An isolate was defined to be virulent on a given genotype if it elicited a susceptible reaction as defined above. Thus, if any two isolates showed the same pattern of virulence/avirulence reactions across the set of six barley genotypes they were placed into the same pathotype group.

Statistical analyses

The effect of line, year, block nested within year and line by year interaction on scald reaction rating was analyzed as a RCBD using a linear mixed effect model in Minitab (v. 21.2; Minitab LLC) for data obtained from the Lacombe field nursery on each of the three RIL populations. Genotype was treated as a fixed effect while year, block and genotype by year were treated as random effects. The effect of line (treated as a fixed effect) on scald reaction rating was analyzed as a CRD using a general linear model for data obtained from the growth chamber on each of the three RIL populations. The assumptions of homogeneous variances and normal distribution of residuals were evaluated using plots of predicted values versus residuals and distribution of residuals, respectively. Best linear unbiased estimators of scald reaction rating for each line were calculated and used for subsequent QTL analyses.

Chi-square analyses were performed on each population for both field and growth chamber data to determine the goodness of fit against the expected segregation ratio for a single gene (1:1 ratio of susceptible:resistant) using a significance level of 0.05.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Total genomic DNA for DArTseq genotyping was purified from fresh leaf tissue grown from four to five seeds using a CTAB extraction method (Procunier et al. 1990) that included a phenol/chloroform purification. DNA concentrations were adjusted 50 ng/uL for DArTseq genotyping. DNA used for the RhNZ6SNP7 TaqMan assay was obtained directly from seed (8 individual seeds per line) using the sodium hydroxide method (Klimyuk et al. 1993) with aliquots of the supernatant used for the assay, rather than the treated leaf tissue as described in the method.

High molecular weight (HMW) genomic DNA for CDC Copper and Harrington were isolated for whole-genome sequencing. Seedlings from each cultivar were raised in nursery pots (17 × 12 × 13 cm) filled with peaty soil in a growth chamber (BioChambers, Saskatchewan, Canada) and grown under 16-h days at 24 °C and 8-h nights at 20 °C until the third leaf stage (about 2–3 weeks old). Seedling leaves were collected at 5–6 h following the resumption of the day cycle and samples were placed in liquid nitrogen immediately after harvesting and ground into fine powder in liquid nitrogen using a ceramic mortar with pestle. The HMW genomic DNA was extracted using the GeneJet™ Plant Genomic DNA Extraction kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The quality and size distribution of DNA was determined on a 1% agarose gel, and DNA concentration was estimated using a spectrophotometer NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific™) and adjusted to 100 ng/µl using TE buffer. DNA from CDC Copper and Harrington were then sheared using g-tube (DMark Biosciences) and size selected for fragments > 15 kbp by BluePippin (Sage Science) using manufacturer recommended protocols. Sequencing libraries were then prepared and sequenced on 8 M SMRTCells on a Sequel IIe instrument (Pacific Biosciences).

Genotyping and QTL analysis

A subset of 94, 94 and 90 lines from the 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5 populations, respectively, were selected for QTL mapping of the scald resistance segregating in each population (Table 1). Lines were selected to represent the full range of scald reactions observed in the field nursery. Lines were genotyped via DArTseq (Diversity Arrays Technology Pty. Ltd., Canberra, Australia). Genotyping data were filtered to remove markers that failed to produce an allele on parental lines, were heterozygous on the parental lines, were monomorphic on the parental lines, displayed > 15% missing data, showed highly skewed segregation (> 65% for a given allele), or failed to be assigned to a linkage group.

In addition, the RhNZ6SNP1 (based on bpb9807) marker that was previously shown to be linked to the Rrs13 locus (Eckstein et al. 2011; 2017) was genotyped on the three populations.

Linkage groups for each population were constructed using MSTmap Online (Wu et al. 2007, 2008). Markers were initially grouped using a LOD score of 10 and then grouped by chromosome, based on the Morex V3 assembly (Mascher et al. 2021), using the physical map available on Barleymap (Cantalapiedra et al. 2015; http://floresta.eead.csic.es/barleymap/). Markers within each chromosome-based group were ordered using the Kosambi mapping method (Kosambi 1944) with the no mapping size threshold set to 1, the no mapping distance threshold set to 2 cM and the no mapping missing threshold set to 10%.

QTL mapping was carried out in MapQTL v.5.0 (Kyazma B.V., Wageningen, Netherlands) under its default settings, except the mapping step size was increased to 50.0. The LOD threshold for each trait was determined by running a permutation test and taking the genome-wide relative cumulative count threshold at 0.95 or higher. Interval mapping was first used to identify potential QTL. The peak markers under the potential QTL were then set as co-factors and the analysis was run using the multiple-QTL model (MQM) mapping. Any loci which subsequently fell below the significance threshold were then considered false positives, removed as co-factors and the analyses repeated. Additionally, co-factors were identified using the co-factors tool and confirmed in the same manner. The resulting linkage maps were visualized using MapChart v.2.3.2 (Voorrips 2002).

Physical positions of QTL markers were determined based on the Morex V3 assembly (Mascher et al. 2021) via Ensembl Barley (Kersey et al. 2014; http://plants.ensembl.org/index.html).

Rrs13 locus marker design and evaluation

DNA sequences (69 bp) and SNP sites within DArTseq markers at or near QTL peaks were obtained from Triticarte. Additional sequence flanking the 69 bp probe sequences (context sequences) was obtained via BLAST (Altschul et al. 1990) to the Morex V3 assembly (Mascher et al. 2021) via Ensembl Barley (Kersey et al. 2014; http://plants.ensembl.org/index.html).

ABI minor groove binder (MGB) TaqMan assays (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, USA) were designed via the Applied Biosystems online design tool, using extended context sequences. Bi-allelic assays utilized FAM and VIC reporter dyes in the presence of ROX to standardize for background. Assays that passed the design criteria were evaluated for polymorphism and segregation in the three populations using the same generation of seed as was used for genotyping. Assays that were not polymorphic or that poorly discerned the alternate alleles were eliminated. Genotyping via the ABI-MGB TaqMan assay was performed under standard conditions recommended by the manufacturer. Reactions were conducted in 10 uL volumes and consisted of Maxima Probe/ROX qPCR Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and MGB assay, both at 1 × concentrations. Template ranged from 10 to 50 ng of purified DNA, or 1 uL aliquots (non-quantified) of template prepared by the sodium hydroxide method. Assay annealing temperatures were standardized at 60 °C (as recommended by the manufacturer). Reactions were cycled on an ABI StepOnePlus platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with standard runs consisting of 40 two-stage cycles (92 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 30 s), following an initial stage of 10 min at 94 °C to activate the heat stable master mix.

Analysis of the 6HS scald locus

Once the 6HS scald locus was defined by QTL mapping in each of the three RIL populations, the physical positions of the DArTseq markers underlying the QTL were determined and ordered, and all genes annotated with high-confidence within the QTL region defined by the markers were identified from the Morex V3 assembly (Mascher et al. 2021). Targeted sequencing of an LRR-RLP annotated to the region (HORVU6Hr1G010730, denoted as RLP#7) from a panel of genotypes (Supplementary Table S1) identified the presence of an 88nt indel present only in resistant germplasm (Supplementary Table S1). Given that Morex is a six-row barley and that two-row and six-row barley fall into distinct genetic pools (Hamblin et al. 2010), the genomic region surrounding the 6HS scald locus was subsequently examined in two-row cultivars for which genome assemblies became available. These included AAC Synergy and Harrington (both carry the RhNZ6SNP7 susceptible allele, 88nt absent, susceptible disease reaction) and CDC Copper (RhNZ6SNP7 resistant allele, 88nt present, resistant disease reaction). In view of the fact that the resistance was assumed to have been introgressed from an accession of H. spontaneum, 37 genome assemblies (made available by Dr. Brian Steffenson, University of Minnesota) which included seven H. spontaneum accessions were BLASTn-searched with sequence of the 88nt insertion, identifying a single accession with the insertion (WBDC_237—RhNZ6SNP7 resistant allele, 88nt present, resistant disease reaction).

Three publicly available and two de novo genome assemblies were used to further characterize the Rrs13 locus. These were publicly available pseudomolecule scale assemblies of Morex V3 (NCBI BioProject PRJEB40589), WBDC_237 V1 (NCBI BioProject PRJEB57567), AAC Synergy V2 (NCBI BioProject PRJNA665698), and newly generated de novo scaffold assemblies of Harrington V1 (NCBI BioProject PRJNA1099674) and CDC Copper V1 (NCBI BioProject PRJNA1099681) from this study. The de novo assemblies of Harrington and CDC Copper were generated by first trimming HiFi PacBio reads for low-quality bases and polyN tails and then assembled using Hifiasm v.0.19.5 (Cheng et al. 2021) with an expected haploid size of 4.5 Gbp and the -S0 flag for producing primary assemblies from inbred homozygous genomes. Summary statistics for the Harrington V1 and CDC Copper V1 genome de novo scaffold assemblies are provided in Supplementary Table S7.

The genes HORVU.MOREX.r3.6HG0547480 and HORVU.MOREX.r3.6HG0547830 were determined to be closest to the telomeric and centromeric, respectively, boundaries of the QTL (as defined by the DArTseq markers underlying the QTL) and were therefore used to delimit the region of interest in the five genome assemblies. To obtain the corresponding sequence in genomes other than Morex and WBDC_237, Mummer v.4.0.0 (Marçais et al. 2018) was used to identify all contigs matching the defined interval in WBDC_237 with a length of > 12 Kbp and subsequently to identify reads in the unassembled hifi data corresponding to certain intervals between contigs.

RNAseq data from all available tissue types derived from Morex were aligned to the candidate regions using Hisat2 v.2.1.0 with default parameters (Kim et al. 2019), converted to sorted bam format using samtools v.1.1.0 (Danecek et al. 2021) and used to aid gene curation.

WBDC_237 and AAC Synergy pseudomolecule regions, as well as Harrington and CDC Copper contigs, were annotated for genes and repetitive elements using Augustus v.3.5.0 in “wheat” mode without training data (Stanke et al. 2008) and RepeatMasker v.4.1.2 using the Ensembl nrTE plants database, respectively (Smit et al. 2015; Contreras-Moreira et al. 2021). Non-repetitive genes were characterized based on NCBI BLASTn web results and in some cases manually curated using RNAseq data or comparisons with other predicted genes of the same class in barley or wheat. Visualization and curation were performed using Geneious (Kearse et al. 2012). At positions 19,940,334 and 20,303,347 in WBDC_237 (each encoding a predicted LRR-RLP gene), no contig from the Harrington assembly matched the WBDC_237 pseudomolecule. In order to ascertain whether this represented a genuine absence of sequence or simply a gap in the assembly due to low read coverage and highly repetitive content, we extracted raw reads matching those positions from the Harrington HiFi data. In both cases, reads closely matching the predicted interval in WBDC_237 were identified, which had been collapsed into the adjacent contig by the hifiasm assembler. This was verified by checking the hifiasm logs and identifying the closest match in that contig, which was extremely similar to the extracted reads, but less so than the absent region in WBDC_237. These reads were treated as additional contigs for the purpose of gene annotation and curation.

LRR-RLP phylogenetic assessment

Curated LRR-RLP predicted coding sequence (CDS) from all genomes was aligned using MUSCLE v.3.8.425 (Edgar 2004) and used to generate a phylogenetic tree with raxml-ng v.1.2.0 in “all” mode using the GTR + G model of nucleotide substitution and 1000 bootstraps (Kozlov et al. 2019). The tree was visualized using IToL and Adobe illustrator.

Reaction of 6HS scald resistance carriers to a panel of scald isolates

Disease reactions obtained for each of the 24 scald isolates showed good levels of infection, with the resistant check line Laurel consistently displaying a strong resistant reaction (ratings of 1.0 or lower), while the susceptible check CDC Copeland showed strong susceptible reactions (predominantly ratings of 3.0) to all isolates except AB22 (Table 3). Differential reaction to the isolates was observed among the three parents harboring scald resistance derived from different H. spontaneum sources, as well as Rrs13 carrier (Table 3). Isolate AB3 showed an avirulent reaction against SB050739 but a virulent reaction against RP41T1, RP40Y5 and the Rrs13 carrier (Table 3). Isolate AB9 had an avirulent reaction against RP41T1 and virulent reactions to the other 6HS resistance carrying lines. There was no isolate that was avirulent against the RP40Y5 resistance while being virulent against the other 6HS resistance sources, but isolate AB17 demonstrated this resistance was different (virulent reaction) from the other 6HS resistance sources (avirulent reactions) (Table 3). By classifying the scald reaction of the six barley lines to the 24 scald isolates as resistant or susceptible, a total of 9 pathotypes could be defined (Table 3). Concordance between field and growth chamber disease ratings for individual lines was excellent with strong agreement between the two datasets with respect to categorizing lines as resistant or susceptible (358 of 383 lines, Supplementary Table S2–S4). QTL mapping and physical location of the three novel 6HS scald resistances

DArTseq datasets were filtered to produce a high-quality set of marker data with a total of 2392, 2222 and 2712 markers from the 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5 populations, respectively, used for linkage map construction. Each population yielded the expected seven linkage groups with markers well distributed across all linkage groups. Summary information for the linkage maps associated with each population is provided in Supplementary Table S5.

QTL analysis revealed a single, well-defined, narrow QTL peak at nearly identical positions on chromosome 6HS in all three populations with scald reaction data from both the field nursery and growth chamber (Figs. 3, 4, 5). The analysis strongly indicates a single major gene is responsible for resistance in all three populations (with no additional QTL peaks located elsewhere in the genome). QTL identified using the Lacombe field data explained over 90% of the phenotypic variation and QTL identified using the growth chamber data explained over 80% of the phenotypic variation in all three populations (Table 5). The 8,626,633|F|0 marker was shown to underlie the QTL peak associated with growth chamber data in both the 145L2 and 40Y5 populations and the field data in the 40Y5 population (Table 5). Chromosome 6HS scald locus marker assay

DArTseq markers underlying, or near, the QTL peaks identified in all three RIL populations were considered for conversion to TaqMan assays amenable to marker-assisted selection. Assay development initially focused on markers that were polymorphic across all three populations. Markers 3,665,087|F|0 and 3,256,705|F|0 met these requirements but did not meet the assay design criteria of the Applied Biosystems online design tool. TaqMan assay development focused next on markers that were polymorphic in two of the three RIL populations. Marker 3,259,122|F|0 was not suitable for use as it produced many false resistant calls, marker 3,398,416|F|0 produced weak amplification curves and excessive failed reactions and marker 3,268,248|F|0 did not meet the design criteria of the Applied Biosystems online design. TaqMan assay design then moved onto markers that were polymorphic in a single RIL population. Marker 3,270,162|F|0 co-segregated with the QTL identified in the RP40Y5 and the resulting RhNZ6SNP3 assay developed from this marker produced clear and accurate results with very few failed reactions that could be used in the 40Y5 population (Table 7). Finally, the 8,626,633|F|0 marker, which was not initially used for mapping because it did not have a map position assigned by Triticarte, was considered for TaqMan assay development. This marker, which mapped under the QTL peaks in the 145L2 and 40Y5 populations and was slightly telomeric of the QTL identified in the 41T1 population, was converted into the RhNZ6SNP7 assay (Table 7). When the RhNZ6SNP7 assay was evaluated against the segregating lines in each of three RIL populations there was very high concordance between the assay and scald ratings (Fig. 7, Supplementary Tables S2-S4). The co-dominant assay also revealed 10, 14 and 19 “heterozygous” marker calls in the 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5 populations, respectively (Supplementary Tables S2-S4). This is not unexpected as there is heterogeneity remaining in populations derived at the F4 generation. As a result, eight individual seeds from each line were evaluated with the RhNZ6SNP7 assay to clarify the genetic composition of the lines. All of the “heterozygous” lines were shown to be a composition of homozygous resistant and homozygous susceptible individuals, thus indicating the line was not “heterozygous” but actually heterogeneous (or segregating) at the 6HS resistance locus. For this to have occurred these lines would have been derived from an F4 plant that was still heterozygous for the 6HS resistance locus, but then had subsequently become fixed in the two or three generations since (since the seed being tested was from F4:7 or F4:8 lines). It was interesting to note that most of these heterogeneous lines were identified in the intermediate scald reaction categories (roughly 3.0–6.0) based on the Lacombe field nursery data. It would be logical that a heterogenous line would receive an intermediate rating since a portion of the plants within the line would receive both lower and higher ratings that would average to an intermediate rating. To understand the specificity of the RhNZ6SNP7 assay to the three novel scald resistances, it was evaluated against a diverse panel of barley germplasm that included lines carrying scald resistance genes mapped to alternate genomic locations (Supplementary Table S1). In addition to 145L2, RP41T1 and RP40Y5, eight other lines carried the resistant allele targeted by the RhNZ6SNP7 assay. These included two Australian genotypes, Tantangara and AB6, the line 4176/10/n/3/2/6 (referred to as 4176n) which was also provided by Dr. Pickering and three Canadian lines, CDC Copper, SB01646 and SB01669. All of these lines are known to be carriers of the Rrs13 locus resistance via their pedigree. Notably, two H. spontaneum accessions (CN48254 and WBDC_237) that have been determined to carry scald resistance (data not shown) also carried the resistant allele.

Analysis of the 6HS scald locus

The Rrs13 locus, as defined by the QTL interval identified in the three RIL populations, exhibits considerable variation between genomes, ranging in length from ~ 0.6 Mbp in Morex, Harrington and AAC Synergy to 1.1 Mbp in CDC Copper and 1.2 Mbp in WBDC_237. The bulk of the physical difference between genotypes comes in the form of a large (0.6 Mbp) insertion composed mainly of repetitive sequence (~ 44% LTRs and ~ 28% other repetitive elements) and containing 1–8 coding genes (indicated as red circles in Fig. 8). Genes outside this region (indicated as green or blue circles in Fig. 8) exhibit a mixture of presence/absence variation, indels and SNPs. Harrington and WBDC_237 both carried a full-length NLR CDS predicted to encode a protein of 1620 amino-acids in length (4863 bp), containing the core NB-ARC and LRR domains, as well as an N-terminal coiled-coil (CC) domain. These proteins differ mainly in the LRR domain with 60 nucleotide and 30 amino acid polymorphisms between them. CDC Copper exhibits an NLR CDS which is identical to that in WBDC_237 except for a single base deletion at position 2125, leading to a frameshift and early stop codon (only the CC and NB-ARC domains are present). Morex contains only the C-terminal 2376 bases of the NLR (i.e., the LRR domain) making it substantially different to the gene encoded by the other three genotypes. This gene was not identified in CDC Copper and no direct orthologues (> 80% homology) could be identified to the predicted sequence in public databases.

A total of seven LRR-RLP genes (designated RLP#1 to RLP#7), which could be categorized into three groups based on their nucleotide sequences, were identified at the Rrs13 locus and retained a conserved order across the five genomes assessed. The number of RLP genes varied from one in AAC Synergy, two in Harrington and Morex, and seven in CDC Copper and WBDC_237 (Fig. 8, Supplementary Table S6). The LRR-RLP gene (HORVU.MOREX.r3.6HG0547600) designated RPL#1 was the only LRR-RLP present in all five haplotypes. The five alleles of this gene formed a dispersed group (Group 1 in Fig. 9) with the allele present in the six-row cultivar Morex being distantly related to the four alleles present in the two-row genotypes, and the two alleles found in WBDC_237 and CDC Copper being identical. The RLP#7 (HORVU.MOREX.r3.6HG0547620) gene was present in four of the five genotypes (only absent in AAC Synergy), again with the alleles in WBDC_237 and CDC Copper sharing the same sequence. These four alleles clustered with two alleles of RLP#6 which was only present in CDC Copper and WBDC_237 (Group 2 in Fig. 9) and was likely derived from the genomic event which produced the large insertion at the Rrs13 locus in these two genotypes. These two RLP#6 alleles were identical to one another and displayed significant divergence from RLP#7 in these two genotypes, with 244 SNPs differentiating the two paralogues in WBDC-237. The remaining four RLPs (RLP#2 to RLP#5; Group 3 in Fig. 9) were present only in WBDC_237 and CDC Copper, and are substantially different to Groups 1 and 2, as well as all publicly available RLP and RLK sequences. Like RLPs#1, #6 and #7, these four genes shared the same sequence between WBDC_237 and CDC copper. In addition, these four RLPs share a high level of sequence identity to one another, but RLP#3 and RLP#5 exhibit an 88 nt indel in the C terminus of the CDS which leads to a slightly early stop codon. The duplication begins at position 2281 and results in a CDS of 2568 bp (856 amino acids). The RLP#2 and RLP#4 genes, which have no duplication, are 2565 bp (855 amino acids) long. In all cases, these RLP genes contain a predicted transmembrane domain, but do not exhibit signatures of a kinase domain, even a non-functional one. PCR amplification of the 88 nt indel indicates that it is only present in germplasm carrying Rrs13 locus scald resistance (Supplementary Table S1).

Phylogenetic RLK sub-family placement is often conducted using the kinase domain, which is more conserved than the ligand-specific LRR domain. In the absence of a kinase domain, simple BLASTp searches using the online NCBI interface identified the closest matches to the Triticum aestivum / Aegilops tauschii RLP EIX1 and the Hordeum vulgare RLP from the GSO1 family (KAE8820581), each with 78–82% homology to the proteins at this locus.

The final immunity-associated gene at the locus was a Lec-RLK located between the RLP#5 and RLP#6 that was present only in WBDC_237 and CDC Copper. The CDS is identical in both genotypes and encodes a 712 amino-acid protein (2139 bp) composed of an extracellular lectin-binding domain, transmembrane domain and a serine–threonine protein kinase domain, which appears to be functional based on the presence of an active site at residues 467–479 (VVHRDIKPSNIML). BLASTp searches identified the closest match as IX.1-like receptor kinases in Triticum aestivum / Aegilops tauschii. PCR amplification of exons 1 and 2 of the Lec-RLK also indicate that this gene is present in germplasm carrying Rrs13 locus scald resistance (Supplementary Table S1).

Discussion

This study evaluated three scald resistances, identified as 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5, that are important to western Canadian barley breeders (and likely to breeders in other barley growing regions where scald is an issue) due to their long-standing effectiveness. These scald resistances, most likely introgressed from H. spontaneum, were determined to be novel sources of resistance that were mapped to the Rrs13 locus on chromosome 6HS and co-located with an island of tandemly repeated receptor-like genes.

To understand if the 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5 resistances were unique resistance alleles/genes in relation to one another and the Rrs13 gene reported by Abbott et al. (1995), disease reaction of barley lines carrying these four resistances were evaluated against a panel of 24 scald isolates. These isolates represent different pathotypes that were collected over a 12-year period from the AAFC-LRDC scald nursery located in Lacombe, AB. Xi et al. (2002) demonstrated that Lacombe, and Alberta in general, hosts a wide pathogenic diversity for Rynchosporium commune with 52 pathotypes identified from a collection of 256 Alberta isolates when screened on a set of 12 barley differentials. Inoculation of the 24 scald isolates against the six barley lines evaluated defined nine different pathotype groups based on their virulence patterns and, more importantly, demonstrated four unique differential reaction patterns against the 145L2, 41T1, 40Y5 and Rrs13 carriers. For example, the 145L2 and RP41T1 resistances were uniquely capable of preventing infection of the AB3 and AB9 isolates, respectively. Similarly, isolates AB10 and AB17 were only capable of infecting the Rrs13 and 40Y5 resistances, respectively. Based on these results, the 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5 resistances appear to be unique from one another and the previously reported Rrs13 resistance (Abbott et al. 1995). This is important information for barley breeders as deployment of these genes/alleles into different cultivars may provide growers the opportunity to rotate cultivars. Because the prevalence of any specific pathotype may increase or decrease over time within the pathogen population in response to selection pressure asserted by specific resistance genes (Cope et al. 2021; Zhan et al. 2007), altering the resistances used over time may limit the expansion of that portion of the pathogen population containing virulence to a given resistance gene. In fact, Turkington et al. (2005) noted that using a rotation involving two barley cultivars reduced scald disease incidence by year three in comparison to using a single cultivar.

It is also noteworthy that the novel 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5 resistances were collectively effective at preventing infection against 20 of the 24 isolates, with only isolates AB1, AB4, AB5 and AB23 demonstrating virulence against all of these sources of resistance. Given that the host cultivars (Table 2) from which the 24 isolates were collected were susceptible to the isolates, one can conclude that the three novel scald resistances reported in this study will complement the scald resistance genes in these host cultivars, and thus extend the effective resistance in barley across a wider spectrum of virulence found in the pathogen population. While the full complement of resistance genes present in the host cultivars is incomplete, it is known that different alleles or genes present at the Rrs1 locus are found in Abyssinian, Altas 46, Hudson, Modoc, Trebi and Turk (Xi et al. 2002; Zhang et al. 2020), Rrs2 is present in Atlas and Atlas 46 (Zhang et al. 2020) and Rrs3 is present in Abyssinian and Kitchen (Bjørnstad et al. 2002). Therefore, the different resistance genes present 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5 should be useful additions to the barley breeding community in its efforts to combat scald.

Analysis of scald reaction segregation data obtained from both the field nursery and growth chamber in the three RIL populations created from crosses involving the 145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5 carrier parents indicated that each resistance segregated as a single gene. Subsequent QTL mapping clearly localized all three resistances to the same narrowly defined region on chromosome 6HS. Although the relatively small population sizes and reduced marker density used in this study could mean that other small-effect QTL may be present, the large degree of variance explained by this locus in both field and growth chamber experiments provides strong evidence that this locus is the main driver of resistance. The QTL for all three resistances were located within a 0.58–1.2 Mbp region bounded by DArTseq markers 3,270,643|F|0, located at 19.41 Mbp, and 4,170,928|F|0, located at 19.99 Mbp (in Morex V3). The Rrs13 resistance locus has been previously mapped to chromosome 6HS and flanked by markers Cxp3 and ABG458 (Abbott et al. 1995) which are physically located on the Morex V3 assembly at positions 14.52 Mbp and approximately 37.81 Mbp, respectively. The QTL identified in this study are located well within the interval defined by Cxp3 and ABG458 that has traditionally defined the Rrs13 locus. The only other major scald resistance locus known to be on the short arm of chromosome 6H is Rrs18, located between 7.89–12.01 Mbp, with a candidate gene (protein kinase superfamily, HORVU6Hr1G005260) at 11.56 Mbp (Coulter et al. 2019). This locus is telomeric of the loci in this study and outside of the interval delineated by Cxp3 and ABG458. This confirms the assertion by Coulter et al. (2019) that the Rrs13 locus is not the same as Rrs18. Several QTL identified by Zantinge et al. (2019) in the cultivar Seebe span an area between 6.1–12.0 Mbp (Zhang et al. 2020) and thus are also clearly not associated with Rrs13.

Once the genomic region harboring the three novel scald resistances was defined, an attempt was made to develop a high-throughput marker that would enable tracking of this region. This would not only be useful for marker-assisted selection in barley breeding programs but would also allow identification of wild germplasm harboring this region that may carry additional novel scald resistance. It was interesting to note that the DArTseq marker (8,626,633|F|0), which was the basis of the RhNZ6SNP7 marker assay, is derived from H. spontaneum according to Triticarte. This lends additional support that the introgressed regions carrying the three novel scald resistances are derived from wild barley. The RhNZ6SNP7 TaqMan assay demonstrated excellent specificity for the locus. It correctly identified the presence of the resistance allele in the three scald donor lines, 145L2, RP41T1 and RP40Y5, and also in Tantangara and AB6 which have been previously reported to carry Rrs13 (Brown et al. 1988). In addition, the assay identified the resistant allele in the scald resistant line 4176n. Although the 4176n line was not part of this study, it also originated from Dr. Pickering’s program (source of the three novel scald resistances used in this study) and was reported to have inherited its scald resistance via introgression from H. spontaneum (Singh et al. 2003a). A RIL population created from the cross SB01669/TR361, where SB01669 carries the resistance from 4176n, was evaluated for scald reaction at the Lacombe scald nursery over three years and the RhNZ6SNP7 marker assay demonstrated perfect concordance between marker allele calls and scald reaction (data not shown), indicating yet another potentially novel source of scald resistance mapping to the same Rrs13 locus on chromosome 6HS. Finally, the identification of RhNZ6SNP7 resistant alleles in CDC Copper, SB01646 and SB01669 can be explained by the fact that they each have 145L2 or 4176n as parents.

Two of the H. spontaneum accessions tested, CN48254 and WBDC_237 (Supplementary Table S1), were also identified as RhNZ6SNP7 resistant allele carriers, once again suggesting the origin of this locus is derived from a H. spontaneum accession. It was interesting to note that both accessions were sourced from Jordan. Clare et al. (2023) identified an abundance of scald resistance genes in wild barley accessions from Turkey, but none of the resistances were mapped to the Rrs13 locus. One Rrs13 resistance was derived from a wild barley collected from Bar Giyyora, Israel (Brown et al. 1988), not far from the Israel-Jordan border. Thus, perhaps scald resistance associated with the Rrs13 locus was localized to wild barley populations in this region of the Fertile Crescent. The RhNZ6SNP7 assay indicated that neither of the two H. bulbosum accessions tested (Supplementary Table S1) carried Rrs13, lending further evidence that H. spontaneum, rather than H. bulbosum, was the original resistance donor in the materials from R. Pickering.

The RhNZ6SNP7 assay has to date been polymorphic in all relevant breeding populations in our program and demonstrates a strong ability to correctly discern resistant from susceptible lines derived from 145L2, 41T1, 40Y5 and 4176n. This indicates it will be a highly useful marker. It was also notable that the cultivars Steptoe and Seebe, both of which harbor scald resistance loci on chromosome 6HS (Coulter et al. 2019; Zantinge et al. 2019), did not amplify the resistant allele which further confirms that the Rrs13 locus is distinct from Rrs18 carried by Steptoe, and the Seebe resistance QTL. The marker assay is based on a single SNP within the CyP450 gene and its specificity suggests that CyP450 is part of the H. spontaneum introgression. A sequence alignment of CyP450 confirms that the CDS is identical in WBDC_237 and CDC Copper, with differing amounts of variation to Morex, Harrington and AAC Synergy.

The genomic region harboring the three novel scald resistances is delineated within a 0.58–1.2 Mbp region bounded by DArTseq markers 3,270,643|F|0, located at 19.41 Mbp, and HORVU.MOREX.r3.6HG0547830, located at 19.99 Mbp (in Morex V3). The total number of genes annotated within this region was 28 but varied from 17–26 depending on the genome assembly. In general, most genes identified within the region were found across multiple genomes, with HORVU.MOREX.r3.6HG0547510 (Myb transcription factor, Gene#4 in Fig. 8) being the only gene unique to one genome, that being Morex. Gene order was preserved across all genomes. Of particular interest to this study were genes which exhibited variation at the Rrs13 locus between scald resistant and susceptible genotypes, as well as genes predicted to have a role in plant immune responses.

A NLR-type resistance gene was identified near the centromeric end of the Rrs13 scald resistance locus. Nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat genes are one of the two major classes of disease resistance genes, along with cell surface receptor genes, which encode proteins involved in intracellular recognition of effector proteins, either directly or indirectly, and produce a powerful and immediate immune reaction (Jones et al. 2016). The NLR within the Rrs13 locus, however, appears to be a pseudogene in both Morex and CDC Copper, due to an early stop codon in CDC Copper and a missing N terminus in Morex resulting in only the LRR domain being present, while a functional (i.e., full length) version appears present in WBDC_237 and Harrington. As such, given that both functional and non-functional alleles of this gene can be found in scald resistant and susceptible genotypes, this eliminated the gene from further consideration as a candidate resistance gene.

However, a single Lec-RLK gene and a cluster of seven predicted LRR-RLP genes were also identified at the Rrs13 locus, all of which fit the criteria of displaying variation that aligned with scald reaction and having roles in plant immunity (as described in the following paragraph). The Lec-RLK identified exclusively in the two scald resistant genotypes (WBDC_237 and CDC Copper) carried an intact and presumably functional kinase domain, and was identical in sequence between the two genotypes. Among the scald susceptible genotypes (Morex, Harrington and AAC Synergy), only two LRR-RLP genes were identified (RLP#1 and RLP#7). In contrast, the two genotypes displaying Rrs13 locus scald resistance not only contained RLP#1 and RLP#7, but also harbored five additional LRR-RLP genes within a substantially enlarged genome region (0.6 Mbp larger in WBDC_237 than in Morex) that lies between RLP#1 and RLP#7. RLP#6 was shown to be more related to RLP#7, though still with a significant number of SNPs differentiating the two genes, while RLPs#2-#5 share substantial sequence with each other, but not with RLP#1 or RLP#7. While all these RLP appear to contain fully intact coding sequences, we were unable to identify transcriptomic resources which allowed us to characterize their expression. In all cases, an LRR domain is present, followed by a transmembrane domain, but no interior kinase domain.

Receptor-like proteins and RLKs form a major class of disease resistance genes. They encode proteins with an extracellular binding domain (including LRR, lectin, lysin, agglutinin and proline-rich repeats), a transmembrane-bound domain for anchorage, and an intracellular domain which defines the protein as either a receptor-like kinase if a functional kinase domain is present, or a receptor-like protein if one is not. Receptor-like proteins and RLKs are often implicated in a plants ability to detect and respond to biotic stresses. The abundance and complexity of RLKs have been reviewed in depth (Liu et al. 2017; Malik et al. 2020; Sun et al. 2020; Tang et al. 2017). In brief, RLK/Pelle genes are abundant in plants and variation in their extracellular structure can not only differentially recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMP) across a wide range of pathogens but also specific races or pathotypes within pathogen species (Holden et al. 2022). Many RLKs require a co-receptor to function, which may be another RLK or an RLP (Jamieson et al. 2018). Interaction between a PAMP and the recognition domain is directed across the cell membrane to the cytoplasmic protein kinase which engages in auto, dual, or reciprocal phosphorylation with the co-receptor, subsequently transducing the signal downstream into a MAPK cascade which induces a wide variety of cellular defense responses appropriate to the PAMP. The leucine-rich repeat RLKs (LRR-RLKs) comprise the largest group of plant RLKs with 19 subfamilies defined based on residue patterns (Liu et al. 2017). Leucine-rich repeat motifs consist of 20–30 amino acid residues and differences in the number of LRR motifs, the composition of the motifs and their arrangement within the LRR-RLK appear to provide the structural framework for differential recognition. In barley, several RLK have been implicated in disease resistance, including one associated with scald resistance (Wang et al. 2020).

In the case of the Rrs13 locus, WBDC_237 and CDC Copper are identical at the nucleotide (and therefore protein) level across the six RL genes (i.e., RLPs#2-#6 and Lec-RLK) identified only in these two genotypes. In contrast, when comparing individual genes from different groups of RLPs at the locus (Fig. 9), there is between 79 and 89% nucleotide identity (68% and 81% amino acid identity). As even a single amino acid change may lead to a change in recognition specificity, or a lack of compatibility with a co-receptor, we therefore cannot narrow down the candidate gene further than these seven LRR-RLPs and single L-RLK.

Given the co-inheritance of the sole Lec-RLK and numerous LRR-RLPs at the Rrs13 locus in the scald resistant genotypes, it is tempting to hypothesize that the Lec-RLK and any one of the LRR-RLP may function as co-receptors (Tang et al. 2017) to provide resistance. Alternatively, the requisite RLK co-receptor may be present elsewhere within the genome, but in either case, the monogenic inheritance pattern in all three populations studied indicates that the RLK co-receptor must be ubiquitous in these lineages. Under this hypothesis, it would follow that different alleles associated with any of the LRR-RLP genes may be responsible for different resistance specificities (e.g., an allele of RLP#2 is responsible for Rrs1341T1, an allele of RLP#3 is responsible for Rrs13145L2 and an allele of RLP#4 is responsible for Rrs1340Y5). It may also be possible that the Rrs13 locus associated with the 40Y5 and 145L2 resistances exhibit totally different structural variation at this locus and resistance is provided by genes for which there is no orthologue in this study. Finally, there is a possibility that the Lec-RLK is the sole determinant of resistance and that the three resistances described in this study, along with the original Rrs13 resistance described by Abbott et al. (1995), exist as an allelic series of this gene, thus rendering attempts to stack these three resistances impossible. Current work focused on sequencing this region from RP41T1, RP40Y5, AB6 and susceptible parents used in this study will complement the 145L2 region already available via the CDC Copper genome and help to characterize the genes associated with each resistance and clarify whether these resistances are unique genes or alleles of the same gene.

The island of LRR-RLP genes identified at the 6HS scald locus is likely to have arisen via tandem duplication events, a phenomenon that has been described in other plant species. For example, the RLK/Pelle family of serine–threonine kinases, which are a major subclass of LRR-RLKs involved in plant defence, have arisen primarily by tandem duplication in rice (Shiu et al. 2004). Such duplication is thought to be a response to selection pressure from pathogens, with the extracellular LRR domain being the focus of diversifying selection which allows for differential recognition of various pathogens and races/pathotypes of pathogens (Afzal et al. 2008). This provides strong reasoning for why the RLP genes are good candidate genes for the resistances described in this study, with the possibility that the three novel resistances, along with the Rrs13 gene, each represent one of the RLP genes identified at the 6HS locus in the various genome assemblies used in this study. The 4176n resistance which was also shown to reside at this locus may represent yet another RL gene as Singh et al. (2003b) provided evidence that the resistances in 145L2 and 4176n were not allelic.

The presence of multiple resistances at a single locus that are targeted against the same pathogen has precedence with the barley genome. In the case of the mildew locus (Mla), a single nucleotide-binding domain leucine-rich repeat-containing receptor (NLR) gene has diversified into 30 different alleles (Maekawa et al. 2018; Seeholzer et al. 2010), each of which recognizes a matching AVRA effector encoded by the barley powdery mildew Blumeria graminis f. sp. hordei pathogen (Lu et al. 2016). However, the arrangement of the tandemly repeated LRR-RLP genes at the 6HS locus may be more analogous to the arrangement of rice resistance genes targeting the rice blast pathogen, Magnaporthe grisea. At least 10, 13 and 14 resistance genes are found clustered on chromosomes 6, 11 and 12, respectively (Wei et al. 2019). Determining if these clustered genes represent distinct genes, alleles or are potentially identical is an ongoing process. For example, work by Meng et al. (2020) demonstrated that the Pita2 resistance gene, located in the chromosome 12 cluster, was identical to Ptr but distinct from Pita. However, what is more relevant is that each rice blast resistance gene, as with the 6HS scald resistance locus, is located within a cluster of nucleotide-binding site (NBS)-LRR genes that display a high degree of sequence similarity. There is also evidence that in some cases these tandemly repeated NBS-LRR genes are associated with different resistance specificities. For example, the Pi2/Pi4 locus on chromosome 6 has been reported to contain seven (cv. Nipponbare) to nine (cv. C101A51) NBS-LRR genes with NBS-LRR4 representing the Pi2 gene (Zhou et al. 2006) and NBS-LRR2 representing the Pi9 gene (Qu et al. 2006). This further supports the possibility that the four unique scald resistance specificities located at the 6HS locus could be traced to four different LRR-RLP genes.

In summary, three novel scald resistances (145L2, 41T1 and 40Y5) derived from H. spontaneum co-located to the Rrs13 locus on chromosome 6HS, and were shown via differential responses to scald isolates to be distinct from one another and from the originally described Rrs13 gene. QTL mapping defined the locus harboring these scald resistances to a 0.58–1.2 Mbp region that contained a cluster of up to seven tandemly repeated LRR-RLP genes (depending on the genome analyzed) with sequence variation between the genes. Due to the well-described role that LRR-RLP genes play in plant defense responses, these genes acting in concert with a RLK (such as the Lec-RLK identified at the 6HS locus) were logical candidate resistance genes and suggested that each unique scald resistance may be encoded by a different LRR-RLP gene, similar to what is observed at the Pi2/Pi4 rice blast resistance locus on chromosome 6 in rice. It is suggested that this resistance locus be referred to as Rrs13145L2, Rrs1341T1 and Rrs1340Y5 in accordance with the naming convention of other scald resistance loci (Pickering et al 2006). Finally, a G/A substitution in HORVU.MOREX.r3.6HG0547640 at position 19,879,858 of chromosome 6HS (the basis of the RhNZ6SNP7 marker) can be used by barley breeders to help integrate these useful sources of scald resistance into breeding lines.
