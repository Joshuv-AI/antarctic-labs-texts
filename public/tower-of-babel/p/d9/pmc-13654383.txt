Sex-Specific Lipid, Apolipoprotein B, and Lipoprotein(a) Responses to Berberine Based on 2 Randomized Placebo-Controlled Trials
Authors: Jie V Zhao, Bing Chen, Yap-Hang Chan, Junmeng Zhang, Yuanqing Xia, Joseph Edgar Blais, Haiyong Chen, Brian Tomlinson, Dennis Kai Ming Ip
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBerberine improves lipid metabolism and may influence sex hormones, raising the possibility of sex-specific treatment responses which remain poorly understood.ObjectivesThe authors aimed to evaluate the sex-specific lipid-modifying effects of berberine.MethodsWe conducted a randomized placebo-controlled trial (NCT06782646) in 100 women aged 20 to 65 years in Hong Kong receiving berberine (500 mg orally twice daily) or placebo for 12 weeks. For sex-specific comparisons, stored samples from a completed randomized trial in 84 men using the same intervention (NCT03770325) were used. The primary outcome was apolipoprotein B (apoB). Other outcomes included total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, triglycerides, lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)), sex hormones, and nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)–based lipid fractions. Biomarkers were measured at baseline and 8- and 12-week postintervention. Linear mixed-effects models were used for repeated measurements. Sex difference was examined using heterogeneity tests.ResultsAt week 12, berberine reduced apoB by −1.18 SD (95% CI: −1.53 to −0.82); reductions were also seen in total cholesterol (−1.15 SD, 95% CI: −1.51 to −0.79), LDL cholesterol (−1.06 SD, 95% CI: −1.44 to −0.69), triglycerides (−0.77 SD, 95% CI: −1.14 to −0.41), and Lp(a) (−0.71 SD, 95% CI: −1.12 to −0.31), with improvements across LDL and very-low-density lipoprotein subclasses. Men showed similar trends, but the magnitude was greater in women for apoB, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and Lp(a). Berberine decreased total testosterone in women (−0.42 SD, 95% CI: −0.82 to −0.03) but increased it in men (0.43 SD, 95% CI: 0.004-0.85).ConclusionsBerberine reduced atherogenic lipids, apoB, and Lp(a), with greater efficacy in women than in men. These findings support berberine as a complementary treatment and underscore the importance of sex-specific treatments.Key words: berberine, sex-specific, apoB, Lp(a), randomized controlled trial

Hyperlipidemia is a major modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease, the leading cause of mortality globally, and a growing public health burden worldwide,1 including in Hong Kong. Statins remain the cornerstone of lipid-lowering therapy, however, statins are not tolerable for everyone because of adverse effects such as myalgia and hyperglycemia. PCSK9 (proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9) inhibitors are a newer class of lipid-lowering drugs, but they are costly and are not widely accessible.2 As such, it is in public interest to find more effective, affordable interventions. Given escalating costs and the long lead time for new drug development, identifying new uses for existing approved drugs (ie, drug repositioning) has become a cost-effective alternative.3 Traditional Chinese medicine, which usually uses natural plant products, has gained global attention as candidates for repurposing. Among these, berberine, an isoquinoline alkaloid abundant in Coptis (huanglian) and Phellodendron (huangbai), has shown particular promise. In the United States and United Kingdom, berberine is available over the counter and is widely used as a nutrient supplement. Compelling evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and meta-analyses indicates that berberine also exerts lipid-lowering effects.4,5 Reflecting this growing evidence base, the International Lipid Expert Panel and the 2019 European Atherosclerosis Society/European Society of Cardiology Guidelines have recommended berberine as an option for patients with statin intolerance.6 Nevertheless, its broader clinical adoption requires more robust evidence of safety and efficacy.

A critical and understudied question is whether berberine exerts sex-specific effects. Sex differences in pharmacokinetics, pharmacodynamics, and metabolic regulation are increasingly recognized in precision medicine, and the U.S. National Institutes of Health is also calling for the consideration of sex differences in clinical trials.7 Much of sex-related variability in drug response arises from differences in the hormonal milieu,8 and accumulating evidence suggests that berberine may modulate sex hormones.9,10

Our preliminary work and published findings also support the possibility of sex-specific effects of berberine. In our completed RCT in men, berberine increased serum total testosterone.11 In contrast, studies in women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) show that berberine reduces testosterone and increases sex hormone–binding globulin (SHBG).10,12 These opposite directions of hormonal response raise the hypothesis that berberine may have sex-specific metabolic effects. Our meta-analysis provided preliminary support: berberine increased high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol in women but showed a tendency to decrease HDL cholesterol in men.5 However, this comparison was limited by differing trial settings and different participant characteristics. A head-to-head evaluation in similar settings and among participants with similar characteristics is needed to generate definitive evidence, which are valuable to inform future guidelines.

To address these gaps, we conducted a randomized placebo-controlled trial of berberine in women and compared the effects to those in our similar trial in men. This design allowed us to compare responses to berberine in men and women under comparable settings. In addition to traditional lipid measures, we also included key (apo)lipoproteins, apolipoprotein B (apoB) and lipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)), given the growing evidence that both are independent causal factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD).13,14 Current guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology,15 American Heart Association,16 and Canadian Cardiovascular Society17 all recommend incorporating Lp(a) into cardiovascular risk assessment. By analyzing previously collected samples from the men's trial alongside samples from the women's trial, we examined whether berberine has sex-specific lipid and (apo)lipoprotein responses.

Methods

Study design

We conducted an RCT in Chinese women in Hong Kong and assessed the effects of berberine on lipids and (apo)lipoproteins in women. We also used the stored samples from our completed RCT in Chinese men, to assess the effects on the same lipids and (apo)lipoproteins. Considering the potential role of sex hormones in berberine's sex-specific effects, we also examined the effects on sex hormones, estrogen (women only), and SHBG and total testosterone in men and women.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The University of Hong Kong/Hospital Authority Hong Kong West Cluster (UW23-548). All participants provided written informed consent. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization guidelines for Good Clinical Practice and followed the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guideline. The study was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06782646).

Participants

We used stored samples from our previous RCT in 84 Chinese men (NCT03770325) and conducted an RCT in women following a similar protocol.

Participants with stored samples in our completed RCT of berberine in men

In our RCT in men,11 completed in November 2020, we enrolled 84 Chinese men aged 20 to 65 years who met criteria for hyperlipidemia, defined as triglycerides >150 mg/dL (1.70 mmol/L), total cholesterol >200 mg/dL (5.16 mmol/L), or low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol >100 mg/dL (2.58 mmol/L).18 Eligible participants needed to be willing to attend follow-up visits and not have received hormone replacement therapies such as testosterone treatment within the previous 12 months. Men currently taking berberine or nutraceuticals containing berberine were excluded, as were those with congenital conditions including familial hypercholesterolemia, active infectious diseases such as seasonal influenza, or any liver or renal dysfunction.

The details of the trial in men have been described in a previous publication.11 Briefly, for the participants satisfying the inclusion criteria, after obtaining their informed consent, each participant was randomly allocated to the berberine group or placebo group. Participants received either berberine (500 mg twice daily) or placebo tablets with the same appearance for 12 weeks. At 8 and 12 weeks after intervention, the participants returned for follow-up, including laboratory tests.

All 84 men (mean age: 46.9 years) attended the baseline assessment, and 80 completed all follow-ups; 4 men (2 per group) withdrew for personal or time-related reasons. Blood samples were assessed for lipid profiles and sex hormones as previously described.11 The rest of the blood samples were stored at −80 °C and were used for the current study.

Participants included in the RCT of berberine in women

We recruited women from our university staff and/or their families, by email and leaflets, as we previously did successfully in our trial in men. We enrolled women between 20 and 65 years of age who were of Chinese ethnicity and met criteria for hyperlipidemia, defined as triglycerides >150 mg/dL (1.70 mmol/L), total cholesterol >200 mg/dL (5.16 mmol/L), or LDL cholesterol >100 mg/dL (2.58 mmol/L). Participants needed to be willing to attend return visits and not have used hormone replacement therapy or any hormone-containing medications, including contraceptives, within the past 12 months. Women taking berberine or nutraceuticals containing berberine were excluded, as were those with congenital diseases such as familial hypercholesterolemia, G6PD deficiency (assessed by rapid diagnostic test), hemolytic anemia, infectious diseases (eg, seasonal influenza), or liver or renal disease. We also excluded women who were pregnant, planning pregnancy within the next 3 months, or currently breastfeeding; pregnancy status was confirmed using ultrasensitive human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) testing.

Sample size consideration

Assuming the effect size of berberine on apoB is ∼0.6 in women, according to 2 previous trials,19,20 at a significance level of 0.05 and 80% power, a sample size of 44 in each arm is needed to detect a difference between berberine and placebo.21 We used apoB for sample size estimation as a conservative choice, given its more limited prior evidence and potentially smaller effect size,5 so that other lipid outcomes were expected to be at least as well powered. Assuming the proportion of noncompliance is 6%, then the sample size in each arm required for randomization is (n = 44/[1 – 0.06]2 = 50).22

Randomization and blinding

Randomization was performed using a computer-generated sequence prepared by a statistician. The statistician generated 100 unique random allocation codes and prepared 100 identical, opaque A5-sized envelopes, each labeled with a unique 3-digit serial number. Corresponding medication packages containing either berberine or placebo were prepared according to these allocation codes.

The allocation sequence was fully concealed from all investigators and participants throughout the duration of the trial. Only the statistician who prepared the allocation sequence had access to the allocation code. All other study personnel, including investigators, research staff, laboratory staff, and data analysts, were masked and unaware of the link between serial numbers and treatment allocation.

Study intervention

As in our previous RCT in men, in this RCT in women, the study drug was berberine (manufactured by Hongyun Pharmaceutical Group Co, Ltd), and the treatment duration was 12 weeks. The placebo was prepared with the same ingredients but without berberine and were identical in appearance and weight. Participants were reminded to take the drugs by text messaging. Participants received either purified berberine (500 mg tablets) taken orally twice a day, as used in the previous RCT in men, or placebo tablets orally twice a day, prepared with the same appearance, for 12 weeks.

Primary outcome: apoB. The trial originally prespecified multiple lipid measures (including LDL cholesterol, total cholesterol, triglycerides, apoB, and Lp(a)) in the registration. As apoB was used for the sample size calculation, it is designated here as the primary outcome, with the others as secondary outcomes.

Secondary outcomes: total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, Lp(a), HDL cholesterol, apoA1, non-HDL cholesterol, estrogen, SHBG, total testosterone, lipid fractions, adverse events, liver function (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and aspartate aminotransferase [AST]), and kidney function (serum creatinine).

Secondary outcomes: total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, Lp(a), HDL cholesterol, apoA1, non-HDL cholesterol, estrogen, SHBG, total testosterone, lipid fractions, adverse events, liver function (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and aspartate aminotransferase [AST]), and kidney function (serum creatinine).

Study procedure

Participants who agreed to take part were first assessed for eligibility at our follow-up site according to the criteria described above, which included lipid measurement using conventional automated laboratory assays. Those who met all requirements were scheduled for a second visit. At this visit, written informed consent was obtained, and participants were provided with a copy of the consent form together with contact information for the study team. Afterward, the participants were randomized. Participants then completed a questionnaire including sociodemographic information, smoking status, alcohol use, physical activity, and use of lipid-lowering therapies (such as statins). Approximately 20 mL of venous blood was collected using multiple vacutainer tubes.

Participants were scheduled for a third visit at week 8 for interim efficacy and safety monitoring, and a fourth visit at week 12 at the end of the intervention. At both visits, the same blood tests were repeated using identical procedures. Each month during the intervention and 4 weeks after completion of intervention, participants were contacted by phone to check their well-being and ask if they had any adverse events. We also used the gastrointestinal symptom rating scale (GSRS) to evaluate gastrointestinal symptoms, a well-established scale which has been used for years,23 with the Chinese version having been used in the Chinese population.24 The GSRS contains 15 questions covering 5 symptom-clusters: constipation, dyspepsia, diarrhea, abdominal pain, and reflux. Participants were asked to rate the frequency and severity of each symptom experienced in the past month using a 1 to 4 Likert scale. The total GSRS score ranges from 15 to 60. Liver and renal function were monitored through routine biomarkers, including ALT, AST, and creatinine. Participants were informed of abnormal results.

LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol, triglycerides, total cholesterol, apoA1, apoB, and lipid fractions were measured using the Nightingale Metabolomics platform. LDL cholesterol was the clinical LDL measure from the Nightingale platform, calculated using the Friedewald equation25; this approach provides measures of lipids highly correlated with the concentrations obtained from routine clinical chemistry.25 Lipoprotein(a) mass (mg/dL) was measured using an immunoturbidimetric assay. Estrogen was measured by competitive immunoassay with chemiluminometric technology, SHBG by solid-phase, 2-site chemiluminescent immunometric assay, and total testosterone by competitive immunoassay using direct chemiluminescent technology. The same lipid measurements were performed in men using the same platform and assay method; sex hormones were not reassessed because they had been measured during the men's trial.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize continuous data, and counts and proportions were used for categorical data. Treatment effects were estimated within each trial using a difference-in-differences approach by comparing changes between the berberine and placebo groups separately in men and women. For both primary and secondary continuous outcomes, changes from baseline to 8 weeks and 12 weeks were calculated as the difference between the postintervention value and the baseline value. For ease of comparison between men and women, the changes in all continuous outcomes were standardized. These changes were compared between the berberine and placebo groups using a linear mixed-effects model, which accounts for repeated measures over time. Analyses were not conducted under a strict intention-to-treat principle. Participants who discontinued before follow-up contributed no postbaseline outcome data and were therefore excluded from the primary analysis, so it was based on complete cases (baseline, 8-week, and 12-week measurements). Treatment group, measurement timepoint, and interaction between treatment group and measurement timepoint were independent variables. Participants were included as a random effect. To address multiple testing, we applied false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 separately for primary outcome, secondary outcomes, and lipid fractions. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding outliers, identified as the change beyond 98th percentile. For sex hormones, we also conducted analyses by menopause status; those who had not experienced a period for over 12 months were categorized as postmenopausal.26 To assess the robustness of the results to missing data, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using a conservative approach, in which participants without follow-up data were assigned no change from baseline (ie, baseline observation carried forward)27 and repeated the analysis.

To examine the sex differences, we first estimated treatment effects within each trial separately, and then conducted meta-analysis to examine whether treatment effects differed between men and women. Sex differences were assessed by contrasting these within-trial treatment effects rather than by directly comparing absolute outcomes across trials. For comparisons involving 2 groups, the test for between-study heterogeneity is mathematically equivalent to a fixed-effect meta-regression model with a single binary moderator (sex).28 Specifically, study-specific treatment effects (TE) and their corresponding standard errors (seTE) from the 2 trials (men and women) were combined within a meta-analytic framework using an inverse-variance weighted random-effects model. The random-effects model assumes that true treatment effects may differ across studies29; the between-study variance was estimated using the DerSimonian-Laird method.

Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using the I2 statistic, representing the proportion of variability attributable to differences between studies rather than sampling error. I2 statistics of 25%, 50%, and 75% are typically interpreted as low, moderate, and high levels of heterogeneity, respectively.30 The analyses were performed using the metagen function in the “meta” package in R (version 4.0.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Effects of berberine vs placebo in women

In the trial in women, 179 women showed interest in the trial and were assessed for eligibility. Of these, we included 100 eligible women, with 50 randomized to the berberine group and 50 in the placebo group (Figure 1). The mean screening total cholesterol was 5.6 mmol/L, LDL cholesterol was 3.6 mmol/L, triglycerides was 1.1 mmol/L. A total of 8 women in the berberine group and 2 women in the placebo group withdrew because of gastrointestinal symptoms and lack of time (Figure 1); baseline characteristics were similar among those who completed versus those who withdrew from the study (Supplementary Table 1). A total of 42 women in berberine group and 48 in placebo group were included for analysis. Most participants (97%) did not take lipid lowering drugs at baseline. Baseline characteristics were similar in the 2 groups (Table 1).

After the 12-week intervention, women receiving berberine showed greater reduction in the primary outcome, apoB, compared with those receiving placebo (Figure 2). Specifically, berberine reduced apoB by −1.18 SD (95% CI: −1.53 to −0.82), corresponding to an absolute change of −0.13 g/L (95% CI: −0.16 to −0.09). Berberine also reduced other atherogenic lipid measures, including total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and Lp(a) (Figure 2). These effects remained statistically significant after correction for multiple testing (Supplemental Table 2). Similar patterns of lipid reductions were evident after 8 weeks of intervention (Figure 2). Although the reductions appeared larger at 12 weeks, the confidence intervals largely overlapped.

For other secondary outcomes, after 12-week intervention, berberine reduced non-HDL cholesterol, ApoA1, and testosterone in women (Supplemental Figure 1 and Supplemental Table 2). Berberine did not alter estrogen or SHBG levels in women overall. However, in analyses stratified by menopausal status, berberine increased SHBG among postmenopausal women (Supplemental Table 3). At 8 weeks, berberine similarly reduced non-HDL cholesterol and ApoA1, whereas the decrease in testosterone did not reach statistical significance (Supplemental Figure 1).

After 12-week intervention, berberine also reduced a broad spectrum of lipoprotein cholesterol, particle concentration, and triglyceride subclasses in women. These included decreases in small HDL cholesterol across LDL, intermediate-density lipoprotein (IDL), and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) cholesterol fractions (from small to extremely large particles); LDL, IDL, and VLDL particle concentrations; and triglycerides within HDL, LDL, and VLDL subclasses (Supplemental Figure 2 and Supplemental Table 4). Comparable patterns were observed at the 8-week time point (Supplemental Figure 2).

In sensitivity analyses, after removing outliers (ie, 4 observations of changes at 8-week or 12-week postintervention with values beyond the 98th percentile), similar findings were observed (Supplemental Figures 3-8). In the women's trial, dropout was higher in the berberine group (8 of 50, 16%) than in the placebo group (2 of 50, 4%); over half (5 of 8, 62.5%) of dropouts in the berberine group were because of side effects. In contrast, dropout in the men's trial was low and balanced between groups (2 of 42, 4.8% in each group). Results from sensitivity analysis using baseline observation carried forward in women were broadly consistent with the primary analysis (Supplemental Figure 9). Effect estimates were slightly attenuated, as expected under this conservative assumption, but the direction and statistical significance of the main effects, as well as the observed sex differences, remained unchanged.

No serious adverse events occurred. ALT and AST remained stable at both 8 and 12 weeks. Berberine also lowered serum creatinine after 8-week and 12-week treatment (Table 2). Mild gastrointestinal symptoms were reported in both groups, and the overall GSRS scores were comparable between groups (mean ± SD: 30.2 ± 8.7 among 20 people in the berberine group vs 26.0 ± 6.3 among 8 people in the placebo group).

Effects of berberine in women vs men

Women and men had similar mean age and a comparable distribution of education levels, whereas more men than women were smokers and alcohol drinkers. Men also had slightly higher lipid levels at baseline than women (Supplemental Table 5).

After the 12-week intervention, berberine reduced apoB in both men and women, although the magnitude of change was smaller in men (Figure 3). Similar patterns were observed for total cholesterol and triglycerides. Berberine also reduced LDL cholesterol and Lp(a) in women but not in men. Substantial heterogeneity by sex was observed for apoB, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, and Lp(a) (I2 = 85.1% for apoB, 83.7% for total cholesterol, 85.2% for LDL cholesterol, 83.3% for Lp(a)).

Discussion

Our study (Central Illustration) extends the current evidence by providing a comprehensive evaluation of sex-specific responses to berberine across traditional lipids, (apo)lipoproteins, and lipoprotein subclasses. Berberine reduced apoB, the primary outcome, with consistent improvements in other lipids and lipoproteins, including Lp(a), supporting its potential cardiovascular benefit given the well-established causal roles of these lipoproteins in ASCVD. We also found a clear sex difference; the lipid-lowering effects were stronger in women than in men across both conventional biomarkers and lipid fractions. We also observed opposing effects on testosterone, with berberine decreasing testosterone in women but increasing testosterone in men. By characterizing subclass-specific lipid responses, our study adds novel evidence that the metabolic effects of berberine are not uniform across sexes and highlights the importance of considering sex in the use of berberine.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare the sex-specific effects of berberine within the same overall research framework. Our previous trial in men remains the only RCT of berberine conducted exclusively in men,11 and the present trial is the first to evaluate berberine in a general population sample of women. Prior studies in women have been limited to 2 trials in participants with PCOS,10,12 a population characterized by marked hormonal disturbances. Findings from those studies therefore may not be generalizable to the broader female population. Our results align with meta-analyses showing lipid-lowering effects of berberine4,5 and with earlier reports that berberine reduces testosterone in women.10,12 However, unlike previous studies in women with PCOS,10,12 we did not observe a significant overall increase in SHBG; instead, this effect was only shown in postmenopausal women. These differences likely reflect the distinct hormonal profiles of the study populations: berberine's effects on sex hormones may be more pronounced in people with underlying hormonal dysregulation, compared with the general population.

Notably, we found sex differences in the lipid-modifying effects of berberine. Women showed larger reductions in apoB, and consistently in total and LDL cholesterol, Lp(a), non-HDL cholesterol, and several lipid subclasses, with heterogeneity tests suggesting stronger effects compared with men. Several mechanisms may underlie these differences. The sex difference may be due to the difference in the pharmacokinetics of berberine between men and women. Despite berberine's low oral bioavailability, women had much higher circulating levels of berberine than men, demonstrated by a 2.8-fold higher area under the curve and a 3.6-fold higher Cmax,31 possibly because of the sex-specific effect of CYP2D6,31 the metabolic enzyme for berberine. Beyond potential pharmacokinetic differences between sexes, variation in downstream biological pathways may also contribute to the magnitude of the observed sex-specific effects. One plausible mechanism involves AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a key regulator of lipid metabolism. Berberine is a potent activator of AMPK,32,33 which plays a key role in regulating lipid metabolism.34 Importantly, AMPK is also modulated by estrogen.35,36 Estrogen has been shown to activate AMPK in skeletal muscle,37 where it stimulates fatty acid oxidation.38 Thus, the female hormonal environment may exhibit a synergistic response to berberine's AMPK activation, leading to more pronounced lipid-modifying effects. Nonetheless, the specific pathways are not clear and warrant further mechanistic investigation.

We observed different hormonal responses to berberine in women by menopausal status, with modest reductions in testosterone in premenopausal women and increases in SHBG in postmenopausal women. This pattern may be explained by differences in the underlying hormonal milieu. In premenopausal women, ovarian androgen production is relatively higher,39 so testosterone remains relatively dynamic and amenable to modulation. In contrast, in postmenopausal women, endogenous androgen production is substantially lower,39 limiting the scope for further reductions in total testosterone; then regulation of androgen bioavailability, rather than absolute androgen concentration, becomes more relevant. SHBG therefore plays a more prominent role in metabolic regulation than total testosterone.40 However, these findings are from stratified sensitivity analyses and should be interpreted cautiously.

Study strengths and limitations

It is one of the largest RCTs to date evaluating berberine's effects on detailed lipid phenotypes, and the only trial, to our knowledge, to comprehensively profile lipoprotein subclasses using nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy and to assess apoB and Lp(a) in a sex-stratified way. However, several limitations merit consideration. First, the study was not originally designed to detect effects across all lipoprotein subclasses, so these exploratory findings on lipid fractions should be hypothesis generating. Second, the trial was conducted in an ethnically Chinese population, which may limit generalizability to other ethnicities. Multiethnic trials are warranted to confirm the generalizability of these findings. Third, while smoking and alcohol-drinking behaviors differed between men and women at baseline, given the study design is RCT, these lifestyle factors are not expected to confound treatment effects. Moreover, the sex differences remained after adjustment for these variables (results not shown). Fourth, sex-specific comparisons in this study were derived from 2 trials conducted at different time periods, and potential differences in recruitment time, seasonal variation, and sample handling, etc, may affect absolute study outcomes. However, sex differences were assessed from contrasts of within-trial treatment effects (based on changes), rather than by directly comparing absolute outcomes across trials. In addition, samples from both trials were processed, stored, and assayed in the same laboratories using identical protocols, minimizing potential laboratory-related variation. Fifth, Lp(a) was measured using different sample types in men and women, which precluded direct baseline comparisons and may affect the precision of cross-sex comparisons. However, the within-sex change and treatment effect estimates should not be influenced because sample type was consistent over time within each sex. Sixth, in the women's trial, higher dropout in the berberine group suggests that the missing-completely-at-random assumption may not be fully satisfied, whereas dropout in men's trial was low and balanced between groups. Although female participants who withdrew had baseline characteristics similar to those who completed the intervention (Supplementary Table 1), it is possible that participants who discontinued because of gastrointestinal intolerance had poorer tolerability and therefore less efficacy. However, sensitivity analyses using a conservative approach yielded results consistent with the main analyses, supporting the robustness of the findings. Seventh, as the study was not designed to assess pharmacokinetics, plasma berberine concentrations were not measured, and thus we could not directly evaluate whether the observed sex differences were attributable to sex-specific pharmacokinetic effects. Eighth, although a statistically significant difference in treatment effects between women and men was observed, the underlying trials were not explicitly designed with a prespecified hypothesis or power calculation to formally test whether the magnitude of treatment effects differed between sexes, so replication in future large-scale studies is worthwhile. Finally, long-term cardiovascular outcomes were not assessed, and it remains unknown whether the observed lipid improvements translate into reduced cardiovascular events. However, the favorable changes in atherogenic lipids and (apo)lipoproteins, all of which are causally related to ASCVD, are promising. It would be worthwhile to conduct a sex-specific trial with cardiovascular events as the primary outcome.

Our findings have several important implications for clinical practice. The broad lipid-lowering effects of berberine, particularly in women, suggest that it may be considered as a useful adjunctive treatment for people with mild to moderate hyperlipidemia, especially those who are statin intolerant or prefer nonpharmacologic options. The reductions in apoB, Lp(a), and multiple atherogenic VLDL and LDL subclasses highlight berberine's potential utility in addressing residual cardiovascular risk not fully captured by traditional lipid panels. The favorable safety profile observed in this study further supports its potential role as a complementary intervention for patients who prefer a nonprescription treatment or those with statin intolerance. Importantly, the pronounced sex differences indicate that women may receive greater benefit from berberine compared with men. It is important to incorporate sex-specific considerations into lipid management strategies, with direct relevance to personalized and sex-specific pharmacotherapy.

Conclusions

In this study, berberine reduced multiple atherogenic lipids, such as LDL cholesterol, apoB, and Lp(a) in women, and showed distinct sex-specific patterns in both lipid responses and sex hormones. Beyond traditional lipid measures, berberine improved a broad spectrum of NMR-based atherogenic lipid subclasses. These findings position berberine as a potentially valuable adjunctive therapy for hyperlipidemia and underscore the need to integrate sex differences into cardiometabolic research and clinical decision-making.
