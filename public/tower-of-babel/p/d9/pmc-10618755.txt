Illuminating (HTLV-1)-induced adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma transcriptomic signature: A systems virology approach
Authors: Mohammad-Moien Forghani-Ramandi, Behnam Mostafavi, Atefeh Bahavar, Maryam Dehghankar, Zeinab Siami, Sayed-Hamidreza Mozhgani
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Bioinformatics analysis pipelines were implemented to investigate ATLL pathogenesis benefiting from six separate datasets.•Pathways related to the regulation of immune responses, T-cell receptor and activation, regulation of cell signaling receptors and messengers, wnt signaling pathway, and apoptosis were highlighted as potentially key players in ATLL.•MAPK3, PIK3CD, KRAS, NFKB1, TNF, PLCB3, PLCB2, PLCB1, MAPK11, JUN, ITPR1, ADCY1, GNAQ, ADCY3, ADCY4, CHEK1, CCND1, SOS2, BAX, FOS and GNA12 were identified as possible biomarkers.•Upregulation of ADCY1 and ADCY3 were confirmed by qRT-PCR.Keywords: Adult T-cell lymphoma/leukemia, HTLV-1, Network analysis, WGCNA, Bioinformatics, RT-qPCR

Introduction

Adult T-cell leukemia/lymphoma (ATLL) is a poor prognosis malignancy of peripheral T-cells and is caused by Human T-cell leukemia virus type 1 (HTLV-1) (Ishitsuka and Tamura, 2014; Mozhgani et al., 2022). HTLV-1 is the first-discovered human retrovirus (Tagaya and Gallo, 2017). Considering the ability to replicate and survive as two key features recommended for natural selection, the virus has evolved to induce proliferation, inhibit apoptosis, and protect the infected cells from the immune system (Toyoda and Matsuoka, 2022). To do so, it alters the host's gene expression patterns, signaling pathways, and normal epigenetic regulations. In 3–5 % of carriers and over time, colonies of immortal and highly proliferative cells evolved from the accumulation of these abnormalities can result in ATLL (Yamagishi et al., 2022). The mechanisms by which HTLV-1 can cause an aggressive and lethal malignancy have yet to be fully discovered. One strategy to better understand the disease is to investigate molecular-level abnormalities through microarray analysis.

Although new developments in microarray analysis have enabled scientists to measure the expression profiles of tens of thousands of genes simultaneously, the enormous size of the collected data made the biological interpretations far more complex. Many bioinformatics analysis approaches had been developed to address this issue, one of which is called weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008). As the name suggests, WGCNA is used to detect groups of genes (modules) with highly correlated patterns of expression throughout the samples and to relate these modules to external sample traits. In the pathogenesis of disorders like malignancies many genes and signaling pathways are affected and this approach has the potential to evaluate genes as groups and uncover hidden relations between genes, which can help us better understand the mechanisms of disease formation. The downside of WGCNA is that its performance relies heavily on the input data. To optimize WGCNA performance, we decided to use an approach for initial gene selection with a more biological point of view.

Herein we aimed to identify genes with abnormal expression patterns responsible for the pathogenesis of ATLL, using WGCNA and also to test our hypothesis using module preservation analysis. In addition, to validate the results, qRT-PCR was performed on genes found as novel potential treatment targets and biomarkers.

Material and methods

Workflow

The workflow of this study is presented in Fig. 1 (Including literature information in Fig). In this study, we went through two phases of bioinformatics analysis. In the first phase, after preprocessing steps, we identified the genes and signaling pathways affected by ATLL using Differentially Expressed Gene (DEG) identification, protein-protein interaction network analysis, and enrichment analysis. Then, based on the results of the previous phase and the available knowledge on ATLL pathogenesis in literature, we selected a set of potentially essential genes for deeper investigations using Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA). Finally, we used Quantitative Real-time PCR to validate our results.

The overview of the methodology.

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) online database was searched for high-throughput gene expression datasets of ATLL patients (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE33615 (based on the GPL4133 platform) was downloaded for the primary DEG analysis and the subsequent WGCNA, and GSE1466 (GPL96 and GPL97), GSE14317 (GPL571), GSE17718 (GPL570), GSE43017 (GPL570), and GSE55851 (GPL10332) were collected for module preservation analysis. Then, preprocessing and Quality Control (QC) pipelines were implemented to evaluate the quality of the chosen dataset. In this regard, Principal Component Analysis (PCA), correlation among samples, and K-means clustering (using the "factoextra" R package) were implemented. Next, to remove the effect of the moderator and intervening parameters, the R function “normalize.quantiles” of package “preprocessCore” was implemented for data normalization.

Differentially expressed genes identification

The “limma” R package was used to screen DEG. Genes with an absolute value of log2 (fold-change) > 1 and Adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered as DEGs. Afterward, the R package “pheatmap” was used to generate a heatmap plot of the first 100 genes with the lower variance of expression between acute ATLL and healthy control samples.

Construction of PPIN, network and enrichment analysis

STRING (https://string-db.org/) database was used to construct the Protein-Protein Interaction Network (PPIN) of previously identified DEGs. Cytoscape software was implemented for network analysis and to construct subnetworks of hub genes based on network centrality measures (degree, betweenness, and closeness). Subsequently, functional and pathway enrichment analysis was conducted on hub genes using the enrichr online tool (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/).

WGCNA and module selection

The R package “WGCNA” was applied to detect gene modules related to the ATLL (Langfelder and Horvath, 2008). In order to select the primary gene set for further analysis, genes of predefined signaling pathways were selected for WGCNA. The pathways were selected based on the results of our enrichment analysis plus the current knowledge about ATLL pathogenesis. To calculate the network adjacency matrix, we used the unsigned formula for correlation coefficient calculation. To obtain a scale-free topology, the soft-thresholding was implemented, and with the use of the R function “picksoftthreshold” of the WGCNA package, the optimal β was identified. Subsequently, the topological overlap matrix (TOM) was derived from the transformation of the adjacency matrix. Finally, the module detection was conducted based on the TOM dissimilarity measure using the hierarchical clustering with parameter method = "average" and the subsequent “cutreeDynamic” function with the parameters of minClusterSize = 15 and deepSplit = 2.

The R function “moduleEigengenes” of the WGCNA package was used to construct module eigengenes of each module. Gene Significancy (GS), defined as the correlation between gene expression and the clinical trait data (disease status; either of ATLL or healthy control) and module-trait correlation or correlation between eigengenes and clinical trait data were computed to identify biologically essential modules. Module Membership (MM), defined as the correlation between each gene in the dataset and module eigengenes, was calculated to detect “hub” module genes. Subsequently, scatterplots between each module's MM and GS values were plotted.

Module preservation analysis and Gene Set Net Correlations Analysis (GSNCA)

To validate the identified modules, the “modulePreservation” function of the WGCNA R package was applied to calculate medianRank and Zsummary scores for each module in the external datasets mentioned above (Langfelder et al., 2011). Modules with a Zsummary > 2 and a medianRank < 12 were considered to be the preserved modules.

To identify whether the modules are differentially co-expressed between ATLL and healthy control samples, GSNCA was performed using the “GSAR” R package. In this study, genes with an MM > 0.6 in each module were selected as the gene sets for further gene set net correlations analysis.

Pathway enrichment analysis of key module

The correlation value of modules with disease status and module preservation results were considered to identify the key ATLL-related module. Then, the identified module was regarded for further GO terms and KEGG pathways enrichment analysis. First, we constructed the Protein-Protein Interaction Network of the selected module's genes via the STRING database. Then, the acquired network was subjected to subnetwork clustering to identify highly functionally-specific submodules. The Community cluster (GLay) method in Cytoscape, which uses a variety of clustering pipelines (Su et al., 2010) was used to identify subnetworks. Finally, identified clusters have been subjected to further GO and KEGG pathways enrichment analysis.

Genes dysregulation evaluation and meta-analysis

For the purpose of evaluating the dysregulation of the selected module's genes, the “MetaVolcanoR” R package (Prada et al., 2022) was implemented to obtain a combined expression value of these genes by integrating their expression data in GSE33615 and the five previously mentioned external datasets.

Sample preparation

Peripheral blood smears were collected from ten ATLL patients (admitted to the Shariati Hospital, Tehran, Iran) and ten samples from normal blood donors. A Ficoll-Paque density gradient (Cederlane corporation, Canada) was implemented to isolate peripheral blood mononucleolar cells (PBMCs). RNA ExtractionRNja PB Kit (ROJE, Iran) and the RT-ROSET kit (ROJE, Iran) were used to extract total RNA from PBMCs and the cDNA synthesis, respectively. The Ethical Committee of Biomedical Research at Alborz University of Medical Sciences has approved this study (IR.ABZUMS.REC.1400.280).

Quantitative real-time PCR

To perform the qRT-PCR, primers of desired viral and human genes were designed (Table 1). PCR evaluation of LTR and HBZ genes’ expressions and subsequent agarose gel electrophoresis were implemented to confirm the infection of ATLL patients with HTLV-1 (Mozhgani et al., 2022; Rafatpanah et al., 2011). SYBR Green-based RT-qPCR (TaKaRa, Otsu, Japan) was used to perform quantitative real-time PCR on cDNAs based on specific primers. The Rotor-Gene Q-6000 machine (Qiagen, Germany) was utilized to measure the expression rate of ADCY1, ADCY3, and ADCY4 genes based on instructions provided by the manufacturer. The expression values of ADCY1, ADCY3, ADCY4, and RPLP0 (reference gene) were analyzed based on the 5-point standard curves. The formula of: copy number of the gene of interest/copy number of the reference gene, was used to calculate the normalized gene expression values.

Lastly, to select a positive control gene previously known to be dysregulated in ATLL, regarding the available studies on the relation between MAP2K1 expression and ATLL (Hosseini et al., 2023; Kozakai et al., 2018; Song et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2021), this gene was considered for this purpose.

Statistical analysis

To compare gene expression values among the ATLL and control groups, the Mann-Whitney U Test was performed using GraphPad Prism software v8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) was chosen to display the data. The gene expression results with a p-value < 0.05 were considered to be significantly dysregulated.

Results

Data acquisition and preprocessing

By investigating online databases, we collected six different microarray expression datasets (one for the primary analysis and five for the validation stage). The primary dataset (GSE33615) contains 73 samples (26 acute ATLL, 20 chronic ATLL, one lymphoma, four smoldering, one unknown subtype, and 22 healthy controls) of total RNA samples from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), mostly CD4+ T-cells. For the QC step, we first evaluated sample discriminability among groups. To this purpose, we implemented the K-means clustering algorithm on the first two Principal Components of the datasets which is illustrated in Fig. 2a. This figure shows two distinct clusters meaning that the ATLL group is entirely distinguishable from the Control group. Furthermore, we considered a high inner-group correlation criterion to be passed which is shown in Fig. 2b, which means each sample in the group treats as similar to each other. In addition, to remove the effect of the moderator and intervening parameters, we normalized the dataset which is shown in Fig. 2c.

DEG identification, network analysis, and enrichment analysis

Based on the DEG analysis results, 2044 up-regulated and 1913 down-regulated genes were detected. BHLHE22 (logFC= 7.15, adjusted P = 3.30e-17) followed by HBA2 (logFC= 6.72, adjusted P = 2.34e-13) and CADM1 (logFC= 6.59, adjusted P = 1.70e-21) were the first three up-regulated genes and PMEPA1 (logFC= −6.99, adjusted P = 4.12e-18), GREM2 (logFC= −5.50, adjusted P = 1.70e-21) and ZSCAN18 (logFC= −5.17, adjusted P = 5.13e-19) were down-regulated genes with lowest logFC values. The Volcano plot, PPIN, heatmap plot of the first 100 genes with lower variance of expression, and primary enrichment results are presented in Fig. 3.

WGCNA

According to the results of our previous enrichment analysis, in addition to the literature review of the publications on the molecular pathogenesis of ATLL (Yamagishi et al., 2022; Machado et al., 2022; Sakihama and Karube, 2022; Ernzen and Panfil, 2022), from 13 signaling pathways (Table 2), a total of 1134 (1097 genes were available in GSE33615) unique genes were selected for network construction. Our sample preprocessing indicated no outlier in samples (Fig 4a). We identified β = 6 as the optimal exponent value to obtain a scale-free co-expression network, as the results are illustrated in Fig 4b and c. After the initial hierarchical clustering and the subsequent module merging, a total of 12 modules were detected (Fig. 4d).

Module validation

The results of module preservation analysis using five independent datasets are presented in Fig. 5b. Four out of 12 modules, namely black, blue, tan, and magenta were preserved in all of the datasets based on both Zsummary and medianRank statistics.

According to the GSNCA results and considering the significance level of 0.001 as the P value cut-off, in agreement with our previous findings on module-trait correlations, black, brown, magenta, blue, and yellow modules were identified to be differentially co-expressed between ATLL and healthy control samples (Table 3). Additionally, the GSAR package function “plotMST2.pathway” was used to identify highly correlated module genes along with their relations in both ATLL and healthy control samples, in which, genes with more contributing weight are emphasized with more reddish, which are shown in Fig. 6. The illustrated minimum spanning tree (MST) is defined as a co-expression network with the shortest links and highest correlations which connects all of the genes and the MTS2 is the combination of first and second MSTs (Rahmatallah et al., 2014).

Genes dysregulation evaluation and meta-analysis

The results of our in-depth expression meta-analysis by integrating 6 microarray datasets confirmed the dysregulations of potentially important genes (based on module membership, protein interaction network analysis, and functional enrichment results) such as ADCY1, ADCY3, ADCY4, MAPK3, PIK3CD, KRAS, NFKB1, TNF, PLCB3, PLCB2, PLCB1, MAPK11, JUN, ITPR1, GNAQ, CHEK1, CCND1, SOS2, BAX, FOS, and GNA12 along with 203 other black module genes (Supplementary File 1). In this regard, we identified the upregulation of ADCY1, ADCY3, ADCY4, AhR, BAX, CHEK1, FZD6, GNAI2, JAG1, KRAS, MAPK11, MAPK3, PLCB2, PLCB1, PLCB3 and the downregulation of FOS, GADD45A, CCND1, GNAQ, ITPR1, NFKB1, PIK3CD, TNF, SOS2 (Forest plots are available in Supplementary File 2).

Discussion

In this study using WGCNA, we analyzed the co-expression patterns of genes belonging to cellular signaling pathways expected to play key roles in the pathogenesis of ATLL. As a result, one module (black module) showed a remarkable correlation to the disease. The black module was further clustered into four functional sub-modules, each of which was highly correlated to one of four major groups of GO terms and KEGG pathways related to the regulation of immune responses and T-cell activation (sub-module one), regulation of cell signaling receptors and messengers (sub-module two), Wnt signaling pathway (sub-module three), and apoptosis (sub-module four).

Among the genes of the black module based on the network analysis (hub genes in the PPIN), correlation of genes with the black module (hub genes of the co-expression module), gene expression profiles, and cellular pathways related to each gene, MAPK3, PIK3CD, KRAS, NFKB1, TNF, PLCB3, PLCB2, PLCB1, MAPK11, JUN, ITPR1, ADCY1, GNAQ, ADCY3, ADCY4, CHEK1, CCND1, SOS2, BAX, FOS, and GNA12 were determined as possible biomarkers. After reviewing the literature, three genes, ADCY1, ADCY3, and ADCY4, were selected for Real-Time PCR evaluation, which confirmed the increased expression of ADCY1 and ADCY3. In addition, it is worth noting that dysregulation in some genes of the black module such as: FZD6 (Ghobadi et al., 2021), GADD45A (in primary CD4+ T lymphocytes with high expression of p12) (Nair et al., 2005), JAG1 (Bellon et al., 2018), and AhR (Hayashibara et al., 2003), were previously reported in PCR studies with similar expression patterns we observed in our microarray datasets.

The adenylate cyclase (ADCY) gene family includes glycoproteins responsible for the regulation of cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) production (Ludwig and Seuwen, 2002; Baron et al., 2015). Dysregulation of ADCYs has been found in malignancies with the highest resistance to chemotherapy, which has made the ADCY protein family an interesting target for gene therapy or considered as a biomarker in determining prognosis (Guo et al., 2022). cAMP is involved in the most basic cellular functions, including cell proliferation. It is responsible for the regulation of the apoptosis and the response to DNA damage through cAMP response element-binding proteins (CREB) which is the activator of the WNT/b-catenin pathway (Bos, 2003). ADCYs are reaction catalysts during which cAMP is produced from ATP (Guo et al., 2022). In a study conducted in 2010 by Andrea K and colleagues, high levels of cAMP were found in T lymphocytes infected with HTLV-1 (Kress et al., 2010), which supports this hypothesis. Therefore, it can be expected that the elevated expressions of ADCY1 and ADCY3 will lead to destructive changes due to high cytoplasmic cAMP concentrations. However, there have yet to be enough studies to investigate the effects of these genes on the pathogenesis and mortality of ATLL disease. The results of this study can indicate the role of ADCY1 and ADCY3 in the pathogenesis of ATLL. Considering the possible effect of these genes on the resistance to chemotherapy, more investigations are necessary to determine possible mechanisms and appropriate treatment methods.

The first sub-module was mainly related to cellular pathways of the activation and differentiation of T cells, the production and secretion of cytokines, and generally, the signaling pathways of the immune system. Studies have shown the key role of T-cell activation anomalies in ATLL activation as the result of infection with HTLV-1 (Tan et al., 2021). Considering the effect of the Tax protein on the NF-κB pathway, which is part of TCR signaling, in addition to the discovery of mutations in the genes, such as CARD11 (also related to TCR signaling) (Tan et al., 2021) dysregulations in T-cell activation may be one of the key pathways resulting in ATLL. However, the exact mechanism by which these changes cause cancer over time is unknown.

The third sub-module is closely related to the Wnt signaling pathway. The Wnt signaling pathway includes two types of canonical and non-canonical. The canonical pathway is activated by Wnt3a and is involved in cell proliferation and development (Nusse and Clevers, 2017). The non-canonical pathway is activated by Wnt5a (over-expressed in three of the datasets examined in this study) and is involved in cell movement by actin polymerization through a calcium ion-dependent cellular pathway (Naskar et al., 2014; Andre et al., 2015). The balance between canonical and non-canonical pathways plays an important role in the aging and development of stem cells (Baumann, 2013). Increased expression of Wnt5a is responsible for the invasiveness and metastasis of cancer cells (Prasad et al., 2018). One of the effects of increased expression of Wnt5a is the conversion of osteoclast precursors into active osteoclasts. This problem is probably involved in the occurrence of hypercalcemia, which is one of the typical features of ATLL (Bellon et al., 2013). Hbz viral protein or bZIP factor of human lymphotropic virus type 1 is able to affect the expression of Wnt5a in virus-infected cells (Ma et al., 2013). This issue has led Wnt5a to be a suitable target for the treatment of ATLL patients. In addition to the role of increased expression of Wnt5a in various cancers, Wnt5a has been reported to be involved in the ability of metastasis and infiltration of ATLL cells (Nakano et al., 2021). The Wnt5a gene is able to activate the NF-κB signaling pathway and facilitates the occurrence of ATLL (Naskar et al., 2014; Kim et al., 2012).

One of the main cellular functions influenced by the fourth sub-module is the regulation of apoptosis. The effect of the HTLV-1 on the resistance to apoptosis is well-known. This ability has a significant impact on the increased survival of cancer cells and resistance to the treatment (Karimi et al., 2017). One of the genes involved in the regulation of cell cycle and apoptosis is Checkpoint Kinase 1 (CHEK1). Based on the results obtained in this study, CHEK1 has increased expression in three datasets. CHEK1 along with ataxia telangiectasia and Rad3-related protein (ATR), play important roles in detecting and repairing DNA damage caused by chemotherapy and radiotherapy (Qiu et al., 2018). Increased expression of CHCK1 has been observed in various cancers such as breast, colon, liver, and stomach malignancies, as well as acute lymphocytic leukemia (ALL) (Boudny and Trbusek, 2020). It has been observed that inhibition of CHEK1 has led to apoptosis caused by B-cell lymphoma protein 2 (BCL-2) in malignant B cells (Schoeler et al., 2019). In a study on acute myeloid leukemia (AML) patients treated with a protocol based on cytarabine, CHEK1 was selected among 72 other genes as an independent factor determining the prognosis of patients (David et al., 2016). The use of CHEK1 inhibitors led to the treatment of imatinib-resistant chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) patients (Lei et al., 2018). Because there are insufficient studies on the role of CHEK1 in ATLL, it is necessary to conduct more studies on the possible role of CHEK1 as a biomarker and therapeutic target in these patients.

Conclusion

Based on the results obtained from our comprehensive bioinformatics analysis, four major groups of signaling pathways and GO terms related to the regulation of immune responses and T-cell activation, regulation of cell signaling receptors and messengers, Wnt signaling pathway, and apoptosis were highlighted to be highly activated in the pathogenesis of ATLL. Also, several genes were candidates as possible biomarkers and treatment targets, such as MAPK3, PIK3CD, KRAS, NFKB1, TNF, PLCB3, PLCB2, PLCB1, MAPK11, JUN, ITPR1, ADCY1, GNAQ, ADCY3, ADCY4, CHEK1, CCND1, SOS2, BAX, FOS and GNA12. Among these, the expression levels of ADCY1, ADCY3 and ADCY4 genes were measured through PCR, which indicated a significant increase in ADCY1 and ADCY3 expression in patients. Because many of these genes and signaling pathways are known to be related to the initiation of chemoresistance features in several malignancies, more studies are needed to evaluate their possible benefits as biomarkers and targets of gene therapies.
