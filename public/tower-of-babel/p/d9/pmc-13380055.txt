R0 of Bundibugyo virus disease and the critical issue of case ascertainment in the Democratic Republic of the Congo
Authors: Seiko Fujiwara, Hiroshi Nishiura
Published: 2026

Dear editor,

Bundibugyo virus is a phylogenetically distinct ebolavirus from Sudan and Zaire ebolaviruses [1], identified as responsible for the 2026 outbreak in Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC) and Uganda. Because phylogenetic distances from other ebolaviruses are substantial, laboratory testing was not widely available in the beginning of the outbreak, and thus, epidemiological surveillance initially relied on suspected cases based on non-specific clinical symptoms. The capacity of reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), the gold standard testing for diagnosing infection with ebolavirus in DRC was as small as 40 testing per day by early June 2026 [1]. Accordingly, an incidence estimation study in DRC took place, analyzing exported cases from DRC to Uganda and suggesting that the actual incidence is substantially greater than reported count [2]. In the meantime, whether such an outbreak develops to a pandemic has been of global concern. Here we aimed to investigate how large the basic reproduction number R0 of Bundibugyo virus disease (BVD) is.

A conventional method to estimate R0 during the very early period of the epidemic is to use and estimate the exponential growth rate of cases. To estimate the growth rate, Fig. 1A shows the cumulative number of suspected and confirmed cases in DRC, while Fig. 1B shows the cumulative number of confirmed cases only [3,4]. For the purpose of comparison, both panels also show the cumulative numbers of Zaire ebolavirus disease cases in 2014 and 2018, respectively, in West Africa and DRC [5].

Cumulative number of cases with Bundibugyo virus disease in Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC), 2026. (A) Suspected and confirmed cases combined, (B) confirmed cases only. The number of days since the declaration of the outbreak is counted from 15 May 2026 as day zero. The vertical axis is logarithmic, so the linear slope of each curve corresponds to the exponential growth rate. Red, 2026 DRC (Ituri); blue, 2018 DRC (North Kivu); green, 2014 West Africa. Confirmed-case data for the 2014 West Africa outbreak are not shown in (B), because confirmed case count was unavailable during the early period (and was first recorded only on day 99). Data as of 8 June 2026 (combined) and 12 June 2026 (confirmed) were retrieved.

Fitting an exponential growth model to suspected and confirmed cases by 30 May 2026, the growth rate was estimated at 0.093 per day (95% confidence interval (CI): 0.086-0.101). This estimate was greater than 0.052 per day in 2014 and 0.060 per day in 2018. Employing a gamma distributed generation time with mean 15.3 days and variance 86.5 days2 [5], R0 of current BVD outbreak was estimated at 3.26 (95% CI: 3.01-3.52). However, in June 2026, the World Health Organization ceased to update the number of suspected cases, and Africa Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reduced the cumulative count of suspected cases who turned out to be negative by laboratory testing. Although controversial, the cumulative number of suspected cases thus decreased as a function of time in the Africa CDC report (Fig. 1A). When we focused on confirmed cases only, the growth rate of cases by 30 May 2026 was 0.212 per day (95%CI: 0.183-0.241), yielding R0 at 8.90 (95%CI: 7.17-10.87), a considerably higher estimate than 1.5—2.5 for 2014 and 2018 outbreaks.

Other than high growth rates, there is no indication that the transmissibility of BVD is considerably higher than other ebolaviruses. Rather, the considerably high estimate is not independent of the issue of difficulty in ascertaining BVD cases [2]. In fact, this is the first ebolavirus outbreak in which suspected cases continued to substantially dominate all diagnosed cases for the first month of the outbreak. Because technical support to improve testing capacity was provided in the midst of the early outbreak period, the growth rate of confirmed cases as a function of time was unfortunately not very useful due to growing capacity of testing with time, and moreover, the cumulative number of suspected cases was even reduced due to reclassification. Accordingly, the growth rate of cases in the very early outbreak period in the DRC was not interpretable in a straightforward manner. If the estimated growth rate was real, the observed speed of BVD propagation would have been terrifying and indicated a need for immediate global action.

Our exercise gives critical insights into ascertainment of cases as part of pandemic preparedness planning. First, the testing capacity can really be a cause of trouble upon emergence. Due to distant lineage, existing rapid diagnostic testing was not helpful for diagnosing BVD as a substitute to RT-PCR. At local levels nearby epicenter, we should assume that the capacity of nucleic acid testing will be always limited. Considering that the same Bundibugyo virus caused an outbreak in the past, a rapid antigenic testing and confirmatory testing capacity with primer should have been prepared in advance at a local level, and even in a remote area, standard thermocycler could have been placed with international assistance. Second, the absence of laboratory testing is a common issue shared by other pandemic pathogens, and epidemiologists need to prepare an action plan to impose multiple case definitions based on clinical signs and symptoms, possibly including very strict definition with high specificity (e.g. capturing severe fraction of cases by loss of consciousness) and preventing too excessive count of the number of suspected cases with non-specific symptoms. Moreover, if fever is included as part of the case definition, cases could have been counted not only by the date of diagnosis but also by the first date of fever. Third, not only counting the cumulative number, reporting the number of new cases per day can help avoid later confusions induced by misclassifications.

Early growth rate of cases offers a key insight into the transmissibility of newly arising epidemic disease, thereby helping us to assess the pandemic potential. To improve the scientific capacity for quantitative risk assessment, we must build a response system that focused on proper ascertainment, overcoming the difficulty in precisely diagnosing and counting the number of cases.
