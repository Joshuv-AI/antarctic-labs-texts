SHAP-driven insights into multimodal data: behavior phase prediction for industrial safety applications
Authors: Xiangchun Li, Shuhao Zhang, Xiaowei Li, Jianhua Zeng, Yuzhen Long, Baisheng Nie, Chenbo Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnsafe behaviors among coal miners are a primary factor contributing to accidents, posing significant challenges for safety management. This study develops a behavior state prediction framework using artificial intelligence and machine learning (ML) to investigate the relationship between workers’ behavioral states and physiological characteristics. The framework employs AI-driven data analysis to support early warning systems and real-time interventions, enhancing coal mine safety protocols. Eight ML algorithms, including K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), are evaluated. XGBoost achieves the highest performance, with 97.78% accuracy, 98.25% recall, and 97.86% F1-score, demonstrating strong generalization in complex coal mining scenarios. Key features influencing behavior prediction are identified using SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis, including the total power of the heart rate variability spectrum (TP/ms2), the median frequency of electromyography signals (EMF), the difference between maximum and minimum respiration values (Range), and the root mean square of electromyography signals (RMS). TP/ms2 and EMF exhibit accelerated growth patterns, whereas Range and RMS show boundary effects. Decision tree segmentation reveals relationships between feature values and SHAP contributions, providing actionable rules to improve safety. This study uses university student participants, which may limit generalizability; future validation with actual miners is recommended. Overall, the results highlight the predictive power of physiological features and the potential of wearable monitoring systems for real-time safety management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-18889-9.Keywords: Artificial intelligence, Coal mine unsafe behavior, Machine learning, Predictive safety management, XGBoost modelSubject terms: Biomedical engineering, Physiology

Introduction

Coal mining plays a critical role in the global energy supply, underpinning a wide range of industrial and economic activities. Nevertheless, it remains one of the most hazardous industries due to the complex and perilous conditions encountered in underground operations. Despite continuous improvements in safety regulations, technological innovations, and management practices, coal mine accidents persist at alarmingly high rates, causing substantial human and economic losses1. Extensive research has identified miners’ unsafe behaviors as a primary contributor to these incidents. Such behaviors include non-compliance with safety protocols, disregard for environmental hazards, and failure to utilize essential personal protective equipment during operations. These unsafe actions not only threaten individual miners’ safety but also exacerbate challenges in overall safety management, particularly within the confined and high-risk environments typical of underground coal mining2.

Traditional coal mine safety management primarily relies on manual inspections, scheduled safety audits, and retrospective accident analyses3. Although these methods have contributed to improving safety standards, they are often reactive and lack the capacity for continuous, real-time monitoring of miners’ behaviors and potential hazards. This limitation is particularly critical under the dynamic and high-pressure conditions of underground mining, where timely detection of unsafe behaviors is essential for accident prevention. Consequently, there is an increasing need to integrate advanced technological solutions—such as wearable sensors, physiological signal analysis, and machine learning-based behavior prediction models—into coal mine safety management systems. These innovations enable proactive, real-time identification and prediction of unsafe behaviors, thereby enhancing early warning capabilities and mitigating accident risks4. In this context, the present study aims to develop a behavior prediction model based on physiological signals to address these challenges and improve overall safety performance in coal mining environments.

The advancement of biometric recognition technologies has progressively emphasized utilizing miners’ physiological signals to assess their psychological and physical conditions, thereby predicting and alerting against unsafe behaviors. Physiological signals, including heart rate and skin conductance response (SCR), indicate miners’ emotional variations, emergency reactions, and attentional states, offering an innovative method for predicting unsafe behaviors5. Electroencephalogram (EEG) signals, serving as direct indicators of brain activity, exhibit heightened sensitivity to psychological stress, anxiety, and other emotional fluctuations encountered by miners during operations, thereby revealing potential unsafe behaviors6. However, the integration of these physiological signals with miners’ behavioral variations to enable efficient real-time prediction and warning via intelligent algorithms continues to pose substantial technical and methodological challenges.Advanced physiological and biochemical instruments facilitate real-time monitoring and analysis of multiple bodily systems, encompassing the cardiovascular, respiratory, endocrine, nervous, muscular, and metabolic systems. For example, heart rate variability (HRV), skin conductance response (SCR)7, and EEG8 provide critical insights into an individual’s emotional state and occupational stress, thereby indirectly reflecting behavioral safety. Additionally, behavioral data, encompassing limb movements, eye-tracking, and gait analysis, elucidate workers’ behavioral tendencies in specific contexts, offering essential evidence for detecting unsafe behaviors.

The rapid progress of wearable sensor technology has garnered growing interest in leveraging these devices to collect physiological data on workers’ physical exertion and movement, aiming to identify unsafe working postures and hazardous conditions9. These studies hold considerable potential for enhancing workplace safety.In biosignal monitoring, electrodermal activity (EDA) signals are frequently utilized and examined using two key metrics: skin conductance level (SCL) and SCR10. SCL represents slow variations in skin conductance, whereas SCR captures rapid responses to external stimuli. Studies have demonstrated that EDA signals effectively capture fluctuations in an individual’s mental state across diverse environments. In hazardous work environments, individuals exhibit markedly heightened biosignal reactivity11. For instance, Choi’s analysis of short-term EDA signal variations revealed substantial physiological differences between low- and high-risk activities, suggesting that EDA signals effectively assess individuals’ attentiveness to workplace hazards12. More broadly, biosignal reactivity has attracted significant attention in behavioral prediction across multiple domains, particularly in analyzing driving behaviors. Liang and Lin identified significant differences in EEG and EDA signal reactivity when drivers encountered road hazards, demonstrating that biosignals effectively reflect drivers’ alertness and responsiveness13. Despite extensive research on biosignal analysis applications in these domains, few studies have investigated the utilization of biosignals for predicting attention lapses in high-risk occupational settings, such as coal mining.

This study aims to predict miners’ psychological states by analyzing biosignals, with a specific emphasis on physiological changes in hazardous scenarios. Exposure to potential threats heightens individuals’ alertness, which is reflected in EEG or EDA signals, indicating sustained attention to external dangers under these conditions14. Furthermore, the integration of machine learning techniques for predicting worker behaviors and improving prediction accuracy remains a critical challenge demanding immediate attention.Machine learning models have been developed to estimate human behavioral states, with the aim of enhancing the accuracy of unsafe behavior detection. To achieve this, the integration of multimodal data for model construction has been explored in numerous studies. By combining features from physiological and behavioral data, subtle variations in workers’ behaviors can be captured, significantly enhancing the accuracy of identification15. For instance, sensors have been employed to record workers’ heart rates and EDA during operations, which are combined with behavioral data from video surveillance. These datasets are then analyzed using machine learning models, which enables more accurate detection of unsafe behaviors16. Traditional machine learning algorithms, such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forests (RF), are widely employed in this process. While these algorithms are capable of producing relatively accurate predictive models, machine learning is still considered a “black box” approach, with inherent challenges in explaining the inner workings of the models. To address this limitation, SHAP models have been applied to interpret prediction results. SHAP, based on game theory principles17, is considered to provide a robust and insightful framework for evaluating feature importance within models. As the advantages of SHAP become increasingly evident, its application is increasingly being applied across various domains. For example, in predicting the availability of electric vehicle charging stations, SHAP is employed to interpret model predictions, which aids in station planning and management18. In another study, SVM was combined with SHAP to visualize the contributions of individual factors to prediction outcomes and to evaluate the reliability of each prediction, providing deeper insights into winter road safety19. Xiao introduced a machine learning-based approach for accurately predicting the failure pressure of corroded pipelines. By integrating physical factors with the SHAP method, this study improved model interpretability and demonstrated its practical value in pipeline integrity management20. Similarly, Cakiroglu applied SHAP in wind power forecasting, revealing wind speed as the most influential factor in improving the accuracy of energy production estimation models21. Salehi incorporated SHAP-based feature importance analysis in sustainable energy assessment, identifying fossil fuel dependence and renewable energy integration as the most critical factors shaping national-level sustainability rankings22. Furthermore, Tahir proposed a federated explainable AI framework for smart agriculture, which combined SHAP, LIME, and Grad-CAM to provide actionable insights for crop monitoring, predictive maintenance, and resource optimization, while enhancing both interpretability and decision reliability23.

Ensuring safety in coal mining critically depends on the accurate identification and early detection of unsafe behaviors. Traditional safety management approaches, which primarily rely on accident reports, are inherently reactive and often fail to provide timely intervention. In contrast, data-driven intelligent recognition technologies present promising solutions for high-risk industries by enabling proactive safety measures. Despite these advancements, significant challenges remain in optimizing the integration of multi-source physiological data and enhancing model interpretability, particularly in capturing the dynamic variations of physiological indicators under hazardous conditions and their relationship with unsafe behaviors. To address these challenges, this study proposes an automated recognition framework based on both physiological and behavioral data. The physiological signals collected include EMG, SKT, EDA, RESP, and PPG. Multiple machine learning models were evaluated for their detection performance, and the SHAP method was employed to interpret the decision-making process of the optimal model24.

The results demonstrated improved accuracy in predicting unsafe behaviors and provided a scientific basis for real-time safety monitoring in high-risk environments, thereby contributing to enhanced process safety management and overall coal mining safety. The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: “Research methods” describes the research methods, including the research process, experimental design, data collection, and preprocessing. “Model development and evaluation” details the development, comparison, and evaluation of machine learning models, accompanied by SHAP analysis for model interpretation. “Discussion” discusses the prediction performance, identifies key influencing factors, and addresses the study’s limitations and potential directions for future research. Finally, “Conclusion” concludes the paper by summarizing the main findings and providing insights for subsequent investigations.

Research methods

Research process

Figure 1 illustrates the analytical framework employed to assess the unsafe behavioral states of underground coal mine workers. The framework enables direct identification of workers’ behavioral states across three stages: baseline, preparation, and execution. Drawing on engineering research related to EEG and physiological signals, data were collected through laboratory-simulated experiments. A circuit maintenance task specifically designed for coal mine workers was first developed using appropriate instruments, after which data collection and labeling were performed. The collected data were then processed, and multiple machine learning algorithms were applied to train predictive models, with the optimal model subsequently selected. Finally, SHAP analysis was conducted to identify the key physiological features, which were further used to establish classification criteria for unsafe behaviors.

Experiment design and data collection

Experiment background

Electrical accidents caused by human error are prevalent not only in China but also worldwide. Research on human factors reliability in the power industry has primarily focused on qualitative analyses and safety regulations, highlighting the need to integrate these findings with more comprehensive investigations25. In the context of coal mining, the safe operation of electrical equipment is critical for maintaining overall mining safety. Even when workers are not directly engaged with electrical equipment, improper handling can still lead to electric shock incidents, which may escalate into severe consequences such as gas explosions. Importantly, workers with limited safety awareness may fail to strictly adhere to operational procedures, thereby increasing the likelihood of electric shock and explosion accidents.

In a real-world incident, an electrician, having failed to establish proper communication or deactivate the front control switch, attempted to maintain a three-way junction box by installing a metal gasket. When workers who had previously disconnected the power supply completed their tasks and restored electricity, the electrician, still in contact with a live wire, unintentionally generated a spark.This spark ignited a gas explosion in the tunnel due to the high methane concentration. Such incidents often occur when overconfident workers, or those seeking to save time and effort, begin tasks without fully disconnecting the main power source. The risk is further exacerbated when methane concentrations exceed safe thresholds at the mining face, posing severe threats to worker safety.Drawing on this case, the present experiment replicates an underground coal mining environment characterized by high gas outburst risk. In the experimental design, equipment failure is deliberately induced through internal malfunctions in a three-way junction box. Participants are required to first assess the work environment according to task requirements, then proceed to open the junction box, remove the metal terminal, and complete the maintenance procedure. The ErgoLAB platform is utilized to capture workers’ psychological and physiological responses during unsafe behaviors.

Experimental subjects and duration

When selecting participants, it was essential to minimize external factors that could potentially influence the experimental outcomes. Therefore, individuals with substantial differences in educational background or knowledge structure were excluded to ensure consistency within the sample. Considering that coal miners in practice are predominantly male and acknowledging the limitations of the experimental setting, 50 male university students were recruited as participants for this study26. Their ages ranged from 21 to 30 years, with an average of 24, which is comparable to the age profile of actual coal mine workers. This selection helps ensure that the experimental findings maintain practical relevance and are representative of real-world conditions. Participants’ pre-experiment activities, such as exercise or stimulant intake, were recorded to control for confounding factors. Before the task, participants received safety instructions and detailed guidelines. Unsafe behavior, defined in accordance with coal mine electrical maintenance protocols, encompassed actions such as working without gloves, failing to disconnect the power supply, or omitting voltage checks. Participants subsequently completed a simulated junction box repair under high-risk conditions, and any unsafe actions were annotated for later physiological data segmentation and predictive model training.

Eligible participants completed informed consent, basic information questionnaires, personality assessment, and emotional state evaluation. The ErgoLAB human–machine interface system was activated, and physiological and EEG devices were connected to a synchronized data acquisition platform. Participants were assisted in properly wearing the ErgoLAB wearable system, including 2-channel ear-clip sensors, a 6-channel chest band, wrist sensors, and 4-channel finger sensors—for accurate real-time monitoring of SpO2, RESP, HR, ECG, EDA, PPG, SKT, and EMG. Device connections and signal stability were checked before the experiment commenced. Figure 4 illustrates the placement of these physiological sensors and EEG equipment.

Physiological signals were initially recorded while participants remained in a calm state for three minutes, sitting in a relaxed posture and avoiding unnecessary movements. This baseline data served as a reference for later analyses. After the baseline recording, participants entered the simulated coal mine environment and performed the assigned repair task according to the experimental instructions. If a participant opened the junction box and removed the metal terminal, the electrostatic generator activated, an explosion video played, and smoke was released to simulate a hazardous event. Throughout the task, participants’ physiological and EEG signals were continuously monitored. The experimental procedure is shown in Fig. 5.

Data extraction and processing

Six types of physiological indicators were statistically analyzed, and the data were processed using the ErgoLAB software, as shown in Fig. 6. The collected physiological indicators are detailed in Table 2. Among these, EEG signals required additional preprocessing to enhance data quality and reduce artifacts. Since typical human brain activity occurs within the frequency range of 0.5–48 Hz, and most EEG energy is concentrated between 4 and 30 Hz, a bandpass filter was applied to retain relevant neural information. Specifically, a high-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 0.1 Hz was used to eliminate slow drifts, while a low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of 30 Hz was employed to suppress high-frequency noise. To reduce physiological artifacts, ocular movements and muscular interference were removed using independent component analysis (ICA). Components associated with eye or muscle activity were identified and excluded based on their spatial and temporal features. This preprocessing pipeline ensured that the extracted EEG features more accurately reflected underlying neural activity relevant to behavioral phase identification and stress assessment.

Model prediction

In the field of behavior prediction, commonly used machine learning algorithms include traditional methods such as RF and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)28. More recently, deep learning approaches, including Convolutional Neural Networks and Recurrent Neural Networks, have attracted increasing attention29. Although these methods often demonstrate strong predictive performance, their practical application remains constrained by the limited size of experimental datasets, particularly in laboratory settings where data are typically scarce. Furthermore, substantial differences exist among studies in terms of methodological approaches, performance evaluation criteria, and reporting formats, which hinders the reproducibility and comparability of findings. In model evaluation, different studies adopt varying criteria to determine the optimal model, with commonly used metrics including accuracy, the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC), and the F1-score30.

To accurately classify behavioral states obtained from multimodal sensor data, this study employed eight widely used supervised machine learning classifiers: XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, Naïve Bayes, K-Nearest Neighbors, and Decision Tree. These algorithms were selected based on extensive evidence from prior studies demonstrating their effectiveness in behavior classification tasks. For instance, JungHo Jeon compared various classifiers—including Decision Trees, KNN, SVM, and XGBoost—for detecting construction hazards and reported promising results. Drawing from such findings, the present study applied these eight supervised algorithms to classify unsafe behavioral states31. The dataset used for model training was balanced, containing 50 samples for each behavioral state (baseline, preparation, and execution), ensuring that the models were not biased toward any specific class and that performance metrics reliably reflected classification performance across all categories. Before training, a 10-fold cross-validation strategy was implemented for hyperparameter optimization. Specifically, the dataset was divided into 10 subsets, with nine subsets used for training and the remaining subset for validation in each iteration32. This approach effectively tuned model hyperparameters, maximizing generalization capability. As a result, the trained models not only achieved high performance on the training data but also exhibited robust predictive stability on unseen data. SHAP model explanation

The SHAP algorithm, introduced by Lundberg and Lee in 2017, is a widely adopted technique for interpreting machine learning model outputs33. It is grounded in the concept of Shapley values from cooperative game theory, which quantify the contribution of each feature to a specific prediction. Specifically, Shapley values estimate the average marginal contribution of a feature by evaluating all possible combinations of features in the model. This approach provides a fair and consistent explanation of how each input variable influences the model’s predictions34. The computational procedure is detailed as follows.

where is the SHAP value of feature i, k is the set of all features, S is the subset of features excluding feature i, |S| is the number of features in subset S, |k| is the total number of features, is the model’s prediction for instance x given the feature set S, and is the model’s prediction for instance x given both the feature set S and feature i. The difference between and represents the marginal contribution of feature i when added to the feature set S in the prediction.

SHAP values provide an accurate measure of each feature’s impact on the model’s output, ensuring a fair and consistent interpretation. Beyond explaining individual predictions, the SHAP method offers a global perspective by summarizing the average effect of all features, thereby revealing the overall behavior of the model35, as illustrated in Fig. 7. In this study, SHAP values were employed to identify the key features driving the model’s predictions and to quantify their specific contributions to the results. This approach enhances both the interpretability and transparency of the model, offering critical support for understanding and trusting predictions of unsafe behaviors.

Model development and evaluation

Model comparison and selection

To accurately classify the three worker states, this study compared and evaluated the performance of multiple machine learning models using key metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, AUC, and F1-Macro36. Accuracy represents the proportion of correctly predicted samples relative to the total number of samples, as shown in Eq. (2). Precision quantifies the proportion of correctly predicted positive samples among all samples predicted as positive by the model, as shown in Eq. (3), indicating how often the model correctly identifies a “positive” outcome. Conversely, recall measures the proportion of actual positive samples correctly identified by the model, as shown in Eq. (4), reflecting the model’s ability to capture all positive instances. To balance precision and recall, the F1-score was employed as the overall evaluation metric, as shown in Eq. (5). The F1-score, defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a better trade-off between these two metrics. Additionally, the F1-Micro score calculates overall precision and recall across all samples, whereas the F1-Macro score computes precision and recall for each category individually and averages them, providing a more comprehensive assessment of model performance in multi-class classification.

Based on a comprehensive comparison of model performance, XGBoost outperformed all other classifiers, achieving an accuracy of 97.78%, substantially surpassing the remaining models. XGBoost also exhibited strong performance across other key evaluation metrics, including Precision (97.61%), Recall (98.25%), and F1-Score (97.86%), indicating its clear advantage in correctly classifying samples while maintaining a balanced overall classification performance. In contrast, although some models achieved competitive results on individual metrics, their overall performance remained slightly inferior to that of XGBoost. For example, RF and CatBoost both achieved high Precision and Recall, close to 96%, but did not exceed the performance of XGBoost. KNN, however, showed relatively poor performance, with an accuracy of only 75.56%, reflecting limited classification capability. While LightGBM demonstrated overall performance comparable to XGBoost, it was slightly lower in terms of Recall and F1-score.

To further assess whether the observed differences in classification accuracy among the models were statistically significant, a series of paired t-tests was conducted. Although XGBoost achieved the highest mean accuracy of 97.78% and consistently outperformed other models on key evaluation metrics, including Precision, Recall, and F1-score, the statistical analysis indicated that its accuracy did not differ significantly from that of other high-performing classifiers, such as CatBoost, Random Forest, and LightGBM. This lack of statistical significance may be attributed to factors such as the limited sample size, variability in model performance across cross-validation folds, or overlapping decision boundaries within the feature space37. These findings suggest that, while XGBoost demonstrated superior performance under the current experimental conditions, its dominance over other top-performing models cannot be claimed with statistical certainty.

In addition to the statistical comparison of overall accuracy, confusion matrix analysis was performed to provide a more granular evaluation of each model’s classification behavior. By examining the distribution of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, confusion matrices enable an intuitive assessment of model performance on a class-by-class basis, thereby facilitating more informed model selection. As shown in Fig. 9, XGBoost consistently demonstrates superior performance in correctly identifying all three behavioral categories, further confirming its effectiveness for detecting unsafe behaviors based on physiological and EEG signals.

SHAP analysis

Feature importance analysis

SHAP values were employed to interpret the contribution of each feature to the XGBoost model’s predictions of behavioral states. The average absolute SHAP values were calculated for each input variable to quantify their global importance, as illustrated in Fig. 10. Features with larger SHAP values exert a greater influence on the model’s predictions. Notably, the magnitude of a SHAP value is independent of the order in which features are considered and remains consistent even if a feature is excluded38. Moreover, Fig. 10 visualizes the impact of each variable on the three behavioral states (baseline, preparation, and execution), providing insights into the key determinants of unsafe behavior. These findings offer a novel perspective on behavior prediction, emphasizing influential factors that may have been previously overlooked.

Global feature importance ranking chart.

Figure 10 presents the ranking of feature importance based on SHAP values, reflecting the extent to which each feature influences predictions across different behavioral states. Among all behavioral states, TP/ms2 shows the highest importance, suggesting that the power spectrum, which encompasses ultra-low-, low-, and high-frequency components, plays a dominant role in behavior prediction. In addition, EMF and Range are identified as significant contributors to the model’s predictions. RMS also demonstrates relatively high importance, particularly in predicting the baseline state. Overall, this figure provides an intuitive visualization of how various physiological features affect behavioral prediction and clarifies their roles in guiding the model’s decision-making process.

Data visualization analysis

To visually illustrate the contribution of each feature in the best-performing model, SHAP global feature importance rankings were generated based on 26 physiological indicators. The SHAP values are displayed using a swarm plot, where each colored dot represents a feature value. When multiple dots overlap at the same position, they are stacked to indicate density. A color scale on the right side of each plot encodes feature values, with red representing higher values and blue representing lower values. This visualization allows for an intuitive understanding of the varying impacts of different feature values on the model’s predictions.

In the SHAP swarm plot, most features are distributed across both sides of the SHAP value axis, indicating that they can have both positive and negative contributions to the model’s output. For features such as TP/ms2 and SPF (the frequency of the median individual temperature within the measurement period), low feature values are located in the negative SHAP value region, suggesting that lower values of these features tend to reduce the model’s output and are less associated with unsafe behaviors. Furthermore, the broad SHAP value distributions observed for features such as TP/ms2 and EMF indicate that these features exert a particularly strong influence on the model’s predictions.

Although this study applied machine learning models to predict physiological signal data across the baseline, preparation, and execution stages of behavior, the primary focus is on the changes in physiological signals before and during unsafe behavior execution. This emphasis is justified because features in these stages provide more direct guidance for identifying high-risk behaviors and implementing interventions to prevent unsafe operations.

These findings suggest that the predictive roles of physiological features vary between the preparation and execution stages, reflecting stage-specific patterns of behavior-related physiological changes.By synthesizing both figs, it can be observed that TP/ms2 consistently ranks as the most important feature in both categories, highlighting its role as a key feature for distinguishing between the preparation and execution stages. However, the ranking and influence direction of secondary features (e.g., Range and EMG-Mean) vary across categories, providing important insights for optimizing the classification model and interpreting predictions for specific categories.

Behavior state rule extraction

The SHAP value analysis of model input features across different behavioral states provides insights into how specific feature value ranges contribute to the prediction results. For example, when the value of γ is very low, the model is more likely to predict the unsafe behavior preparation stage. To further quantify the relationship between feature values and their contributions, a regression tree-based splitting method is employed. Specifically, a decision tree regression model is fitted to the SHAP values of each input feature, enabling the identification of key split points that drive changes in SHAP values. This approach facilitates a more precise understanding of how variations in feature values influence model predictions across different behavioral states.

The essence of decision tree regression lies in partitioning the feature value space into multiple intervals and minimizing prediction errors within each interval39. This approach enables the identification of significant change points in SHAP values. These split points indicate the ranges of feature values within which their influence on the model prediction varies. By partitioning the dataset such that the SHAP value changes within each sub-region are as consistent as possible, key threshold values can be accurately extracted. This method not only quantifies the contribution of features to predictions across different behavioral stages but also generates behavioral prediction rules based on the split points, offering more granular insights into interpreting complex models. Discussion

Model prediction performance and significance

Behavior prediction models are generally developed by training machine learning algorithms on curated datasets40. In this process, raw data are collected from diverse sources, followed by feature engineering to extract relevant variables that serve as model inputs. While most studies address behavior prediction as a binary classification task, distinguishing the presence or absence of a specific behavior, some extend the approach to multi-class classification41. This approach aims to capture more complex behavioral characteristics and patterns, thereby enhancing the comprehensiveness and accuracy of predictions.

This study developed a behavior state classification model using multimodal data, integrating behavioral states (baseline, preparation, execution) with physiological signals (EEG, EDA, ECG, EMG, HRV). Several machine learning algorithms were employed to predict the three behavior states. The results demonstrate that ensemble learning algorithms based on the gradient boosting framework achieved superior performance, with the XGBoost model attaining the highest accuracy of 97.78% and an F1-score of 0.9786, highlighting its substantial advantage in handling the heterogeneity and complex interactions inherent in multimodal data42. The CatBoost model also exhibited strong performance, achieving an accuracy of 94.89% and an F1-score of 0.9531, reflecting robust classification capability and adaptability in real-time scenarios. Similarly, the Random Forest model achieved an accuracy of 95.56% and an F1-score of 0.9577, demonstrating its effectiveness in efficiently managing complex data structures. LightGBM closely followed, achieving an accuracy of 93.33%, with slightly higher precision (0.9375) and recall (0.9474) compared to Random Forest, indicating its computational efficiency and stability in processing large-scale datasets.

In contrast, the KNN model underperformed, achieving an accuracy of only 75.56% and an F1-score of 0.7706. This suboptimal performance can be primarily attributed to KNN’s high sensitivity to noisy data and its susceptibility to the “curse of dimensionality” in high-dimensional feature spaces, which markedly degraded its classification capability. These results confirm that KNN is not well-suited for complex tasks involving multimodal data43. Overall, the XGBoost algorithm demonstrated superior performance due to its ability to capture nonlinear relationships between features, low sensitivity to noise, and strong robustness in handling heterogeneous multimodal data. This makes it particularly suitable for behavior state recognition tasks, especially in real-time monitoring and prediction scenarios. The findings underscore the advantages of ensemble learning algorithms for modeling the fusion of behavioral and physiological signals and provide technical support for real-time detection of unsafe behaviors in industrial settings.However, the generalizability of the model may be limited by the current sample size. Future research could further enhance model performance by expanding the dataset and optimizing feature selection techniques. Such improvements would lay the groundwork for more accurate multimodal data fusion models and more reliable real-time monitoring systems.

Interpretation of key influencing factors

This study employed the XGBoost algorithm to classify behavioral states using multimodal data, including EEG, EDA, ECG, EMG, and HRV signals, along with behavioral observations across baseline, preparation, and execution stages. Among all evaluated models, XGBoost achieved the highest performance, with an accuracy of 97.78% and an F1-score of 0.9786, outperforming other ensemble methods such as CatBoost, Random Forest, and LightGBM, as well as KNN, which showed limited classification capability due to its sensitivity to noisy, high-dimensional data43. These results demonstrate the advantage of gradient boosting-based ensemble models in handling heterogeneous, complex multimodal datasets and their suitability for real-time unsafe behavior detection in industrial settings.

SHAP value analysis provides a quantitative assessment of the impact of each input feature on behavior states during the prediction process. The results reveal that TP/ms2 consistently ranks among the most important features across all behavior states. Spectral power features, including ultra-low frequency, low frequency, and high frequency components, reflect the operator’s physiological load and neural responses, which are critical for behavior state prediction. This finding is consistent with other studies that associate total power with heart rate variability and psychological load44. Features such as EMF (median frequency) and Range (respiratory amplitude) also demonstrated notable influence on behavior state predictions, though their contributions were slightly less than that of TP/ms2. Notably, RMS (root mean square) exhibited higher feature importance in predicting unsafe behavior during the preparation phase, suggesting that RMS is a strong indicator of the operator’s physiological stress response. This aligns with previous research showing that RMS is linked to psychological fluctuations and stress perception45.

The visualization of SHAP values revealed that most features exhibit both positive and negative distributions, indicating that their impact on the model’s output varies depending on the context. Specifically, higher TP/ms2 values were generally associated with positive SHAP values, suggesting that this feature is predictive of high-probability behaviors during both the preparation and execution phases. In contrast, Range values had stage-dependent effects, with higher values in certain phases linked to negative impacts, implying that this feature may signal potential risks under specific conditions. By combining global SHAP feature importance with swarm plots, this study provides a more comprehensive understanding of feature behavior across different behavioral states, surpassing the insights offered by individual feature assessments. Furthermore, piecewise regression was employed to quantify the complex relationship between feature values and model predictions, identifying critical thresholds for features such as TP/ms2 and Range. These findings not only elucidate the mechanisms through which physiological and behavioral variables influence the XGBoost model but also offer a theoretical foundation for establishing early-warning thresholds and optimizing prediction strategies across different behavior stages.

Limitations and future directions

While this study provides valuable insights into the influence of key features, several limitations should be considered. First, the SHAP value analysis was conducted using the current dataset, which may limit its generalizability. Further testing across diverse contexts is needed. Second, although segmented feature effects were examined, causal relationships were not explored; future work could incorporate experimental or mechanistic models to investigate underlying drivers. Moreover, the predictive mechanisms of different algorithms may lead to variations in results, so caution is required when generalizing findings.

It is also important to note that participants were university students rather than actual coal miners. While convenient for controlled studies, their physiological and behavioral responses may differ from trained miners, potentially limiting ecological validity. Future studies should include actual workers to capture context-specific patterns and enhance practical relevance.The results offer insights for optimizing behavior detection models. The high importance of TP/ms2 emphasizes the need for efficient physiological signal acquisition and analysis modules. Features such as Range and EMF suggest that behavioral responses during preparation may be influenced by maintaining these variables within specific ranges, with piecewise regression thresholds providing practical guidance for intelligent monitoring systems.

To improve practical applicability, the proposed model is designed for real-time monitoring and early warning. By continuously analyzing physiological signals, subtle changes indicating risky behavior can trigger immediate alerts for timely intervention. This predictive capability supports proactive hazard mitigation and intelligent safety management.For real-world deployment, hardware requirements include multi-core processors, 2–4 GB memory, BLE/Wi-Fi connectivity, and high-quality sensors for heart rate, EDA, and PPG. Limitations include device battery life, comfort affecting signal quality, environmental interference (e.g., dust, humidity), and data privacy concerns, which may impact real-time performance and should be considered during implementation.

Finally, SHAP explains correlations but cannot infer causation. Future research could apply causal inference frameworks or experimental designs to manipulate physiological parameters and directly observe behavioral effects, strengthening the scientific validity of model interpretations.

Conclusion

This study developed a machine learning–based framework for predicting behavioral states, emphasizing model selection, feature extraction, and the analysis of nonlinear feature relationships. Beyond the technical contributions, the research highlights the critical link between coal miners’ behaviors and mining accidents, demonstrating that unsafe behaviors substantially increase disaster risks in underground mining environments. The proposed system leverages physiological signals, including EDA, ECG, PPG, and heart rate variability, as indicators of miners’ real-time physical and emotional states. Based on these inputs, the system predicts the likelihood of unsafe behaviors and provides an early warning mechanism, enhancing miners’ safety awareness and reducing the occurrence of accidents. This framework bridges the gap between human behavioral factors and accident prevention, offering a practical approach to integrating behavioral monitoring into coal mine safety management. The key conclusions of this study are as follows:

Ensemble learning algorithms, especially XGBoost, demonstrated outstanding performance in multimodal behavioral prediction tasks. Their robustness in handling complex and noisy data environments highlights their potential for real-time deployment in industrial settings, significantly enhancing the efficiency of unsafe behavior detection and early warning systems.
