Core competencies and training needs of specialist-nurse clinical educators: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Junye Tian, Meng Zhang, Lichuan Zhang, Yujie Wang, Qian Lu, Yanming Ding
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe competency of specialist nurse clinical educators is crucial for the effectiveness of specialist nurse training programmes. However, variability in teaching competency and training needs among educators remains insufficiently studied, especially in the context of rapidly evolving healthcare education in China.ObjectivesThis study aimed to identify distinct core competency profiles among clinical educators for specialist nurses, examine associated socio-demographic factors, and explore differences in training needs across profiles.MethodsA cross-sectional online survey was conducted with 3,945 specialist nurse clinical educators from 30 Chinese regions. The Chinese version of the Nurse Educator Core Competency Scale (NECCS) and a self-developed training needs questionnaire were used. Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) identified competency subgroups, while multinomial logistic regression and Kruskal–Wallis tests examined associated variables and training needs.ResultsLatent Profile Analysis identified three competency profiles: foundational (8.6%), intermediate (43.0%), and advanced (48.4%), with mean scores of 43.89, 68.24, and 91.68, respectively. Educators without prior training were significantly more likely to belong to the foundational (OR = 3.195, p < 0.001) and intermediate (OR = 1.676, p < 0.001) groups compared to those with training experience. Advanced-competency educators showed the highest demand for curriculum design training, with 75% rating it as highly necessary. In contrast, educators in the intermediate group identified clinical teaching methods and techniques as their top training need (58.7%). Those in the foundational group prioritised common pedagogical methods and instructional technologies (54.7%).ConclusionsClinical educator competencies vary by background characteristics and training exposure. Tailored, competency-based training is needed to address these gaps and enhance the quality of specialist nursing education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-025-04006-8.

Introduction

Specialist nurses play pivotal roles in high‑acuity and technology-intensive settings—such as intensive care, oncology, perioperative services, emergency and critical care—where clinical decisions carry immediate and consequential effects on patient outcomes. The concept of specialist nursing took shape in the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries and has since evolved internationally as health systems grappled with demographic ageing, chronic disease burden, and the rapid diffusion of new diagnostics and therapeutics [1, 2]. In China, specialist nursing emerged in the late 1980–1990 s and typically refers to registered nurses who have undertaken focused education in a defined clinical domain and accumulated substantial practice experience, enabling them to deliver high‑quality care and serve as clinical resources for teams [3, 4]. Over the last two decades, national and provincial nursing associations have spearheaded specialist‑nurse preparation, complemented by initiatives from health authorities, teaching hospitals, and universities [5, 6]. As of 2023, association‑led programmes span more than two dozen specialities and have trained tens of thousands of specialist nurses. These programmes generally combine short didactic blocks with supervised clinical rotations at designated training bases, where trainees are immersed in the real‑world complexity of specialist practice [5, 6].

Within this practice‑integrated training architecture, clinical educators—hereafter referred to as specialist‑nurse clinical Educators (SNCEs)—are central to learning and assessment. SNCEs organise and sequence learning activities, coach clinical reasoning at the point of care, provide formative and summative feedback, evaluate performance against speciality standards, and ensure that learners can safely translate theoretical knowledge into context‑appropriate actions [7]. In high-stakes environments, the pedagogical quality and assessment literacy of SNCEs are associated with safer care processes and underpin the reliability of competency-based progression decisions, although direct patient-outcome evidence in nursing is more limited than in medicine [8–10]. Consequently, conceptual clarity about what constitutes SNCE competency and robust measurement to diagnose strengths and gaps are essential prerequisites for effective educator development and for the reproducibility of competency‑based education (CBE) outcomes. In the health professions, CBE denotes an outcomes-based approach to the design, implementation, assessment, and evaluation of programmes, with competencies serving as the organising framework [11].

Nurse educator competency is a multi‑dimensional construct that has been variously labelled as roles, tasks, characteristics, skills, competencies, or capabilities across decades of scholarship [12]. Among widely adopted frameworks, the World Health Organisation’s Nurse Educator Core Competencies (NECC) provide a comprehensive and globally applicable definition of educator competency covering pedagogy and adult learning, curriculum design and implementation, nursing practice, communication and collaboration, ethics and professionalism (including supervision), assessment and evaluation, and leadership/management [13]. These domains function both as standards and as a design map for educator training programmes. To enhance local applicability, Chinese researchers culturally adapted and psychometrically validated an instrument based on the WHO framework, confirming an eight‑domain structure suitable for assessing educator competency and planning targeted training in the Chinese context [14]. Accordingly, these domains provide a standardised reference for competency appraisal and targeted faculty development.

Background

Against this backdrop, evidence on competency levels and domain profiles suggests a generally positive yet heterogeneous landscape. Across countries, nurse educators tend to self-rate overall competency in the good-to-high range; studies on clinical educators show that feedback and evaluation behaviours score comparatively lower than other domains, signalling an area for targeted support [15, 16]. A quantitative study with 242 Taiwanese preceptors reported a mean of 4.03/5 for teaching competency and identified internal locus of control and self-directed learning as independent predictors of higher competency, net of demographics and experience [17]. However, scores vary by rater (educators, managers, students), by country/region, and by competency domain. In a comparative study spanning Finland, Malta, Slovakia, and Spain, mean scores on a validated nurse-educator competency scale were consistently above the mid-point, yet significant between-country differences and domain-specific gaps persisted, particularly in pedagogical and assessment-related areas [18, 19].

Converging evidence shows that nurse-educator competency is heterogeneous and shaped by individual (e.g., demographics, academic preparation, speciality field, years of practice) and organisational/contextual factors (e.g., workload, role clarity, resources, institutional culture), rather than being uniform across settings. Extending beyond mean differences, profile-analytic and cross-sectional studies identify distinct competency profiles among clinical nurse educators and link speciality, experience, academic preparation, workload/role characteristics, and workplace attributes to profile membership and domain-specific scores, rather than competency clustering at a single level [20–22]. Predictive analyses also indicate that age, gender, and prior training significantly explain variance in effective clinical teaching, underscoring the role of individual background and exposure [23].

For educator development, intervention studies indicate that structured preparation can produce measurable gains in educator behaviours and related outcomes. A blended programme that combined a web‑based pedagogy module with case‑based, face‑to‑face workshops significantly increased overall clinical‑teaching competency among registered‑nurse educators, and also improved self‑efficacy and attitudes towards technology‑enhanced learning [24]. A 2024 quasi‑experimental study from Iran reported that a targeted preceptorship workshop increased nurses’ participation in teaching internship students compared with controls, demonstrating transfer to educator engagement in practice [25]. In addition, needs‑assessment studies indicate that preceptors themselves prioritise critical thinking, prioritisation under workload, effective teaching techniques, conflict management, and teamwork—domains that align closely with the above [16]. While structured educator development is demonstrably beneficial, the heterogeneity observed across educators means that training needs diverge across subgroups and therefore require tailored approaches. Implementation studies in low-resource settings emphasise the necessity of context-sensitive faculty preparation and resourcing, arguing against one-size-fits-all upskilling [26, 27]. Consistent with this, formal needs assessments of nurse educators confirm that development priorities vary by role and setting [28], supporting a strategy that aligns faculty-development programmes with the specific training needs identified.

However, a persistent gap concerns transferability to SNCEs. Much of the extant literature evaluates academic faculty or general preceptors working with undergraduate students or newly hired staff. SNCEs, by contrast, supervise learners who are already qualified and are now acquiring advanced, speciality-specific competencies under time pressure and in risk‑laden environments. Qualitative and mixed‑methods studies in hospital settings repeatedly surface tensions in dual roles (care provider vs. teacher), heavy cognitive load, and relationship strains, alongside calls for managerial recognition and structured support [16]. In master’s-level specialist programmes, student evaluations of educator competency are consistently lower than self-ratings and managerial appraisals, with recurrent deficits identified in assessment literacy and scholarly engagement [29]. These context-specific demands caution against simple extrapolation from academic or general educator samples to SNCEs—particularly for high-stakes assessment, advanced reasoning coaching, simulation-based learning and debriefing, and interprofessional coordination in procedure-intensive environments. Absent targeted attention to SNCE‑specific requirements, variability in specialist training quality is likely to persist and may jeopardise the reproducibility of CBE outcomes.

To secure reproducible CBE outcomes in specialist contexts, it is necessary to assess SNCEs’ educator competency at the domain level using recognised frameworks. Given the heterogeneity highlighted above and the paucity of SNCE-specific evidence, subgroup differences in competency and the associated training needs should be modelled explicitly rather than inferred from mean scores or single-predictor regressions. This calls for a person-centred analytic tool capable of detecting unobserved subpopulations and linking them to differentiated development priorities. Latent profile analysis (LPA) provides an appropriate person-centred approach: it estimates unobserved classes with distinct patterns across multidomain competency indicators and assigns probabilistic membership to each educator [30]. In nursing and allied health education, LPA has been used to delineate competency configurations and to relate class membership to work characteristics and organisational climate, supporting its validity and utility [31–33]. Combining an NECC-aligned domain assessment with LPA enables profile-responsive planning of SNCE development and supports rational allocation of educator-development resources.

Research hypotheses

Accordingly, this study asks whether discrete competency groups can be identified among SNCEs and whether background variables are differentially associated with subgroup membership. We further examine whether the identified competency subgroups are associated with distinct perceived training needs, with the aim of informing subgroup-specific continuing professional development planning in an international context.

The study tested the following hypotheses.

H1

There are distinct latent subgroups of core competencies among clinical nursing educators participating in specialist‑nurse training.

H2

Socio‑demographic characteristics exhibit differential associations with subgroup membership across competency profiles.

H3

The identified competency subgroups function as significant moderators explaining variations in the perceived necessity of training programs.

Method

Research design

This cross-sectional online survey was conducted between May and July 2023 among SNCEs. Reporting followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist to ensure transparency in design, conduct, and analysis [34].

Sampling and setting

A convenience sampling method was employed, allowing the survey to reach a broad audience of SNCEs through online distribution channels. To maximise geographical coverage and improve representativeness, a nationwide recruitment strategy was used. Data were collected from 30 of the 31 provincial-level administrative divisions in mainland China; one division was excluded because no officially accredited specialist nurse teaching bases were available at the time of data collection.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) currently serving as an SNCE; (2) at least 5 years of overall work experience; (3) at least 3 years in the relevant speciality; and (4) holding at least a junior professional title. Nurses on leave or off duty due to additional training were excluded. These criteria were aligned with the Chinese Nursing Association’s requirements for clinical educators in specialist nurse training programmes, which emphasise both teaching competency and contextual expertise.

Sample size

Sample size considerations were addressed at both design and analysis stages. Given the absence of a nationally standardised qualification framework for clinical nurse educators, a maximum‑reach nationwide strategy was adopted to capture diverse perspectives and practice variations.

For latent profile analysis (LPA), empirical guidelines generally recommend a minimum of 300–500 participants to achieve stable and reliable profile estimation [35]. Prior simulation research further suggests that some model-selection indices—such as the Lo–Mendell–Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test—may underextract classes when sample size is below 1,000, which can affect accurate profile identification [36–38]. Accordingly, we set an a priori target of at least 1,000 respondents to meet or exceed these thresholds, mitigate underextraction risk, and enhance the stability and interpretability of the final profile solution.

Data collection

An electronic questionnaire was administered via the Wenjuanxing platform (https://www.wjx.cn/vm/exmYKB3.aspx#). The survey link was first emailed to regional nursing associations accessible to the research team, which then forwarded it to clinical teaching bases (primarily tertiary hospitals). Nursing departments distributed the link to SNCEs through internal channels (work email, WeChat groups) and via QR code posters posted in staff areas. Because the survey link was disseminated through institutional channels, the number of recipients was unknown and a conventional response rate could not be estimated.

To minimise missing data and duplicate entries, all items were mandatory; each participant could submit only once; and platform settings prevented repeat submissions from the same device or IP address. Data were exported directly from the platform for analysis to avoid manual entry errors.

Measures

Demographic variables included age, gender, educational level, professional title, length of employment, and training experience. These factors were analysed to explore whether background characteristics were associated with membership in specific competency profiles.

Core educator competencies were assessed using the Chinese version of the Nurse Educator Core Competency Scale (NECCS) [14], grounded in the World Health Organisation framework. The NECCS consists of 37 items across eight dimensions. Items are rated from 0 (non‑compliance) to 3 (full compliance), and total scores are standardised to a 0–100 scale [100 × total score / (3 × 37)]; higher scores indicate higher competency. Previously reported total Cronbach’s alpha was 0.974, with dimension alphas from 0.827 to 0.922 [26]; in this study, the total alpha was 0.984.

Training needs were assessed using a self‑developed checklist covering 13 programme topics derived from key areas in Nursing Education [39] and aligned with established educational frameworks [40]. Each topic was rated on a 4‑point Likert scale (1 = “Not Necessary” to 4 = “Highly Necessary”) to prioritise perceived needs. The instrument was designed for descriptive needs assessment rather than as a latent construct scale; therefore, no formal psychometric testing (e.g., internal consistency, factor analysis) was performed. To mitigate measurement bias, an expert panel of senior nursing educators and clinical educators reviewed items for clarity, relevance, and alignment with study aims. This lack of formal validation is acknowledged as a limitation.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in Mplus (version 8.5) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26). After data cleaning, descriptive statistics were computed for all variables (means and standard deviations for continuous variables; counts and percentages for categorical variables).

Latent profile analysis (LPA) identified subgroups of core competencies from the eight NECCS dimensions. Model selection followed established recommendations [41] and used a combination of information criteria, likelihood ratio tests, and classification indices. The information criteria were the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and the sample-size adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC). The likelihood ratio tests were the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-A) and the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT). Classification quality was evaluated using entropy, average posterior probabilities (AvePP), and the odds of correct classification (OCC). Lower AIC, BIC, and aBIC values indicate improved fit. Significant LMR-A and BLRT p values support a model with k profiles rather than k−1. Entropy values greater than 0.80 were considered acceptable, with values closer to 1 indicating more certain classification. Considering statistical fit, interpretability and classification quality, we selected the final solution and excluded profiles with very small class sizes (e.g., < 5% of the sample) or with indistinguishable mean structures across competency dimensions, to ensure stable and interpretable profiles.

Group differences in socio‑demographic characteristics across profiles were examined with chi‑square tests; effect sizes were quantified using Cramér’s V with conventional thresholds for small (0.10), medium (0.30), and large (0.50) effects [42]. Multinomial logistic regression was used to examine factors associated with profile membership. To enable all pairwise contrasts, two models were estimated: one with the ‘foundational’ profile as the reference category (yielding intermediate vs. foundational and advanced vs. foundational), and one with the ‘advanced’ profile as the reference (yielding intermediate vs. advanced).

Training‑needs ratings were compared across profiles using Kruskal–Wallis tests because distributions were ordinal and potentially non‑normal. A two‑sided p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance for all analyses.

Ethical considerations

The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Peking University Biomedical Ethics Committee (IRB00001052‑22013). Before starting the questionnaire, participants viewed an electronic information sheet and provided informed consent. Participation was voluntary and uncompensated, and respondents could withdraw at any time without penalty. All data were collected anonymously and stored under coded identifiers for research use only.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 4,223 SNCEs completed the anonymous questionnaire. After excluding 278 responses (6.5%) that did not meet the inclusion criteria, 3,945 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding a valid response rate of 93.4%. There were no missing data; all analyses were conducted on the complete-case dataset.

Latent profiles of core competencies

Latent profile analysis (LPA) was conducted to identify distinct competency profiles based on the eight dimensions of the Nursing Educators’ Core Competency Scale (NECCS). Models with two to five profiles were compared using multiple fit indices, likelihood ratio tests, and classification quality measures (Table 1). AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased as the number of profiles increased, but the rate of decline was marginal beyond the three-profile model. Both the LMRT and the BLRT were significant (p < 0.001) for models with two to five profiles, indicating improved fit over models with fewer profiles. Entropy values exceeded 0.80 in all solutions, with the highest value observed in the three-profile model (0.953). Considering statistical fit, parsimony, and interpretability, the three-profile model was selected for subsequent analyses. Classification accuracy for this solution was high, with average posterior probabilities (AvePP) ranging from 0.965 to 0.984 and odds of correct classification (OCC) between 27.6 and 61.5—well above the accepted thresholds (AvePP ≥ 0.80, OCC ≥ 5) (Table 2).

Distribution of core competencies across profiles

The three profiles exhibited distinct patterns of competency scores across all NECCS dimensions (Fig. 1). Differences among profiles were statistically significant for all dimensions (p < 0.001). Profile 1 (foundational competencies, n = 338; 8.6%) had the lowest scores (43.89 ± 10.01). Profile 2 (intermediate competencies, n = 1,696; 43.0%) had moderate scores (68.24 ± 6.08), while Profile 3 (advanced competencies, n = 1,911; 48.4%) had the highest scores (91.69 ± 6.17). The largest proportion of SNCEs fell into the advanced group, while the foundational group represented the smallest proportion.

Profile-based differences in socio-demographic characteristics

Chi-square tests indicated significant differences among the three profiles in age, education, professional title, length of employment, and prior training experience (all p < 0.05), with small-to-moderate effect sizes (Cramér’s V = 0.065–0.185) (Table 3). The advanced group had higher proportions of SNCEs aged ≥ 41 years, holding supervisor nurse titles, with longer employment duration, and with prior training experience. In contrast, the foundational group included more junior nurses, younger SNCEs, and those without prior training.

Factors associated with profile membership

Multinomial logistic regression (Table 4) identified educational level, professional title, length of employment, and training experience as significant predictors of profile membership; age was not significant. Compared with supervisor nurses, those holding junior or senior nurse titles were more likely to belong to the foundational group. SNCEs without prior training had markedly higher odds of being in either the foundational or intermediate groups compared to the advanced group. Shorter employment duration predicted intermediate group membership relative to the advanced group. Educational level also differentiated intermediate from advanced groups, with bachelor’s degree holders more likely to be in the intermediate group. The full regression results, including odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for all predictors, are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Profile-based differences in training needs

Perceived necessity of 13 training programmes was compared across profiles using Kruskal–Wallis tests. Significant differences emerged for all programmes (p < 0.05). Overall, SNCEs in the advanced group reported the highest training needs, followed by the intermediate group, with the foundational group rating most programmes lower in necessity. Training priorities also varied: the advanced group ranked ‘Curriculum and course design’ highest (75.0%), the intermediate group prioritised ‘Clinical teaching methods and techniques’ (58.7%), and the foundational group emphasised ‘Common pedagogical methods and instructional technologies’ (54.7%). Post-hoc comparisons indicated multiple pairwise differences between profiles for specific programmes, with results summarised in the heatmap (Fig. 2). The detailed data for each programme, including exact percentages and post-hoc grouping letters, are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Discussion

Using a person-centred latent profile analysis anchored in the WHO Nurse Educator Core Competencies, we identified three distinct competency profiles among specialist-nurse clinical educators. The selected three-profile solution showed robust between-profile separation and high classification accuracy, indicating meaningful subgroups rather than sampling fluctuation. Profile membership varied by education level, professional title, and training experience. Training needs also varied by profile, with advanced-profile educators reporting a higher overall demand for development and giving priority to curriculum and course design, while those in the intermediate and foundational profiles placed greater emphasis on clinical teaching methods and instructional technologies. Taken together, these results characterise a structurally heterogeneous educator workforce with divergent training needs and they support profile-responsive development, rather than one-size-fits-all upskilling, to strengthen specialist-nurse education.

Competency distribution across profiles and associated characteristics

Based on participants’ responses, the advanced and intermediate profiles accounted for 48.4% and 43.0% respectively, indicating that most clinical nurse educators demonstrated moderate-to-high levels of competency. The concentration of competency in the mid-to-high range is consistent with reports from academic and tertiary-hospital settings, where nurse-educator and preceptor competency is generally evaluated positively [23]. Consistent with this distribution, a large cross-sectional study in a university hospital likewise identified three discrete competency profiles and found that a large proportion of participants reported intermediate-to-advanced orientation competency [43].

The three profiles identified in this study differed in demographic characteristics and training exposure. In multivariable regression, professional title, employment duration and prior educator training were independent correlates of advanced-profile membership, whereas age was not. This pattern indicates that competency is shaped more by role exposure and formal preparation than by chronological ageing [44]. It is consistent with evidence that participation in educator development is associated with more effective clinical teaching and that higher competency clusters among educators who complete orientation or mentoring education and who hold more senior roles [23, 43]. Studies of clinicians transitioning into educator roles provide additional context, describing lower preparedness and marked transition shock driven by workload, limited pedagogical preparation and role ambiguity, which depresses capability early in the educator trajectory [45, 46]. Taken together, these findings point to training as a strong and actionable lever, and interventional evidence links educator education to subsequent gains in competency, supporting a causal pathway from educator preparation to learner outcomes [47]. Accordingly, systematic educator training and structured orientation should be treated as core requirements, and the present study provides empirical support for the necessity of orientation in strengthening the specialist-nurse educator workforce.

Domain-level strengths and gaps in educator competency

In this study, three profiles exhibited significant disparities across the eight competency domains; in domain-specific analyses, all profiles consistently ranked ethical/legal principles and professionalism as the highest-scoring domain. This finding is aligned with prior evidence emphasising strong professional-ethics competency among SNCEs [28]. Ethical competency has been conceptualised from both nursing and education perspectives [48]. In light of a changing clinical environment and rising professional stressors, ethics teaching is critical for developing healthcare professionals’ ethical competency [49]. From a nursing perspective, specialist nurses function as clinical experts and confront ethical decisions routinely in daily practice [50]; ethics education should therefore equip them to identify ethical problems and to develop ethical competency through guided practice and reflection [51]. From an education perspective, clinical educators’ ethical competency comprises a foundation of ethical values and attitudes and a personal commitment to ethical action [48]; it is evidenced by the promotion of equality, the capacity to make ethically sustainable decisions, and appropriate conduct in morally challenging situations [49, 52]. However, self-ratings in ethics and professionalism are vulnerable to social desirability and halo effects, and the accuracy of self-assessment in health professions is often limited, with overestimation more common among lower performers [53]. Accordingly, interpretation of the uniformly high scores should be cautious, and future evaluations should triangulate self-ratings with observed behaviours and multi-source feedback to establish convergent validity and mitigate self-assessment bias [53, 54].

Meanwhile, three groups of SNCEs all rate lowest on their “communication, coordination and partnership”. According to the World Health Organisation (WHO), this domain involves nurse educators demonstrating effective communication skills that promote collaborative teamwork and enhance partnerships among health professionals [13]. Effective communication is essential in clinical settings, as it influences the learning environment, motivates trainees, and enhances their acquisition of technical, psychological, and interpersonal skills [55]. The competency of communication and collaboration involves reciprocal interest or communication between two or more people, which is critical for the relationship with trainees [56]. However, a qualitative study focused on the teaching experience of those clinical educators has shown that they often face challenges due to a lack of time for communication [57], which may be the reason leading to potential deficits in confidence and relationship-building with trainees. Besides, Communication behaviours are also sensitive to psychological safety, since low safety climates suppress speaking up and timely clarification and thereby weaken collaboration [58]. Therefore, these findings underscore the need for administrators to ensure that SNCEs have sufficient time to engage in meaningful communication and collaboration with their trainees. Additionally, implementing targeted training programmes to enhance the communication and cooperation skills of clinical educators could further improve their effectiveness in fostering an inclusive and supportive learning environment.

Training needs by competency profile

When further exploring the training needs of SNCEs within three competency profiles, we find that higher-competency educators have a greater demand for training than their less competent peers. Similar findings have been reported in other settings; for example, a study in Thailand showed that although nurse educators rated their current competencies at a high level, they simultaneously expressed a desire to further strengthen these competencies [28]. This apparent paradox can be understood through several complementary theoretical perspectives. Self-determination theory (SDT) posits that individuals are motivated to grow and change by three inherent and universal psychological needs: competency, autonomy, and relatedness [59]. Higher-competency professionals, who already experience a strong sense of competency, are inclined to seek further training that preserves autonomy in decision-making, deepens mastery of advanced role components and sustains meaningful professional relationships; such participation is typically driven by autonomous motivation and alignment with personal goals rather than external pressures [60, 61]. This pattern is also compatible with mastery goal orientation, which predicts preference for challenging learning opportunities and deliberate practice in competency-based education contexts [62, 63]. By contrast, lower-competency groups may under-signal training needs when expectancy of success is low or when perceived costs are high. Situated expectancy–value theory predicts that participation hinges on perceived value relative to cost, and recent reviews emphasise that value, cost and contextual supports shape engagement and persistence [64]. Nursing continuing professional development syntheses similarly show that organisational culture, access, and support strongly influence willingness to engage, with barriers reducing uptake even when needs are present [65, 66]. In practical terms, these mechanisms imply that motivating the lower-competency subgroup is pivotal. Programmes should raise expectancy through scaffolded mastery experiences and credible feedback, increase perceived value by linking development to role requirements and career progression, and reduce perceived cost via protected time and streamlined access, while maintaining autonomy-supportive design to sustain intrinsic motivation [67, 68].

The three competency profiles assigned different priorities across the surveyed training projects. Among advanced-competency SNCEs, the most frequently cited training need was curriculum and course design. Effective curriculum design involves a structured, iterative process of problem identification, needs assessment, goal setting, strategy selection, implementation, and evaluation [69]. Unlike general nursing education, which primarily emphasises knowledge acquisition and skill development, specialist nursing education focuses on fostering critical thinking, leadership, and advanced clinical skills [70]. Advanced-competency SNCEs likely recognise the importance of refining curriculum design to meet the evolving demands of speciality nursing education. Future research should examine best practices in curriculum development for speciality nursing and assess the effectiveness of targeted training programmes in improving educators’ curriculum-design skills.

Among SNCEs in the intermediate competency group, clinical-teaching methods and techniques received the highest proportion of training-need endorsements, indicating a prioritisation of method-focused development at this stage. Workplace-based teaching in speciality contexts benefits from a repertoire that combines unstructured approaches—modelling, think-aloud reasoning, brief coaching and guided reflection—which can be integrated into routine encounters [71, 72]. Structured, portable methods with evidence of effectiveness include the One-Minute Preceptor microskills for focused on-shift teaching, SNAPPS for structured case discussion, and Peyton’s four-step approach for procedural instruction [73–77]. Supervision intensity can be modulated to task complexity and observed performance using entrustment frameworks and levels of supervision, including nursing adaptations of entrustable professional activities [78, 79]. Accordingly, faculty development for the intermediate group should prioritise practical rehearsal of these unstructured and structured approaches to enable consistent and time-efficient instruction during clinical service.

For SNCEs in foundational competency profiles, the highest training needs centred on common pedagogical methods and instructional technologies. While not specific to bedside teaching, this domain encompasses core pedagogical competencies such as organising instructional content, using multimedia resources, facilitating group learning, and applying digital tools in teaching. In contemporary nursing education, clinical educators are increasingly involved in structured teaching formats beyond traditional supervision—such as simulation-based instruction, clinical-skills workshops, and blended-learning modules. The increasing integration of digital learning tools has highlighted the importance of technological proficiency in nursing education [80, 81]. Innovative teaching methodologies such as problem-based learning (PBL) and flipped classrooms have demonstrated effectiveness in fostering critical thinking and bridging theoretical knowledge with clinical application [82]. However, SNCEs with foundational competency levels may lack prior exposure to, or training in, these methods, limiting their instructional effectiveness. Faculty-development programmes targeting foundational SNCEs should thus emphasise basic pedagogical principles, instructional organisation, and the practical application of educational technologies within clinical-teaching settings.

Implications for specialist nursing education and clinical practice

The present findings have several implications for specialist-nursing education. First, as educators involved in specialist programmes often supervise heterogeneous learner groups, clinical teaching bases should ensure that, when SNCEs are assigned to teach specialist nurses who already practise at an advanced level, a minimum competency threshold is met, particularly in communication, coordination and partnership, so that instructional quality is maintained across settings. Second, the associations between competency profiles and prior educator training highlight the importance of preparation; institutions may therefore prioritise structured orientation for newly appointed SNCEs and provide sustained continuing professional development, with engagement supports to encourage participation among lower-competency subgroups. Third, because training-need priorities differed across profiles, faculty development can remain uniform in standards while varying its emphasis by profile, thereby directing limited resources to domains of greatest marginal benefit and supporting all SNCEs to meet a shared performance threshold. Tailored programmes might adopt short, work-integrated modules; for foundational profiles, the focus may be basic pedagogy and digital fluency; for intermediate profiles, workplace teaching methods, calibrated supervision and routine formative assessment; for advanced profiles, curriculum and assessment design and programme evaluation. A cross-cutting component on communication, coordination and partnership should be embedded in all strands, and progress may be monitored with simple milestones and periodic re-profiling to adjust individual plans. Finally, given that the standards and expectations for teaching specialist nurses differ substantially from those for other learner groups, further research should pay more attention to this educator group to support standard-setting and targeted faculty development in specialist nursing education.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers several notable methodological strengths. It is among the few empirical investigations to apply Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) to examine SNCEs’ competencies within the context of specialist nursing education, enabling a nuanced, person-centred exploration of heterogeneous competency patterns. The large sample size (n = 3945) exceeded recommended thresholds for LPA, enhancing statistical power, precision of profile estimation, and representativeness. The use of a validated WHO-based competency scale with high internal consistency further ensured measurement reliability and contextual relevance. In addition, integrating LPA with supplementary statistical analyses allowed for a comprehensive understanding of both profile characteristics and their associated training needs.

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while the convenience-sampling strategy facilitated broad reach, it was non-random and may have introduced selection bias; therefore, generalisability to all clinical educators should be interpreted with caution. Second, because the survey link was distributed by participating institutions, the denominator for a response-rate calculation was not available. The response rate is therefore unknown, and non-response bias cannot be ruled out. Third, both competency and training needs were assessed using self-reported instruments: while the competency scale was validated, the training needs questionnaire was self-developed without formal psychometric evaluation, which may reduce measurement accuracy and consistency. Fourth, reliance on self-assessment rather than third-party evaluations may produce discrepancies between perceived and actual competency levels, and may introduce response bias that inflates ratings in socially valued domains; results should therefore be interpreted with caution. Another methodological consideration concerns common method bias, as both competency and training needs data were collected via self-report at a single time point. Although the two instruments served distinct measurement purposes, subsequent analyses examined relationships between them. No procedural remedies, such as temporal separation or alternative data sources, were applied; therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted with caution.

Finally, we did not conduct a formal a priori sample size calculation. Although the achieved sample size was adequate for latent profile analysis, the absence of predefined parameters may limit the robustness and generalisability of the results. Larger samples increase statistical power and can raise the likelihood of detecting statistically significant but practically trivial differences between competing models. To mitigate these risks, we used a transparent model selection procedure and reported classification diagnostics, including entropy, average posterior probabilities and odds of correct classification, to evaluate model quality and reduce the potential influence of sample size on the chosen profile solution.

Future studies could enhance methodological rigour by employing probability-based sampling, conducting formal sample size estimations, incorporating validated instruments for all key variables, and combining self-assessment with objective competency measures. Such refinements would further improve the reliability, validity, and applicability of research findings in specialist nursing education.

Conclusion

This study provides a person-centred analysis of the core competency profiles of clinical educators in specialist nursing education, revealing distinct competency patterns and training needs across subgroups. Three latent profiles—foundational, intermediate, and advanced—were identified, each associated with differing levels of self-assessed competency. Among the eight competency domains, professionalism, supervision and evaluation consistently received the highest ratings, while communication and coordination were rated the lowest, suggesting a critical area for targeted improvement. The findings further demonstrate that educational level, professional title, length of employment and training experience significantly influenced profile membership. SNCEs with higher academic qualifications and more senior titles were more likely to belong to the advanced competency group, while those without prior training were substantially more likely to fall into the foundational or intermediate profiles. These results highlight the importance of both formal education and structured teaching preparation in fostering instructional competency. Moreover, training needs varied by competency level. SNCEs in the advanced group prioritised curriculum and course design. Those in the intermediate group most frequently rated clinical teaching methods and techniques as highly necessary, while SNCEs in the foundational group reported greater needs for common pedagogical methods and instructional technologies. These insights underscore the necessity of developing tailored, competency-based professional development programmes that address the specific needs of educators at different stages of professional growth. Despite these contributions, the use of convenience sampling and reliance on self-reported data may limit the generalisability of the findings. Future research should incorporate objective assessments and probabilistic sampling strategies to further refine educator training models and support the advancement of specialist nursing education.
