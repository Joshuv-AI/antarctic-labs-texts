Event-related potentials reveal age-related differences in sensitivity to positive and negative feedback from social and nonsocial sources
Authors: Lindsay A Santacroce, C Santhiya Purohit, Kieryn E A Carlse, Benjamin J Dyson, Julia Spaniol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesUsing external feedback to adjust behavior relies on dopaminergic pathways that are sensitive to age-related change. Prior research using electrophysiological methods has yielded mixed findings regarding age differences in feedback processing. The current study was designed to test predictions of two influential theories—the dopamine hypothesis of aging and socioemotional selectivity theory—regarding the effects of age and feedback properties (valence and social content) on behavioral and electrophysiological markers of feedback processing.MethodsHealthy community-dwelling adults (47 younger, 48 older) completed a time-estimation task with social and nonsocial feedback stimuli while EEG data were acquired. Key outcome measures included behavior as well as feedback-sensitive event-related potentials (ERPs): P2, feedback-related negativity (FRN), and P300.ResultsIn both age groups, negative feedback elicited greater behavioral adjustments than positive feedback, and social feedback elicited higher overall performance than nonsocial feedback. Furthermore, all ERP components were sensitive to feedback valence and social content. Whereas both factors were more influential for older versus younger adults during early processing, valence was more influential for younger versus older adults during later processing.DiscussionUsing a well-powered design, this study shows that age differences in electrophysiological correlates of feedback processing exist even when younger and older adults show similar behavioral responses to feedback. Neither the dopamine theory of aging nor the socioemotional selectivity theory fully accounts for these complex findings, highlighting the need for theoretical refinement and demonstrating the utility of EEG methods in the cognitive neuroscience of aging.

Responding to feedback is critical for adaptive decision making across the lifespan. Imagine, for example, giving a presentation and noticing that some members of the audience are frowning and craning their necks. This negative social feedback may prompt you to adjust your behavior (e.g., increasing volume), which in turn may trigger positive feedback from the audience. The current study used behavioral and electrophysiological markers of feedback processing to examine the impact of feedback dimensions (valence and social content) in younger and older adults.

Feedback processing is an aspect of performance monitoring—the set of processes that enable decision makers to detect errors, register gains and losses, and engage cognitive control to adjust behavior (Ferdinand & Czernochowski, 2018). Two influential theories predict age-related changes in feedback processing. First, the dopamine hypothesis of aging (DHA) suggests that age-related decline in dopamine pathways contributes broadly to reductions in fluid cognition (Bäckman et al., 2010). Although recently challenged (Frank & Seaman, 2023; Juarez et al., 2019; Karalija et al., 2022), the DHA remains relevant for understanding age-related alterations in feedback processing, given the critical role of dopamine circuits in reward and motivation. Second, socioemotional selectivity theory (SST; Carstensen et al., 1999) suggests that the salience of socioemotional goals increases with age, producing a positivity preference in information processing. While support for the positivity effect has primarily come from research on attention and memory (Reed et al., 2014), it is less well established in the domain of feedback processing (e.g., Ferdinand & Kray, 2013). Given SST’s emphasis on socioemotional selectivity (also seen in related frameworks, such as strength and vulnerability integration theory; Charles, 2010), one would also predict age differences in response to feedback varying on the social dimension. Studies with younger adults have shown differential sensitivity to social (thumbs up/down) versus nonsocial (plus sign/minus sign) feedback stimuli (Braunwarth & Ferdinand, 2025; Pfabigan et al., 2019; Pfabigan & Han, 2019). If aging is associated with an increase in the saliency of socioemotional motives, these effects should be magnified in older adults (Ferdinand & Hilz, 2020; Gorlick et al., 2013; Nashiro et al., 2011). For example, Gorlick et al. (2013) found that age-related deficits in reward-based learning were reduced when emotional faces served as feedback stimuli, compared to nonsocial feedback (although task complexity modulated these feedback effects). However, behavioral data are silent on the neural basis of feedback effects in younger and older adults.

Insights into the neurobiological mechanisms of feedback processing have come from EEG studies examining the feedback-related negativity (FRN; Miltner et al., 1997), a negative-going event-related potential (ERP) component that peaks approximately 200–300ms following feedback presentation, is maximal over fronto-central sites, and is believed to reflect activity in the anterior cingulate cortex (Holroyd & Coles, 2002), which receives input from the dopamine system (Schultz, 2002). In younger adults, the amplitude of the FRN is typically modulated by feedback valence (negative > positive; Miltner et al., 1997), expectancy (unexpected > expected; Holroyd & Coles, 2002), and social content (social > nonsocial; Pfabigan et al., 2019; Pfabigan & Han, 2019). The FRN has been interpreted in terms of both conflict monitoring (Botvinick et al., 2004) and reinforcement learning accounts, the latter suggesting that the difference between expected and actual outcomes (i.e., reward prediction error) serves as a reinforcement learning signal (Holroyd & Coles, 2002).

Studies comparing the FRN in younger and older adults have typically shown reduced negative deflections or smaller valence effects on the FRN amplitude in older adults (e.g., Eppinger et al., 2008; Nieuwenhuis et al., 2002; Wild-Wall et al., 2009). These electrophysiological effects are often accompanied by age-related reductions in behavioral metrics of learning–findings that are consistent with the DHA. However, null effects of age have also been reported (e.g., Bellebaum et al., 2011; Ferdinand & Kray, 2013). This heterogeneity may partly reflect variability due to small sample sizes in the EEG literature (commonly <20 participants/age group) or variability in task parameters across studies. For example, Wild-Wall et al. (2009) observed an age-related reduction in the FRN during a time-estimation task, but in their study, older adults received negative feedback more often than younger adults, raising the possibility that the age difference was partly due to differences in feedback expectancies. In fact, a similar study that used adaptive feedback, holding expectancies constant, showed no significant age difference in the FRN (Ferdinand & Kray, 2013).

In addition to the FRN, studies of feedback processing often report the P300, a positive-going component that peaks approximately 300–500 ms post-feedback and is most pronounced over parietal sites (Polich, 2007), although a more frontal distribution has been observed in older adults (e.g., Ferdinand & Kray, 2013; Wild-Wall et al., 2009). Following the FRN, the P300 is considered to reflect more elaborative outcome processing and working memory updating (Polich, 2007). Studies examining the P300 in the context of feedback processing (e.g., Bellebaum et al., 2011; Ferdinand & Hilz, 2020; Ferdinand & Kray, 2013; Kardos et al., 2017) have yielded mixed findings. For example, Ferdinand and Kray (2013) time-estimation study observed greater P300 amplitudes for unexpected positive versus negative feedback in both age groups, but greater P300 amplitude for unexpected negative versus expected feedback in younger adults only. The latter finding may suggest that, due to working memory constraints, older adults focus selectively on task-relevant (positive) feedback. In an ERP study employing a probabilistic reward learning task in which feedback differed on valence and emotional content, P300 amplitudes were greater for negative than positive feedback in both younger and older adults, and emotional feedback elicited a larger P300 amplitude in older adults only, regardless of feedback valence (Ferdinand & Hilz, 2020). In summary, the P300 tracks feedback valence and socioemotional qualities of feedback stimuli, but its sensitivity to these feedback dimensions may differ across age groups and task contexts.

A final ERP component of interest is the P2, an early positivity typically observed around 180 ms that reflects attentional capture and early affective responses to stimuli (Cuthbert et al., 2000). While the early affective responses are key to feedback processing, to our knowledge, no prior studies have isolated age differences in the P2 from age differences in the FRN.

To summarize, previous research has shown that feedback processing is a critical element of adaptive decision making, depends on the integrity of dopaminergic circuits, and is sensitive to age-related changes. Given the dominance of SST in research on aging and motivation, further study of the role of socioemotional factors in feedback processing is needed. The ERP technique offers a window into early brain responses to feedback processing in younger and older adults, but existing studies have often used small samples that limit the robustness of statistical results. Furthermore, studies have often employed complex learning tasks in which age differences in feedback sensitivity may be confounded with other factors. Finally, to our knowledge, only one ERP study to date (Ferdinand & Hilz, 2020) has investigated the impact of socioemotional feedback dimensions on early brain responses in younger and older adults. Therefore, the current study enlisted a relatively large sample of younger and older adults, closely reproduced an experimental study design (Pfabigan & Han, 2019) that controlled outcome expectancies via performance-adaptive feedback, and, in addition to FRN and P300, separately examined the P2 component.

We aimed first to replicate Pfabigan and Han (2019) behavioral and EEG findings in younger adults: increased ERP amplitudes for social versus nonsocial feedback, greater behavioral adjustments following negative feedback, and higher estimation performance following social feedback. Critically, we proposed two sets of pre-registered hypotheses regarding age effects. First, we tested hypotheses derived from DHA (Bäckman et al., 2010), which predicted main effects of age (older adults < younger adults) on behavioral measures (i.e., trial-wise time estimation adjustments and overall time estimation performance) as well as feedback-related ERP amplitudes (i.e., P2, FRN, and P300). Second, we tested hypotheses derived from SST (Carstensen et al., 1999), which predicted a more complex pattern of age differences: reduced sensitivity to negative feedback (i.e., Age × Valence interaction), as well as increased sensitivity to social feedback (i.e., Age × Social Content interaction), in older versus younger adults, on behavioral measures and ERP amplitudes.

Method

All procedures were reviewed and approved by the Toronto Metropolitan University Research Ethics Board (no. 2023-203). Stimuli and de-identified data are posted on the Open Science Framework (OSF; https://osf.io/p8tby/). The current methods (based on Pfabigan & Han, 2019) were preregistered prior to the start of data collection (https://osf.io/tce2w), although some EEG preprocessing steps and ERP amplitude measurements deviated slightly from both the preregistration and Pfabigan and Han (2019) to accommodate the current study (for details, see Supplementary Material).

Participants

The sample included 47 younger adults (26 women, 20 men, 1 nonbinary; age range: 18-35) and 48 older adults (31 women, 16 men, 1 nonbinary; age range: 60–94). The target sample size of 48 participants per group was determined using MorePower 6.0.4 (Campbell & Thompson, 2012) to achieve 80% power to detect medium effects (ηp2 = 0.08) of the interaction of age (younger, older), valence (positive, negative), and social content (social, nonsocial) in a mixed analysis of variance with repeated measures on the last two factors, at alpha = 0.05. Data from seven additional participants were excluded based on pre-registered exclusion criteria: Failure to respond on >20% of the time estimation task trials, scoring below 23 on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; Nasreddine et al., 2005), or having >20% of trials rejected during EEG preprocessing (due to data acquisition problems, artifacts, etc.). All participants reported that they were fluent English speakers, had no neurological conditions (stroke, tumor, aneurysm, dementia, epilepsy, prolonged periods of unconsciousness, memory loss, or severe head injury), psychiatric disorders, learning disabilities, uncorrected vision problems, diabetes, or uncontrolled cardiovascular disease, and had not taken psychotropic medications (e.g., antidepressants, sleeping pills) in 6 months.

Procedure

After providing informed consent, participants completed a battery of cognitive and affective measures (see Table 1). They were seated ∼64 cm from a Dell 27” Monitor (P2723QE, 2160p 24-bit resolution) and were fitted with an EEG cap. Then they completed the time estimation task (Figure 1A) based on Pfabigan and Han (2019) study in which participants estimated the duration of one second (see also Miltner et al., 1997; Pfabigan et al., 2014, 2015, 2019). The task was built using Presentation software (version 23.1, Neurobehavioral Systems, Inc., Berkeley, CA, www.neurobs.com) and ran on a Dell Latitude 5430 laptop (12th generation Intel Core i5). Each trial began with a fixation dot for 1,400 to 1,600 ms, followed by an “X” for 250 ms, cueing the start of the time estimation. A blank screen was then presented for 1,750 ms, during which participants were instructed to press the 1 key on their keyboard after they believed one second had passed following the presentation of the “X.” Finally, each trial ended with a feedback stimulus presented for 1,000 ms before the next trial began automatically. Failure to respond within 1,750 ms timed out the trial and resulted in negative feedback. If the participant responded, they received either positive or negative feedback, indicating a correct or incorrect time estimation. Crucially, to ensure that participants received an approximately equal number of positive and negative responses, we used an adaptive staircase procedure. The initial time window for a correct time estimation was 1,000 ± 100 ms, which increased by 20 ms (1,000 ± 110 ms) after each incorrect response and decreased by 20 ms (1,000 ± 90 ms) after each correct time estimation. With this method, participants received positive feedback on an average of 46.96% (SD = 3.89%) of trials.

All stimuli were presented centrally on a gray screen. The fixation dot and “X” cue appeared in black 90-pt. Arial font. Feedback stimuli (Figure 1B) were taken from Pfabigan and Han (2019) and varied with respect to valence (positive, negative) and social content (social, nonsocial). The social stimuli were photographs of a thumbs up (positive) or thumbs down (negative), and the nonsocial stimuli were photographs of a plus sign (positive) or minus sign (negative). Originally created by Pfabigan et al. (2019), the social and nonsocial feedback stimuli were matched on size, luminance, and objective and subjective measures of visual complexity. All feedback images were grayscale and measured 157 px × 197 px (approximately 2.25° × 2.82° in visual angle). Social (S) and nonsocial (N) feedback were presented block-wise. The four 50-trial blocks were presented in random order, with the constraint that participants never started or ended with two of the same block types in a row. Participants received detailed task instructions and completed six social and six nonsocial practice trials to ensure they understood the task.

Following the time estimation task, participants rated the motivational significance (not motivating to very motivating) and the arousal (very calm to very arousing) of each feedback stimulus on 7-point Likert scales. Both younger and older adults rated positive feedback stimuli as more motivating than negative feedback stimuli; negative feedback stimuli were rated as more arousing than positive feedback stimuli, with greater arousal for social than for nonsocial feedback stimuli. These effects did not differ significantly for younger and older adults, although older adults rated the feedback stimuli as more arousing overall (see Supplementary Results for details).

Finally, participants completed the Mill Hill Vocabulary Scale (Raven, 1958), and older adults completed the MoCA (Nasreddine et al., 2005) to screen for possible cognitive impairment. At the end of the session, participants were debriefed and received a CAD$40 gift card for their participation.

Behavioral data analysis

Following Pfabigan et al. (2019), we examined two behavioral measures: time estimation adjustments, reflecting trial-by-trial influences of feedback, and interval length, reflecting overall time estimation precision at the block level. Time estimation adjustments were calculated as the absolute value of the difference between the response times from the current trial and the previous trial (RTtrial—RTtrial-1). Adjustments were classified as correct if the estimate was closer to the 1,000 ms goal than the previous trial, and incorrect if the estimate was further. These values were subjected to a 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 mixed ANOVA with age (younger adults, older adults) as a between-subjects factor, and valence (positive, negative), social content (social, nonsocial), and adjustment accuracy (correct, incorrect) as within-subjects factors. Interval length was determined as the average block-level time window to receive positive feedback from the adaptive staircase procedure, with larger time windows indicating lower estimation precision for that block. We analyzed these values with a 2 (age group) × 2 (social content) mixed ANOVA. Significant interactions were followed up with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc t-tests.

EEG collection and preprocessing

EEG was recorded using the mBrainTrain Smarting PRO (mBrainTrain, www.mbraintrain.com) wireless system with 32 electrodes embedded in a fabric cap and a sampling rate of 500 Hz. The event triggers embedded in the Presentation experiment were streamed to the mbtStreamer (version 4.1) software to precisely time-lock the feedback stimuli presentation within the EEG recording.

EEG data were analyzed using MATLAB’s EEGLAB/ERPLAB extensions (Delorme & Makeig, 2004; Lopez-Calderon & Luck, 2014). The initial preprocessing began with applying a high-pass (0.1 Hz) and a low-pass (30 Hz, roll-off 12 dB/octave) Butterworth filter on the raw data. The data were then re-referenced to the average of the posterior temporal electrodes (TP9 and TP10) in lieu of a mastoid reference. As recommended by Luck (2022), an extreme filtered dataset was created for independent component analysis (Bell & Sejnowski, 1995), by applying a high-pass (1.0 Hz) and a low-pass (30 Hz, roll-off 48 dB/octave) Butterworth filter, deleting extended time periods without events (i.e., breaks between blocks, etc.), and performing continuous artifact detection to reject amplitudes exceeding ±200 μV. Extended infomax ICA analysis was then run on this extremely filtered dataset, and the ICA weights were transferred back to the re-referenced dataset for further processing. The pop_iclabel and pop_icflag functions (Pion-Tonachini et al., 2019) were used to automatically classify the independent components and flag and remove those labeled as eye blinks with over 90% confidence. The feedback presentation events were binned into the four different feedback conditions (social + positive, social + negative, nonsocial + positive, nonsocial + negative). Segments were extracted starting 2,200 ms prior to feedback onset and lasting for 3,300 ms, using the 200 ms prior to stimulus onset for baseline correction (Pfabigan & Han, 2019). One final artifact correction was applied to the epoched data to reject amplitudes exceeding ±75 μV in the channels of interest before computing the averaged ERPs. An average of 1.85% (SD = 2.84%) of trials were rejected using these methods, leaving an average of 46.93 (SD = 7.39) social + positive trials, 51.26 (SD = 7.63) social + negative trials, 45.21 (SD = 7.93) nonsocial + positive trials, and 53.09 (SD = 8.22) nonsocial + negative trials for analyses.

ERP analyses

After computing the average ERPs for each condition, a Butterworth filter (20 Hz half-amplitude cut-off, 48 dB/oct roll-off) was applied to the ERP waveforms before extracting component information. The P2 component was defined as the positive peak between 150 and 300 ms after feedback stimulus onset at the fronto-central electrode cluster (Fz, Cz, FC1, and FC2 electrodes). The FRN component was defined as the negative peak between 200 and 350 ms after feedback stimulus onset at the fronto-central electrode cluster. The peak-to-peak FRN amplitude was calculated by subtracting the FRN (mean amplitude ±25 ms surrounding the negative peak latency between 200 and 350 ms after feedback stimulus onset) from the P2 amplitude at the fronto-central electrode cluster (Fz, Cz, FC1, and FC2 electrodes). The P300 component was defined as the positive peak between 250 and 600 ms after feedback stimulus onset at the parietal electrode cluster (Pz, CP1, and CP2 electrodes). The component latencies were individually determined for each participant as the absolute position of the component for each condition. The component amplitudes were calculated as the mean peak amplitude within ±25 ms of the average latency for each condition. P2, peak-to-peak FRN, and P300 amplitudes were each subjected to a 2 (age group) × 2 (valence) × 2 (social content) mixed ANOVA. Significant interactions were followed up with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc t-tests.

Time estimation adjustments

The 2 (age) × 2 (valence) × 2 (social content) × 2 (adjustment accuracy) mixed ANOVA on estimation adjustments revealed a significant main effect of valence, F(1, 93) = 369.32, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.80, with greater adjustments after negative (M = 251.31, SD = 63.29) than positive (M = 132.00, SD = 63.29) feedback. There was also a main effect of adjustment accuracy, F(1, 93) = 41.13, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.31, such that correct adjustments (M = 180.66, SD = 58.04) were smaller than incorrect adjustments (M = 202.64, SD = 58.04). Finally, there was a significant Valence × Adjustment Accuracy interaction, F(1, 93) = 212.44, p < .001, ηp2 = 0.70. Post hoc tests indicated that, following positive feedback, correct adjustments (M = 90.14, SD = 34.31) were smaller than incorrect adjustments (M = 173.96, SD = 51.29). Conversely, following negative feedback, correct adjustments (M = 271.28, SD = 91.06) were greater than incorrect adjustments (M = 231.44, SD = 82.17). There were no significant main effects or interactions involving age or social content (ps > .054).

Interval length

The 2 (age) × 2 (social content) mixed ANOVA on interval length revealed a significant main effect of social content, F(1, 93) = 8.15, p = .005, ηp2 = 0.08, with shorter intervals (i.e., higher estimation precision) in social (M = 147.30, SD = 61.66) compared to nonsocial (M = 155.65, SD = 61.66) feedback blocks. There were no significant effects involving age, ps > 0.649.

P2 amplitude

The ANOVA on P2 amplitudes yielded significant main effects of age and valence, as well as significant Age x Valence and Age x Social Content interactions. Post hoc tests revealed a significant effect of valence in older adults (positive: M = 12.28, SD = 5.14; negative: M = 10.74, SD = 5.06), t(47) = 5.32, p < .001, d = 0.28, but not in younger adults (positive: M = 6.30, SD = 5.14; negative: M = 5.96, SD = 5.07), t(46) = 1.34, p = .187, d = 0.07. Similarly, there was a significant effect of social content in older adults (social: M = 13.07, SD = 5.49; nonsocial: M = 9.95, SD = 5.18), t(47) = 5.54, p < .001, d = 0.57, but not in younger adults (social: M = 6.64, SD = 5.49; nonsocial: M = 5.61, SD = 5.18), t(46) = 2.09, p < 0.281, d = 0.20.

Finally, there was a significant Social Content x Valence interaction. Post hoc tests revealed that positive feedback elicited a greater P2 amplitude than negative feedback for nonsocial feedback (positive: M = 9.32, SD = 5.40; negative: M = 6.31, SD = 5.07), t(93) = 11.05, p < .001, d = 0.56, but the opposite was true for social feedback (positive: M = 9.37, SD = 5.41; negative: M = 10.45, SD = 5.72), t(93) = 4.15, p < .001, d = 0.21.

FRN amplitude

The results of the Age × Valence × Social Content ANOVA on peak-to-peak FRN amplitudes revealed significant main effects of age, valence, and social content. There were also significant Age × Valence and Age × Social Content interactions. For younger adults, the peak-to-peak FRN amplitude was significantly greater for negative feedback (M = 4.42, SD = 4.79) than for positive feedback (M = 2.60, SD = 5.04), t(47) = 4.89, p < .001, d = 0.34, but there was no significant valence effect for older adults (positive: M = 5.51, SD = 5.04; negative: M = 5.90, SD = 4.78), t(46) = 1.07, p = 1.000, d = 0.07. Whereas both age groups showed a greater peak-to-peak FRN for social feedback than for nonsocial feedback, this effect was stronger for older adults (social: M = 7.60, SD = 5.59; nonsocial: M = 3.81, SD = 4.41), t(47) = 7.74, p < .001, d = 0.71, than for younger adults (social: M = 4.24, SD = 5.59; nonsocial: M = 2.77, SD = 4.42) adults, t(46) = 2.97, p = .02., d = 0.28. Finally, there was a significant Social Content × Valence interaction. Post hoc tests revealed a greater peak-to-peak FRN for social negative feedback (M = 7.27, SD = 6.20) than for social positive feedback (M = 4.58, SD = 5.39), t(93) = 7.30, p < .001, d = 0.51. By contrast, there was no significant valence effect for nonsocial feedback (positive: M = 3.52, SD = 5.16; negative: M = 3.06, SD = 4.07), t(93) = 1.43, p = .936, d = 0.09. Probing this interaction differently, there was a stronger effect of social content for negative feedback (social: M = 7.27, SD = 6.27; nonsocial: M = 3.06, SD = 4.11), t(93) = 8.98, p < .001, d = 0.79, than for positive feedback (social: M = 4.58, SD = 5.45; nonsocial: M = 3.52, SD = 5.21), t(93) = 2.96, p = .023, d = 0.20.

P300 amplitude

The ANOVA on P300 amplitudes revealed significant main effects of age and valence, which were qualified by a significant Age × Valence interaction. Post hoc tests indicated that positive feedback (M = 9.77, SD = 5.13) elicited a greater P300 amplitude than negative feedback (M = 8.18, SD = 5.43) for younger adults, t(46) = 3.82, p < .001, d = 0.30, but not for older adults (positive: M = 12.20, SD = 5.14; negative: M = 11.88, SD = 5.43), t(47) = 0.93, p = .358, d = 0.05. Of note, the Age x Valence interaction was no longer significant when affective and cognitive measures were included as covariates (see Supplementary Table 5). Finally, there was a significant main effect of social content, such that the P300 amplitude was greater for social (M = 11.73, SD = 5.64) versus nonsocial (M = 9.32, SD = 5.19) feedback.

Discussion

The aim of the current study was to shed light on adult age differences in feedback processing, with a specific focus on the dimensions of valence and social content. Using both behavioral and electrophysiological measures, we sought to test pre-registered predictions derived from two influential theories, the dopamine hypothesis of aging (DHA; Bäckman et al., 2010) and socioemotional selectivity theory (SST; Carstensen et al., 1999). The DHA predicts that, compared to younger adults, older adults are generally less sensitive to feedback. By contrast, SST predicts reduced sensitivity to negative feedback, and heightened sensitivity to social feedback, in older compared to younger adults. To test these hypotheses, we evaluated the effects of feedback valence and social content on behavior and feedback-related ERPs in the context of a time estimation task (Pfabigan et al., 2019; Pfabigan & Han, 2019).

The behavioral results paralleled those of prior studies employing the same task with younger adults (e.g., Pfabigan et al., 2019; Pfabigan & Han, 2019). Feedback valence affected trial-by-trial behavioral adjustments, such that participants made greater adjustments immediately following negative versus positive feedback. Furthermore, time estimation precision was higher in blocks with social than with nonsocial feedback. Despite evidence for normative age-related deficits in fluid cognition in the current sample, there were no significant age differences in time estimation performance. Previous studies using time estimation tasks with younger and older adults had found age-related decrements in task performance (Ferdinand & Kray, 2013; Wild-Wall et al., 2009). Small methodological differences may help explain these discrepancies. Wild-Wall et al. (2009) task used fixed feedback criteria, which led older adults to receive negative feedback more often, whereas the current study used an adaptive procedure that may have minimized age differences. Furthermore, the current study featured only two feedback types per block (i.e., positive and negative feedback), whereas Ferdinand and Kray (2013) task included three feedback types (positive, negative, and intermediate), potentially increasing working memory load, particularly for older adults. Overall, the current behavioral results indicate that younger and older adults performed at a similar level and used feedback effectively to improve their time estimation.

In contrast to the behavioral data, the ERP analyses revealed age differences in feedback-related P2, FRN, and P300 components. The P2 component, associated with attentional capture and early affective responses to feedback (Cuthbert et al., 2000), was more pronounced for older adults overall. Older adults also showed more robust effects of valence (positive > negative) and social content (social > nonsocial) on P2 amplitude. For both age groups, social content boosted P2 amplitude for positive but not for negative feedback. Overall, the P2 results fail to support the DHA, which would have predicted reduced sensitivity to feedback in older adults. By contrast, the age-related increase in sensitivity to positive and social feedback is consistent with SST. Given the early processing stage captured by the P2, the age differences are less likely to reflect cognitive control effects and may instead be due to higher motivational salience of positive and social stimuli in older compared with younger adults (see also Riis et al., 2009).

The analysis of the peak-to-peak FRN—a component that tracks expectancy violations and is associated with reinforcement learning (Holroyd & Coles, 2002)—revealed the typical pattern of greater amplitude following negative than positive feedback. However, this valence effect was significant only for social feedback, and only for younger adults. Age-related reductions in valence effects on FRN have been reported previously (e.g., Eppinger et al., 2008; Nieuwenhuis et al., 2002; Wild-Wall et al., 2009), although the literature has been mixed (see, e.g., Ferdinand & Hilz, 2020; Ferdinand & Kray, 2013). As expected, the FRN was sensitive to social content in both age groups, with social feedback eliciting a stronger FRN than nonsocial feedback. Interestingly, this effect was especially pronounced in older adults. Overall, the FRN findings are not consistent with the predictions of DHA, and they are only partially consistent with SST, which would have predicted an Age x Valence effect in addition to the Age x Social Content effect. The dissociation between age differences observed for P2 and FRN underscores the importance of assessing these components separately (see also Potts et al., 2006).

Finally, the P300 component, considered to reflect elaborative outcome processing and working memory updating (Polich, 2007), showed greater P300 amplitudes for social than for nonsocial feedback, suggesting that social content exerts a sustained effect across processing stages in both age groups. In younger adults, the P300 also showed greater amplitude to positive feedback than negative feedback. This valence effect may reflect the fact that positive feedback is more goal-relevant because it provides unambiguous guidance for future behavior (repeat the response on the next trial), whereas negative feedback is ambiguous (speed up or slow down on the next trial; see also Ferdinand & Kray, 2013). As such, the lack of a significant valence effect on P300 amplitude in older adults may suggest a decline in the ability to extract task-relevant information during working memory updating. Of note, the Age × Valence interaction disappeared when controlling for cognitive and affective variables, suggesting that these factors may contribute to age differences in working memory updating. Overall, these findings contribute further nuance to the mixed P300 results reported in the aging literature (e.g., Bellebaum et al., 2011; Ferdinand & Hilz, 2020; Ferdinand & Kray, 2013; Kardos et al., 2017). Critically, they fail to support either DHA or SST predictions.

Implications for theory

Across behavioral and ERP measures, the current study yielded no evidence in favor of the DHA (Backman et al., 2010). Although ERP data provide no direct measure of neuromodulatory function, the modest age differences on behavioral and electrophysiological indices previously shown to be sensitive to dopaminergic activity add to a body of literature that casts doubt on the utility of DHA in explaining age differences in neurocognitive function (e.g., Juarez et al., 2019; Karrer et al., 2017; but see Karalija et al., 2024). It is an open question how older adults achieve younger-adult-like performance in tasks thought to depend heavily on the dopamine system. Of note, Karrer et al. (2017) reported that although dopamine transporters and receptors show significant age-related decline, dopamine synthesis capacity is preserved in aging. Preserved dopamine synthesis capacity may therefore serve as a compensatory mechanism that allows older adults to achieve younger-adult-like performance in goal-relevant contexts, such as when processing social information (Karrer et al., 2017). Finally, the current findings provide only partial support for SST (Carstensen et al., 1999) and related lifespan theories of emotion and motivation (e.g., Charles et al., 2010). Consistent with SST, older adults were more sensitive to feedback valence and social content during early feedback processing (P2). However, at subsequent processing stages (FRN, P300), valence effects were stronger in younger adults, whereas the effect of social content remained robust in both age groups. Thus, the relative influence of feedback valence and social content, and age differences therein, fluctuated across processing stages. Overall, these findings suggest that motivational theories such as SST are not well-suited, in their current form, to explain age differences in specific cognitive processes and their neural correlates. Going forward, it would be important to incorporate tenets of SST in neuroscientific theories that make testable predictions about the effects of motivational signals on distinct processing stages (e.g., stimulus evaluation, prediction error and reinforcement learning, context updating, long-term memory; see also Ferdinand & Czernochowski, 2018). Given the dissociations observed between the effects of emotional valence and social content, it would also be important for lifespan theories to differentiate more clearly between these distinct dimensions of motivation.

Limitations

One limitation of the current study is that social and nonsocial feedback stimuli may have differed on dimensions other than social content. Even though the stimuli were matched on visual complexity and were used successfully in prior studies (Pfabigan et al., 2019; Pfabigan & Han, 2019), it would be useful to examine whether the social content effects would replicate with different stimulus types. A second limitation was the lack of a neutral feedback condition, which could have helped pinpoint the specific locus of age differences in responses to negative versus positive stimuli (e.g., Ferdinand & Kray, 2013).

Conclusion

Overall, the current behavioral and ERP results converge with previous research examining the effects of feedback valence and social content during a time estimation task in younger adults (Pfabigan et al., 2019; Pfabigan & Han, 2019). Interestingly, whereas the behavioral results did not reveal age differences, age differences emerged in the ERP results, which showed that the time course of valence and social-content effects differed for younger and older adults. This finding highlights the importance of complementing behavioral assessments with electrophysiological measures to shed light on age differences in neurocognitive function. The findings also suggest that current theoretical frameworks (DHA, SST) cannot adequately explain age differences in feedback processing. On a practical level, this research may have implications for applications in the domain of human factors design. For example, the finding that social signals robustly enhance neural processing and behavioral performance in older adults could be leveraged to optimize fast and accurate responding in high-stakes environments (e.g., motor vehicle operation).
