Genome-wide identification and expression profiling of the ERF subfamily in Polygala tenuifolia seedlings under drought stress
Authors: Yujiao Sun, Dilare Tuohetuohuli, Jing Liu, Qianqian Wang, Zenghui Wang, Shanrong Han, Hong Zhang, Qi Xie, Yanjun Li, Shuai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPolygala tenuifolia Willd. (P. tenuifolia) is one of the source plants of the traditional Chinese medicine ‘Yuanzhi’ and is widely used in clinical practice. As a meso-xerophytic medicinal plant with strong environmental adaptability, it is listed as a nationally protected wild medicinal species in China. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying its drought tolerance remain unclear. Therefore, a genome-wide identification of the ERF subfamily in P. tenuifolia was conducted to provide candidate genes for drought adaptation research and functional validation.ResultsEthylene-responsive factors (ERFs) play important roles in plant responses to drought stress. In this study, 78 PtERF genes were systematically identified for the first time in P. tenuifolia. Phylogenetic analysis classified these genes into five subgroups (B1–B5), with members within the same subgroup exhibiting similar gene structures and conserved motifs. Collinearity analysis revealed that segmental duplication was the primary driving force underlying PtERF family evolution. Cis-acting element analysis revealed that PtERF promoter regions were enriched with drought-responsive cis-elements, including abscisic acid and methyl jasmonate (MeJA)-responsive elements. Transcriptome analysis combined with RT-qPCR verification identified five core drought-responsive genes, among which PtERF33 expression exhibited the highest level of upregulation. Further investigation showed that PtERF33 produced two alternatively spliced transcripts: the full-length PtERF33.1 transcript and the intron-retained PtERF33.2 transcript. These two transcripts exhibited opposite expression patterns under drought stress, suggesting that they may regulate drought responses through functional antagonism.ConclusionsThese findings provide a systematic basis for identifying drought-resistant ERF genes in P. tenuifolia and establish a foundation for clarifying the role of alternative splicing in drought adaptation in this species.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09298-9.

Introduction

Plants encounter complex environmental conditions throughout their life cycle. For example, abiotic stresses such as drought, cold, salinity and high temperature can limit plant growth and reduce crop yield [1]. Among these stresses, drought is considered one of the major constraints on agricultural productivity, often causing greater yield losses than all other environmental factors [2]. Since environmental conditions are continuously changing, plants are unable to avoid adverse environments. Therefore, they perceive diverse environmental signals and respond to stress through coordinated changes in gene expression, metabolism, and physiology [3]. Transcription factors (TFs)—key regulators of gene expression—function across multiple signalling networks. They selectively bind to promoter regions of target genes to regulate gene expression and diverse physiological processes, thereby enhancing plant stress tolerance [4]. Many studies reveal the roles of different TFs in plant stress signal transduction, including major gene families such as APETALA2/Ethylene Response Factor (AP2/ERF), NAC, bZIP, bHLH, WRKY and ABF [5].

The AP2/ERF gene family is one of the largest TF families in plants. Initially identified in Arabidopsis thaliana, it has since been characterised in major crops including tobacco, rice, maize and wheat [6–8]. In addition, members of this family have been implicated in regulating bioactive compound biosynthesis, plant growth and development, and stress responses in medicinal plants, including Panax ginseng, Salvia miltiorrhiza and herbaceous peony [9–11]. A defining feature of the AP2/ERF gene family is the presence of at least one conserved AP2 DNA-binding domain, comprising 58–70 amino acid (aa) residues arranged in three antiparallel β-sheets and a single α-helix [12]. Two conserved motifs—YRG and RAYD—are characteristic of this domain [13]. Based on the number and similarity of DNA-binding domains, AP2/ERF family members are classified into five subfamilies: AP2, DREB, ERF, RAV and Soloist [14]. The AP2 subfamily is distinct as it typically contains two complete AP2 domains, while the other subfamilies generally possess a single AP2 domain. RAV TFs differ from the DREB, ERF and Soloist subfamilies as they contain an additional B3 DNA-binding domain [15]. While the ERF and DREB subfamilies possess a single AP2 DNA-binding domain, they are distinguished by specific conserved residues. DREB-type genes contain valine and glutamic acid at the 14th and 19th positions of the conserved AP2 domain, respectively, while ERF-type genes contain alanine and aspartic acid at these positions [16]. The Soloist subfamily also contains a single AP2 domain, but its sequence is highly different from those of the other subfamilies [17]. AP2/ERF family members are key TFs in plants, and their functions are irreplaceable.

Some members of the ERF subfamily specifically bind to cis-acting elements such as the GCC-box or DRE, thereby regulating plant responses to biotic and abiotic stresses [18]. In recent years, numerous studies have demonstrated that the ERF family plays a central role in plant responses to drought stress [19]. For example, overexpression of AtERF1 in Arabidopsis has been shown to effectively enhance plant resistance to drought and salinity stress through its binding to GCC-box and DRE cis-acting elements [20]. In addition, under drought stress, ERF5 and ERF6 genes of Arabidopsis thaliana co-ordinately regulate plant growth and stress tolerance to adapt to adverse conditions [21]. Similar functional roles have also been identified in crops. For example, overexpression of the tomato-derived ERF TF JERF1 in rice significantly enhances drought tolerance via activating pathways involved in abscisic acid (ABA) and proline biosynthesis [22]. In addition, based on phylogenetic relationships, ERF subfamily proteins have been classified into six groups, B1–B6 [23].

Alternative splicing (AS) is a key mechanism in precursor mRNA processing in eukaryotes. By regulating mRNA stability, transport, localisation and translation, it contributes to maintaining the intracellular balance between functional and non-functional transcripts and substantially increases transcriptome and proteome diversity via altering the molecular properties of encoded proteins [24]. AS is generally divided into five basic types: alternative 5′ splice site, alternative 3′ splice site, cassette exon (exon skipping), intron retention (IR), and mutually exclusive exons. Among these, IR is the most prevalent type in plants [25].

Polygala tenuifolia is a perennial herb in the family Polygalaceae. Species of the genus Polygala contain various phytochemical constituents, including saponins, xanthones, oligosaccharide esters and flavonoids. They possess pharmacological activities such as antidepressant, cognition-enhancing and anti-tumour effects [26] P. tenuifolia and P. sibirica are included in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, with their dried roots used medicinally for calming the mind, resolving phlegm, restoring consciousness, detoxifying, and reducing swelling [27]. In China, P. tenuifolia is mainly distributed across provinces such as Shanxi, Shaanxi, Hebei, and Henan [28]. The high-quality material is reported from Yulin in Shaanxi, which is recognised as one of the major sources of Qin medicine in the province [29]. During the growth and development of P. tenuifolia, water availability is a key factor affecting its growth and medicinal quality [30]. Northern Shaanxi is a major cultivation region for P. tenuifolia, where spring and summer droughts often occur. Therefore, its growth and development are often constrained by drought stress, despite its moderate drought tolerance. While ERF TFs play important roles in drought resistance in many plant species, the functions and characteristics of ERF genes in the medicinal plant P. tenuifolia remain unknown.

In this study, ERF transcription factors from the AP2/ERF superfamily were identified using genomic data from P. tenuifolia. The identified ERF TFs were further analysed for phylogenetic relationships, physicochemical properties, conserved motifs, gene structure, synteny, and cis-acting regulatory elements. Based on the gene expression heatmap under drought stress, representative ERF TFs were selected for expression pattern validation using quantitative PCR (qPCR). ERF TFs associated with drought response were further confirmed, and their transcriptional changes and AS under drought stress were analysed using reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), providing a theoretical basis for further functional validation of ERF genes in drought tolerance of P. tenuifolia.

Materials and methods

Genome-wide identification and physicochemical properties of PtERF TFs

ERF TFs in P. tenuifolia were identified using a stepwise pipeline. The genome sequence and annotation files of P. tenuifolia were retrieved from public databases (BIG: CRA009096; TCMPG: TCMPG20196) [31]. First, the hidden Markov model (HMM) profile of the AP2 domain (PF00847) was retrieved from the Pfam v32.0 database (http://pfam.xfam.org/search) [32]. The local HMM search program was used to compare against the P. tenuifolia protein sequences with an E-value threshold of < e⁻5 to preliminarily identify PtAP2/ERF candidate genes [33]. Next, the NCBI-Conserved Domain Database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/bwrpsb/bwrpsb.cgi) was used to analyse the predicted P. tenuifolia AP2/ERF sequences and confirm the presence of the AP2 domain [34]. Proteins containing two AP2 domains or a B3 domain were excluded. As the ERF, DREB and Soloist subfamilies each contain a single AP2 domain, the remaining protein sequences were aligned using MEGA11.0 software (https://www.megasoftware.net/) [35]. Proteins with alanine and aspartic acid at the 14th and 19th positions of the AP2 domain, respectively, were identified as PtERFs. Finally, the SMART program (http://smart.embl.de) was used to verify complete AP2 domains [36]. Ultimately, the putative members of the ERF subfamily in P. tenuifolia were obtained.

The ExPASy ProtParam tool (http://web.expasy.org/ protparam/) was used to calculate the isoelectric point (pI), molecular weight (MW), and grand average of hydropathicity (GRAVY) of the PtERFs [37]. Subcellular localisation was predicted using WoLF PSORT (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp) [38]. Chromosomal location information for the PtERF genes, including chromosome number, length and gene start and end positions, was obtained from the P. tenuifolia genome annotation file. TBtools software was used to visualise the chromosomal distribution of AP2/ERF genes in P. tenuifolia [39], and the genes were designated PtERF –PtERF78 according to their chromosomal positions.

Classification and phylogenetic analysis of PtERF protein

Multiple sequence alignment of ERF proteins from Arabidopsis and P. tenuifolia was performed using the Clustal W program in MEGA11 [40]. A neighbour-joining phylogenetic tree was constructed with 1000 bootstrap replicates. Arabidopsis ERF protein sequences were obtained from the TAIR database (http://www.arabidopsis.org/) [41]. The phylogenetic tree was visualised and annotated using iTOL (https://itol.embl.de) [42]. Based on the classification of AtERF proteins, the phylogenetic tree was used to classify ERF family members in P. tenuifolia.

Conserved motif, gene structure, promoter, synteny, and gene duplication events analysis of PtERFs

Conserved motifs were identified using the online MEME suite (https://meme-suite.org/meme/tools/meme) [43]. TBtools software was used to visualise conserved motifs and gene structures. The 2.0-kb genomic sequence upstream of the transcription start sites of PtERF genes was submitted to the PlantCARE database (https://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be/webtools/plantcare/html/) to predict cis-acting regulatory elements [44]. Genome and GFF3 annotation files for Arabidopsis thaliana, Oryza sativa, Zea mays and Glycine max were obtained from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [45]. Intra- and interspecific collinearity analyses were performed using MCScanX implemented in TBtools [46]. The Circos program was used to generate collinearity maps of PtERF genes and to identify segmentally duplicated gene pairs [47]. To investigate selection pressure acting on PtERFs, TBtools was used to calculate non-synonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates for segmentally duplicated gene pairs.

Plant materials and treatments

P. tenuifolia seedlings were cultivated in a greenhouse using vermiculite as the growth medium. The greenhouse conditions were maintained under a 16 h light/8 h dark photoperiod at 25 °C, with a light intensity of 12,000 lx and relative humidity of ~ 65%. After 40 days of cultivation, uniformly growing and healthy seedlings were selected for drought stress treatment P. tenuifolia seeds were obtained from Zizhou Panyang Planting and Breeding Co., Ltd., and taxonomically identified by Professor Hong Zhang. Drought stress was imposed using a hydroponic system, in which seedling roots were transferred into a 20% PEG6000 solution. Leaf samples were collected at 0, 1, 3, 6, and 9 h after treatment (0 h served as the control), rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. Three biological replicates were used for each treatment.

Measurement of phenotypic and physiological indicators

To observe stomatal status, epidermal layers from the same region of P. tenuifolia leaves were carefully peeled using fine-tipped forceps and mounted as temporary slides. Stomatal condition in control and drought-stressed leaves was examined under an upright microscope. Five physiological parameters were measured to evaluate the effects of drought stress on the seedlings. Proline content was determined using the acidic ninhydrin colourimetric method [48]. Other antioxidant-related parameters were measured using commercial assay kits. These included total superoxide dismutase (SOD; Total SOD Assay Kit, NBT method; Beyotime, S0109), Peroxidase (POD; POD Assay Kit; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A084-3–1, plant samples), catalase (CAT; Catalase Assay Kit; Beyotime, S0051), and malondialdehyde (MDA; MDA Assay Kit, TBA method; Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, A003-1). All physiological mea- surements were performed using three independent bio- logical replicates, with each biological replicate analyzed in technical triplicate. Statistical analysis and graphical visualization were performed using Origin 2021.

Transcriptomics analysis

For transcriptomic sequencing, leaf samples were collected from the control group (DS0) and the 20% PEG6000-treated drought-stressed group (DS). Each treatment comprised three biological replicates (DS0-1, DS0-2, DS0-3; DS1-1, DS1-2, DS1-3; DS3-1, DS3-2, DS3-3; DS6-1, DS6-2, DS6-3; DS9-1, DS9-2, DS9-3). cDNA library construction and RNA-seq were performed using Novogene. The transcriptome data were deposited in the NCBI database under accession number PRJNA1417523.

Raw sequencing data were quality-controlled and filtered using CASAVA software. The resulting high-quality reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 v2.0.5 [49]. Differential gene expression analysis between groups was performed using DESeq2 v1.20.0, with a threshold of |log2(foldchange)|≥ 1 and padj < 0.05 [50]. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were conducted using clusterProfiler v3.8.1, with a significance threshold of padj < 0.05 [51].

Gene expression analysis

Expression levels (FPKM values) of the 78 PtERF genes at different time points under drought stress were extracted from the previously generated transcriptome dataset of P. tenuifolia. PtERF genes with FPKM value ≥ 10 in at least one time point were retained, and FPKM values were log2-transformed for heatmap visualisation. TBtools was used to construct expression heatmaps and perform cluster analysis to visualise the up- and downregulation of PtERF genes under drought stress. An FPKM value < 1 was considered as no expression.

DEGs were filtered at padj < 0.05. Upregulated genes with log2FC > 2 and FPKM ≥ 10 across all treatment groups were ranked in descending order on maximum log2FC, and the top 50 genes were selected. Downregulated genes with log2FC < −2 in at least one comparison and FPKM ≥ 10 in the control group were ranked in ascending order based on minimum log2FC, and the top 50 genes were retained. Expression heatmaps were constructed based on FPKM values. DEGs information is provided in Table S4.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, 9109), and RNA concentration and integrity were measured. Subsequently, cDNA was synthesised through reverse transcription using the One-Step gDNA Removal Kit (AT341, TransGen Biotech, China). qPCR was performed using TransStart® Top Green qPCR SuperMix Kit (AQ131-01, TransGen Biotech, China) with PtGAPDH as the internal reference gene [52] on a Bio-Rad CFX96 system, with each reaction run in triplicate. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method based on cycle threshold values [53]. Statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA in SPSS 27.0, and graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0. All primers were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 (Additional file 1: Table S1).

Alternative splicing analysis of PtERF genes

Based on RT-qPCR results, PtERF33, which showed significant expression differences and a trend most consistent with the transcriptome data, was selected for AS analysis. Using RNA-seq data, the Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) was employed to examine splicing patterns of the candidate gene [54]. To validate PtERF33 transcripts, SnapGene software was used to design upstream and downstream primers spanning two exons flanking an intron in the PtERF33 genomic sequence. Semi-quantitative RT-PCR was used to simultaneously detect the full-length transcript and the IR AS variant. PtGAPDH was used as an internal reference gene to normalise cDNA samples, and transcript levels were assessed using grayscale analysis of electrophoretic bands.

RNA samples from different time points (0, 1, 3, 6, and 9 h) were reverse transcribed into cDNA. PCR amplification was performed using gene-specific primers for the internal reference and target transcript with a high-fidelity enzyme (PrimeSTAR® Max DNA Polymerase, TaKaRa). PCR products were electrophoresed on a 1% agarose gel containing ethidium bromide, and AS isoforms were visualised using the ChemiDoc XRS + imaging system.

To quantify the relative expression levels of the two transcript variants, 1 μg of total RNA from each sample was reverse transcribed into cDNA. qPCR was performed using PtGAPDH as the internal reference to examine the expression of the full-length PtERF33 transcript and the IR transcript under drought stress. Primers were designed using Primer Premier 5.0 (Additional file 1: Table S1).

Result

Identification, physicochemical properties, and subcellular localisation of PtERF TFs

ERF TFs in P. tenuifolia were identified by performing an HMM search of protein sequences using the AP2 domain HMM profile (PF00847). In total, 245 putative AP2/ERF TFs were identified in the P. tenuifolia genome. Subsequently, the NCBI-CDD was used to identify the number of AP2 domains, and only genes containing a single AP2 domain were retained (Fig. S3C). Using ERF-specific structural features—including a single AP2 domain with alanine and aspartic acid at the 14th and 19th positions, respectively (Fig. S2)—we identified 78 PtERF family members.

All predicted PtERF TFs were designated PtERF1–PtERF78 according to chromosomal order (Fig. S1). Among these, 77 PtERFs genes were unevenly distributed across 19 chromosomes. Chromosome Chr 2 is the largest, containing nine PtERFs, while Chr 4, Chr 11 and Chr 17 are smaller, each containing two PtERFs. PtERF proteins ranged in length from 87 (PtERF25) to 518 (PtERF12) aa. PI value ranged from 4.80 (PtERF11) to 10.79 (PtERF25). MW, aliphatic index and GRAVY ranged from 9.942 (PtERF25) to 57.916 (PtERF12) kDa, 41.29 (PtERF51) to 78.51 (PtERF25), and −1.024 (PtERF69) to −0.364 (PtERF20), respectively. Five ERF TFs exhibited instability indices < 40 and were classified as stable proteins, while the remaining 75 were unstable proteins (instability index > 40). All proteins exhibited negative GRAVY values, consistent with hydrophilic proteins. Subcellular localisation prediction using WoLF PSORT online showed that most proteins (70/78) are located in the nucleus. Additional file 2: Table S2 provides detailed information on the 78 PtERF TFs.

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

Previous studies have identified the AAEIRD motif as a signature motif of ERF subfamily members [16]. The multiple sequence alignment results showed that alanine (A) at position 14 and aspartic acid (D) at position 19 within the AP2 domain were highly conserved across the 78 PtERF protein sequences (Fig. S2). This finding further verified the characteristic of the AAEIRD motif in the ERF subfamily. In addition, all sequences contained a conserved WLG motif within AP2 domains. Conserving aa residue and characteristic motifs likely regulate PtERF binding to DRE/GCC elements and protein–protein interaction within the signalling pathway [13].

Based on the classification of Arabidopsis ERF family members, the phylogenetic analysis revealed that PtERF genes can be divided into B1–B5 subgroups (Fig. 1). No PtERF gene exhibited homology with B6 subgroup genes, including AT1G49120. Among these, group B3 is the largest, comprising 30 PtERF members, while group B2 is the smallest, with 8 PtERF members. Gene structure and conserved motif analyses

Gene structure analysis results showed that (Fig. S3D), with the exception of PtERF12, which contained six introns, all other PtERFs ranged from 1–2. Most PtERFs in the B1, B3 and B5 subgroups are intron-less.

Conserved protein motifs are usually related to specific functions. Fig. S3B shows 10 conserved motifs. Motif 1 and 2 exist in all PtERF TF members and represent the most conserved motifs. B4 subgroup contains only motifs 1 and 2. Members of the B3 subgroup contain the widest diversity of conserved motifs (motifs 1–8). Notably, motifs 3 and 6 are unique to this subgroup. Members of the B1, B2 and B5 subgroups contain 1–4, 2–3 and 3–4 motif types, respectively. Among these, motif 9 and motif 10 are restricted to B1 and B2, respectively. Members in the same subgroup exhibit similar motif types, numbers and positional distribution sequences.

Gene duplication events and synteny analysis

Gene expansion resulting from gene duplication occurs via two main patterns. The first type is tandem duplication, where two genes are located on the same chromosome and separated via a few loci (gene positions on the chromosome < 100 kb). Segmental duplication represents a second mechanism of gene family expansion [55]. In the P. tenuifolia genome, 21 tandemly duplicated ERF gene family members (21/78, 26.92%) were identified. These PtERF genes are distributed across eight tandem duplication clusters on seven chromosomes, namely: chromosomes 2, 3, 10, 12, 14, 15 and 16 (Fig. S1). Notably, all genes exhibiting tandem duplication belong to the ERF subfamily groups B1, B2 and B3.

Segmental duplication within the ERF gene family in the P. tenuifolia genome was analysed using a Circus diagram (Fig. 2A). In total, 44 segmentally duplicated gene pairs were identified in the P. tenuifolia genome, involving 51 ERF genes (51/78, 65.38%). Ka to Ks substitution ratio is a commonly used metric for assessing selection pressure. Ka/Ks = 1 indicates neutral selection, Ka/Ks > 1 indicates positive selection and accelerated evolution, and Ka/Ks < 1 indicates purifying selection [56]. Ka, Ks and Ka/Ks values were calculated for 44 collinear PtERF gene pairs (Additional file 3: Table S3). Ka values exhibited a peak within 0.00–0.57, Ks values within 0.01–3.67 and the peak of the Ka/Ks values fell within the range of 0.00–0.88. Cis-acting element analysis of PtERF gene promoters

To understand the regulatory networks of PtERF genes, putative cis-acting elements were predicted in the 2 kb promoter regions upstream of each gene (Fig. 3). After excluding basic elements (TATA-box, CAAT-box) and elements with unknown functions, 57 cis-acting element types were identified. These were classified into four categories: light-responsive (27 types), plant development-related (11 types), stress-responsive (11 types), and hormone-responsive (9 types). Among these, light-responsive elements such as Box4 and G-Box were the most abundant in both type number and frequency. Several elements are known to be associated with drought stress responses, including ABRE, MBS, ARE, CGTCA-motif, and TGACG-motif, and are present in many PtERF promoters [57]. The most frequently identified elements were G-Box (295), ABRE (285), Box4 (277), ARE (164), CGTCA-motif (133), and TGACG-motif (133). These results suggest that PtERF genes may be involved in photosynthesis, hormone signaling, stress responses, and plant growth and development, potentially with temporal and tissue-specific expression patterns.Fig. 3Cis-acting element prediction in the promoter region of the ERF subfamily genes in P. tenuifolia. Effects of drought stress on Polygala tenuifolia

Following treatment with 20% PEG6000 to simulate drought stress, phenotypic and physiological changes were observed in P. tenuifolia seedlings. Figures 4a–b show phenotypic changes—including mild wilting, slight leaf yellowing and partial stomatal closure—were evident within 1 h of treatment. Contents of proline, MDA, SOD, CAT and POD were measured from 0–9 h treatment period. Figures 4c–g show that proline content in P. tenuifolia leaves decreased 1 h after drought treatment and subsequently increased after 3 h. MDA content increased slightly at 1 h after treatment and increased significantly at 6 h, suggesting that severe lipid peroxidation damage occurred during the later stage of stress. Furthermore, SOD activity gradually increased from 1–3 h, dropped sharply after 3 h and began to rise significantly after 6 h, suggesting the initiation of an adaptive antioxidant defence. However, CAT and POD activities showed no significant changes. Transcriptome analysis based on RNA-sequencing data

A total of 15 cDNA libraries (control DS0 and four drought-stress groups DS1, DS3, DS6, DS9; three biological replicates per group) were constructed. Sequencing generated an average of 45.8 million clean reads per library, with Q20 > 97%, Q30 > 95%, and GC content ranging from 42 to 44% (Table S5). Over 79% of clean reads were mapped to the P. tenuifolia reference genome, of which approximately 74% were uniquely mapped (Table S6). High reproducibility was indicated by Pearson correlation coefficients (r ≥ 0.8; Fig. 5A) and clear inter-group separation in PCA (PC1 > 40% variance; Fig. S5A), confirming the suitability of the data for subsequent differential expression analysis.

A total of 29,447 DEGs were identified by comparing control (DS0) with drought-treated samples at four time points. The Venn diagram (Fig. 5B) showed 5,442 (DS1), 9,396 (DS3), 7,386 (DS6), and 7,223 (DS9) DEGs per comparison, with 1,497, 2,782, 891, and 1,199 unique DEGs, respectively, and 1,446 core DEGs consistently identified across all four comparisons, suggesting a potential role in drought tolerance. Volcano plots (Fig. S5B–E) revealed up-/down-regulated DEGs of 3,558/1,884 (DS1), 4,799/4,597 (DS3), 3,427/3,959 (DS6), and 3,154/4,069 (DS9). DS3 exhibited the most upregulated DEGs, approximately 1.5-fold more than DS9. Downregulated genes outnumbered upregulated genes in DS6 and DS9, which may correspond to decreased leaf water content as drought stress duration increased [58].

To explore DEG functions under drought stress, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. GO terms were classified into three categories, and the top 30 enriched terms were visualized in scatter plots (Fig. S5F–I). Specifically, 67 DEGs in DS0 vs DS1 were enriched in sequence-specific DNA binding (GO:0043565, padj ≤ 0.05); 131 DEGs in DS0 vs DS3 were enriched in heme binding (GO:0020037) and tetrapyrrole binding (GO:0046906); 98 DEGs in DS0 vs DS6 were enriched in oxidoreductase activity (GO:0016705); and 125 DEGs in DS0 vs DS9 were enriched in heme binding and tetrapyrrole binding.

Subsequently, KEGG pathway analysis was performed on the DEGs. Based on the KEGG enrichment results, the 20 most significant KEGG pathways were selected and visualised using bar charts to display them (Fig. 5C–F). In the DS0 vs DS1 and DS0 vs DS3 comparisons, 88 and 162 DEGs, respectively, were enriched in the Plant hormone signal transduction pathway (ath04075), which represented the most significantly enriched pathway in the groups (Fig. 5C–D). In the DS0 vs DS6 and DS0 vs DS9 comparisons, the photosynthesis pathway (ath00195) was the most significantly enriched one, containing 51 and 58 DEGs, respectively. Similarly, the plant hormone signal transduction pathway (ath04075) exhibited the largest number of DERs with 130 and 124 DEGs significantly enriched in the two groups, respectively (Fig. 5E–F).

Expression profiles of P. tenuifolia ERF subfamily genes under drought stress and RT-qPCR analysis.

To evaluate the potential functions of ERF subfamily genes, especially their roles in environmental stress responses, their expression profiles in P. tenuifolia were analysed using whole transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) data and RT-qPCR. The transcriptome-based expression heatmap clearly shows the expression patterns of 41 PtERF (FPKM ≥ 10) gene family members across different time points under drought stress treatment (Fig. 6). Cluster analysis classified these genes into three major groups based on their expression patterns. For example, genes located at the top of the heatmap, including PtERF70, PtERF39, PtERF59, PtERF24, PtERF32, PtERF49, PtERF27, and PtERF48, consistently show stable expression across control and drought stress conditions, with PtERF59 and PtERF39 peaking at 1 h and 3 h post-stress, respectively, before declining.

In contrast, several genes at the bottom of the heatmap, including PtERF34, PtERF74, and PtERF26, exhibited suppressed expression as early as 1 h following drought stress, whereas PtERF38 and PtERF9 were rapidly and strongly induced within 1 h. Several genes located between the upregulated and downregulated clusters included PtERF19, PtERF41, PtERF44, PtERF30, PtERF37, and PtERF33, and they exhibited diverse expression patterns. Specifically, PtERF19 and PtERF41 exhibited high expression at 1 h, followed by a significant decline after 3 h; PtERF30 peaked at 3 h; PtERF37 was significantly upregulated between 3 and 6 h; and PtERF33 expression gradually increased from 1 to 6 h before decreasing at 9 h. These findings indicate that PtERF genes play diversified regulatory roles in the drought stress response of P. tenuifolia.

Furthermore, to verify the reliability of the transcriptome sequencing data, nine PtERF genes were randomly selected based on the expression patterns in the heatmap for qPCR validation analysis: PtERF30, PtERF9, PtERF59, PtERF37, PtERF19, PtERF39, PtERF38, PtERF41 and PtERF33 (Fig. 7). Among these genes, PtERF30 and PtERF33 exhibited their highest expression levels at 6 h of treatment, showing 4.95- and 31.2-fold increases relative to that of the control, respectively. In contrast, PtERF39, PtERF37 and PtERF38 reached peak expression at 3 h, with expression levels that were 5.77-, 10.99- and 5.78-fold higher than those of the control, respectively. PtERF9, PtERF59, PtERF19 and PtERF41 exhibited their highest expression levels at 1 h after treatment, showing 3.78-, 2.24-, 1.25- and 4.47-fold increases relative to that of the control, respectively. These genes were upregulated at different time points after stress; however, their expression levels declined by 9 h after treatment. The RT-qPCR results were largely consistent with those of the RNA-seq heatmap expression patterns, thereby confirming the reliability of the transcriptome sequencing data.

Beyond examining the expression profiles of ERF family genes, the top 50 upregulated and top 50 downregulated DEGs were further screened. After excluding several genes with unknown functions, all remaining genes were identified as TFs. An expression heatmap was subsequently constructed based on their family annotation information (Fig. S6).

The heatmap analysis showed that most of the upregulated TFs exhibited low expression levels in the control group (DS0) and during the early drought stress stage (DS1, 1 h). Their expression levels increased markedly at 3 h and remained elevated from 6–9 h of treatment. These genes were mainly classified into the ERF, MYB, NAC and bHLH families, and also included members of the HD-ZIP, WRKY, LBD and other TF families.

The downregulated TFs exhibited high expression levels in the control group and during the early stage of drought stress. Their expression levels gradually decreased with increasing stress duration. Most genes showed low expression levels during the middle and late stages of stress, whereas only a few genes exhibited a slight increase in expression at the late stage. These genes were distributed across multiple TF families, including bZIP, MADS-box, C3H and ERF.

Transcript analysis and expression levels of the PtERF33 gene

IGV visualisation of the RNA-seq data revealed read coverage within the intronic region of the PtERF33 gene (Fig. 8a), suggesting that PtERF33 may possess an IR AS isoform. Quantitative RT-PCR was then performed to detect transcript variants. Agarose gel electrophoresis showed two specific bands of the expected sizes (147 and 354 bp) (Fig. 8c). The two bands represent the full-length transcript and the IR alternative splice variant, respectively. The two isoforms were designated PtERF33.1 and PtERF33.2, and the sequencing results are shown in Fig. S7. Notably, the expression level of PtERF33.1 was significantly higher than that of the IR variant PtERF33.2. To quantify the expression level of the IR transcript PtERF33.2, upstream and downstream primers targeting the 5′ exon and intronic region were designed for qPCR analysis. The results (Fig. 8d & e) showed that the expression level of PtERF33.2 continuously declined within 3 h of drought stress treatment, reaching ~ 9.29% of the control level, followed by varying degrees of recovery. Discussion

The AP2/ERF TF family is functionally diverse and plays key roles in plant growth, development, stress responses, and metabolite biosynthesis. Most studies have focused on its identification and characterization in model and crop species. Polygala tenuifolia, an important medicinal herb from Shaanxi Province, faces increasing demand due to its pharmacological properties. However, environmental constraints such as drought and soil salinization have driven its cultivation under artificial conditions [60]. Although P. tenuifolia seedlings exhibit a certain degree of drought tolerance, the identification and functional characterisation of TFs that enhance drought tolerance in this species represent an effective strategy for improving yield. Therefore, a genome-wide identification and analysis of the ERF subfamily in P. tenuifolia was conducted, which has not been previously reported. This study provides a preliminary basis for future research on the functions of ERF genes in drought tolerance regulation in P. tenuifolia and other medicinal plants.

Characteristics and major evolutionary patterns of PtERF subfamily transcription factors

In this study, 78 PtERF genes were identified. Based on the classification of AtERF genes, these 78 PtERF genes were divided into B1–B5 groups. The largest group (B3) contains 48 ERF proteins (30 PtERFs and 18 AtERFs), suggesting that the B3 group may have undergone frequent gene duplication events during evolution and retained more genes. The variation in MW (9.942–57.916 kDa) and pI (4.80–10.79) of P. tenuifolia ERF proteins indicates functional diversity. The predicted subcellular localisation shows that PtERF TFs are mainly located in the nucleus, possibly involved in transcriptional and post-transcriptional regulatory processes and consistent with their fundamental role as TFs [61]. It is well established that gene structure analysis plays a crucial role in elucidating gene function. Generally, ethylene response factors are characterised by having few introns [62]. In this study, 71.79% of PtERF members lack introns, whereas the remaining members contain between one and six introns. This pattern is consistent with observations in other plant species. Previous studies show that the number and distribution of introns are associated with selective pressure during plant evolution. Such selective pressures may drive genes to acquire or lose introns, thereby generating diverse exon–intron structures that support functional differentiation [63]. Therefore, intron loss in members of the P. tenuifolia ERF subfamily during evolution may have influenced their functional properties. Conserved domains and motifs of TFs mediate essential functions, including DNA binding, transcriptional regulation and protein–protein interactions [64]. Most PtERF genes contain motif-1, motif-2, motif-3 and motif-4 related to the AP2 domain, indicating that these genes are highly conserved during evolution. Additionally, motifs 3, 6, 9 and 10 were specifically detected in different groups of the PtERF subfamily, suggesting that they play important roles in this subfamily. Overall, genes within the same subgroup of the P. tenuifolia ERF family exhibit similar conserved motifs and gene structures, indicating that they may share similar biological functions.

Tandem and segmental duplications, as the main gene duplication modes, may drive the formation of novel genes, thereby helping plants survive in different environments [65]. In this study, 44 pairs of segmentally duplicated genes were identified among the 78 PtERFs. The number of segmental duplication events was significantly higher than that of tandem duplication events. Therefore, segmental duplication appears to play a key role in the evolution of the PtERF gene family. This finding further indicates that the widespread intron loss observed in most PtERF members may be associated with segmental duplication events. Moreover, the Ka/Ks values of these segmentally duplicated gene pairs indicate that most ERF genes in P. tenuifolia have undergone purifying selection during evolution. This selection pressure further constrains functional divergence among duplicated genes, thereby maintaining their original functions [66].

Among other well-characterised species, Arabidopsis thaliana contains 65 ERF members [6], Oryza sativa 77 [67], Zea mays 105 [68]and Glycine max 152 [69]. The ERF number in P. tenuifolia is comparable to that of Arabidopsis thaliana and Oryza sativa. Collinearity analysis revealed that P. tenuifolia shares 64 syntenic ERF gene pairs with Arabidopsis, accounting for 82% (64/78) of the total PtERF genes. The highest number of syntenic pairs (118) was observed between P. tenuifolia and Glycine max, which may be related to the whole-genome duplication events that occurred in Glycine max. Overall, the ERF family in P. tenuifolia is structurally conserved and does not undergo dramatic lineage-specific expansion.

Expression levels of the PtERFs are regulated by multiple factors

Cis-acting elements in gene promoter regions play a key role in regulating gene expression. TFs recognise these cis-acting elements and thereby mediate tissue-specific and stress-related gene expression [70]. In this study, cis-acting elements associated with light response, stress response, plant hormones and plant growth and development were identified in the promoter regions of P. tenuifolia ERF genes.

PtERF promoters are enriched with ABA and methyl jasmonate-responsive elements, including ABRE, CGTCA-motif and TGACG-motif. These phytohormones are core regulators of drought tolerance and modulate downstream stress-responsive TFs such as ERF and NAC [71]. Notably, 68 PtERF genes contain the widely distributed ABRE element, further supporting their potential involvement in ABA-mediated drought regulation [72]. Additionally, light-responsive elements, particularly the G-box, are among the most abundant cis-elements identified. These findings suggest that PtERF genes may be involved in the growth and development of P. tenuifolia.

ERF subfamily genes play important roles in drought response of Polygala tenuifolia

Using RNA-seq data, ERF gene expression under drought stress was validated via RT-qPCR, and key ERF genes with significant induction were identified. Most PtERFs are activated by drought stress, although the duration varies. Some genes maintain high expression under normal and drought conditions, others show peak expression at 6 h, while some respond rapidly, respond slowly or show no significant change. These patterns indicate that PtERF genes play diverse roles in the drought stress response. PtERF30, PtERF33, PtERF39, PtERF37 and PtERF38 were strongly upregulated under drought stress. Notably, PtERF33 was the most significantly up-regulated, with its expression increasing 30-fold. Its Arabidopsis homolog, AT3G16770, acts in the ethylene signalling pathway downstream of EIN2 and CTR1 and activates the antioxidant gene GST6. Heterologous overexpression of this gene in tobacco confers enhanced tolerance to oxidative and heat stress [73]. Drought stress often leads to reactive oxygen species accumulation and frequently co-occurs with high temperature stress. We speculate that drought-induced ethylene signalling activates this gene, whose antioxidant and heat stress tolerance functions may indirectly improve drought adaptability. As a homolog of this gene, PtERF33 likely performs a similar protective role in P. tenuifolia under drought stress, thereby enhancing plant survival. Additionally, homologous genes such as RAP2.12 (At1g53910), RAP2.2 (At3g14230) and RAP2.3 (At3g16770) exhibit functional redundancy in abiotic stress regulation. Overexpressing any of these genes activates downstream targets, including ADH1 and PDC1, thereby significantly improving tolerance to hypoxic stress, oxidative stress (H2O2) and osmotic stress induced by mannitol, while increasing ABA sensitivity [74]. Based on the evolutionary principle that homologous genes often retain conserved functions, PtERF33 likely performs regulatory roles similar to those of the three Arabidopsis RAP2 genes. It may regulate abiotic stress tolerance by controlling downstream target genes and participating in ABA-mediated stress signalling pathways. Additionally, the Arabidopsis homologs of PtERF37/30 (ABR1; AT5G64750) and PtERF38/39 (ERF5; AT5G47230) respond rapidly to stress and phytohormone signals and are involved in stress resistance regulation. ABR1 is rapidly induced by abiotic stresses (cold, high salt, drought and osmotic stress) and ABA, whereas ERF5 is activated by ABA, multiple abiotic stresses (low temperature, drought and high salinity), auxin and oxidative stress [75, 76]. These findings reveal that these PtERFs may actively participate in drought tolerance and broader abiotic stress responses in P. tenuifolia.

Transcriptome analysis showed that, with the exception of several genes with unknown functions, most of the top 50 DEGs encode TFs, indicating that multiple families synergistically regulate plant drought response. Stress-responsive TF families, including ERF, MYB, NAC and bHLH, were significantly upregulated during the middle and late drought stages. These families are known to play key roles in drought responses in Arabidopsis thaliana, rice, soybean and other crop species, likely enhancing drought tolerance by activating downstream resistance pathways. Conversely, TF families such as bZIP, MADS-box and C3H were significantly downregulated, which may imply that plants suppress growth and development to prioritise metabolic resources for stress adaptation [77].

Two alternative splicing transcripts of PtERF33 exhibit opposite expression patterns under drought stress

AS represents an important mechanism regulating growth, development and responses to external environmental changes in higher organisms. It can encode different protein isoforms, change protein subcellular localisation, activity or binding properties, thereby leading to functional diversification [78]. In this study, RNA-seq analysis combined with RT-qPCR validation reveals that PtERF33 produces a novel AS transcript whose expression is significantly suppressed under drought stress. This transcript contains a PTC and likely encodes a truncated protein lacking the AP2 domain. This phenomenon is similar to findings reported for two transcripts generated from the AtARRE gene under salt stress. This gene was designated Salt-Responsive Alternatively Spliced gene1 (SRAS1). Salt stress-induced AS of this gene produces two variants: SRAS1.1 encodes a full-length protein containing a RING domain with E3 ubiquitin ligase activity, whereas SRAS1.2 encodes a truncated protein lacking the RING domain and E3 ligase activity. Under salt stress, SRAS1.1 expression is upregulated, while SRAS1.2 expression is downregulated. Functional studies show that overexpression of SRAS1.1 enhanced plant salt tolerance, while overexpression of SRAS1.2 or knockout of SRAS1 increases plant sensitivity to salt stress. Salt stress regulates the SRAS1.1/SRAS1.2 ratio through AS, thereby modulating the stability of the target protein CSN5A and balancing plant growth and development with salt tolerance [79]. Based on the opposite expression patterns of the full-length transcript PtERF33.1 and the truncated variant PtERF33.2 under drought in this study, we speculate that these two transcripts may exhibit a functional competitive relationship. This mechanism may represent a transcriptional regulatory strategy employed by P. tenuifolia in response to drought stress. In general, transcripts containing a PTC are subject to degradation via the nonsense-mediated mRNA decay (NMD) pathway [24]. This process helps regulate transcript abundance, modulate gene expression and ultimately improve plant adaptation to abiotic stress. Therefore, PtERF33.2 identified in this study may be degraded through this pathway, thereby reducing the overall transcript-level expression of this gene. Alternatively, it may be translated into a truncated protein that potentially acts as a negative regulator of the full-length protein. However, the exact molecular mechanism underlying this regulatory model remains unknown.

In summary, these PtERF genes may play important roles in responding to abiotic stresses; however, further experimental validation is required.

Conclusions

In this study, 78 PtERF genes were identified in P. tenuifolia and classified into five subgroups. The physicochemical properties, phylogenetic relationships, gene structures and syntenic relationships of this gene family were systematically analysed. The expression levels of most PtERF genes were significantly upregulated under drought stress, and PtERF30/33/37/38/39 were identified as core candidate genes potentially regulating drought tolerance in P. tenuifolia. AS prediction revealed the existence of two splice variants of PtERF33. It is suggested that drought stress may regulate drought tolerance by modulating the relative abundance of these transcript variants. This study represents the first comprehensive identification and analysis of the ERF subfamily in the medicinal plant P. tenuifolia, providing a foundation for future functional investigations.

Concurrently, this study has some notable limitations. The roles of PtERF33.1 and PtERF33.2 in the drought stress response of P. tenuifolia have not been comprehensively characterised at the transgenic plant level. Furthermore, the regulatory interactions between PtERF proteins and downstream genes involved in drought response pathways remain experimentally unvalidated. The CDB method, developed by the research group led by Zhu Jiankang, offers a rapid approach for generating genetic materials in non-model plant species. The research team is endeavouring to apply this method to generate Polygala tenuifolia genetic materials with overexpression and gene-edited variants of PtERF33.1 and PtERF33.2, with the aim of facilitating more detailed functional analyses.
