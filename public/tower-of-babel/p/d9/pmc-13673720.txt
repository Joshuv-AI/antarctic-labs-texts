Hypoxia tumor-associated macrophages facilitate hepatocellular carcinoma metastasis via uPA-uPAR pathway
Authors: Yijun Wang, Xiaomin You, Xiangyuan Luo, Wenjie Huang, Zerui Zhang, Limin Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) arises within a hypoxic and immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), where tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) constitute a major immune population. The impact of hypoxia on TAM functional heterogeneity and their contribution to HCC growth and metastasis remain incompletely understood.MethodsWe integrated multiple single-cell RNA sequencing datasets and developed a machine learning framework to map cellular hypoxia at single-cell resolution. Hypoxic TAMs (H-TAM) were characterized using transcriptomic and functional assays, including pseudotime trajectory analysis, regulatory network inference, in vitro co-culture, and orthotopic mouse models. Drug sensitivity correlations and in vivo validation were performed to evaluate therapeutic strategies.ResultsH-TAM represented the most hypoxic immune population in HCC and exhibited enhanced interactions with malignant hepatocytes. Transcriptomic profiling revealed HIF-1α–dependent hypoxia signaling and upregulation of plasminogen activator, urokinase (uPA, encoded by PLAU). H-TAM-derived uPA engaged its receptor uPAR on HCC cells, promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), migration, invasion, and lung metastasis. In line with this, genetic silencing of the corresponding mouse gene Plau in hypoxia-exposed bone marrow-derived macrophages (H-BMDM) markedly attenuated these pro-tumorigenic effects. Integrative drug sensitivity analysis identified dasatinib as a potential therapeutic agent in HCC with high uPAR expression, and in vivo administration selectively suppressed H-BMDM mediated tumor progression and metastasis while prolonging mouse survival.ConclusionsHypoxia drives TAM heterogeneity in HCC via an H‑TAM‑intrinsic HIF‑1α–uPA axis that engages uPAR on HCC cells to promote metastasis. uPAR is a potential prognostic biomarker, and dasatinib is a promising therapy to block this axis and improve outcomes.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08634-9.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide and ranks among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality [1]. Its development and progression are driven by intrinsic genetic alterations of tumor cells and by interactions among diverse cellular and structural components of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [2]. The TME of HCC consists of malignant hepatocytes, cancer-associated fibroblasts, aberrant vasculature, and diverse immune cells [2]. Within this system, the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) represents a highly plastic and functionally active compartment that fuels HCC progression and therapy resistance.

Among immune populations within the TIME of HCC, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) represent the most abundant innate immune cells, accounting for approximately 30–50% of infiltrating immune cells, and function as central hubs in the TME [2]. TAMs exhibit remarkable functional plasticity in response to local microenvironmental cues and play dual roles in tumor promotion and suppression. During early tumor development, circulating monocytes are recruited into the HCC TME by chemokines and subsequently differentiate into either pro-inflammatory, anti-tumorigenic M1-like TAMs or alternatively activated M2-like TAMs characterized by immunosuppressive, pro-angiogenic, and pro-metastatic properties [2–4]. However, the M1/M2 binary model oversimplifies the functional spectrum of TAMs by assuming stable polarization states, relying on markers that often overlap or vary across tissues, and failing to capture the influence of specific microenvironmental factors such as hypoxia, a hallmark of HCC that has been shown to profoundly reprogram TAMs functions [5, 6].

Hypoxia arises from aberrant angiogenesis, insufficient perfusion, and increased metabolic demand [7, 8]. TAMs are highly sensitive to hypoxic stress and undergo profound transcriptional and functional reprogramming under low-oxygen conditions [9]. In HCC, hypoxia has been shown to promote TAMs differentiation toward immunosuppressive and tumor-promoting phenotypes, thereby facilitating immune evasion, angiogenesis, and tumor invasion. Hypoxia-induced reprogramming of TAMs results in altered secretion of a wide range of cytokines, chemokines, enzymes, and other bioactive molecules, which in turn modulate the behavior of neighboring tumor cells, stromal fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and additional immune populations, thereby reshaping the local TME and promoting tumor progression [10]. Recent single-cell transcriptomic analyses of immunologically infiltrated tumors, including bladder and ovarian cancers, have identified a distinct hypoxia-associated TAM subset characterized by coordinated activation of NF-κB and HIF-1α signaling pathways [11, 12]. Nevertheless, it remains largely unexplored how hypoxia shapes the interactions between immune cells and HCC cells at single-cell resolution.

The plasminogen activation system is a tightly regulated proteolytic cascade composed of urokinase-type plasminogen activator (uPA, encoded by PLAU), its receptor uPAR, and the inhibitor PAI-1. Upon binding to uPAR on the cell surface, uPA catalyzes the conversion of plasminogen into plasmin, a broad-spectrum serine protease [13]. Plasmin degrades extracellular matrix and basement membrane components and further amplifies proteolytic activity, thereby facilitating tumor invasion and metastasis [13–14]. Aberrant activation of this system has been implicated in multiple malignancies, such as esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, and triple-negative breast cancer, where it promotes tumor proliferation, migration, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) through diverse downstream signaling pathways [14–16]. In HCC, elevated circulating uPA level has been associated with poor clinical outcomes, suggesting its potential prognostic relevance [17]. However, the mechanistic basis underlying the contribution of the uPA-uPAR system to tumor progression and TME regulation, particularly in the context of hypoxia, remains incompletely understood.

In this study, we developed an integrated machine learning-based classifier to infer cellular hypoxia states from single-cell transcriptomic data and applied this framework to map the hypoxic landscape of HCC at single-cell resolution. Using this approach, we found that TAMs constitute the most consistently hypoxic immune population within the HCC TIME. Our analyses revealed activation of the uPA-uPAR axis as a key effector associated with hypoxic TAMs (H-TAM), functionally linking hypoxic adaptation of TAMs to enhanced tumor invasion and metastatic potential. Furthermore, integrative drug-gene correlation analyses identified dasatinib as a candidate therapeutic agent capable of preferentially inhibiting H-TAM-mediated tumor progression.

Results

Development of a machine learning-based classifier to identify hypoxic states in HCC at single-cell resolution

Cellular hypoxia significantly impacts the progression of HCC. However, there is a lack of reliable methods to evaluate the hypoxic status of individual cell types within the TME. Existing approaches often lack robustness and generalizability across diverse datasets. To better investigate the hypoxic states of cells in the HCC TME, we integrated multiple machine learning models and developed a cellular hypoxia classifier based on scGPT-derived single-cell embeddings (Fig. 1A). We first analyzed 245,145 cells from 42 untreated HCC samples across three independent datasets (GSE149614, GSE202642, GSE242889) to characterize cellular hypoxia. To define reference hypoxia states, we calculated cellular hypoxia activity scores using seven canonical hypoxia-related gene sets. For each score, a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) was applied to stratify cells into putative hypoxic and normoxic populations. We then generated consensus hypoxia labels by integrating the classification results across all seven gene sets. Only cells consistently classified as hypoxic across all seven scores were defined as high-confidence hypoxic cells, whereas cells consistently classified as normoxic across all seven scores were defined as high-confidence normoxic cells. Cells with inconsistent classifications were considered low-confidence cells and excluded from initial classifier training. In total, 3,580 high-confidence hypoxic cells and 28,994 high-confidence normoxic cells were retained for subsequent training.

Next, we utilized the pretrained single-cell model scGPT to generate cell embeddings. These embeddings, which are based on large language model (LLM) technology, provide high-dimensional feature representations for classifier training and enable subsequent prediction of hypoxia states in low-confidence cells. We randomly selected 20% of the samples from the high-confidence single-cell cohort as the training set and used the remaining 80% as the validation set to evaluate model robustness under limited training data. In addition, four independent hypoxic cell line datasets were used as external real-world validation cohorts. After systematically evaluating 10 different machine learning models, we found that classifiers based on the embedding matrix consistently outperformed conventional methods under all experimental conditions, demonstrating improvements in both accuracy and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) (Fig. 1B, C). These findings highlight the high information density of the embedding matrix and its superior generalization ability in scenarios with limited sample sizes. Finally, based on the average ranking across all evaluation metrics, CatBoost was identified as the best-performing classifier, which was subsequently chosen for classifying the hypoxia status of low-confidence cells. Using established cell markers, we identified nine distinct cell types within the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data (Fig. 1D, E). The classifier was then used to assess cellular hypoxia states, revealing a higher proportion of hypoxic cells among myeloid cells and fibroblasts (Fig. 1F, G).

H-TAM demonstrate stronger interactions with tumor cells

To investigate cell-cell interactions within HCC TME, we utilized the CellphoneDB algorithm, which indicated that hypoxic myeloid cells exhibited the highest interaction frequency with malignant epithelial cells (Fig. 2A). To further characterize their transcriptional and phenotypic heterogeneity, we stratified myeloid populations into three canonical subtypes: dendritic cells (DCs), TAMs, and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) (Fig. 2B, C). Notably, hypoxia-associated transcriptional programs were detected across multiple myeloid lineages rather than being restricted to a specific subset, indicating a shared hypoxia-responsive state among canonical myeloid populations (Fig. 2B, C). In addition, the expression profiles of classical myeloid marker genes showed no major differences between hypoxic and normoxic conditions, suggesting that hypoxia is not associated with overt alterations in myeloid lineage composition. Instead, these results indicate that conventional myeloid markers are insufficient to fully resolve hypoxia-associated transcriptional heterogeneity (Fig. 2C). Among the identified subpopulations, TAMs exhibited the highest proportion of hypoxia-associated cells (Fig. 2D), suggesting a preferential enrichment of hypoxic states within the TAM compartment.

Given this observation, we next evaluated the prognostic significance of hypoxic TAMs (H-TAM) in HCC. Patients with higher expression of the H-TAM gene signatures exhibited significantly poorer survival outcomes (Supplementary Figure S1). Consistently, multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIF) at the tissue level demonstrated that increased infiltration of H-TAM (HIF-1α + CD68+) was associated with higher recurrence rates and reduced overall survival (Fig. 2F, G). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that, compared to normoxic TAMs (N-TAM), H-TAM exhibited biological functions related to NF-κB activation, lipid metabolism, and immune-related signaling pathways (Fig. 2H, I). Collectively, these data identify H-TAM as a critical immune cell population within the HCC TIME, characterized by enhanced interactions with tumor cells and a strong association with poor clinical outcomes.

Characterization of the transcriptomic signature of H-TAM

Next, we sought to define the transcriptional programs that distinguish H-TAM from their normoxic counterparts in the context of HCC progression. To this end, we performed differential expression analyses between N-TAM and H-TAM, as well as between TAMs and macrophages derived from para-cancerous tissues (Fig. 3A, B). Our analysis revealed that multiple inflammation-related genes (e.g., IL1B, CXCL1) and metabolism-related genes (e.g., PLAU, SERPINE1) were significantly upregulated in H-TAM. By integrating these two sets of differential expression profiles, we identified 17 shared genes (Fig. 3B). Cox regression analysis demonstrated that, among these shared genes, ZNF217, CSF1, BAG3, TMEM158, PLAU, MMP9, and SERPINE1 were significantly associated with poor prognosis and acted as potential risk factors (Fig. 3C). Concurrently, CellChat analysis revealed that PLAU was among the key ligands mediating cell-cell communication between H-TAM and tumor cells (Fig. 3D, Supplementary Figure S2). Therefore, we next focused on PLAU in HCC, especially its association with hypoxia.

Consistently, analysis across independent transcriptomic cohorts and proteomic tissue microarray datasets showed that high uPA expression was strongly correlated with unfavorable clinical outcomes in HCC patients (Fig. 3E, F). In vitro, hypoxia markedly induced uPA expression in THP-1 (Fig. 3G, H). Moreover, ELISA analysis revealed a significant elevation of secreted uPA protein in the supernatants of hypoxia-treated THP-1 (Fig. 3I). In another independent patient cohort (Tongji cohort II, n = 260), uPA expression was significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared with adjacent non-tumorous tissues (Fig. 3J, K). To further validate these findings at the spatial tissue level, mIF staining for HIF-1α, CD68, and uPA was performed in Tongji cohort I. Notably, uPA expression was predominantly observed in CD68 + HIF-1α + macrophages, whereas CD68 + HIF-1α- macrophages exhibited minimal uPA expression, suggesting that hypoxia is a key determinant of TAM-derived uPA production. Based on this, CD68 + HIF-1α + uPA+ cells were defined as a refined H-TAM subpopulation. Patients with a higher abundance of these cells exhibited significantly worse overall survival and increased recurrence rates compared with those with lower abundance (Supplementary Figure S3). Notably, compared with the CD68 + HIF-1α + definition alone, this combined marker-based stratification achieved comparable prognostic performance for patient survival and recurrence, yet it more precisely identified macrophages with activation of the uPA-uPAR axis. Next, we sought to explore the molecular mechanisms by which hypoxia drives uPA expression in TAMs.

HIF-1α drives hypoxic reprogramming of TAMs and transcriptionally upregulates uPA expression

To delineate the hypoxia-associated transcriptional dynamics underlying TAM state transitions, we reconstructed pseudotime trajectories using Monocle algorithms. The inferred trajectory revealed a progressive increase in hypoxia pathway activity along pseudotime, accompanied by stepwise upregulation of hypoxia-responsive genes, consistent with a gradual shift toward an H-TAM state (Fig. 4A, B). Subsequent validation through the single-cell regulatory network inference and clustering (SCENIC) algorithm showed that HIF1A was robustly and specifically active in H-TAM (Fig. 4C-E). Correlation analysis further corroborated that both the regulatory activity and expression levels of HIF1A were positively associated with pseudotime (Fig. 4F). Moreover, HIF-1α exhibited pronounced expression and regulatory specificity in H-TAM compared with other myeloid populations (Fig. 4G).

Given the enrichment of PLAU in H-TAM identified in the preceding analyses, we next examined whether HIF1A regulates uPA expression during the N-TAM to H-TAM transition. Along the pseudotime trajectory, PLAU expression closely paralleled that of HIF1A (Fig. 4H, I), and single-cell correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between HIF1A and PLAU expression levels (Fig. 4J). Functionally, lentivirus-mediated overexpression of Hif-1a in bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) resulted in a marked upregulation of uPA, whereas Hif-1a knockdown significantly reduced uPA expression (Fig. 4K). At the mechanistic level, chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR) demonstrated significant enrichment of HIF-1α at the PLAU promoter, and luciferase reporter assays further confirmed that HIF-1α directly enhanced PLAU promoter activity (Fig. 4LN). Collectively, these data demonstrate that HIF-1α drives the hypoxic reprogramming of TAMs and transcriptionally upregulates uPA expression directly.

HIF-1α- induced uPA production in macrophages promotes HCC progression and metastasis

To systematically evaluate the functional contribution of TAM-derived uPA under different oxygen conditions in vivo, we silenced Plau in hypoxia‑conditioned BMDMs (H‑BMDM) and ectopically overexpressed Plau in normoxic‑conditioned BMDMs (N‑BMDM). These genetically manipulated BMDMs were then co-injected with Hepa1-6 cells into the livers of C57BL/6J mice to establish orthotopic HCC models (Fig. 5A). The expression levels of HIF-1α and uPA in these BMDM groups were confirmed prior to implantation (Fig. 5B).

Compared with N-BMDM, tumors formed by Hepa1-6 cells co-injected with H-BMDM exhibited significantly accelerated growth and metastasis. In vivo bioluminescence imaging revealed markedly increased tumor burden in mice receiving H-BMDM (Fig. 5C, D), accompanied by a significantly shortened overall survival (Fig. 5E). Moreover, analysis of lung tissues demonstrated a substantially higher number of pulmonary metastatic nodules in the H-BMDM group compared with the N-BMDM group (Fig. 5F, G).

In gain-of-function experiments, overexpression of Plau in N-BMDM markedly increased tumor burden compared with control N-BMDM, as evidenced by accelerated orthotopic tumor growth, shortened survival, and increased pulmonary metastasis (Fig. 5C–G). Conversely, in loss-of-function experiments, silencing of Plau in H-BMDM significantly attenuated their pro-tumorigenic effects relative to control H-BMDM, leading to suppressed tumor growth, prolonged survival, and reduced lung metastasis (Fig. 5C–G). Collectively, these findings indicate that HIF-1α–dependent induction of uPA in H-TAM contributes to HCC growth and metastatic dissemination, supporting the existence of a TAM-intrinsic HIF-1α–uPA axis.

uPA derived from H-TAM promotes EMT in HCC cells

To explore how H-TAM influence tumor cell behavior via uPA, we performed differential expression analysis on tumor cells from tissues with high versus low H-TAM infiltration. Functional enrichment revealed significant activation of EMT-related gene signatures in tumor cells from tumors with high H-TAM abundance (Supplementary Figure S4), suggesting a potential role of H-TAM in promoting EMT programs in HCC cells.

In vitro assays demonstrated that Hepa1-6 cells cultured with conditioned medium (CM) from H-BMDM exhibited significantly enhanced migratory capacity. Overexpression of Plau in N-BMDM further enhanced HCC cell migration, whereas Plau knockdown in H-BMDM markedly attenuated this effect (Fig. 6A). Similar results were obtained using PLC/PRF-5 cells cultured with CM from hypoxic THP-1 (H-THP-1) or normoxic THP-1 (N-THP-1) (Fig. 6B).

Furthermore, IF staining, IHC analysis, and immunoblotting of tumor tissues from the mouse orthotopic HCC model revealed a pronounced EMT phenotype in the H-BMDM group, characterized by decreased expression of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and increased levels of the mesenchymal markers N-cadherin and vimentin (Fig. 6C–F). Similar EMT-associated protein changes were observed in the Plau overexpression group, whereas the opposite expression patterns were detected upon Plau knockdown (Fig. 6C–F). Together, these findings demonstrate that uPA derived from H-TAM promotes EMT and enhances the migratory and invasive capacity of HCC cells.

uPA derived from TAMs promotes HCC progression by engaging its receptor uPAR on tumor cells

uPA is a well-characterized extracellular serine protease whose primary receptor is uPAR, encoded by the PLAUR gene and widely implicated in tumor invasion and metastasis [18, 19]. Based on our previous findings, we next investigated whether uPA secreted by H-TAM exerts its tumor-promoting effects primarily through uPAR expressed on HCC cells. Human HCC cells (PLC/PRF-5-shControl) and cells with PLAUR knockdown (PLC/PRF-5-shPLAUR) were treated with recombinant uPA (r-uPA) or PBS. We found that r-uPA significantly enhanced the migratory capacity of PLC/PRF-5 cells, whereas this effect was largely abolished upon PLAUR knockdown (Fig. 7B). Consistently, CM derived from H-BMDM markedly promoted the migration of Hepa1-6 cells, while this effect was substantially attenuated in Hepa1-6 with Plaur silenced (Hepa1-6-shPlaur) (Fig. 7B). Protein expression analyses further revealed that stimulation with either exogenous uPA or CM from H-BMDM led to increased expression of mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin and vimentin) accompanied by a reduction in epithelial markers (E-cadherin). These EMT-associated changes were largely abolished following PLAUR (Plaur) silencing (Fig. 7C, D). We next examined intracellular kinase signaling in HCC cells following r-uPA stimulation. r‑uPA markedly induced phosphorylation of FAK, Src, AKT, ERK, and p38, whereas JNK and p65 showed no significant changes. Notably, these effects were abrogated upon Plaur knockdown, indicating that uPA binds to uPAR on the surface of HCC cells and subsequently promotes the EMT phenotype, possibly through these signaling pathways (Fig. 7E). At the in vivo level, Hepa1-6-shControl or Hepa1-6-shPlaur cells were co-injected with differentially treated BMDMs into the livers of mice to establish orthotopic HCC models. Supplementation with H-BMDM significantly increased tumor burden; however, this tumor-promoting effect was no longer evident when Plaur was knocked down in tumor cells (Fig. 7F–J). Collectively, these data demonstrate that the uPA-uPAR axis contributes to EMT in HCC cells.

To determine whether the uPA-uPAR axis was spatially associated with H-TAM in tumor tissues, we analyzed spatial transcriptomic data from HCC samples (Fig. 7K). H-TAM and N-TAM were both distributed within tumor enriched regions. However, neighborhood composition analysis revealed that H-TAM neighborhoods contained a higher proportion of PLAUR+ tumor cells compared with N-TAM neighborhoods (Fig. 7K, L). Consistently, PLAU expression was significantly higher in H-TAM than in N-TAM (Fig. 7M). These spatial findings support that the uPA-uPAR axis is not merely a transcriptional observation but is associated with the local organization of H-TAMs and uPAR-expressing tumor cells within the tumor microenvironment.

Next, we analyzed the correlation between uPAR expression and prognosis using public databases. Public data showed that uPAR was highly expressed in HCC tissues, and elevated uPAR expression was associated with poor prognosis (Fig. 7N, O, Supplementary Figure S5A, B). To validate these findings, we examined uPAR expression by IHC in an independent HCC cohort (cohort II, n = 260), which revealed that 151 patients were uPAR‑positive and 109 were uPAR‑negative (Fig. 7P, Supplementary Table S1). uPAR expression was significantly associated with poor clinical outcomes. Patients with uPAR positivity showed higher recurrence rates and significantly worse overall survival compared with PLAUR-negative patients (Fig. 7Q). Further analysis of the association between uPAR expression and clinicopathological features revealed that, patients with uPAR positivity exhibited more aggressive tumor features, including more frequent loss of tumor encapsulation, microvascular invasion, poorly differentiated tumors, and advanced TNM stage (Supplementary Table S2). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses showed that uPAR may serve as an independent prognostic risk factor (Supplementary Table S3). Collectively, these findings demonstrate the clinical relevance of uPAR in HCC and have prompted us to investigate the potential therapeutic implications of targeting the uPA-uPAR axis.

Dasatinib suppresses H‑TAM‑mediated HCC progression

Based on the GDSC_v2 and CTRP databases, we analyzed the association between PLAUR expression levels and drug sensitivity in HCC samples using the BEST website [20]. In both databases, uPAR expression exhibited significant correlations with the response to multiple anti-cancer drugs (Supplementary Figure S5C, D). Among these drugs, PLAUR expression showed a significant negative correlation with dasatinib sensitivity. Dasatinib is a clinically approved multi‑kinase inhibitor that potently targets Src family kinases [21, 22]. Given our observations that activation of the uPA‑uPAR axis leads to Src and other downstream kinase phosphorylation, the association between high uPAR expression and increased dasatinib sensitivity may reflect the dependency of uPAR‑high HCC cells on Src‑associated signaling pathways (Supplementary Figure S5C, D). These findings suggest that uPAR expression may serve as a potential biomarker for predicting the therapeutic response to dasatinib in patients with HCC.

We next assessed the effects of dasatinib on HCC cell migration in vitro. Dasatinib treatment significantly suppressed the enhanced migratory capacity of tumor cells induced by CM from H-BMDM, whereas it had minimal effect on migration in the N-BMDM group (Fig. 8A). To evaluate therapeutic efficacy in vivo, Hepa1-6 cells were orthotopically co-injected with either H-BMDM or N-BMDM into mouse livers. Dasatinib markedly reversed the expression of EMT-associated proteins induced by H-BMDM (Fig. 8B). In parallel, dasatinib significantly reduced the phosphorylation of Src (Tyr416), accompanied by partial suppression of FAK, AKT, and ERK signaling pathways (Fig. 8C). In contrast, these inhibitory effects were less pronounced in tumors co-injected with N-BMDM (Fig. 8C). Consistently, dasatinib treatment markedly attenuated orthotopic tumor growth mediated by H‑BMDM, prolonged overall survival, and reduced the number of pulmonary metastatic nodules (Fig. 8D–I). In contrast, dasatinib had no significant impact on tumor progression in the N-BMDM group, indicating that its anti-tumor effects are largely dependent on the presence of H-TAM (Fig. 8D–I). Overall, these results suggest that the multi‑target tyrosine kinase inhibitor dasatinib can suppress the pro‑tumorigenic and pro‑metastatic effects mediated by H‑TAM, likely acting partly through inhibition of Src kinase signaling downstream of uPAR. These findings highlight the potential utility of dasatinib in the H‑TAM‑rich HCC.

Discussion

In this study, we provide evidence that H-TAM drives HCC progression and metastasis through a HIF-1α–uPA–uPAR axis. By integrating multiple single-cell datasets with machine learning-based hypoxia classifiers, we identified TAMs as the major hypoxic myeloid population in the HCC TIME. Patients with elevated infiltration of the H-TAM transcriptional signature exhibited worse overall survival, highlighting the clinical relevance of hypoxia in TAMs.

TAMs have long been implicated in HCC progression, immune evasion, and metastatic dissemination [23, 24]. Prior studies have largely focused on TAM density or classical M1/M2 polarization, without resolving the impact of microenvironmental hypoxia on TAM functional heterogeneity [5, 6]. Our data extend these observations by demonstrating that TAMs respond dynamically to hypoxic stress, undergoing transcriptional reprogramming characterized by HIF-1α activation and inflammatory signaling. This reprogramming results in a functional state (H‑TAM) that is distinct from normoxic TAMs (N‑TAM).

Functionally, H-TAM exhibits a marked shift in its secretory profile, producing factors that directly modulate tumor cell behavior rather than merely suppressing anti-tumor immunity. Among these factors, we identified the uPA-uPAR axis as a key downstream effector. uPA and its receptor uPAR are well-established regulators of extracellular matrix degradation, cell migration, and tissue remodeling [25]. In multiple cancers, uPA binding to uPAR initiates protease cascades that degrade the extracellular matrix through activating multiple signaling pathways (such as FAK, Src, MAPK, and PI3K), thereby promoting EMT, proliferation, and migration [25–27]. uPA can also release matrix‑bound growth factors such as FGF2, HGF, and TGF‑β1, enhancing tumor growth and angiogenesis [26, 27]. In our study, we observed that uPA secreted by H‑TAM engages uPAR on HCC cells and promotes an EMT‑like phenotypic shift, supporting H‑TAM as a potential source of pro‑invasive cues in HCC and providing a mechanistic link between hypoxic adaptation of TAMs and tumor cell invasiveness.

Current first-line therapies for advanced HCC, such as sorafenib and lenvatinib, often show limited efficacy and patients frequently develop resistance [28–30]. To explore alternative options, we performed a correlation analysis between drug sensitivity and uPAR expression. This analysis highlighted dasatinib, an FDA-approved oral ABL/SRC tyrosine kinase inhibitor for chronic myeloid leukemia [31], as the compound most closely associated with uPAR expression in HCC cells. Dasatinib has been previously reported to inhibit HCC cell proliferation, adhesion, migration, and invasion via Src/FAK and PI3K/PTEN/Aktsignaling [32]. However, its effect on signaling mediated by H‑TAM has not been investigated. We then tested dasatinib in models of HCC progression driven by H‑BMDM [31, 33]. Our results showed that dasatinib effectively suppressed tumor cell migration induced by H‑TAM in vitro, reduced orthotopic tumor growth, lung metastasis, and improved survival in vivo. Importantly, dasatinib had minimal impact on N-BMDM, suggesting a preferential effect on H-TAM signaling. Although the precise mechanisms underlying this hypoxia-specific sensitivity remain unclear, these findings support the potential repurposing of dasatinib to counteract the pro-metastatic effects of H-TAM in HCC.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. Firstly, our classification of H‑TAM was inferred from transcriptomic hypoxia signatures rather than direct measurements of oxygen tension in situ. Potential biases may arise from the selection of hypoxia‑related gene sets and batch effects. Although spatial transcriptomics and multiplex immunostaining supported our findings, future studies incorporating direct hypoxia mapping will be needed to refine this classification. In addition, the single‑cell trajectory and drug sensitivity analyses are primarily correlative and computational in nature and require further experimental validation. Secondly, we did not systematically evaluate other hypoxia‑induced factors (e.g., VEGF, CXCL8, IL‑10) in functional assays, as our study focused on the uPA‑uPAR axis. Thirdly, the in vivo co‑injection model used here represents an acute and simplified system that does not fully recapitulate endogenous monocyte recruitment and differentiation in the TME. Moreover, this model does not allow rigorous evaluation of the necessity of the uPA–uPAR axis in an intact immune system. Genetic approaches, such as myeloid‑specific conditional knockout models, will be essential to further validate the causal role of this pathway. Finally, the impact of H‑TAM on therapeutic responses, particularly in the context of immune checkpoint blockade, warrants further investigation.

In conclusion, our study identifies hypoxia as a central regulator of TAMs reprogramming in HCC and reveals a HIF‑1α–uPA axis intrinsic to H‑TAM that promotes tumor cell invasion and metastasis through engagement of uPAR on HCC cells, with uPAR representing a potential prognostic biomarker for this pathogenic process. Dasatinib represents a promising therapeutic strategy to counteract the pro-metastatic effects mediated by this axis and to improve patient outcomes.
