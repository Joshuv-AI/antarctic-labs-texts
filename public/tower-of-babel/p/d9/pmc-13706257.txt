Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection-Associated Increased Risk of Monozygotic Twins Following Elective Single Embryo Transfer: A Retrospective Cohort Analysis
Authors: Liduan Zhou, Jing Chen, Xiyang Wang, Qingchen Cui, Lili Pan, Youzhu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:To explore the primary factors influencing monozygotic twinning (MZT) in patients undergoing assisted reproductive technology (ART) at a single center to clarify the mechanisms of MZT formation.Methods:This retrospective cohort study analyzed 2952 elective single embryo transfer (eSET) cycles conducted between December 2014 and December 2023 at the First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University, which resulted in 1363 clinical pregnancies. The examined variables included parental age, fertilization method, cycle type (fresh vs. frozen-thawed), and embryo stage (cleavage vs. blastocyst). Clinical pregnancy was diagnosed through human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) levels 12–14 days post-transfer and confirmed via transvaginal ultrasound at 6–7 weeks of gestation. MZT was defined by the presence of additional gestational sacs (dichorionic) or two embryos within a single sac (monochorionic), while a monozygotic singleton (MZS) was defined as one embryo per sac.Results:The overall MZT incidence was 0.85% (25/2952) per cycle and 1.83% (25/1363) per pregnancy (1 morula, 4 cleavage-stage, and 20 blastocyst transfers). After excluding the morula case, 24 MZT pregnancies were matched 1:4 with singletons. After matching, embryo stage and cycle type (16 fresh and 8 frozen-thawed MZT cases) demonstrated no significant effect on MZT rates. When the analysis was restricted to blastocyst transfer (20 MZTs and 80 singletons), neither parental age nor cycle type showed a significant correlation with MZT occurrence, whereas the fertilization method was significantly associated with MZT frequency. Regarding embryo morphology, all 20 MZT blastocysts featured high-quality (grade A or B) inner cell masses and trophectoderms, with no grade C embryos observed. Multivariate logistic regression confirmed that the use of intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) significantly increased the risk of MZT, whereas parental age and cycle type did not.Conclusion:The fertilization method affects MZT incidence, with the ICSI procedure and sperm quality serving as key correlates. Furthermore, high-quality blastocysts may possess a higher potential for MZT, providing new insights for the development of individualized embryo transfer strategies.

Introduction

Monozygotic twinning (MZT) is a unique form of multiple pregnancy resulting from the splitting of a single fertilized egg into two independent embryos. In natural conception, the incidence of MZT is approximately 0.4%.1 While the continuous advancement of assisted reproductive technology (ART) helps a greater number of infertile couples achieve parenthood, it also increases the risk of multiple pregnancies. Although the implementation of single embryo transfer (SET) aims to reduce these occurrences, the risk of MZT—and even monozygotic higher-order pregnancies—persists. Statistics show that the incidence of MZT in ART-assisted pregnancies has risen to 1.36%, which is triple the rate observed with natural conception.2 Numerous studies have confirmed that MZT pregnancies are associated with a significantly higher risk of adverse outcomes—including miscarriage, preterm birth, preeclampsia, low fetal birth weight, and intrauterine growth restriction—and the fetal mortality risk is three to six times higher than that in the general population.3 Additionally, the incidence of severe complications unique to MZT—such as twin-to-twin transfusion syndrome, twin reversed arterial perfusion sequence, and twin anemia-polycythemia sequence (TAPS)—has markedly increased, posing a serious threat to the lives of both the mother and the fetuses.4

The different types of pregnancies associated with MZT originate from a single fertilized egg that splits at various developmental stages. These classifications include dichorionic diamniotic (accounting for 20%–30%), monochorionic diamniotic (accounting for 70%–75%), and monochorionic monoamniotic (accounting for 1%–2%) pregnancies. The specific pregnancy type depends on the timing of embryonic splitting.5 Notably, MZT is inherently a human-specific physiological phenomenon, and the underlying mechanisms and inducing factors remain incompletely understood. Studies have suggested that maternal age is a potential factor influencing the occurrence of MZT, though the direction of this association remains under debate. While some studies have shown that MZT is more common in younger women, potentially due to superior ovarian function,6 others have indicated that it occurs more frequently in older women, attributable to the thinning of the zona pellucida associated with advancing maternal age.7 Several factors may contribute to these inconsistent findings. First, variations in study populations—including differences in age stratification, ethnicity, region, distribution of infertility etiologies, and sample size—may lead to biased conclusions. Second, assisted reproductive strategies vary across centers; differences in fertilization methods, embryo transfer policies, cycle types, assisted hatching protocols, and operator techniques may also result in inconsistent outcomes. Additionally, emerging research indicates that potential correlations exist between blastocyst transfer, embryonic morphology, and the incidence of MZT.8 Given that the specific causes and factors contributing to the elevated incidence of MZT following ART remain unclear, this study aims to explore the key variables affecting MZT occurrence among ART-conceived pregnancies at this center. The objective is to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying MZT formation. Our findings are expected to be translated into clinical practice to adjust transplantation protocols for high-risk populations, provide more comprehensive counseling for patients undergoing in vitro fertilization (IVF), and offer theoretical support for the development of individualized embryo transfer strategies at our institution.

Materials and methods

In this retrospective cohort study, data were collected from patients who underwent IVF treatment at the Department of Reproductive Medicine, The First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University, between December 2014 and December 2023. A total of 2952 elective single embryo transfer (eSET) cases were included, of which 1363 achieved successful clinical pregnancy. Exclusion criteria included the transfer of more than one embryo, the use of donor sperm or donor eggs, and the presence of dizygotic multiple pregnancies. Patients were advised to adhere to strict contraception during IVF treatment. While cases involving gender differences in newborns would have been excluded, no such cases have been recorded at the center to date.

The collected data were analyzed to compare MZT and singleton outcomes according to parental age, fertilization method, embryo transfer type (fresh vs. frozen), and developmental stage at transfer (cleavage vs. blastocyst). Blastocysts were comprehensively evaluated using the Gardner grading system across three dimensions: expansion degree, inner cell mass (ICM) quality, and trophectoderm (TE) quality. The expansion degree was scored from 1 to 6, representing the progression from an early blastocyst with a small blastocoel to a fully hatched blastocyst; these grades correspond to progressive blastocoel enlargement and thinning of the zona pellucida. The ICM was graded A through C to reflect cell quantity and arrangement density, where grade A denoted numerous, tightly arranged cells and grade C indicated that very few cells were present. Similarly, the TE was graded A through C to indicate epithelial cell layer density, with grade A referring to a dense layer with abundant cells and grade C representing sparse cells.9

Clinical pregnancy was confirmed through serum or urine human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) tests performed 12–14 days after embryo transfer, followed by transvaginal ultrasound starting at 6–7 weeks of gestation. A diagnosis of clinical pregnancy was made when gestational sacs and fetal cardiac activity were visualized. The MZT group comprised cases where the number of gestational sacs exceeded the number of transferred embryos, representing dichorionic twins, or where two embryos were identified within a single gestational sac, representing monochorionic twins. Conversely, the singleton pregnancy group included cases with only one embryo visualized within a single gestational sac.

Statistical analysis

All data in this study were processed and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27 software. Following 1:4 random matching by sample size, categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. For intergroup comparisons, the Pearson Chi-square test was applied when all expected frequencies exceeded 5; otherwise, Fisher exact test was used. Taking MZT pregnancy as the primary outcome measure, a binary logistic regression model served as the primary multivariate tool to identify independent risk or protective factors influencing MZT pregnancy. A one-tailed test was specifically employed for the insemination method because previous studies support the directional hypothesis, namely, that ICSI increases the risk of MZT.7,10 A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Ethical approval

The study was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Xiamen University and was conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations (ethics approval number: [2024] KYLSZ No. 120; approval date: August 22, 2024). All enrolled subjects provided informed consent prior to inclusion, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

A total of 2952 eSET procedures were performed, resulting in 1363 clinical pregnancies. Among these, 25 cases resulted in MZT, comprising 1 case from morula transfer, 4 from cleavage-stage embryo transfer, and 20 from blastocyst transfer. Consequently, the incidence of MZT per eSET procedure at this center was 0.85% (25/2952), while the incidence among clinical pregnancies following eSET was 1.83% (25/1363). The single morula case in the MZT group was excluded from further analysis to maintain statistical consistency. Subsequently, the remaining 24 MZT cases were randomly matched with the singleton pregnancy group at a 1:4 ratio. Of these MZT cases, 16 occurred in fresh cycles, and 8 occurred in frozen-thawed cycles. No statistically significant differences in MZT incidence with respect to embryo stage or transfer cycle type were observed between the two groups (Table 1).

To optimize success rates, blastocyst transfer is predominantly selected for eSET. Cleavage-stage embryo transfer for eSET is rarely performed—occurring only in specific scenarios such as a scarred uterus, cervical insufficiency (which mandates eSET), or instances where only one cleavage-stage embryo is available—resulting in small sample sizes for cleavage-stage eSET cases. To ensure data comparability, this study focused solely on blastocyst transfers. Twenty MZT cases resulting from single blastocyst transfer were randomly matched with 80 singleton cases at a 1:4 ratio. The results indicated that maternal age, paternal age, and cycle type had no significant impact on MZT incidence. However, the fertilization method was associated with MZT occurrence. Additionally, among the 20 MZT cases, the ICM and TE of the blastocysts were all graded as A or B, with no grade C embryos present. Discussion

The data from this center showed that the incidence of MZT among patients undergoing ART was 0.85% (25/2952), which is significantly higher than that observed in natural pregnancies. Subsequently, the MZT group was randomly matched with the singleton pregnancy group at a 1:4 ratio. The results revealed no association between MZT risk and embryo type or transfer cycle type, indicating that embryo cryopreservation and blastocyst culture do not affect the incidence of MZT.

To optimize success rates for SET, blastocyst transfer is the predominant method used. Cleavage-stage embryo transfer for SET is rarely performed, occurring only in specific cases such as a scarred uterus, cervical insufficiency, or in instances where only one cleavage-stage embryo is available. These factors resulted in a small sample size of cleavage-stage SET cases at our center. To ensure data comparability, we focused solely on blastocyst transfers. There were 20 MZT cases resulting from SET, which were randomly matched with 80 singleton cases at a 1:4 ratio. The results indicated that maternal age, paternal age, and cycle type had no significant impact on MZT incidence, whereas fertilization method showed a marked association with MZT occurrence. Multivariate logistic regression analysis of single blastocyst transfer cycles—adjusted for parental age, cycle type, and fertilization method—confirmed that parental age and cycle type did not influence MZT incidence, whereas the fertilization method—specifically, ICSI—was associated with an increased risk of MZT.

Regarding maternal age, this study found no statistically significant association between advanced maternal age (≥ 35 years) and MZT incidence (25.00% in the MZT group vs. 17.50% in the singleton group, P = 0.525). This finding is inconsistent with the hypothesis that changes in the zona pellucida associated with aging, such as thinning or increased fragility, may lead to embryo splitting and, consequently, an increased risk of MZT.7 However, this negative result must be interpreted with caution. Given the overall low incidence of MZT, the absolute number of older women in the MZT subgroup was small (n = 5). This limited sample size likely resulted in insufficient statistical power to detect a true effect, potentially leading to a Type II error. Consequently, these results do not definitively exclude age as a risk factor but rather highlight the need for larger, multicenter cohorts to provide the statistical power necessary for subgroup analyses regarding advanced maternal age.

At our center, IVF is typically indicated for tubal factor infertility or unexplained infertility, while ICSI is primarily used for patients with severe male infertility, such as oligozoospermia or asthenozoospermia. Although the mechanism underlying MZT formation remains unclear, it is widely accepted that the splitting of the ICM during early development leads to embryo duplication. This process is often triggered by ART-associated zona pellucida manipulation, including assisted hatching and ICSI.10 At this center, assisted hatching is routinely performed for both blastocyst and frozen-thawed embryo transfers. However, we found no significant difference in MZT incidence between fresh and frozen-thawed embryo transfers, nor between cleavage-stage and blastocyst transfers. This consistency suggests that assisted hatching does not increase MZT incidence at this center, indicating that simple mechanical damage to the zona pellucida has a negligible effect on MZT risk. In contrast, ICSI was significantly associated with an elevated MZT risk. Some studies suggest that ICSI directly disrupts the cytoplasmic environment and spindle structure in oocytes.11 The oocyte cytoplasm contains key signaling molecules that regulate early embryonic division. The disturbance of cytoplasmic flow or the uneven distribution of components caused by microinjection may disrupt these signals, interfering with the regulation of symmetric zygote division and thereby increasing MZT risk.12 In IVF, natural sperm-oocyte interaction maintains a more stable cytoplasmic environment and division-regulating signals, resulting in a lower MZT incidence compared to that seen with ICSI.

Additionally, in IVF, sperm must penetrate the zona pellucida and cumulus cells through their own motility—a natural selection process that allows only high-quality, motile sperm to fertilize. ICSI bypasses this natural screening. Studies have shown that compromised sperm quality indirectly affects the stability and rhythm of early embryonic division, leading to abnormal separation during zygote cleavage.13 Sperm used for ICSI are generally of lower quality than those used for IVF, and this difference may be amplified during embryo formation and development. Furthermore, ICSI increases the risk of sperm DNA damage, which can induce mitotic errors, genomic instability, and dysregulation of embryonic division during zygote development.14 These factors likely account for the higher MZT incidence observed in ICSI compared to IVF.

Furthermore, the data revealed that all 20 MZT cases originated from high-quality blastocysts graded A or B for both the ICM and TE, with no Grade C embryos involved. Although statistical analysis did not identify a significant difference between Grade A and B subgroups, the concentration of MZT in high-quality embryos suggests that a correlation may exist between superior morphological grade and MZT risk. High-quality blastocysts are characterized by vigorous cellular activity and robust expansion capabilities. During the hatching process, blastocysts with strong expansion forces generate high internal hydrostatic pressure. If the opening in the zona pellucida—whether spontaneous or iatrogenic via assisted hatching—is relatively narrow, the herniating ICM may be subjected to significant mechanical shear stress, creating a constricted, “figure-of-eight” shape. This hatching mechanism, where part of the ICM remains inside the zona while the rest protrudes, is hypothesized to act as a key trigger for embryo splitting.15 Our observation that MZT cases were predominantly at expansion stage 4 (95.00%), whereas singletons showed a wider distribution across stages 4 to 6, further supports the hypothesis that the dynamic process of active expansion and hatching in high-potential embryos may predispose them to splitting. Larger studies are needed to confirm this morphological correlation. However, clinicians should be aware that transferring top-quality, vigorously expanding blastocysts, while optimizing implantation rates, might carry a theoretically elevated risk of MZT.

The internationally accepted Gardner Blastocyst Scoring System evaluates blastocysts across three dimensions: expansion degree (grades 1–6), ICM quality (grades A–C, related to fetal development), and TE quality (grades A–C, related to nutrient supply). High-quality blastocysts are specifically defined as grades 4AA, 4AB, 4BA, and 4BB, and are characterized by high morphological quality of both the ICM (6–10 cells) and TE (50–100 cells).9 It is widely believed that the splitting of the ICM, which can occur during blastocoel formation or the hatching process, is the critical event for MZT at the blastocyst stage. If the zona pellucida opening is too small during hatching, the ICM may be physically separated when passing through the narrow opening, creating a constricted, “figure-of-eight” shape. This specific hatching mechanism, in which partial ICM retention occurs within the zona pellucida alongside partial protrusion, is considered a key driver of MZT, ultimately leading to monochorionic multiple pregnancies.15 All 20 MZT cases from single blastocyst transfers involved blastocysts with ICM and TE graded A or B, with no Grade C embryos represented. Analysis of these data showed no significant association between specific ICM/TE grading (A vs. B) and MZT incidence. These findings suggest that high-quality blastocysts may be more prone to MZT.

Studies have shown that the high cellular activity and strong TE expansion capacity of high-quality blastocysts are the main drivers of increased MZT risk. High-quality blastocysts exhibit elevated cellular activity and mobility during hatching, while those with strong hatching ability show increased expression of genes such as cathepsin V and GATA-binding protein 3. In these high-quality embryos, such genetic and cellular characteristics likely underpin the morphological changes and positional movements observed during the hatching process.16,17 Additionally, high-quality blastocysts display tighter TE connections and superior blastocoel expansion, resulting in an increase in internal pressure and, consequently, the generation of a stronger extrusion force when the embryo passes through narrow openings.18 However, these hypotheses require further validation with larger sample sizes.

While this study primarily focused on blastocyst transfers due to their predominance in the eSET strategy, the relationship between cleavage-stage embryo transfer and MZT warrants discussion. Studies have indicated that cleavage-stage transfers, particularly when combined with assisted hatching or extended culture to the blastocyst stage in vitro, may be associated with an increased risk of MZT.10 Research suggests that the manipulation of the zona pellucida at the cleavage stage might compromise its structural integrity, predisposing the embryo to splitting during subsequent expansion.5 In this cohort, although the number of cleavage-stage eSET cycles was small and resulted in only four MZT cases, the MZT rate for cleavage-stage embryos did not differ significantly from that of blastocysts. This lack of difference may be attributed to the limited sample size of cleavage-stage transfers, as such transfers are now reserved for specific clinical indications, such as poor embryo development or uterine factors. Nevertheless, existing evidence suggests that the timing of zona pellucida manipulation and the developmental stage at transfer interact in a complex manner. Future studies with balanced cohorts of cleavage and blastocyst transfers are essential to disentangle these effects and provide comprehensive guidance for clinical practice.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size of the MZT group was small, and data were derived from a single center, which limits generalizability. Additionally, differences in assisted reproductive strategies, fertilization methods, the distribution of embryo transfer cycles, and patient population characteristics (including age, ethnicity, region, and causes of infertility) across reproductive centers may lead to biased results. Therefore, multicenter and large-sample studies are needed in the future to verify these conclusions. Because of the rarity of MZT, the MZT group was significantly smaller than the singleton pregnancy control group; consequently, a 1:4 random sampling matching method was used for statistical analysis. This matching approach may affect the accuracy of interpreting statistical significance and introduce potential bias, meaning that the results should be interpreted cautiously. Second, the retrospective design of this study is prone to treatment and information bias. Conducting a prospective study was challenging due to the relatively low incidence of MZT. Third, the number of MZT cases derived from cleavage-stage embryos was too small to allow targeted analysis. Future multicenter, large-sample studies are needed to explore these issues further.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that the fertilization method may affect the incidence of MZT, and the procedural aspects of ICSI and sperm quality are key factors that explain this association. Additionally, high-quality blastocysts may have a higher probability of developing into MZT. These observations offer valuable perspectives that help clinicians formulate personalized embryo transfer strategies. Therefore, when providing patients with counseling on the application of current ART and informing them of the risk of MZT, it would be prudent to consider these aforementioned factors.
