Bystander and emergency medical service responses to and outcomes of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest among domestic and non-domestic visitors in Japan
Authors: Kentaro Omatsu, Tomoyuki Ushimoto, Hideo Inaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to compare characteristics of emergency medical service (EMS)-transported emergencies and outcomes of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) among domestic and non-domestic visitors in Japan. Utstein-style OHCA registry data from 2018 to 2023 were merged with relevant transport data to identify patient residency status (domestic or non-domestic visitors). EMS-transported emergencies involving non-domestic visitors were characterised by higher incidences during non-daytime hours, in Tokyo, and at public locations and higher proportions of severe emergency cases. Non-domestic visitor OHCAs were associated with shorter EMS response time intervals, younger age, and lower proportions of witness, presumed cardiac aetiology, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation, and worse outcomes. Neurologically favourable 1-month outcomes of bystander-witnessed OHCA were significantly worse in non-domestic visitors than in domestic visitors, regardless of witness status and aetiology: 8.2% in non-domestic visitors (312/3817) vs. 14.4% in domestic visitors (2880/20,020) (adjusted odds ratio [95% confidence interval], 0.61 [0.55–0.68]). Bystander-witnessed OHCA outcomes remained worse in non-domestic visitors, even after propensity score matching (9.1% vs. 14.2%, 0.65 [0.54–0.79]; estimated difference [95% confidence interval], 3.7 [2.5–4.9] %). Introduction

In many countries, local authorities are responsible for introducing and managing public emergency medical services (EMS)1. These regional services cover both residents and visitors, including domestic and international travellers. The likelihood of local EMS responding to emergencies is increasing because of the growing number of long-distance commuters, business travellers, and tourists. Both domestic and non-domestic visitors may experience medical or non-medical emergencies that lead to out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) and require immediate EMS activation2. However, little is known about the differences in the characteristics, severity of emergencies, and outcomes of OHCA between domestic and non-domestic visitors3. Furthermore, these differences may be accentuated in Japan because the concept of a “single-ethnic nation” is deeply ingrained within Japanese society.

The number of foreign tourists and business travellers to Japan is increasing, although a sharp and marked decline was observed during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic (2020–2021)4. Responses to emergencies and the outcomes of OHCA may differ between these two groups due to variations in EMS systems across countries and changes in bystander reactions to emergencies and OHCA.

Since 2005, the Fire and Disaster Management Agency has provided an Utstein-style5 OHCA database6 and, since 2015, a database of EMS-transported emergency cases7. The latter includes the residency classification of patients transported, which is based on Resident Basic Registry System in Japan as follows8: Japanese and foreign residents of local municipalities that maintain regional EMS; domestic visitors, including commuters, business travellers, and private travellers, who have residency in Japan; and others, including foreign business travellers and tourists without residency in Japan, and persons with unknown residency.

In this study, we combined nationwide EMS transport and Utstein-style OHCA databases to provide an overview of the epidemiology of EMS-transported emergency cases among visitors and to compare the characteristics and neurological outcomes of OHCA between domestic and non-domestic visitors in Japan. We hypothesized that neurological outcomes after OHCA would differ by visitor status after adjustment for established prognostic factors.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study. Data from the All-Japan Utstein Registry, a prospective, nationwide, population-based registry of patients with OHCA, and nationwide data on EMS transportation in Japan from 1 January 2018 to 31 December 2023 were analysed after obtaining permission from the Fire and Disaster Management Agency (FDMA). This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines for observational studies. Patients were not involved in the planning, design, data management, or evaluation of the results of this study.

Sample size

Statistical sample size calculations were not performed before this extensive population-based observational study, and the sample size was determined based on the number of emergencies and OHCA cases transported by the EMS during the study period. This decision was based on the retrospective nature of the study and the feasibility of collecting all eligible cases within a defined timeframe. To ensure the robustness of the results, post hoc power calculations were performed for the primary outcome in each analysis using a chi-squared test with continuity correction and a two-sided significance level of P < 0.059. The observed power levels were greater than 99% for the primary and secondary outcomes, indicating sufficient statistical confidence.

Japanese EMS system

In Japan, each ambulance team comprises three or more EMS personnel, including at least one paramedic. EMS teams are not allowed to terminate resuscitation efforts in the field unless the patient in OHCA is decapitated, severely burned, or shows signs of postmortem decomposition. All paramedics are authorised to perform specific resuscitation procedures, including the use of airway devices, such as supraglottic airways or laryngeal mask airways, and administer a peripheral venous infusion of Ringer’s lactate for OHCA cases. However, only specially trained and authorised paramedics are permitted to insert tracheal tubes and administer intravenous adrenaline to patients. Unlike paramedics in other countries who offer advanced prehospital emergency care, Japanese paramedics can only administer adrenaline and glucose. EMS teams manage individuals with OHCA according to protocols established by their regional medical control councils, which are based on the guidelines of the Japan Resuscitation Council10. Since 2014, Japanese paramedics qualified to administer adrenaline for OHCA have been allowed to establish intravenous access before OHCA, followed by fluid transfusion for severely injured and sick patients aged ≥ 8 years, and glucose administration for unconscious patients11.

Databases and participants

All fire departments in Japan collect data for the EMS transport and Utstein-style5 OHCA databases. The Fire and Disaster Management Agency cleans the data collected from these fire departments and ensures the quality of the database by requesting corrections and re-examination for logically inconsistent data. Because the Utstein-style registry does not contain detailed information on occurrence location and residency, the OHCA data were merged with EMS transport data to identify visitor status. The resident/visitor classification of patients, in this database was as follows:

Residents: Both foreigners and Japanese registered as residents under the jurisdiction of the fire department headquarters in the unique resident registration system8.

Residents: Both foreigners and Japanese registered as residents under the jurisdiction of the fire department headquarters in the unique resident registration system8.

Domestic visitors: Commuters, business travellers, or private travellers with residency in Japan.

Non-domestic visitors: Foreign business travellers and private tourists without residency in Japan, including rare cases where residency status is unknown, even after efforts to identify residency or nationality in cooperation with the police department and the hospital.

Non-domestic visitors: Foreign business travellers and private tourists without residency in Japan, including rare cases where residency status is unknown, even after efforts to identify residency or nationality in cooperation with the police department and the hospital.

Analyses comparing outcomes were restricted to domestic and non-domestic visitors.

As a result, a comprehensive database of 56,980 EMS-transported visitor patients with OHCA was created and subjected to the main outcome analysis.

We defined data-matching success for our merging process as cases where two or more of the three time records (emergency call, EMS arrival at the scene, and EMS hospital arrival) were matched, along with the prefecture of occurrence. Patient sex- and age-matches were also checked, retrospectively. Cases (0.96% [31,478]) with missing information on variables essential for linkage (age, sex, prefecture, residency, location, or time records) were excluded from the EMS-transported emergency database (N = 32,868,323) before this database was merged with the Utstein-style OHCA database (N = 792,364) (Fig. 1). Although missing data and input errors might cause the failure of merging (combining and reconciling), the rate of failure was low at 1.9% (14,682/792,364). For propensity score matching and inverse probability weighting (IPW) analyses, there were no missing data for variables in the final visitor (non-resident) OHCA cohort; therefore, no imputation was performed.

Outcome measures and severity

The primary outcome was neurologically favourable 1-month survival, defined as a Cerebral Performance Category12 of 1 or 2. The secondary outcomes were prehospital return of spontaneous circulation and 1-month survival rates. Emergency severity was initially categorised as outpatient discharge, hospital admission, or outpatient death. In this study, outpatient death was defined as death declared in the emergency department or before hospital admission.

Statistical analyses

Differences in variables between the two groups were first analysed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables. Multivariable logistic regression analyses of parameters, including age, sex, witness status, OHCA aetiology, initial electrocardiography (ECG) rhythm, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), at-home OHCA, and EMS response time interval (time interval between emergency call receipt and EMS contact with patients), were performed to compare primary and secondary outcomes between the two groups among subgroups of OHCA. Furthermore, multivariable logistic regression analyses with interaction tests were repeated after propensity matching for bystander-witnessed OHCA to compare outcomes between the two groups. Propensity score matching included detailed characteristics, bystander and EMS responses, and time-related factors. As a sensitivity analysis, IPW based on propensity scores was performed to estimate the average treatment effect on the treated (ATET), comparing non-domestic visitors with domestic visitors. The null hypothesis was evaluated for each analysis at a significance level of P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using the JMP software (JMP Pro 18, SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and StataMP 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive analyses and propensity score matching were conducted using JMP Pro. IPW was performed using Stata.

Ethical consideration

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Kanazawa Medical University (project I-729). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Ethics Committee because this study used retrospective, anonymised registry data. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Results

Study cohort and overview (Fig. 1)

As this study focused on visitor classification and other background characteristics of EMS-transported emergency cases, 31,347 cases with unknown residency, age, sex, and/or location were excluded from the 32,868,323 emergency cases transported by EMS between 2018 and 2023. In the remaining 32,836,976 EMS-transported emergency cohort cases, the characteristics of the patients were compared between the two visitor groups. Utstein-style data for 792,363 OHCA cases were merged with the emergency cohort according to age, sex, prefecture, and time records, creating an extended database for 777,502 OHCA cases (all OHCA cohort), and then 56,980 visitors’ OHCA cases, which were subjected to comparisons of characteristics and outcomes of OHCA among the two groups, using univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses. Since the bystander response to OHCA and the time from the witness’s perspective are expected to be associated with outcomes, the most critical comparisons of outcomes between the two groups of visitors were conducted in bystander-witnessed visitor OHCA cohort (N = 23,837) after propensity score matching.

The proportions of visitor emergencies in all EMS transported emergency cases were 10.5% (1,099,894/10,467,631) in 2008/2009, 8.8% (838,465/9,513,019) in 2020/2021, and 9.1% (1,168,331/12,856,330) in 2022/2023. The proportion was the highest in 2018/2019 and the lowest in 2020/2021 (χ² = 20128.6, P < 0.001).

As shown in Supplementary Table 1, the proportion of EMS-transported emergency cases during the pandemic peak period (2020/2021) was lower among non-domestic visitors than among domestic visitors. The proportion of EMS-transported emergency cases during weekends/holidays and daytime was higher among domestic visitors than among non-domestic visitors. The incidence in Tokyo was markedly higher among non-domestic visitors, and emergencies among them more frequently occurred in public locations. As shown in Table 1B, in terms of case characteristics, non-domestic visitors had a higher proportion of male patients, and non-elderly adults. Conversely, non-domestic visitors had a higher proportion of medical emergencies. Although non-domestic cases were more frequently transported to tertiary emergency medical centres, death was more commonly declared in emergency departments (outpatient deaths) (Supplementary Table 2).

Fundamental characteristics of OHCA among the domestic and non-domestic visitors

Non-domestic visitors were characterised by a younger age and shorter response time interval than domestic visitors (Table 1). Regarding arrest circumstances, unwitnessed arrests were more frequent among non-domestic visitors, whereas bystander-witnessed cases were more common among domestic visitors. Non-domestic visitors also had a higher proportion of arrests occurring at home (including private lodging) and lower proportions of presumed cardiac aetiology and initial shockable rhythms. The rate of bystander CPR was markedly lower among non-domestic visitors (Table 1).

Differences in outcomes of OHCA subgroups among domestic and non-domestic visitors

Comparisons of domestic and non-domestic visitors in the univariate analyses revealed that the outcomes of OHCA for domestic visitors were significantly better than those for non-domestic visitors who experienced OHCA; the neurologically favourable 1-month survival rate was 8.6% for domestic visitors compared to 4.5% for non-domestic visitors (P < 0.001). These differences were significant even when analyses were performed within the OHCA subgroups. Furthermore, unlike the former comparisons between domestic and non-domestic visitors, the differences remained significant after adjustment for multivariable logistic regression analyses (Table 2).

Propensity score matching analysis for the primary outcome of bystander-witnessed OHCA

To study the possible contribution of time factors from the time of witness and bystanders’ responses to the difference in outcomes between domestic and non-domestic visitors, propensity score matching was conducted for bystander-witnessed OHCAs among visitors. Before propensity score matching, the proportions of cases during the daytime, with presumed cardiac aetiology, involving medical causes, with shockable initial rhythms, involving bystander-performed prehospital defibrillation (public access defibrillation), and involving dispatcher-assisted CPR instruction, bystander CPR provision, and prehospital adrenaline administration were considerably higher among domestic visitors than among non-domestic visitors. The proportion of cases in the core prefectures was much higher among non-domestic visitors than among domestic visitors. Furthermore, the bystander-patient relationship primarily differed between the two groups of visitors (Supplementary Table 3, left). After propensity score matching, there was a small but significant difference only in the proportion of core prefectures between the two groups (Supplementary Table 3, right).

Contrary to expectations, the rates of neurologically favourable 1-month survival in both groups were not considerably altered by propensity score matching: 9.1% in non-domestic visitors compared with 14.2% in domestic visitors (adjusted OR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.54–0.79). Furthermore, multivariable logistic regression analysis with the interaction test showed that neither aetiology nor initial ECG rhythms significantly altered the association of visitor classification with neurologically favourable outcomes (P for interaction, 0.30 and 0.27, respectively) (Table 3).

Discussion

In this nationwide study, we briefly reviewed differences in characteristics of EMS-transported emergencies between domestic and non-domestic visitors. We next performed detailed comparisons of characteristics and outcomes of OHCAs among domestic and non-domestic visitors to Japan. We found that the non-domestic visitors had significantly worse outcomes than domestic visitors, even after adjustments for background factors and propensity score matching. Sensitivity analyses using IPW confirmed that the association between non-domestic visitor status and poorer neurological outcome was robust across analytic approaches. In addition, subgroup analyses during the COVID-19 pandemic suggested a disproportionate decline in bystander CPR among non-domestic visitors, indicating that situational disruptions may exacerbate existing outcome disparities. These findings indicate the presence of outcome disparities associated with visitor status; however, the underlying mechanisms should be interpreted cautiously.

The expansion of social and private activities beyond municipalities and countries is a natural consequence of a more interconnected world13. In many core municipalities of Japan, the daytime population can be much higher than the nighttime population owing to long-distance commuting from surrounding areas14. While the number of foreign tourists and business travellers to Japan has increased every year, a sharp and significant decline was observed during the COVID-19 pandemic’s peak (2020 and 2021)4. Since 2023, the number of foreign tourists and business travellers to Japan has increased sharply and is higher than that in 20194. However, little is known about the actual and current differences in the characteristics, severity, and outcomes of OHCA between domestic and non-domestic visitors3.

Our analyses revealed that OHCAs in non-domestic visitors were characterised by a shorter response time interval and a younger age, which are known to be associated with better outcomes. However, OHCAs in non-domestic visitors were characterised by a higher proportion of unwitnessed cases and a lower proportion of presumed cardiac aetiology and shockable initial rhythms, all of which are known to be associated with worse outcomes. In addition to low bystander CPR rates, non-domestic visitors more frequently experienced unwitnessed arrests and non-shockable rhythms, which are empirically associated with poorer outcomes. Emergency phone numbers and medical and insurance systems vary by country15–19. While FDMA and the Japan Tourism Agency provide information to foreign tourists, it is unclear to what extent this information is recognised and understood20,21. Furthermore, non-domestic visitors may not be fully familiar with Japan’s healthcare system, which could result in delays in the activation of EMS and other medical services before the occurrence of OHCA. The finding that EMS-transported cases in non-domestic visitors were more severe than those in domestic visitors may support this assumption. The outcomes of OHCAs for non-domestic visitors were significantly worse than those for domestic visitors, even after adjusting for disadvantageous factors using multivariable logistic regression and IPW.

To exclude the possible involvement of other characteristics and time factors not included in these analyses, we matched propensity scores and compared the outcomes of bystander-witnessed OHCA between the two visitor groups. Before matching, the proportions of medical cases and friend/colleague-witnessed OHCA and prehospital defibrillation by bystanders were lower, and the proportion of passerby-witnessed OHCA was higher. Although these characteristics are disadvantageous for better outcomes22, propensity score matching did not alter the outcome differences or the results of the univariate or multivariable logistic regression analyses. Neurologically favourable outcomes were unexpectedly worse in non-domestic visitors than in domestic visitors. Therefore, factors not included in ordinary data collection may associated with disparities of non-domestic visitors.

The quality of CPR23,24 and other resuscitation procedures performed by bystanders and EMS may be one of the potential factors associated with the worst outcomes in non-domestic visitors25–27. In addition, race was not measured in the present study; however, it may represent an unmeasured factor potentially associated with OHCA outcomes28,29. Another possible factor is the severity of illness and accidents causing OHCA (Supplementary Table 1B). More dangerous actions by non-domestic visitors may increase the severity of accidents and injuries, decreasing the chance of survival from subsequent OHCA30.

This study highlights the need for targeted strategies to address disparities in OHCA outcomes between domestic and non-domestic visitors in Japan. The poorer outcomes among non-domestic visitors compared with domestic visitors suggest the presence of communication, cultural, or situational barriers that hinder timely bystander intervention and optimal EMS care. Strengthening multilingual emergency communication, improving awareness of Japan Tourism Agency resources, and enabling foreign visitors to preregister key medical information via dedicated apps could close this gap. Expanding CPR and AED training for staff in tourist hubs, particularly in Tokyo and other central metropolitan prefectures, and enhancing public emergency preparedness in high-traffic areas may improve patient outcomes. Continued linkage of EMS transport and OHCA registries is essential for monitoring these disparities and evaluating the impact of policy and practice interventions. The system-level organization of post-OHCA care, including the availability of specialized cardiac arrest centres, may influence outcomes beyond prehospital factors alone31. Future studies comparing OHCA cases among domestic and non-domestic visitors occurring in the same locations may help clarify differences in bystander response and emergency care activation.

Study limitations

This study has some limitations. First, detailed patient background data were not available. Consequently, important determinants of OHCA outcomes, including pre-existing comorbidities, socioeconomic status, substance use, and the time interval between collapse and discovery, could not be assessed. Second, the database did not include detailed information on the nationality or country of origin of non-domestic visitors; therefore, subgroup analyses by nationality could not be performed. Third, data specific to bystanders, such as age, sex, and training experience, were not included in the database and were therefore unavailable for the study. This may have influenced the results. Fourth, the study was conducted in Japan, a monoethnic country, which may have influenced the results. Finally, as with other observational studies, data validity is another potential limitation.

Conclusion

Non-domestic visitors experienced significantly worse neurological outcomes after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest than domestic visitors, even after adjusting for known confounders and propensity score matching. These findings suggest the presence of communication, cultural, or situational barriers that hinder timely bystander intervention and optimal EMS care.
