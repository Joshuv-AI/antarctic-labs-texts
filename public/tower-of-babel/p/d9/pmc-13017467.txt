Characteristics Associated With Disseminated Gonococcal Infections vs Uncomplicated Gonorrhea: A California Case-control Study, 2021–2022
Authors: Kelly A Johnson, Roshani Fernando, Rosalyn Plotzker, Nicole O Burghardt, Robert E Snyder, Wyatt Hanft, Kurtis Mohr, Lizzete Alvarado, Gwenyth Crise, Eric C Tang, Kathleen Jacobson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOdds of disseminated versus uncomplicated gonorrhea increased among people not prioritized for routine uncomplicated gonorrhea screening, including men who only had sex with female partners (odds ratio [OR] 10.8; 95% confidence interval [CI], 4.8–24.1), people using substances (OR 10.0; 95% CI, 5.4–18.6), and older people (OR 1.09 per year; 95% CI, 1.07–1.11).

Disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI) occurs when Neisseria gonorrhoeae invades the bloodstream from a mucosal site and travels to distant sites, resulting in potentially serious sequalae including septic arthritis, meningitis, endocarditis, and even death [1]. California (CA) has a high burden of DGI, with >400 reported cases and 8 deaths between July 2020 and May 2024. Because characteristics associated with disseminated infection remain incompletely understood, we conducted a case-control study comparing people with DGI versus uncomplicated gonorrhea (GC), aiming to assess whether specific patient factors or sexual behaviors increased odds of disseminated infection.

METHODS

We leveraged CA sexually transmitted disease (STD) surveillance data from 1 July 2021 through 30 April 2022 to compare people with DGI (“cases”) to people with uncomplicated GC (“controls,” defined as having localized GC at a mucosal site—the pharynx, rectum, or urogenital tract). DGI cases included people who fulfilled the verified (Neisseria gonorrhoeae identified at a disseminated site [ie, blood or synovial fluid]) or likely (GC identified at a mucosal site plus clinical signs/symptoms of DGI) surveillance case definitions [2]. We included data from all CA counties except San Francisco and Los Angeles, which use separate surveillance systems.

We restricted our analysis to DGI cases with case report forms completed by state/local health departments and GC controls who had been interviewed as part of enhanced CA surveillance efforts (representing a random sample of GC infections). Controls and cases were matched by gender and race/ethnicity at a 3:1 ratio (with controls randomly selected from a pool of 786 interviewed GC cases). We selected gender and race/ethnicity for matching as they were otherwise considered potential confounders: GC and DGI have differential impacts by gender (since females are historically at increased risk of DGI [1, 3]) and—due to a variety of structural inequities [4]—there are racial disparities in sexually transmitted infection (STI) morbidity [5]. Our race/ethnicity categories were mutually exclusive: anyone reporting Hispanic ethnicity was categorized as Hispanic (regardless of race) [6].

We compared between-group sociodemographic and behavioral characteristics—including age, gender of sex partners, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status, prior bacterial STIs (syphilis, chlamydia, or a prior GC diagnosis reported to public health within 12 months of the current GC/DGI diagnosis), and self-reported substance use (cocaine, heroin, and/or methamphetamine) in the past 12 months—using Wilcoxon 2-sample or Pearson chi-squared tests for continuous and categorical variable as appropriate. We then estimated the odds of developing DGI versus uncomplicated GC by each sociodemographic/behavioral characteristic using unadjusted logistic regression.

The CA Health and Human Services Agency's Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects determined this study was exempt from review as all data were originally collected for public health practice.

RESULTS

There were 128 reported DGI cases within our study dates, of which 119 (93%) had completed case report forms and were eligible for inclusion. These 119 DGI cases were matched to 357 GC controls. More than half of the study population was Hispanic (52%) and male (59%).

Compared to GC controls (Table 1), DGI cases were older (median 42 vs 29 years; P < .0001) and differed by gender of sex partners (P < .0001). Among DGI cases, 44% were males with female-only partners (men who have sex with women [MSW]) and 7% were male with male-only partners (men who have sex with men [MSM]), whereas controls were 19% MSW and 31% MSM. No females reported female-only partners, but a slightly higher proportion of cases versus controls identified as females with male-only partners (35% vs 33%).

Cases were less likely to have had another bacterial STI in the prior 12 months (12% vs 38% had prior syphilis, chlamydia, or GC; P < .0001) but more likely to report substance use. Over one third of cases (34% vs 5%) reported using any substances (heroin, cocaine, and/or methamphetamine) and nearly one quarter (24% vs 4%) reported using methamphetamine specifically (both P < .0001). There were no significant differences between cases and controls in HIV status (91% cases and 74% controls were HIV-negative) (P > .05).

In unadjusted logistic regression, odds of DGI (Supplementary Table 1) were statistically higher among people who reported: being MSW compared to MSM (odds ratio [OR] 10.8, 95% confidence interval [CI], 4.8–24.1), using substances (OR 10.0; 95% CI, 5.4–18.6 for any substance), and older age (OR 1.09 per year; 95% CI, 1.07–1.11). HIV status and a prior bacterial STI in the 12 months were not independently associated with increased odds of DGI versus uncomplicated GC. Instead, persons with GC who had a prior diagnosed/reported bacterial STI within 12 months had lower odds of developing DGI.

CONCLUSIONS

In this case-control study, we found that people who were older, MSW (compared to MSM), or reported substance use (heroin, cocaine, and/or methamphetamine) had higher odds of DGI compared with uncomplicated GC. These findings have important implications for clinicians and public health programs, particularly because taking a comprehensive sexual history and performing routine GC screening may not always be prioritized for these populations.

National STI screening guidelines [7] recommend routine GC screening for certain populations, including at least once among women younger than age 25 years as well as (at minimum) annual screening for MSM and people with HIV. There are no national recommendations for GC screening among MSW, people who use substances, or people older than age 25 years in the absence of identified risk factors. Accordingly, clinicians may not prioritize GC screening and may neglect to obtain comprehensive sexual health histories as part of routine healthcare maintenance in these groups. Because GC can often be asymptomatic (particularly early in the disease course and at nonurethral sites), these missed opportunities to assess individual behaviors and perform GC screening (when appropriate) could lead to missed GC diagnoses and the potential for complications like DGI.

For this reason, supported by our findings, clinicians should remain vigilant to the possibility of DGI in patients with compatible syndromes—including arthritis with dermatitis, migratory polyarthralgia, tenosynovitis, and septic arthritis [1]—even when occurring in individuals older than age 25 years or not MSM. Similarly, although females have been reported to be at increased risk for DGI (perhaps due to biochemical changes associated with menstruation, pregnancy, and postpartum states) [1, 3], most of the DGI cases in CA have occurred in men—hinting, as has been noted by the Centers of Disease Control and Prevention—that the epidemiology of DGI may be changing [1] and serving as a reminder that all people can be affected by disseminated GC. DGI should thus remain on the differential for any patients presenting with compatible symptoms, regardless of age or gender.

For public health, it is likely not feasible to adjust GC screening guidelines to capture all populations we identified as having increased odds of DGI (ie, older and heterosexual individuals at large) without becoming cost prohibitive. That said, STD surveillance programs could analyze GC and DGI rates within their jurisdictions, and—where supported by jurisdictional epidemiology—consider expanding asymptomatic screening recommendations. In CA, for example, routine GC screening is now recommended for all people using substances (especially methamphetamine) regardless of sexual orientation based on observed disproportionate impacts of DGI in this population [8, 9]. Of note, methamphetamine use may be a proxy for other less favorable social determinants of health and/or lead to immune dysregulation via reduced activity of the complement system [10], perhaps in part explaining the observed association between methamphetamine and DGI.

Our study had several limitations. First, given our control group, we were only able to compare DGI risk factors among people diagnosed with gonorrhea; we do not know how many people had undiagnosed GC infections that never resulted in DGI. Second, there may have been unmeasured sociodemographic/clinical characteristics that contributed to the development of DGI. Such characteristics could have included baseline health, comorbidities, immune status, or social determinants of health (eg, healthcare access or housing security). In terms of comorbidities, however, we do not suspect that known DGI risk factors [1, 3]—such as lupus, complement deficiencies, or use of the medication eculizumab—could have confounded our results: there was only 1 reported DGI case with lupus and none with either of the other 2 conditions. Third, we were unable to perform adjusted regression analyses since—due to the small number of DGI cases—such models would have become quickly oversaturated and provided unstable estimates. Fourth, it is conceivable that our results are not generalizable outside CA (or in San Francisco and Los Angeles), although substance use and/or age >25 years have been reported in DGI clusters elsewhere [9, 11, 12]. Fifth, while an analysis of pathogen-specific variables (ie, expression of virulence factors or genetic variation) was outside our scope, we acknowledge that additional research in this area would be helpful. Finally, since our DGI cases did not routinely undergo 3-site (pharynx, rectum, and urogenital tract) testing for GC, we were unable to determine which mucosal site(s) might be most likely to result in GC dissemination.

In summary, among those with gonorrhea, odds of DGI were increased in people who reported using substances, being MSW (compared to MSM), or being of older age. Clinicians should keep DGI on the differential diagnosis for all people with compatible syndromes (even those not traditionally prioritized for routine GC screening); STD control programs should consider expanding asymptomatic GC screening criteria if appropriate based on local epidemiology.
