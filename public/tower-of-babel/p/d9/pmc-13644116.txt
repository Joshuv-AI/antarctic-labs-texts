Obesity and diabetes associated with dysregulation of mevalonate pathway markers and progression from pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasia to invasive pancreatic tumor

AbstractBackgroundCholesterol metabolic reprogramming plays a key role in pancreatic cancer progression by regulating the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and influencing responses to immunotherapy. Background

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) ranks among the most aggressive and lethal malignancies worldwide, with poor prognosis and high mortality rates [1, 2]. PDAC progresses through a series of morphological changes, originating from pancreatic intraepithelial neoplasias (PanINs), which are histologically recognized as precursor lesions [3]. Several studies have identified significant risk factors for PanIN development, primary tumor formation, and subsequent metastasis, notably obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [4–8]. Interestingly, research on pancreatic cancer supports the notion that epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) may begin in PanIN lesions prior to tumorigenesis [9, 10]. Thus, assessing body mass index (BMI) and T2DM status alongside isolating PanIN lesions and invasive PDAC from pancreatic neoplasia may reveal mechanisms underlying PDAC initiation, progression, and invasion. Should high BMI and T2DM precede PanIN or tumor detection, as observed in murine models [11], a critical window could exist for prophylactic interventions in high-risk individuals.

Accumulating evidence indicates that dysregulation of the mevalonate pathway across multiple malignancies, including PDAC, promotes tumorigenesis and supports cancer cell survival [12–16]. Cholesterol, which is synthesized via the mevalonate pathway, is vital for cell membrane formation, impacting the immune microenvironment, tumor cell proliferation, and PanIN development [17, 18]. Inhibiting the mevalonate pathway—such as with statins—shows potential for delaying PanIN progression, EMT, and reducing pancreatic cancer risk [19].

SOAT1 and SREBP2 play pivotal roles in tumor progression and metastasis, impacting lipid storage and signaling pathways and correlating with poor prognosis [20, 21]. Sterol-regulatory-element-binding protein 2 (SREBP2) is a transcription factor and key regulator of cholesterol metabolism, overseeing the mevalonate pathway and cholesterol uptake [22]. Sterol-O-acyltransferase 1 (SOAT1), also known as ACAT1, another crucial enzyme in cholesterol homeostasis, converts excess cholesterol into inert cholesterol esters (CEs), which are stored in lipid droplets (LDs). LDs are abundant in pancreatic cancer tissue, supporting tumor growth and therapeutic resistance [23]. Growing evidence suggests that key regulators of the mevalonate pathway, such as SOAT1 and SREBP2, are frequently dysregulated in cancer, and that pharmacological or genetic inhibition of this metabolic axis can impede tumor progression across multiple malignancies, including hepatocellular carcinoma [24], cholangiocarcinoma [25], glioblastoma [26], melanoma [27], prostate [28], colorectal [29], lung [15], breast [30], and gastric [31] cancers with concordant evidence emerging from large-scale pan-cancer analyses [25]. However, the mechanisms underlying the roles of SOAT1 and SREBP2 in PDAC progression remain to be explored.

Research in PDAC mouse organoids further reveals that SOAT1 expression is regulated by P53 status, with mutant or inactivated P53 correlating with higher SOAT1 levels compared to tumors with functional P53 [16]. SOAT1 knockdown significantly decreases tumor cell proliferation, an effect amplified under cholesterol-rich conditions, likely due to cholesterol-induced lipotoxicity in the absence of adequate esterification [16].

Although upregulation of the mevalonate pathway is documented in PDAC models [32, 33], the interplay between SOAT1, SREBP2, P53, and CD8+ T lymphocytes in PDAC progression and pathology is not well understood. Additionally, the relationship between BMI, T2DM, cholesterol metabolism, and the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), as well as their combined effects on progression of PanIN lesions and PDAC, remains unclear [4]. Here, we investigate the co-expression patterns of SOAT1, SREBP2, P53, and CD8 and assess their associations with PanIN progression, tumor aggressiveness, and clinical outcomes. Our primary objective is to characterize metabolic–immune alterations during pancreatic tumor progression and to evaluate the potential of these markers as candidates for early detection and risk stratification in PDAC.

Materials and methods

Study design and dataset selection

In this study, we analyzed gene expression profiles in pancreatic cancer using publicly available datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Datasets were identified through a systematic search using predefined keywords, including “pancreatic cancer,” “PanIN,” “microarray,” “sequencing,” and “tumor vs. normal.” Eligible datasets were required to meet predefined inclusion criteria: (i) availability of raw or normalized gene expression data, (ii) clearly annotated sample groups, (iii) adequate sample size for reliable differential expression analysis, and (iv) overall data quality suitable for downstream bioinformatic processing. To minimize technical heterogeneity and enhance analytical robustness, datasets with consistent sampling characteristics, balanced group representation, and high signal-to-noise ratios were prioritized. Exploratory analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), were performed to confirm appropriate separation between biological groups.

Based on these criteria, two independent human datasets, GSE16515 and GSE15471, were selected, both comprising gene expression data derived from pancreatic tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues. GSE16515 included 52 samples (36 tumor and 16 normal), while GSE15471 contained 78 samples (39 tumor and 39 normal). To further investigate gene expression dynamics across pancreatic tumor progression, GSE63348—a mouse pancreatic ductal organoid dataset—was incorporated. This dataset includes organoid lines derived from normal pancreas, PanIN lesions, and pancreatic tumors, enabling structured evaluation of gene expression changes along the normal–PanIN–PDAC sequence. In total, GSE63348 comprised 19 samples (7 normal-derived, 6 PanIN-derived, and 6 tumor-derived organoid lines). All raw data were pre-processed and normalized prior to downstream analyses.

Differential gene expression analysis

We applied the Limma package (version 3.58.1) in R to assess the differential expression of SOAT1 and SREBF2 across defined sample groups within the datasets included in this study. Differential expression analysis was performed independently for each dataset. Genes with a P-value ≤ 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed.

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis

To investigate the functional relationships among key genes of interest, a PPI network was constructed centered around four main target genes: SOAT1, SREBF2, TP53, and CD8A. Interacting proteins for these genes were retrieved from the STRING database (https://string-db.org/), and the network was visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.10.3). This analysis facilitated the identification of essential interacting proteins and functional clusters potentially involved in the progression of pancreatic cancer.

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis

To better understand the biological roles of the DEGs and their associated proteins, GO enrichment analysis was performed on the genes constituting the SOAT1- and SREBF2-centered PPI network using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.10.1) in R. The adjusted P-value threshold was set at 0.05 using the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) correction method. Enrichment results were obtained from the Enrichr database (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) and analyzed in two main GO categories: Biological Process (BP) and Molecular Function (MF). GO terms with adjusted P-values ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Patients and tissue specimens

For RNA expression analysis, 15 FFPE and 20 fresh PDAC tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples were immediately preserved in RNA stabilization solution, transported to the laboratory, and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Samples collected between 2017 and 2018 at Imam Khomeini Hospital, Tehran, Iran, were gathered for the study.

To analyze protein expression, FFPE tissue samples were obtained from a consecutive cohort of 222 PDAC patients who underwent pancreatic resection between 2015 and 2021 at Imam Khomeini Hospital, Tehran. This cohort consisted of two groups: The PanIN-present group (with 65, 40, and 35 patients for SOAT1, SREBP2, and P53, respectively) and the non-PanIN group (with 157, 114, 101, and 60 patients for SOAT1, SREBP2, P53, and CD8, respectively). Adjacent non-tumorigenic pancreatic tissue samples were collected from 98 of these patients.

Patients with a history of pancreatic cancer, pancreatic cysts, IgG4 disease, pancreatectomy, or those who had undergone preoperative radiation or chemotherapy were excluded from the study. Ethical approval was obtained from the Iran University Medical Sciences Research Ethics Committee (Approval Code: IR.IUMS.REC.1399.722).

Laser capture microdissection (LCM)

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections of 35 pancreatic cancer tissues (15 FFPE and 20 fresh cases) were reviewed by the pathologist (F.A.A. and M.R.). LCM was used to isolate pure cell populations from 4 FFPE and 13 fresh samples, focusing on PanIN lesions and adjacent normal tissues identified morphologically, while preserving complex cellular interactions [34, 35]. Fresh tissues were sectioned at 10 μm thickness, fixed in ethanol, and stored at − 80 °C. FFPE samples were sectioned at 8 μm, rehydrated, and stained. Microdissection was then performed on both fresh and FFPE samples using the same PALM MicroBeam system (Carl Zeiss, Göttingen, Germany), yielding approximately 10,000 cells per sample.

Gene expression analysis via reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from microdissected tissues using the Qiagen RNeasy Mini Kit (Cat No. 74104) for fresh tissues, and the Azma Elixir Pajooh Fast RNrich FFPE Tissue Kit (Cat No. AEFRX-1156) for FFPE tissues. Reverse transcription of 1 µg of RNA was performed using the PrimeScript 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (TaKaRa, Cat #RR037A). Specific RT-qPCR primers (Table S1), designed with Primer-BLAST and OligoAnalyzer 3.1., were utilized on a Qiagen Rotor-Gene Q system. GAPDH served as the internal control gene, with relative expression calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [36].

Pathologic examination

PanIN lesions and normal pancreatic tissues were analyzed at least 2 cm from the tumor to reduce chronic pancreatitis risk. A blind histological review was conducted by pathologists (F.A.A. and M.R.) to confirm tissue characteristics.

Tissue microarray (TMA) construction

For the generation of a TMA, we used FFPE material from a patient cohort of 222 patients with primary PDAC. Whole-mount FFPE blocks were sliced into 4‐µm‐thick sections, mounted on slides, and stained with H&E. Subsequently, an experienced pathologist (F.A.A.) marked the respective areas on the slides, each tumor, PanIN, and adjacent normal pancreas in the three most representative areas and across various sections of each block. To generate the TMA, cores of 1 mm diameter were cut out of the donor block and placed into the recipient TMA block using a precision arraying tool (Galileo CK3500 TMA, ISENET, Milan, Italy). Two µm tissue sections were extracted from the completed array blocks and mounted onto superfrost slides. All the replicas were evaluated individually, and the final score was calculated as the average of the three cores.

Histological staining

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining were performed on 2-µm sections of FFPE tissues. H&E staining was done according to routine diagnostic protocols. Immunohistochemical staining was performed as previously described [37, 38]. Briefly, after deparaffinization and rehydration, the slides were immersed in a 3.0% H2O2 for 20 min. Primary antibodies against SOAT1, SREBP2, P53, and CD8, together with an IgG-isotype control (Table S2), were incubated at 4 °C overnight. Slides were then loaded with secondary antibodies for 30 min. Afterward, the immunoreaction was visualized by using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) and hematoxylin. Images were captured using an OPTIKA B-350 microscope.

Evaluation of immunostaining and scoring system

Two independent pathologists (F.A.A. and M.R.), blinded to the sample details, assessed staining intensity and the percentage of positive tumor cells in the TMA sections. The staining intensity was classified as absent (0), weak (1), medium (2), and strong (3). The percentage of positive cells was categorized as 0 (< 5%), 1 (5–25%), 2 (25-50%), 3 (50-75%), and 4 (> 75%). The final immunohistochemical score (H-score) was obtained by multiplying the staining intensity value by the corresponding positive-cell percentage category [39]. Interrater variability and agreement levels were assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, ensuring a robust and reliable evaluation of the immunohistochemical data.

Patients data collection

Tumor Grading and Staging:

PDAC samples were graded and staged according to the AJCC TNM classification system. Tumor grade was classified as well-differentiated (Grade 1), moderately differentiated (Grade 2), or poorly differentiated (Grade 3) based on cellular differentiation, mitotic activity, and histological features. Staging assessed tumor size (T), lymph node involvement (N), and distant metastasis (M), ranging from Stage I (localized) to Stage IV (metastatic) disease [40].

Pancreatic Intraepithelial Neoplasia (PanIN):

PanIN lesions, precursors to PDAC, were classified morphologically and are recognized for their potential to progress to invasive carcinoma [41].

Body mass index (BMI):

BMI was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²) and categorized per WHO guidelines: Underweight (< 18.5), normal (18.5–24.9), overweight (25–29.9), and obese (≥ 30). The association between BMI and SOAT1 expression with SOAT1 expression and cholesterol metabolism in PDAC was analyzed.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM):

T2DM was defined using standard clinical criteria. Fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 126 mg/dL or a documented medical diagnosis of T2DM was used to classify participants.

Definition of Survival Indices:

Survival indices were defined as follows: overall survival (OS) was measured from the date of Whipple surgery to the date of death from any cause; disease-specific survival (DSS) was defined as the time from surgery to death specifically due to PDAC; and progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the period from surgery to the last follow-up visit without evidence of disease relapse or metastasis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by GraphPad Prism version 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, USA) and SPSS version 27.0 (SPSS, Inc., IBM Corp., USA). Pearson’s chi-square test was carried out to determine the significance of correlation analysis. One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post hoc test, Kruskal–Wallis, and Mann–Whitney U tests were conducted to compare the study groups pairwise. Furthermore, the Benjamini‒Hochberg procedure was used to adjust P-values. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

GEO datasets analysis revealed altered expression of SOAT1 and SREBF2 in PDAC tumors

In the analyzed human datasets, 15,032 and 18,822 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified in GSE16515 and GSE15471, respectively. A total of 13,757 genes were shared between the two datasets, including SOAT1 and SREBF2 (Fig. 1A). Both SOAT1 and SREBF2 were more highly expressed in tumors in GSE16515, whereas in GSE15471, SOAT1 was upregulated but SREBF2 was downregulated in tumors (Fig. 1B). These differences likely reflect biological and technical variability, despite the fact that the majority of studies have reported increased SREBF2 expression in tumor tissues [42, 43].

To further evaluate the expression patterns of these markers during pancreatic tumor progression, we analyzed the mouse pancreatic ductal organoid dataset GSE63348, which includes organoid lines derived from normal pancreas, PanIN, and tumor tissues. In this dataset, both SOAT1 and SREBF2 were significantly upregulated in tumor-derived organoids compared with normal- and PanIN-derived organoids, indicating a progressive increase during the PanIN-to-tumor transition (Fig. 1B).

PPI analysis identified hub genes interacting with SOAT1 and SREBF2 aligned with DEG results

The resulting PPI network consisted of 34 nodes and 263 edges (Fig. 1C), with an interaction score threshold > 0.4. Proteins directly interacting with SOAT1 and SREBF2 were extracted, and only those overlapping with the common DEGs were retained. These genes were identified as hub genes within the network.

GO enrichment analysis reveals key pathways associated with PDAC progression

GO enrichment analysis was performed on genes in the PPI network centered on SOAT1 and SREBF2 to explore their functional roles in PDAC progression. Enriched pathways were identified and visualized using the clusterProfiler package (Fig. 1D).

A total of 9 significant Molecular Function (MF) terms and 126 significant Biological Process (BP) terms were identified (adjusted P-value < 0.05), with the top five terms in each category highlighted in the analysis (Fig. 1E; Supporting Data Values). Notably, the enriched GO terms were mainly related to lipid metabolic processes, sterol biosynthesis, membrane organization, and regulation of metabolic pathways.

SOAT1 and SREBF2 mRNA expression increase during PDAC progression

To determine how two key genes of cholesterol metabolism, SOAT1 and SREBF2, change across PDAC progression, we sought to compare their expression, in tumor tissues (T, n = 17), microdissected PanIN lesions (P, n = 17), and microdissected normal cells (N, n = 17) using qPCR (Fig. 2A). A representative photograph before and after the microdissection of the PanIN is shown in Fig. 2B. Consistent with bioinformatic findings, mRNA expression of SOAT1 and SREBF2, the master regulators of the cholesterol biosynthetic pathway, showed a progressive increase during tumor progression, with markedly higher levels observed in tumor tissues compared to PanIN lesions and normal cells (Fig. 2C).

Increased mRNA expression of SOAT1 and SREBF2 is associated with worse clinicopathological outcomes

SOAT1 and SREBF2 mRNA expression have been proposed to correlate with clinicopathological features of malignancy [31–45]. In PanIN cells, SOAT1 expression correlated with advanced TNM stage. SREBF2 expression was significantly associated with perineural and lymphovascular invasion. In tumor tissues, higher SOAT1 expression was significantly associated with higher histological grade. Furthermore, SREBF2 expression in tumor tissues showed significant associations with several clinicopathological parameters, including gender, larger tumor size, advanced TNM stage, and the presence of microscopic tumor extension (Table S2).

For further analysis, the Mann–Whitney U test was conducted and showed similar results (Fig. 2C). Taken together, these results indicate that increased expression of SOAT1 and SREBF2 is associated with worse clinicopathological outcomes in PDAC patients during tumor progression.

Association of mRNA co-expression of SOAT1 and SREBF2 with poor survival outcomes in PDAC patients

We next investigated the impact of combined mRNA expression of SOAT1 and SREBF2 on OS, DSS, and PFS in patients following surgery, as shown in the Kaplan–Meier survival curves. No significant differences in OS or PFS were observed between patients with high and low SOAT1/SREBF2 expression. In contrast, a significant difference in DSS was detected between the two groups (Fig. 2D).

Proteins associated with the mevalonate pathway and TIME are dysregulated during PDAC progression

To investigate whether similar changes occur at the protein level during PDAC progression, we examined the clinical significance and subcellular localization of SOAT1, SREBP2, P53, and CD8 in both PanIN-present and no-PanIN cases (Fig. 3A, B). P53 was observed in the nucleus (Fig. 3C) of pancreatic tumors, PanIN lesions, and adjacent normal tissues, while CD8 was detected in the cytoplasm within the tumor microenvironment (TME) of pancreatic tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples (Fig. 3D). SOAT1 (Fig. 4A) and SREBP2 (Fig. 4B) were detected in the cytoplasm with varying intensities. The mean H-scores for P53, SOAT1, and SREBP2 were 42, 117, and 84, in PanIN lesions, and 120, 180, and 140 in tumor tissues, respectively (Table S4). Hyperactivation of the mevalonate pathway, possibly accompanied by cholesterol-rich conditions leading to increased SOAT1, SREBP2, and P53 expression, may contribute to reduced CD8 expression [27, 46–49]. The mean H-scores for CD8 in the tumor tissue were very low compared to SOAT1, SREBP2, and P53 with an average of approximately 29 (Table S4).

As shown in Fig. 5A, the protein expression of SOAT1, SREBP2, and P53 was significantly upregulated in tumors compared with both PanINs and normal tissues. In addition, PanINs showed higher expression levels of SOAT1 and SREBP2 than normal tissues. In contrast, tumor samples exhibited a significant reduction in CD8 expression compared with adjacent normal tissues. Together, these findings suggest that upregulation of SOAT1 and SREBP2 is associated with increased P53 expression and decreased CD8 levels.

The protein expression of mevalonate pathway and TIME markers associated with worse clinicopathological outcomes

To this end, we performed Pearson’s chi-square test.

Association of SOAT1 protein expression with clinicopathological parameters in PanIN-present and non-PanIN patients’ groups

In PanIN-present PDAC cases, SOAT1 expression was directly associated with high BMI and histological grade, as well as the presence of T2DM and distant metastasis. In non-PanIN PDAC patients, significant associations were observed with high BMI, T2DM, larger median tumor size, as well as increased the likelihood of tumor development in the pancreatic head, the presence of perineural invasion, lymphovascular invasion, and distant metastasis (Table S5). These findings suggest that higher SOAT1 protein expression is associated with more aggressive tumor characteristics and increased metastatic potential in PDAC in both patient groups.

Association of SREBP2 protein expression with clinicopathological parameters in PanIN-present and non-PanIN patients’ groups

We then analyzed the association of SREBP2 expression with clinicopathological parameters. In PanIN-present PDAC cases, SREBP2 expression was significantly associated with the increased median age of patients and the presence of perineural invasion. Additionally, in non-PanIN PDAC cases, SREBP2 expression showed significant direct associations with high BMI and histological grade, as well as the likelihood of tumor development in the pancreatic head, the presence of T2DM, perineural invasion, and lymphovascular invasion (Table S6). These findings highlight the potential role of SREBP2 as a biomarker for tumor aggressiveness and invasive potential in PDAC.

Association of P53 protein expression with clinicopathological parameters in PanIN-present and non-PanIN patients’ groups

In PanIN-present PDAC patients, significant direct associations were found with high BMI, the presence of T2DM, perineural invasion, lymphovascular invasion, and microscopic tumor extension. For non-PanIN PDAC cases, P53 expression was significantly associated with high BMI, histological grade, and TNM stage (Table S7), which suggests a relationship between higher P53 levels and worse malignancy in PDAC.

Association of CD8 protein expression with clinicopathological parameters in non-PanIN patients’ group

Finally, our analysis indicated that there was no association between the expression of CD8 and clinicopathological features (Table S8).

Association of dysregulation of mevalonate pathway and TIME markers with obesity, T2DM, and metastasis

The analysis of mean expression levels of SOAT1, SREBP2, and P53 in tumor tissues of non-PanIN PDAC patients revealed direct significant associations with BMI, distant metastasis, and T2DM. Specifically, SOAT1 expression was significantly higher in obese patients compared to those with underweight and healthy weight (Fig. 5B). SREBP2 expression was also elevated in overweight patients relative to their underweight counterparts, and P53 levels were significantly higher in obese patients compared to those classified as underweight (Fig. 5B). In contrast, CD8 expression was higher in healthy weight patients compared to other groups (Fig. 5B). Regarding metabolic abnormalities, SOAT1 and SREBP2 expression was notably higher in diabetic patients compared to non-diabetic patients (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, SOAT1 expression was significantly elevated in patients with distant metastatic lesions compared to those without metastasis (Fig. 5D). Overall, these results suggest a potential association between obesity or overweight, T2DM, and elevated expression of key markers in the mevalonate pathway. While CD8+ T cell infiltration is suppressed in obese or overweight patients, obesity might trigger lipid uptake in CD8+ T cells, which could cause a metabolic shift toward fatty acid oxidation and away from glycolysis, potentially leading to impaired anti-tumor activity.

Co-expression signatures are associated with aggressive clinicopathological features in PDAC

The association between the co-expression of markers and various clinicopathological parameters in PDAC patients was assessed across all possible combinations. Co-expression of SOAT1/SREBP2 (Table 1), as well as higher-order co-expression involving SOAT1, SREBP2, P53, and CD8 (Table 2), was significantly associated with multiple clinicopathological features in PDAC samples. Specifically, these co-expression patterns correlated with higher BMI, T2DM, distant metastasis, and an increased risk of PanIN lesions.

Diagnostic assessment of SOAT1 and SREBP2

The diagnostic value of cytoplasmic expression of SOAT1 and SREBP2 in pancreatic tumors, compared to adjacent normal tissues and PanIN lesions, was assessed using ROC curve analysis. The analysis for SOAT1 cytoplasmic expression in pancreatic tumors yielded significant results when compared with adjacent normal tissues but not with PanIN lesions. Meanwhile, the cytoplasmic expression of SREBP2 in pancreatic tumors demonstrated significantly higher diagnostic value compared to both adjacent normal tissues and PanIN lesions (Fig. 5E). Taken together, SOAT1 and SREBP2 may have potential as biomarkers for distinguishing pancreatic cancer from precursor lesions and normal tissues, although further validation is required to confirm their reliability.

Prognostic evaluation in PDAC patients

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate the clinical significance of various parameters that might affect OS, DSS, or PFS in PDAC patients with SOAT1 expression and co-expression of SOAT1/SREBP2 (H-score).

Prognostic evaluation of SOAT1 expression

The univariate analyses identified gender, age, TNM stage, and distant metastasis as potential prognostic factors for OS, DSS, and PFS. Tumor size and lymph node metastasis were recognized as independent prognostic factors for OS and PFS, while histological grade was associated with PFS. In the multivariate Cox regression analysis, which included statistically significant factors from the univariate analyses, gender and age emerged as independent risk factors influencing OS, DSS, and PFS. Additionally, tumor size and TNM stage were identified as independent risk factors affecting OS and PFS. Histological grade and distant metastasis were also found to be significantly associated with PFS and DSS, respectively (Table S9).

Prognostic value of co-expression signatures

As shown in Tables 3 and 4, univariate analysis identified T2DM, PanIN presence, and distant metastasis as significant risk factors for OS, DSS, and PFS in PDAC patients with SOAT1/SREBP2 co-expression, as well as in patients with combined SOAT1/SREBP2/P53 expression and low CD8⁺ T cell infiltration. In multivariate analysis, a subset of these variables remained independent prognostic factors for patient outcomes. These associations suggest a potential association between metabolic dysregulation and aggressive clinicopathological characteristics in PDAC patients, although causality cannot be inferred from survival analyses alone.

Survival analysis

Patients had a median follow-up duration of five years.

PanIN-present PDAC patients with increased expression of SOAT1 and SREBP2 have poorer survival outcomes than non-PanIN patients

The Kaplan-Meier survival curves comparing the cytoplasmic expression levels of SOAT1 and SREBP2, as well as the nuclear expression levels of P53, between PDAC patients with and without PanIN lesions (Fig. 6A-C). In patients with cytoplasmic expression of SOAT1, no significant difference in OS was observed. In contrast, the PanIN-present group demonstrated significantly shorter DSS and PFS compared to the non-PanIN group (Fig. 6A). Cytoplasmic expression of SREBP2 was correlated with poor OS in patients with PanIN (Fig. 6B). Surprisingly, nuclear expression of P53, showed no significant differences in survival outcomes between the PanIN-present and non-PanIN groups (Fig. 6C). Collectively, these findings suggest that the expression levels of SOAT1 and SREBP2, along with the presence of PanIN, may serve as valuable prognostic biomarkers for PDAC.

Low expression of CD8 is associated with shorter OS in PDAC patients

Cytoplasmic expression of CD8 was a prognostic factor for OS. However, PDAC patients with low CD8 expression did not exhibit any significant differences in DSS and PFS compared to those with high CD8 expression (Fig. 6D).

3.13.3. Co-expression signatures are associated with worse survival outcomes

Patients with high co-expression of SOAT1/SREBP2, or with high co-expression of SOAT1/SREBP2/P53 combined with low CD8 expression, exhibited shorter OS, DSS, and PFS (Fig. 6E, F).

Discussion

Cancer cells exhibit metabolic reprogramming, a hallmark enabling energy production, biosynthesis, and the signaling pathways necessary for sustained survival, rapid proliferation, metastasis, and drug resistance [50]. Lipid metabolism enzymes, lipid-derived metabolites, and pathways regulated by lipid metabolism impact gene expression, chromatin remodeling, cellular differentiation, and stress response pathways, all contributing to tumorigenesis [51]. Cancer cells, unlike normal cells, often depend on the mevalonate pathway, making it a target for anti-cancer strategies, particularly through cholesterol synthesis reduction [19]. The mevalonate pathway is regulated by SREBP transcription factors, particularly SREBP2, which plays a key role in cholesterol and fatty acid metabolism [52]. SOAT1, an enzyme critical for cholesterol homeostasis, converts excess cholesterol into inert CEs stored in LDs [53]. Moreover, SOAT1 suppresses CD8+ T cell functionality, vital for antitumor immunity. Inhibiting SOAT1 can enhance CD8+ T cell function, facilitating immune checkpoint responses and the immunological synapse formation [27]. Recent studies link SOAT1 to immune infiltration within the TME, as SOAT1 expression correlates with chemokine and checkpoint gene expression, suggesting a role in immune evasion [29]. The TME significantly influences cancer progression and resistance to therapy.

This study identifies SOAT1, SREBP2, and P53 as progression factors in PDAC, consistently upregulated in tumor tissues compared to PanINs and normal counterparts. These findings corroborate recent research on organoid and mouse models indicating a progressive increase of these markers from normal pancreatic tissues to PanIN lesions and invasive tumors [16]. Metabolic shifts linked to SOAT1, SREBP2, and mutant P53 upregulation may modulate extracellular matrix (ECM) composition and dampen CD8+ T cell activity in PDAC [54].

Obesity and T2DM, known risk factors for PDAC, further dysregulate SOAT1, SREBP2, and cholesterol metabolism while concurrently impairing the immune system through inflammation-related mechanisms, potentially contributing to PanIN initiation, progression, and invasion [4]. The impact of these factors on PDAC development, progression, and metastasis, particularly in humans, remains incompletely understood. Elucidating alterations in the mevalonate pathway prior to PanIN formation and clarifying their potential role in PDAC prognostic stratification may provide valuable insights for early detection and the development of novel therapeutic strategies. The present study aims to further characterize these associations.

SOAT1 overexpression leads to an increased number of LDs, and the resulting lipid storage contributes to obesity, diabetes, and the risk of metabolic-driven PDAC progression [55]. Especially in individuals with obesity, elevated insulin levels may stimulate insulin and IGF-1 receptors in pancreatic cells, potentially enhancing proliferative signaling in PDAC [56]. Evidence suggests that weight loss through lifestyle changes or bariatric surgery reduces cancer risk, reinforcing the link between obesity and cancer [57]. Furthermore, the observed elevation of SOAT1, SREBP2, and P53 in PanIN lesions is noteworthy, particularly in the context of high BMI and T2DM, as these conditions are associated with an increased risk of PanIN lesions [4] and may contribute to early EMT and metastatic potential prior to overt tumorigenesis [10, 16]. In support of this, Rhim and colleagues declared that metastasis may begin before tumor formation, during the PanIN stage [10], particularly with dysregulated cholesterol biosynthesis.

Previous studies have reported an association between mevalonate pathway dysregulation and poor survival outcomes in several cancer types [58–61]. Notably, our results are in parallel with previous investigations showing that T2DM, perineural invasion, and distant metastasis were identified as risk factors for OS, DSS, and PFS in univariate analysis, and that co-expression of SOAT1 and SREBP2 provided additional prognostic value for DSS in multivariate analysis among PDAC patients with perineural invasion and distant metastasis [10, 16]. Our findings also showed that patients with high co-expression of SOAT1/SREBP2, as well as those with combined SOAT1/SREBP2/P53 expression and low CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, had worse prognosis for OS, DSS, or PFS compared with patients exhibiting SOAT1 expression alone.

Based on the previous studies, targeted inhibition of the dysregulated mevalonate pathway—specifically through SOAT1, SREBP2, and mutant P53 suppression—could be a promising therapeutic strategy for PDAC [22, 61–63]. SOAT1 inhibition enhances the effectiveness of immunotherapies, such as immune checkpoint blockade, cancer vaccines, and CAR-T cell therapies, by boosting T cell function [62, 64]. CD8+ T cell activation strategies, including TIM3 targeting approaches, hold promise for achieving enhanced efficacy in tumor control, thereby presenting a potential avenue for advancing pancreatic cancer immunotherapy.

In conclusion, given the limited availability of robust diagnostic markers for pancreatic cancer, preventive strategies may warrant particular emphasis alongside therapeutic approaches. T2DM and high BMI, as established epidemiological risk factors for PDAC, underscore the potential value of preventive screening in high-risk populations. Our findings highlight biologically meaningful associations between metabolic–immune alterations and pancreatic tumor progression. While metabolic risk management may represent an important consideration in high-risk individuals, further mechanistic studies are needed to clarify the pathways linking systemic metabolic dysregulation with PanIN development and PDAC progression. Screening high-risk groups could facilitate earlier detection and improved risk stratification. Such approaches may contribute to addressing the growing global burden of PDAC.

Strengths and limitations

Our study’s main strengths include a relatively large, clinically annotated pancreatic cancer cohort despite the disease’s rarity. Using laser-capture microdissection enabled precise separation of PanIN lesions and matched normal tissues, allowing accurate assessment of molecular changes during early tumor progression. Long-term patient follow-up supported robust survival analyses, highlighting the prognostic value of our markers in real-world settings. Limitations include the retrospective design, which may introduce selection bias, and a sample size adequate for initial analyses but requiring larger cohorts for validation. Functional activity of SOAT1 and SREBP2 was not assessed; future research should address this to better understand their roles in PDAC.

Conclusions

Our study showed that SOAT1, SREBP2, and P53 expression was elevated in pancreatic tumor tissues compared with PanIN lesions and normal tissues, whereas CD8 expression was significantly reduced within the tumor microenvironment of PDAC tissues. These altered marker levels correlated with disease progression, aggressive behavior, advanced stages, and worse survival. High BMI and T2DM, key independent risk factors for PDAC, are associated with dysregulation of the mevalonate pathway and alterations in the tumor immune microenvironment, which may contribute to PanIN and PDAC progression. Optimizing BMI and glycemic control may help reduce PDAC risk and improve patient survival outcomes.
