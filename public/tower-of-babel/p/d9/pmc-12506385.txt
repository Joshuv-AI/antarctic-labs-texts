Transcriptome analysis of five-toed jerboa organs reveals high-altitude adaptation mechanisms
Authors: Shan-shan Sun, Hao-ting Zhang, Hai-wen Yan, Xiao-yu Kang, Qi-qige Buren, Qian-cheng Wang, Ming Ming, Jie-ru Feng, Na Zhu, Xin Li, Yu Ling, Dong Zhang, Xiao-dong Wu, Shuai Yuan, He-ping Fu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-altitude environments are characterised by extreme conditions, including hypoxia, low temperatures, and intense ultraviolet radiation. Mammals inhabiting these environments have evolved unique adaptive mechanisms, the study of which elucidates survival strategies and evolutionary pathways under extreme conditions. Understanding how native high-altitude animals respond to such environments is highly important. This study investigated the high-altitude adaptation mechanisms of the five-toed jerboa (Orientallactaga sibirica) distributed in Qinghai Province (4229 m) and Hebei Province (498 m), China, through comparative transcriptomic analysis of heart, lung, and kidney tissues. The results revealed greater mRNA transcriptional differences in the lung tissue than in the heart and kidney tissues of high-altitude jerboas, indicating heightened lung sensitivity to high-altitude conditions. In lung tissue, high-altitude jerboas show differential expression of genes related to the Complement and Coagulation cascades, Heme binding, Oxidation-reduction process (such as MASP1, A2M, SERPING1, CD55, FGA, C5AR1, and KNG1), which may be associated with modulating immune functions to mitigate hypobaric hypoxia, intense radiation, and cold-induced damage and reducing thrombosis and inflammation risks. Heart tissue exhibits differential expression of Oxidative phosphorylation and Lipid metabolism genes (such as NDUFC2, NDUFA3, NDUFS4, COX4I2, PAFAH1B3, SGMS2 and PPAR2B), which may help maintain energy equilibrium under hypoxic and cold challenges. Kidney tissue exhibits differential enrichment of pathways such as arachidonic acid metabolism and steroid hormone biosynthesis mediated by genes including CYP4A11, CYP2C29, GPX2, PTGDS, CBR1, and UGT2B31, which may help coordinate vascular regulation, immune response, and oxidative balance to maintain systemic homeostasis. These pathways and genes are differentially enriched and expressed between high-altitude and low-altitude five-toed jerboas, which may be candidates for further functional studies of plateau environmental adaptability. Our findings provide candidate genes and pathways for intraspecies adaptations across microenvironments and highlight the need for further functional validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12096-6.Keywords: Orientallactaga sibirica, High-altitude adaptation, Hypoxic adaptation, Comparative transcriptomics

Introduction

High-altitude regions exhibit distinct climatic characteristics, including hypobaric hypoxia, extreme temperature fluctuations, and elevated UV radiation, which are environmental stressors that create significant physiological demands on endemic species [1]. The rapid advancement of modern sequencing technologies has provided robust methodological tools for investigating genetic adaptations in wildlife to high-altitude environments. Integrated multiomics approaches (genomics, transcriptomics, and proteomics) have revealed molecular adaptation strategies in altitude-specialised species. Notable model organisms include Bos grunniens [2–4], Pantholops hodgsonii [5], Sus scrofa [6, 7], Rhinopithecus bieti [8], Ovis aries [9–11], Canis lupus familiaris [12], Theropithecus gelada [13], and Neodon fuscus [14]. These comparative studies collectively illuminate evolutionary convergence in hypoxia-responsive pathways across mammalian taxa. These studies have identified critical pathways and genes associated with adaptation, the immune response, energy metabolism, and hypoxia tolerance, including the HIF-1 signalling pathway, the VEGF signalling pathway, DNA repair mechanisms, and key genes such as EPAS1, EGLN1, and MAFG, which are crucial for high-altitude adaptation. The aforementioned studies on high-altitude adaptation in different animals have provided strong support for elucidating the molecular mechanisms of environmental adaptation in plateau animals. However, most of these studies have used different species for comparison to determine the adaptation mechanisms between high and low altitudes. For example, Sus scrofa was compared with Duroc pigs [6] and Yorkshire pigs [7], Ovis aries was compared with low-altitude Canis lupus [11], and high-altitude Neodon fuscus was compared with low-altitude Lasiopodomys brandtii [14]. In our study, the intraspecific adaptation of Orientallactaga sibirica (five-toed jerboa) was studied on a smaller evolutionary scale using the same experimental object.

The five-toed jerboa, a member of the order Rodentia and family Dipodidae, is the most widely distributed jerboa species in China. This species occupies ecologically disparate habitats ranging from lowland deserts (< 500 m) to high-altitude plateaus (> 4000 m) [15–17]. Therefore, this study focused on the five-toed jerboa inhabiting the Qinghai‒Tibet Plateau, used low-altitude five-toed jerboas as controls, and employed RNA-Seq technology to perform transcriptome sequencing of their heart, lung, and kidney tissues to reveal the high-altitude adaptation mechanisms of the five-toed jerboa. These findings not only contribute to a deeper understanding of high-altitude adaptation mechanisms in mammals but also establish a theoretical framework for studying microenvironmental adaptation in different geographical populations of the same mammalian species.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Animal samples were collected from Maduo County, Guoluo Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Qinghai Province (33°50′~35°40′N, 96°50′~99°20′E) and Zhuolu County, Hebei Province (39°40′~40°39′, 114°55′~115°31′N) (Fig. 1 and Table S1). Five-toed jerboas were captured using Live-trapping methods, with 12 individuals sampled from each site, yielding a total of 24 animals. The live traps (55 × 30 × 30 cm) were baited with fresh peanuts to attract the jerboas and were set at multiple locations within the study areas during the active period of the animals (five-toed jerboas are nocturnal, so we capture them at night). Check the traps 2–3 times a night. In our study, we euthanized the five-toed jerboas with An overdose of sodium pentobarbital. First, we Anaesthetised them via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital at 100mg/kg body weight. After confirming unconsciousness, we performed euthanasia by cervical dislocation. Following euthanasia, the heart, lung, and kidney tissues were promptly harvested, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent RNA extraction. The whole procedure followed the ethical guidelines approved by the Research Ethics Review Committee of Inner Mongolian Agricultural University (NND2017012).

Schematic diagram of the sampling sites of the five-toed jerboa

RNA extraction and sequencing

Sample RNA was extracted using the TRIzol method, And tissue RNA samples from 4 individuals were randomly mixed. There were 3 biological replicates in each group, including the heart (HH1, HH2, HH3), lung (HL1, HL2, HL3), and kidney (HK1, HK2, HK3) of high-altitude five-toed jerboa and the heart (LH1, LH2, LH3), lung (LL1, LL2, LL3), and kidney (LK1, LK2, LK3) of low-altitude five-toed jerboa. The RNA quality was verified using An Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Strand-specific Libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Kit with poly-A selection And 150bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq platform.

Data analysis

FastQC was used for quality inspection to obtain clean reads. Trimmomatic was employed for quality control to obtain clean reads. It mainly includes the removal of reads with adapters, the removal of reads containing N (N indicates that the base information cannot be determined), and the removal of low-quality reads (Qphred < = 20 reads with more than 50% of the total read length). At the same time, Q20, Q30 and GC content the clean data were calculated. The reference Genome was aligned using HISAT2 2.0.5 (with parameters: --dta --no-softclip --rna-strandness RF -p 8). featureCounts 1.5.0-p3 were used to calculate the gene expression level of FPKM. DESeq2 v1.20.0 was used to analyze the differential expression of genes, and the interaction between population and tissue factors was excluded. Error detection rate (FDR) correction was applied using the Benjamini-Hochberg program, where DEGs were defined as showing | log2 (fold change) | ≥ 1, p value (padj) ≤ 0.05. ClusterProfiler 3.4.4 was used to perform GO and KEGG functional annotations of the DEGs (with parameters: pvalue Cutoff = 0.05, qvalue Cutoff = 0.2, pAdjustMethod = Benjamini‒Hochberg).

RT-qPCR validation

To verify the reliability of the transcription data, nine genes were selected for RT-qPCR detection, and the samples used for RT-qPCR were consistent with the transcriptome sequencing samples (Table S2). GAPDH was used as the internal reference Gene, And relative quantitative Analysis was performed using the 2−△△Ct method [18].

Results and analysis

Sequencing metrics and quality assessment

Transcriptome sequencing of heart (Hh1, Hh2, Hh3, Lh1, Lh2, Lh3), lung (Hl1, Hl2, Hl3, Ll1, Ll2, Ll3), and kidney (Hk1, Hk2, Hk3, Lk1, Lk2, Lk3) tissues of high- And low-altitude five-toed jerboas was performed using paired-end sequencing. A total of 874,466,586 raw reads were obtained. After quality control, 863,821,752 clean reads were obtained. High-throughput sequencing Generated 874million raw reads, with 863 million high-quality reads retained after adapter trimming and quality filtering (Q20 ≥ 97.1%). The key quality metrics included the following: base call accuracy Q30 ≥ 91.73% (0.025%±0.003) And GC content 49.87%±0.92 (Table S3). The above data revealed that the sequencing results were of high quality and that the sequencing data were accurate and reliable, making them suitable for subsequent analyses.

Reference genome alignment

After quality control, the clean reads were aligned to the reference genome, which was derived from the whole-genome data of the five-toed jerboa assembled by our research group (PRJNA1181308). The genome alignment efficiency reached > 90%, with unique mapping rates ranging from 66.08% to 87.23% (Table S4). These results indicate that the samples have a high mapping rate and are suitable for subsequent analyses.

Quantitative analysis of gene expression

The gene expression levels of the heart, lung and kidney tissues of the five-toed jerboas at different altitudes were evenly distributed (Fig. 2a). Intersample correlations showed high reproducibility (Pearson’s R²>0.8, Fig. 2b). PCA clustering demonstrated tissue-specific segregation along PC1 (62% variance) and altitudinal differentiation along PC2 (18% variance, Fig. 2c). These results show that the selection of research samples is reasonable and meets the experimental requirements.

DEG validation

The differences in the expression of 9 DEGs (NDUFC2, RPL37A, SEZ6, GSTK1, EI24, HSP90B1, COX7C, PRDX2, and RPS9) detected using RNA-Seq were verified using RT-qPCR. The results revealed that the FC values of the 9 genes determined via RT-qPCR and RNA-seq exhibited the same trend. In heart tissue, the correlation between RT-qPCR and RNA-seq expression was R2 = 0.8607, P = 0.0158; in lung tissue, R2 = 0.7574, P = 0.0014; and in kidney tissue, R2 = 0.8798, P = 0.0370 (Figure S1). The results revealed that the RNA-Seq data were accurate.

Discussion

Different altitudes and organ differences

In this study, DEGs in the heart, lung, and kidney tissues of five-toed jerboa at different altitudes were identified through comparative transcriptomics. Notably, lung tissue exhibited the highest number of DEGs (1734), followed by kidney (1445) and heart (1225), with only 239 genes shared across all three tissues. This tissue-specific transcriptional divergence suggests organ-specific adaptation strategies, particularly highlighting the lung’s heightened sensitivity to high-altitude conditions. These findings are different from those of yaks and goats, which usually show more significant adaptive changes in heart tissue [3, 19, 20]. In terms of high-altitude adaptation, gene expression in small rodent tissues is not the same as that in large mammal tissues. The evolution of gene expression levels varies across organs, lineages, and chromosomes, and many potential factors also affect the expression of genes in specific mammalian organs [21]. The transcriptome and proteome of the heart tissues of Tibetan pigs and Yorkshire pigs raised at high altitudes revealed that they used different genes to adapt to hypoxia even if they lived in the same environment [7]. On the other hand, yaks, Tibetan goats, Tibetan pigs and five-toed jerboas are quite different in body size, and their body weights are one Hundred times greater than those of five-toed jerboas. In addition. Compared with that of low-altitude Animals, the heart weight of high-altitude yaks And Tibetan goats accounts for a greater proportion of their body weight, usually between 0.5% And 0.8%, whereas the heart weight of low-altitude Animals accounts for between 0.5% And 0.6%. High-altitude animals need greater heart function to provide sufficient energy and nutrients to maintain the normal metabolism and function of cells [19]. Therefore, the results of this study revealed that the mRNA transcriptome results differed from those of the above studies. In addition, as the main organ of gas exchange, lung tissue needs to optimise oxygen uptake and utilisation through the regulation of gene expression. In addition, the plateau environment may trigger immune and inflammatory responses, and lung tissue regulates these responses through changes in gene expression. For example, the lung tissue of Ochotona curzoniae presented significantly upregulated expression of Genes related to the hypoxia inducible factor 1 (HIF-1) signalling pathway and immune regulation, whereas other tissues, such as muscle and brain tissue, presented decreased transcriptional differences [22]. Therefore, similar to O. curzoniae, the lung tissue of high-altitude five-toed jerboa shows greater transcriptional differences in plateau adaptation, which optimises gas exchange, regulates the immune response and maintains tissue function through the adjustment of gene expression.

Characteristics of heart tissue adaptation at different altitudes

The observed enrichment of lipid metabolism pathways and upregulation of oxidative phosphorylation-related genes in the heart tissue of high-altitude five-toed jerboas suggests a unique metabolic strategy to balance energy demands under chronic hypoxia and cold stress. While previous studies in Tibetans [23, 24], yak [25], Heterocephalus glaber [26], and Phyllotis andium [27] have reported a shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis as the primary ATP-generating mechanism under hypoxia, our findings reveal a different pattern: lipid metabolism pathways were upregulated, whereas glycolysis-related pathways (e.g., pentose phosphate and fructose/mannose metabolism) were suppressed. This discrepancy may reflect species-specific adaptations to divergent environmental pressures. Although glucose oxidation per mole of O2 produces approximately 15% ATP [28], Lipids account for more than 80% of the total energy reserves of mammals, and the energy density of lipids is one order of magnitude greater than that of carbohydrates [29]. These findings suggest that lipid oxidation represents a more effective strategy for high-altitude five-toed jerboas to cope with chronic cold stress and provide energy than carbohydrate oxidation (Fig. 10a). Previous studies have shown that the DEGs enriched in the lipid metabolism pathway of the high-altitude five-toed jerboa, namely SGMS2/1 and PAFAH1B3/H2/B2/B1, have been identified as candidates for hypoxia tolerance [30, 31]. Moreover, Elaphurus davidianus at high altitudes has evolved a stronger ability to oxidise lipids as a major fuel source than carbohydrates during hypoxia-induced thermogenesis [32, 33]. The results of this study are similar.

Schematic diagram of candidate genes involved in high-altitude adaptation in the heart (a), lung (b), and kidney (c). Red represents up-regulated genes, and green represents down-regulated genes

Mitochondria are known as the ‘energy factories’ of animal cells, producing most of the ATP required to maintain cellular processes by consuming oxygen. This function of mitochondria depends on the partial pressure of intracellular O2 and is therefore affected by hypoxia at high altitudes [34]. The oxidative phosphorylation pathway was the most significantly enriched pathway. A total of 13 Genes were detected in the heart tissue of five-toed jerboa at high altitudes, including 4 upregulated DEGs (COX7C, ATP4B, COX4I2, and MT-CO2) And 9 downregulated DEGs (NDUFC2, NDUFA3, NDUFS4, NDUFC1, NDUFB3, ATP6V1F, ATP5ME, COX8B, and COX6A2). The oxidative phosphorylation system consists of five enzymes (complexes I-V) [35]. NDUFC2, NDUFA3, and NDUFS4 are subunits of complex I. Complex I is the largest of the five OXPHOS complexes, contributing approximately 40% of the proton power and driving ATP synthase to synthesise ATP [36]. Downregulation of NDUFC2 [37], NDUFS4 [38] and NDUFA3 [39] resulted in decreased stability and activity of complex I and inhibited mitochondrial energy production under hypoxic conditions. Complex IV-cytochrome C oxidase (COX) is the last enzyme in the respiratory chain, And subunit 4 (COX4) optimises respiratory efficiency at different oxygen concentrations [40]. Therefore, mammalian cells respond to a hypoxic environment by changing the composition of COX subunits [41, 42]. COX4I2 is preferentially expressed under hypoxia and oxidative stress and exhibits an enhanced response under hypoxic tension [43, 44] to increase ATP levels and improve cell respiratory efficiency [45]. In the process of oxidative phosphorylation, the high-altitude five-toed jerboa regulates the composition of gene regulatory complexes I and IV, such as NDUFC2, NDUFA3, NDUFS4, and COX4I2; stabilises the oxidative phosphorylation process; promotes oxidative phosphorylation energy metabolism; and subsequently improves the hypoxia tolerance of high-altitude five-toed jerboa (Fig. 10a). O. curzoniae also regulates and adapts to the production and consumption of energy during cold and hypoxia by regulating mitochondrial cytochrome C oxidase activity [46], which is consistent with the results of this study.

Characteristics of lung tissue adaptation at different altitudes

In the lung tissue of the high - altitude five - toed jerboa, upregulated genes are enriched in oxidation - reduction processes and immune responses (like complement and coagulation cascades), while downregulated genes are associated with mitochondrial and extracellular functions. The redox process is the most enriched process of GO. In the transcriptome of the lung tissue of Maiwa yak under different altitude feeding treatments, the redox process and oxidoreductase activity were the most enriched [47]. In addition, compared to the aboveground rodent Brandt’s vole, Lasiopodomys mandarinus exhibits significant enrichment in a series of processes related to redox reactions [48]. Thus, redox reactions play an important role in hypoxia adaptation.

Heme, an iron - containing porphyrin that is critically important for cellular energy metabolism, exerts its effects not only in oxygen transport and - storage proteins and cellular signal transduction, but also in the regulation of inflammatory and innate immune programs [49]. During this study, the up - regulation of some heme - binding - related genes has been detected (e.g., CYP1A2, CYP2E1). It has been shown that the expression of CYP1A1 decreases under hypoxic conditions, and that CYP1A1 - knockout mice are more susceptible to acute lung injury (ALI) [50]. Moreover, CYP2E1 may be associated with the adaptation of Tibetans to high - altitude environments [51]. The enrichment of heme may also be connected with the oxygen - binding in bovine cells in the plateau environment [4]. It can be inferred that the large - scale enrichment of heme - related items in the lung tissue of the five - toed jerboa is mainly related to preserving pulmonary ventilation as well as immune activities (Fig. 10b).

High-altitude environmental factors, such as ultraviolet exposure, cold, and low-pressure hypoxia, can affect the immune system, increasing susceptibility to cancer, various infections, and even autoimmune diseases [1]. In this study, the DEGs associated with the complement and coagulation pathways were the most significantly enriched. The complement system plays an important role in both innate immunity and adaptive immunity, resisting infection and attacking foreign pathogens in the body’s defence process [52]. The coagulation cascade plays an important role in maintaining normal blood flow in the body and preventing blood loss, rapid curing and preventing spontaneous bleeding [53]. In this study, a total of 33 DEGs (upregulated F5, SERPINA5, PLAT, C5AR1, C6, A2M, MBL2, ITGAM, SERPINC1, MBL1, CFH, FGA, MASP1, FGB, SERPINF2, KLKB1, C4BPB, KNG1, C5, FGG, VTN, F11, PROC, F2, F7, C9, C2, FGA, PLG, and F12 and downregulated C7, C3, and CD55) were enriched in this pathway. The complement system can be activated via three mechanisms: the classical pathway, alternative pathway and lectin pathway. MASP1 plays a central role in lectin pathway activation. MASP1 activates MASP2 And complement component 2 (C2), whereas MASP2 activates both C2 and C4 [54–56], which subsequently promotes the formation of the invertase complex C4b2b and activates the complement response. Moreover, A2M and SERPING1 inhibit MASP1 and MASP2 activity [57–59]. Under hypoxic conditions, CD55 inhibits complement activation by inhibiting the function of C3/C5 invertase in the classical and alternative pathways [60]. The coagulation cascade is a self-repair process involving vascular injury and is divided into endogenous and exogenous pathways. Fibrinogen regulates various physiological and pathological events by interacting with vascular endothelial cells and plays a role in both endogenous and exogenous pathways [61]. Studies have shown that fibrinogen binds to specific integrins through the RGD residues of FGA, binds to vascular endothelial cells, and promotes angiogenesis [62]. FGA can also control VEGFA secretion and promote angiogenesis by activating the VEGFR2-FAK signalling pathway [63]. The activation of platelet C5AR1 is a regulatory mechanism of angiogenesis [64, 65]. In addition, KNG1 plays a central role in the genetic determinants of the entire coagulation pathway and thrombosis/clot formation [66]. Therefore, by regulating gene cooperation in the complement and coagulation cascade pathway, high-altitude five-toed jerboa regulate the body’s immune function and reduce the potential risk of thrombosis and other diseases caused by high-altitude stress (Fig. 10b). Transcriptome analysis of the brain tissues of L. mandarinus and L. brandtii under hypoxia revealed that the upregulated DEGs were related to DNA repair, damage prevention and angiogenesis [48]. Eospalax fontanieri and N. fuscus living in high-altitude environments presented the same trend in terms of the expression levels of related genes [14], further confirming that complement and coagulation cascades play important roles in the adaptation of high-altitude five-toed jerboas to high-altitude environments.

Characteristics of kidney tissue adaptation at different altitudes

In the five - toed jerboa kidney tissue from different altitudes, the GO analysis of DEGs showed that the up and downregulated DEGs were enriched in the same processes as those in the heart and lung tissues. Similarly, pathways related to fatty acid metabolism (fatty acid metabolism, fatty acid biosynthesis, and unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis) were significantly upregulated. However, in the kidney tissue, KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment in pathways such as arachidonic acid metabolism and steroid hormone synthesis. Thus, the roles of these two aspects in the high - altitude five - toed jerboa’s adaptation to the environment should be prioritized in kidney tissue research.

Arachidonic acid (AA) has very important biological activities and physiological functions in vivo, and its metabolites play important roles in many physiological and pathological processes. There are three main metabolic pathways of arachidonic acid: the cyclooxygenase (COX) pathway, the lipoxygenase (LOX) pathway and the cytochrome P450 (CYP) pathway [67]. In this study, a total of 14 DEGs were enriched in the arachidonic acid pathway (upregulated CYP2C7, CYP2C25, CYP2C26, CYP4A10, EPHX2, GPX2, CYP4A11, and CBR1; downregulated CYP2C27, CYP2C29, CYP2C37, PTGDS, CBR3, and PLA2G4A). Studies have shown that hypoxia can induce CYP4A11 gene expression [68]. CYP4A11 is An important 20-HETE synthase [69]. 20-HETE is a product of the CYP pathway and is an important proangiogenic factor [70, 71], mainly by inducing the proliferation, migration, survival and tube formation of vascular endothelial cells or vascular smooth muscle cells and promoting the secretion of angiogenic growth factors [72, 73]. Of note, 20-HETE stimulates the production of VEGF and hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α), which are important regulators of Angiogenesis. Importantly, 20-HETE also contributes to angiogenesis after hypoxic injury [74]. The main products of CYP2C29 in blood vessels are 14,15-EET And 11,12-EET [75]. EETS is also a product of the CYP pathway, which mainly plays a role in vasodilation, promoting angiogenesis and inhibiting inflammation [76]. Long-term exposure to a hypoxic environment in mice increases CYP2C29 expression and CYP cyclooxygenase activity, which contributes to pulmonary vasoconstriction and initiates pulmonary vascular remodelling in mice [77]. GPX2 mainly plays a role in the LOX pathway, producing 5-HETE And 15-HETE. HETEs play a role in regulating vasoconstriction, vasodilation and vascular remodelling [78]. In addition, GPX2 has antioxidant-related functions and plays an important role in hypoxia-induced oxidative stress injury [79]. PTGDS, CBR1 and CBR3 are involved in the production of prostaglandin compounds. The COX pathway produces prostaglandins, which play a role in regulating platelet aggregation, vasoconstriction, inflammation, fever, pain and other biological processes [80]. The AA pathway is a highly regulated metabolic pathway that plays a key role in many physiological processes, such as platelet aggregation, pain perception, immune regulation, and the regulation of other inflammation-related responses [81]. On this basis, the high-altitude five-toed jerboa regulates the dynamic balance of angiogenesis, immunity, inflammation, and antioxidant processes in the body through the arachidonic acid pathway and its metabolites. This promotes the ability of the five-toed jerboa to cope with the high-altitude environment (Fig. 10c).

Steroid hormones play crucial roles in the homeostasis of water and salt, metabolic regulation, and adaptive stress responses [82]. Cytochrome P450 (CYP) and hydroxysteroidal dehydrogenase (HSD) are two major types of enzymes involved in the steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway, and similarly, the CYP11A1, CYP1A1, CYP2C25, CYP2C2526, AKR1C18, HSD17B3, UGT2B31 and other Genes were mainly enriched in the steroid hormone biosynthesis pathway. Under acute hypoxic stress, Hypoxia-Inducible Factor 1α (HIF1α) regulates the expression of steroidogenic enzymes (such as STAR and CYP11A1) and the production of steroids [83]. UGT2B31 is likely to compensate for the thermogenesis in wild boars in cold regions, thereby enhancing their cold - adaptation to higher - latitude areas [84]. Therefore, in kidney tissue, high-altitude five-toed jerboas promote the synthesis of steroid hormones by upregulating the above genes, so as to maintain homeostasis and adapt to the high-altitude environment (Fig. 10c).

This study identified DEGs associated with high-altitude adaptation, but the following limitations should be noted: First, the relatively small sample size (18 gene expression samples in total) may limit statistical power and generalizability of the results, and the inclusion of only single high- and low-altitude populations restricts comprehensive representation of genetic diversity and adaptive variations. Second, the analysis focused exclusively on heart, lung, and kidney tissues; while these organs are critical for hypoxia response, other tissues may participate in adaptive processes through synergistic mechanisms. Additionally, DEGs reflect transcriptional-level differences only, leaving potential mechanisms such as post-transcriptional modifications, protein functional divergence, or allele fixation unexplored. Future research should systematically elucidate the molecular networks of high-altitude adaptation by expanding sample sizes, incorporating geographically diverse populations, extending tissue coverage, and integrating functional validation (e.g., gene editing), proteomics, and population genetic analyses.

Conclusion

In this study, cross-altitude transcriptomic profiling of O. sibirica heart, lung, and kidney systems revealed three adaptive strategies to hypobaric hypoxia. The results revealed that the heart tissue of high-altitude five-toed jerboas may promote energy metabolism by regulating oxidative phosphorylation and lipid metabolism and responded to high-altitude hypoxia and chronic cold stress. Lung tissue is the most sensitive to high-altitude environments. It may regulate complement and coagulation cascade pathways to regulate immune function; prevent and repair damage caused by low-pressure hypoxia, strong radiation and cold; and reduce the risk of thrombosis and inflammation caused by high-altitude stress. Kidney tissue may upregulate the arachidonic acid metabolic pathway to help the body regulate angiogenesis, immunity, inflammation, antioxidation and other processes to maintain homeostasis. As a small mammal distributed in both high- and low-altitude environments, the five-toed jerboa can be used as a model organism to study the adaptation of mammals to high altitudes. This study provides a basis for understanding the microenvironment adaptability of different geographical populations of small mammals, enriches the field data, and provides insight into the mechanism of high-altitude adaptability in mammals and the study of hypoxia-induced human diseases.
