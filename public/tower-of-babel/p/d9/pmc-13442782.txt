SHH drives gastric cancer-associated fibroblast activation via ACLY-dependent acetyl-CoA metabolism and histone acetylation
Authors: Xianhu Zhang, Longhe Sun, Youlei Zhang, Yulong Wang, Yong Huang, Yongjun Jiang, Daorong Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Gastric cancer, the fifth most common malignancy worldwide, is characterized by poor prognosis due to its aggressive progression and therapeutic resistance, largely driven by the tumor microenvironment (TME). Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), as the predominant stromal component, contribute to tumor progression, metastasis, and chemoresistance through metabolic reprogramming and epigenetic modifications. Here, we investigated the role of Sonic Hedgehog (SHH) signaling in regulating ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY)-dependent acetyl-CoA metabolism and histone acetylation during gastric CAF activation. Primary CAFs exhibited elevated fibroblast activation markers (FAP1, α-SMA) compared to normal gastric fibroblasts (NFs). SHH stimulation or co-culture with gastric cancer cells robustly upregulated ACLY transcription and protein expression in NFs, synergistically enhancing acetyl-CoA production. Genetic ACLY knockdown or pharmacologic inhibition attenuated SHH-induced CAF activation markers (α-SMA, FAP1, FSP1) and reduced intracellular acetyl-CoA levels by > 50%, concomitant with diminished global histone H3 acetylation. Chromatin immunoprecipitation revealed SHH-driven enrichment of H3 acetylation at promoters of CAF marker genes, which was reversed by ACLY silencing or histone acetyltransferase inhibition. Moreover, tumor cell-conditioned medium recapitulated these metabolic-epigenetic changes in NFs. Our findings establish SHH/ACLY/acetyl-CoA as a novel axis linking stromal metabolism to epigenetic reprogramming in gastric CAFs, wherein ACLY-generated acetyl-CoA fuels histone acetylation to activate pro-tumorigenic transcriptional programs. Introduction

Gastric cancer ranks as the fifth most common malignancy globally, causing approximately 769,000 annual deaths with a five-year survival rate below 30% [1]. Its aggressive progression and therapeutic resistance are closely associated with dynamic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [2]. As the most abundant stromal component in TME, CAFs play a pivotal role in tumor progression, metastasis, and chemoresistance by secreting cytokines, remodeling the extracellular matrix, and mediating immune suppression [3–5]. While classical pathways such as TGF-β/Smad and Wnt/β-catenin are known to regulate CAF activation, emerging evidence highlights significant links between CAF functional remodeling and metabolic reprogramming (e.g., enhanced glycolysis, glutamine dependency) as well as epigenetic abnormalities (e.g., DNA methylation, histone acetylation) [6–10]. For instance, aberrant activation of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) in pancreatic CAFs induces cancer stemness through lactate secretion, whereas inhibition of histone deacetylases (HDACs) in breast CAFs reverses their pro-fibrotic phenotype [11]. These findings suggest that a metabolism-epigenetics crosstalk network may serve as a novel regulatory axis for CAF activation.

ACLY, a key metabolic enzyme bridging glycolysis and lipid synthesis, catalyzes citrate cleavage to generate acetyl-CoA, an essential substrate for fatty acid synthesis and histone acetylation [12, 13]. Although the pro-tumorigenic role of ACLY in cancer cells is well-documented, its function and regulatory mechanisms in CAFs remain poorly understood. Meanwhile, the SHH signaling pathway is frequently dysregulated in gastric cancer and can induce stromal phenotypic transformation via paracrine mechanisms [14, 15]. While the canonical SHH pathway operates through the Patched-SMO-Gli axis to regulate target gene expression, recent studies reveal its non-canonical roles in metabolic regulation [16]. For example, it affects myelin formation and repair by acetylation of histones [17]. This suggests that it may influence CAFs through metabolic-epigenetic cross-talk. However, whether SHH drives CAF activation via ACLY-mediated metabo-epigenetic crosstalk remains an unresolved scientific question.

This study focuses on the regulatory role of the SHH/ACLY/acetyl-CoA axis in CAF activation. By combining primary cell culture, genetic perturbation, metabolomics, and epigenomic profiling, we systematically investigate how SHH secreted by gastric cancer cells upregulates ACLY expression in fibroblasts, thereby promoting acetyl-CoA production. This metabolic shift enhances histone acetylation to drive transcriptional activation of CAF signature genes (e.g., α-SMA, FAP1), ultimately fostering a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment. Our findings not only establish a novel molecular framework for understanding TME regulation in gastric cancer but also provide a foundation for developing anticancer therapies targeting stromal metabolism-epigenetics interactions.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

Primary gastric fibroblasts, including NFs and CAFs, were obtained from commercial sources. NFs were purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc. (HUM-iCell-d006, Shanghai, China) and cultured using the iCell Primary Fibroblast Culture System (PriMed-iCell-003, iCell Bioscience Inc., Shanghai, China), which includes a fibroblast-specific complete medium. CAFs were purchased from Procell Life Science&Technology Co., Ltd. (CP-H206, Wuhan, China) and cultured in CAF-specific complete medium (CM-H206, Procell, Wuhan, China). Both cell types were maintained in T25 flasks pre-coated with 0.1% gelatin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) to enhance cell attachment.

Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. The medium was replaced every 2–3 days, and cells were passaged at 80–90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For all experiments, cells between passages 3 and 6 were used to ensure consistency and minimize phenotypic drift.

The human gastric cancer cell line AGS was purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA). AGS cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ and passaged using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA when they reached 80–90% confluence.

Co-culture of NFs and AGS cells

To mimic tumor-stroma crosstalk, NFs were co-cultured with AGS cells in a transwell system (Corning, USA). NFs were seeded in the lower chamber of a 6-well plate, while AGS cells were seeded in the transwell inserts with a pore size of 0.4 μm to prevent direct cell-cell contact. Both cell types were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. After 48 h of co-culture, NFs were harvested for subsequent analysis.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Cells were seeded on glass coverslips, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 10 min. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 1 h, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Vimentin (10366-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:200) and FAP1 (AF5344, Affinity, 1:200). Secondary antibodies conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Images were captured using a Leica DMi8 fluorescence microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany).

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA synthesis was performed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Japan). qPCR was conducted using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Primer sequences for ACLY, FAP1, α-SMA, and FSP1 were designed using Primer-BLAST (NCBI) and synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method, normalized to GAPDH. The primer sequences used are listed in Table S1.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, Switzerland). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk (Bio-Rad, USA) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against ACLY (1:1000, Proteintech, 67166-1-Ig), FAP1 (1:1000, Affinity, AF5344), α-SMA (1:1000, Proteintech, 67735-1-Ig), FSP1 (1:1000, Proteintech, 16105-1-AP), Vimentin (1:1000, Abcam, UK), and GAPDH (1:5000, Proteintech, 60004-1-Ig). Secondary HRP-conjugated antibodies (1:10000, Absin, abs20040) were applied for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized using the ECL Detection Kit (Bio-Rad, USA) and captured with a ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad, USA).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR

Logarithmically growing gastric fibroblasts were used for further experiments. Fresh medium was added, followed by recombinant SHH protein at a final concentration of 0.08 µg/mL, and the cells were cultured for an additional 24 h to activate the Hedgehog signaling pathway. The experiment included the following five groups:

NC group (negative control): Gastric fibroblasts cultured in standard medium without any treatment.

SHH group: Cells treated with 0.08 µg/mL SHH protein for 24 h.

SHH + shACLY group: After SHH treatment, cells were transfected with ACLY (ATP citrate lyase)-targeting shRNA lentivirus and cultured for another 24 h.

SHH + SB 204,990 group: After SHH treatment, cells were treated with 30 µM ACLY inhibitor SB 204,990 for 24 h.

SHH + C646 group: After SHH treatment, cells were treated with 10 µM p300 inhibitor C646 for 24 h.

Cells from each group were collected and crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min. The reaction was quenched with glycine, and cells were washed with PBS. Cell lysis was performed using SDS lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors. Chromatin was fragmented by sonication (Bioruptor: 30 s ON/30 seconds OFF, 10 cycles), generating DNA fragments ranging from 200 to 500 bp, which were verified by agarose gel electrophoresis.

For ChIP, 100 µg of fragmented chromatin was incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies targeting histone H3 acetylation (H3K27ac, ab4729, Abcam, 1:100). The immune complexes were captured using Protein A/G magnetic beads and washed sequentially with low-salt and high-salt buffers. The complexes were eluted with a buffer containing 1% SDS, and crosslinking was reversed by incubation at 65 °C overnight in a solution containing NaCl and proteinase K. Specific primers targeting the promoter regions of α-SMA, FAP1, and FSP1 genes were designed for qPCR analysis. ChIP-DNA enrichment was quantified using SYBR Green qPCR, and relative enrichment levels for each group were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, with the Input DNA serving as the reference. The primer sequences used are listed in Table S2.

ELISA for acetyl-CoA quantification

Intracellular acetyl-CoA levels were measured using the Acetyl-Coenzyme A Assay Kit (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, USA).

SHH knockdown

AGS cells were seeded and grown to approximately 60–70% confluence in antibiotic-free medium prior to transfection. Specific siRNA targeting human SHH (siSHH) and negative control siRNA (siNC) were separately diluted in Opti-MEM medium, mixed with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen, USA), and incubated at room temperature to allow complex formation. The siRNA‒liposome complexes were then added dropwise to the cell cultures. After 6 h of transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Knockdown efficiency was assessed at 48–72 h post-transfection by qPCR and Western blot. The siRNA sequences used are listed in Table S3.

Functional ACLY knockdown and overexpression

shRNA-mediated knockdown

Three shRNAs targeting ACLY (shACLY-1, shACLY-2, shACLY-3) and a non-targeting control shRNA were purchased from GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Lentiviral particles were produced in HEK293T cells using the Lenti-X Packaging System (Takara Bio, Japan) and used to transduce NFs. Knockdown efficiency was validated by qPCR and Western blot. The shRNA sequences used are listed in Table S4.

Plasmid overexpression

Wild-type ACLY and phosphorylation site mutants (H760A, S455A) were cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector (Invitrogen, USA) and transfected into NFs using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA). Overexpression efficiency was confirmed by qPCR and Western blot.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate unless otherwise specified. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test, as appropriate, in GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, USA). P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Phenotypic characterization and molecular markers of CAFs

To elucidate the activation status of fibroblasts in the gastric cancer microenvironment, we first performed phenotypic characterization of primary NFs and CAFs derived from gastric tumor tissues. Vimentin, a mesenchymal cell-specific marker widely expressed in fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and immune cells [18]. Vimentin was used to assess the purity of mesenchymal-derived cell populations and confirm their non-epithelial origin, while specific identification of CAFs relied on the expression of FAP1. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that both NFs and CAFs exhibited Vimentin positivity in over 90% of cells (Fig. 1A), confirming their mesenchymal origin and excluding contamination by epithelial or hematopoietic cells during isolation and culture. Fibroblast activation protein-α (FAP1), a core activation marker of CAFs, is closely associated with tumor stromal remodeling, immunosuppression, and chemoresistance [19]. Quantitative immunofluorescence demonstrated significantly higher FAP1-positive cell proportions in CAFs compared to NFs (Fig. 1B), with Western blot further validating FAP1 protein upregulation in CAFs (Fig. 1C). Notably, Vimentin protein levels showed no significant differences between the groups (Fig. 1C–D), excluding potential confounding effects from altered mesenchymal properties during activation analysis.

SHH signaling activation drives ACLY transcription and protein expression

To investigate the regulatory role of the SHH pathway in metabolic reprogramming of gastric fibroblasts, we focused on functional validation of the SHH-ACLY axis. ACLY, a rate-limiting enzyme linking glycolysis to acetyl-CoA synthesis, may drive CAF activation via epigenetic modifications when dysregulated. qPCR analysis revealed significantly elevated ACLY mRNA levels in CAFs compared to NFs (P < 0.001), indicating transcriptional activation of ACLY as a hallmark of CAF metabolic remodeling (Fig. 2A). Exogenous SHH stimulation (NFs + SHH) induced ACLY mRNA upregulation in NFs (P < 0.01), while co-culture with AGS gastric cancer cells (NFs + AGS), mimicking tumor-stroma crosstalk, similarly increased ACLY expression (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). Notably, combined SHH and AGS treatment (NFs + AGS+SHH) synergistically enhanced ACLY mRNA levels. Western blot analysis corroborated these findings at the protein level (Fig. 2B and C). Moreover, both the gene and protein expression levels of SHH exhibited trends consistent with those of ACLY, suggesting that the SHH pathway is activated in CAFs or upon AGS stimulation (Figure S1A–S1C).

To clarify the regulatory role of SHH on ACLY expression, we further knocked down SHH in AGS cells and examined ACLY expression levels (Figure S2A and S2B). The results showed that SHH knockdown significantly reduced both mRNA and protein expression of ACLY (P < 0.001), while exogenous supplementation of SHH reversed this effect and restored ACLY expression (Figure S2C and S2D). Notably, when co‑culturing SHH‑knockdown AGS cells with NFs, no significant change in ACLY expression was observed in the NFs, confirming that SHH indeed acts as an upstream regulator of ACLY.

Construction of ACLY knockdown and overexpression cell models

To delineate the direct role of ACLY in CAF activation, we generated functional intervention models using shRNA-mediated knockdown and overexpression plasmids. Three ACLY-targeting shRNAs (shACLY-1/2/3) reduced ACLY mRNA levels by 89%, 85%, and 80%, respectively (P < 0.001), with corresponding protein downregulation (P < 0.01) (Fig. 3A, C and E). Conversely, overexpression of wild-type ACLY (ACLY-OE) and its phosphorylation site mutants (H760A, S455A) significantly elevated both mRNA and protein levels (P < 0.01, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3B, D and F). The above results confirm that the model was successfully constructed and can be used for subsequent experiments.

ACLY drives CAF activation via acetyl-CoA-mediated histone acetylation

To elucidate the pro-activation mechanism of ACLY, we investigated the regulatory role of its metabolic product acetyl-CoA in epigenetic modifications. SHH stimulation in NFs significantly increased protein expression of activation markers α-SMA, FAP1, and FSP1 (P < 0.05, P < 0.01), an effect reversed by ACLY knockdown (P < 0.01) (Fig. 4A and H). ELISA analysis demonstrated a 3.2-fold increase in intracellular acetyl-CoA levels upon SHH treatment (P < 0.001), which was reduced by 58% and 63% following ACLY inhibitor (SB 204990) or shACLY treatment, respectively (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4I). Consistently, SHH-induced global histone H3 acetylation (H3K9/K14/K18/K23/K27ac) was abolished by ACLY inhibition or knockdown (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4J and K). These results indicate that ACLY catalyzes citrate-derived acetyl-CoA production, providing essential substrates for histone acetyltransferases to epigenetically activate pro-activation genes.

Histone acetylation directly regulates transcriptional activation of CAF marker genes

It is known that p300/CBP serves as one of the key histone acetyltransferases (HATs) mediating acetyl-CoA dependent histone acetylation and is closely associated with tumor progression and stromal cell activation [20, 21]. Using a specific inhibitor (C646) to modulate p300/CBP activity, we investigated its potential role in CAF activation. ChIP-qPCR analysis revealed significant enrichment of histone H3 acetylation at promoter regions of FAP1, FSP1, and α-SMA genes following SHH treatment (P < 0.05, P < 0.01), which was partially attenuated by ACLY knockdown or the histone acetyltransferase inhibitor C646 (Fig. 5A and C). Furthermore, gastric cancer cell-conditioned medium increased acetyl-CoA levels by 2.8-fold (P < 0.001) in NFs, concomitant with elevated H3 acetylation and CAF marker expression (**P < 0.01) (Fig. 5D and F). This suggests that p300/CBP-mediated histone H3 acetylation directly regulates the activation process of CAFs by promoting the transcriptional activity of the promoter region of CAF marker genes.

Discussion

This study uncovers a novel mechanism by which the SHH signaling pathway drives the activation of gastric CAFs through ACLY-dependent acetyl-CoA metabolism and histone acetylation. By integrating primary cell culture, genetic perturbation, metabolomics, and epigenomic profiling, we demonstrate that SHH signaling upregulates ACLY expression in CAFs, leading to increased acetyl-CoA production and global histone H3 acetylation. These epigenetic modifications activate transcription of critical CAF marker genes, including FAP1, α-SMA, and FSP1, thereby promoting a pro-tumorigenic phenotype. Furthermore, SHH stimulation enriches histone acetylation at the promoter regions of these genes, directly linking metabolic reprogramming to transcriptional activation. This study establishes the SHH/ACLY/acetyl-CoA axis as a critical driver of stromal remodeling in gastric cancer and highlights its therapeutic potential for disrupting tumor-stroma interactions.

Previous studies have demonstrated that classical signaling pathways, such as TGF-β/Smad and Wnt/β-catenin, orchestrate CAF activation by regulating transcriptional programs [6, 7]. However, increasing evidence suggests that metabolic reprogramming also plays a pivotal role in CAF activation [22]. For instance, enhanced glycolysis and glutamine metabolism have been shown to fuel CAF-mediated tumor progression [23]. LDHA activation in pancreatic CAFs promotes cancer stemness through lactate secretion, while inhibition of HDACs in breast CAFs reverses their pro-fibrotic phenotype [11, 24]. Our study builds upon these findings by identifying a novel metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk mechanism in CAF activation. Specifically, we demonstrate that ACLY, a key metabolic enzyme linking glycolysis to acetyl-CoA synthesis, plays an essential role in CAF activation by providing substrates for histone acetylation. ACLY, a key node connecting glycolysis to epigenetics, has been well explored in tumor cells, but its role in CAFs has not been elucidated [12]. This finding is particularly significant as it connects metabolic shifts to epigenetic regulation, bridging two critical aspects of CAF biology that were previously studied in isolation.

Mechanistically, SHH promotes CAFs activation by regulating ACLY expression. The data from siSHH experiments confirmed that SHH plays a central driving role in this paracrine effect. However, the specific transcriptional pathway by which SHH signaling upregulates ACLY remains to be elucidated. On one hand, the promoter region of the ACLY gene may contain Gli-binding elements, allowing SHH to directly enhance ACLY transcription through the canonical Patched-SMO-Gli axis. On the other hand, SHH might also induce ACLY expression via SMO-independent pathways, such as activating MAPK or PI3K/AKT kinases, which in turn regulate other transcription factors (such as SP1, SREBP) to drive ACLY transcription. Therefore, clarifying the upstream mechanism of SHH-mediated ACLY transcriptional regulation (specifically whether it is Gli-dependent) represents an important direction for future research. Meanwhile, other factors potentially present in the CM, such as TGF-β and PDGF, may act synergistically with SHH, forming a more comprehensive signaling network for activation. The complex bidirectional interaction between NFs and AGS cells (co-culture) induced a stronger upregulation of ACLY expression compared to stimulation with the SHH ligand alone. This suggests that in the realistic heterogeneous tumor microenvironment, metabolic reprogramming in CAFs is synergistically driven by multiple paracrine signals, with SHH being one of the key, but not the sole, contributing factors. This phenomenon may arise because the interaction between NFs and AGS cells involves other unknown soluble factors, which activate a stronger or more integrated pro-metabolic signaling network compared to SHH signaling alone, thereby driving ACLY expression to a greater extent. By constructing an ACLY knockdown/overexpression model, this study demonstrated for the first time that ACLY activity is a necessary condition for SHH-induced CAFs activation, and its effect depends on the production of acetyl-CoA. Although the ACLY phosphorylation mutants (H760A and S455A) can influence the acetylation process to some extent, it remains unclear whether these mutants specifically affect the acetylation of particular lysine sites or exert global effects through non-canonical mechanisms such as altering the intracellular distribution or availability of acetyl-CoA. Specific phosphorylation modifications might preferentially influence particular histone marks by modulating ACLY enzymatic activity, subcellular localization, or interactions with other regulatory proteins (e.g., HAT complexes).

In addition, in terms of epigenetic mechanisms, studies have suggested that histone modification is involved in CAFs activation [25], but the specific regulatory targets and upstream signals are unknown. This study found that SHH signaling induced global acetylation of sites such as H3K9/K14 through ACLY upregulation, and was specifically enriched in the promoter region of genes such as FAP1, which is different from the inhibitory epigenetic regulatory pattern dominated by H3K27me3 modification in PDAC-CAFs reported by Ohlund et al. [26]. This difference may reflect tumor type-specific regulatory mechanisms: the gastric cancer microenvironment may rely more on the SHH-ACLY axis to activate pro-oncogenic epigenetic programs, while TGF-β signals in pancreatic cancer may induce repressive chromatin remodeling through the Polycomb complex. Moreover, the ACLY phosphorylation mutant (H760A/S455A) can still partially promote histone acetylation, suggesting that it has non-classical functions, which is similar to the ACLY function found by Dominguez et al. in human bone marrow cells, but it is the first report in CAFs [27]. As we all know, p300/CBP and other HAT family members such as GCN5/pCAF are sensitive to acetyl-CoA concentration, serve as primary acetyltransferases for histone H3, and are broadly involved in transcriptional regulation during cell fate determination and activation processes [21, 28, 29], making them the most plausible candidate enzymes mediating this mechanism. Building upon the established role of the SHH/ACLY axis in elevating global histone acetylation levels and promoting CAF activation, we employed the highly specific inhibitor C646 to validate the functional necessity of p300/CBP within this pathway. The results demonstrate that p300/CBP acts as a critical HAT for histone acetylation in CAFs and actively participates in the CAF activation process. In fact, acetylation modifications are jointly regulated by HATs and HDACs. We hypothesize that under SHH signaling stimulation and/or co-culture with tumor cells, fibroblasts in the tumor microenvironment may undergo significant epigenetic reprogramming, with one of the core mechanisms likely being a dynamic shift in the balance between HATs and HDACs. On one hand, the SHH pathway may directly or indirectly regulate the activity of specific HATs (such as p300/CBP), promoting histone acetylation and the expression of pro-fibrotic genes. On the other hand, certain HDACs, particularly Class IIa members such as HDAC4/5/7/9, may play a “scaffolding” role beyond their canonical deacetylase function in this process. They can interact with transcription factors (such as MEF2, FOXO, SMAD) or chromatin-modifying complexes to regulate downstream target gene networks independently of their enzymatic activity [30–32]. This mechanism may integrate SHH signaling with other paracrine signals (such as TGF-β), collectively driving CAF activation and establishing a pro-fibrotic microenvironment that supports tumor progression.

Despite these advances, our study has several limitations. First, although the role of the SHH/ACLY/acetyl-CoA axis was rigorously validated in vitro, the specific signaling pathway through which SHH regulates ACLY remains unknown, and its physiological relevance in vivo remains to be fully elucidated. Future studies should employ ChIP to validate the binding between Gli and ACLY, and utilize animal models or patient-derived xenografts to confirm the role of this axis in a more complex and physiologically relevant context. Second, while we identified histone acetylation as a key mechanism, the specific HATs and HDACs mediating this process remain unknown. Identification and characterization of changes in the activity, subcellular localization, and protein protein interactions of these enzymes within this pathway will further elucidate the molecular details of the signaling cascade and may uncover additional therapeutic targets. Furthermore, the impact of ACLY phosphorylation mutants and HATs on histone acetylation is not yet fully understood. Subsequent studies should employ chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) combined with enzymatic active-site mutagenesis, RNA interference, or gene knockout techniques to verify the site-specific effects of the mutants and the involvement of specific HATs. Finally, the potential off-target effects of ACLY inhibitors or SHH pathway modulators need to be carefully evaluated to ensure therapeutic specificity. Comprehensive pharmacological studies and the development of more selective inhibitors could address this issue.

In conclusion, this study provides critical insights into the metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk underlying CAF activation in gastric cancer. By identifying the SHH/ACLY/acetyl-CoA axis as a key regulatory mechanism, we highlight a novel avenue for therapeutic intervention targeting stromal components of the TME. Targeting the SHH/ACLY/acetyl-CoA axis identified in this study, particularly through the use of ACLY inhibitors (such as SB-204990) or selective HDAC inhibitors in combination with existing standard chemotherapy, may represent a novel therapeutic strategy against the gastric cancer microenvironment. This combined approach aims to sensitize tumors to chemotherapy by reversing the tumor-promoting functions of CAFs and disrupting the CAF-mediated protective tumor niche, thereby offering a new perspective for overcoming tumor drug resistance.
