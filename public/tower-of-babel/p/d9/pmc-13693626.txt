Did COVID show us how to address climate change? An infectious diseases physician and a climate‐health researcher think so
Authors: Nina Lansbury, Andrew M Redmond
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe write as an infectious diseases physician and a climate‐health researcher who can see four breakthroughs during COVID that are instructive to effective climate action. These are the whole‐of‐government approach on COVID, targeted and rapidly available funding, prevention as a key focus and acting as a community despite curtailment of our liberties. These are all relevant and applicable to another of our world's wicked health problems: climate change.Keywords: COVID, infectious disease, climate change, public health, health policy

Discussion

Late last year, around 700 climate researchers of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) met in Paris to commence writing the next global assessment report. 1 This gathering was in person, which contrasts with the IPCC author meetings between 2020 and 2022, where severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (COVID) lockdowns across the world forced the meetings and report to launch online. 2

We write as an infectious diseases physician (A. M. Redmond) and a climate‐health researcher who is an IPCC coordinating lead author (N. Lansbury). For us, the experience of COVID is not just a memory; it is also an insight into how rapid and systemic change could occur in our personal and professional lives and guide us in how to address climate change. In my (A. M. Redmond) infectious diseases medical work over 20 years, I have consistently been preparing for ‘the next pandemic’. I was in leadership roles during the COVID years, implementing rapid policy implementation and practice change. In my (N. Lansbury) health‐related climate‐change research over 25 years, I have repeatedly heard reasons against political, economic and social action for transformative shifts to build a low‐emission and high‐efficiency energy system that is supported across jurisdictions, accepted by society and sufficiently funded.

On reflection, we note four breakthroughs made in the COVID response that are instructive to one of the other ‘wicked’ health problems facing our society: how to enact effective climate action. While we acknowledge these are very different public health crises as they diverge across key dimensions that shape governance, policy feasibility and public engagement, we consider they provide a motivating comparison.

First, COVID required various governmental jurisdictional levels to reach agreement across party and ideological lines. COVID responses included implementation of mandatory lockdowns, mask‐wearing and vaccination. In Australia, where we live and work, this required an unprecedented whole‐of‐government approach, including state and territorial governments successfully sitting and working at the same ‘table’ as the national government. 3 For climate change, political divisions have been fraught with several Australian elections being won (Rudd) then lost (Gillard) over climate action. 4 Of course, causality is clearer in the pandemic context, while climate change involves globally distributed causes – thereby increasing uncertainty and political contestation. In addition, governance differs in scale; pandemic responses were enacted largely within national jurisdictions, while climate change is inherently transnational. Yet cross‐party co‐operation on climate change could, like that achieved during COVID responses, adopt the IPCC's guidance for climate‐resilient development to be achieved by overcoming jurisdictional and organisational barriers. 5

Second, sufficient, targeted and rapidly available funding was needed during COVID for an effective response to prevent transmission and mitigate the effects of infection. In Australia, spending on COVID‐related programmes between 2019 and 2023 was estimated at AU$27.7 billion, of which one‐third was directed to vaccine development and delivery. 6 Although COVID was an acute crisis with immediate, visible harms and climate change has a slow onset with long lags between cause and effect, the need for funding remains. Climate‐change action funding is also costly; the COP30 climate meetings last November in Brazil recommended global climate finance rising to at least US$1.3 trillion annually by 2035. 7 Australia's Climate Change Authority, the statutory body tasked with advising the Australian Government, warns that not investing now in climate action could cost AU$8.7 billion a year by 2050 through damage from extreme weather events. 8 Spending upfront in COVID‐style could avoid this costly outcome being realised.

Third, health protection was a priority during COVID. Prevention efforts are often hard to finance adequately, as there can be a ‘large gap between rhetorical and substantive commitment to prevention’. 9 This is likely because an ‘averted disaster’ is less visible than a disaster that occurs. In Australia, this perspective is manifested in public health funding receiving less than 3% of the total government health expenditures. 10 This is despite the return on investment in public health being found in multiple evaluations to reduce flow‐on costs for health services and improve quality of life. 11 The response to COVID was a refreshing contrast, where prevention was a key focus, and monitoring actual against projected infections became global and national aspects of interest. 12 , 13 Public education on transmission prevention through behaviours and protective equipment was a sometimes daily phenomenon from the states' and national Chief Health Officers. 14 Climate change is also a public health issue worthy of ‘prevention before cure’. The latest IPCC report emphasises to policymakers that ‘early action and enabling conditions create future opportunities for climate resilient development’, while delaying action will result in dangerous global warming and an inability to meet the UN Sustainable Development Goals. 15

Finally, much was asked of us during COVID to accept limitations on our individual liberties in order to be part of a society, much as John Locke explained many centuries ago. 16 As the realities of COVID set in back in March 2020, Prime Minister Scott Morrison advised Australia that ‘it is important that we who are healthy … do what we can to limit the spread to ensure that those who are more vulnerable are not affected. If we slow the spread, then we do save lives’. 17 Australians had to adopt a community perspective with lockdowns, limited gatherings, mandated vaccine schedules and mask‐wearing balanced against individual freedoms to protect those with greater vulnerability. 18 In doing so, transmission rates of COVID were much lower than projected, with Australia's total excess mortality rate the fifth lowest of OECD countries. 6 Despite their severity, COVID measures were temporary and with tangible and immediate benefits of reduced numbers of cases, along with reduced attendant morbidity and mortality. In contrast, climate mitigation and adaptation efforts require long‐term structural transformation. Yet the similarity remains, where climate change actions also necessitate a curtailment of a range of liberties and expectations for societal safety. The IPCC explains that for industries, this means reducing fossil fuel reliance through transitions to renewable energy and energy efficiency, as agreed by national governments at COP28 in Dubai in 2023. 19 For individuals, especially those with high incomes and, thus, likely high emissions, this means adopting changes to lifestyles, behaviours and technologies, such as dietary changes, reduced air travel and transition to electric vehicles. 15

Climate change action has previously been delayed or diluted due to politicisation, reluctance to spend on prevention and resistance to curtailment of our liberties. Yet COVID action overcame all these same barriers in a short time frame and protected our population against a much grimmer alternative outcome. Indeed, COVID led to the creation of the Australian Centre for Disease Control that formally launched through the Australian CDC Act this year with the purpose of ‘helping Australia prepare for, respond to and prevent public health emergencies … and bring together the best science and expertise to provide trusted, evidence‐based advice that protects people and communities across the country’. 20 When opening last year's meeting in Paris, the IPCC chair made a similar pledge: to provide national governments with the ‘best available science for effective climate action and policymaking’. 1 Can these lessons from COVID show us how to address climate change?
