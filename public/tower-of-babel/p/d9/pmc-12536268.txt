Lactate Metabolism in Intervertebral Disc Degeneration: Unveiling Novel Mechanisms Through Bioinformatics
Authors: Haiyan Sun, Mingwei He, Jinlei Pang, Xiangfei Guo, Yansong Huo, Jun Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIntervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is a widespread issue associated with chronic lumbar pain and disability. This study aimed to identify lactate metabolism‐related genes in IDD and elucidate their mechanistic roles in disease progression.MethodsIDD datasets were analyzed using R packages GEOquery, sva, and limma for data retrieval, batch correction, and normalization. Differential gene expression analysis identified significant genes between IDD and control groups, from which lactate metabolism‐related differentially expressed genes (LMRDEGs) were derived. Relationships among the LMRDEGs were assessed using Spearman's correlation analysis, and functional enrichment was conducted using ClusterProfiler. Gene set enrichment analysis identified biological processes associated with IDD. Diagnostic models were assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Immune cell infiltration and correlations with core genes were analyzed via the CIBERSORT algorithm. Regulatory networks were constructed, and reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) was employed to validate the expression of hub LMRDEGs in IDD.ResultsA total of 1325 differentially expressed genes were identified, yielding seven LMRDEGs: TGFβ2, GSR, MB, MMP2, SLC16A7, PER2, and STAT3, which are enriched in blood circulation regulation and hypoxic response, as well as pathways like AGE–RAGE signaling in diabetic complications. ROC analysis indicated potential hub genes (MMP2, MB, TGFβ2, and PER2), while immune infiltration analysis uncovered significant variations in immune cell distribution. RT‐qPCR confirmed MMP2, MB, and SLC16A7 as molecular indicators reflecting lactate metabolism abnormalities in IDD.ConclusionThis study clarifies how lactate metabolism contributes to IDD through molecular mechanisms and its interplay with immunological features, providing a theoretical basis for understanding the early pathogenesis of IDD.

Introduction

Intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) is one of the most common degenerative spinal diseases, with its prevalence varying significantly across studies and populations, affecting approximately 7.6%–37% of individuals worldwide [1]. This variability is primarily attributed to differences in diagnostic criteria, sample characteristics, methodology, and regional socioeconomic factors. For example, data from US Federal Health Insurance showed that the prevalence of IDD based on radiographic interpretation was 27.3%, which mainly reflects cases diagnosed through the Medicare system among elderly Americans [2]. Notably, elderly individuals exhibit significantly higher rates of IDD compared to younger populations. The Wakayama Spine Study by Teraguchi et al. [3] demonstrated an increase in IDD prevalence with age. IDD prevalence was 71% in men and 77% in women under 50 years, whereas it exceeded 90% in both sexes aged over 50 years. Low back pain is a common symptom of IDD, significantly affecting patients' quality of life and increasing the burden on family and public healthcare systems. Common clinical interventions such as conservative treatment, including pain relief, physical therapy, and anti‐inflammatory medications, often fail to prevent the progression of IDD. For patients with acute neurological injury, cauda equina syndrome, or chronic pain who do not respond to conservative treatment, surgical treatment like discectomy and laminectomy becomes necessary. However, complications related to surgery cannot be ignored, such as intraoperative nerve damage and postoperative adjacent segment lesions. The intricate etiology and pathogenesis of IDD are the main reasons for the lack of effective treatment measures. Therefore, in‐depth investigation into the pathological mechanisms of IDD is expected to provide a theoretical basis for the development of innovative therapies, thereby offering new directions and guidance for the clinical treatment of affected patients.

IDD involves progressive structural changes driven by metabolic imbalances within a hypoxic and nutrient‐deprived microenvironment of the intervertebral disc (IVD). Due to its unique avascular structure, the nucleus pulposus (NP) tissue exists in an inherently hypoxic microenvironment under physiological conditions. Hypoxia represents a normal physiological feature for maintaining metabolic homeostasis of this tissue. This microenvironment relies on diffusion between NP and the cartilage endplate for oxygen and nutrients [4, 5]. However, the limited diffusion efficiency thereby facilitates metabolite accumulation [4]. Lactate, a byproduct of anaerobic glycolysis produced predominantly by NP cells in hypoxic disc microenvironments [4], requires precise regulation of its production, transport, and clearance to maintain intracellular pH and tissue homeostasis [6]. Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) catalyzes the reversible conversion between pyruvate and lactate during glycolysis. Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate is decarboxylated into acetyl CoA for entry into the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, ultimately generating CO2, H2O, and adenosine triphosphate (ATP). In anaerobic conditions, LDH converts pyruvate into lactate. In IDD progression, pathological hypoxia exacerbates cellular metabolic dysfunction: impaired glucose/oxygen utilization restricts pyruvate entry into the TCA cycle, while defective lactate transport (through efflux inhibition or excessive uptake) causes pathological accumulation. Studies show that lactate concentrations rise significantly in degenerative IVD areas [7], often 8–10 times higher than in plasma [8]. Notably, intradiscal lactate injection in healthy rats directly induces degenerative phenotypes [9], demonstrating lactate homeostasis disruption as a central driver of IDD progression.

Elevated lactate levels are associated with extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation, IVD cell dysfunction, apoptosis, and activation of inflammatory pathways [5, 7]. Recent studies have demonstrated that high lactate concentrations directly suppress glycosaminoglycan expression while significantly increasing matrix metalloproteinase 3 (MMP3) production [5]. Lactate promotes NP cell senescence through Akt interaction [10], which further contributes to the progression of IDD. Although the role of lactate metabolism imbalance in IDD has been partially elucidated, its precise regulatory networks (including key genes and signaling pathways) and clinical translational potential (as biomarkers or therapeutic targets) remain unclear. Investigating lactate metabolism‐related genes (LMRGs) may help reveal novel insights into pathogenesis and inform therapeutic strategies for IDD.

This study aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms linking abnormal lactate metabolism to IDD pathogenesis using bioinformatics. We identified lactate metabolism‐related differentially expressed genes (LMRDEGs), performed correlation and enrichment analyses, constructed diagnostic models and regulatory networks, and evaluated immune cell infiltration. The results provide insights into the molecular mechanisms and immune landscape related to lactate metabolism in IDD, enhancing understanding of early pathological processes related to metabolic disorders in IDD.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

Three IDD datasets (GSE34095 [11], GSE147383 [12], and GSE70362 [13]) were downloaded from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) [14] (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) using the R GEOquery package [15]. All samples were obtained from the NP tissues of human IVDs and were analyzed using microarray platforms GPL96, GPL570, and GPL17810, respectively. GSE34095, GSE147383, and GSE70362 contain 3 IDD and 3 control samples, 2 IDD and 2 control samples, and 16 IDD and 8 control samples, respectively. Detailed sample information is provided in Table S1.

We searched for “Lactate Metabolism” in the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/) [16], retrieving 54 LMRGs, and obtained an additional 19 LMRGs from the published literature using the same search keyword in PubMed (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) [17]. After consolidation and deduplication, 73 unique LMRGs were identified (Table S2). Batch effects in the datasets were corrected with the R sva package [18] to construct a combined GEO dataset comprising 21 IDD samples (group: IDD) and 13 control samples (group: control). Data normalization and probe annotation were performed using the R limma package. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to visually assess batch effect correction and to reduce data dimensionality for intuitive 2D/3D visualization.

Identification of Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs)

Samples in the combined dataset were categorized as either IDD or control. R limma was used for differential gene expression analysis, with |logFC| > 0 and p < 0.05 as screening thresholds for DEGs. Genes with logFC > 0 and p < 0.05 were considered upregulated, whereas those with logFC < 0 and p < 0.05 were considered downregulated. The Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) test was used to correct p‐values, and the results were visualized as a volcano plot using ggplot2 software.

Intersecting DEGs (|logFC| > 0, p < 0.05) with LMRGs yielded LMRDEGs associated with IDD, as visualized in a Venn diagram. Spearman's correlation analysis was conducted on the LMRDEGs, with results plotted as correlation heatmaps using the R packages igraph and ggraph. Correlation coefficients (r values) were categorized as follows: < 0.3 (weak/no correlation), 0.3–0.5 (weak), 0.5–0.8 (moderate), > 0.8 (strong). Subsequently, a simple heatmap was constructed using pheatmap. The Mann–Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test) was used to compare LMRDEG expression across groups, as depicted in the group comparison maps. The R package RCircos was used to map the locations of LMRDEGs on human chromosomes.

Functional Enrichment Analysis

Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, including biological processes (BPs), cell components (CCs), and molecular functions (MFs), is a prevalent technique for large‐scale functional enrichment studies. The Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), a vital resource for data on genomes, biological pathways, diseases, and drugs, was also utilized. We performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of LMRDEGs using clusterProfiler [19], with adjusted p < 0.05 and false discovery rate (FDR, q value) < 0.25 as screening thresholds. The BH test was used to correct p‐values.

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA)

GSEA assesses gene distribution patterns within predefined sets, ranked by their phenotypic correlation, to gauge their phenotypic contribution. This study prioritized genes in the combined GEO datasets using logFC values and employed clusterProfiler for the GSEA of all genes. The GSEA parameters included a seed of 2022, 1000 permutations, and gene sets spanning 10–500 genes. Gene set c2.cp.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt (3050) from the Molecular Signature Database (MSigDB) [20] facilitated this analysis. The screening thresholds of GSEA were set as adjusted p < 0.05 and FDR value (q value) < 0.25, with the BH test applied for p‐value correction.

Establishment of a Diagnostic Model for IDD

Logistic regression, which is utilized for binary classification of IDD and control subjects, employs the logit function to convert gene expression measurements into probabilities of disease occurrence, thereby facilitating the identification of genes significantly associated with IDD. Using LMRDEGs as independent variables and the corresponding group status as the dependent variable, genes with p < 0.05 are identified as significant. This analysis is conducted using standard configurations within R packages, which include the incorporation of an intercept term and the application of l2 regularization, to effectively filter for key genes associated with IDD and to refine the selection of potential candidate genes.

The support vector machine (SVM) algorithm was then employed to build the SVM model using the LMRDEGs identified from the univariate logistic regression analysis. SVM differentiates groups by finding the optimal classification hyperplane, using kernel functions for nonlinear data and optimizing parameters to minimize classification errors. Based on the genes screened by logistic regression, model parameters were optimized via cross‐validation to retain genes with the best classification performance, filtering LMRDEGs to maximize accuracy and minimize error rate and enhance discriminative ability for IDD.

Subsequently, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was performed using the R package glmnet with parameters set.seed(500) and family = “binomial,” based on the 6 LMRDEGs retained after SVM model optimization: MMP2, MB, GSR, TGFβ2, SLC16A7, and PER2. LASSO applies an L1 penalty (λ × |coefficients|) to reduce overfitting while improving generalization performance by shrinking nonessential gene coefficients to zero, enabling variable selection. The optimal λ was determined via cross‐validation, and genes with non‐zero coefficients were designated as hub genes. The results were visualized using coefficient trajectory plots and cross‐validation error curves, with the selected LMRDEGs (hub genes) constituting the final IDD diagnostic model. Finally, the LASSO risk score (RiskScore) was calculated using the following formula: RiskScore=∑iCoefficientgenei×mRNA Expressiongenei The comparative assessment of GO annotations offers a quantitative approach to measuring the similarity between genes and genomes, underpinning numerous bioinformatic analysis methodologies. Using the R package GOSemSim, we quantified the functional correlations among hub genes through functional similarity (friends) analysis to understand their interactions.

Validation of a Diagnostic Model for IDD

A nomogram was created using the R package rms to visually represent the interactions among hub genes based on LASSO regression. This graphical tool illustrates the functional relationships between multiple independent variables. A calibration curve was used to assess the accuracy and precision of the IDD diagnostic model derived from the LASSO results. The R package ggDCA was used to generate the decision curve analysis (DCA), providing evaluations of clinical forecasting models and biomarkers. Finally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted using the pROC package to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) values, helping to evaluate the diagnostic impact of the LASSO RiskScore on IDD onset.

IDD samples were stratified into high‐ and low‐risk groups based on the median risk score, followed by group comparison plots to examine differential expression of hub genes. ROC curves for the hub genes were also plotted, with AUC calculations to assess their diagnostic potential for IDD. An AUC of 0.5–1 indicates diagnostic performance, with values closer to 1 reflecting better accuracy. Specifically, AUCs of 0.5–0.7, 0.7–0.9, and > 0.9 indicate low, moderate, and high accuracy, respectively.

GSEA Between High‐ and Low‐Risk Groups

IDD samples from the combined GEO datasets were classified into high‐ and low‐risk groups based on the median LASSO risk score. The R package limma was used for differential analysis. Subsequently, ggplot2 was used to visualize the genes with |logFC| > 0 and p < 0.05 between the risk groups via a volcano plot. Finally, clusterProfiler was employed to conduct GSEA on all genes using MSigDB c2.cp.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt (all canonical pathways, 3050 sets) as a reference. GSEA parameters included a seed of 2022, gene set sizes spanning 10–500, significance thresholds of adjusted p < 0.05, and FDR (q value) < 0.25, with BH applied for p‐value correction.

Construction of Regulatory Network

Transcription factors (TFs) regulate gene expression at the transcriptional level by interacting with hub genes. The TFs were retrieved from the ChIPBase database (https://rna.sysu.edu.cn/chipbase/), and their regulatory role in LMRDEGs was analyzed. Cytoscape software was used to visualize the messenger RNA (mRNA)–TF regulatory network. To explore the interplay between hub genes and miRNAs, we used the StarBase v3.0 database (https://starbase.sysu.edu.cn/) to identify miRNAs related to hub genes. The mRNA–miRNA regulatory network was then visualized with Cytoscape.

Immune Infiltration Analysis of High‐ and Low‐Risk Groups

We deciphered the transcriptome data to assess the composition and distribution of immune cells in mixed cell populations using CIBERSORT [21]. By integrating the LM22 gene signature matrix, we identified specific immune cell infiltration patterns in IDD samples from the combined GEO dataset. We visualized the proportion of LM22 immune cells in the high‐ and low‐risk IDD groups with stacked bar charts using ggplot2. Additionally, we assessed immune cell correlations using Spearman's rank correlation and presented the findings as a heatmap using pheatmap. The association between hub genes and immunological cells was determined based on the Spearman's rank correlation, retaining significant results with p < 0.05 and depicting a correlation bubble plot using ggplot2. Finally, we highlighted the top two positively and negatively correlated hub gene–immune cell pairs with scatter plots generated using ggplot2 to illustrate these relationships.

Patients

A total of 10 patients with low back pain or radiating leg pain due to single‐level lumbar disc degeneration, as confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (Pfirrmann Grades IV–V [22]), were recruited as the IDD group from the Pain Department of Beijing Anzhen Hospital, Capital Medical University (Beijing, China) between May 2024 and August 2024. Individuals with a history of degenerative spondylolisthesis, lumbar spinal stenosis, ankylosing spondylitis, renal disease, pelvic infection, diabetes mellitus, or other systemic inflammatory diseases were excluded from the IDD group. During the same period, 10 healthy volunteers without back/leg pain (MRI‐confirmed Pfirrmann Grades I–III) were recruited as the control group from the physical examination center of the same institution. All control participants had no history of spinal disorders, metabolic diseases, or chronic inflammatory conditions, as confirmed by medical records and physical examination. Fasting whole blood (5 mL) was collected from the left median cubital vein of each participant between 6:00 and 6:20 a.m. and immediately stored at −80°C within 1 h for subsequent RNA extraction and RT‐qPCR analysis.

RNA Extraction and Reverse Transcription Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (RT‐qPCR) Analyses

Total RNAs were isolated from blood samples using a TRIzol Reagent Kit (Solarbio Scientific and Technology Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) following the manufacturer's guidelines. Subsequently, the RNAs were reverse‐transcribed into first‐strand complementary DNAs using a cDNA Synthesis Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China). RT‐qPCR was then performed on the MA‐6000 real‐time fluorescence qPCR system (Molarray Co. Ltd., Suzhou, China) with Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen Biotechnology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China). The RT‐qPCR was conducted using the following protocols: predenaturation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 10 s, annealing at 60°C for 20 s, and extension at 72°C for 20 s, culminating in a melting curve analysis using the instrument's default settings. Relative expression of hub genes was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method (n = 10 per group) and normalized to GAPDH. The qPCR primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Data processing and analysis were conducted using R (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.3). For pairwise comparisons of continuous variables, Student's t‐test was used to assess the significance of normally distributed data, except where mentioned otherwise. The Mann–Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank‐sum test) was used to analyze nonnormally distributed data. The Kruskal–Wallis test was employed to compare multiple groups. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to determine relationships between molecules. RT‐qPCR results, presented as means ± standard deviation, were analyzed using GraphPad Prism and assessed with a paired Student's t‐test. Unless stated otherwise, all p‐values were two‐tailed, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Determination of LMRDEGs

The combined GEO datasets were divided into IDD and control groups. Differential gene expression analysis using the R limma package identified 1325 DEGs (|logFC| > 0 and p < 0.05), including 640 upregulated (logFC > 0 and p < 0.05) and 685 downregulated (logFC < 0 and p < 0.05) genes. A volcano plot was constructed to visualize these findings (Figure 2A). All the DEGs satisfying |logFC| > 0 and p < 0.05 were intersected with the LMRGs to obtain the LMRDEGs, which were displayed as a Venn diagram (Figure 2B). A total of seven LMRDEGs were obtained: TGFβ2, GSR, MB, MMP2, SLC16A7, PER2, and STAT3. Specific LMRDEG‐related information is presented in Table 2.

Establishment of a Diagnostic Model for IDD

To evaluate the diagnostic potential of the seven LMRDEGs for IDD, a logistic regression model was developed incorporating these genes, as detailed in Table 5. Statistical significance (p < 0.05) validated the relevance of TGFβ2, GSR, MB, MMP2, SLC16A7, PER2, and STAT3. Following this, an SVM model was constructed using the SVM algorithm. The model was optimized to minimize error (Figure 4A) and maximize accuracy (Figure 4B), achieving peak performance with six genes—MMP2, MB, GSR, TGFβ2, SLC16A7, and PER2—all associated with lactate metabolism. The LASSO regression analysis then refined the diagnostic model based on these six genes. The results were visualized as a LASSO regression model diagram (Figure 4C) and variable trajectory plot (Figure 4D). The LASSO model identified five hub genes: MMP2, MB, GSR, TGFβ2, and PER2. Functional similarity analysis (Figure 4E) highlighted the critical role of MMP2 in IDD, positioning it closest to the critical threshold (cut‐off = 0.60).

Notably, GSR, MMP2, and PER2 exhibited highly significant expression differences (p < 0.001). MMP2 was overexpressed in the high‐risk group, whereas PER2 was elevated in the low‐risk group. Finally, ROC curve analysis (Figure 5F–J) using the pROC package in R demonstrated moderate to high accuracy (0.7 < AUC < 0.9) for MMP2, MB, TGFβ2, and PER2 in distinguishing between groups, whereas GSR exhibited lower accuracy (0.5 < AUC < 0.7).

Discussion

IDD affects the structure and function of the spine, impairing its mobility and flexibility. This condition causes chronic back pain and neurological dysfunction, resulting in muscle atrophy, progressive disability, and decreased quality of life [23]. Beyond localized spinal damage, IDD can also increase the risk of venous thrombosis, pulmonary infections, cardiovascular disease, and metabolic disorders due to reduced mobility and prolonged bed rest [24, 25, 26, 27], highlighting its role as a major geriatric morbidity factor. Current treatments, including conservative and surgical interventions, have limited efficacy in halting or reversing the natural progression of IDD, emphasizing the urgent need to clarify its molecular mechanisms and identify innovative therapeutic targets. Metabolic alterations, particularly lactate metabolism, have been implicated in the pathophysiology of IDD [7, 8, 10, 28]. To investigate the specific molecular mechanisms of lactate metabolism in IDD, we conducted a comprehensive genomic data analysis. We first filtered 1325 DEGs, including 640 upregulated and 685 downregulated genes, and subsequently identified seven LMRDEGs.

These LMRDEGs were significantly enriched in BPs and pathways critical to IDD pathophysiology, including blood circulation regulation, vascular processes of the circulatory system, rhythmic processes, and response to hypoxia—all of which are crucial for maintaining disc cell metabolism homeostasis [4, 6]. Furthermore, the LMRDEGs were notably concentrated in pathways, including the AGE–RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, diabetic cardiomyopathy, protein glycosylation in cancer, inflammatory bowel disease, and acute myeloid leukemia. In particular, the AGE–RAGE signaling pathway plays a significant role in the progression of age‐related diseases, especially diabetes and cardiovascular diseases [29]. Activation of AGE‐RAGE signaling triggers oxidative stress and inflammation, both known as key factors of IDD [30]. The association between LMRDEGs and pathways associated with diabetic complications suggests shared mechanisms between IDD and metabolic disorders like Type 2 diabetes mellitus and obesity, such as altered glucose metabolism and cellular stress responses [25]. Our results thus offer insights for the identification of common therapeutic targets for both conditions.

Moreover, we identified five hub genes among the LMRDEGs through machine learning analyses: MMP2 (p = 0.026, AUC = 0.788), MB (p = 0.012, AUC = 0.725), TGFβ2 (p = 0.011, AUC = 0.758), PER2 (p = 0.007, AUC = 0.769), and GSR (p = 0.012, AUC = 0.679). Together, these genes form a multi‐dimensional panel that captures IDD pathogenesis across ECM breakdown (MMP2), hypoxia (MB), fibrosis (TGFβ2), circadian disruption (PER2), and oxidative stress (GSR). The combined model achieves an AUC of 0.938, offers clarity on molecular mechanisms, and facilitates the selection of targeted therapies. Validation via RT‐qPCR indicated the roles of MMP2, MB, and SLC16A7 as potential signature genes reflecting lactate metabolism dysregulation in IDD. While our mechanistic framework focused on five core genes (MMP2, MB, TGFβ2, PER2, GSR) identified through integrative analysis, we further examined SLC16A7 (encoding monocarboxylate transporter 2 [MCT2]) and STAT3 (a TF that cooperates with HIF‐1α in hypoxia [31]) due to their critical mechanistic links to lactate accumulation and IDD [5, 6, 32]. Notably, the significant upregulation of SLC16A7 (p < 0.001, Figure 8G) highlights impaired lactate transport as a driver of metabolic dysfunction in IDD, aligning with prior evidence that lactate shuttling disruptions exacerbate disc pathology [5, 6]. This multigene validation strengthens the biological plausibility of our mechanistic insights, reinforcing the central role of lactate homeostasis dysregulation in IDD pathogenesis.

MMPs play a crucial role in ECM degradation and remodeling as well as NP fibrosis [33, 34, 35]. The increased MMP2 expression in IDD samples from our study, consistent with previous studies [33, 36, 37], highlights its role as a potential signature gene and therapeutic target. The pro‐fibrogenic activity of MMP2 in myofibroblastic NP cells suggests that its inhibition could potentially preserve IVD integrity [34, 38]. Our risk score model further highlighted the crucial role of MMP2, confirmed by its near‐critical value in the functional similarity analysis, indicating a significant impact of MMP2 on IDD. Consequently, targeting MMP2 activity presents a promising strategy for mitigating degenerative processes and preserving disc integrity. However, MMP2 not only promotes matrix degradation under pathological conditions but also participates in normal physiological remodeling of healthy tissues [39]. This dual role raises significant concerns regarding the systemic inhibition of MMP2, which could inadvertently impede the repair and reconstruction of normal tissues [40]. To mitigate these adverse effects, future strategies should focus on localized delivery mechanisms, such as drug microspheres, gels, or sustained‐release systems specifically engineered for IVD [41, 42]. Such innovations would enable targeted drug release at the injury site, minimizing systemic exposure and potential side effects [43]. Moreover, the implementation of tissue‐specific regulatory techniques, including the use of specific promoters, delivery vectors, or targeting molecules, could facilitate the modulation of MMP2 activity exclusively within IVD, preserving surrounding healthy tissue function [44, 45]. The development of highly selective molecular inhibitors that can accurately identify and inhibit the pathological activity of MMP2 is also essential for enhancing both the safety and effectiveness of treatment strategies [46]. Nevertheless, the in vivo efficacy and safety of these intervention approaches remain to be systematically validated. Future research must rigorously evaluate the underlying mechanisms and potential side effects associated with local delivery, tissue‐specific regulation, and selective inhibition of MMP2, utilizing animal models and other methodologies. Ultimately, any strategy aimed at modulating MMP2 activity will require careful balancing of therapeutic benefits for IDD treatment and the preservation of healthy tissues before progressing to clinical trials.

MB Is Traditionally Recognized for Its Role in Oxygen Storage and Transport Within Muscle Tissues [47]

It has been used in clinical studies as a biomarker for muscle injury, providing insights into tissue damage and inflammation [47]. Recent studies have reported that MB is involved in lipid transport and storage, interacts with glycolytic byproducts such as lactic acid and pyruvic acid within cellular environments, and exhibits aberrant expression in certain cancer cell types and brown adipose tissue. These findings suggest that MB may function as an oxygen sensor, modulating molecular signaling pathways in response to intracellular levels of partial oxygen pressure and nitric oxide [47]. In the present study, MB was upregulated in IDD, reflecting adaptation to hypoxic conditions in degenerated discs [48]. This adaptation mechanism of MB may facilitate the maintenance of cellular metabolism and survival under stress, warranting further exploration of its role in disc pathophysiology.

SLC16 Comprises 14 Members That Encode MCT Proteins

The MCT family is crucial for shuttling cellular nutrients across cell membranes, thereby performing vital functions in the regulation of intracellular metabolism and pH homeostasis [6, 49]. Among them, MCT1–4 are individually encoded by SLC16A1, SLC16A7, SLC16A8, and SLC16A3 and have been thoroughly investigated for their involvement in the H + ‐coupled translocation of levolactic acid, pyruvic acid, short‐chain fatty acids, and single‐carboxylate medications across various tissues. Notably, SLC16A7 encodes MCT2, which has the highest affinity for lactate [50]. In general, MCT‐mediated lactate transport is directionally dependent on its concentration gradient. Under hypoxic conditions within IVDs, the extracellular lactate concentration increases, driving MCT‐mediated lactate influx. Moreover, MCT‐1‐mediated lactate accumulation was reported to promote NP degeneration [5]. In our study, altered expression of SLC16A7 likely reflects adaptive changes in metabolic demand and lactate handling within IVDs. These alterations could lead to disruption of intracellular pH homeostasis, thereby contributing to ECM degradation and disc cell dysfunction [6]. Despite its functional significance, SLC16A7 was not included in the final diagnostic model for IDD, likely due to the model's focus on multivariate efficacy rather than individual gene importance. However, RT‐qPCR validation confirmed its marked upregulation in IDD samples (p < 0.001), even exceeding some model‐included hub genes, highlighting its potential as a key player in lactate‐driven IDD mechanisms.

Hypoxia‐inducible factor 1α (HIF‐1α) serves as a central transcriptional regulator of cellular adaptation to hypoxia. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF‐1α escapes proteasomal degradation, dimerizes with HIF‐1β, and activates target genes [31]. In degenerated IVDs, upregulated HIF‐1α levels correlate with apoptosis severity [51] and drive metabolic reprogramming through multiple mechanisms. First, lactate stabilizes HIF‐1α by inhibiting proline hydroxylase activity [52], and HIF‐1α then induces the expression of glycolysis‐related genes such as glucose transporter 1 (Glut1), hexokinase 2 (HK2), and pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase‐1 (PDK1), forming a metabolic positive feedback loop [53]. Second, HIF‐1α regulates the expression of MCT1 [54], influencing intracellular lactate levels and disc cell function [55]. Our findings through GO/KEGG analysis demonstrate significant enrichment of lactate metabolism genes in the hypoxia response pathway. GSEA results further showed that signals such as “hypoxia adaptation” were abnormally activated in degenerated IVDs. These results are highly consistent with the metabolic regulatory networks mediated by HIF‐1α and suggest functional coordination between MCT2 and HIF‐1α in lactate metabolism during IDD progression. Targeting the MCT2‐HIF‐1α axis to regulate lactate homeostasis may provide a new therapeutic strategy for IDD. However, current discussions on the regulatory relationship between lactate metabolism and HIF‐1α are primarily based on bioinformatics analyses and literature evidence, lacking direct experimental validation. Future studies should employ chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP‐seq) and functional assays to validate physical interactions within the MCT2‐HIF‐1α axis and elucidate its mechanistic contributions to IDD pathogenesis.

Lactate metabolism, a critical branch of the energy metabolic networks, forms a highly coupled network with other pathways such as glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation through intricate cross‐talk [4]. In the hypoxic and nutrient‐deprived microenvironment of IVDs, glycolysis serves as the primary energy‐harvesting pathway for cells [48, 56]. Pyruvate generated via glycolysis is predominantly converted to lactate by LDH under hypoxic conditions, leading to its accumulation both intra‐ and extracellularly. Concomitantly, the reduced efficiency of oxidative phosphorylation further promotes lactate production and accumulation. Lactate not only acts as a metabolic intermediate to influence intracellular pH, apoptosis, and inflammatory responses but also regulates the expression of glycolysis‐related genes through feedback mechanisms involving signaling pathways like HIF‐1α, thereby maintaining dynamic equilibrium between metabolic pathways [57]. Additionally, transmembrane transport of lactate relies on MCTs, whose expression and activity are modulated by cellular energy status and the redox environment [50]. This dynamic interplay is crucial for maintaining energy supply, antioxidant defense, and cellular homeostasis in disc cells. During the progression of IDD, imbalances in energy metabolism may exacerbate cellular damage and matrix degradation by affecting the expression of genes associated with lactate metabolism and their synergistic interactions with other metabolic pathways [5, 6, 10, 58]. Elucidating the interactive mechanisms between lactate metabolism and pathways such as glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation will facilitate a comprehensive understanding of the metabolic basis of IDD, providing a theoretical foundation for integrative interventions targeting the metabolic network.

The process of feature selection and model optimization is systematically illustrated in Figure 4 to support our mechanistic exploration. Specifically, Figure 4A assesses how the performance of the model varies with different numbers of features when using the SVM algorithm. In Figure 4B, we observe the relationship between model accuracy and the number of features employed, which highlights performance trends. Figure 4C presents the cross‐validation curve for LASSO regression, aiding understanding of the model's reliability across various data subsets. Meanwhile, Figure 4D shows how the coefficients of features change throughout the LASSO regression process, providing insights into the significance of each feature. Finally, Figure 4E reveals the results from the functional similarity analysis conducted on core genes, illuminating their relationships and interactions. Focusing on the SVM‐based feature selection depicted in Figure 4A,B, we systematically reduced the number of features to optimize the model's error rate and accuracy. A temporary decline in performance was observed when the feature count was reduced to five, likely due to the loss of critical information during recursive feature elimination, which disrupted the optimal complementarity among features. This non‐monotonic behavior is typical of SVM‐RFE, where performance may fluctuate before stabilizing. The model reached its peak performance with six features (MMP2, MB, GSR, TGFβ2, SLC16A7, and PER2), which were then refined through LASSO regression to identify five key hub genes, as illustrated in Figure 4C,D. To enhance the robustness of the model, future studies will aim to increase the sample size, improve feature evaluation methods, and conduct analyses across varying feature counts.

As mentioned above, we identified five hub genes in our study, with GSR demonstrating a significant contribution in the variable trajectory of LASSO regression (Figure 4D). This highlights its important statistical role in the model–building process for exploring IDD mechanisms. Notably, although MMP2 had a near‐zero final LASSO coefficient, it remains biologically significant; it serves as a crucial indicator of fibrosis in our enrichment analyses and has been experimentally confirmed to be overexpressed in IDD samples, emphasizing its involvement in the disease's pathogenesis. This discrepancy between statistical and biological relevance arises because LASSO prioritizes the predictive power and independence of variables, whereas biological function reflects the actual roles of genes in disease processes. Therefore, the statistical contribution of GSR and the biological significance of MMP2 together provide a more comprehensive understanding of the underlying mechanisms regarding IDD. This finding highlights the importance of combining statistical metrics derived from genomic data with biological evidence, as it significantly enhances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms involved in IDD.

In our study, immune infiltration analysis using the CIBERSORT algorithm revealed the intricate interaction between immune cells and LMRDEGs. The significant correlations observed between certain immune cell types and specific LMRDEGs underscore the potential mechanistic roles of these genes in modulating immune responses. For instance, TGFβ2 exhibited a strong positive correlation with M0 macrophages (r = 0.601, p < 0.01) and plasma cells (r = 0.600, p < 0.01), indicating its involvement in fibro‐inflammation and suggesting its role in T cell‐mediated immune regulation [59]. Specifically, TGFβ2 may promote ECM degradation by recruiting M0 macrophages, synergizing with the lactate‐induced inflammatory microenvironment [60]. This interaction could amplify matrix breakdown in degenerated discs, further exacerbating ECM remodeling. The correlation between macrophages and MMP2 likely reflects the involvement of this gene in ECM remodeling, a process influenced by macrophage activity during tissue repair and fibrosis [60, 61]. Conversely, MMP2 was found to have an inverse correlation with resting mast cells (r = −0.521, p < 0.05), indicating its disruptive effect on the ECM–immune balance. Additionally, PER2 negatively correlated with T follicular helper (Tfh) cells (r = −0.474, p < 0.05), suggesting a link between circadian rhythms and immune homeostasis that may further affect immune responses in the context of IDD. Identification of these correlations offers a profound perspective on the potential crosstalk between LMRDEGs and immune cells, suggesting that these genes could serve as novel targets for modulating the immune components of IDD. Targeting these immune‐metabolic axes, particularly, the roles of TGFβ2 and MMP2, enhances our understanding of IDD pathophysiology and opens avenues for innovative treatment strategies aimed at disrupting detrimental immune responses.

In conclusion, this study used bioinformatics to identify several LMRDEGs implicated in IDD. Immune infiltration analysis differentiated immune profiles across risk groups, whereas regulatory network analysis illuminated the regulatory roles of TFs and miRNAs in LMRDEGs. However, our study has some limitations. First, targeting lactate metabolism genes may induce potential side effects—such as impairing normal tissue repair due to inhibition of MMP2—yet these risks were not fully assessed in the current study, necessitating additional safety analyses through in vitro functional assays and animal models in future studies. Second, the sample size was relatively small and predominantly relied on public databases, lacking support from large‐scale, clinically well‐annotated datasets, which may have constrained the statistical power and robustness of the conclusions. To address this, we integrated multicenter data, performed batch effect correction, and employed multi‐method cross‐validation. Additionally, we conducted preliminary RT‐qPCR validation in clinical samples. However, validation experiments did not fully recapitulate the complexity of the IDD microenvironment, and protein‐level validation remains incomplete due to experimental limitations. Notably, in clinical settings, patients may exhibit comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, autoimmune disorders, or trauma), which could induce cross‐expression of identified molecules and compromise their specificity and diagnostic accuracy. Future investigations should expand sample diversity and size, systematically evaluating biomarker performance under varying comorbid states to enhance their clinical utility and generalizability. Although no significant age difference was found between groups (p > 0.05), the older average age of the IDD group may have contributed to the upregulation of genes such as MMP2 and MB, as aging is a well‐established risk factor for IDD. To confirm these findings, future research should involve age‐matched cohorts. Going forward, we plan to expand independent cohort validation, supplement proteomic and single‐cell sequencing data, and protein‐level validation (e.g., Western blot or immunohistochemistry) to more comprehensively elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying lactate metabolism. Furthermore, the interactive effects of lactate metabolism with other metabolic pathways, such as glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, were not fully explored in this study. This gap hinders a holistic understanding of the metabolic interplay underlying IDD, as lactate metabolism does not operate in isolation but within a complex network. Future research will incorporate metabolic network analyses for in‐depth investigation. The regulatory relationship between lactate metabolism and HIF‐1α, currently speculated based on literature, requires us to verify through molecular mechanism experiments in subsequent work. Finally, while MRI enables the diagnosis of advanced IDD, the identified molecules show promise for early diagnosis and disease stratification. This study primarily explores mechanistic insights into lactate metabolism, rather than clinical biomarker validation. Given the sample‐related constraints and validation limitations of this study, the term “biomarker” should be used with caution. Larger‐scale, multicenter clinical validation with comprehensive follow‐up is required to substantiate their translational value.

Taken together, these findings clarify the molecular mechanisms of lactate metabolism imbalance in IDD and its associated immunological background, providing a theoretical basis for future research on disease stratification, risk prediction, and individualized management. As a primary mechanistic exploration, this work will require further validation with larger samples and long‐term clinical follow‐up investigations to confirm the translational potential of the identified genes and pathways associated with lactate metabolism.
