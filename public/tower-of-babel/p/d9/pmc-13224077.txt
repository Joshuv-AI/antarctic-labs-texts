Rad23b exacerbates pathological aggregates through disrupting proteasome functions in Spinocerebellar ataxia type 3
Authors: Yi-Ching Chen, Chih-Wei Tung, Siew Chin Chan, Po-Ming Wu, Pei-Hsun Cheng, Chuan-Mu Chen, Shang-Hsun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Spinocerebellar ataxia type 3 (SCA3) is the most common autosomal dominant ataxia globally, caused by expanded CAG repeats in the ATXN3 gene and consequent pathogenic accumulation of mutant ATXN3 (mATXN3) aggregates. The formation of these aggregates perturbs neuronal functions and leads to progressive neurodegeneration, yet the molecular mechanisms controlling mATXN3 proteostasis remain incompletely understood. Here, we identify RAD23 homolog B (Rad23b), a ubiquitin-binding shuttle factor, as a potential regulator of mATXN3 aggregates and toxicity upon high throughput proteomic analysis. Functional assays reveal that Rad23b overexpression enhances, while Rad23b knockdown or knockout reduces, mATXN3 aggregates and neuronal cell death. Mechanistically, Rad23b directly interacts with mATXN3, promotes its ubiquitination, and facilitates its delivery to the proteasome. Paradoxically, Rad23b disrupts proteasome catalytic activity, preventing mATXN3 degradation and exacerbating aggregate formation. Immunohistochemical analysis in SCA3 transgenic mice confirms colocalization of Rad23b with mATXN3 aggregates in cerebellar neurons. These findings highlight Rad23b as a crucial modulator of mATXN3 proteostasis, and imply Rad23b as a potential therapeutic target in SCA3.

Introduction

Spinocerebellar ataxia type 3 (SCA3), also known as Machado–Joseph disease (MJD), is the most prevalent autosomal dominant neurodegenerative ataxia worldwide, with a prevalence of approximately 1–5 per 100,000 individuals [1,2]. This disorder is caused by a 55–87 repeats of cytosine-adenine-guanidine (CAG which translates to glutamine) trinucleotide expansion in ataxin-3 (ATXN3) gene, resulting in the production of mutant ATXN3 protein (mATXN3) that forms misfolded aggregates [3,4]. Due to that ATXN3 ubiquitously expresses in various peripheral and neuronal tissues [5], mATXN3 disrupts neuronal functions and leads to neurodegeneration, further manifesting a wide spectrum of phenotypes, including extrapyramidal signs, neuropathy, spasticity, ophthalmoplegia and muscle atrophy [4,6,7]. To date, no effective treatment currently exists for SCA3[1, 6], suggesting that understanding the pathogenesis of SCA3 is essential for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.

ATXN3 contains N-terminal Josephin domain (JD), ubiquitin-interacting motifs (UIMs) with a polyglutamine tract and a C-terminal region. Functionally, it has been reported to act as a deubiquitinating enzyme; however, its catalytic activity is lost when mATXN3 forms aggregates, in which the catalytic JD undergoes structural changes from its native α-helical configuration to a β-sheet-enriched conformation [8]. As a result, mATXN3 aggregates may not only impair the self-degradation of mATXN3, but also disrupt ubiquitination status in ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS), one of principal proteolytic systems. The UPS plays a vital role in maintaining cellular protein homeostasis and function by covalently tagging target proteins with ubiquitin. Once polyubiquitinated, these target proteins are recognized and degraded by the 26S proteasome, containing a 20S and 19S regulatory particles, in both the cytoplasm and nucleus [9]. Especially, the 20S regulatory particle exhibits different catalytic activities, such as caspase-like, trypsin-like, and chymotrypsin-like activities, to cleave different peptide bonds, further degrading target proteins to maintain cellular functions [10]. In SCA3, UPS function has been reported to be impaired due to mATXN3 aggregates, allowing mATXN3 to escape UPS-mediated degradation and thereby trigger neurotoxicity [11,12]. Moreover, restoring UPS activity has been shown to mitigate mATXN3 toxicity, suggesting the critical roles of UPS during the pathogenesis of SCA3.

RAD23 homolog B (Rad23b) is an important protein involved in proteasome degradation, and it comprises an N-terminal ubiquitin-like (UBL) domain that docks with the proteasome and a C-terminal ubiquitin-associated (UBA) domain that selectively binds polyubiquitylated substrates [13]. As a result, Rad23b could interact with ubiquitinated proteins and function as a shuttle factor for proteasomal degradation [[14], [15], [16]]. Rad23b normally presents at only sub stoichiometric level in 26S proteasome, and modest increases of Rad23b abundance lead to obvious impairment of proteasome activity [17]. In neurodegenerative diseases, Rad23b has been reported to colozalize in neuronal inclusions in fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome, Huntington’s disease (HD), Spinocerebellar ataxia type 7, Parkinson’s disease, and others [18]. In SCA3, prior studies have established interactions between ATXN3 and Rad23 homologs (Rad23 A/B or hHR23 A/B) in SCA3 pathogenesis, and suggested these interactions may modulate DNA repair, proteasomal degradation, protein aggregation, and neurotoxicity [[19], [20], [21], [22], [23]]. However, the mechanisms by which dysregulation of Rad23b contributes to UPS impairment and neuropathological aggregates remain poorly understood.

Because pathological aggregates are critical for parthenogenesis of several neurodegenerative diseases, suppressing these aggregates may be a potential therapeutic strategy. In our previous studies, we showed one microRNA, miR-196a, could significantly suppress the pathological aggregates in HD and other neuronal diseases [[24], [25], [26], [27], [28]]. In this study, we applied high-throughput proteomic analyses to identify one potential target, Rad23b which may be regulated by miR-196a, in SCA3 models, and demonstrated the regulatory roles of Rad23b in modulating UPS activity to affect mATXN3 degradation in SCA3 models.

Materials and Methods

Cell culture

Mouse neuroblastoma N2a cells (ATCC: CCL-131) and Rad23b knockout (Rad23b−/−) cells were cultured in Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (Invitrogen), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Hyclone) and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich, P4562). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2, and applied for different experiments.

Plasmids and transfection

The constructs used in this study include F-mATXN3, T-mATXN3, F-mATXN3-EGFP, FUW, FU-Rad23b, shLuciferase, shRad23b, UbG76V-GFP and PX459-Rad23b. The F-mATXN3 construct expresses the full-length mATXN3 gene (GenBank: BC033711.1) with a Flag-tag under the control of an ubiquitin promoter. The T-mATXN3 encompasses the C-terminal 291 amino acids of the ATXN3 gene. The F-mATXN3-EGFP expresses the full-length mATXN3 gene with a Flag-tag, fused to enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP), under the control of an ubiquitin promoter. FUW was derived from the FUGW vector (Addgene, #14883) with the deletion of EGFP gene, which is used as a control vector. FU-Rad23b expresses the mouse Rad23b gene (accession number: NM_009011.4) under the control of an ubiquitin promoter. The shRad23b and shLuciferase constructs target the coding sequence of Rad23b and luciferase gene, respectively, driven by an U6 promoter. UbG76V-GFP expresses a mutant form of ubiquitin (G76V) fused to the N-terminus of GFP under the control of a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter. The PX459-Rad23b construct includes a single guide RNA targeting the mouse Rad23b gene, driven by a human ubiquitin promoter, and the coding sequence of Streptococcus pyogenes type II CRISPR RNA-guided endonuclease (Cas9) driven by a CMV promoter. Transfection of above plasmids into cells was using Lipofectamine™ 3000 Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, L3000015) followed by the manufacturer’s manual. Cells were transfected for 48 h and harvested for subsequent analyses. For UPS and autophagy pathway experiments, Rad23b−/− cells were transfected with the F-mATXN3 plasmid for 48 h. At 25 h post-transfection, cells were treated with either 1 μM MG132 or 50 μM chloroquine (CQ) for 24 h to inhibit proteasomal degradation or autophagic flux, respectively.

Western blotting analysis

Cells and mouse brain tissues were lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. Extracted proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Ataxin-3 (GeneTex, GTX101343 or Proteintech, 13505-1-AP), Rad23b (Proteintech, 12121-1-AP), Ubiquitin (Dako, Z0458), GFP (GeneTex, GTX113617), Cleaved Caspase 3 (Cell Signaling Technology, #9661), PARP (CST, #9542), LC3B (Sigma, L7543), FLAG M2 (MilliporeSigma, F1804), and γ-tubulin (Sigma-Aldrich, T6557). Membranes were then washed by PBS, and incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies(Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein signals were detected using the T-Pro LumiLong Plus Chemiluminescent Substrate Kit (T-Pro Biotechnology, JT96-K004 M) and visualized with an automatic gel imaging system (Wealtec Corp.). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH) for statistical analysis.

Proteomic analysis

N2a cells were co-transfected mATXN3 with miR-196a or non-relative control (miR-NC) for 48 h, and raw proteins were extracted by RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors. Proteins were precipitated using acetone, and then dissolved in 6 M urea. 50–100 μg of proteins per sample were sent for analysis by Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) using an Orbitrap Elite Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Scientific™) at the NTU Consortia of Key Technologies and NTU Instrumentation Center, Taiwan. Proteomic data were analyzed using MetaboAnalyst 5.0.

Generation of Rad23b−/− N2a cell line

The CRISPR-Cas9 system was used to generate Rad23b knockout (KO) N2a cell lines. N2a cells were transfected with PX459-Rad23b plasmids, and then seeded at a density of approximately one cell per well into a 96-well plate to generate single-cell clones. After one month of culture, individual colonies were isolated and expanded. The cells were lysed in RIPA buffer, and Rad23b expression was evaluated by Western blot analysis to confirm the KO efficiency.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

N2a cells were co-transfected with Rad23b and F-mATXN3 plasmids and harvested 48 h post-transfection. Cells were crosslinked with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) and quenched with 1.25 M glycine. Cell pellets were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors. 100 μg protein lysates were incubated with anti-FLAG M2 (MilliporeSigma, F1804) or anti-Rad23b (Proteintech, 12121-1-AP) antibodies overnight at 4 °C, and mouse or rabbit normal IgG was used as a negative control. Immunocomplexes were captured by incubation with Dynabeads Protein G (Thermo Fisher Scientific) overnight at 4 °C, and beads were collected using a magnetic separator. Bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 1 × Laemmli SDS sample buffer at 100 °C for 10 min, separated by SDS-PAGE, and analyzed by Western blotting.

UbG76VGFP reporter assay

N2a cells were co-transfected with Rad23b and F-mATXN3 plasmids, along with the UbG76V-GFP construct (Addgene, #11941). After 48 h of transfection, fluorescent images were captured using a fluorescent microscope (Leica; DMi8). Cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen™, H1399). The fluorescent signal intensity was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH) for statistical analysis. Expression levels of UbG76V-GFP were further confirmed by western blotting.

Proteasome activity assay

N2a cells were transfected for 48 h, and cells were lysed in proteasome lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 1 mM EDTA, 2 mM ATP, and 1% Triton). The protein concentration was determined using the Coomassie Bradford protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #23200). For the proteasome activity assay, 1 μg protein from each sample was incubated with fluorogenic peptide substrates tagged with 7-amino-4-methylcoumarin (AMC; Suc-LLVY-AMC for determining the chymotrypsin-like activity, Enzo Life Sciences, BML-P802-0005; Boc-LRR-AMC for determining the trypsin-like activity, Enzo Life Sciences, BML-BW8515-0005; Z-LLE-AMC for determining the caspase-like activity, Enzo Life Sciences, BML-ZW9345-0005) in a white 96-well plate (Nunc®). A standard curve was generated using a geometric series of AMC-tagged peptides (Enzo Life Sciences, BML-KI107-0001) at concentrations of 0, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 pmol per well. To determine background fluorescence and basal proteasome activity, the proteasome inhibitor, MG132 (100 μM), was added to inhibit catalytic activity. The release of fluorescent AMC was measured kinetically at 37 °C using a multi-mode microplate reader (SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices), with excitation at 380 nm and emission at 460 nm. Fluorescence readings were collected every 5 min over a total period of 120 min.

Immunocytochemistry

N2a cells were fixed with 4% PFA, and then permeabilized and blocked in a solution containing 0.2% Triton X-100, 3 mM sodium azide, 0.1% saponin, 2% BSA, and 5% donkey serum in PBS for 1 h. After blocking, cells were incubated with primary antibodies ATXN3 (GeneTex, GTX101343 and Millipore, MAB5360), Rad23b (Proteintech, 12121-1-AP) or PSMB5 (Abcam, ab167341) overnight at 4 °C. Cells were then washed and incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen; Alexa Fluor 488: A-11034, Alexa Fluor 594: A-11007, Alexa Fluor 647: A-21244), and counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, H1399). Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope, and image quantification was performed using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were anesthetized with Zoletil 50 (50–60 mg/kg) and xylazine (2.3–2.7 mg/kg), followed by transcardial perfusion with PBS. Brains were fixed in 4% PFA for one week and cryoprotected in 30% sucrose prepared in 0.1 M phosphate buffer. Coronal brain sections (25 μm) were obtained using a cryostat. Sections were permeabilized and blocked in a solution containing 0.2% Triton X-100, 3 mM sodium azide, 0.1% saponin, 2% BSA, and 5% donkey serum in PBS. Primary antibodies against ATXN3(GeneTex, GTX101343 and Millipore, MAB5360) or Rad23b(Proteintech, 12121-1-AP) was applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The sections were washed, incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen; Alexa Fluor 488: A-11034, Alexa Fluor 594: A-11007, Alexa Fluor 647: A-21235) and stained with Hoechst 33342 (Invitrogen, H1399) for 1 h at room temperature. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope, and signal intensity was quantified using ImageJ software for statistical analysis.

Propidium iodide (PI) staining

Transfected N2a cells were incubated in medium containing 1 μg/mL PI and 1 μg/mL Hoechst 33342 for 15 min at room temperature. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope, and PI-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software for statistical analysis.

Generation of SCA3 transgenic mice

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at National Cheng Kung University(NCKU), Taiwan. SCA3 transgenic mice were generated by perivitelline space injection of lentiviral constructs encoding F-mATXN3 under the control of an ubiquitin promoter. Transgenic mice were genotyped using forward (GAGGCGTCAGTTTCTTTGGTC) and reverse (CAGGGGGACCTATCAGGACAGA) primer set with the approximate 1,449 bp amplicon. These mice were maintained at 21 ± 1 °C with a 12-h light-dark cycle, and fed with food and water ad libitum. The laboratory animal center at NCKU has been fully accredited and certified by Association for Assessment and Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care International (AAALAC International).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 6; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Comparisons between two independent groups were conducted using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons among three or more groups were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Significance levels are indicated as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001.

Results

Proteomic analysis identifies Rad23b

Our previous studies revealed one specific microRNA, miR-196a, suppresses the mATXN3 aggregates in SCA3 cell models (Supplementary Fig. 1), but miR-196a does not target on mATXN3 gene directly, suggesting that other proteins may be involved in miR-196a-regulatory mechanism. To identify the potential candidates contributing to this effect, we performed high throughput proteomic screening using cells transfected mATXN3 with miR-196a or non-relative control(miR-NC) for LC-MS/MS proteomic analysis. As a result, 3,501 proteins were identified, of which 17 were significantly upregulated and 61 were significantly downregulated (≥2-fold change) (Fig. 1A and B). We further intersected these proteins with predicted targets of miR-196a from five miRNA target prediction databases, including TargetScan, miRDB, miRSystem, PicTar and miRmap, and identified 5 potential candidates (Fig. 1C and D). Through KEGG Brite analyses, we found one candidate, Rad23b, is highly related to proteasome functions which may influence mATXN3 accumulation and degradation. Rad23b positively correlates with mATXN3 expression

Since Rad23b is identified as a candidate potentially involved in miR-196a-mediated suppression of aggregates and UPS activity, we first assess the endogenous expression of Rad23b in N2a neuroblastoma cell models of SCA3. Cells were transfected with full-length mATXN3 constructs carrying either mutant 88 CAG repeats (F-mATXN3) or 22 CAG repeats (F-ATXN3) as a control, and then subjected for Western blotting (Fig. 2A). Results show that F-mATXN3 leads to accumulation of mATXN3 aggregates in the stacking gel, whereas Rad23b is only slightly increased in the F-mATXN3 group (Fig. 2B). We further cotransfected F-mATXN3 with Rad23b (overexpression) or shRad23b (knockdown) into N2a cells for Western blotting (Fig. 2C and E), showing Rad23b significantly increases mATXN3 aggregates (Fig. 2D), whereas shRad23b significantly decreases them (Fig. 2F). We also delivered F-mATXN3 into Rad23b knockout cells (Rad23b−/−) for Western blotting (Fig. 2G), showing Rad23b−/− significantly decreases mATXN3 aggregates. Most importantly, as we overexpressed Rad23b into Rad23b−/− cells transfected with F-mATXN3, accumulation of mATXN3 aggregates is observed again (Supplementary Fig. 2), highlighting the critical role of Rad23b in modulating mATXN3 aggregation. Moreover, since the full length of mATXN3 could be proteolyzed to produce truncated C-terminal fragments [29], we also used truncated forms of mATXN3 with mutant 88 CAG repeats (T-mATXN3) or 22 CAG repeats (T-ATXN3) to conduct above experiments. In the Supplementary Fig. 3, T-mATXN3 also leads to accumulation of mATXN3 aggregates in the stacking gel (Supplementary Fig. 3A), and significantly increased Rad23b compared to T-ATXN3 (Supplementary Fig. 3B). In addition, overexpression of Rad23b significantly increases T-mATXN3 aggregates (Supplementary Fig. 3C and 3D), whereas shRad23b (Supplementary 3E and 3D) or Rad23b−/− (Supplementary 3G and 3H) significantly decreases T-mATXN3 aggregates. However, overexpression of Rad23b in T-ATXN3 cells with 22 CAG repeats did not affect ATXN3 expression (Supplementary Fig. 4). To examine this interaction, reporter constructs were used, including F-mATXN3 fused with green fluorescent protein(GFP) gene and Rad23b fused with Discosoma red fluorescent protein(DsRed) gene (Fig. 4A). These two constructs were transfected into N2a cells, and colocalization signals are visible in fluorescence images (Fig. 4B, top panel). Results show that the Flag antibody pulldowns Rad23b (Fig. 4D), and the reciprocal pulldown using the Rad23b antibody also detects mATXN3 (Fig. 4E). Rad23b affects mATXN3 accumulation through the UPS pathway

Rad23b is an ubiquitin binding protein [31], and ubiquitin is reported to involve in two major protein degradation pathways, UPS and autophagy [32]. Since Rad23b interacts with mATXN3 and increases mATXN3 aggregates, we further determine which protein degradation pathway dominantly mediates in Rad23b-induced mATXN3 aggregates. We transfected F-mATXN3 into Rad23b−/− cells, and then treated cells with MG132, a proteasome inhibitor, or chloroquine (CQ), an autophagy inhibitor, to observe mATXN3 aggregates using Western blotting. As shown in the Supplementary Fig. 5 and Fig. 5A, the MG132 treatment restores mATXN3 aggregates in the Rad23b−/− cells (Fig. 5B), whereas CQ does not affect mATXN3 aggregates. In addition, we also manipulated Rad23b expression in SCA3 cells, and examined the expression profiling of endogenous ubiquitin via Western blotting. Since Rad23b specifically promotes the ubiquitination of mATXN3, we next examine whether Rad23b facilitates the delivery of mATXN3 to the proteasome. We performed immunofluorescence staining to assess the localization of Rad23b, mATXN3 and proteasome 20S subunit beta 5 (PSMB5; a marker for proteasome localization) after overexpression of Rad23b in SCA3 cells (Fig. 6A and B). Especially, as Rad23b is overexpressed in SCA3 cells, colocalization among Rad23b, PSMB5 and mATXN3 is observed (Fig. 6C). Rad23b disrupts proteasome catalytic functions

Despite that Rad23b enhances the mATXN3 delivery to the proteasome (Fig. 6), Rad23b overexpression still leads to accumulation of mATXN3 aggregates (Fig. 2), indicating Rad23b may impair proteasome functions. To examine this possibility, we performed an UbG76V-GFP reporter assay using mutant ubiquitin fused with GFP to indicate UPS activity and status of protein degradation [34]. We cotransfected F-mATXN3, Rad23b and UbG76V-GFP reporter into N2a cells, and then determined UbG76V-GFP expression levels, showing Rad23b overexpression increases the UbG76V-GFP fluorescent signals (Fig. 7A and B). Consistently, Western blotting also shows Rad23b overexpression increases GFP expression in SCA3 cells (Fig. 7C and D), indicating accumulation of UbG76V-GFP due to impairment of UPS. We further conducted the same reporter assay in SCA3-Rad23b−/− cells, showing knockout of Rad23b not only decreases UbG76V-GFP fluorescent signals (Fig. 7E and F), but also lowers GFP level upon Western blotting (Fig. 7G and H). Importantly, reintroduction of Rad23b into SCA3-Rad23b−/− cells restored UbG76V-GFP fluorescence (Supplementary Fig. 6A and 6B) and GFP accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 6C and 6D), highly suggesting the critical role of Rad23b involved in this UPS activity in SCA3 cells. Discussion

SCA3 displays neuropathological aggregates of mATXN3 in neurons, disrupts neuronal functions, and further leads to neuronal death. As a result, clearance of mATXN3 is one of directions for potential therapy in SCA3. In this study, Rad23b is identified through proteomic high-throughput screening. Rad23b overexpression uniquely promotes mATXN3 ubiquitination, facilitates its delivery to the proteasome, but paradoxically disrupts proteasome catalytic activities, leading to aggregate accumulation and neurotoxicity in SCA3 models. Unlike prior works focusing on Rad23 stabilizing ATXN3 or sequestration into aggregates [19,23], this reveals Rad23b’s detrimental gain-of-function in UPS impairment specific to mATXN3 proteostasis, positioning it as a direct therapeutic target via knockdown. Due to that Rad23b has also been found in pathological aggregates of several neurodegenerative diseases [18], this study not only shows the critical role of Rad23b in aggregates of SCA3, but also implies the potential regulation on aggregates in different diseases.

In SCA3 cell models, we observed that Rad23b expression was only modestly elevated in the F-mATXN3 cell model (Fig. 2B), but significantly upregulated in the T-mATXN3 model (Supplementary Fig. 2A). These findings suggest that Rad23b expression may be influenced by the pathological processes associated with mutant ATXN3 [29]. Based on this observation, Rad23b may serve as a potential biomarker for monitoring the progression of SCA3. However, further longitudinal studies are warranted to evaluate Rad23b expression dynamics in SCA3 patients. Moreover, as Rad23b has been detected in pathological inclusions of several neurodegenerative diseases, such as fragile X-associated tremor/ataxia syndrome, HD, SCA7, and Parkinson’s disease [18], it would be of interest to characterize Rad23b expression throughout the course of these disorders. Such studies could enhance our understanding of the association between Rad23b expression patterns and disease progression in a broader neurodegenerative context.

Neurodegeneration is a neuropathological characteristic in SCA3, finally leading to neuronal cell death in brains [29]. Based on our results in Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Rad23b positively corelates with the aggregate formation and cell death. Although cleaved-caspase3 is also positively corelates with Rad23b expression in SCA3 cells, Rad23b does not significant alter PARP expression (Fig. 3D and H). Due to that PARP is the downstream target of caspase 3 and PI staining does be positively corelated with Rad23b expression, it suggests non-canonical apoptosis pathway or necrosis/necroptosis may be occurred [38,39]. To clarify the observed discrepancy, a time-course experiment to determine the PARP expression profiling could be conducted. Additionally, incorporating Annexin V staining alongside PI could help distinguish between early apoptosis, late apoptosis and necrosis. These steps will help define whether the observed cell death is due to canonical apoptosis, secondary necrosis, or an atypical, caspase 3-dependent pathway.

Interestingly, we found Rad23b forms puncta-like signals with mATXN3 in vitro (Fig. 4B) and in vivo (Fig. 8E and F), suggesting that Rad23b may be sequestrated inside aggregates. This similar phenomenon has been reported in other neuronal diseases [40,41], indicating Rad23b may participate in aberrant protein quality control and degradation processes in these aggregates. Most importantly, the Rad23b paradoxically contributes to both neuroprotection and neurotoxicity depending on the disease context. For example, Rad23b has shown to attenuate poly(GA) aggregates and toxicity in neuronal cultures [42], but downregulated Rad23b also exerts neuroprotective effects in mammalian neurons with SOD1 and TDP-43 mutations [43]. As a result, the redistribution or sequestration of Rad23b in association with other proteins may be closely linked to the pathogenesis of different aggregate diseases. Although we found Rad23b deteriorates the mATXN3 aggregates in this study, it would be important to further demonstrate the role of Rad23b in other disease aggregates, which may benefit the understanding of these aggregate diseases.

Rad23b includes both a UBL domain that binds the proteasome and a UBA domain that binds ubiquitinated substrates [44]. While this enables Rad23b to act as a proteasome shuttle factor, we do observe that overexpression of Rad23b facilitates the transport of mATXN3 to the proteasome (Fig. 6). However, mATXN3 is not effectively degraded, and instead accumulated in cells (Fig. 4B). This paradox may be explained by competitive binding of excessive Rad23b-substrate complexes or by interfering with proper substrate processing [19,45]. For example, previous studies demonstrate that Rad23a disrupts the proteasome degradation of polyubiquitinated substrates by competing with the proteasome’s polyubiquitin receptors in Lys48-linked ubiquitin chains, which are crucial for substrate recognition and degradation [46,47]. These findings suggest that excessive Rad23b may sequester polyubiquitinated substrates, potentially limiting their accessibility to the proteasome and thereby inhibiting proteasome-catalyzed proteolysis. As a result, the binding domains and binding efficiency with proper substrate processing should be further demonstrated.

In this study, we have shown Rad23b knockdown or knockout significantly reduces mutant mATXN3 aggregates, restores proteasome activity, and mitigates neuronal cell death in different models, suggesting Rad23b as a promising therapeutic target for SCA3. Potential conceptional approaches include RNA interference (e.g., shRNA), CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout, or miRNA-based strategies, such as miR-196a, which downregulates Rad23b to enhance mATXN3 clearance and neuroprotection [48,49]. Specifically, blocking Rad23b’s ubiquitin-associated domain via antibody therapy to prevent mATXN3 binding and shuttling to the proteasome, while sparing its ubiquitin-like domain docking, may also potentially restore UPS catalytic activity without full knockout risks [50]. Small-molecule inhibitors targeting Rad23b-proteasome interactions may offer another promising avenue, enabling brain-penetrant delivery via optimized chemical scaffolds to enhance mATXN3 degradation and mitigate neurotoxicity in vivo [51,52]. These approaches, combined with miR-196a-like regulators, merit high-throughput screening and structure-based design for the development of potential SCA3 therapies. However, further validation and advanced experimental evidence are still required to substantiate these insights.

One limitation of this study is that the effects of Rad23b modulation were primarily examined in cell models without chronic assessment at the functional or behavioral level in transgenic mice in vivo. This is crucial for evaluating the therapeutic potential of Rad23b as a drug target in SCA3. Recently, we have generated Rad23b and SCA3 transgenic mice, established double transgenic lines and are currently performed longitudinally pathological and behavioral analyses, showing Rad23b modestly exacerbates clasping behavior in SCA3 transgenic mice (Supplementary Fig. 7). However, due to the limited number of mice, more variations in vivo and more comprehensive examinations needed, future investigations, including molecular, neuropathological and behavioral analyses, could be examined. Additionally, deeper mechanistic studies on Rad23b silencing, particularly its impact on UPS recovery and mATXN3 clearance dynamics, would strengthen its translational relevance. An intriguing avenue for exploration is the potential link between Rad23b-proteasome foci and accelerated cellular senescence in SCA3 pathogenesis, which could reveal novel intersections with aging-related neurodegeneration.

Conclusions

Taken together, we identify Rad23b as a critical regulator of mATXN3 aggregates and toxicity in SCA3 models, and demonstrate that Rad23b directly interacts with mATXN3, enhances its ubiquitination and facilitates its transport to the proteasome. However, Rad23b overexpression paradoxically impairs proteasome catalytic activities, leading to mATXN3 aggregate accumulation and increased neuronal cell death, while Rad23b knockout reduces both aggregation and cell toxicity. In addition, Rad23b colocalizes with mATXN3 aggregates in the cerebellum of SCA3 transgenic mice in vivo, confirming its role in disease pathology. These findings reveal Rad23b as a key modulator of mATXN3 proteostasis and a potential therapeutic target for SCA3.
