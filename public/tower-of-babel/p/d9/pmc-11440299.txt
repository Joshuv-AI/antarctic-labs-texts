Ultrasound effect on a biorefinery lignin-cellulose mixture
Authors: Kait Kaarel Puss, Peeter Paaver, Mart Loog, Siim Salmar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractForest biorefineries provide multiple new avenues for applied research. The main concept lies in the malleability of the processes and their stepwise organization. The core element of the biorefinery concept addressed in the present study is the pretreatment step; here, wood biomass is converted into free hemicellulosic sugars, lignin and cellulose. In traditional approaches, the pretreatment step is a starting point for isolating and separating lignin or cellulose through different processes. In this study, instead of performing any separation, a lignin-cellulose mixture was used as its own material, and the effects of ultrasound treatment with a probe system at 20 kHz, with various amplitude, sonication time and dry matter content were investigated with the aim of assessing the formation of a nanocellulose structure with a high lignin content (>30 %) and investigating the stability of the lignin-cellulose mixture under aqueous conditions. We demonstrated the importance of dry matter content for the specific particle size and water retention values for this mixture. US treatment of lignin-cellulose mixtures <4 % dry matter formed a gel-like material, with low particle size (90 % below 30 μm and smallest at nanoscale). Low dry matter loading led to better US transfer and higher conversion of cellulose to <100 nm nanoparticles. Our study can serve as a baseline for future developments in the field of stable emulsions, filtering materials or inputs for material synthesis.Keywords: Forest biorefinery, Lignin-cellulose mixture, Power ultrasound, Nanocellulose

Introduction

Lignocellulosic biomass has become a highly researched field with many novel prospects [1]. Lignocellulosic biomass is composed of three main biopolymers: hemicellulose (25–35 %), cellulose (35–45 %) and lignin (20–30 %) [1], [2], [3], [4]. These biopolymers and their derivatives have various targeted applications. In most cases, only isolated biopolymers are used in the production of renewable chemicals, building materials or paper [5], [6], [7], [8]. Several separation technologies can be employed to access lignocellulosic biopolymers or sugars from polysaccharides [9], [10], [11], [12]. Depending on the extraction method, lignin and cellulose have different properties and applications.

In most applications, only isolated and purified cellulose has been used [1], [13], [14], [15]. The final quality of the end product is dependent on the purity of the starting polymer, such as for cellulose acetate, carboxymethylcellulose and nitrocellulose [16], [17], [18], [19]. In industry, high-purity cotton linter cellulose is often used to obtain cellulose derivatives; however, due to water consumption and the use of pesticides during cultivation, this process is not ecofriendly. For wood-derived pulp, acid sulfite or Kraft pulp processes are used, where hash bleaching is applied during postprocessing. Both treatments require toxic chemicals as well as extensive amounts of energy [13], [19], [20].

More environmentally friendly and sustainable biorefinery concepts combine several innovative pretreatments, such as steam explosion and extrusion combined with thermochemical processing [1], [2], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26], [27], [28]. These novel biorefineries are expected to become significant suppliers of cellulosic materials, wood sugars and native lignin in the near future [1]. Most of these approaches involve similar processes, and the initial decomposition of wood starts by converting hemicellulose into water soluble monosaccharides and an insoluble mixture of lignin and cellulose (LCM, lignin-cellulose mixture) [1], [16], [29]. The downstream processing of LCM can occur through two main pathways: 1) cellulose is enzymatically hydrolyzed into glucose, and the insoluble so-called hydrolysis lignin (HL) is separated [30]; 2) lignin is solubilized (e.g., with aqueous NaOH solution to obtain soluble lignin), and through bleaching, microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) can be separated [31].

However, less attention has been given to the direct modification and application possibilities of LCM, where both natural polymers are complementary [1]. Lignin and cellulose are naturally present in wood and have a single structure, and their specific properties have been exploited; however, in LCM, one binding component in the form of hemicellulose has been removed. Recently, this has been shown in some applications, such as wood adhesives, carbon and nanofibers and food packaging [1], [6], [32], [33], [34]. Lignin-containing nanocellulose biocomposite films have better mechanical, barrier, antimicrobial and thermal properties, which are critical for food packaging [32], [34]. Other options for LCM include sunscreen products, where lignin dispersion has UV protection and antioxidant properties [35], [36] and cellulose acts as a stabilizer for oil–water emulsions [4]. Thus, both materials fulfill their functions in one application. Biorefinery processes are anticipated to allow greater flexibility in the production of raw materials for tailoring to specific applications than traditional pulping processes [1].

Ultrasound (US) has been proven to be a clean and versatile process intensification method for different industrial-scale environmental remediation, synthesis, extraction and food industry applications [37]. It could have various applications in the sustainable biorefinery field, such as in the pretreatment of biomass, the intensification of cellulase activity, increasing lignin solubility, and the creation of nanomaterials from lignin or cellulose, thereby reducing the use of harmful chemicals [12], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43].

US is an acoustic wave that propagates in liquid media and causes multiple physico-chemical phenomena, such as heating, acoustic cavitation, acoustic streaming and radical formation [39]. Cavitation creates local high pressure and shear forces, as well as extreme heat. Lower frequency US (so-called power US, frequency ranges from 20 to 100 kHz) is biased more toward the physical effects of cavitation, as well as the high pressures and temperature variations. When energy is transferred to biopolymers in this way, it can cause them to break down into smaller units. Sonication has been described for the disruption of H-bonds as well as polysaccharide bonds; thus, US irradiation can reduce the diameter of fibers or even the scission of long cellulose fibers [44], [45]. This is also one of the main reasons for the practical use of US in nanomaterial preparation from pure crystalline cellulose. The small fibers are inherently present but are in crystalline form. US leads to the separation of cellulose fibers from crystals and thus creates nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC) or nanocellulose [46]. This nanoscale cellulose can be used for the creation of films, food packaging and stable emulsions for different applications [4], [6], [16], [43], [47]. US treatment has also been efficiently applied for lignin solubilization and depolymerization as well as nanoparticle formation [12], [42], [48], [49]. As shown in these examples, high-intensity US could have an effect on both components of biorefinery-derived LCM, but the extent is unknown (Fig. 1) [12], [38], [39], [40], [41], [42], [43].

Illustration of the biorefinery concept. Wood, a lignocellulosic biomass, is locally sourced, and through the biorefinery process, it is separated into C5 (xylose rich), C6 (glucose rich) sugars and lignin. This occurs through a multiple-stage process, with an initial pretreatment to depolymerize hemicellulose into free sugars and deconstruct the wood fibers. Through solid–liquid separation, a lignin-cellulose mixture (LCM), which is an intermediate material in the biorefinery process, is obtained. Traditionally, the US effect on A) cellulose is well known and leads to a reduction in cellulosic fiber size and the B) intermediate LCM material with a high lignin content is unknown.

Comprehensive studies on the pretreatment of lignocellulosic biomass or on the modification of pure cellulose with US have been carried out [4], [34], [42], [47], [50]; however, limited attention has been given to the effect of sonication on cellulose with a high lignin content (>30 %) to produce materials with novel properties. Therefore, the main purpose of this study was to examine the effect of US on LCM from a birch biorefinery. Here, we investigated whether and how sonication affects the LCM at high lignin content without any additional chemicals. This includes the effect on cellulose microcrystals, as well as the formation of nanocellulose, stable emulsions or suspensions that could be used in cosmetics. This effect was tested by varying the conditions of US treatment (sonication time, amplitude) as well as the LCM content in the solution and measured via particle size, water retention, solution stability and morphology (SEM, XRD).These data show the conditions for treating high lignin content cellulose and how the material is structurally altered by sonication.

Materials and methods

Materials

The LCM was obtained from Fibenol OÜ and contained 38.3 ± 0.8 % lignin and 56.7 ± 0.7 % cellulose; the other additives included 2.27 % ± 0.1 % ash and free saccharides.

US treatment of LCM

Heterogenous LCM–water solutions were made with varying dry matter content, from 0.5 % to 8 % in total 70 g. US experiments were performed at 10–50 % power by Bandelin Sonopuls HD 4200 (20 kHz, the maximum power of the ultrasonic transmitter was 200 W, the energy output was 34 W in water at 30 % amplitude) with the TT213 titanium tip, immersed by 1.5 cm into the solution, as provided by the manufacturer, with temperature control at 25 ± 1 °C, for 5–60 min (Conditions shown in Table 1. and Table 2.).

LCM characterization

Dry-matter determination of LCM

LCM is a high-water content paste; thus, accurate determination of dry matter is needed. The dry matter content of LCM was determined gravimetrically by drying at 85 °C until a constant weight was achieved.

Klason method

The lignin content was determined by the Klason method [51]. Briefly, 0.3 g of dry LCM was weighed into a high-pressure glass vessel, 3 ml of 72 % H2SO4 was added. This was mixed for 1 h at 25 °C, afterwards it was diluted with 84 g of H2O. The reaction was carried out at 120 °C for 1 h. Then, it was cooled to room temperature and filtered. The initial filtrate was analyzed with HPLC (Shimadzu MS-3030 HPLC equipped with Shimadzu RI detector and Biorad Aminex HPX-87P column with H+/CO3– deashing guard column. Mobile phase was MilliQ water with isocratic flow of 0.6 ml/min) to determine saccharide content, and the acid soluble lignin (ASL) was measured via UV–Vis.

The insoluble material was dried to obtain the acid insoluble lignin (AIL). From ASL and AIL the lignin content was determined.

FT-IR analysis of LCM

The FT-IR spectra were measured with a Perkin-Elmer Spectrum BXII FT-IR spectrometer. The samples were analyzed with the KBr disk method, where the sample concentration was 1 %. The KBr and the sample were finely ground in an agate mortar, and the disks were pressed under 10 T pressure. FT-IR spectra were recorded with 16 scans in the range of 4000–600 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1.

Particle size distribution analysis (PSD)

The undried US and non-US LCM materials were diluted into water and mixed thorughly. These samples were then transferred to measure particle size distribution by MICROTRAC S3500 Bluewave particle size analyzer using dynamic light scattering. Water was used as the solvent for the analysis.

Water retention value (WRV)

The method was adapted from previously reported study [52]. To measure the difference in water retention values, 30 g of sample was used. After centrifugation at 5000 rpm (3435g) for 10 min with an Eppendorf Centrifuge 5804 and a FA-45–6-30 rotor, all free water was removed. This process was repeated 3 times. The resulting matter was weighed before and after drying to determine the amount of water removed. The samples were dried under vacuum at 0.3 mbar until a constant weight was reached. The WRV is calculated by water g/dry LCM g.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

SEM was used to visualize the samples after drying. The samples were coated with Pt by a Leica EM SCO 500. Zeiss EVO15MA SEM, Oxford X-Max was used with a backscatter detector, and images were captured by SmartSEM version 6.05.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis

Dry LCM was measured by a Bruker D8 Advance instrument with CuKα radiation, 2.5° Soller pulses and a LynxEye detector. The range measured was 3–73° with a 2Θ step of 0.017°, with summative time of 178 sec per datapoint. The data were analyzed with a Bruker DIFFRAC.EVA and ICDD database PDF-5+ (2024). Quantitative assessment was performed with the full profile analysis software Topas 6 (Bruker, Germany).

Results and discussion

Ultrasound transfer into LCMs

The material in this study is produced in a birch biorefinery by pretreatment of wood biomass into a liquid slurry. The water-soluble material is removed through solid–liquid separation, resulting in an LCM material of 24–27 % dry matter. LCM consists of lignin and cellulose, and hemicellulose is separated. However, this mass is very thick and requires the addition of water to make the LCM transferable and accessible for further processing in a biorefinery. Similarly, increasing the fluidity of the LCM is necessary for the successful application of sonication. In addition, sonication can increase the viscosity, further complicating the treatment [46].

LCM solutions at a dry matter content of 0.5–4 wt% remained flowable after US treatment of 60 min (30 % amplitude). Above 4 wt% LCM, a stable gel-like mass was formed during irradiation, and US was no longer transferred to the LCM (Fig. 2). At 5 wt%, this gel formed in 16 min, and at 8 wt%, it formed in 10 min (the gel-like behavior is shown in Fig. 1S). The lower wt% US-treated LCM formed uniform dispersions in water (Fig. 2S) and were stable over two months.

Illustration of the LCM sonication at LCM wt% below (left) and above (right) 4 %. Below 4 %, the US waves propagate in the liquid phase even after a long treatment time (60 min); however, on the right (above 4 wt% LCM), the US energy transfer in the liquid stops at approximately 16 min (at 5 wt% LCM), and a gel-like material is formed around the US probe. Thus, the probe lost contact with the LCM dispersion.

The formation of a strong gel-like material during sonication resulted in the loss of probe-solution contact, as indicated by sonication whistle disturbance, and the US was no longer transferred to the LCM (Fig. 2). Thus, the increase in viscosity during US treatment of LCM caused an unstable transfer of ultrasonic energy into the solution. This disruption was not resloved via intensifying the mixing speed of the magnetic stirrrer. However, this problem could be solved with a flow-through setup, leading to a scale-up or an increase in the wt% of LCM, which was not tested in this study. The results from the experiments led to the use of lower wt% LCM (0.5–4 wt%) to evaluate the effect that US had on the properties of LCM.

FT-IR analysis

FT-IR analysis was used to measure the spectra of the dried initial LCM and sonicated LCM (30 and 60 min) to evaluate the possible changes in chemical functionality. As shown in the spectra, no major chemical modification occurred (Fig. 3). However, a relative decrease in the intensity of some peaks could be observed in the sonicated samples. Based on cotton absorbances at 3374, 2902, 1430, 1373, and 897 cm−1 are associated with cellulose I [53]. The OH stretching is a broad band from 3000-3700 cm−1. The 2900 cm−1 band shows the C-H stretching of different methyl groups. For 1430 cm−1 the signal is attributed to CH2 scissoring, the signal at 1373 cm−1 is from C-H bending and C-H deformation vibration and 897 cm−1 is attributed to C-O-C linkage. However, these signals are also present in both lignin and cellulose. This potentially indicated some US-induced changes in LCM.

FT-IR spectra of the original and sonicated LCM at different times, measured with the KBr method.

Nevertheless, cellulose and lignin signals overlap in the main fingerprint regions, and any minor or major changes could be overshadowed or unnoticed. Changes in the particle size of the LCM

The impact of US for LCM is best illustrated via particle PSD, which uses a mathematical model for the calculation of size from light scattering. Fig. 4 shows the starting LCM distribution in comparison to that at 5, 15 and 60 min of sonication. Initially, the particle sizes of LCM range from 3 μm to 600 μm and decrease with US treatment (The data for cumulative distributions for all tested conditions are shown in Table 1S and Table 2S).

Particle size distribution of 1 wt% LCM at different sonication times at 30 % power. The y-axis shows the relative signal per particle size, shown on the x-axis on a logarithmic scale in μm.

At 5 min, the large particles are converted to smaller sizes, as indicated by the relative increase in the 6- and 10-μm specific signals in the sample. These are also present in the starting LCM, but their relative distribution is significantly altered. After 15 min, US particles less than 1 μm in size appear, and the overall <1 µm signal increases up to 60 min, with the smallest particles occurring at 0.02 μm.

These smallest particles are visibly converted from the initially increased peaks at 6 and 10 μm. However, even after 60 min of sonication, large particles remain in the sample. Most likely, the larger particles are from lignin since we have observed in our experiments that water–HL treatment with US shows no significant change in the PSD or solubility, there 10 % and 15 % of HL was US treated in water at 30 % amplitude at 1 h (20 kHz) and no gelling was observed [12] (Fig. 3S).

The overall viscosity increase is most likely due to the smaller cellulose particles, which increase the surface area available for the water-cellulose hydrogen bonding interfaces. This leads to gel-like material formation at higher LCM loadings (<4 wt%) and is less connected to the overall cellulose concentration. From these data, the high lignin content in LCM still enables the formation of nanoscale cellulose under US treatment [52], [56], [57].

Fig. 5 shows the overall distribution of the particle changes. For the untreated LCM, most of the particles are greater than 10 μm in size (70 %), and the largest particles are 100–500 μm in size. The percentage of specific particles shifted toward smaller sizes in correlation with the US treatment time. After 5 min of US treatment, 50 % of the particles shifted in size from 18.5 to 9.25 μm, and approximately 10 % of the particles were less than 1 μm in size. At 60 min of US treatment, 50 % of the particles were under 1 μm in size.

Cumulative distribution of particle sizes for 1 wt% LCM at different sonication times with 40 % US power. The y-axis shows the cumulative % of specific particle sizes in the sample, shown on the x-axis on a logarithmic scale.

Overall, the shift in the particle sizes is gradual. The larger particles decrease in size toward smaller particles (below 20 μm), and additional US treatment converts these particles into particles that are mainly 0.3–2 μm in size. The longer US treatment times additionally increase the number of particles in this range, and after 20 min, the fraction below 0.2 μm starts to increase.

The gradual changes are most likely due to the initial disruption of the cellulose crystals into more fibrillar or smaller crystal structures [43]. Longer US irradiation times could lead to the cleavage of longer cellulosic fibers and to nanoscale cellulose formation. Thus, lignin itself is not highly inhibitory in nature, but further research into the specific effects of lignin content in the range of 10–30 % is needed.

For different LCM loadings, the main objective was to determine the energy-to-material relationship. Fig. 6 shows that increased amounts of material lead to a decrease in the accessibility of US energy as well as an increase in the energy requirement. To elucidate the optimal conditions for LCM treatment with US, water retention tests were carried out as described in Section 3.3. The main aim of this study was to verify the importance of US energy accessibility for different dry matter loadings.

Cumulative distribution of particle sizes for different wt% LCMs after 20 min of sonication at 50 % US power. The y-axis shows the cumulative % of specific particle sizes in the sample, and the x-axis is shown on a logarithmic scale.

Water retention value of the US-treated LCM

The water retention value (WRV) is used to measure the ability of a cellulosic sample to retain water, and this value has been widely used in the pulp and paper industry. This value provides indirect information on the morphology of the material, where the value increases with increasing fibrillation of fibers or a larger surface area of cellulose [52], [56]. Herein, we adapted the method to our conditions.

Overall, the baseline for LCM WRV after centrifugation was 2.5 ± 0.2; this was independent of the specific loading of LCM and was measured in the range of 1 %-8% dry matter. The value was calculated based on the ratio of water/dry matter (g/g). This result shows the innate water adsorption present for the LCM material used here. Thus, this value was used as a baseline for all measurements.

However, US increases the WRV of the LCM. This is in correlation with the particle size changes since the total area and water absorbance are increased [57]. This change can only be caused by the effect of US on cellulose; thus, nanoscale formation or a decrease in the largest particles are potential explanations. US treatment increases the surface area and allows water to access the cellulosic structures present in LCM; hence, an increase in the WRV is observed.

As shown in Fig. 7, for any dry matter loading after 20 min of US treatment, the WRV plateaus at 30 % US power. A higher input energy yields little further change for the LCM WRV. This value is universal for 0.5 % to 4 % LCM dry matter loading. The main difference between the treatments is the final WRV. The increase in water absorption increases from 5.5 to 10.4 with 4 % and 0.5 % LCM, respectively (Numeric values in Table 3S). Interestingly, a higher LCM loading does not result in the same level of cellulose modification. Thus, the effect is not only connected to the US energy input but also to the dry matter input.

WRV at different US amplitudes after 20 min of treatment with various dry matter loadings of LCM ranging from 0.5–4 wt%. WRV was calculated as water g/dry LCM g.

This leads to the main conclusion regarding the applicability of US to dilute heterogeneous mixtures under these conditions. The limitation of US accessibility for cellulose may be due to the batch system; if that is the case, flowthrough systems could alleviate this issue. In terms of scale-up, this is a plausible solution.

These results show that even though lignin is highly present in the material, US still has a strong, measurable effect. Changes in LCM can have different effects on material production or downstream processing for lignin or cellulose isolation. This occurs because the higher LCM dry matter treatment results in a gel-like material; otherwise, the lignin itself would sediment. The cellulose network helps to stabilize this material, as shown previously [56]. Similar to oil–water, cellulose can function as a matrix to assist in stabilizing the lignin-water mixtures. One of the challenges for lignin application at large scales is its separation from liquids.

Morphology of the ultrasonicated LCM

SEM was measured for select samples, 0.5 % LCM non-US and with US (20 min at 50 % US amplitude), with largest WRV and PSD differences, to find out the possible morphological changes in materials. The overall crystallinity of the cellulose in LCM is unclear in the SEM images, and this is mainly due to the amorphous cellulose itself as well as lignin. An assessment of the natural appearance of LCM in wet paste is difficult, because the material has to be dried before imaging and cellulose hornification is known to occur due to the hydrogen bonding of cellulose polymers, which creates clusters of particles [46]. In addition, in this case, lignin is also present and can interact with cellulose or itself through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic forces [58]. The main change observed is the increase in the overall surface area after sonication. This shows that our treatment method is viable even at high lignin contents; however, the effect of lignin needs further investigation.

Morphologically, LCM shows rectangular and needle-like structures in addition to long fibers. With US treatment, the amorphous regions tended to homogenize and distribute more evenly. These results agreed with the changes observed in the PSD graphs, as the larger particulates disintegrated into fibrous branching networks with large surface areas.

Importantly, the drying method greatly affected the LCM structure; here, freeze-drying was employed to reduce the thermoplastic and adhesive-like properties of lignin [32], [46]. Other drying methods have been observed to lead to a greater degree of aggregation and loss of porous structures.

Fig. 8 shows the overall size difference of the US-treated LCM material. Overall, the particle matrix is more porous and has a greater surface area. Both materials have amorphous structures with relatively few defined repeating structures. Cellulose itself is crystalline in nature, but high amounts of lignin can interact with the cellulose structures [58]. Based on the SEM images, the US treatment has a drastic effect on the LCM structural matrix.

SEM images of a) non-US- and b) US-treated (20 min at 50 % US amplitude) LCM materials at 2000× magnification, with 0.5 wt% LCM dry matter.

The XRD diffractograms were measured for selected samples to understand the sonication effects for LCM. The XRD measurements showed that the overall crystallinity remained constant at approximately 47 % (For 0.5 % LCM US treated material crystallinity was 47.3 %, 2 % LCM US treated the crystallinity was 47.3 % and for non-US sample it was 47 %); however, the crystal size of the cellulose decreased with USultrasound treatment. For LCM, the crystalline part was only cellulose and not lignin. This indicated that US disrupted the crystal structure of the LCM; interestingly, XRD differences between the different dry matter contents were not visible. According to other data, the sizes of the sonicated 0.5 % and 2 % LCMs should change; however, for both, the crystal size was approximately 3.2 nm (Fig. 9). Several reasons could account for this discrepancy; these include the following: the dried LCM having different structure in comparison to the wet material and the decreased lignin-cellulose interactions due to low dry matter content and improved disruption via ultrasonication [46].

Crystallinity size calculated based on the XRD measurements. Irradiation was applied at 50 % amplitude for 20 min with different dry matter loadings; for comparison, the LCM without US treatment is shown.

Conclusions

In this study, the sonication effects on novel biorefinery intermediate product of LCM were investigated for the first time. It was found that LCM was suitable for the formation of nanocellulose induced by US and this at a short sonication time (5 min).

The high content of lignin in the mixture with crystalline cellulose showed little inhibition of the nanoscale formation of cellulose induced by US (below 0.1 μm particle size after US treatment); however, the impact may be difficult to characterize in this study. Further research is needed to elucidate the behavior of this type of cellulose with less lignin present. This will be addressed in future work since delignification of LCM can be based on lignin solubility.

Through the characterization of the WRV and PSD, the effect of sonication on cellulose in LCM was shown; under these conditions, US had a minimal effect on HL. The dry matter content and US amplitude played important roles in cellulose modification. WRV could be altered through more intense sonication or through a decrease in dry matter content. In both cases, the LCM loading was the main contributor to the effects, most likely through viscosity changes, at 0.5 % LCM dry matter the WRV was 10.4 and at 4 % it was at 5.5, thus an almost two fold change. For PSD the 50 % particle size was over 10 μm at 4 % LCM and below 1 μm at 0.5 % LCM at 50 % US amplitude at 20 min. Based on the SEM results, lignin contributed to the overall amorphous structure of the LCM. Thus, lignin potentially encapsulated many cellulose structures, and thus, a greater overall US energy was needed to reduce the size of the crystalline cellulose. Even with a high lignin content, the formation of small cellulose structures was observed. This nanomaterial could be used in future applications where the combination of lignin and cellulose properties is desired. Under these conditions, lignin remained in a stable mixture under aqueous conditions and thus could lead to new novel applications. This would be in different cosmetic applications of oil–water stabilization and UV-protection, coming from cellulose and lignin respectively.
