Hidden diversity of coronaviruses in high-altitude plateau mammals
Authors: Lin Xu, Edward C. Holmes, Weifeng Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Pikas and marmots from plateaus host divergent α- and β-CoVs, including the candidate subgenera Pibecovirus and Mabecovirus.•The plateau CoVs show distinctive genomic features (relocated HE gene, variable ORFs), indicating lineage-specific evolution.•High-altitude plateau ecosystems are a key yet understudied frontier of CoV evolution, warranting systematic surveillance.

Research into the origins and diversity of coronaviruses (CoVs) has largely focused on tropical and subtropical regions that are characterized by high mammalian species richness (Hu et al., 2017; Li et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2021). However, high-altitude plateau ecosystems characterized by extreme climatic conditions and unique endemic fauna, such as the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau in China that spans the provinces of Qinghai, Sichuan, Gansu, and Yunnan, the Xizang (Tibet) Autonomous Region, and the Xinjiang Uygur Autonomous Region, represent an equally important but largely neglected setting for CoV evolution and an ideal natural environment to study host-virus evolutionary relationships (Mao et al., 2021). Mammals have been the initial focus of CoV surveillance among the plateau fauna, and the available data already point to a largely unexplored diversity of CoVs.

In particular, plateau mammals such as pikas (Ochotona spp.) and marmots (Marmota spp.) are abundant, widely distributed, and ecologically influential, shaping grassland ecosystems and forming interfaces with livestock, predators, and human populations (Smith and Foggin, 1999; Sarkar et al., 2008). Despite this ecological importance, until recently their viromes were poorly characterized. However, studies have now demonstrated that these keystone plateau mammals act as natural reservoirs for a diverse and highly divergent array of RNA viruses, including CoVs (Zhu et al., 2021a; Cui et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2025). The CoV diversity in other sympatric plateau mammals, such as carnivores and ungulates, remains virtually unexplored and represents an important target for future surveillance.

Previous studies identified an alphacoronavirus (plateau pika coronavirus, PPCoV) in plateau pikas (Ochotona curzoniae) from the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau in Qinghai Province (Zhu et al., 2021a), and later uncovered betacoronaviruses in plateau pikas and Gansu pikas (Ochotona cansus) from Sichuan Province that formed a sister lineage to the subgenus Embecovirus (e.g., murine hepatitis virus, human CoV HKU1, and human CoV OC43) (Cui et al., 2023).

We conducted a five-year (2019–2023) longitudinal surveillance around Qinghai Lake and documented sustained co-circulation of both alpha- and betacoronaviruses in pika populations, with an overall positivity rate of 14.5% (Xu et al., 2024). The pika alphacoronavirus was closely related to PPCoV (Zhu et al., 2021a), and both clustered within the subgenus Luchacovirus (e.g., Lucheng Rn rat coronavirus Lucheng-19) (Wang et al., 2015), indicating that a diversity of alphacoronaviruses circulate in plateau lagomorphs. In contrast, the pika betacoronaviruses (e.g., OCu-NQ06/Qinghai/2022) were closely related to pika strains from Sichuan Province (e.g., SC/C3-17.18/2021) which together showed substantial genetic and phylogenetic divergence from all previously described betacoronaviruses. Notably, these viruses also possess a distinctive genomic architecture, with the hemagglutinin-esterase (HE) gene relocated downstream of the spike (S) gene (Fig. 1A), a configuration that differs from classical embecoviruses and suggests lineage-specific evolution (Zeng et al., 2008). Moreover, the Qinghai strain encoded two predicted proteins (ORF2a and ORF2b), whereas the Sichuan strain contains only a single ORF2. Parallel investigations in long-tailed marmot (Marmota caudata) from Xinjiang identified another highly divergent betacoronavirus lineage, designated Marmota caudata coronavirus (MCCoV) (Yi et al., 2025). Maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees were estimated based on the concatenated amino acid sequences of five conserved replicase domains (3CLpro, NiRAN, RdRp, ZBD, and HEL1) (Fig. 1B) and the spike protein (Fig. 1C). In the tree of the concatenated five domains, pika betacoronaviruses formed a sister lineage to the subgenus Embecovirus, while MCCoV occupied a more basal position outside this clade (Fig. 1B). Conversely, the spike protein phylogeny placed MCCoV as the sister lineage to Embecovirus, with pika betacoronaviruses in a basal position (Fig. 1C). Despite this topological discordance between the replicase and spike trees, both phylogenies clearly separated the plateau betacoronaviruses from all established subgenera. The genetic divergence of these sequences was further reflected by Pairwise Patristic Distances (PPD) calculated from the concatenated five-domain tree. Intra-subgenus PPDs largely fell below the International Committee on Taxonomy of Viruses (ICTV) demarcation threshold of 0.200–0.221 (mean: Embecovirus 0.200, Pibecovirus 0.003, Mabecovirus 0.141). In contrast, inter-subgenus PPDs were significantly greater (two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test): Pibecovirus vs. Embecovirus mean 0.377 (range 0.324–0.565, P = 2.04 × 10−7); Mabecovirus vs. Embecovirus mean 0.238 (range 0.147–0.542, P = 0.0029); and Pibecovirus vs. Mabecovirus mean 0.364 (range 0.345–0.383, P = 0.0075). Together, these phylogenetic and genetic distance analyses support the classification of these two highly divergent lineages as new subgenera within the genus Betacoronavirus, provisionally termed “Pibecovirus” (i.e., pika-derived betacoronavirus) and “Mabecovirus” (i.e., marmot-derived betacoronavirus) (Yi et al., 2025).

Functional analyses using surface plasmon resonance assays showed that the novel pika betacoronavirus did not bind to human or pika angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) or dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4) receptors in vitro, suggesting limited zoonotic potential (Xu et al., 2024). Nevertheless, the high prevalence of these viruses in wild populations, their broad tissue tropism (especially in intestinal and lung tissues), and their sustained circulation highlight long-term persistence and ongoing evolution.

The convergence of these findings from different high-altitude mammalian species reveals several clear patterns: (i) The Qinghai-Tibet Plateau and adjacent high-altitude ecosystems harbor remarkable CoV diversity, yet current sampling remains extremely limited; the identification of two candidate subgenera suggests a vast, unsampled viral reservoir, underscoring the importance of further surveillance (Hill et al., 2023; Xu et al., 2024; Yi et al., 2025). (ii) The distinct genomic features of these high-altitude CoVs – including the relocated HE gene and variable accessory ORFs – indicate lineage-specific evolution (Fig. 1A). (iii) As abundant and social burrowers, pikas and marmots create dense intra-species transmission networks, and their habitats overlap with livestock and other wildlife, forming complex interfaces that could facilitate viral exchange. (iv) Beyond mammals, some birds inhabiting the plateau are known to carry gammacoronaviruses and deltacoronaviruses (Zhu et al., 2021b; Zhou et al., 2026). Although they are not recognized as natural hosts of mammal-associated alpha- or betacoronaviruses, their capacity for long-distance movement positions them as potential ecological connectors that could facilitate viral dispersal across regions, warranting dedicated investigation.

Several hypotheses may explain these patterns, although they remain to be tested. The distinctive genomic architectures in plateau CoVs could reflect evolutionary trajectories shaped by the unique physiological and ecological pressures of high-altitude hosts – such as hypoxia, low temperatures, and intense ultraviolet radiation – but direct experimental evidence for such selective forces is currently lacking. The complex interfaces between pikas, marmots, livestock, predators, and humans offer plausible routes for cross-species transmission; however, the frequency and directionality of spillover events are unknown. Climate change, land-use shifts, and increasing human encroachment may further alter these interfaces, potentially influencing viral transmission dynamics in ways that warrant proactive investigation. To address these questions, we recommend enhanced longitudinal surveillance of plateau-dwelling lagomorphs and rodents, targeted functional studies to assess host range and receptor usage, and integrated ecological research at wildlife-livestock-human interfaces. Expanding monitoring to sympatric carnivores, ungulates, and birds will be essential to determine whether the hidden diversity suggested by current data extends across a broader plateau wildlife community.

In sum, pikas and marmots represent previously underappreciated reservoirs of CoV diversity. The discovery of highly divergent CoV lineages in these high-altitude mammalian hosts emphasizes the importance of incorporating plateau ecosystems into global wildlife-virus surveillance frameworks for pandemic preparedness.
