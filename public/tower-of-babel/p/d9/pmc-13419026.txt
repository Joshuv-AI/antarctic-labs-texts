Adolescent obesity and risk of serious chronic morbidity and mortality in young adulthood: a systematic review of cohorts from national registries
Authors: Naomi Fliss Isakov, Yulia Balmakov, Laura Sol Grinshpan, Eyal Tsur, Nitzan Issler, Moran Blaychfeld Magnazi, Zivan Aviad Beer, Orit Pinhas-Hamiel, Gilad Twig, Avi Shina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rising prevalence of adolescent obesity necessitates a precise understanding of its corresponding risks for severe chronic morbidity and premature mortality during young adulthood and midlife.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review of nationwide longitudinal cohort studies, using validated national registries, aimed at assessing the short- and long-term risk of morbidity and mortality associated with adolescent overweight and obesity.ResultsWe included 38 studies in this systematic review. Adolescent overweight and obesity were associated with severe morbidity and disease related mortality, from young adulthood. Within < 4 years of follow-up and before the age of 25 years, severe adolescent obesity was associated with increased risk of serious morbidity (hazard ratio (HR) = 5.1 among men and HR = 7.3 among women), type 2 diabetes (HR = 27.0 among men, and HR = 45.3 among women), and obesity was associated with type 1 diabetes (HR = 2.0). Before the age of 30 years, adolescent severe obesity was associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD), obesity was associated with colorectal cancer (CRC) and with cardiovascular disease (CVD) mortality. Before the age of 40 years adolescent overweight and obesity were associated with ischemic stroke. Last, before the age of 50 years, adolescent severe obesity was associated with all-cause mortality, obesity was associated with diabetes mortality, and cancer.ConclusionsAdolescent obesity is strongly associated with an elevated hazard for severe disease morbidity and mortality beginning in young adulthood, with associations progressing with obesity severity and time. The findings of this study demonstrate the need for public health mitigation and preventive strategies, which may yield substantial short, and long-term health benefits in this population.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03221-0.Keywords: Adolescent obesity, Life course epidemiology, Nationwide cohort studies

Background

Adolescent overweight and obesity are major public health concerns with marked disparities by sex, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status [1, 2]. Adolescent obesity prevalence is rising worldwide [3], with profound implications for long-term health outcomes, including increased risks of adulthood type 2 diabetes (T2D) [4, 5], site-specific cancers [6], chronic kidney disease (CKD), cardiovascular disease (CVD), heart failure, and premature mortality [7, 8]. Several systematic reviews have been published in order to provide evidence for the health effects of overweight and obesity in adolescents. They have concluded that a relatively large and fairly consistent body of evidence now demonstrates that overweight and obesity in childhood and adolescence have adverse consequences on physical morbidity and premature mortality in adulthood [9–11], and young adulthood [12], but not early adulthood (age 20–25). The notion that this population is at extensive immediate health risks is not yet established. Recently, the Lancet Commission on adolescent health underscored that the age group of 10–24 years as one that is misperceived as having good health and is therefore overlooked when prioritizing primary and secondary prevention policies [13]. Though global death rates of adolescents are decreasing, morbidity and disability are increasing [14]. According to the recent call-to-action by the second Lancet Commission on adolescent health and wellbeing, little progress has been made in reducing adolescent obesity rates and non-communicable diseases in every region of the world. The global rise in obesity-related diseases in young people is attributed to the global food system, producing and advertising ultra-processed foods high in fat, salt, and sugar which change adolescent food culture worldwide, and shifts from rural to urban environments, which offer fewer opportunities for physical activity [15].

The early health consequences of adolescent obesity are of major importance to public health due to several reasons. First, under-recognition of the immediate health consequences of adolescent obesity may contribute to the rise in the early development of adverse health outcomes, years lived with disease and disability, and the progression of obesity into adulthood, raising adult obesity rates. Second, morbidity in this age group, especially if debilitating, has major social and economic implications. Adolescence is characterized by dynamic development in which the interaction with the social environment shapes the capabilities an individual takes forward into adult life. During adolescence, an individual acquires the physical, cognitive, emotional, social, and economic resources that are the foundation for later life health and wellbeing [13]. Poor health in young adulthood may have far-reaching societal consequences, because individuals in this life stage represent a key foundation for academic development, labor force participation, and broader economic expansion [16].

National cohort studies leveraging pre-recruitment medical assessments for military service have provided unique opportunities to examine the long-term health consequences of adolescent body mass index (BMI). These cohorts are large and population-based due to mandatory pre-conscription medical assessments and are linked to comprehensive national morbidity and mortality registries, across multiple countries including Israel, Sweden, Norway, and the Netherlands. Since these registries are based on mandatory assessments, they enable observing a large, representative, and generalizable population. Other than that, they enable a targeted observation to a specific age group, of otherwise healthy individuals, which may reduce the heterogeneity in the association between obesity and morbidity observed in adults.

Unlike previous systematic reviews on this topic, which have included cross-sectional and retrospective, and prospective cohort studies, this review synthesizes evidence on adolescent obesity risks from representative nationwide registry-based, cohorts. It aims to look at the most updated research on both short and long-term health outcomes of adolescent obesity. The primary objectives of this systematic review are: (1) to synthesize evidence from nationwide, registry-linked longitudinal cohort studies on the association between adolescent overweight and obesity and the risk of serious morbidity and premature mortality across multiple disease domains; and (2) to stratify this association by severity of overweight and obesity, and age of onset of these adverse outcomes.

We specifically focus on military pre-conscription cohorts because they offer a combination of methodological strengths unattainable in conventional study designs: population-wide mandatory enrollment (minimizing selection bias), standardized and measured anthropometric assessments, linkage to validated national morbidity and mortality registries, and long follow-up from a defined adolescent baseline. This framework enables assessment of BMI-associated risk in a defined age group of otherwise healthy young individuals, reducing the confounding heterogeneity inherent in other cohort studies.

Methods

The primary objectives of this systematic review are: (1) to synthesize evidence on the association between adolescent weight category and the risk of serious morbidity and premature mortality: CVD, DM (type 1 and type 2), kidney disease, liver disease, cancer, and any serious disease or all-cause mortality; and (2) to stratify this association by both the severity of overweight and obesity, and age of onset of these adverse outcomes.

Search strategy and study eligibility criteria

This study was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews – PROSPERO CRD42024629140 and conducted according to the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses) guidelines [17]. A comprehensive search of Embase, MEDLINE, and PubMed databases was conducted through May 2025. Study search strategy and eligibility criteria are depicted according to the Population Exposure Comparator Outcome (PECO) framework in Supplementary file 1. No restrictions were applied on year of publication, or publication quality. The review included retrospective cohort studies of adolescents assessed prior to military recruitment, with outcomes ascertained through linkage to national morbidity and mortality databases. This framework uniquely provides population-wide mandatory enrollment of the study population, therefore minimizing selection bias. It also enables standardized, measured (not self-reported) anthropometric assessments at a precisely defined adolescent baseline; and linkage to validated national morbidity and mortality registries enabling long-term, comprehensive outcome ascertainment. This design reduces the confounding heterogeneity inherent in adult or volunteer-based cohorts and enables rigorous observation of an otherwise healthy, age-defined population.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Four reviewers (NFI, YB, ET, NI) independently screened titles and abstracts, assessed full texts for eligibility, and extracted data using a standardized form. Screening documentation was performed through the Covidence platform. Inter-rater agreement for title and abstract screening was assessed between pairs of reviewers using Cohen’s kappa statistics. Extracted data included cohort characteristics, BMI classification, outcome definitions, follow-up duration, statistical methods, and effect estimates (e.g., hazard ratios, and odds ratios) for morbidity and mortality across BMI categories.

Exposure was elevated weight categories, categorized as high-normal weight, overweight (including high overweight), mild obesity, obesity and severe obesity. Due to heterogeneity in exposure classifications, these were harmonized as depicted in Supplementary Table 1. This wide category range enables the assessment of a severity-response gradient across BMI categories, beginning at levels below current clinical intervention thresholds such as high-normal weight.

Outcomes were categorized as morbidity or mortality from serious disease including: CVD, kidney disease and liver disease, DM (type 1 and type 2), cancer, serious disease or all-cause mortality. We also documented age of outcome onset reported in the studies.

For each included study, we extracted publication details, cohort characteristics, exposure and outcome definitions, and main findings.

Quality assessment was performed using a modified version of the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale [18], focusing on cohort selection, comparability, and outcome ascertainment. Each study was assessed for quality by one reviewer for each of the following: data source and outcome ascertainment (information bias), exposure assessment (measurement bias), study population coverage and representativeness (selection bias), follow-up duration (attrition bias), confounding, and generalizability.

Synthesis of findings

To enable meaningful cross-study comparisons despite heterogeneous BMI categorizations, exposure classifications were harmonized as described in Supplementary Table 1, mapping study-specific BMI categories onto a standardized framework of normal weight, overweight, mild, moderate, and severe obesity. The primary metric for synthesis was the adjusted hazard ratio (HR), supplemented where available by odds ratios and population-attributable fractions. For each disease domain, we report the range of effect estimates across studies, note the direction and consistency of findings, and highlight patterns of severity-response across BMI categories. Where studies reported discordant findings, potential explanations—including differences in follow-up duration, sex proportion, or outcome definitions—are discussed explicitly. Formal meta-analysis was not performed due to substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity across included studies, including differences in BMI classification systems, outcome definitions, follow-up durations, confounder adjustment sets, and study periods spanning several decades. Instead, findings were synthesized narratively, organized by disease domain and by age at outcome ascertainment (before ages 25, 30, 40, and 50 years).

Results

Study selection

The selection process is shown in Fig. 1. We identified studies through a systematic search of MEDLINE (n = 2,060), Embase (n = 2,009), and PubMed (n = 97), yielding a total of 4,166 records. An additional search of reference lists and grey literature did not yield additional studies. After removing 816 duplicates (810 identified by Covidence and 6 manually), 3,350 records were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 3,251 were excluded (97%) (Supplementary Table 2). Inter-rater agreement was substantial to almost perfect, with 96.8% agreement for title and abstract screening, with Cohen’s kappa values ranging from 0.54 to 0.70.

PRISMA Flow Diagram of Study Selection. Legend: a A list of studies excluded by title and abstract is depicted in supplementary Table 2

Full texts of 99 articles were assessed for eligibility. Sixty-one studies were excluded for the following reasons: wrong outcomes (n = 9), adult population (age > 25 years) (n = 3), incorrect study design (n = 22), pediatric population (n = 2), inappropriate patient population (n = 24), and incorrect definition of exposure (n = 1). No studies were excluded on quality grounds. Inter-rater agreement for full text screening was moderate, with 75.7% raw agreement and a Cohen’s kappa of 0.51. Information on articles excluded from the analysis would be shared by researchers upon request. Study quality, sources and magnitude of potential bias were largely uniform across studies due to the same fundamental study designs and data sources. These are presented in Supplementary file 1.

Studies included

A total of 38 retrospective cohort studies, comprising 57,493,277 cumulative study participants, were included in the review (Supplementary Table 3). Follow-up durations ranged from 8 to 25 years, with some extending up to 40 years. The majority of studies reported at least 20 years of follow-up. This extended follow-up period allows for a comprehensive assessment of long-term health risks attributable to adolescent overweight and obesity.

Study population

All studies were based on nationally representative populations of adolescents who were obligated to undergo physical examinations as part of mandatory military service. In the Israeli studies, the population consisted of mostly Jewish individuals. Most studies (n = 23) examined both men and women participants, while fourteen included only men and one included only women. The majority of studies included adolescents aged 16 to 21 years, examined from 1996 onward. Inclusion required a baseline BMI measurement at conscription. Common exclusion criteria included missing data, extreme adolescent BMI values, minimal follow-up periods (according to the outcome investigated), and pre-existing medical conditions identified at baseline assessment. Sample sizes ranged from 25,000 to over 2.3 million participants. Eventually, Israeli and Swedish studies used the same study population source to perform different analyses. In both cases, this population source was a database of mandatory pre-military examinations performed among adolescents. In this sense, there may be overlap between the actual study population examined between reports, with differences attributed to years of data examined, and inclusion criteria used according to the outcome measured.

Adolescent obesity and risk of morbidity

Risk of hypertension and CVD in adulthood

Higher obesity categories in adolescence were associated with an increased hazard of hypertension in adulthood, in a dose–response manner across increasing adolescent BMI categories. Compared to mild obesity, severe obesity was associated with a higher hazard of high blood pressure, with risks that were two-fold higher in men and 1.9-fold higher in women [19]. CVD incidence, specifically hemorrhagic stroke, ischemic stroke and any stroke, increased across adolescent weight categories, compared with normal weight, even within the high-normal weight category. Among adolescents with obesity the HR for hemorrhagic stroke was 1.7 (95% CI 0.9–3.2), ischemic stroke 2.4 (95% CI 1.9–3.0), and for any stroke 2.2 (95% CI 1.7–2.8) [20].

Likewise, overweight and obesity in adolescence were associated with increased risk of any stroke (HR = 1.5, 95% CI 1.5–1.6) [21], ischemic heart disease (HR = 1.8, 95% CI 1.7–1.8) [22], and heart failure (HR = 1.5, 95% CI 1.3–1.6) [23]. Further evidence indicated that severe obesity in adolescence was strongly associated with early atrial fibrillation (AF), (HR = 3.7, 95% CI 2.4–5.6). Among individuals diagnosed with AF, adolescent obesity was associated with increased risk of all-cause mortality (HR = 2.8, 95% CI 2.3–3.5), incident heart failure (HR = 3.4 95% CI 2.5–4.6), and ischemic stroke (HR = 2.3, 95% CI 1.5–3.6) [24]. Adolescent men with obesity were also at higher risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) (HR = 4.3, 95% CI 3.1–5.9) [25], and venous thromboembolism (VTE) (HR = 2.9, 95% CI 2.6–3.2 for mild-moderate obesity and HR = 4.9, 95% CI 4.1–5.9 for severe obesity) [26] (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table 4).

Risk of kidney disease

Adolescent BMI was significantly associated with early chronic kidney disease (CKD) in young adulthood, even among individuals with high-normal BMI, before the age of 30 years. Compared with low-normal BMI in adolescence, the HR for CKD ranged from 1.8 (95% CI 1.5–2.2) to 9.4 (95% CI 6.6–13.5) in men and from 1.4 (95% CI 1.2–1.6) to 4.3 (95% CI 2.8–6.5) in women [28]. The hazard for CKD was 5.2 times higher in men and 3.7 times higher in women with severe obesity compared with high-normal weight adolescents (Supplementary Table 4). Notably, even after excluding individuals who developed diabetes or hypertension during follow-up, the risk of CKD remained elevated by 1.5- to 2.7-fold among those with overweight or obesity in adolescence. Associations were further found between adolescent overweight and obesity and end-stage renal disease (ESRD), with HR ranging from 3.0 (95% CI 2.5–3.6) to 6.8 (95% CI 5.5–8.5) respectively. The association was especially high for diabetic ESRD, with HRs ranging from 5.9 (95% CI 4.4–8.0) to 19.3 (95% CI 14.1–26.5), but was also significant for non-diabetic ESRD, ranging from 2.1 (95% CI 1.7–2.7) to 3.4 (95% CI 2.4–4.7) [27] (Supplementary Table 4). Figure 2 summarizes the hazard of hypertension, CVD or kidney disease by adolescent weight category.

Risk of diabetes

Compared with adolescents with overweight, those with severe obesity had a 6.7-fold higher hazard for incident T2D in men, and a 2.8-fold higher hazard of T2D incidence in women [19] (Supplementary Table 4). Weight categories were associated with T2D incidence in a dose–response manner, reaching an HR of 25.8 (95% CI 21.0–31.6) among men with severe obesity, and 44.7 (95% CI 32.4–61.5) among women with severe obesity [30]. Further, risk of T2D ranged from an HR of 1.4 (95% CI 1.3–1.4) for high-normal weight to 15.1, (95% CI 13.7–16.6) for severe obesity, compared to low normal-weight [31] (Fig. 3, Supplementary Table 4). A similar pattern was observed for incident type 1 diabetes, although with a smaller effect size HR of 2.0 for incident islet autoantibody-confirmed type 1 diabetes for adolescents with obesity [32] (Supplementary Table 4).

Risk of site-specific malignancies in adulthood

Twelve studies investigated the association between adolescent weight categories and cancer incidence. Overall, adolescent overweight and obesity were consistently associated with increased risks of several site-specific malignancies, with stronger associations observed for obesity than for overweight. An analysis of 2.3 million adolescents found an association between adolescent obesity and cancer incidence in men, before age 30 years (HR = 1.1, 95% CI 1.1–1.2 for overweight and HR = 1.2, 95% CI 1.1–1.3 for obesity) [33]. Among women, adolescent BMI was not associated with overall cancer incidence, although an analysis excluding breast and cervical cancers demonstrated a modest significant increase in risk (HR = 1.2, 95% CI 1.1–1.2 for overweight and HR = 1.2, 95% CI 1.1–1.4 for obesity).

Strong positive associations between adolescent overweight and obesity, and multiple site-specific malignancies were reported among Swedish men. Compared with normal weight, the hazard associated with overweight and obesity was positively associated with a wide range of cancer types, and negatively associated with prostate cancer (HR = 0.9, 95% CI 0.8–0.9 and HR = 0.5, 95% CI 0.4–0.7 respectively) [34]. Similar findings demonstrated a significant association with renal cell carcinoma (HR = 1.7, 95% CI 1.1–2.6 for overweight and HR = 2.8, 95% CI 1.2–6.2 for obesity) [35]. Risk of colon and rectal cancer [36], and pancreatic cancer [37] were assessed in two cohorts of 1.1 million and 720,000 Israeli male adolescents. Significant positive associations were observed between adolescent overweight and obesity and adulthood colon cancer (HR = 1.5, 95% CI 1.1–2.0) and pancreatic cancer (HR = 2.0, 95% CI 1.2–3.0), but not for rectal cancer (HR = 1.09, 95% CI 0.6–1.7). In three additional studies Levi et al. [38–40] followed a cohort of 1.79 million Israeli adolescents for a median of 23 years, in which increased risks were most consistently observed for pancreatic and colon cancers, whereas associations with rectal cancer were less consistent (Fig. 4). In a cohort of 2.3 million participants, adolescent overweight and obesity were associated with increased hazard for gastro-entero-pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (GEP-NET), with overweight and obesity: gastric GEP-NET (HR = 2.2, 95% CI 1.1–4.4), rectum GEP-NET (HR = 2.6, 95% CI 1.1–5.9) and total GEP-NET (HR = 1.4, 95% CI 1.03–2.0) [41].

Risk of breast cancer

Two nationwide cohort studies assessed the risk of breast cancer in 951,480 women [42] and 1,382,093 men [43], followed for a mean of 11 and 21 years, respectively. Among men, adolescent overweight and obesity were positively associated with breast cancer risk (HR = 2.0, 95%CI 1.1–3.5 for overweight and HR = 4.9, 95% CI 2.1–11.5 for obesity), but among women these were inversely associated (HR = 0.9, 95% CI 0.8–0.9 and HR = 0.6, 95% CI 0.5–0.8). Figure 4 summarizes the hazard of cancer incidence in adulthood by adolescent weight category.

Adolescent obesity and risk of mortality

Risk of early all-cause mortality

A single study assessing the risk of all-cause mortality in adulthood found significantly increased mortality rates among participants with BMI in the 50th -75th percentile, compared with those with BMI in the 25th-50th percentile (HR = 1.05, 95% CI 1.005–1.106). Mortality rates gradually increased across BMI percentiles, reaching an HR of 1.8 (95% CI 1.7–2.1) among adolescents with BMI > 97th percentile. The median follow-up duration for these adolescents was considerably shorter than that of individuals with BMI at the 25th percentile (12.5 years compared with 21.5 years), which reflects the recent increase in childhood obesity [46]. Among women, increased hazard was observed only for BMI > 85th percentile, while a BMI > 97th percentile was associated with an HR of 2.1 (95% CI 1.5–3.0) for all-cause mortality.

Risk of disease-specific mortality

Six Israeli studies investigated the association between adolescent weight categories and disease-related mortality. Weight categories showed a dose–response association with death due to CVD etiologies [47, 48], including overall CVD, [47, 48], diabetes [49], chronic kidney disease [50], and cancer [33], starting from high-normal weight values. The highest hazards of death for adolescents with obesity were observed for diabetes [49], kidney disease [50], hypertensive heart disease, and heart failure [47]. Among CVD-specific outcomes, hypertensive mortality showed the strongest association with adolescent BMI [47]. In sex-specific analyses, the association between weight categories and CVD mortality increased in both men and women [51]. Overweight and obesity were positively associated with ischemic heart disease death, respiratory system diseases, and sudden death in both men and women [52]. In Dutch male adolescents, obesity was positively associated with coronary heart disease mortality, and negatively associated with cancer mortality [53] (Fig. 5).

Age of death

Compared with adolescents within normal weight percentiles, mortality rates were higher among those with overweight and highest among those with obesity. Across all studies reporting mortality outcomes, the highest rates were observed for all-cause mortality (8.9% for men with obesity) [46], and for diabetes-related mortality (6.1% among adolescents with obesity of both sexes) [49] (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Age of death or the end of follow-up was younger among adolescents with overweight or obesity, potentially reflecting the rise in prevalence of overweight and obesity in more recent years, shorter survival from disease, or both (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

The magnitude of the association between weight categories and CVD mortality increased with older age or longer follow-up duration. By 10 years of follow-up (before age 30 years), obesity was associated with a borderline significant elevated risk, whereas after 20 years (before age 40 years), the HRs were 1.3 (95% CI 1.06–1.7) 1.8 (95% CI 1.3–2.5), and 3.5 (95% CI 2.1–5.7) for the high-normal, overweight, and obese categories respectively [47].

Recently, a nationwide retrospective cohort study, including 1.22 million healthy adolescents who were medically eligible for army service and who were followed until incidence of serious morbidity for a period of 3–4 years (approximately until age ~ 21 years) found that obesity nearly doubled the risk of all serious morbidities in both sexes (HR = 1.7 (95% CI 1.6–1.8) among men, and HR = 2.2 (95% CI 2.0–2.4) among women) [54]. Importantly, when the HRs were assessed by obesity class the HRs were considerably higher; Among those with obesity class 3, men had HR of 5.1 (95% CI 4.3–6.0), whereas women had HR of 7.3 (95% CI 5.6–9.4) [54]. Overweight and obesity in adolescents were associated with hazard of cancer in young adulthood in men (HR = 1.4, 95% CI 1.03–1.8 and HR = 1.1, 95% CI 0.8–1.7 respectively) and in women, excluding breast and cervical cancers (HR = 1.2, 95% CI 0.7–1.8 and HR = 1.4, 95% CI 0.7–2.6 respectively) [54]. In both sexes, results were consistent when analyses were restricted to healthy adolescents at baseline, and revealed that 65.3% and 57.1% of serious morbidity cases in men and women respectively, representing new-onset disease, within the short follow-up period of the study [54].

Available data on certain outcomes showed a positive trend in hazard of outcome, with obesity severity and with time of follow-up. The hazard for type 2 diabetes increased by severity of obesity before the age of 25 years (HR = 12.1 for obese vs HR = 27.0 for severely obese men, and HR = 12.8 for obese vs HR = 45.3 for severely obese women respectively). Likewise, hazard for all-cause mortality increased by severity of obesity before the age of 50 years (HR = 1.4 for obese vs HR = 1.8 for severely obese men, and HR = 1.5 for obese vs HR = 2.1 for severely obese women respectively). The hazard for CVD mortality increased with time (HR = 2.1 before 30 years, HR = 3.5 before 40 years and HR = 4.2 before 50 years), as the hazard for cancer incidence (HR = 1.1 for men and HR = 1.01 for women before 30 years, HR = 1.2 for men and HR = 1.2 for women before 50 years). Evidently, the hazard for early CKD increased by both obesity severity and with age. Compared to the hazard for CKD assessed before the age of 30 years among adolescents with obesity vs. severe obesity (HR = 2.2 vs HR = 4.2 for men, and HR = 1.7 vs HR = 2.1 for women), hazard increased when assessed before the age of 40 years (HR = 6.0 vs HR = 9.0 for men, and HR = 2.7 vs HR = 4.5 for women respectively) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes evidence from extensive nationwide longitudinal cohort studies, drawing a consistently clear and concerning association between elevated BMI in adolescence, and an accelerated risk of severe chronic disease and premature mortality throughout young adulthood and midlife. The principal finding underscores that high adolescent BMI is not merely a predictor of late-life chronic disease, but rather a direct severity-response risk factor for serious morbidity and mortality commencing in early young adulthood, even before the age of 25 years. Crucially, for individuals with obesity, a substantial proportion of these adverse outcomes represent new-onset disease with approximately two-thirds of cases (65% in men and 57% in women) occurring within a short follow-up period. The highest HRs observed for any disease group were for endocrine and metabolic diseases, with severe obesity strongly associated with risk of early-onset T2D diagnosed before age 25 years, and risk of diabetes-related mortality in midlife. Likewise, adolescent BMI was strongly linked to early CKD, particularly diabetic ESRD, and several site-specific cancers, including pancreatic, renal cell, and colorectal cancers, with the overall cancer risk in men confirmed to be evident before age 30 years.

The short latent period between adolescents' BMI and severe clinical outcomes observed in our review is consistent with established biological mechanisms that are uniquely amplified during adolescence. Obesity-associated insulin resistance (IR), has been shown to lead to increased fat deposition in liver and muscle tissue, and adipose tissue inflammation [56, 57]. Adolescent IR is further amplified by the physiological rise in growth hormone, insulin growth factor-1, and sex steroids which induce fat mass growth and a transient insulin-resistant state that persists and compounds in obese adolescents [58–60]. Unlike adult-onset IR, youth-onset IR is considered particularly aggressive, with rapid beta-cell function decline and early-onset complications [61]. Studies have shown that early life and adolescent obesity are associated with lower insulin sensitivity compared to adults [62], rapid metabolic, vascular, and renal complications, explaining serious morbidity within several years [63]. Finally, it is certainly possible that unmeasured factors such a lifestyle – especially poor diet and physical inactivity – have negative, cumulative and potentially synergistic metabolic effects beyond leading to excessive weight [64, 65].

High adolescent BMI predicted all-cause mortality, beginning at BMI values within the mid-normal range (50th–74th percentile) for men. Furthermore, adolescents with overweight and obesity did not exhibit the decline in mortality rates observed in normal-weight teens over the last four decades, suggesting that the public health gains in survival were effectively negated by persistent obesity. It may also reflect a common public health misperception that young people are healthy, and that excess weight in this age group may not have detrimental effects on life expectancy. High adolescent BMI was also strongly associated with ischemic stroke in young adulthood, a risk demonstrated to be evident before the age of 30 years. Available data on certain outcomes showed a positive trend in hazard, with obesity severity and with time. The magnitude of cardiovascular mortality risk increased with longer follow-up duration, starting from a two-fold increase in risk of total CVD mortality in the obese before 30 years and reaching a four-fold increase in risk well before age 50 years. Likewise, risk of early CKD increased from HR = 2.2 among obese men and HR = 1.7 among obese women before 30 years, to HR = 6.0 and HR = 2.7, respectively before 40 years. In this case, risk also increased with obesity severity, reaching HR = 9.0 and HR = 4.5 among men and women with adolescent severe obesity, before the age of 50 years.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis assessing the global spread of severe obesity in adolescents concluded that between 1980 and 2015 there was a 1.7 fold increase in the prevalence of severe obesity [66]. In many European countries approximately one in four children is severely obese [67]. Importantly, trends in the prevalence of childhood and adolescent obesity, present a disproportionate rise in the more severe obesity categories [66, 68, 69], and a lack of survival benefit associated with obesity, contrary to an observed increase in life expectancy in the Israeli population with time [46].

In the present review, we looked at serious morbidity, mainly CVD, T2D, kidney disease and cancer, as well as disease-specific, and all-cause mortality. Together, these outcomes represent the majority of chronic non-communicable disease that drive the burden of obesity on health care systems worldwide [70] due to high treatment costs [71], increased risk of disability [72], low productivity [73], lower cognitive function [74], and lower social mobility in adulthood [75].

Our results call for improvement in prevention strategies for childhood and adolescent obesity. Risk factors include personal and familial traits such as dietary habits including high sugar consumption, fast food, ultra-processed foods, and rapid eating, insufficient sleep, lack of daily physical activity, and excessive daily screen time [76] [77, 78], but also obesogenic food environments. A recently published UNICEF report concluded that unhealthy foods and beverages, including ultra-processed foods and beverages, are widely available, inexpensive and aggressively marketed in the places where children live, learn and play [79].

According to the World Health Organization, low-cost solutions exist for governments and other stakeholders to reduce the common modifiable risk factors. It has been recommended that governments, health and private sectors act to promote a health-supportive environment by many different means, including ensuring the availability and affordability of healthy food options, improving community infrastructure to encourage physical activity, integrating health education into school curricula alongside nutrition and physical activity programs and supporting families in modeling and fostering healthy behaviors [80–82]. In New Zealand the prevalence of obesity decreased by 2% and that of severe obesity by 0.6% between 2010–2011 and 2015–2016, potentially as a result of improved diet quality and increased activity levels [83]. Similarly, a decrease in the prevalence of severe obesity among children was documented in New York city, potentially resulting from a number of federal programs promoting healthy food choices, increased support for families and communities, and the implementation of comprehensive public health strategies aimed at preventing and manage childhood obesity [84, 85].

Last, our results, showing early serious morbidity and mortality in early young adulthood necessitate a reassessment of screening and intervention strategies among adolescents. Today, most chronic diseases are considered a threat during adulthood, and screening procedures are advised in early adulthood. Our results challenge this notion. First, risk of morbidity and mortality was detected starting in the mid-normal BMI range (50th–74th percentile), which is today not indicative of medical attention. Second, for several serious outcomes, results present a short latent period of 2–3 years, as outcomes were observed before age 25 years. According to these two major findings, the years of adolescence represents a timely and crucial window for early screening and intervention strategies, with significant public health implications. Clinical awareness, education for a healthy lifestyle, and reduction of preventable risk factors, including obesity prevention, are essential.

Study strengths and limitations

Our study has a major strength that distinguishes it from other reviews of large cohort studies as it included only studies in which both exposure and outcomes were derived from nationwide, health and mortality registries. These national databases provided consistent, comprehensive, and validated outcome ascertainment, thereby strengthening the reliability of the study findings. The likelihood of information bias due to misclassification or underscoring is small due to the standardized methods of data collection, as adolescent weight status was measured rather than reported. The included cohorts offer a narrow age range at study entry, coupled with long and comprehensive follow-up, allowing rigorous assessment of exposure and long-term outcomes. These nationwide datasets may therefore reduce the heterogeneity in the association between obesity and morbidity observed in adults. The large sample size, and the extended follow-up, provide adequate power to examine narrow subgroups of BMI percentiles. In addition, the extensive collection of socioeconomic, educational, physical fitness, and cognitive data further allowed for robust multivariable adjustment for strict control of potential confounders, and the conduction of rigorous sensitivity analyses to strengthen the reliability of results. Unlike typical clinical or volunteer-based studies, mandatory pre-conscription medical examinations create nearly complete population coverage of adolescents at a critical developmental stage.

This systematic review specifically focused on these cohorts because they offer an unparalleled combination of scale, standardization, and longitudinal follow-up that is virtually impossible to replicate through traditional research designs. Last, the studies covered populations during pre-military examination, making them representative of their source population. The study populations from Israel are generalizable to most of the Israeli Jewish population, who are mandated for military service at 18 years of age. Some studies from Sweden, Denmark and Norway add to the results though they are less representative of their source population because military service is mandated for men only. Altogether, these studies present a unique opportunity to investigate real world, population based, prospective clinical outcomes of adolescents' overweight and obesity. The heterogeneous study populations, especially that of Israel, which is an immigrant nation [86] support the generalizability of our findings to other high-income countries. This claim is reinforced by study results obtained from this cohort population, which are comparable to other cohorts from high-income countries regarding the association between adolescent BMI and the onset of diabetes [32], cancer [33], and cause-specific mortality [48]. Last, as opposed to previously published systematic reviews on this topic, which examined outcomes in adulthood [9, 11, 12], this study emphasizes on early life, short term outcomes, which are of detrimental importance for health-care management and future planning.

Nevertheless, the studies which were included and reviewed in this report have similar limitations which should be noted. First, the relationship between the outcomes measured and the duration of follow-up. The severe outcomes observed in the studies reviewed here may be attributable to the relatively young age of the population examined, which are nonetheless generally regarded as healthy, and may not be targeted for primary prevention strategies. This may lead to a long exposure period among the exposed. The duration of follow-up of the studies reviewed was not long enough to include the older age groups in which most outcomes studied predominate, and therefore, mostly small proportions of participants experienced outcomes. Second, unmeasured confounding, especially given the heterogeneous nature of the study outcome, is another potential limitation. Studies were unable to account for important confounders to the association, which include both disease related lifestyle factors in adolescence (such as diet, smoking, alcohol intake, and family history), as well as adulthood risk factors for morbidity and mortality, including BMI or other measures of adiposity and metabolic health. In the absence of adult measures of BMI, we could not assess an independent effect of adolescent BMI on outcomes in adulthood. Nonetheless, it has been shown that obesity is an independent risk factor for metabolic morbidity, even among metabolically healthy adolescents [87, 88]. Among the studies reviewed here, three studies specifically assessed the interaction between adolescent BMI and physical fitness on cardiovascular outcomes (stroke, Ischemic Heart Disease (IHD) and Heart Failure (HF) [21–23]) and found that high BMI and low aerobic capacity had similar independent effect magnitudes, and their combination was associated with the highest risk for outcomes, but they did not have multiplicative interaction (Supplementary Table 5). Third, adiposity was only expressed by BMI, and not by other measures which may be more sensitive to the metabolic outcomes of obesity such as waist circumference. Thus, the reported obesity-related disease burden may be an underestimation of the actual risk. Fourth, this review is composed of 26 Israeli studies, and 12 Swedish, Norwegian and Danish studies, potentially leading to two sources of selection bias. Both cohorts were highly representative of the adolescent male population, however, they were less representative of women, and our findings need to be confirmed in racially and ethnically diverse populations. Second, the generalizability of results to other populations may be questionable. However, both Israeli and Swedish populations are ethnically and genetically diverse population [86, 89], with obesity prevalence and trends parallel those observed in other high-income countries [90, 91], and associations estimates between adolescent obesity and outcomes comparable to those reported in cohorts from Denmark, Norway, and the Netherlands. On the other hand, the inclusion of multiple studies examining different outcomes from the same population base, using the same valid methodology, allows for complementary evidence, with robust comparisons of effect sizes across different health conditions. The consistency of findings across these related but independent analyses strengthens confidence in the observed associations. Further, the inclusion of manuscripts which are only based on national nationwide registries may lower the generalizability of results to low-middle income countries which cannot produce such data. Last, this review also includes potential publication bias.

Conclusions

This systematic review of large-scale retrospective cohort studies provides compelling evidence that overweight and obesity in late adolescence are associated with accelerated morbidity with severe health consequences, and mortality well before midlife. These trends are detrimental to health care systems and economies worldwide. Early screening and risk stratification of children and adolescents, together with multilevel prevention and management strategies for overweight and obesity in adolescents, must be prioritized as an urgent public health strategy, to mitigate the increasing burden of severe, early-onset chronic disease.
