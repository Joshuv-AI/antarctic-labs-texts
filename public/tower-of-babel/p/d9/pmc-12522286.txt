Use of 3D visualization system in deep sclerectomy: preliminary results from a pilot study
Authors: Agostino S Vaiano, Antonio Greco, Luca Scartozzi, Maria Marenco, Fabio Garavelli, Alessandro de Filippis, Gerardo Deplano, Giuseppe Giannaccare, Michele Reibaldi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the application of three-dimensional (3D) visualization technology during non-penetrating glaucoma surgery (deep sclerectomy, DS), analyzing its advantages in terms of surgical ergonomics, operative precision, and educational potential.MethodsThe outcomes of a prospective series of 12 patients with open-angle glaucoma undergoing DS assisted by 3D technology were reported. An educational questionnaire was administered to ophthalmology trainees to rate the level of satisfaction on a 5-point Likert scale. Duration of surgery was compared to a previous cohort of patients undergoing DS using conventional optical microscope.ResultsSurgical procedures of 12 patients (6 male, 6 females, mean age 72,5 y.o.) undergone DS were reviewed. The application of 3D technology in DS demonstrated tangible advantages, including improved visualization of anatomical structures (trabecular meshwork, Schlemm’s canal, Descemet’s membrane), better postural comfort for the surgeon, and more effective sharing of the surgical field with the entire team. No cases of intraoperative perforation were observed. Median operative times were 45.0 min (IQR 4.3) in the 3D group and 44.6 min (IQR 5.1) in the conventional group (p = 0.78). A mean score of 4,23/5 was registered.ConclusionsThe integration of 3D visualization in DS represents a promising evolution, enhancing surgical precision and improving ergonomics. The educational benefits are particularly relevant in the academic setting. Controlled prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings in long-term clinical parameters.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04374-4.Keywords: Deep Sclerectomy, Glaucoma surgery, Three-dimensional heads-up glaucoma surgery, Non penetrating glaucoma surgery

Introduction

The use of three-dimensional (3D) visualization in ophthalmic surgery has demonstrated significant advantages in various surgical procedures. Its role is well established in posterior segment surgery, particularly in vitreoretinal procedures, where it has enhanced ergonomic posture and improved depth perception [1–3]. More recently, 3D technology has also been introduced for surgical procedures on the anterior segment of the eye, notably in cataract surgery and in keratoplasty, such as Descemet Membrane Endothelial Keratoplasty (DMEK) [4–8]. In these contexts, 3D systems have been shown to improve anatomical visualization and facilitate more precise surgical maneuvers.

However, the applications of 3D visualization in glaucoma surgery remains limited [9, 10]. To date, no published clinical experiences have reported the use of 3D systems in deep sclerectomy (DS), a non-penetrating filtering procedure that requires high precision in tissue dissection and preservation of the Trabeculo-Descemet membrane (TDM). Our study represents, to the best of our knowledge, the first documented clinical application of 3D visualization in DS. The aim of the study was to assess the benefits of the advanced stereoscopic perception provided by 3D systems, superior to conventional microscopes, for a better identification of tissue layers, improved precision in scleral dissection, reduced risk of intraoperative complications, and enhanced ergonomics. Particular attention was also given to the potential educational advantages, especially in academic settings.

Methods

This prospective clinical study aimed to assess the impact of 3D visualization technology during DS procedures. Consecutive patients with medically uncontrolled open-angle glaucoma (OAG) were enrolled and underwent DS assisted by a high-resolution NGENUITY® 3D visualization system (Alcon, Fort Worth, TX, USA). All procedures were performed by the same experienced glaucoma surgeon (A.S.V.). The halogen lamp operated at a color temperature of 3600 K. The light intensity of the OPMI Lumera 700 microscope was set to 40% of its maximum capacity. For 3D surgery, the iris diaphragm of the digital video camera was adjusted to an opening of approximately 30–40%. All patients provided informed consent, and the study was approved by the local ethics committee in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

The 3D visualization platform allowed real-time stereoscopic display of the surgical field on a high-definition monitor, supposing to enhance depth perception and anatomical detail. The primary outcomes evaluated were the precision of scleral dissection, the correct identification of surgical landmarks (deep scleral flap, scleral spur, and TDM), and the incidence of intraoperative complications. Operative times were recorded and compared to a retrospective control group of patients who had undergone the same procedure using conventional optical microscopy.

Ophthalmology trainees who observed the surgical procedures completed a structured questionnaire evaluating their satisfaction about educational benefits from the 3D system. For this purpose, a composite tool combining the Mini-STEEM (Surgical Theatre Educational Environment Measure) to assess the perceived quality of the educational environment, and selected items of the O-SCORE (Ottawa Surgical Competency Operating Room Evaluation) to evaluate perceived improvements in surgical understanding and autonomy was administered. Ratings were scored using a 5-point Likert scale. Feedback on visual comfort, anatomical detail, and interaction with the surgical field was also collected (Appendix – Educational Assessment Questionnaire).

Surgical technique (video and figure A)

All surgeries are performed under local anesthesia. Traction sutures are placed at the 3 and 9 o’clock positions of the corneal limbus to optimize the exposure of the interventional site. A fornix-based conjunctival flap, approximately 12 mm in width, is meticulously crafted, selecting a scleral region that avoids the anterior ciliary arteries. Subsequently, a rectangular flap measuring 5 × 5 mm, representing approximately 40% of the scleral thickness, is created with an anterior hinge and extended 1–1.5 mm into the clear cornea. Sponges impregnated with a solution containing 0.2 mg/mL of mitomycin C (MMC) are applied for 2 min to the area between Tenon’s capsule and the deep scleral bed. Thorough rinsing with balanced saline solution (BSS) is performed. Thereafter, a rectangular deeper sclerocorneal flap measuring 4 × 4 mm is sculpted using a diamond blade, and dissection is continued anteriorly until the TDM is reached. The inner wall of the Schlemm canal and the juxtacanalicular trabeculum are carefully dissected using fine, smooth forceps. The superficial scleral flap is left unsutured in the instance of absolute certainty regarding the absence of micro/macro perforations. The conjunctiva is subsequently closed utilizing a 10 − 0 nylon suture. Postoperative treatment

Postoperative treatment includes moxifloxacin 0.5% (Vigamox, Alcon) administered three times daily for one week, along with dexamethasone 0.15% (Etacortilen, Sifi), which is tapered over six months. If an increase in IOP is observed during the follow-up period, gonioscopy is usually performed to identify the underlying cause. If necessary, YAG laser goniopuncture is performed.

If a steroid response is suspected, corticosteroids are discontinued and replaced with a weaker corticosteroid or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory medication.

Results

Data from 12 patients (6 male, 6 females, mean age 72,5 y.o.) who underwent DS with the use of a 3D visualization system were analyzed. Surgical procedure was performed uneventfully in all patients and no cases of intraoperative perforation of the TDM were reported. The use of 3D technology facilitated clear visualization of surgical landmarks, including the deep scleral flap, the scleral spur, and the TDM. This enhanced visualization allowed for precise flap sculpting and improved assessment of aqueous percolation. Ergonomic comfort for the surgeon improved significantly, and intraoperative collaboration among the surgical team was enhanced. Median operative times were 45.0 min (IQR 4.3) in the 3D group and 44.6 min (IQR 5.1) in the conventional group (p = 0.78).

In this limited cohort of patients who underwent DS using the Ngenuity 3D visualization system, no intraoperative perforations were observed.

Discussion

3D-assisted surgery represents a promising advancement in DS. The improved depth perception and high-definition anatomical visualization [1–3], could help reduce surgical complications, enhance the learning curve for less experienced surgeons, and improve long-term surgical outcomes. A major advantage of 3D technology is its ability to enhance surgical ergonomics, reducing fatigue associated with prolonged use of conventional binocular microscopes [11]. Prior studies in vitreoretinal and anterior segment surgery have demonstrated that 3D systems allow for improved posture and better visualization of fine ocular structures than traditional microscopy [11, 12].

When compared with analog microscopes (including the Zeiss OPMI Lumera 700), the NGENUITY® 3D Visualization System seems to provide up to 48% greater magnification, up to a fivefold increase in depth of field, and up to a 42% improvement in depth resolution (Alcon, Technical Data Sheet; available at myalcon.com). This translates theoretically to an effective perceived magnification equivalent to approximately 3.5× versus the 2.4× maximum optical magnification of the Lumera 700 (Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, product specifications). In addition, the 3D digital platform maintains a broader depth of field and improved depth resolution (up to + 42%), allowing for clear identification of delicate anatomical structures—such as the trabeculo-Descemet membrane—without frequent refocusing. These optical advantages, combined with the large high-definition stereoscopic display, may explain the improved tissue layer recognition and ergonomic posture reported by surgeons and documented in previous literature on both posterior and anterior segment surgery. These benefits are particularly valuable in DS, which requires precise tissue handling to optimize filtration outcomes. Additionally, real-time projection of high-definition images facilitates an immersive experience for trainees and assisting personnel. Teaching hospitals may benefit from this technology by providing an interactive learning environment that enhances surgical training and standardization of techniques [13]. The findings of our study align with these potential advantages. The absence of intraoperative perforations in this preliminary cohort of patients undergoing DS with the aid of the Ngenuity 3D visualization system is a noteworthy finding. At our institution, the historical perforation rate for this procedure stands at 1.7% (data in preparation), and this compares to a 2.9% rate reported in the broader literature [14].

The complete lack of perforations in the 3D-assisted group, though based on a limited sample, suggests a potential role of enhanced visualization in improving surgical precision and safety. A potential confounding factor is that all procedures were performed by a single experienced surgeon (ASV), proficient in both anterior and posterior segment surgery and highly accustomed to the use of the 3D visualization system in retinal surgery.

The Ngenuity 3D system provides high-definition, stereoscopic imaging with greater depth perception and magnification compared to conventional ocular microscopes [15].

These advantages may facilitate finer dissection and better tissue plane identification during the critical steps of DS, thus reducing the likelihood of inadvertent perforation of the TDM. While the current data are not powered to draw definitive conclusions, the observed trend aligns with the growing body of evidence supporting the use of digital visualization systems in microsurgery. Further studies with larger sample sizes and controlled designs are needed to validate these findings and to explore whether this technology translates into improved long-term surgical outcomes. Furthermore, the educational outcomes were particularly noteworthy and all trainees reported high satisfaction with the 3D system, noting improvements in their comprehension of surgical anatomy and procedural steps. The highest scores were assigned to visual clarity, depth perception, and the ability to remain actively engaged in the procedure. These results confirm the value of 3D visualization as a teaching tool, particularly for procedures requiring detailed tissue dissection. Importantly, operative times were comparable between procedures performed with the conventional microscope and those using the 3D visualization system; therefore, the adoption of the 3D system should not be regarded as a constraint to performing the surgery. Nonetheless, this observation should be interpreted with caution, as no formal case–control comparison was undertaken. Such an analysis lies beyond the scope of the present study, which is primarily intended to demonstrate the feasibility of performing non-penetrating glaucoma surgery with the 3D system.

The ergonomic advantages observed by the primary surgeon further support the integration of this technology, particularly in procedures demanding prolonged focus and precision. In addition to DS, the application of 3D visualization may be extended to other surgical procedures for controlling glaucoma, such as trabeculectomy or bleb forming minimally invasive glaucoma surgeries (MIBS), where fine anatomical dissection and intraoperative control are equally critical. Furthermore, the future integration of intraoperative optical coherence tomography (iOCT) with 3D systems holds the potential to further enhance intraoperative decision-making by enabling real-time imaging of tissue planes and fluid dynamics.

Despite these promising outcomes, limitations remain and should be mentioned.The findings should be interpreted with caution given the relatively small number of surgical cases and the limited number of students who completed the questionnaire. Further studies with larger cohorts and broader participation are warranted to confirm these preliminary observations and to better assess the educational value of 3D visualization systems in surgical training.The cost may restrict accessibility in low-resource settings, and although the learning curve for experienced surgeons may be short, adaptation may take longer for less skilled operators.

Conclusions

The integration of 3D technology in DS provides a promising approach for enhancing surgical precision, reducing intraoperative complications, and improving surgeon ergonomics [16]. Our study demonstrates that 3D-assisted surgery enhances visualization of key surgical landmarks, facilitates safe tissue dissection, reduces intraoperative complications and may contribute to improved surgical outcomes. Future randomized studies with larger sample sizes and long-term follow-up are warranted to confirm the advantages of 3D visualization in terms of surgical outcomes.
