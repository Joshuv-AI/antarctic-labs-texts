Tract-specific white matter microstructure associated with early cognitive development in infants
Authors: Eun Soo Kim, Martin A. Styner, Weili Lin, Jed T. Elison, Sooyeon Sung, Mi Jung Kwon, Dae Young Yoon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and purposeEarly infancy is characterized by rapid and regionally specific changes in white matter microstructure, which can be quantified using diffusion tensor imaging (DTI). We examined whether diffusion-derived microstructural measures during early postnatal life are associated with emerging cognitive functions.Material and methodsTwenty-three infants from the UNC Brain Connectome Project underwent 3T MRI between 2 weeks and 3 months of age. Fractional anisotropy (FA), axial diffusivity (AD), and radial diffusivity (RD) were extracted from major white matter tracts using atlas-based tractography. Cognitive performance was assessed using the Mullen Scales of Early Learning (MSEL). Associations between tract-specific DTI metrics and cognitive scores were evaluated.ResultsTract-specific associations were observed between white matter microstructure and cognitive outcomes. Diffusion metrics in the corticothalamic tract and fornix were associated with gross and fine motor performance, while FA, AD, and RD in the optic tract and optic radiation were related to visual reception. Language measures were associated with diffusion properties of the arcuate and uncinate fasciculi. Several corpus callosum subdivisions also showed significant associations with early learning outcomes.ConclusionThese findings demonstrate that early postnatal white matter microstructure is selectively associated with emerging cognitive functions, supporting the utility of DTI for investigating brain–behavior relationships during early infancy.

Introduction

Human brain development is exceptionally rapid and dynamic during the first two years of life, with critical neurodevelopmental milestones concentrated in the first few months post-birth (Howell et al., 2019). During this period, active myelination in key white matter (WM) structures—such as the posterior limb of the internal capsule, optic radiation, and the corpus callosum—establishes the structural foundation for future cognitive and motor functions (Dubois et al., 2014; Rose et al., 2014). Although WM myelination begins in utero and continues through late adolescence, it peaks during the first year of life (Dubois et al., 2014). This process proceeds at varying rates across brain regions, following a specific caudo-rostral gradient and progressing from central to peripheral regions (Dubois et al., 2014). Unlike the adult brain, infant brain development is highly asynchronous; sensorimotor regions mature rapidly, while associative regions develop more slowly over several decades (Dubois et al., 2014; Kipping et al., 2017). To quantify these maturation processes, DTI serves as a vital non-invasive tool (Geng et al., 2012). DTI measures the diffusivity of water molecules to reflect WM integrity: FA indicates high directional diffusivity in mature WM, while AD and RD typically decrease as myelination and axonal density increase (Catani and Thiebaut de Schotten, 2008; Geng et al., 2012; Girault et al., 2019). Prior research, such as the large-scale study by Girault et al. (2019), has demonstrated that DTI metrics at 1 and 2 years of age are significantly associated with general cognitive ability, motor, and language skills (Girault et al., 2019). Despite this clinical significance, a critical knowledge gap remains. Most neuroimaging research has focused on infants aged 1–2 years, leaving the relationship between “ultra-early” microstructural changes—occurring within mere weeks of birth—and long-term developmental trajectories largely under-explored. Identifying neuroimaging biomarkers during this highly plastic, neonatal-to-infant transition is crucial for the early detection of developmental delays. To address this gap, the present study focuses on the ultra-early postnatal period between 2 weeks and 3 months. We aimed to investigate the correlation between DTI indices (FA, AD, and RD) across major fiber tracts and subsequent diagnostic and cognitive outcomes. By targeting this narrow and dynamic window, this study seeks to provide new evidence regarding the early structural precursors of human cognitive and behavioral development.

Material and methods

Participants

Participants were recruited as part of the UNC Brain Connectome Project, a longitudinal study of early brain development approved by the Biomedical Institutional Review Board of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. Infants were recruited from multiple sources in central North Carolina, including obstetric/gynecology clinics, psychiatric facilities, email campaigns, and community-based outreach. Recruitment occurred between 2003 and 2014.

The cohort included both infants considered at higher risk for neurodevelopmental vulnerability and control infants, based on established project criteria. Exclusion criteria for all participants included multiple births, severe neonatal illness, and major structural abnormalities on MRI, with the exception of minor intracranial hemorrhage, which is commonly observed in neonates. Additional exclusion criteria for the control group included a family history of major psychiatric illness and maternal psychotropic medication use during pregnancy.

For the present study, 37 infants who underwent MRI scanning between 2 weeks and 3 months of age and had useable diffusion data were included. Analyses were conducted on the combined sample, without stratification by risk group, to examine associations between early white matter microstructure and cognitive outcomes.

Image acquisition

Structural Magnetic Resonance Imaging (Structural MRI)

Structural MRI scans were acquired using 3 T S Prisma scanners with 32-channel head coils at two imaging sites: the Center for Magnetic Resonance Research at the University of Minnesota and the Biomedical Research Imaging Center at the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill. The protocol was adapted from the Human Connectome Project (Van Essen et al., 2013) and Lifespan Human Connectome Project to be appropriate for imaging children from infancy through age five. The imaging session began with a modified quiet localizer scan to minimize acoustic noise during natural sleep. The repetition time was extended from 8 ms to 30 ms, and echo time from 4 to 5 ms. T1-weighted and T2-weighted scans were prioritized and acquired using high-resolution three-dimensional sequences. Specifically, T2-weighted scans used a variable flip angle turbo spin-echo sequence (Siemens SPACE), allowing improved contrast and anatomical detail (Mugler et al., 2000).

All incoming structural data underwent visual quality assessment for motion, field-of-view coverage, and ghosting artifacts, using a four-point rating scale: reject, borderline, pass, and excellent. If multiple acquisitions were available and rated borderline or worse, a co-registration and weighted averaging method was applied to enhance image quality. After quality control (QC), all structural data were rigidly aligned to a pediatric brain template (Hazlett et al., 2017) representing children aged 1 to 2 years, regardless of participant age, to avoid age-related alignment bias. Intensity inhomogeneity correction (Tustison et al., 2010) was performed using the N4 algorithm. Skull stripping (Wang et al., 2014) was achieved using a multi-atlas method followed by manual corrections. These preprocessed structural images served as the anatomical reference for subsequent analyses.

Diffusion Magnetic Resonance Imaging (Diffusion MRI)

DTI was performed at 3T using either a Siemens Allegra head-only scanner or a Siemens Tim Trio scanner (Siemens Medical Systems, Erlangen, Germany). To accommodate the longitudinal design of the study and optimize data quality across a wide developmental age range (from neonates to 6 years), three DTI acquisition protocols were employed. The initial longitudinal stabilization protocol consisted of a 6-direction diffusion-weighted sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 5200 ms, echo time (TE) = 73 ms, b-value = 1000 s/mm2, slice thickness = 2 mm, in-plane resolution = 2 × 2 mm2, and 45 axial slices. One non-diffusion-weighted image (b = 0) and six diffusion-weighted images were acquired per repetition, and the sequence was repeated five times to improve signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). This protocol was implemented at the beginning of the longitudinal study and applied consistently across participants when applicable. For higher angular resolution, a 42-direction DTI protocol was used with TR = 7680 ms, TE = 82 ms, and a b-value of 1000 s/mm2, including seven interleaved b = 0 volumes. In addition, a multi-shell diffusion protocol was acquired in a subset of participants to enable advanced microstructural analyses (Garic et al., 2025; Li et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2012). This protocol employed a 1.5 mm isotropic spatial resolution, a multi-band acceleration factor of 5, and consisted of 454 vol, allowing estimation of advanced diffusion metrics such as fiber orientation distribution functions. Diffusion images were screened offline for motion artifacts, missing and corrupted sections by using an automatic DWI analysis quality control tool DTIPrep (http://www.nitrc.org/projects/dtiprep/). The five repeated sequences were combined into a single DWI volume after correction for motion and removal of outliers and FA and diffusivity maps were then estimated using standard weighted least square fitting (Geng et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2010).

Diffusion-weighted MRI data were collected using single-shot echo-planar imaging sequences with reverse phase encoding directions (anterior-posterior and posterior-anterior) to correct susceptibility-induced distortions using the TOPUP algorithm (Andersson et al., 2003) from the FMRIB Software Library (Jenkinson et al., 2012). All datasets were processed using DTIPrep software (Oguz et al., 2014) to identify and exclude corrupted volumes and to correct for motion and eddy current artifacts. Motion metrics were recorded and later used in statistical modeling. Datasets with over 20 percent of diffusion volumes rejected were excluded from further analysis.

Image process

ITK snap-brain mask

Brain masks were created to isolate brain regions from MRI scans using the ITK-SNAP software (version 3.8.0). Masking was primarily guided by FA atlases specifically designed for infant brain MRI scans between 2 weeks and 3 months of age. FA maps were preferred due to their relatively high tissue contrast in the developing infant brain, particularly within the cortex and fiber-rich white matter regions such as the corpus callosum, internal capsule, and external capsule. The preference for FA maps stems from their inherent ability to generate a high tissue contrast. Mean diffusivity (MD) images were used only as a secondary visual aid for boundary refinement and were not used for quantitative analyses. Although aligned structural images were available, they were not used as the primary basis for mask delineation because structural contrast in early infancy can be variable and less reliable for consistent whole-brain masking across subjects. The skull and other non-brain tissues were excluded from the mask using ITK-SNAP built-in tools. Manual corrections were applied when necessary to refine the masks. Brain mask creation were drawn by the first author (ESK, with 15 years of experience) and reviewed by the corresponding author (MAS, with 25 years of experience). The color-coded FA maps facilitated the identification of brain structures with consistent architecture (high FA) and well-defined orientation, as indicated by the underlying eigenvectors. For complete brain mask coverage, this study employed two primary tools within ITK-SNAP: the “polygon” and “paintbrush” tools. The polygon tool empowers users to meticulously outline specific areas of interest (e.g., the brain) within a given slice view. Conversely, paint brush tools offer greater flexibility in generating masks of various shapes and sizes. This tool allowed for the rapid drawing and editing of mask boundaries using mice. Notably, the “Adaptive Paintbrush Mask” feature offers an advantage by automatically assigning foreground labels only to neighboring voxels exhibiting similar intensity values to the initial click location.

Atlas building-DTI atlas builder

To account for the rapid developmental changes in infant neuroanatomy, we constructed a study-specific atlas using the DTI Atlas Builder (version 1.6). The process followed a rigorous two-phase pipeline. First, a 1-2 year infant template was utilized as an initial global reference for scaling and orientation to create a preliminary atlas tailored to a typical baby brain. The second phase involved iterative refinement to address natural variations in size and shape among subjects. This fine-tuning integrated two advanced techniques: (1) adjustments based on joint, group-specific heterogeneity atlas construction, and (2) a final round of individual adjustments using symmetric diffeomorphic registration. This approach allowed for the integration of individual data while preserving the inherent anatomical variability of the neonatal cohort. Finally, all adjusted scans were averaged to produce a highly detailed, comprehensive average baby brain map. To ensure accuracy, the first and corresponding authors (with 15 and 25 years of experience, respectively) performed rigorous visual quality assurance (QA) on all registration results.

Tractography-3D slicer

After constructing the DTI atlas, we used WM fiber tractography to delineate the neural pathways. This process utilized the “Tractography Display” module within 3D Slicer (version 5.4.0). Seeding for fiber tractography relies on predefined atlas-based regions of interest (ROIs) or manually tracked ROIs. Notably, all fiber bundles, except for the corpus callosum, were bilaterally reorganized.

A two-step approach was used to improve the initial results of fiber tractography. First, stopping criteria based on the FA and radius of curvature were implemented. This ensured that the generated pathways remained within WM boundaries. Second, potential inaccuracies and outliers were addressed using a multistage filtering procedure. This procedure includes initial pre-cleaning within the 3D slicer and advanced cluster-based filtering. Quality control and sample selection using FADDTster

To ensure the integrity of the imaging data, we designed and implemented age-specific DWI and DTI quality control (QC) protocols using FADDTster (version 3.3.1; Contributors: Jean Noel, Juan Carlos Prieto; License: Apache 2.0). FADDTster is a user-friendly adaptation of FADTTS, a MATLAB-based tool (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). While the original FADTTS typically requires advanced coding knowledge, FADDTster provides both command-line and graphical user interfaces, democratizing access to sophisticated DTI analyses for researchers without extensive programming backgrounds. Using this tool, we calculated tensor eigenvalues to derive key diffusion properties, including FA, AD, and RD. While 37 infants were initially recruited and scanned between the ages of 2 weeks and 3 months, we applied a rigorous QC threshold of 0.74 ± 0.11 (Mean ± SD; range: 0.55–0.85) to ensure data reliability. Consequently, 14 subjects were excluded for failing to meet these strict quality criteria. Cognitive assessments

Cognitive ability was assessed using MSEL at different ages: 10 months (one child, 4%), 11 months (three children, 13%), 12 months (10 children, 43%), and 13 months (nine children, 39%). Assessments were conducted by trained examiners and included measures of gross motor (GM), fine motor (FM), visual reception (VR), expressive language (EL), and receptive language (RL). Gross motor skills reflect large-muscle movements supporting posture, balance, and locomotion (e.g., sitting, crawling, standing, and walking), whereas fine motor skills involve coordinated movements of small muscles of the hands and fingers, including reaching, grasping, and object manipulation. Visual reception assesses nonverbal cognitive abilities related to visual perception and processing, such as visual tracking, spatial awareness, and visually guided problem-solving. Receptive language refers to the ability to understand spoken language, including words and simple commands, while expressive language reflects the production of vocalizations, words, and early sentence forms. Performance across these domains was evaluated using raw scores and age-standardized T-scores. Standardized scores from the Visual Reception, Fine Motor, Receptive Language, and Expressive Language domains were combined to generate the Early Learning Composite (ELC), a global measure of cognitive functioning (range: 87–127; mean = 105.7, SD = 10.1). In addition, raw scores for each domain were analyzed separately to examine domain-specific associations with white matter microstructure without age normalization.

Statistical analysis

Associations between WM microstructure and early cognitive performance were examined using cross-sectional generalized linear models (GLM). Percentile scores from MSEL served as outcome variables, with separate models fitted for each subdomain (Gross Motor, Fine Motor, Visual Reception, Receptive Language, Expressive Language, and ELC). Each subject contributed data from a single time point. Tract-averaged diffusion metrics (FA, RD, and AD) were entered as continuous predictors in separate linear regression models. WM tracts were selected a priori based on prior neurodevelopmental literature and included motor, language-related, association, and commissural pathways. Post-conceptual age at scan and sex were included as covariates in all models. in all models to control for potential confounding effects and scanner-related variability, as diffusion MRI data were acquired using two different scanners (Allegra vs Tim Trio).

The GLM for each cognitive outcome was specified as:MSEL outcome = β0 + β1(DTI metric) + β2(Post-conceptual age) + β3(Sex) + β4(Scanner type) + εwhere β0 represents the intercept, β1–β4 represent regression coefficients for each predictor, and ε represents the residual error term.

Statistical significance was assessed using Wald chi-square tests with a two-tailed threshold of p < 0.05. Multiple-comparison correction was not applied due to the exploratory nature of the analysis. All analyses were performed using JMP version 17. FA values are unitless, whereas RD and AD are reported in mm2/s. MD was not included by study design, as RD and AD were considered more biologically informative in the developing infant brain.

The studies involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill (UNC IRB 16-1943) (date April 11, 2016 – now). Written informed consent to participate in this study was obtained from the participants’ legal guardians or next of kin.

Standardized Regression Coefficients for DTI Metrics Associated with Mullen Scale Subtests.

This multi-panel figure (A-F) illustrates the associations between white matter microstructure, as assessed by DTI metrics, and various subtest scores from the Mullen Scales of Early Learning. Each forest plot displays standardized regression coefficients (β) and their corresponding standard errors (SE) for (FA, red circles), Radial Diffusivity (RD, green squares), and Axial Diffusivity (AD, blue triangles) across specific white matter tracts. Data are presented on a linear scale to reflect the true magnitude of associations, and all P-values are explicitly displayed for statistically significant findings (∗p < .05, ∗∗p < .01, ∗∗∗p < .001). All anatomical names are provided in full.

• (A) Gross Motor: This panel highlights key microstructural predictors of gross motor skills. Significant positive associations for FA and negative associations for RD are prominent in the Fornix (p < .0001), emphasizing its role in motor development. The Corticothalamic tract (Motor) also shows significant negative estimates for FA and AD.

• (B) Fine Motor: Similar to gross motor, fine motor development shows robust FA and RD associations with the Fornix (p < .0001). The Corticothalamic tract (Motor) consistently exhibits significant negative associations for FA and AD, suggesting a shared microstructural basis for motor functions.

• (C) Visual Reception: This panel demonstrates the strong dependence of visual reception on the integrity of primary visual pathways. Both the Optic tract and Optic radiation show highly robust relationships (p < .0001), where higher FA and lower RD values are strongly predictive of better visual processing performance.

• (D) Receptive Language: This plot reveals complex patterns within major language pathways. Widespread significant associations (p < .001) for AD and RD are observed across segments of the Arcuate Fasciculus (Frontotemporal, Temporoparietal, FrontoParietal) and Uncinate Fasciculus, indicating the critical roles of axonal integrity and myelination in receptive language development.

• (E) Expressive Language: This panel highlights distinct lateralized patterns. The Right Arcuate Fasciculus Temporoparietal segment shows an exceptionally strong positive FA association (β ~ 800, p < .0001), underscoring its significant contribution. Various tracts also show significant AD associations, reflecting diverse microstructural underpinnings of expressive language.

• (F) Early Learning Composite (ELC): This panel examines the Corpus Callosum's role in overall cognitive development. Most segments, particularly the Parietal, Motor, and Splenium regions, exhibit highly significant positive FA associations (p < .001), indicating the importance of interhemispheric connectivity. The Parietal and Splenium segments also show significant negative RD associations, suggesting enhanced myelination as a key predictor of higher ELC scores.

For gross motor function, the corticospinal tract did not demonstrate significant associations with FA, RD, or AD. Similarly, no significant relationships were observed between gross motor performance and the corticofugal tract, corticoreticular tract, or inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus. In contrast, the motor component of the corticothalamic tract showed significant associations with FA (p = .0104) and AD (p = .0004), and the fornix demonstrated strong associations with FA and RD (both p < .0001), indicating the involvement of thalamocortical and limbic pathways in gross motor performance.

A similar pattern was observed for fine motor function. The corticothalamic tract showed a significant association with AD (p = .0056), while the fornix again demonstrated robust associations with FA and RD (both p < .0001). Other motor-related tracts, including the corticospinal, corticofugal, corticoreticular, and inferior fronto-occipital fasciculi, did not show significant associations with fine motor outcomes.

Visual reception scores were significantly associated with diffusion metrics of the optic tract and optic radiation. FA, RD, and AD of both tracts showed significant relationships with visual reception, consistent with their central role in transmitting visual information from the retina to cortical visual processing areas.

Receptive language performance was associated with microstructural properties of multiple segments of the arcuate fasciculus and the uncinate fasciculus bilaterally. Within the frontotemporal segments of the arcuate fasciculus, FA was not significantly associated with receptive language; however, both left and right segments demonstrated strong positive associations with RD and AD (all p < .0001), suggesting that diffusivity-related microstructural features contribute more prominently than anisotropy in these regions.

In the temporoparietal segment, the left arcuate fasciculus showed significant associations across all diffusion metrics (FA, RD, and AD), whereas the right temporoparietal segment showed significant associations with RD and AD but not FA, indicating hemispheric asymmetry. Similarly, frontoparietal arcuate segments demonstrated significant associations primarily with RD and AD bilaterally, with FA significance limited to the right hemisphere. Both left and right uncinate fasciculi were significantly associated with receptive language, with the left uncinate showing associations across FA, RD, and AD, and the right uncinate showing associations with RD and AD.

Expressive language showed a distinct pattern. Significant associations were predominantly observed for FA across multiple arcuate fasciculus segments, including bilateral temporoparietal and frontoparietal components, as well as the right frontotemporal segment. RD showed limited involvement, with a significant negative association observed only in the left frontoparietal arcuate fasciculus. AD demonstrated significant associations in select arcuate segments. In addition, both left and right uncinate fasciculi showed significant associations with expressive language, characterized by bilateral FA and AD effects, whereas RD was not significant.

ELC scores demonstrated significant associations with multiple subregions of the corpus callosum. FA showed robust positive associations in the genu, motor, parietal, splenium, and tapetum segments, with the largest effect observed in the parietal corpus callosum. AD was also positively associated with ELC scores in the genu, motor, parietal, and tapetum segments. Conversely, RD demonstrated significant negative associations in posterior corpus callosum regions, including the parietal segment and splenium, consistent with reduced perpendicular diffusion and potentially greater myelination. No significant associations were observed in the premotor corpus callosum segment.

Discussion

This study investigated the relationship between early WM maturation and cognitive development during the early infant using DTI and MSEL. The findings demonstrate clear, domain-specific associations between WM microstructure and early cognitive functions, indicating that distinct neural pathways and diffusion metrics contribute differentially to motor, visual, language, and early learning abilities (Basser and Jones, 2002; Batalle et al., 2017). Collectively, these results emphasize that early cognitive development is supported by the coordinated maturation of distributed WM networks rather than by isolated tracts (Fields, 2008; Lebel and Beaulieu, 2011; Suminaite et al., 2019). Early infancy is characterized by rapid myelination and structural reorganization of WM, processes that are essential for efficient neural communication and the emergence of higher cognitive functions (Short et al., 2013). DTI metrics such as FA, AD, and RD serve as sensitive indicators of WM microstructure, reflecting axonal coherence, caliber, and myelination (Figley et al., 2022). In line with previous studies, higher FA and lower RD were generally associated with better cognitive performance, supporting the notion that more coherent and well-myelinated pathways facilitate faster and more reliable information transfer (Coelho et al., 2021). Importantly, the present findings extend prior work by demonstrating that these relationships are highly tract- and function-specific during early development. This specificity was evidenced by selective associations of motor outcomes with the corticothalamic tract and fornix but not the corticospinal tract, dissociable diffusion profiles for receptive versus expressive language within overlapping arcuate and uncinate fasciculi, and additional segment- and hemisphere-dependent effects across language-related pathways. Motor performance showed selective associations with the corticothalamic tract and the fornix, whereas the corticospinal tract did not show significant relationships with either gross or fine motor outcomes. This pattern suggests that early motor development may rely more heavily on thalamocortical integration and limbic-related pathways involved in motor modulation and coordination, rather than exclusively on primary descending motor tracts (Carmona et al., 2025; Umphred et al., 2007). The involvement of the fornix further implies that broader limbic–cortical networks, which integrate motor behavior with arousal and memory-related processes, may play an important role in early motor skill acquisition (Kostović and Judaš, 2010; Umphred et al., 2007). These findings highlight that early motor abilities emerge from distributed neural systems rather than from a single canonical motor pathway. Visual reception demonstrated robust and consistent associations with the optic tract and optic radiation across all diffusion metrics. These tracts constitute the primary conduits for visual information transfer from the retina to primary and associative visual cortices, and their strong structure–function correspondence supports the biological validity of diffusion-based measures in the developing visual system (Bassi et al., 2008; Dubois et al., 2008; Groppo et al., 2014). The convergence of FA, RD, and AD effects suggests that multiple aspects of WM microstructure, including axonal organization and myelination, are critical for early visual processing (Li et al., 2023; O'Muircheartaigh et al., 2014; Roberts et al., 2013). These results are consistent with prior neurodevelopmental studies emphasizing the foundational role of intact visual pathways in early perceptual and cognitive development (Bassi et al., 2008; Dubois et al., 2008; Groppo et al., 2014). Language abilities showed distributed and partially dissociable WM correlates involving the arcuate fasciculus and uncinate fasciculus. Receptive language was primarily associated with RD and AD across multiple segments of these tracts, particularly within temporoparietal and frontoparietal pathways. This pattern suggests that microstructural properties related to axonal caliber, packing density, or myelination may be especially relevant for receptive language processing during this developmental period (Sket et al., 2019). In contrast, expressive language demonstrated more widespread associations with FA across several arcuate fasciculus segments, along with significant AD effects in the uncinate fasciculus (Salvan et al., 2017). These findings indicate that the organization and coherence of long-range frontotemporal and frontoparietal connections may play a more prominent role in expressive language abilities. The differential diffusion profiles observed for receptive and expressive language support the concept that these language domains rely on overlapping but distinct neural substrates even in early life. Early learning outcomes, as indexed by the ELC, were strongly associated with microstructural properties of multiple corpus callosum subregions. Increased FA and AD, together with reduced RD in posterior callosal regions, suggest enhanced interhemispheric connectivity, potentially reflecting advanced axonal organization and myelination. The prominent involvement of parietal and posterior callosal regions aligns with the multisensory integration and higher-order processing demands of early learning. Efficient interhemispheric communication likely facilitates the integration of sensory, motor, and cognitive information, thereby supporting more complex learning behaviors during infancy (O'Muircheartaigh et al., 2014). Several methodological considerations should be noted when interpreting these findings. Although this study benefited from advanced diffusion imaging techniques, standardized cognitive assessments, and a rigorous analytical framework, the relatively small sample size may limit generalizability. Additionally, tractography in infants remains technically challenging due to low anisotropy and immature WM. Despite the use of an atlas-guided and conservative tractography approach to enhance anatomical plausibility, reconstructed tracts should be interpreted with caution. Future longitudinal studies incorporating larger cohorts and advanced diffusion models may further clarify developmental trajectories and strengthen the reliability of infant WM analyses.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that early cognitive development is supported by domain-specific and network-level maturation of WM pathways. Diffusion metrics revealed tract- and region-specific patterns across motor, visual, language, and early learning domains, highlighting that different aspects of WM microstructure contribute differentially to early neurodevelopment. The complementary information provided by FA, RD, and AD underscores the importance of a multi-metric approach when examining structure–function relationships in the developing brain and supports the potential utility of diffusion imaging as a noninvasive tool for early developmental assessment.
