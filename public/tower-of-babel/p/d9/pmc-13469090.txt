Modulation of intra-oceanic trench bending and along-trench thermochemical transport by mantle toroidal flow
Authors: Liang Liu, Zebin Cao, Jason P Morgan, Lijun Liu, Shi-Mai Long, Yuan-Yuan Hua, Hong-Yan Li, Yi-Gang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOceanic trench bending over 1000 km occurs globally, with notable along-trench variations in mantle wedge thermochemistry. However, the mechanisms influencing both shallow trench geometry and deep mantle state remain unclear. Here we focus on the Mariana Trench as a representative case. Our 3D model successfully replicates its curved geometry. Modeling results further reveal that extensive intra-oceanic trench retreat and bending occur when the incoming plate features sharp density variations along the trench accompanied by a large buoyant segment (e.g., the Caroline Plate). In this scenario, significant dynamic pressure fluctuations develop in the mantle wedge, generating vigorous toroidal flow extending >1000 km from the buoyant segment, even under non-linear mantle rheology. The model-predicted toroidal flow transports warm mantle materials, reshaping local topography, rejuvenate the mantle wedge, and heating the slab surface to facilitate volatile release. These experiments highlight toroidal flow as a key regular of both large-scale trench bending and along-trench thermochemical variations.Subject terms: Geodynamics, Geophysics

Introduction

An oceanic trench is referred to as the tectonic boundary between a subducting oceanic plate and its overriding upper plate; its evolution appears to be largely controlled by the interactions between the upper and lower plates1–5. In previous studies, an increase in the buoyancy of the subducting plate was shown to potentially induce trench advance, enhance upper-plate compression, and/or dampen arc magmatism2,6–9, while a decrease in buoyancy could cause trench retreat, back-arc extension, and/or magmatic flare-up3–5,10. Apart from a few abrupt trench jump events inferred from plate tectonic reconstructions2,8,11,12, a buoyant continental upper plate was often suggested to preferentially couple with its lower plate, favoring a stationary or advancing trench position13–15, but has also been proposed to enhance trench retreat, as the upper plate collapses towards the subducting plate due to the local gradient in gravitational potential energy16. Along a finite length of a trench where the properties of the upper and lower plates barely change, the trench geometry is therefore anticipated to remain straight, with only occasional motions perpendicular to the strike2. However, for most sufficiently long subduction systems, the motion perpendicular to the strike rarely maintains a consistent pace along the entire trench, leading to the formation of either local or regional trench curvature with an arc-like form.

Many curved oceanic trenches are observed worldwide, ranging from mildly crescent-like to U or V-like, and even severely folded S/Z-like shapes1,3,17,18. As noted above, the along-trench inhomogeneity of the upper and lower plates’ buoyancy may contribute to trench bending, for example, due to the lower plate having varying thermal ages or carrying harder-to-subduct buoyant fragments2,12,19–21; or because the nature of the upper plate changes along the trench, e.g., from thicker to thinner lithosphere13–15,22. However, previous field-work-based paleo-reconstruction studies have generally implied a finite contribution from these factors due to their limited scale relative to the curved tectonic boundary3. Instead, continuous large-scale slab rollback and the induced plate rotation could be primarily responsible for (paleo-)trench bending3,17,23. Nevertheless, many published analog and numerical models have shown that without a prominent effect from the surrounding plates, large-scale intra-oceanic slab rollback mainly develops in scenarios where the subducting plate is only a few hundred kilometers wide24,25, such as when it is surrounded by continents as in the modern Mediterranean26,27, or when it is confined by buoyant fragments in the incoming plate28–30. In addition, prior numerical models indicate that a stationary or advancing trench position tends to develop in the central segment of an intra-oceanic subduction system where the surrounding plates barely affect the subduction evolution13,31. Thus, unless the subducting plate is confined to a narrow width, torn apart, or severely compressed by surrounding plates, large-scale slab rollback is unlikely to be sustained for long enough in a broad intra-oceanic subduction system, thereby preventing the development of a strongly curved trench geometry. Notably, in the aforementioned numerical models, toroidal flow—crucial for lithospheric motion32,33 and the near-subduction-zone flow field24,34–36—can effectively develop around a narrow, not a broad, subducting slab, and has been identified as an important factor driving local slab rollback. Overall, the specific role of along-trench inhomogeneities of the subducting plate(s) in inducing large-scale trench bending remains controversial. Their relationship with the slab rollback phenomenon, as proposed in geological studies, and the mantle toroidal flow suggested by modeling research as a potential driver of slab bending, still requires clarification through natural examples and more in-depth numerical investigations.

Among natural examples, the Mariana Trench represents one of the youngest regions for studying intra-oceanic trench bending phenomena37–40. The Mariana Trench forms part of the larger Izu-Bonin-Mariana (IBM) subduction system, which developed following the Cenozoic subduction initiation of the Pacific Plate41, and which now marks the tectonic boundary between the Pacific and Philippine plates (Fig. 1a). It is truncated by the subduction of the Ogasawara and Caroline Plateaus in the north and south, that separate it from the Izu-Bonin and Yap Trenches (Fig. 1a). Although its initial geometry is typically reconstructed as being straight40,42,43 (e.g., as traced by the Kyushu-Paula Ridge and Western Mariana Ridge), it currently displays an asymmetric U-shaped geometry, with the central axis pointing southeast (Fig. 1a). The evolution of the Mariana Trench has been linked to the opening of the western Philippine Sea Basin, followed by the formation of the Parece-Vela back-arc basin and the Mariana Trough, which represent three distinct episodes of regional upper-plate extension44 (Fig. 1c). Today, both the physical properties and the kinematics of the subducting plate vary significantly along the trench. For example, in addition to the two previously-noted subducting oceanic plateaus, the subducting plate abruptly transitions from the older Pacific Plate to the younger (36–25 Ma) Caroline Plate at roughly its southern edge44–46. The convergence rate decreases from ~4 cm/yr at the northern edge to ~2 cm/yr at the south47 (Fig. 1d). Many studies also identify a trench advance period for after ~10–5 Ma, with a current advance rate of ~0.7–3 cm/yr31,47,48 (Fig. 1d).

The present-day depths of the seafloor, Moho, and slab tip, and the slab materials’ contribution to the origins of magmatism and earthquakes, also exhibit systematic changes along the Mariana Trench. For example, the seafloor depth rapidly decreases while the Moho depth only mildly increases in the southern Mariana Trough (Fig. 1e). Based on seismological studies, the current Mariana slab largely maintains a vertical geometry in the upper mantle (Fig. 1a). To the south, the slab depth appears to decrease, reaching ~50–100 km below the Yap Trench (Fig. 1a). Although the slab tip becomes shallow beneath southern Mariana (Fig. 1a), the contribution of slab components to the source of modern arc lavas has been found to be elevated, whereas a similar feature is only locally identified at ~22˚N in the northern portion38 (Fig. 1f). Between ~15–5 Ma, the contribution of a slab component to arc magma formation may have been even higher than it is today37 (Fig. 1c). Similar variations in magma composition and distribution along broad and curved trenches have been reported in other long-term subduction systems, such as at southern Tibetan and Eastern Mediterranean-Anatolia region49,50, potentially also linked to the along-trench inhomogeneities of the previously subducting plates2,6. Additionally, with a roughly similar distribution pattern, the frequency of intermediate-depth earthquakes is abruptly enhanced in the southern portion51, with a mild elevation at ~22˚N (Fig. 1g).

Regarding the formation mechanism of the U-shaped Mariana Trench, previous studies typically emphasized the contribution of the incoming plate, as the upper young and thin Philippine Plate appears to have remained relatively unchanged40,42,52–54. Specifically, the “pinning” of the Caroline and Ogasawara Plateaus was proposed to have contributed to the bending of the Mariana Trench40,44,53–55. The initial collision of the Caroline Plateau with Mariana occurred around ~24–13 Ma44, while that of the Ogasawara Plateau (and its predecessors) appears to have started at~8–1 Ma40. In numerical and analog models, slab rollback can develop spontaneously in narrow subducting plates, and the resulting toroidal flow appears to facilitate trench bending24,34,56, although how well these models can be applied to scenarios involving the broader Pacific Plate remains to be explored.

In addition to trench-bending phenomena, previous literature has discussed potential reasons for the along-trench thermochemical variations. For instance, back-arc extension events may have remobilized the mantle wedge to shallower depths38, i.e., decreasing the depth at which mechanical coupling of the upper and lower plates occurs57, thereby facilitating slab component release and influencing magma compositions. The spatially uneven release of slab materials within the warmer regions of the mantle wedge could also explain the distribution pattern of earthquakes along the trench58. However, in general, the formation of the observed trench curvature and along-trench variations has generally been treated as separate issues.

In this study, by modeling the formation of a Mariana-like intra-oceanic trench using a 3D forward modeling method, we reveal that: 1) large-scale slab rollback and trench retreat can rarely develop spontaneously during the prolonged subduction of a broad oceanic plate, especially in the central segment away from the slab edge; 2) the presence of a large buoyant tectonic block adjacent to the subducting segment can induce active toroidal flow around the subducted slab, sustaining regional slab rollback and causing the trench to adopt a > 1000 km wide curved geometry; and 3) induced toroidal flow can cause along-trench asthenosphere transport and “rejuvenate” the mantle wedge, thereby inducing or enhancing arc magmatism and earthquakes, e.g., through improved slab dehydration.

Results

Numerical modeling of oceanic trench bending

We used an open-source version of CitcomS59–61 to investigate the mechanism controlling the formation of a Mariana-like trench curvature. In previous studies, this method has been employed to study the processes of oceanic subduction and continental collision62–64. More details of the model setup and parameters can be found in the Methods section and supplementary materials.

We considered the effects of the along-trench buoyancy inhomogeneity of the incoming plate from two perspectives (Fig. 2): 1) adding one or two oceanic plateaus, namely the Caroline and/or Ogasawara Plateaus; and 2) placing a buoyant tectonic block adjacent to the fast-subducting segment—i.e., the Caroline Plate on the side of the Pacific—and testing the influence of its size on trench dynamics. We also tentatively explored the effects of the Ogasawara Plateau’s timing of arrival and the proposed rotation of the incoming plate on trench bending (a summary of the sensitivity tests is in the Methods section).

Models without a large buoyant block within the incoming plate

At the beginning of each model, the preexisting slab segment rapidly separates from the incoming plate, enabling direct contact between the mantle domains on its sides (see Method) (e.g., Fig. 3 and S1-S2). After this, thermally-induced mantle convection promotes a new subduction event at ~10 Myr concurrently along the entire trench (Fig. 3 and S2d), like that currently observed in the IBM region41. When there is no buoyant fragment in the incoming/lower plate (i.e., neither the oceanic plateaus nor a large buoyant block), the subducted slab will maintain a high dip angle throughout the model (Fig. 3 and S1-S2).

As in previous 2D models, the convergence rate between the upper and lower plates can reach >8 cm/yr65 (Fig. S1f). In general, the trench can locally develop small-scale trench advance or retreat within a < 500 km wide segment, with the vertical distance from the curvature apex to the strike typically <200 km, consistent with previous 3D models (Fig. 4a and S1)24,34,66. In later stages of the model run, as the subducting plate ages and strengthens (see Method), flattening of the subduction angle and trench advance will develop, especially in the central portion of the subduction zone, consistent with findings from previous studies13,24,31 (Fig. 4a and S2b). Meanwhile, trench retreat occurs near the edge of the incoming plate (Fig. 4a and S1g)24. Therefore, a long-lasting, broad and homogeneous subduction zone does not appear to spontaneously develop a footwall-directed curved trench like Mariana (Fig. 4c).

In models in which the two buoyant oceanic plateaus (Fig. 1a) are considered, the trench remains with a straight strike until the arrival of the plateaus at the trench (Fig. 4b and S3). Plateau subduction induces local trench advance, with the bending extent corresponding to the contact width between the plateau and the upper plate (Fig. 4b and S3k). Because we assumed an inclined strike for the Oceanic Plateau 1 (Fig. 2), i.e., the “Caroline Plateau”, its contact width with the upper plate varies over time, and the central axis of the curvature rotates anticlockwise (Fig. 4b). The induced curvature dissipates rapidly after the plateau is fully subducted (Fig. 4b and S3n). During the period when both plateaus are subducting, a mildly crescent-shaped curvature can form in between them (Fig. 4b and S3j), but this is insufficient to reproduce the geometry of the Mariana Trench (Fig. 4c). Notably, unlike observations (Fig. 1a), the Caroline Plateau alone does not generate enough trench bending to mimic the prominent advance of the Yap Trench and/or Apu Trough (Fig. 4b and S3l).

In the models with buoyant oceanic plateaus, the arrival of a plateau at the trench induces slab tearing beneath it, creating a “hole” within the slab (Fig. 5)28,39. By analyzing the mantle flow pattern, we find that the surrounding mantle can pass through the slab tear and join the corner flow, predominantly sinking downward (Fig. 5).

Models with a large buoyant block within the incoming plate

In addition to the two oceanic plateaus, the Mariana trench is also adjacent to the younger and warmer Caroline plate to its south44,46 (Fig. 1a). The Caroline plate is surrounded by the Ontong Java Plateau with a mantle-plume-origin67 and New Guinea Island with a continental affiliation68. Therefore, all three tectonic plates are more buoyant than the Pacific Plate due to their hotter geotherm and/or thicker crust and depleted lithospheric mantle. For simplicity, we treated the three tectonic blocks as a single entity and tested the effect on trench bending by varying their along-trench width.

Based on the modeling results, models with a buoyant adjacent block wider than 1200 km can more easily reproduce the observed Mariana Trench geometry (Fig. 6a–c vs. 1a). As the plate width decreases, the distance between the curvature apex and the trench segments on either side gradually diminishes, and the central axis of the curvature shifts toward the north (Fig. 6a–c), contrary to observations at Mariana (Figs. 1a, 4c). In the model where the buoyant block is ~600 km wide, the induced trench bending closely resembles that in Run 2 (Fig. 6d vs. 4b). We also note that, in models with a narrow buoyant plate, significant trench retreat occurs in the area south of the buoyant block (Figs. 4b and 6d), but the trench segment between the two plateaus does not replicate Mariana’s geometry (Fig. 6d vs. 1a).

Discussion

Development of toroidal flow and curved intra-oceanic trench geometry

Based on previous studies of paleogeographic reconstruction, continuous slab rollback has been considered to be responsible for severely curved (paleo-)trenches, with the trench width prior to folding potentially reaching thousands of kilometers3,17. However, according to published modeling studies and this study, a broad subducting plate alone does not tend to spontaneously develop large-scale, persistent slab rollback in the central portion of an intra-oceanic subduction zone like that in Mariana24,25,34 (Figs. S1–S2). Instead, without fundamental effects from the surrounding plates, as the subduction process continues, particularly if the subducting plate becomes old and strong in the late stage, like the western Pacific (Fig. 1a), trench advance can occur more readily31 (Fig. S2b). Therefore, although slab rollback is undoubtedly a key process explaining slab curvature, the specific mechanisms driving it in scenarios involving a broad subducting plate require further clarification. As noted in the Introduction, the specific contribution of the upper plate to trench bending appears to vary among different natural examples and has been well explored in previous studies13–16. Here, by focusing on the scenario of the Mariana trench bending where the upper plate exerts a relatively limited role (Fig. 1), we aim to better reveal the role of along-trench inhomogeneities of a subducting plate with negligible vertical tearing on slab rollback and trench bending.

According to our models, the previously emphasized two oceanic plateaus40,44 can locally bend the trench (Fig. 4b). However, in the models explored here they do not induce large-scale slab rollback in between the plateaus (Fig. 4b and S3). Earlier numerical models proposed that if the along-trench property of the subducting plate rapidly transitions from buoyant to dense, then the buoyancy difference between the two segments could cause a vertical and complete slab tear, after which slab rollback would quickly develop in the denser portion29,36,69. This would occur because slab tearing creates a narrow subducting block, allowing transient toroidal flow to develop near the new slab edge within a few hundred kilometers34–36 and so push it away from the trench24. However, since we did not assume a weak boundary between buoyant fragments and the surrounding oceanic portion, after their arrival at the trench, slab tearing can only occur locally, as apparently observed39; this does not permit the formation of strong toroidal flow and continuous slab rollback (Fig. 5).

In contrast, in models where a broad buoyant block south of the Pacific (i.e., the present-day Caroline, Ontong Java, and New Guinea blocks) is included, the buoyant slab tip can only reach depths of 10 s to 200 km (Fig. 1a), ~50 Myr after the initiation of new subduction (Fig. 7a, b). In this scenario, the dynamic pressure within the background mantle beneath the buoyant block can be >60 MPa higher than that above the dense slab (Fig. 7a, b), driving the formation of vigorous toroidal flow of shallow upper mantle that pushes the subducted slab away from the trench (Fig. 7e, f, i, j). More specifically, after subduction initiation, the developing corner flow first causes a negative dynamic pressure in the mantle wedge portion above the dense slab (Fig. S15d)70. The magnitude and distribution of the dynamic pressure in our models is comparable to that in previous studies (Figs. S15-S16)71. As the curved slab geometry develops, the extent of the negative dynamic pressure also expands, filling the mantle wedge portion above the curved slab portion (Figs. S15e–g, S16). Because the incoming materials are mainly located in areas below the buoyant block (i.e., the Caroline plate), deeper depths than the slab, after bypassing the slab edge, they quickly develop an upward velocity component toward the shallower negative pressure area (Fig. 7 and S18). The magnitude of the dynamic pressure does not diminish over time but rather increases (Figs. S15g, h, S16), continuously driving toroidal flow toward the curved slab area and potentially explaining the up to ~1000 km distribution of the toroidal flow in the models with a non-linear rheology34,35. Therefore, when compared to a short-lived slab tearing event29,72, the far-field effect of the broad buoyant block within the incoming plate can create long-lasting toroidal flow within the mantle that drives long-lived regional trench bending (Fig. 8).

Comparison between numerical modeling and Mariana tectonic evolution

In addition to aligning with previous numerical and analog models, our model can also help explain the tectonic evolution of the Mariana region and its surroundings from the onset of Cenozoic subduction. For example, three major trench retreat episodes are identifiable in the central portion of the curved trench (Fig. 8), i.e., Mariana. The first episode coincides with the initiation of a new subduction process (Fig. S4d, e), and may partially contribute to the rapid expansion of the Philippine Sea Basin (i.e., the expansion component perpendicular to the trench) and the Palau-Kyushu magmatism (Fig. 1c); the second results from the strengthening of the toroidal flow around the dense slab, which leads to continuous slab rollback (Figs. S18c–S18e), contributing to Parece-Vela back-arc extension and Western Mariana Ridge magmatism (Fig. 1c); the third episode is primarily caused by the collision of the Caroline Plateau with the trench (Figs. S4j–S4k), which locally enhances toroidal flow and regional slab rollback (Figs. S18f–S18g), so potentially explaining the growth of the Mariana Trough and its decreasing width toward the north, and along-strike variations in arc magmatism (Fig. 1c). Note that the individual extensional episodes from the model are defined as periods when the extension rates obviously increase (Fig. 8b). Given that the trench is also continually retreating (Fig. 8a), these transient episodes only account for a portion of the observed amounts of ~east-west backarc opening of the Parece Vela basin (<100 km in the model vs. 600–900 km in nature) and Mariana Trough (~50–100 km in the model vs. ~160–220 km in nature).

In our models, the presence of a buoyant block to the south of the Pacific Plate explains the limited volume of the subducted slab beneath the southern Mariana region and the Yap Trench (Fig. 1a). The models also predict an overall trench advance in the late stage, as the Pacific Plate becomes older and stronger (Fig. 8b vs. 1c). Additionally, the subduction of the dense Pacific Plate contributes to the apparent trench advance in the buoyant block region (Fig. 8a), supporting the previous hypothesis that the subduction of surrounding oceanic plates can drive the convergence of a broad buoyant plate toward the upper plate2,6,8.

As shown in Fig. 1e, in the southern Mariana trough, the seafloor depth deepens by ~1.5 km at ~14.2˚N, while the Moho depth increases by <1 km. Theoretically, a 1 km increase in crustal thickness can result in ~130 m seafloor elevation (e.g., Δdepth=Δcrustalthicknese×(ρmantle−ρcrust)/ρcrust70, where ρmantle = 3390 kg/m3 and ρcrust = 3000 kg/m3). Therefore, additional factors must have contributed to the observed seafloor depth variations in this region. Based on the modeling results, the toroidal flow primarily develops in regions near the southern part of the curved trench segment, while away from this area, poloidal flow becomes dominant (Fig. 7 and S18). This pattern mimics the current flow characteristics of the upper mantle as revealed by seismic anisotropy73,74. Consequently, the along-trench transport of the shallow upper mantle materials may have led to the thickening of the asthenosphere75, providing additional support to the elevated topography in the southern Mariana Trough (Fig. 1e).

Along-trench variations of the upper mantle’s thermochemical state

As noted above, a “two-dimensional” mechanism, i.e., the steepening of the subduction angle during slab rollback, which enhances upward corner flow to warm the mantle wedge and slab surface, was proposed to remobilize the mantle wedge and facilitate slab component release in Mariana37,38. In this region, a rapid slab rollback event can occur immediately after subduction initiation but cannot be sustained long enough to account for the recent mantle wedge conditions in Mariana (Fig. 8b)65,76,77. Previous studies also emphasized the role of the Western Pacific Seamount Province (WPSP), whose arrival could slow subduction rates and cause the subduction angle to steepen. However, compared to large oceanic plateaus, such as Caroline and Ogasawara, the WPSP does not seem buoyant enough to significantly slow down subduction42. Even though it may have been subducted, the WPSP likely mainly causes localized trench advance, subduction angle flattening, and slab tearing—as predicted by our model (Figs. 4–5)—rather than inducing slab rollback.

Slab tearing has also been regarded as an important mechanism for mantle wedge remobilization and arc magmatism flare-up49,78. However, after the localized slab tearing in the scenario of fast steep subduction (e.g., Fig. S5), although the background mantle behind the slab can penetrate the tear, the mantle flow primarily joins the fast corner flow and sinks downward (Fig. 5), supporting the theoretical analysis in previous research79. This phenomenon remains consistent in scenarios where the tearing occurs at depths shallower or deeper than 200 km (Fig. 5). The only situation in which mantle flow through the tear can migrate upward occurs in models with observable trench bending, but the flow acts more like a small-scale toroidal flow (Fig. 5e, f and S6). In this case, the negative pressure beneath the curved trench segment is mainly responsible for the formation of toroidal flow (e.g., Fig. 7a), and the resulting upward flow component can mildly warm the surrounding slab (Fig. 9e). Therefore, our modeling results suggest that localized slab tearing may not be sufficient to mobilize the Mariana’s mantle wedge broadly; although active magmatism has been noted to occur during the a large scale slab tear event49, especially when mantle materials from the backside of the slab are buoyant enough to overcome the downward corner flow79,80 or the upper plate rapidly drafts toward the trench like in the case of North America78.

Alternatively, our modeling results may reveal a critical role of a “three-dimensional” toroidal-flow mechanism in modulating the along-trench variations of Mariana’s upper mantle state. Specifically, the toroidal flow exhibits a more prominent upward component compared to localized slab tearing, consistent with previous studies27 (Fig. 7i–k and S18). It can continuously transport warm (and sometimes geochemically distinct81) asthenospheric mantle from the sides and back of the slab (Fig. 7). As a result, the slab portion near the southern edge can be >200 ˚C hotter than the inner part, promoting slab fluid release (Fig. 9b, c). As noted above, the enhanced contribution from slab materials has also been identified as a key factor in explaining the current magma composition and the origin of intermedia-depth earthquakes in this region (Fig. 1c, f, g)35,38,58. Furthermore, the vigorous toroidal flow responsible for the Parece-Vela extension (Fig. 8b and S18c–e) could also have contributed to the elevated slab component release observed in tephra geochemistry between ~15–5 Myr (Fig. 1c)35,37. Locally, especially near the Ogasawara Plateau, slab tearing could also play a similar role (Fig. 5e, f).

Model limitations

Currently, there are some limitations in these models, particularly regarding how to properly assess toroidal-flow-related magmatism. One shortcoming is the absence of sophisticated melting modeling; for simplicity, we primarily compare the slab temperature evolution with phase diagrams of potential slab lithologies (Fig. 9). This omission constrains the model’s ability to clarify distinct melting regimes linked to spatial variations in lithologies or slab bending-linked hydration. Furthermore, due to the scarcity of submarine samples in the study region, our study should be interpreted primarily as a framework for comparing modeling results with the overarching trends in magmatism (Fig. 1), rather than offering a detailed analysis of more specific implications, such as magmatic geochemistry.

In the modeling domain, the proximity of model boundaries to both plate edges and slab edges may influence the internal flow field. A larger regional model with less artificial side boundary conditions (e.g., Morgan et al., 202082) would allow freer “natural” regional flow to the current trench edges. To mitigate such influences, we adapted buffer zones (Fig. 2) with low viscosity near all vertical boundaries to accommodate the possible return flow from the vertical walls. This technique has been widely used in regional three-dimensional models63,83 and results showed that this technique can minimize the wall effects on the internal flow field, particularly in the center part of the model. This study focused on the trench bending phenomena in the central portion of a broad subduction system, which occurred in the central part of our model domain, so the wall effects on the bending phenomena could be neglected, while they may minimally impact the flow near the southern edge of the subducting slab.

For simplicity, we use a single buoyant block to represent the Caroline Plate, Ontong Java Plateau, and/or the New Guinea Island (Fig. 1a). This simplified initial condition is enough for the model to reproduce the advanced position of the Yap trench and Ayu Trough and the southward shift of the central axis of the Mariana curvature (Fig. 1a vs. Figs. 7 and 8a). Note that the Caroline Plate has been found to have formed after Cenozoic subduction initiation44,46. Prior to that, this region may have been largely continental or buoyant-arc-affiliated52,84, which would again imply a higher buoyancy than the old Pacific Plate. Therefore, this simplified model setup should be treated as a relatively crude approximation, with the buoyant block acting more like a boundary condition. The model is not expected to study the detailed evolution of the Yap and Palau regions, where more detailed initial conditions would be needed.

Relative rotation between the upper and lower plates does not occur in our models, in part because the regional model is not large enough to include other subduction zones or plume-lithosphere interaction centers far from the research region85,86. While previous studies have suggested that Pacific Plate rotation may play a significant role in the formation of the overall Izu-Bonin-Mariana geometry40, this potential factor shaping Mariana Trench bending is not assessed here. Note that the rotations of the Philippine and Pacific plates appear to be coupled40,42, which implies that far-field boundary forces may not be the primary drivers of U-shaped trench curvature, e.g., the initial strike of the “Caroline Plateau” is not the major control on the final trench geometry (Fig. S14m vs. S3m). Nevertheless, when modeling more complex folded V- or Z-shaped trench bending, the rotation of the trench segment around a buoyant block should not be neglected3,30.

Additionally, we note that the model-predicted trench advance rates only match the lower values inferred from previous plate reconstructions (Fig. 8b vs. 1d). This discrepancy may primarily result from the omission of upper plate subduction31,47,87. According to previous modeling studies, when two subduction systems with similar polarities are sufficiently close, the one closer to the hanging wall can induce a trench advance in the latter47,87. However, we agree with previous work that the influence of Philippine subduction is likely to mainly enhance the overall rate of trench advance rather than inducing Mariana trench retreat31,47,87. Considering that the estimated trench advance rate varies significantly between previous studies88, here we chose to assign less weight to the Mariana trench advance in evaluating the potential viability of a numerical model.

In summary, 3D forward models were used to investigate the formation of Mariana-like trench curvature. In contrast to the previously predicted relatively localized scale of toroidal flow in numerical models with a non-linear rheology (hundreds of kilometers wide to the slab edge), the presence of a large buoyant segment within the subducting plate, along with the resulting dynamic pressure fluctuations in the mantle wedge, can extend the distribution of active toroidal flow to >1000 km from the segment boundary (Fig. 7). This large-scale toroidal flow, in turn, shapes local topography and leads to continuous and extensive trench bending (Fig. 1f, g). Our numerical models imply that the contribution of local slab tears to arc magmatism may sometimes be overestimated. In scenarios involving broad and fast oceanic subduction, the mantle flow across the local slab tear largely merges with the surrounding corner flow and descends (Fig. 10a). Only when the buoyancy of the incoming plate changes sharply along the trench and a slab tear occurs within the dense slab portion can the negative dynamic pressure in the mantle wedge draw mantle materials upward across the tear, potentially contributing to enhanced arc magmatism (Fig. 10b). Alternatively, the model-predicted toroidal flow demonstrates a more significant upward component in comparison to flow arising from localized slab tearing, thereby more effectively heating the adjacent slab and promoting the release of slab fluids and melts, e.g., inducing or enhancing arc magmatism (Figs. 1f and 10b) and subduction zone earthquakes (Fig. 1g). Consequently, our model results suggest that the 4D shallow-deep-coupling role of mantle toroidal flow may be underestimated when interpreting along-trench time-space variations of topography, trench curvature, magmatic and earthquake distribution, and magma-source geochemistry, as these have often previously been attributed solely to local fluctuations in the properties of the slab and/or upper plate.

Mantle flow patterns in purely oceanic subduction and composite subduction (modified after Magni et al., 202535).

a Poloidal flow dominates the mantle flow pattern in scenarios of broad and fast oceanic subduction, without prominent along-trench buoyancy variations in the incoming plate (Figs. S1-S2). Even though the arrival of small buoyant fragments at the trench can cause local slab tearing, the mantle flow across the tear largely joins the surrounding corner flow and sinks downward (Fig. 5a–d). b When the buoyancy of the incoming plate sharply changes along the trench—e.g., from old to young oceanic block—toroidal flow can develop actively from the boundary between the different segments (Fig. 7 and S18). The induced toroidal flow further drives continuous trench bending, heating mantle wedge and slab surface from the segment boundary, causing along-trench migrations of asthenosphere and magmatism (Fig. 9b, c). Additionally, when small-scale slab tearing occurs, the negative dynamic pressure within the mantle wedge beneath the curved trench can also draw mantle materials across the tear upward locally (Figs. 5f and 7).

Methods

3D forward modeling

We modeled the convective mantle and lithosphere as a highly viscous fluid under the Boussinesq approximation. The non-dimensionalized conservation equations of mass, momentum, and energy were numerically solved by the three-dimensional (3D) spherical finite element code CitcomS59–61. In all numerical models, the potential vigor of thermal convection was controlled by the dimensionless Rayleigh number, Ra = (αρ0ΔTRE3)/(κη0), where α is the thermal expansivity, ρ0 is the reference density, ΔT is the temperature difference across the mantle, RE is Earth’s radius, κ is the thermal diffusivity, and η0 is the reference viscosity. We set the Raleigh number at 5.16e8 for all models, and details of model parameters are given in Supplementary Table S1. The model domain spans 80˚ × 60˚ × 2870 km, discretized into 256 × 512 × 128 elements. The element size varies spatially, with the finest resolution being 34 × 11 × 7.5 km in the central portion of the model above ~400 km depth, and the coarsest resolution is 34 × 45 × 30 km near the 5˚ buffer zone close to the domain edge. In each element, 26 Lagrangian tracers are embedded. The number of tracers within each element is monitored at every time step to ensure it remains no less than 26. A similar adaptive tracer adding/removing strategy was used in Liu et al., 2018a&b89,90.

The composition field is defined using Lagrangian tracers. Following Liu et al. (2024)65, the upper plate consists of two portions: a “craton” portion with a 200 km thick lithosphere and a continental margin portion with a 100 km thick lithosphere, with the upper-plate crust thickness set at 35 km. Based on previous studies84,93, we assumed a buoyant upper plate (Table S1) to facilitate the development of a new subduction zone. This setup also helps prevent significant entrainment of upper-plate materials into the subduction94 (Fig. 3 and S2), allowing a clearer evaluation of the effects of the lower plate and mantle flow on trench bending. The lower plate (incoming plate) is primarily composed of oceanic lithosphere (i.e., the Pacific Plate), with/without Oceanic Plateaus 1-2 (i.e., Caroline and Ogasawara Plateaus, respectively), or a buoyant block located south of the oceanic one (i.e., the Caroline Plate and its surrounding buoyant tectonic blocks, such as Ontong Java and New Guinea), all separated from a pre-existing 600 km long subducted oceanic slab (Fig. 2). Generally, the seafloor age of the lower plate increases gradually from 10 Myr at the trench to 80 Myr65, and its thickness and thermal profile follows a half-space cooling model70 (Fig. 2e). The lower plate contains a crust approximately 10 km thick, except for the two oceanic plateaus. Oceanic Plateau 1 (3˚ wide × 10˚ long) has a crust thickness of 20 km95, while Oceanic Plateau 2 (3˚ wide × 6˚ long) has a crust thickness of 15 km96. The lithospheric mantle of the buoyant block and the oceanic plateaus in some models (Tables S1–S2) is about 100 kg/m³ more buoyant than the oceanic lithospheric mantle, e.g., due to a high degree of melt extraction97,98. In Runs 2–3 and 11, Oceanic Plateaus 1 and 2 are initially located 10˚ and 25˚ away from the trench, respectively, while in Runs 4–8 and 10, Oceanic Plateau 2 (i.e., Ogasawara Plateau) is positioned 2˚ farther (Table S2).

Following a previous study, we assume the upper mantle beneath the upper plate of a long-lasting subduction zone like the western Pacific is cooler than the portion beneath the subducting plate65. This temperature difference between the mantle regions on either side of the preexisting subducted slab can drive the overriding plate’s convergence and initiate a new subduction process when a previous one ceases (Figs. S1-S2)65,76. With this assumption, we do not (and need not) impose additional velocity or stress boundary conditions to allow subduction to occur naturally. In the numerical models, the cooler upper mantle portion gradually descends into the lower mantle when the preexisting “Izanagi” slab detaches from the incoming “Pacific” plate, e.g., at least below the present-day Japanese Islands99,100. Then, pervasive subduction of the Pacific-Izanagi ridge and subsequent slab-breakoff could then trigger a shift to a more westward Pacific Plate motion and subduction in the Mariana region65,76. Based on previous studies of plate reconstruction, the ridge subduction event at ~52 Ma likely occurred mainly to the north of the future Mariana subduction zone99–101. Although there is disagreement among different models, the size of the proto-Philippine Sea Plate at ~52 Ma is relatively small40,99,101. Therefore, we did not set up a localized transform-fault-like plate boundary102 for simplicity. The foundering of the upper mantle above the western Pacific slabs has also been found necessary to explain the observed subduction angle in the western Pacific subduction zone103. In nature, initial horizontal compression between the original western Pacific plate and its proto-hanging wall, which can initiate initiation, may also be due to the “mantle wind” formed due to the long-lasting paleo-Pacific or even Tethys subduction104–106, which could act as a driving mechanism similar to the one assumed here.

More sensitivity tests

In models with the buoyant adjacent block, we also examined the contribution of the Ogasawara and/or Caroline Plateaus to trench bending separately (Fig. 6e, g and S11–S13). Based on the model results, without incorporating a buoyant lithospheric mantle—by only assigning a thick crust to the oceanic plateaus—the localized trench advance could still be reproduced (Fig. 6g vs. 1a). Regarding the overall formation of the U-shaped feature, the two oceanic plateaus do not play a key role except that a Caroline Plateau with a buoyant lithospheric mantle seems to be critical to shift the central axis of the curvature towards the south (Fig. 6e–g vs. 6a). However, in models where the oceanic plateaus lack a buoyant underlying depleted lithospheric mantle, slab tearing cannot occur beneath the Ogasawara Plateau (Fig. S13), which is inconsistent with observations39,107.

We also tested the effect of the Ogasawara Plateau's arrival time. An earlier arrival—~1.5 Myr—better reproduces the localized bending toward the upper plate in this region (Fig. S3l vs. S7l), supporting the idea that the predecessor of this oceanic plateau could have been larger than its present scale40. The initial strike of the Caroline Plateau does not appear to affect the resulting trench geometry (Fig. S14m vs. S3m).

Calculation of the saturated water content in oceanic crust

We calculated the water capacity of oceanic crust (Fig. 9) with Perple_X108,109 as a function of temperature and pressure based on the average Pacific N-MORB composition (Table 3 in Gale et al., 2013)110. Major elements (MgO, SiO2, FeO, CaO, Na2O, Al2O3, TiO2, K2O, and MnO) are considered in the calculation. The solution models used in the calculation include Bio(HP), Chl(HP), Cpx(HP), Gt(WPH), Mica(CHA1), O(HP), GlTrTsPg, Pl(I1,HP), Opx(HP), San, IlHm(A), and Stlp.
