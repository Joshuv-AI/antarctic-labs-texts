Signaling Connectomics: A Brain-wide Framework for Intercellular Communication
Authors: Jongpil Shin, Hyeonsik Oh, Yeonji Jeong, Yungyeong Heo, Won Do Heo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding the brain requires mapping not only neuronal circuits but the full landscape of intercellular communication. Here we introduce “signaling connectomics”, a systems framework for interpreting divergent signaling context. It charts brain-wide signaling across synaptic, neuromodulatory, immune, glial, and vascular pathways. By integrating targeted perturbations with multiplexed molecular and imaging readouts, this approach infers causal signaling networks that operate beyond conventional synapses. It challenges circuit-centric models by emphasizing how dynamic receptor landscapes, extracellular cues, and non-synaptic interactions collectively shape neural function and dysfunction. Combining cell-type–specific optogenetics, biosensors, and spatial transcriptomics, signaling connectomics enables systematic mapping of intercellular signaling dynamics and cross-talk in vivo, providing a multidimensional view of how the brain coordinates activity across diverse cell types. Ultimately, this framework provides conceptual and methodological foundations for linking multicellular signaling to circuit-level plasticity and behavior via explicit causal chains.

INTRODUCTION

Cell-to-cell signaling has long provided the conceptual backbone for understanding biological regulation [1, 2]. Canonical pathways such as ligand binding, receptor activation, second-messenger cascades, and transcriptional or post-translational responses explain how cells encode, integrate, and act upon information [2]. In the nervous system, these pathways diversify across transmitter classes (fast amino acid transmitters, monoamines, neuropeptides, purines, gases, lipids) and receptor families (ionotropic and metabotropic), enabling both rapid synaptic transmission and slower, state-shaping modulation [3-5]. Yet despite this biochemical richness, most mechanistic accounts of brain function are still framed through the lens of circuits and synapses [6-8].

Circuit-based neuroscience has yielded transformative insights by linking anatomical wiring to computation and behavior [6, 9]. This paradigm excels at explaining millisecond-scale transformations of spiking activity within local motifs and long-range pathways [10, 11]. While action potentials unfold on millisecond scales, the downstream signaling that couples spiking to transcriptional programs and protein remodeling typically evolves over minutes to hours [12-15]. This intrinsic lag means that stable shifts in behavioral state often emerge on much slower timescales than the spiking events that initiate them [16-18]. Moreover, many brain phenomena unfold across spatiotemporal scales that extend far beyond point-to-point synapses [5, 19]. Contemporary connectomics predominantly catalogs neuron-to-neuron structural projections; it captures wiring but not the extra-synaptic signaling milieus that modulate and reconfigure those circuits [5-7].

Recent optogenetic advances enable causal and time-resolved control of intracellular signaling, including receptor tyrosine kinase, GPCR pathways, PI3K-Erk cascades, and Ca2+ dynamics [20-25]. By varying light duration and patterns, studies have shown that pulsed signaling enables subcellular modules to transduce millisecond-scale electrical events into transcriptional changes lasting minutes to hours [26-28].

Despite these advances, a key limitation remains: manipulations confined to a target cell type seldom reveal how perturbations propagate to neighboring cells and circuits [29, 30]. Non-cell-autonomous effects—via paracrine factors, extracellular vesicles, metabolic coupling, and glial or vascular intermediaries—can reshape network state in ways not captured by spike-centric readouts [5, 31-33]. Recent studies highlighting neuro–immune interactions underscore that brain activity is modulated by multi-cellular signaling axes rather than neurons alone [34-36]. These insights motivate a “signaling connectomics” program to systematically map intercellular communication—who signals to whom, over what distances and timescales, and with what causal impact on behavior and disease [5, 37, 38].

Spatial transcriptomics preserves tissue architecture while profiling thousands of genes, recovering cellular neighborhoods, boundaries, and gradients [39-41]. This spatial context enables inference of ligand-receptor interactions and niche effects, turning correlative cell catalogs into testable communication maps [40, 42, 43], and motivating a systematic “signaling connectomics” framework [44-46].

We therefore propose signaling connectomics: a brain-wide framework to map and model intercellular communication across synaptic, neuromodulatory, paracrine, immune, glial, and vascular modes (Fig. 1). Rather than an already standardized field term in neuroscience, we use the term here to define a conceptual framework. We distinguish it from molecular connectomics, which primarily integrates molecular identity and cellular morphology within anatomical circuits [6, 9], and from ligand-receptor interactome mapping [42] or cell-cell communication network analyses [45], which mainly infer candidate communication structures from transcriptomic data [42, 45].

By contrast, signaling connectomics is defined here as a brain-wide, state-dependent, and perturbation-coupled framework. It links inferred intercellular signaling relationships to temporal dynamics [12], causal propagation across cell types, circuit remodeling [18], and behavioral output in vivo [17]. Within this framework, the effective units of analysis span ligand–receptor pairs and their combinations; cell classes (neuronal and non-neuronal); and interfaces that support long-range broadcast and clearance (perivascular spaces and cerebrospinal fluid pathways). In this view, classical wiring diagrams remain essential constraints, complemented by state-conditioned maps of signaling capacity and flow.

FROM CIRCUITS TO SIGNALING NETWORKS

Traditional connectomics has mapped the brain’s wiring diagram with extraordinary structural detail from dense EM reconstructions to cubic-millimeter-scale mouse cortex volumes co-registered with in vivo calcium imaging, yet this architecture alone cannot explain how brain states emerge or change [6, 47, 48]. Signaling connectomics reframes the connectome as a dynamic, state-dependent communication network in which the effective connectivity between cells is defined by receptor–ligand availability and context, not merely by anatomical contact [42, 45, 49].

Beyond neuron-to-neuron communication, interactions between neurons and glial cells play a central role in shaping circuit architecture. Astrocytes and microglia actively monitor synaptic activity and regulate synapse formation and elimination through diverse signaling pathways. For example, complement-mediated signaling between neurons, astrocytes, and microglia has been shown to drive activity-dependent synapse pruning in Alzheimer's disease models, thereby dynamically reshaping circuit connectivity [50]. These findings illustrate how intercellular signaling among neuronal and glial populations can modulate circuit structure and function beyond the constraints of anatomical wiring.

Neuroimmune signaling provides another example of communication that extends beyond classical synaptic circuits. Cytokines and immune mediators released by microglia or infiltrating immune cells can alter neuronal excitability, synaptic plasticity, and network stability. Increasing evidence indicates that immune signaling pathways regulate neural activity and behavior across physiological and pathological contexts, including neurodegeneration and stress-related disorders [34, 35]. These observations highlight how immune-derived signals can propagate through neural circuits and reshape brain function through mechanisms that are largely invisible to traditional connectomic maps.

Temporal dynamics further distinguish signaling networks from synaptic circuits. While electrical transmission occurs within milliseconds, neuromodulatory, paracrine, or immune signals can persist for minutes to hours, integrating over time to reshape circuit excitability and gene expression [51, 52]. For instance, optogenetic activation of hippocampal FGFR1 engages a sequential FGFR1–Notch–BDNF signaling axis that induces neurogenesis and ameliorates depressive-like behavior, illustrating how signaling cascades bridge rapid events to day-scale outcomes [25]. Capturing these temporally extended interactions is essential for linking molecular communication to long-term behavioral outcomes.

Finally, signaling operates across multiple spatial scales. Local neuron-glia and glia-vascular loops interface with mesoscale neuromodulatory gradients and macroscale CSF/vascular pathways, forming a multilayered communication network that transcends conventional synaptic boundaries [53, 54]. Recognizing this multiscale organization positions signaling connectomics as a conceptual bridge between cellular physiology and systems neuroscience—an approach that views the brain not simply as a network of neurons, but as an evolving ecosystem of interacting cell types, each contributing to the brain’s adaptive computations.

FROM MOLECULAR PERTURBATION TO BEHAVIORAL OUTPUT

To operationalize signaling connectomics, we integrate perturbation and measurement and analysis layers into a closed perturb–profile–behavior loop that supports causal system identification across states. On the perturbation side, we employ three actuator classes for cell-type–specific, axis-level control: (i) light-programmable RTK/GPCR/PI3K–ERK modules [23, 25, 26, 55-57], (ii) Ca2+ dynamics controllers [22], and (iii) storage/release systems for proteins or mRNAs [58]. These actuators are programmable in amplitude, duration, duty cycle, and spatial pattern, enabling pulse-coded stimulation to probe transfer functions and kinetics under defined brain states. Genetic targeting and subcellular tags confine activation to specified cell types and compartments, reducing off-target effects and allowing compartment-resolved tests of causality. This optogenetic toolkit enables precise activation or inhibition of growth-factor and GPCR pathways across physiological and disease states, and when integrated with spatial transcriptomics, yields quantitative input–output relationships that link molecular perturbations to network-level consequences.

Multimodal readouts provide complementary resolution across molecular and cellular scales. Real-time biosensor imaging captures immediate second-messenger or kinase responses, while single-cell RNA sequencing, western blotting, and immunohistochemistry quantify downstream transcriptional and proteomic consequences [59]. Spatial transcriptomics preserves tissue context to infer and validate ligand-receptor interactions across regions, linking perturbations to molecular outcomes within intact tissue [44].

At the systems level, AI-assisted behavioral analytics, such as the AVATAR [60] and CLOSER [61] platform, can be coupled with optogenetic perturbations to monitor how local molecular interventions reshape animal behavior in naturalistic environments. While AVATAR integrates 3D pose–based tracking with optogenetic control to capture causal links between neural modulation and behavior, CLOSER applies deep-learning–based motion analysis to derive quantitative, individualized behavioral signatures for early diagnosis of neurological disorders [62]. For instance, in Parkinson’s disease mouse models, a 3D pose–based AVATAR pipeline combined with optoRET stimulation detects disease severity earlier than conventional metrics and ameliorates motor symptoms, slowing progression. Complementing this, closed-loop systems such as DeepLabStream use real-time, markerless pose estimation to trigger posture-contingent optogenetic stimulation and to label neural ensembles in freely moving animals. Integrating these layers—optical control, multimodal molecular profiling, and AI-based behavioral mapping—creates a closed experimental loop from molecular signaling to organismal output [63]. The overarching goal is to bridge the long-standing gap between molecular modulation and behavioral change, providing a quantitative framework that connects intracellular signaling dynamics to adaptive functions of the whole brain.

FROM PATHWAYS TO COMMUNICATION NETWORKS: REFRAMING BRAIN DISORDERS

Many neurological and psychiatric disorders cannot be fully explained by single molecular pathways or isolated circuit dysfunctions [64-66]. Signaling connectomics offers a framework to reinterpret these complex disease states as disruptions in multicellular communication networks that integrate neuronal, glial, immune, and vascular signaling.

In Parkinson’s disease, dopaminergic degeneration has traditionally been viewed as cell-autonomous [67, 68]. Yet growing evidence implicates glial and immune signaling loops that modulate neuronal vulnerability and compensatory plasticity; in mouse models, microglial reactivity exacerbates α-synuclein propagation and neurodegeneration, whereas microglial inhibition mitigates both [69, 70]. A signaling connectomics approach delineates how inflammatory cues, trophic factors, and extracellular-matrix remodeling collectively shape disease progression. In depression, chronic stress can reconfigure growth-factor networks—for instance, the FGFR1–Notch–BDNF axis in the hippocampus—and causal manipulation of this axis alters transcription, adult neurogenesis, and depressive-like behaviors [25]. Similarly, after ischemic stroke or with aging, crosstalk among endothelial, astrocytic, and immune compartments within the neurovascular unit governs both recovery and decline, ranging from microglia–astrocyte interactions that tune inflammatory tone to endothelial–astrocyte signaling that maintains barrier integrity and repair [71].

By mapping these intercellular signaling architectures, signaling connectomics identifies hidden control points—nodes or pathways at which diverse perturbations converge. This perspective moves beyond reductionist “one mechanism–one disease” models to a multidimensional understanding of brain pathology. Ultimately, the framework could yield unified hypotheses that explain how multicellular signaling imbalances drive divergent clinical phenotypes, paving the way for more integrative and causally grounded interpretations of brain disorders. Realizing this program will require standardized ligand–receptor inference and open, cross-species resources, together with the integration of spatial multi-omics, whole-brain imaging, and AI-based behavior—so that causal, state-conditioned signaling graphs can be compared across development, aging, and disease.

CHALLENGES AND LIMITATIONS OF SIGNALING CONNECTOMICS

Despite its conceptual promise, realizing signaling connectomics faces several technical challenges. First, many current approaches infer intercellular communication from ligand-receptor expression patterns in single-cell and spatial transcriptomic datasets [42, 45]. While these methods provide valuable hypotheses about potential signaling relationships, they do not directly measure signaling activity or causal signal propagation [45]. Similarly, optogenetic or chemogenetic perturbations can reveal functional consequences of signaling pathway activation, but isolating cell-type-specific signaling effects in complex brain circuits remains difficult [20, 56]. Achieving precise perturbation and readout across multiple interacting cell types therefore remains a central experimental challenge.

A second challenge lies in integrating multiscale measurements into coherent models of brain-wide communication [9]. Signaling events unfold across spatial scales, ranging from intracellular biochemical reactions to brain-wide neuromodulatory gradients, and across temporal scales from milliseconds to days [12, 16]. Bridging these scales requires combining optical perturbation tools, spatial multi-omics, physiological recordings, and behavioral analysis within unified experimental and computational frameworks [44]. Developing methods that can capture these multilevel interactions will be essential for transforming signaling connectomics from a conceptual framework into a quantitative systems-level map of intercellular communication in the brain.

CONCLUSION AND PERSPECTIVES

This Perspective advances signaling connectomics as a brain-wide, state-conditioned map of intercellular communication that complements traditional wiring diagrams. We argue that circuit-only accounts miss temporally extended and cross-compartment processes—spikes unfold in milliseconds, whereas neuromodulatory, paracrine, and immune signals integrate over minutes to days to reshape excitability, gene expression, and behavior—and we outline a perturb–profile–behavior workflow that couples optogenetic control of signaling axes with biosensors, single-cell and spatial profiling, and AI-based behavioral analytics. Applied to disease, the framework reframes Parkinson’s disease, depression, stroke, and aging as network-level dysregulations of multicellular signaling rather than isolated neuronal defects, and it exposes hidden control points—ligand–receptor programs, cellular interfaces, and transport pathways—where diverse perturbations converge, thereby linking molecular communication to circuit remodeling and behavior in vivo. Looking ahead, extending signaling connectomics toward a truly multicellular systems neuroscience will require the integration of spatial multi-omics and AI-based modeling, along with standardized ligand–receptor inference frameworks. Taken together, these elements should enable comparative, causal maps across development, aging, and disease, and reveal the organizing principles that govern brain-wide communication.
