Using human placenta as a simulation model for neurosurgical training: enhancing micro-vascular dissection skills
Authors: Reem Yousef AlMarshad, Ahmed Hafez Mousa, Zahraa AlSadah, Faisal AlMatrafi, May Adel AlHamid, Hosam Al-Jehani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:This study evaluates the use of human placenta as a simulation model for improving micro-vascular dissection skills among neurosurgical trainees.Methods:Thirteen neurosurgical residents (PGY1-PGY6) participated in this study. They completed a 15-question qualitative survey adapted from the Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) following two simulation sessions.Results:Ten residents reported improvements in key areas such as tissue handling, hand-eye coordination, and instrument control. All participants appreciated the feedback received and expressed interest in attending future sessions. Additionally, ten residents were willing to mentor junior colleagues.Conclusion:The human placenta offers a highly beneficial simulation model for neurosurgical training, particularly in micro-vascular dissection. It provides a low-stress, educational environment that promotes skill development, making it an ideal tool for surgical trainees.

Introduction

Neurosurgical training has evolved significantly, incorporating simulation models to enhance technical skill development. One promising model involves the use of human placenta, initially introduced by Magaldi in 2014 for cerebral aneurysm simulations. Due to its availability and anatomical similarity to cerebral structures, the placenta offers an affordable and realistic training environment for neurosurgical residents[1]. This study explores the effectiveness of the human placenta in improving residents’ micro-vascular dissection skills and provides evidence for its use as a valuable educational tool.

Methods

Collection and preparation of specimens

Human placentas were ethically procured from postpartum patients, with explicit informed consent obtained in accordance with institutional guidelines. The placentas underwent a meticulous preparation process, which included thorough cleaning and flushing. A segment of the umbilical cord was retained specifically for subsequent dissection activities. The work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria[2].

Simulation sessions

Thirteen neurosurgical residents, ranging from Post-Graduate Year 1 (PGY1) to Post-Graduate Year 6 (PGY6), engaged in two structured simulation sessions, each lasting between 30 minutes and 1 hour. During these sessions, residents utilized standard neurosurgical instruments and magnifying loupes to effectively dissect and skeletonize placental vessels.

Task complexity and supervision

To enhance the educational experience, senior residents were presented with more intricate dissection tasks that challenged their existing skill sets. Additionally, they were encouraged to assume supervisory roles over their junior counterparts, fostering a collaborative learning environment.

Feedback mechanism

At the conclusion of each session, attending neurosurgeons provided constructive feedback to the residents. This feedback aimed to reinforce learning and facilitate the refinement of their surgical techniques.

Assessment of skill improvement

To evaluate the residents’ perceptions of their skill enhancement, a 15-question online survey was administered. This survey, adapted from the Objective Structured Assessment of Technical Skills (OSATS) framework, assessed various competencies, including tissue handling, micro-vascular techniques, instrument control, and overall surgical confidence.

Results

A total of 13 residents, ranging from Postgraduate Year 1 (PGY1) to Postgraduate Year 6 (PGY6), participated in the study and completed the post-session survey. The distribution of residents included four first-year residents, two second-year residents, one third-year resident, one fourth-year resident, two fifth-year residents, and three sixth-year residents. Respect for tissue

In assessing respect for tissue, the majority of participants (53.8%) received a mid-range score of 3, suggesting a moderate level of proficiency. Lower scores of 1 and 2 were given to 7.7% of participants each, while 23.1% of participants achieved a score of 5, highlighting a smaller group with excellent tissue handling skills.

Time and motion

Most participants displayed moderate efficiency in time and motion, with 38.5% scoring a 3. A smaller group received a score of 1 (23%), indicating a need for improvement, while 15.4% achieved scores of 2 and 4. Only 7.7% of participants were rated at the highest level of 5, reflecting limited optimization in time management.

Instrument handling

Instrument handling scores mirrored those of other competencies, with 38.5% of participants scoring a 3. Scores of 2 and 5 were each awarded to 15.4% of participants, while only 7.7% received a score of 1. This distribution suggests a consistent, moderate skill level among participants in instrument handling.

Knowledge of instruments

In terms of knowledge of instruments, scores again centered around a rating of 3, with 38.5% of participants achieving this score. A smaller proportion received scores of 2 and 5 (15.4% each), while 7.7% scored a 1. These scores reflect a general, moderate familiarity with surgical instruments.

Flow of operation

The flow of operation demonstrated a broader range of proficiency, with the highest score of 5 awarded to 38.5% of participants. A mid-range score of 3 was given to 23%, while scores of 1 and 2 were each assigned to 15.4% of participants, and only 7.7% scored a 4. This variation indicates a mix of high proficiency and areas for potential enhancement in operational flow.

Use of assistants

A significant proportion of participants (53.8%) received a score of 1 for their use of assistants, indicating a need for improvement in this area. A smaller percentage (23.1%) scored a 3, while scores of 2, 4, and 5 were each awarded to 7.7% of participants, suggesting limited optimization in the effective utilization of assistants.

Knowledge of specific procedures

Knowledge of specific procedures was most frequently rated at a score of 2, achieved by 53.8% of participants, reflecting a foundational level of understanding. Additionally, 23.1% scored a 3, while 7.7% received scores of 1, 4, and 5. This distribution suggests a baseline familiarity with specific procedures, with a few participants demonstrating higher competency.

Overall, most parameters clustered around the mid-range score of 3, though variations in areas such as the use of assistants and flow of operation indicate both strengths and opportunities for further skill development among participants. Discussion

Neurosurgery demands advanced precision and skill, particularly in micro-vascular procedures[3]. Simulation-based learning has proven effective in bridging the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical surgical experience. The human placenta offers a cost-effective and accessible model that mimics the cerebral arachnoid membrane, providing a realistic environment for practicing micro-dissection techniques[4]. While alternative models, such as cadaveric specimens and virtual reality simulations, exist, the human placenta provides superior haptic feedback and accessibility[5,6]. However, it is important to acknowledge that this model may not fully replicate the complexities of human cerebral anatomy and may present ethical concerns[7]. Incorporating objective evaluation methods, such as OSATS or a standardized scoring system, could further enhance the learning experience[8]. Future research could explore integrating virtual reality into placental simulations for a more immersive experience. Moreover, simulation training should supplement, not replace, clinical experience, and the balance between simulation and live surgeries must be carefully maintained.

Conclusion

Human placenta models offer an effective, low-cost, and educationally enriching tool for neurosurgical training, particularly for micro-vascular dissection skills. This model enables residents to practice in a stress-free, feedback-oriented environment. Although subjective perceptions indicate skill improvement, objective assessments should be incorporated in future studies to measure technical progress more accurately.
