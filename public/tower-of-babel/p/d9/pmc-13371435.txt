We synthesized a series of amide-embedded pyrenes (AmPys) incorporating cis-amide bonds within the π-framework. This work establishes the amide bond as a versatile double-bond unit where the C–N bond length serves as a design parameter for tuning electronic properties.

Introduction

Incorporating heteroatoms is an effective strategy for modifying the electronic structures of polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs).1–4 Specifically, replacing CC double bonds with heteroatom-containing motifs that have double-bond character (such as CN,1,5–7 B–N,8–12 or CP13,14 bonds, Fig. 1a) enables modulation of the HOMO (Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital) and LUMO (Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital) energy levels, molecular polarity, triplet-state energies, and diradical characters. These double-bond motifs preserve π-conjugation while allowing heteroatoms to tune the electron distribution within the PAH framework. The bond order of such double-bonds is sensitive to the external environment and can therefore be leveraged to develop multi-stimuli (e.g. thermo-, mechano-, photo-, and electro-) responsive molecules.15 However, structurally modifying the double-bond motifs to modulate their electronic properties remains a challenge.

Here, we propose a new design concept that utilizes the amide C–N bond as a double-bond motif to tune the degree of π-conjugation. According to Pauling's resonance theory, amides possess partial C–N double-bond character originating from π-conjugation between the nitrogen lone pair and the carbonyl group (Fig. 1b).16,17 The degree of C–N double-bond character, often defined by “amidicity”18,19 index, is known to be altered by chemical or geometrical modifications.20–22 For example, the amide C–N bond can be shifted to exhibit a single bond character via structural twisting,23,24 and has been applied to C–N bond cleavage reactions.25,26 In the present work, we focus on the tunability of the amide's double-bond character while maintaining its planarity. This can be done through structural modification at the nitrogen and/or oxygen and by non-covalent interactions (such as hydrogen bonding27 or even protonation).21 Therefore, PAHs that are embedded with amide groups should not only exhibit electronic properties distinct from those of the parent PAHs but also provide a versatile platform for further tuning.

To demonstrate this concept, we synthesized a series of amide-embedded pyrenes, AmPys, in which the CC bond at the K-region of pyrene is replaced by an amide unit (Fig. 1d). Structural modification of the amide unit modulates the degree of C–N bond delocalization and the aromatic character of the two K-region rings, leading to unprecedented tuning of the triplet-state energy (Fig. 1b). Such energy control can be rationalized by a transition from a pyrene-like π-conjugated system at short C–N bond lengths to a biphenyl-like π-conjugated system at long bond lengths. Electronic-structure and aromaticity analyses reveal that the triplet-state energy is governed by the aromatic character of the K-region rings (Fig. 1c). Short C–N bonds promote localized [4n + 2]π conjugation at K-region rings that electronically “insulate” and spatially separate the unpaired electrons, thereby lowering the T1 energy (e.g.AmPyOMe, Fig. 1c). Conversely, long C–N bonds are less involved in π-conjugation at the K-region rings and allow interaction between the unpaired electrons, thereby raising the T1 energy (e.g.AmPyNMe, Fig. 1c). This study establishes the amide C–N bond embedded in a π-conjugated framework as a design motif for controlling excited-state properties through bond-length modulation.

Results and discussion

Molecular design and synthesis

Our motivation to synthesize AmPy1NH and AmPy2NH (Fig. 1d) was inspired by their structural resemblance to pyrene, a fluorophore well-known for its high luminescence quantum yield and versatility.28 We were particularly interested in exploring the relationship between the double-bond character of the embedded amide moiety and the emission properties of these molecules. Despite an initial synthetic report in the 1960s,29,30 these molecules have remained largely underexplored, with only a recent publication detailing the thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF)31 and photovoltaic properties32 of alkylated AmPy1 derivatives. The synthesis of AmPy1NH and AmPy2NH was carried out only with four steps from commercially available compounds (see SI). The N-methylated derivatives (AmPy1NMe, AmPy2NMe, Fig. 1d) were synthesized via a methylation reaction utilizing iodomethane in conjunction with Cs2CO3,33 while the O-methylated derivatives (AmPy1OMe, AmPy2OMe, Fig. 1d) were prepared using iodomethane together with Ag2CO3 (see SI).34 All the products were unambiguously characterized by 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy, high-resolution mass spectrometry, and single-crystal X-ray crystallography (see SI).

Optical properties of AmPy1OMe and AmPy1NMe

The electronic absorption spectra of AmPy1OMe and AmPy1NMe (2.0 × 10−6 M in diethyl ether (Et2O)/1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoropropan-2-ol (HFIP) = 9/1 at 298 K) displayed longest-wavelength absorption bands at 363 and 371 nm, respectively (Fig. 2 a and e, black lines). Given that pyrene exhibits its longest-wavelength absorption at 331 nm (Fig. S17), the amide moiety, even in the N-methylated form, extends π-conjugation beyond the pyrene framework. The emission spectra of AmPy1OMe and AmPy1NMe recorded at 298 K with an excitation wavelength (λex) of 340 and 350 nm (fluorescence spectra), exhibited the shortest-wavelength bands peaking at 364 and 373 nm, respectively, with vibrational structures (Fig. 2 a and e, blue lines). In contrast, their emission spectra at 100 K (phosphorescence spectra) were markedly different. The emission spectrum of AmPy1OMe at 100 K (delay time = 10 ms), obtained upon excitation at 340 nm, exhibited a peak at 509 nm (Fig. 2a, red line). On the other hand, that of AmPy1NMe was observed at 435 nm upon excitation at 350 nm (Fig. 2e, red line), which was significantly blue-shifted compared to AmPy1OMe. This observation is particularly intriguing, given that the electronic absorption and fluorescence spectra of the two compounds did not show such a large difference. It is noted that the emission spectrum of pyrene excited at 340 nm recorded at 298 K (fluorescence) exhibited the shortest-wavelength bands peaking at 373 nm, which is similar to that for AmPy1OMe and AmPy1NMe (Fig. S17). In contrast, the emission spectrum of pyrene recorded at 100 K (phosphorescence, in polystyrene film) exhibited the peak at 589 nm, which is largely different from that of AmPy1OMe and AmPy1NMe (Fig. S17). This indicates that embedding an amide group into the K-region of pyrene framework leads to a large modification in the phosphorescence while maintaining the fluorescence.

Both AmPy1OMe and AmPy1NMe are methylated versions of the parent amide, AmPy1NH. Yet, the effects of methylation are opposite depending on whether the methyl groups are attached to the O or the N atom; the C–N bonds in AmPy1OMe display enhanced double-bond character while those in AmPy1NMe exhibit greater single-bond character relative to the NH form. We attribute the spectroscopic differences between AmPy1OMe and AmPy1NMe to variations in the amide C–N bond length. As determined by X-ray crystal structure analysis, the C–N bond lengths are 1.294 Å for AmPy1OMe (Fig. S39) and 1.382 Å for AmPy1NMe (Fig. S38). In agreement, the calculated C–N bond lengths are 1.290 Å for AmPy1OMe and 1.393 Å for AmPy1NMe. These geometric trends suggest a correlation between bond length and phosphorescence wavelength. Similar trends were observed for AmPy2OMe and AmPy2NMe (Fig. S19, S41 and S42).

Optical properties of AmPy1NH, and AmPy1NH with 2-pyridone (PyrNH) or H2SO4

Since the amide group of AmPy1NH is part of a pyridone ring, it is capable of forming complementary H-bonds with a 2-pyridone molecule (PyrNH) (see SI section 5).27 The shortest-wavelength emission band of AmPy1NH, peaking at 370 nm (2.0 × 10−6 M in Et2O/HFIP = 9/1 at 298 K, λex = 350 nm), remains unchanged upon addition of 1.0 × 103 eq. of PyrNH (Fig. 2c, blue line, cf.Fig. 2d). However, in the presence of PyrNH, the emission spectrum at 100 K (delay time = 10 ms, λex = 350 nm) (Fig. 2c, red line) is red-shifted by 3 nm relative to that of AmPy1NH (Fig. 2d, red line). Notably, both emission wavelengths are blue-shifted compared to that of AmPy1OMe (Fig. 2a, red line).

Comparisons to computed geometries at the ωB97X-D/def2-SVP35,36 level are instructive. The calculated C–N bond length of AmPy1NH upon formation of the complementary H-bond with PyrNH is 1.361 Å (see SI). As expected, this value is in-between that of non-H-bonded AmPy1NH (1.384 Å) and AmPy1OMe (1.290 Å), confirming that the phosphorescence spectral wavelength correlates with the C–N bond length. Protonation of AmPyNH (see SI 6) further shortens the computed C–N bond length to 1.329 Å.

When spectral measurements of AmPy1NH were performed in H2SO4 (2.0 × 10−6 M at 298 K), the emission spectrum (λex = 350 nm) (Fig. 2b, blue line) exhibited a nearly identical spectral profile to that obtained in the presence of PyrNH (Fig. 2c, blue line). However, at 100 K (delay time = 10 ms, λex = 350 nm), the emission spectrum of protonated AmPy1NH (Fig. 2b, red line) showed a 46 nm red-shift relative to that measured in the presence of PyrNH (Fig. 2c, red line). This red-shifted phosphorescence is consistent with the trend associated with shorter C–N bond lengths.

A similar trend was also observed in polymer films with different functional groups (Fig. S18). When AmPy1NH was dispersed in polystyrene (PS), which lacks H-bonding functional groups, it exhibited the electronic absorption, emission at 298 K (λex = 340 nm), and emission at 100 K (delay time = 100 ms, λex = 340 nm) peaking at 371, 374, and 436 nm, respectively. In contrast, when dispersed in polyacrylic acid (PAA), which contains H-bonding functional groups, AmPy1NH displayed similar electronic absorption (371 nm) and emission at 298 K (375 nm) to those observed in PS. However, the emission at 100 K appeared at 449 nm, which was red-shifted by 13 nm from that in PS (Fig. S18). Similar results were obtained for the AmPy2 series (Fig. S19 and S20). These results demonstrate that the excited-state properties of AmPys can be modulated through H-bonding in the external environment. Moreover, our findings suggest that AmPys and related PAH systems can be leveraged to construct functional molecular assemblies with tunable excited-state energies.

Relationship between C–N bond length, spin delocalization, and T1 state energies

Correlations between the S1 and T1 state energies of the AmPy1 series and the calculated C–N bond length in the S0 states are plotted in Fig. 3. The S1 and T1 state energies were estimated from the onset of the fluorescence and phosphorescence spectra, respectively (Fig. S22). Surprisingly, the S1 state energies remain relatively constant regardless of the C–N bond length, while the T1 state energies increase with longer C–N length (Fig. 3a), resulting in narrower ΔEST (Fig. 3b). Similar results were obtained for the AmPy2 series (Fig. 3c, d and S19 and S24). Although selective modulation of the T1 state energy has been achieved by replacing a CC bond in dibenzo[g,p]chrysene with a B–N bond,11 systematic tuning through minimal structural modification of a single framework, without extensive reconstruction, remains unprecedented.

Computational analyses reveal that the relationship between C–N bond length and the T1 state energy is dictated by the aromatic character of the K-region rings (Fig. 1c), which in turn governs the degree of communication between the unpaired electrons. The key point is that, as the C–N bond lengthens, AmPy systems shift from a pyrene-like to a biphenyl-like π-conjugated system. Triplet spin densities were obtained from Natural Bond Orbital (NBO)37 analyses at the (U)ωB97X-D/def2-SVP level (Fig. 4a). Like pyrene, AmPy1OMe and AmPy1NH + H+ exhibited localized triplet spin densities at the apical non-K region carbons. As a result, the unpaired electrons occupy distal positions, thereby lowering the T1 state energy in accordance with Hund's rule. As the C–N bond length increases, the triplet spin becomes increasingly more delocalized in AmPy1NH + PyrNH, AmPy1NH, and AmPy1NMe. In these cases, the unpaired electrons of the same spin occupy overlapping regions of the pyrene framework, thereby raising the T1 state energy, as expected by Hund's rule. Plots of triplet spin densities are consistent with these results (Fig. S48 and S49).

Nucleus independent chemical shifts (NICS) calculations38,39 for the S0 (Fig. 4d) and T1 (Fig. 4b) state structures of pyrene, AmPy1OMe, AmPy1NH + H+, AmPy1NH + PyrNH, AmPy1NH, and AmPy1NMe were carried out to quantify the aromatic character of the K-region rings. NICS(1)zz values were computed at the (U)B97-2/6-311+G(d,p) level40–42 with bq points placed 1 Å above each six-membered ring. In the S0 state, the K-region rings of pyrene, AmPy1OMe, and AmPy1NH + H+, exhibit large negative NICS(1)zz values (−16, −11, and −8 ppm, respectively) (Fig. 4d). In contrast, the K-region rings of AmPy1NH + PyrNH, AmPy1NH, and AmPy1NMe display near-zero NICS(1)zz values (−3, −2, and −1 ppm, respectively) (Fig. 4d), and can therefore be considered as nonaromatic spacer rings. In the T1 state, the K-region rings in pyrene and AmPy1OMe exhibit near-zero NICS(1)zz values (+1, and +4 ppm, respectively) (Fig. 4b) and become a nonaromatic spacer; note near-zero triplet spin densities in the K-region rings (Fig. 4a). In contrast, the K-region rings in the T1 states of AmPy1NH and AmPy1NMe, display large positive NICS(1)zz values (+27 and +26 ppm, respectively), suggesting excited-state antiaromatic character (Fig. 4b);43 this is consistent with the delocalized triplet spin densities at the K-region rings (Fig. 4a).44AmPy1NH + H+ (+14 ppm) and AmPy1NH + PyrNH (+14 ppm) illustrate in-between cases and show moderate antiaromatic character in the T1 state (Fig. 4).

Hence, the presence of a “naphthalene-like” π-conjugated subunit is a critical indicator of the T1 state energy of AmPys. In the T1 state, the “naphthalene-like” unit in AmPy1OMe and AmPy1NH + H+ resists participation in the overall π-conjugation pathway in order to preserve a local [4n + 2] circuit. As a result, it spatially separates the unpaired electrons to the apical non-K region, and separation of the triplet spins lowers the T1 energy. On the contrary, in AmPy1NH and AmPy1NMe, the lack of a “naphthalene-like” unit enables the unpaired electrons in the T1 state to delocalize and interact with each other, therefore raising the T1 energy. Similar trends were found for the AmPy2 series (Fig. S46, S47 and S50). Computed gauge-including magnetically induced current plots for the AmPy species are supportive and consistent with trends of the computed NICS analyses (Fig. S65–67).

We next examined the exchange integral (KHL) between HOMO and LUMO for the AmPy1 and AmPy2 series (Fig. S52 and S54). Following a simple two-electron, two-state model,45 the S1–T1 energy gap (ΔEST) is equal to twice the exchange integral (KHL), consistent with the computed values listed in Table S15. The trend in the computed vertical ΔEST energies reproduced the experimental adiabatic ΔEST energies derived from fluorescence and phosphorescence spectra (Fig. 2 and 3a, b), and follow the order: AmPy1OMe > AmPy1NH + H+ > AmPy1NH + PyrNH > AmPy1NH >AmPy1NMe. In addition, the C–N bond length correlates with KHL and the spatial orbital overlap (SHL) between the HOMO and LUMO (Fig. S53). The AmPy2 series exhibits the same trend (Fig. S54, S55, and Table S16). Consequently, these computational results suggest that increasing the amide C–N bond length reduces HOMO–LUMO overlap, weakens the exchange interaction, and lowers the T1 state energy.

We further investigated the AmPn series—a molecular scaffold in which the K-region double bond of phenanthrene is replaced with a cis-amide (Fig. 5a). In sharp contrast to the AmPy series, no correlation was found between the T1 state energies and C–N bond lengths (Fig. 5b and c). For all compounds in the AmPn series (2.0 × 10−5 M in ethanol), the peak maxima of the electronic absorption appeared at around 340 nm. Similarly, the peak maxima of the emission spectra (λex = 300 nm) at 298 K (appeared at around 345 nm), and the emission spectrum at 100 K (delay time = 10 ms, λex = 300 nm) (appeared at around 430 nm) were nearly the same among the series (Fig. S21). Consequently, no clear relationship between the phosphorescence wavelength and the C–N bond length was observed. NBO calculations in the T1 state revealed that AmPnOMe, AmPnNH, and AmPnNMe all exhibited delocalized triplet spin densities across the π-system (Fig. 5c(i) and S51). Computed KHL, ΔEST, and SHL values for AmPnOMe, AmPnNH, and AmPnNMe show little variation (Fig. S56 and Table S17). In the S0 state, computed NICS(1)zz values suggest that AmPnOMe resembles phenanthrene (i.e., two strongly aromatic rings and a modestly aromatic central ring), whereas AmPnNH and AmPnNMe resemble two aromatic rings bridged by a nonaromatic pyridone unit (Fig. 5c (iv)). However, in the T1 state, NICS(1)zz values indicate similar aromaticity patterns across the series, consistent with delocalized unpaired electrons (Fig. 5c (ii)). Accordingly, computed gauge-including magnetically induced current plots for the AmPn species reveal global paratropic ring currents along the perimeter of the phenanthrene framework, consistent with a delocalized triplet state (Fig. S68).

Why do the AmPy systems exhibit tunable T1 states but not the AmPn series? we provide an explanation in Fig. 6 by comparing the dominant resonance structures of AmPy1OMe with AmPnOMe and AmPy1NMe with AmPnNMe in the triplet state. The locations of the triplet spins are assigned based on the results of spin density calculations (Fig. 4 and 5). In AmPy1OMe and AmPnOMe, the C–N bond lengths are short, and the amides participate in π-conjugation, but the locations of the aromatic π-sextets in these systems are distinct. In triplet AmPy1OMe, the K-region rings are “naphthalene-like” and act as an electronic insulator that separates the two unpaired electrons46,47 (Fig. 6a (i)), thus lowering the T1 energy. In triplet AmPnOMe, the dominant resonance forms exhibit localized aromatic π-sextets at the non-K region rings, and therefore do not spatially separate the two unpaired electrons (Fig. 6a (ii)). In AmPy1NMe and AmPnNMe, the C–N bond lengths are long, and the unpaired electrons are delocalized within a biphenyl motif in the AmPy and AmPn frameworks (Fig. 6b), resulting in no T1-energy lowering effect.

Dominant resonance structures with unpaired electrons at the T1 state when amide C–N bond lengths are (a) shorter and (b) longer for (i) AmPy series and (ii) AmPn series.

We next examined the S1 state of AmPy series and AmPn series by approximating NICS(1)zz values in the T1 state for the S1 state geometries (Fig. 4c). Natural transition orbital (NTO) and Singly Occupied Molecular Orbitals (SOMOs) analyses rationalize the use of T1 state NICS(1)zz values for the S1 geometries (see SI section 8.5).48–50 The computed T1 state NICS(1)zz values for the S1 state geometries revealed a similar aromaticity pattern across pyrene, AmPy1OMe, AmPy1NH + H+, AmPy1NH + PyrNH, AmPy1NH, and AmPy1NMe. The same is true for AmPnOMe, AmPnNH, and AmPnNMe. In all species, large positive NICS(1)zz values for the six-membered rings indicate that the electrons are distributed throughout the molecular framework. In the S1 state, the electrons are delocalized across the π-system, and therefore the S1 energy is independent of the C–N bond length.

These results demonstrate that strategic placement of amide groups within π-conjugated frameworks at the position where cyclic conjugation is localized at the T1 state, can be used to modulate T1 state energies while leaving the S1 state energy largely unaffected. Modulation of the C–N bond length can selectively influence the T1 state through controlling the degree of “insulation” (lower T1) or “interaction” (higher T1) between the unpaired electrons, following Hund's rule. This design principle, enabling selective tuning of the T1 state energy while preserving the S1 state energy, is unprecedented.

Conclusions

Amides are among the most abundant molecular units, and their partial double-bond character is primarily used to explain their planarity and chemical stability. However, its π-conjugating double-bond character has scarcely been utilized in the design of π-conjugated systems. Here, we demonstrate the potential of the amide as a tunable π-conjugated unit and show that embedding an amide within a π-framework provides a powerful strategy for designing molecules with precisely controlled electronic structures and excited-state properties. Specifically, the present study demonstrates the ability to tune the T1 state energies and ΔEST. Further investigations of this system may reveal a new principle for designing molecules with unique photophysical processes, such as thermally activated delayed fluorescence (TADF),51 by varying substituents with strong electron-donating or withdrawing character on N or O within the embedded amide group to expand the degree of T1 energy tunability. Another important feature is the H-bonding capability of amide-embedded π-systems, which enables controlled solid-state assembly. Molecular optical properties in the solid state differ greatly from those in solution and are often hard to predict due to uncertain packing modes. The ability to regulate the assembly mode through H-bonding is important.52 Given also that the amide-embedded π-system can control optical properties via H-bonding, this system may provide a new design principle for solid-state optical materials and devices. Aside from optical materials, the amide-embedded π-system can also provide a molecular motif for designing ground-state diradicals with a tunable degree of diradical characters, through the strategic separation of the unpaired electrons with aromatic K-region rings to lower the T1 energy.
