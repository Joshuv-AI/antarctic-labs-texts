From Thanatos to transcription: the emerging biology of THAP proteins
Authors: Bryce Rampal, Alexandre Brisson, Louise Ben Hamou Kuijpers, Laurent Cappadocia, Éric Samarut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Thanatos-associated (THAP) protein family, named after the Greek god of death, comprises zinc-finger proteins characterized by a DNA-binding domain called the THAP domain. Although THAP1 and THAP11 have been extensively studied, the broader family of twelve human THAP-domain containing proteins is increasingly recognized as an important but underexplored group of transcriptional regulators. Evidence now links THAP proteins to a wide range of cellular processes, including cell-cycle progression, DNA repair, apoptosis, proteostasis, and mitochondrial function. Dysregulation of these proteins has also been implicated in cancer and neurological disorders, underscoring their medical relevance. In this review, we summarize current knowledge of their structure, localization, transcriptional roles, and disease associations. We also present new structural analyses that shed light on conserved and divergent features across the THAP family. Finally, we will highlight their newly appreciated functions in stem and progenitor cell biology.Keywords: THAP proteins, Molecular biology, Transcription factors, Development, Cancer, Stem cells

THAP proteins depict conserved structures and domains

The canonical DNA-binding THAP Domain

The defining feature of all THAP proteins is the presence of a so-called THAP domain, an 80–90 amino acid zinc-coordinating C2CH module. This domain was first identified about 20 years ago by Roussigne et al., who described a conserved 80-amino-acid region found in cellular proteins with substantial similarity to the DNA-binding domain of the Drosophila melanogaster P-element transposase [1, 2]. Subsequent studies using electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSA) confirmed that a recombinant THAP domain is capable of binding DNA. Importantly, this activity was shown to be zinc-dependent, as demonstrated by its inability to bind DNA in the presence of metal-chelating agents, which could be restored with the addition of zinc, but not calcium, magnesium, or iron [3]. Site-directed mutagenesis of key cysteine and histidine residues in the C2CH-type zinc finger further demonstrated that these residues are essential for DNA-binding activity, underscoring the central role of this signature in THAP domain function.

In contrast to classical C2H2 zinc finger proteins, which typically contain multiple tandem repeats of DNA-binding domains, THAP proteins harbour only one THAP domain at their N-terminus [4]. Structural studies of the Drosophila THAP domain have revealed that DNA recognition relies on a β-sheet inserting into the major groove of DNA (Fig. 1A), while a basic C-terminal loop makes additional contacts into the adjacent minor groove [5]. THAP domains are highly conserved

Comparative analysis shows that the THAP domain is primarily restricted to metazoa, being present in vertebrates and invertebrates but not widely found outside of animals [6]. In humans, the family consists of 12 paralogs (THAP1-12) with highly conserved N-terminal THAP domains (Fig. 1B-E) and variable C-termini (Fig. 2). Lineage-specific expansions of these proteins are quite common. Zebrafish contain 32 THAP genes due to the teleost-wide 3rd round of whole-genome duplication, followed by secondary losses [3, 7]. Nematodes also have an expansion of this family, with C. elegans encoding 8 THAP/THAP-like proteins, some of which interact with the DREAM complex to repress target genes involved in cell cycle regulation [8]. Altogether, this evolutionary conservation underscores the importance of THAP proteins in transcriptional regulation beyond humans.

Host-cell-factor-1 (HCF-1) interaction of THAP proteins

A short host-cell-factor-1 (HCF-1) binding motif is positioned near a coiled-coil region (to be discussed in Section E) in many THAP proteins and mediates recruitment of HCF-1 to target promoters [9–11]. HCF-1 is a chromatin-associated transcriptional co-regulator that plays a crucial role in regulating cell-cycle progression [12]. This HCF-1 binding motif (HBM) is absent in THAP8, THAP10, and THAP12 (Table 1) [13]. It should also be noted that THAP4 has two non-canonical HBM-like sequences, but was unable to bind to the HCF-1 Kelch region, which acts as the protein–protein interaction domain (Table 1) [14]. Interestingly, the presence of a canonical HBM does not perfectly predict association with HCF-1. THAP5, for example, which contains an HBM, was found to be unable to associate with HCF-1 through co-immunoprecipitation studies, whereas THAP8 (which contains no HBM) was capable of associating with HCF-1 [13]. These findings suggest that HCF-1 recruitment by THAP proteins may depend on additional structures or partner interactions beyond the presence of the HBM.

The HBM consists of an Asp/Glu-His-X-Tyr consensus sequence. Structurally, an AlphaFold model of a THAP1/HCF-1 complex reveals that the HBM of THAP1 sits on top of the β-propeller region of HCF-1, stabilized by an intricate network of hydrogen bonds and salt bridges (Fig. 3A). Precisely, the aromatic moiety of the tyrosine residue is located in a hydrophobic/aromatic cavity of HCF-1, while the hydroxyl group of the tyrosine residue forms a hydrogen bond with the conserved histidine, which, in turn, interacts with a glutamate residue of HCF-1 (Fig. 3B). The acidic residue of the HBM (Asp in THAP1) forms salt bridges with an arginine of HCF-1. This binding mode is generally consistent with the biochemical characterization and AlphaFold structure of a CREBZF/HCF-1 complex reported in a previous study [15]. Given that this mode of interaction brings several residues of THAP1 in close proximity with the β-propeller region of HCF-1, we anticipate that residues adjacent to the HBM may modulate the binding of individual THAP to HCF-1, perhaps consistent with prior finding that not all HBM allow HCF-1 recruitment [13].

THAP proteins govern multiple cellular functions via transcription regulation

Transcriptional regulation is the process by which cells control the timing, location, and level of gene expression, primarily mediated through proteins which interact with chromatin to influence RNA polymerase activity. Because THAP proteins are capable of zinc-dependent, sequence-specific DNA recognition through their highly conserved THAP domain and can recruit co-regulators such as HCF-1, they are well positioned to regulate transcriptional programs. Therefore, we will now discuss how THAP proteins regulate gene expression at target loci and how these transcriptional networks relate to broader cellular functions (Table 2).

THAP1

THAP1 was one of the earliest functionally characterized members of this family and was initially described as a nuclear pro-apoptotic factor that links prostate apoptosis response-4 (Par-4) to promyelocytic leukemia (PML) nuclear bodies. Subsequent studies expanded its function beyond apoptosis, showing that THAP1 regulates transcriptional programs involved in cell-cycle progression, proteasome function, DNA repair, and myelination. THAP1 is broadly expressed across tissues and predominantly localized to the nucleus, as shown by immunoblotting and immunofluorescence, consistent with its role as a transcription factor [16], [17].

Cell proliferation

THAP1 was initially shown to have a crucial role in regulating endothelial cell proliferation [18]. After silencing THAP1 by siRNA knockdown, a decrease in proliferation and impairment of G1/S phase progression was observed. At the molecular level, these cellular defects were also accompanied by downregulation of retinoblastoma tumor suppressor protein (pRB) and E2F transcription factor cell-cycle target genes. One of these genes, ribonucleotide reductase catalytic subunit M1 (RRM1), encodes a protein that catalyzes the conversion of ribonucleotides to deoxyribonucleotides, a process which is essential for DNA synthesis and cell replication [19]. The RRM1 gene was found to be directly bound by THAP1 in vitro via electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) and in vivo via Chromatin Immunoprecipitation Sequencing (ChIP-seq). Furthermore, it was demonstrated that THAP1 recruits the transcriptional coactivator, HCF-1, to the RRM1 promoter. Similar to THAP1, HCF-1 was also shown to have an essential role in the activation of RRM1, where its knockdown using siRNA resulted in significant downregulation of RRM1 mRNAs.

DNA repair

Beyond its regulatory role in DNA synthesis, THAP1 has also been shown to regulate DNA repair by upregulating Shieldin activity [20]. The Shieldin complex, composed of SHLD1, SHLD2, SHLD3, and MAD2L2/REV7 binds DNA ends to block resection and promote classical non-homologous end joining [21]. Through a CRISPR/Cas9 loss-of-function screen designed to identify genes whose disruption confers PARP-inhibitor resistance in BRCA1-deficient cells (where homologous recombination is impacted), THAP1 was identified as a novel hit. Transcriptomic analysis and immunoblotting showed that loss of THAP1 led to downregulation of SHLD1 mRNA and protein, with no impact on SHLD2 or SHLD3. ChIP-seq analysis confirmed that THAP1 binds to a motif within the Shld1 promoter, and deletion of this site phenocopied a loss of THAP1. Moreover, HCF-1 and YY1 co-occupied the promoter alongside THAP1, suggesting a cooperative transcriptional complex which is formed by these proteins. Similar to THAP1, YY1 is a zinc finger protein ubiquitously expressed that can act as either a transcriptional activator or repressor [22]. Together, these findings indicate that THAP1 is part of a transcriptional complex which sustains SHLD1 expression to balance DNA end protection, linking its transcriptional activity to genome stability.

Myelination

Recent in vivo studies have uncovered a novel role for THAP1 in central nervous system (CNS) myelination [23]. Using a CNS-specific conditional knockout (cKO) mouse model of Thap1, it was found that its loss results in motor dysfunction and behavioural impairments, including abnormal limb clasping during tail suspension, poor performance in beam-crossing experiments, and increased rearing during open-field tests. At the cellular level, this loss of THAP1 delays OL lineage maturation and reduces the expression of key myelin-associated genes, including Mag, Plp1, Cnp, and Mbp. Mechanistically, THAP1 and YY1 co-occupy promoters of OL maturation genes, and loss of THAP reduces YY1 occupancy at these loci, suggesting that THAP1 may recruit YY1 or promote its binding stability to regulate transcriptional programs required for myelination. The role of THAP1 in oligodendrocyte progenitor cell differentiation is discussed further in Section C.

Proteosome function

New evidence has also emerged supporting a role of THAP1 in proteosome function [24]. By examining co-essential relationships from genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 screens, Wang et al., identified a previously uncharacterized association of THAP1 with PSMB5 and PSMB6, both of which encode subunits of the 20S proteosome core. Functional validation in THAP1-KO HEK293FT cells showed a significant reduction in PSMB5 mRNA expression, leading to impaired proteasome assembly. Using ChIP-seq assays, they confirmed that THAP1 binds a regulatory region upstream of the PSMB5 transcriptional start site. It was then confirmed that THAP1 activates PSMB5 transcription via luciferase reporter assay. Collectively, these findings suggest that THAP1 plays a previously unrecognized role in maintaining proteasome function via PSMB5, extending its influence beyond its canonical roles in cell cycle regulation and DNA repair.

THAP3

THAP3 was first recognized as a THAP-domain-containing protein through the original classification of the THAP family and was later functionally characterized as a transcriptional regulator that associates with chromatin-associated cofactors [2].

Co-immunoprecipitation from HeLa cells demonstrated that the endogenous THAP3 interacts with HCF-1 and O-linked N-acetylglucosamine transferase (OGT). Interestingly, an HBM (HCF-1 binding motif)-mutant THAP3 fails to bind HCF-1 and shows reduced OGT association, indicating that THAP3 interacts indirectly with OGT via HCF-1 [9]. Chromatin immunoprecipitation at a Gal4-UAS reporter locus showed that Gal4-THAP3 tethers to promoter chromatin and recruits HCF-1 to promoter chromatin, and luciferase assays confirmed transcriptional activation in an HBM-dependent manner. However, whether THAP3 recruits HCF-1/OGT to endogenous cell-cycle gene promoters and whether OGT enzymatic activity contributes to THAP3-dependent transcriptional regulation remains unknown.

In addition, transcriptomic analysis linking transcription factors with oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) genes identified a positive correlation between THAP3 expression and OXPHOS gene activity [25]. Functional validation in Huh7 cells (liver cancer cell line) showed that shRNA-mediated THAP3 depletion coincided with a downregulation of nuclear-encoded OXPHOS genes containing a distinct 21-nucleotide consensus sequence. Mechanistically, THAP3 was found to associate with the histone methyltransferase SMYD3, recruiting it to OXPHOS promoters where it increases H3K4Me3 levels to promote transcriptional activation. This regulatory function mirrors its interaction with HCF-1, although whether HCF-1 and SMYD3 act in concert at OXPHOS loci is yet to be determined. A more complete understanding of the complexes THAP3 forms across cell types and states will be critical for gaining a more complete understanding of its role in transcription regulation and possible disease associations.

THAP5

Originally, THAP5 was isolated in a yeast two-hybrid screen as an interactor of the mitochondrial serine protease Omi/HtrA2 in cells undergoing apoptosis. In this study, ectopic expression of THAP5 was found to induce cell-cycle arrest [26]. Building on this connection to apoptosis, the authors next examined THAP5 in melanoma under DNA-damaging conditions known to promote cell death (UV irradiation and cisplatin), finding that THAP5 protein levels increased with treatment, and this induction closely mirrored apoptotic death in the population [27]. Consistent with this, overexpression of THAP5 N-terminally fused to GFP sensitized melanoma cells to UV-induced apoptosis compared to controls. This GFP-THAP5 also revealed that THAP5 is predominantly localized to the nucleus and excluded from the nucleoli, consistent with its function as a transcription factor. To further explore its potential as a transcription factor, a CASTing approach was employed, in which nuclear extracts were incubated with a pool of 25 random nucleotides. The endogenous THAP5, along with the bound sequences, was then immunoprecipitated, cloned, and sequenced. This identified a consensus binding motif of THAP5, which was further validated by EMSA. To determine whether THAP5 acts as a transcriptional activator or repressor, THAP5 was fused to the Gal4 DNA-binding domain, and transfection with a Gal4 luciferase reporter in HEK293T cells resulted in a significant reduction in reporter activity. Altogether, this suggests that THAP5 acts as a transcriptional repressor activated in response to stress and contributes to increased apoptosis. Given that THAP5 is highly expressed in the heart and reduced in the myocardial infarction region in patients with coronary artery disease, future work defining its endogenous genomic targets in cardiomyocytes could clarify whether it contributes to injury responses or repair programs [26].

THAP7

THAP7 was originally identified in a yeast two-hybrid screen for TAF-Iβ partners and shown to associate with TAF-Iβ via its C-terminal coiled-coil domain [28]. THAP7 also binds histone H3/H4 tails and, together with TAF-Iβ, masks histone lysines from acetylation, resulting in repressed chromatin. In cells, promoter-tethered THAP7 recruits HDAC3 and NCoR to drive H3 hypoacetylation and repress transcription. THAP7 has also been experimentally shown to interact with HCF-1 through its HBM, however, the specific genes bound by THAP7 have not been identified and whether HCF-1 is recruited or necessary for its regulation of transcription is yet to be determined [13].

More recently, it was discovered that THAP7 is upregulated in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), and that individuals with higher THAP7 expression had poorer prognoses [29]. Further in vitro analysis with lung carcinoma cells revealed that THAP7 promotes cell proliferation by facilitating progression through the G1/S phase. It was revealed that THAP7 significantly reduces p21 promoter activity in a dual-luciferase reporter assay, and that this effect is partially mediated by HDAC3. Altogether, this suggests that THAP7 acts as an oncogene recruiting HDAC3 to deacetylate histone H3 of the p21 promoter, ultimately leading to decreased expression of p21, and to the subsequent promotion of cell cycle progression, furthering tumor progression.

THAP11

THAP11, also known as Ronin, was first functionally characterized as an essential regulator of embryogenesis and embryonic stem cell pluripotency (refer to section C). Subsequent studies expanded its role to transcriptional regulation of proliferation-associated genes, including c-MYC and HCF-1-associated cell-cycle genes [30], [10].

In hepatoma cells, THAP11 binds the c-MYC promoter and represses its activity, as shown through EMSA and ChIP assays. Overexpression of THAP11 inhibits proliferation across multiple cell types, which can be rescued by expression of c-MYC, implying that c-MYC is a downstream target of THAP11 that acts as a master regulator of cell proliferation.

This role of THAP11 in regulating proliferation through transcription is further supported by its association with HCF-1 in colon cancer cells [10]. THAP11 and HCF-1 directly interact and co-occupy promoters of cell-cycle genes (e.g., RBL1 and CDC25A). THAP11 binding at these loci correlates with active promoter features and RNA polymerase II, and depletion of HCF-1 or THAP11 disrupts the others’ ability to associate with chromatin, indicating a reciprocal dependence. Additional evidence comes from E2F-regulated promoters, which drive expression of cell cycle genes [31]. At these sites, HCF-1 recruitment depends on joint occupation by THAP11 and another zinc finger protein, ZNF143. [31], [32]. Interestingly, this study did not detect the THAP11/HCF-1 complex at the c-MYC promoter, suggesting that THAP11 may regulate proliferation through alternative mechanisms depending on cell type and availability of specific cofactors such as HCF-1 and ZNF143.

Other THAP genes

While some THAP proteins, such as THAP2, THAP4, THAP6, THAP8, THAP10, and THAP12, have not been shown to bind to DNA directly, this is likely due to a lack of functional studies. All members of the THAP family proteins contain a C2CH zinc finger domain, which is a canonical domain enabling DNA binding, and importantly, nearly all of these proteins contain an HCF-1 binding motif [9]. The presence of an HBM in all but three of the THAP proteins (THAP8, THAP10, and THAP12) further strengthens their potential to regulate transcription, given that this cofactor acts as a key chromatin-associated protein required for the regulation of cell-cycle gene expression [33–35]. Interestingly, increasing evidence suggests that THAP12, which lacks an HBM, likely plays a role in transcription as well. THAP12 has been recently shown to form a complex with another zinc finger protein, ZNF574, to regulate cell cycle progression by promoting the transition from G1-to-S phase in B cell malignancies [36]. Prior to this, THAP12, also commonly known as DAP4, PRKRIR, THAP0 and P52ripk (a 52kD truncated form) was shown to be exclusively expressed in the nucleus, further supporting its potential to act as a transcription factor [37–39] p.52, [40]. Altogether, this suggests that despite limited direct binding evidence, nearly all THAP proteins contain the molecular features necessary for transcriptional regulation.

THAP proteins are emerging as novel regulators of stem and progenitor cells

Stem and progenitor cells are the foundation of tissue development, maintenance, and repair. Stem cells are defined by their ability to self-renew and to give rise to multiple differentiated lineages. In contrast, progenitor cells are more lineage-restricted and typically proliferate rapidly before differentiating into mature cell types [41]. During embryonic development, these cell populations are essential: embryonic stem cells generate all cell types of the body, while tissue-specific progenitors organize organ and system formation by providing differentiated cells in a tightly regulated manner. Even in adulthood, progenitors remain important for processes such as hematopoiesis, neurogenesis, and tissue regeneration following injury [42]. The fate of stem and progenitor cells is governed by intricate networks of transcription factors, epigenetic regulators, and signalling pathways, which influence the complex balance between self-renewal and differentiation. Consistent with their function in transcription regulation of cell-cycle genes, THAP genes are important candidates which can also regulate stem cell homeostasis and fate.

THAP1

In line with its ability to regulate cell-cycle progression in endothelial cells, THAP1 has also been shown to regulate embryonic stem cell survival [43]. Recently, it was shown that deletion of Exon 2 or mimicking the dystonia-causing mutation C54Y of THAP1 in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs) resulted in significant decreases in proliferation and viability. Additionally, the loss of THAP1 in these cells appeared to result in an arrest at the S phase of the cell cycle, as shown by propidium iodide (PI) staining followed by flow cytometry. These defects were believed to be due to prolonged expression of the pluripotency markers Nanog, Prmd14, and REX, with decreased expression of ectodermal markers (Pax6, Sox11, Nestin, and Tuj1). Interestingly, through ChIP-seq, it was found that THAP1 does not directly bind and regulate these markers but instead binds to genes involved in cell cycle control (Aurka, Trp53), apoptosis (Bad), and RNA processing (Sf3a2). These findings suggest that THAP1 maintains stem cell homeostasis not by directly controlling pluripotency pathways, but by regulating key cellular functions which have secondary effects, highlighting the need for future studies to elucidate how these indirect mechanisms control early development.

In addition to embryonic stem cells, THAP1 has experimental support as a regulator of oligodendrocyte progenitor cell (OPC) differentiation. Yellajoshyula et al. showed that conditional loss of Thap1 delays oligodendrocyte-lineage maturation and CNS myelination, while oligodendrocyte-lineage specific deletion phenocopied the CNS conditional knockout, suggesting a cell-autonomous role for THAP1 in this lineage ([23]. A subsequent study further showed that THAP1 regulates OPC maturation through extracellular matrix remodelling by controlling glycosaminoglycan (GAG) catabolism [44]. GAGs are long extracellular matrix polysaccharides that influence cell signalling and differentiation. In Thap1-KO OPCs, excess GAGs accumulated and were secreted, which impaired OL maturation. Mechanistically, it was shown that Thap1 binds and regulates GusB, which encodes β-glucuronidase, an enzyme required for GAG degradation. Consistent with this, GAG-degrading enzymes or β-glucuronidase overexpression rescued Thap1-KO OL maturation defects.

THAP4

The involvement of THAP4 in progenitor and stem cells comes from studies of the pet (“petit”) rat syndrome, caused by a frameshift mutation in Thap4, which truncates the C-terminal nitrobindin domain [45]. This domain normally supports nitric oxide binding and peroxynitrite detoxification, and when disrupted by this truncating mutation, animals show dwarfism, severe thymic hypoplasia, testicular degeneration, and high postnatal lethality. Developmental abnormalities such as these have been shown in other conditions to reflect impairments in stem and/or progenitor cell proliferation, such as primary microcephaly [46] and Hirschsprung disease [47].

Further evidence stems from THAP4 belonging to a genomic region associated with the 2q37 microdeletion syndrome. This syndrome results in intellectual disability, dysmorphism, autism, and epilepsy [48]. While the causative gene(s) whose deletion results in this syndrome are yet to be identified, THAP4 may be a potential candidate. Given that THAP4 is highly expressed in the cortical plate and intermediate zone during early development and is also enriched in neural progenitors according to the Allen Brain Atlas, one could speculate that it plays a role in stem/progenitor maintenance, similar to other THAP proteins.

THAP9

The current evidence linking THAP9 to OL differentiation is primarily correlative. Bhardwaj et al. found that THAP9 expression is upregulated during the transition from OPCs to mature oligodendrocytes (MOs), as recently revealed by RNA-seq datasets [49]. This upregulation was also strongly correlated with the expression of MOG and MBP- key myelin genes, as well as SOX5, SOX6, AND SOX11- transcription factors involved in cell fate determination and differentiation [50]. These findings suggest that THAP9 may be associated with OL maturation,however, they do not establish that THAP9 directly regulates OPC differentiation. Functional studies, including THAP9 loss- or gain-of-function experiments and the identification of chromatin-binding sites, will be required to determine whether THAP9 makes a direct contribution.

THAP11

The most widely studied function of THAP11 is its crucial role in embryonic stem (ES) cells. Indeed, THAP11 was identified during a search for Caspase-3 targets that may be critical for ES cell pluripotency, such as Nanog [51]. Northern Blot analysis revealed that THAP11 was not highly expressed in most adult tissues and is predominantly expressed in pluripotent cells during early development. It was found that loss of THAP11 results in rapid ES cell death and embryonic lethality in mice, whereas its forced expression in ES cells inhibits differentiation and maintains pluripotency. Mechanistically, THAP11 directly binds to HCF-1, where they co-occupy promoters upstream of transcriptional start sites and regulate expression of genes required for ES cell growth (e.g. protein biosynthesis (Rpo1-2 and Rpo1-4), transcription initiation (Ctd, Cnot4/8, and Med4), mitochondrial ribosomal proteins (Mrpl19, Mrpl32, Mrpl50, and Mrpl54), mitochondrial translation factors (Tufm) and oxidative phosphorylation subunits (Atp5e and NADH dehydrogenase)) [52]. These target genes of the THAP11/HCF-1 complex are highly expressed in ES cells and are downregulated during differentiation, as shown by DNA microarray analysis over 14 days of ES cell differentiation.

In addition to ES cells, THAP11 has also been shown to play a key role in retinal progenitor cell (RPCs) proliferation, which are multipotent cells that differentiate into specific types of retinal cells [53]. Using cKO mice to deplete THAP11 in RPCs specifically, Poché et al., found that this results in photoreceptor degeneration [54]. These defects are believed to arise from the RPCs prematurely exiting the cell cycle and differentiating into early retinal neurons. Interestingly, ChIP-seq of THAP11 did not reveal significant enrichment of cell cycle-related genes,rather, its targets are strongly associated with mitochondrial function and share many targets with ES cells [54]. This was confirmed through the abnormal morphology of THAP11 mutant mitochondria and disruptions to electron transport chain complexes I, III, and IV.

Together, both studies illustrate how THAP11 regulates ESCs and RPCs proliferation by promoting increased biosynthesis of metabolic components needed to meet the increased energy demand of stem cells undergoing mitosis prior to exiting the cell cycle. This contrasts with its previously mentioned role in hepatoma and colon cancer cell models, where THAP11 regulates proliferation by directly altering the transcription of key cell cycle genes, suggesting that THAP11 may have distinct functions depending on cell type and state. Further studies examining how THAP11 target genes and interaction partners shift between distinct cellular contexts will be essential for clarifying how its functions differ between stem, progenitor, and differentiated cells.

THAP proteins are associated with a broad spectrum of human disorders

Cancer

Consistent with their roles in transcription regulation of basic yet essential cell functions, THAP proteins have been extensively associated with cancer. Some of these connections are direct, where transcriptional regulation by a THAP protein influences tumor growth or suppression, while others are more indirect, arising from changes in interacting partners or expression patterns. For instance, THAP3 directly functions as a tumor promoter in liver cancer cells, where it binds to OXPHOS genes to promote cell growth [25]. In contrast, THAP1 demonstrates an indirect role through its interaction with the tumor suppressor Par-4 [1, 2]. THAP4 also appears to be indirectly connected to cancer, as its expression is upregulated in T-cell lymphomas, potentially supporting aberrant proliferation, although its direct transcriptional targets remain unknown [55]. This highlights the complex, context-dependent roles of THAP proteins in cancer pathways. However, several important questions remain unresolved. While direct oncogenic or tumor-suppressive functions have been described for a subset of THAP proteins, the transcriptional targets and molecular mechanisms of most family members remain poorly characterized. Future studies integrating genome-wide target identification, protein-interaction mapping, and functional perturbation approaches across different tumor types will be necessary to determine whether THAP proteins act through shared regulatory pathways or possess distinct cancer-specific functions. Such efforts may also clarify whether THAP-associated protein complexes represent potential therapeutic targets in cancer.

Dystonia

THAP1 has been associated with primary dystonia, a neurological disorder characterized by excessive and involuntary muscle contractions [56], [57], [58]. Dystonia can be early or late onset and has a wide range of clinical presentations. It may occur sporadically or be inherited, emphasizing the need to identify novel genetic causes.

Variants in THAP1 were identified as pathogenic candidates through genome-wide linkage analysis on affected Amish Mennonite and European families, which mapped a locus referred to as DYT6 [59]. Within this region, THAP1 emerged as a likely candidate due to its role in transcription regulation, which was confirmed through Sanger Sequencing in affected patients, revealing heterozygous mutations. These mutations impair THAP1’s ability to bind DNA or recruit HCF-1, thereby disrupting transcription of its target genes and leading to defects in the previously mentioned essential cellular pathways (refer to Section C) [60]. However, which of the disrupted cellular functions (myelination, proteosome function, cell cycle regulation) is most responsible for the pathology of dystonia remains unclear.

Spinocerebellar Ataxia

Spinocerebellar ataxias (SCAs) are severe, progressive neurological disorders primarily affecting the cerebellum and manifesting as problems with coordination and balance [61]. Most SCAs are inherited in an autosomal dominant manner and are genetically heterogeneous, with polyglutamine (CAG) repeats often being the causative variants.

Long-read whole-genome sequencing and linkage analysis have recently identified CAG (45–100) repeats in exon 1 of THAP11 responsible for a distinct autosomal dominant SCA subtype, referred to as SCA51 [62]. This was found in a multi-generational Chinese family consisting of 22 ataxia patients who lacked repeats in known causative SCA genes. The age of onset and severity was found to increase with repeat expansion size in THAP11. Electron microscopy of patient derived fibroblasts confirmed the presence of cytoplasmic aggregates of polyQ-expanded THAP11 which was recapitulated in Neuro-2a and HEK293 cells transfected with 54 and 100 CAG repeats. Ectopic expression of mutant THAP11-47Q in vivo, results in cerebellar degeneration and impaired motor functions [63]. Interestingly, it was also found that expression of this mutant THAP11 results in microglial activation as evidenced through upregulation of microglial activation markers from RNA-seq results. Furthering the transcriptomic profile, recent RNA-seq analysis on patient derived fibroblasts revealed a significant dysregulation of genes involved in the P13K-Akt-mTOR pathway [64]. Consistent with this, a Drosophila SCA51 model expressing THAP11 repeats showed neurodegeneration and locomotor defects which could be alleviated by rapamycin. These findings suggest that THAP11-polyQ pathology may involve two downstream responses- altered P13K-Akt-mTOR signalling and heightened neuroinflammatory signalling- which together can contribute to cerebellar degeneration.

Future work is needed to define population-specific normal and pathogenic THAP11 CAG repeat sizes to improve molecular diagnosis of patients with SCA51. More targeted studies could also examine how these polyQ expansions drive Purkinje cell loss, whether this is due to cytoplasmic aggregation of mutant THAP11, a loss of THAP11’s transcriptional regulation, or both.

CblX

CblX is a rare X-linked inborn error of cobalamin (vitamin B12) metabolism with a similar phenotype to cblC, but is also accompanied by severe neurodevelopmental abnormalities, including seizures and developmental delay [65]. Unlike classic cblC, which typically results from biallelic variants in MMACHC leading to impaired processing of vitamin B12, cblX arises from loss-of-function variants in HCFC1 or THAP11, disrupting the HCF-1/THAP11 (Ronin) transcriptional complex — leading to epigenetic silencing of MMACHC and other target genes, rather than direct promoter binding.

These pathogenic variants in THAP11 were first identified through Sanger Sequencing of three cblX patients with no known variants in HCF-1 or MMACHC, where it was found that one of these patients contained a homozygous missense variant in THAP11, located within the THAP domain, c.240C > G (p.Phe80Leu) [66]. The pathogenicity of this variant was then validated in vivo through morpholino knockdown of thap11 in zebrafish, which resulted in severe craniofacial defects, altered brain morphology, and impaired proliferation and differentiation of neural progenitor cells, consistent with loss-of-function variants of HCF-1 in mice.

In order to better understand the molecular mechanisms, the THAP11 p.Phe80Leu variant was introduced into HEK-293 cells using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knock-in [13]. In these cells, mRNA expression was unchanged compared to WT levels, whereas protein expression was reduced, suggesting that this mutation likely leads to protein destabilization and degradation. ChIP-seq and RNA-seq revealed that the p.Phe80Leu variant causes a loss of promoter occupancy at normal THAP11 targets, leading to downregulation of these target genes, which are primarily zinc-finger proteins involved in transcription regulation. In addition, MMACHC was downregulated in the RNA-seq dataset and no longer bound by the mutant THAP11-p.Phe80Leu, providing a direct mechanistic link to pathogenic HCF-1 variants.

Future studies could test whether restoring expression of targets of THAP11/HCF-1 beyond MMACHC (such as those involved in transcriptional regulation) can alleviate the neurodevelopmental phenotypes in vivo, clarifying which targets are responsible for this severe neurological presentation more commonly observed in cblX compared to classic cblC.

Epileptic encephalopathy

Epileptic encephalopathies are a severe neurodevelopmental disorder which presents in infancy or early childhood and often includes multiform and drug-resistant seizures, neurological deficits, and/or decreased life expectancy [67]. Given their high resistance to standard anti-seizure medications (ASMs), identifying novel mechanisms of epileptic encephalopathies is key to identifying suitable targets for future treatments [68].

Recently, our lab has identified ultra-rare biallelic variants in THAP12 to be associated with Infantile Spasms (IS) and Lennox-Gastaut Syndrome (LGS), an epileptic encephalopathy resulting in neurodevelopmental delay, motor impairment, absent language, and seizures (Ochenkowska et al., unpublished). Through whole-genome sequencing (WGS), it was revealed that two siblings symptomatically diagnosed with LGS had inherited compound heterozygous mutations in THAP12, with a paternally inherited missense variant (p.P277T), and a maternally inherited frameshift variant resulting in a premature stop codon (p.E105NfsTer2). Immunoblotting against the endogenous THAP12 in patient-derived fibroblasts revealed a significant loss of the THAP12 protein, suggesting a loss-of-function mechanism. Modelling this loss-of-function in zebrafish results in severe neurodevelopmental defects phenocopying the clinical presentation of the probands. Furthermore, mice harbouring the patient variants, as well as complete knockout models, exhibit early embryonic lethality, underscoring the importance of THAP12 in early embryogenesis. Future studies are needed to elucidate the specific molecular mechanisms by which THAP12 regulates brain development to better understand how its loss leads to these severe neurodevelopmental phenotypes.

THAP proteins form dimers

A recurring theme across the human THAP family is the presence of a conserved C-terminal coiled-coil segment that is predicted to mediate homodimerization. However, coiled-coil annotation differs depending on the prediction tools used. Sanghavi et al. identified coiled-coil forming regions in 11 of the 12 human THAP-domain-containing proteins, with none detected in THAP10, using Multicoild and LOGICOIL [11]. In contrast, Dehaene et al. used COILS and PairCoil2 and did not detect coiled-coil regions in THAP9, THAP10, or THAP12 [13]. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in the prediction algorithms and thresholds used in the analyses and suggests that the coiled-coils predicted in THAP9 and THAP12 should be considered of low confidence. Consistent with this interpretation, THAP9 and THAP12 oligomerize through their N-terminal regions, which we have further supported using in silico predictions with AlphaFold (Fig. 3C, D), contrasting with the classical dimer formation of THAP proteins mediated through C-terminal coiled-coils [39], [69]. As for THAP10, current evidence supports the absence of a recognizable coiled-coil region, and whether it is capable of oligomerization through alternative regions is currently unknown.

The functional role of homodimer formation is currently unknown, but evidence suggests that THAP protein homodimerization may be necessary for DNA binding and/or transcriptional regulation. In THAP1, a consensus binding sequence with a core GGCA motif was identified and often appeared twice as direct or inverted repeats in target sequences [3] (Fig. 3E). The presence of direct and indirect repeat consensus sequences supports the notion that THAP proteins form homodimers, thereby mediating their ability to bind cis-regulatory elements, which may strengthen their binding specificity relative to a single domain binding to DNA [70]. This is further supported by the finding that genetic disruption of the coiled-coil region in THAP7 and THAP11 reduces cell viability in HEK293FT cells, suggesting that THAP7 and THAP11 homodimer formation is essential for their ability to bind DNA and regulate cell cycle progression [13].

In addition to mediating homodimerization, coiled-coil domains commonly function as protein–protein interaction modules and can serve as molecular scaffolds to help assemble larger regulatory complexes [71], [72]. Consistent with this, THAP7 was shown to interact with the chromatin regulator TAF-Iβ through its C-terminal predicted coiled-coil region (refer to Section B), indicating that THAP coiled-coils can mediate recruitment of non-THAP partners involved in transcriptional regulation. Future studies aimed at identifying protein partners of THAP proteins (through in silico predictions or experimental testing using co-immunoprecipitation) that are mediated by these coiled-coil regions could clarify how coiled-coil-dependent interactions contribute to specific co-regulator recruitment.

In addition to forming homodimers, there is evidence that THAP proteins may directly bind and interact with one another, also forming heterodimers. This is supported by previous work showing that THAP9 colocalizes with THAP1, THAP10, and THAP11 [69]. However, whether this colocalization is due to heterodimer or higher-order heterooligomer formation is unknown, as is the functional outcome of these potential complexes. Structural predictions using AlphaFold suggest that most THAP proteins likely form both homodimers and heterodimers (Fig. 3C, D). That said, predicting correct pairs of proteins that interact through coiled coils remains challenging and warrants further experimental confirmation to elucidate their molecular functions [73]. In particular, since THAP1 and THAP11 have been extensively linked to neurological disease, understanding whether and how these two proteins interact will have significant implications for our understanding of their variants’ pathogenicity and may reveal shared mechanisms of pathology.

Future directions and concluding remarks

To date, many investigations of THAP proteins have been limited to in-vitro systems. Expanding this work to in-vivo models will be crucial for revealing their developmental and physiological roles within complex biological environments. Our DIOPT orthology analysis, based on the DRSC Integrative Ortholog Prediction Tool (DIOPT), which integrates predictions from multiple independent orthology algorithms, indicates that THAP paralogs are broadly conserved in mammals, with generally lower orthology support in non-mammalian vertebrates and limited orthologs detected in invertebrates and yeast (Fig. 4D) [74, 75]. To facilitate visualization, DIOPT scores were grouped into high-confidence (> 10 supporting algorithms), intermediate-confidence (3–10 supporting algorithms), and low-confidence (< 3 supporting algorithms) categories. These results suggest that vertebrate models are best suited for future functional studies of THAP paralogs. Notably, several THAP genes, particularly THAP6, THAP8, THAP9, and THAP10, show weak or absent orthology in commonly used non-human models, raising the question that they have evolved lineage-specific functions contributing to human biology. A key next step will be to define where and when these poorly conserved THAP genes are expressed in humans throughout development. If these paralogs are enriched during cortical development, they could regulate human-specific transcriptional programs, which may have contributed to the expansion and specialization of the human cortex.

Although still relatively underexplored, the THAP protein family is emerging as a versatile group of transcriptional regulators with critical roles in cell cycle control, biogenesis, and stem/progenitor cell function. Accumulating evidence suggests that distinct THAP paralogs can act as either promotors or restrictors of proliferation depending on the cellular context, linking them to developmental disorders and cancer (Fig. 5). Future work should focus on establishing the subcellular localization of THAP proteins with antibodies validated in knockout (KO) tissues or cells, to confirm the subcellular localization of certain THAP proteins evaluated by immunocytochemistry in the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) [76]. In addition, many associations of THAP proteins remain correlative (without evidence of direct associations) this key gap must be addressed by defining their exact molecular function, including direct genomic targets, interacting cofactors, coiled-coil functions, and lineage-specific activities. Addressing this through genome-wide and molecular interactome studies will be critical for understanding how their transcriptional control directly shapes cell fate decisions.
