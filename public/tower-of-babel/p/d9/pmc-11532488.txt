Rheumatoid arthritis associated recurrent pleural effusion
Authors: Ramsha Abbas, Muhammad Ahmed Khan, Anthony El Khoury, AlBatool AlMahdy, Mohammad Abu Hishmeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPleural effusions and pulmonary nodules are known complications of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) though pleural extra-articular manifestations without clinical arthritis are rare. We present a 66-year-old male with dyspnea and weight loss, whose imaging revealed pleural effusions and lung nodules, multiple exudative pleural effusions, and a thoracoscopic biopsy showing chronic pleuritis and granulomatous inflammation. Subsequent joint stiffness and positive rheumatoid markers led to a RA diagnosis. Patient received prednisone and methotrexate, leading to symptomatic improvement but with recurrent pleural effusions, requiring regular thoracenteses. RA can cause pleural effusions and lung nodules without arthritis necessitating advanced treatments for persistent effusions.

Introduction

Pleural effusions and pulmonary nodules are known complications of rheumatoid arthritis (RA); however, it is rare for patients to have an initial presentation of pleural extra articular manifestations in the absence of clinical arthritis. In fact, one study showed that pulmonary manifestation developed after articular symptoms in 98 % of patients [1]. We present the case of a 66-year-old male with recurrent unilateral pleural effusion, requiring multiple thoracentesis procedures, and lung nodules; who was subsequently diagnosed with RA. The aim of this paper is to describe a case with a rare initial presentation that may result in diagnostic difficulty.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old man with a known history of coronary artery disease, and asbestos exposure presented with worsening dyspnea on exertion and unintentional weight loss. He denied a history of tobacco use or prior pulmonary disease. Patient's home medications at this time included metoprolol and atorvastatin. One month prior to presentation, he underwent chest x-ray imaging with his primary care provider for concern of dyspnea, which found a mild to moderate sized right sided pleural effusion. Further imaging with computed tomography (CT) of the chest demonstrated the right effusion as well as bilateral spiculated lung nodules. He subsequently underwent thoracentesis with 360ml of serosanguinous fluid removed. Pleural fluid analysis was consistent with a lymphocytic exudative effusion with negative cytology (Table 1).

He was scheduled for a biopsy of the nodules, however, he returned to the hospital two weeks following the thoracentesis with worsening dyspnea. Repeat CT of the chest revealed reaccumulated moderate right pleural effusion mild atelectasis, a lobulated nodule in the lateral left lower lobe measuring 1.7 × 0.9cm and few other nodular densities that appear stable compared to previous CT (Fig. 1A, Fig. 1B).

Thoracentesis was performed and 520ml of serosanguinous fluid was drained. Fluid analysis revealed neutrophil-predominant exudative effusion with cytology negative for malignancy. Fluid gram stain, bacterial, fungal, and acid-fast bacilli cultures were negative. Given his recurrent exudative pleural effusions, he underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) with pleural biopsy and decortication. Right pleural nodule frozen section revealed dense hyalinized connective tissue with associated necrotic inflammatory infiltrate (Fig. 2).

Right parietal pleura: chronic pleuritis with lymphoid aggregates, prominent plasma cells, and eosinophils. (100x and 200x).

Right pleural peel frozen section revealed fibrinous exudate with necrotic purulent tissue. CT-guided lung biopsy of the left lung nodule revealed necrotizing granulomatous inflammation without evidence of malignancy (Fig. 3).

Left lung nodule: necrotizing granulomatous inflammation (stains negative for acid-fast and fungal organisms) (100x and 200x).

Fungal and mycobacterial stains were negative. A follow-up chest x-ray three months later revealed reaccumulating moderate right pleural effusion with loculated components. At this time, he developed symptoms of leg, ankle, and hip pain with ambulation. Repeat CT chest demonstrated recurrent moderate pleural effusion with mild loculations and stable nodules. Over the next few months, he continued to have recurring dyspnea followed by upper extremity joint stiffness and synovitis prompting rheumatological work-up, which was notable for positive antinuclear antibodies, rheumatoid factor, and anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide. Based on his serologies and clinical presentation, it was deemed that his recurrent unilateral pleural effusion and pulmonary nodules were a likely result of RA. He was initially started on prednisone and was later transitioned to methotrexate with significant improvement in his arthralgia although there was recurrence of his unilateral pleural effusion. Patient underwent two more subsequent thoracentesis procedures in the span of 6 months. CT scan about a year after initial presentation demonstrated enlarging left upper lobe nodules (Fig. 4A, Fig. 4B). Patient was transitioned to methotrexate injections by rheumatology with hopes of resolution of extra articular manifestations, though this was also discontinued due to the increasing size of nodules. At this time, no further investigations were pursued to determine an alternate diagnosis as the progression of nodules was attributed to methotrexate use. The patient was started on infliximab injections which lead to improvement of his articular symptoms and lack of recurrence of his pleural effusion over the course of one year.

Clinical discussion

Our patient initially presented with dyspnea on exertion and weight loss. Given his history of asbestos exposure, the concern for a thoracic malignancy and mesothelioma was high. The presence of lung nodules on imaging elevated our concern, however, following lack of pathological evidence of malignancy, and a negative infectious workup, the diagnosis became more challenging to obtain. After the patient expressed symptoms of arthralgias of multiple joints, RA was considered as a potential etiology. RA as a cause for pleural effusion and nodules should be considered in the absence of rheumatoid features once other conditions, such as malignancy and infection, have been ruled out. After each thoracentesis, the patient reports improvement of dyspnea with recurrence of symptoms when the fluid reaccumulates.

Discussion

Though pleural disease is a known manifestation of RA, it is usually subclinical; thus, this patient's extensive and recurrent dyspnea are unique presentations. Pleural disease is most common in patients with longstanding RA but can precede joint disease [2]. It is more common in men and coexists with rheumatoid nodules or interstitial lung disease in up to 30 percent of patients. RA associated pleural involvement is commonly an exudative effusion with characteristically low pH, low glucose, and elevated lactate dehydrogenase levels with neutrophilic predominance such as in our patient [3]; although cholesterol effusions, non-expandable lung due to pleural inflammation, drug-induced pleuritis disease from modifying agents or biologics can be seen. Eventually these findings may progress to an empyema, bronchopleural fistulas or pneumothorax. Of note, patients with rheumatoid pleural effusions are at an increased risk for developing empyema [4].

Rheumatoid pleural effusions usually do not require targeted treatment as they commonly resolve spontaneously or with treatment of RA joint disease [5]. If they are symptomatic and do not resolve spontaneously, as in our patient, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents, oral glucocorticoids, or in advanced cases, intrapleural glucocorticoids are efficacious. Therapeutic thoracentesis is vital treatment for acute relief of symptoms. In cases of recurrent pleural effusions despite RA treatment, as was described in our case, anti-tumor necrosis factor agents have been proven beneficial and can be considered if management with methotrexate is not successful [6].

Multiple rheumatoid lung nodules can be found and biopsy findings overlap with those of granulomatous infections. These nodules can remain stable, such as in our patient, regress or even enlarge and therefore should be monitored [5,7]. Of note, patients with rheumatoid nodules tend to have a greater incidence of severe disease phenotypes, including advanced joint destruction [8]. In the absence of complications such as cavitation and rupture, no specific treatment is needed for rheumatoid lung nodules. The prognosis is good, with spontaneous resolution and infrequent complications [7]. Of note, certain medications such as methotrexate, can cause accelerated pulmonary nodulosis which was considered a possibility in our patient prompting a change in management [9].

As in our patient, RA pleural fluid analysis typically reveals a white cell count <5000/mm3, a pleural fluid glucose <60 mg/dL, or a pleural fluid to serum glucose ratio less than 0.5, a pH less than 7.3, and high pleural fluid lactate dehydrogenase level (i.e. greater than 700 international units/L). Further absence of infection or malignancy rule out other common etiologies of exudative effusions. Effusions secondary to medications may be considered in differentials, though would be unlikely in our patient who takes metoprolol. Effusions have been described to be seen with other beta blockers such as practolol and oxyprenolol with associated pleural fibrosis [10].

Cytologic examination of rheumatoid pleural fluid often reveal multinucleated macrophages and necrotic background debris, though the specificity of these findings is not established [11]. Characteristic features of rheumatoid lung nodules include a central area of necrosis surrounded by concentric layers of palisading macrophages and lymphocytes which was characteristically seen in this patient's lung nodule biopsy, cementing the diagnosis whilst ruling out malignancy [12].

Our patient's initial erythrocyte sedimentation rate and C-reactive protein were elevated at 35mm/hr and 2.8 mg/dL, respectively, and initial complete blood counts revealed a normocytic anemia with hemoglobin 9-11 gm/dL and mean corpuscular volume around 94 fL, consistent with RA's inflammatory etiology. The patient's positive rheumatoid factor at 384.3 IU/ml (normal <14), and anti-cyclic citrullinated antibody assay at >250 units (reference range 0–19 units) are very specific (85 and 95 percent) for a serological diagnosis of RA [13].

Conclusion

As clinicians, RA should be kept in mind as a differential in those with recurrent pleural effusions and pulmonary nodules in absence of evidence of malignancy and infection, even if no other rheumatologic features are present.

Further rheumatologic investigation should be considered if no specific etiology has been identified.

In the setting of recurrent effusion despite RA therapy, other modalities should be considered like Anti-TNF agents or pleural effusion management with long term catheter placement.
