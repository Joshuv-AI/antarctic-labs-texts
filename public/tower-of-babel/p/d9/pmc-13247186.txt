Genetic variants of glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) signalling as proxy for body weight reduction and cardiovascular risk
Authors: Frida Emanuelsson, Børge G Nordestgaard, Marianne Benn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsAnti-obesity therapies co-targeting the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor achieve greater weight loss compared with glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists. However, the implications for cardiovascular risk reduction and the mechanisms involved remain unclear. This study aimed to (i) investigate whether genetically proxied body weight reduction via the GIP receptor pathway lowers cardiovascular disease risk and (ii) compare the effect to polygenic weight reduction, excluding GIP-related genes, to assess if the observed benefits are attributable to weight loss per se or involve additional GIP-related effects.MethodsGenetic scores were constructed using four variants in GIP-related genes associated with lower body mass index (BMI) and 31 variants linked to lower BMI in general. Observational and one-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses were performed in 408 056 individuals from the UK Biobank and two-sample MR analyses using summary statistics from 419 821 individuals in FinnGen.ResultsIn one-sample MR analyses, 1 kg/m² lower BMI via the GIP/GIPR score was associated with 29% lower risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) (P = .0002) and 43% lower risk of heart failure (P = 5 × 10⁻⁵). Corresponding lower risks with the polygenic BMI score were 3% (P = .002) and 13% (P < 1 × 10−300). Two-sample MR analyses showed similar results. Of the reduced risk via the GIP/GIPR score, BMI and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) mediated 13% and 22% of the lower risk of MACE and 16% and 17% of the lower risk of heart failure (all P ≤ 7 × 10−5).ConclusionsGenetic proxies for body weight reduction via GIP receptor targeting reduces the risk of MACE and heart failure, mediated partly through lower BMI and partly through lower HbA1c.Keywords: Obesity, Cardiovascular disease, Mendelian randomization, Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor

See the editorial comment for this article ‘More than a sidekick: GIP signalling and cardiovascular outcomes’, by B. Kurt and F. Kahles, https://doi.org/10.1093/eurheartj/ehaf944.

Introduction

The development of incretin-based therapies has marked a significant advancement in the management of Type 2 diabetes and obesity. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists, initially designed as glucose-lowering agents for Type 2 diabetes, have been successfully repurposed as anti-obesity treatment, achieving up to a 12% reduction in body weight compared with placebo in individuals with obesity.1,2 Moreover, GLP-1 receptor agonists have demonstrated efficacy in reducing the risk of cardiovascular events in individuals with overweight or obesity and a history of cardiovascular disease.3,4

In parallel, the glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor has gained attention as a pharmacological target. However, the complexity and limited understanding of GIP receptor signalling in human metabolism have posed significant challenges to the development of drugs targeting this receptor. Beyond its well-established role in glucose metabolism, GIP exerts various effects, including effects on lipid metabolism, the heart, and the cardiovascular system.5–7 In clinical trials, a dual GLP-1 and GIP receptor co-agonist, tirzepatide, has demonstrated superior reductions in plasma glucose levels and body weight compared with a GLP-1 receptor agonist alone, with a change in body weight of 20% with tirzepatide compared with 14% with the GLP-1 receptor agonist semaglutide after 3 years of treatment.8–10 However, it remains unclear whether these larger reductions in glycaemia and body weight also translate into a larger reduction in cardiovascular risk.11,12

We aimed to investigate the effect of body weight reduction mediated by the GIP/GIP receptor pathway on risk of cardiovascular disease. We used genetic variants associated with lower body mass index (BMI) and glucose or glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) in the GIP and GIPR genes, encoding the GIP peptide and its receptor, as proxies for the body weight- and glucose-lowering effects of pharmacological GIP receptor targeting. For comparative purposes, we also examined the effect of body weight reduction per se on the risk of cardiovascular disease using a polygenic proxy for lower body weight not including the GIP and GIPR genes, a novel approach. This approach allows us to assess whether the potential reduction in cardiovascular risk associated with genetically proxied GIP receptor targeting mirrors the beneficial effects of weight loss in general. Similar effects would suggest that the potential beneficial effects of GIP receptor targeting on risk cardiovascular disease is attributable to the weight loss per se and related cardiometabolic improvements.

Methods

Study design and participants

Overall, 408 056 individuals of European ancestry from the UK Biobank with available baseline measurements of weight and height were included for observational and one- and two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses. Summary data of up to 419 821 individuals of Finnish ancestry from the FinnGen Consortium were included for two-sample MR analyses.

The UK Biobank

The UK Biobank is a prospective general population study initiated in 2006–2010, including ∼500 000 individuals between 40 and 69 years of age from the UK.13 Data collection at the initial assessment visit included a self-administrated questionnaire, physical measurements including anthropometric measures, blood sampling, and DNA extraction. A detailed description of the study design, participants, and measurements including quality control has been published previously.14 Written informed consent was obtained from all individuals and ethics approval from the North West Multicentre Research Ethics Committee (MREC) (ethical approval number 16/NW/0274).

The FinnGen consortium

The FinnGen consortium is a large-scale genomics initiative initiated in 2017, including over 500 000 Finnish biobank samples with the aim to correlate genetic variation with health data from national health registries to understand disease mechanisms and predispositions.15 Participants have a median age of 63 years and are predominantly recruited at hospitals. The project is a collaboration between research organizations and biobanks within Finland and international industry partners. We used summary data from FinnGen data freeze 12, released 4 November 2024. FinnGen is approved by the Coordinating Ethics Committee of the Helsinki and Uusimaa Hospital District (ethical approval number: HUS/990/2017).

Body mass index and covariates in the UK Biobank

Measured weight and height at study entry were used to calculate BMI as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. Sex was obtained from the National Health Service registry at recruitment and corrected by the participant at inclusion if incorrect. Information on current smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and menopausal status for women was self-reported. HbA1c was measured by high-performance liquid chromatography (VARIANT II Turbo, Bio-Rad, Hercules, California, USA). Plasma low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol and triglycerides were measured by standard enzymatic assays, and high sensitivity C-reactive protein by an immunoturbidimetric assay. Hypertension was defined according to the European Society of Cardiology guideline as systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg (≥135 mmHg for individuals with diabetes), diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg (≥85 mmHg for individuals with diabetes),16 and/or self-reported use of antihypertensive drugs specifically prescribed for hypertension. Blood pressure was measured in the clinic during the assessment visit as mean of two automated measurements taken a few moments apart, and a manual sphygmomanometer was used if the standard automated device could not be employed. Missing values for laboratory measurements (0%–4.9% missing) and self-reported covariates (0%–36% missing) were not imputed. Variables used in the UK Biobank are listed in Supplementary data online, Table S1.

Genotypes and instrument selection

In the UK Biobank, we used genotype data analysed on two closely related purpose-designed arrays, the UK BiLEVE Axiom array and the UK Biobank Axiom array. Details regarding the platforms and genotyping including quality control are described elsewhere.13 FinnGen individuals were genotyped with Illumina and Affymetrix chip arrays (Illumina Inc., San Diego, and Thermo Fisher Scientific, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

To construct a genetic proxy for weight loss by the GIP/GIP receptor pathway, we first identified genetic variants associated with BMI and/or glycaemic traits and Type 2 diabetes in or within 50 kb on either side of the GIP and GIPR genes in publicly available summary data from the largest genome-wide association studies available in individuals of European descent and not including the UK Biobank.17–19 This was done to justify the use of internal weights in the UK Biobank and to reduce the risk of ‘winner’s curse’.20 A liberal significance threshold of P ≤ 5 × 10−5 was chosen in this first step to accommodate for the low number of included individuals in these datasets and the low number of available variants. Two larger genome-wide association study (GWAS) datasets for BMI and Type 2 diabetes, including the UK Biobank,19,21 were used for further verification, with a significance threshold of P < 1 × 10−8. Linkage disequilibrium was examined using the web-based tool LDlink22 and variants in linkage disequilibrium, defined as r2 ≥ 0.2, were excluded. The selected variants from the first step were then identified in the UK Biobank, and variants that also associated with BMI and HbA1c in the UK Biobank, and with directionally concordant effects, were selected for the GIP/GIPR allele score, rs4794008 and rs2291725 in GIP and rs2302593 and rs35568293 in GIPR (see Supplementary data online, Table S2 and Supplementary data online, Table S3). A locus in GIPR previously identified as a top hit for BMI in GWAS, rs1800437, was not included, as this variant had discordant effects on BMI and HbA1c both in publicly available GWAS data and in individual level data in the UK Biobank.23

As a polygenic proxy for general weight loss, we identified genetic variants associated with BMI in publicly available summary data in the largest available GWAS in individuals of European descent not including the UK Biobank, with a significance threshold of <5 × 10−8 and excluding variants within 50 kb on either side of the GIP and GIPR genes (n = 1503).17 We excluded variants in linkage disequilibrium with a threshold of r2 < 0.001, according to the 1000 Genomes European reference panel and using the clumping function in the TwoSampleMR package in R. Of 33 variants passing the linkage threshold, 31 were also associated (P < 5 × 10−8) with BMI in the UK Biobank and were selected for further analyses (see Supplementary data online, Table S4).

Using the selected GIP/GIPR variants and the polygenic BMI variants, respectively, genotypes were ordered according to BMI-lowering effect, and weighted allele scores were generated for each individual by coding genotypes 0, 1, and 2 for non-carriers, heterozygous, and homozygous carriers, respectively, and multiplying by the adjusted β-coefficient for each variant. The adjusted β-coefficients were derived from linear regression of the effect of the genotype on BMI, adjusted for age, sex, and for the GIP/GIPR score, the remaining variants included in the score. This was done to account for any remaining bias due to linkage structure.

Endpoints

The cardiovascular disease endpoints included were 3-point major adverse cardiovascular event (3-point MACE), 4-point MACE, and heart failure and were based on diagnosis codes from national health registries. A 3-point MACE was a composite of non-fatal myocardial infarction, non-fatal stroke, and cardiovascular death. A 4-point MACE was 3-point MACE plus coronary revascularisation (percutaneous coronary intervention and coronary artery bypass graft). In the UK Biobank, diagnoses according to the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 9th and 10th revisions and procedures according to the Office of Population Censuses and Surveys Classification of Interventions and Procedures (OPCS-3 and OPCS-4) were extracted from the Hospital Inpatient Data and Death register fields, which links to inpatient hospital data and death registries from the UK. Detailed information is available at https://biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/crystal/ukb/docs/inpatient_mapping.pdf and https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/ukb/docs/DeathLinkage.pdf. For the two-sample MR analyses, summary-level data for each endpoint were extracted from FinnGen data Release 12, available at www.finngen.fi. In FinnGen, endpoints were defined by diagnoses by ICD versions 8–10, Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification codes, and KELA reimbursement codes, and procedures by the Nordic Medico-Statistical Committee (NOMESCO) Classification of Surgical Procedures, Finnish Hospital league, and Heart patient codes. For detailed information and codes for each endpoint in UK Biobank and FinnGen, respectively, see Supplementary data online, Table S5. In observational analyses in the UK Biobank, follow-up began at the first inclusion into the study and ended with censoring at the date of death or event, on 31 May 2022 (corresponding to the latest update including updates for the UK, of the Hospital Inpatient Data field). During a median follow-up time of 13 years (range 0–16 years), 19 288 individuals were diagnosed with 3-point MACE, 23 890 with 4-point MACE, and 13 878 with heart failure. As an individual’s genotype is constant throughout life, follow-up for the genetic analyses began at the first date including updates of the Hospital Inpatient Data field for the UK (1 January 1999), i.e. including events registered before the date the individual entered the study and provided DNA for genetic analyses. Of the 401 677 individuals with available GIP/GIPR genotypes, this yielded 23 869 with 3-point MACE, 31 997 with 4-point MACE, and 15 441 with heart failure. Of the 408 056 individuals with available polygenic BMI score, corresponding numbers were 24 262, 32 519, and 15 702, respectively.

Statistical analyses

We used Stata SE 17.0 for all statistical analyses. The observational associations between continuous BMI and risk of 3-point MACE, 4-point MACE, and heart failure were examined using restricted cubic splines with three knots incorporated in a Cox proportional hazards model and using the population median BMI of 26.7 kg/m2 as reference. The number of knots selected were based on Akaike’s information criterion, to obtain the best fit while limiting the likelihood of overfitting the data.24 The models were adjusted for age (as underlying variable), sex, birth year (to adjust for changes in diagnostics and treatment practises over time), current smoking, physical activity level, and menopausal status for women, well-known, non-metabolic risk factors of cardiovascular disease.25 The association between the genetic variants, the GIP/GIPR score, and the polygenic BMI score with BMI and HbA1c were tested using linear regression, adjusted for age and sex. To test for trend across the genetic scores in quintiles and BMI and HbA1c, we used the non-parametric Cuzick’s extension of a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Cox proportional hazards regression models adjusted for age and sex were used to estimate the risk of endpoints as a function of the gene scores in quintiles. One-sample MR analyses of the potential causal effect of BMI on the endpoints was performed by instrumental variable analysis and the MR ratio estimator,26 using the weighted GIP/GIPR score and the polygenic BMI score as instruments, respectively. Estimates for the GIP/GIPR score and the polygenic BMI score were compared using a two-sided test. Because our analyses included four endpoints, a multiple-testing corrected (by the Bonferroni method, 0.05/3) P-value of P < .017 corresponded to P < .05. The strength of the genetic instruments (i.e. the association between the genetic scores and BMI) was assessed by F statistics in the first-stage regression.27,28 Potential pleiotropic effects of the genetic scores on MACE and heart failure through the measured confounders sex, age, level of physical activity, smoking, and alcohol intake and the potential mediators, hypertension, HbA1c, LDL cholesterol, triglycerides, and high sensitivity C-reactive protein, were investigated using logistic regression. For sensitivity analyses and external validation, two-sample MR analyses were performed for the four GIP/GIPR genetic variants, the 31 polygenic BMI variants, and the endpoints, using inverse variance weighted, MR Egger, and MR weighted median regression.29,30 The genotype–outcome association was derived from the UK Biobank, and the genotype-endpoint associations from the UK Biobank and FinnGen, respectively. An overview of the different MR analyses performed is shown in Supplementary data online, Table S6.

The proportion of lower risk of 3-point MACE, 4-point MACE, and heart failure caused by GIP/GIPR genetic variation that was mediated by BMI and glycaemic control, respectively, was estimated using the proportion of coefficients method.31

To estimate the absolute 10 year risk reduction for MACE and heart failure, we first calculated the association between genetically instrumented BMI and each outcome using the inverse variance weighted method for the GIP/GIPR allele score and polygenic BMI allele score. Hazard ratios (HR) per a 1 kg/m² lower genetically predicted BMI were scaled to a 5 kg/m² lower BMI to represent a clinically meaningful difference. We then estimated the absolute 10 year risk for 3-point MACE, 4-point MACE, and heart failure using Kaplan–Meier cumulative incidence functions. The absolute risk reduction was derived by applying the scaled HR to the observed 10 year absolute risks in the population.

Genetic variation in GIP and GIPR and risk of disease

The genetic variants in GIP and GIPR were separately and combined into a genetic score associated with stepwise lower mean BMI and HbA1c (P for trend <1 × 10−300 for both) (Figure 2, upper panels). Individuals in the highest quintile of the GIP/GIPR genetic score had on average 0.2 kg/m2 (0.73%) lower BMI and 0.27 mmol/mol (0.75%) lower HbA1c compared with individuals in the lowest GIP/GIPR genetic score quintile. Using linear regression, the β-coefficient for the association between the GIP/GIPR genetic score and BMI was 0.5 [standard error (SE):0.05], P = 1 × 10−21; and 0.61 (0.07), P = 1 × 10−18 for HbA1c per unit higher weighted GIP/GIPR allele score (Figure 2, lower panels).

Individuals in the higher quintiles of the GIP/GIPR genetic score had lower BMI and HbA1c and had stepwise lower risk of 3-point and 4-point MACE and heart failure (Figure 3). The corresponding HRs were 0.93 (0.89–0.96), P = 1 × 10−5 for 4-point MACE and 0.92 (0.88–0.97), P = .001 for heart failure (Figure 3). One- and two-sample Mendelian randomization analyses

One-sample MR analyses of the association between genetically predicted lower BMI by the GIP/GIPR genetic score and by the polygenic BMI score and risk of 3-point MACE, 4-point MACE, and heart failure are shown in Figure 4. Instrument strength for the allele scores were confirmed by F-values of 90 for the GIP/GIPR score and 4925 for the polygenic BMI score, explaining 0.02% and 1.3% of the variation in BMI, respectively. There was a lower risk of all endpoints with genetically predicted lower BMI and with overall lower estimates for the GIP/GIPR score vs the polygenic BMI score. For 3-point MACE, there was a HR of 0.75 (0.62–0.92), P = .007 per 1 kg/m2 lower BMI by the GIP/GIPR genetic score and 0.97 (0.94–0.99), P = .008 per 1 kg/m2 lower BMI for the polygenic BMI score, with a P for comparison between the two estimates of P = .01. In two-sample MR analyses, using the UK Biobank only and combining data for the genotype-BMI association from the UK Biobank with data for the genotype–outcome association from FinnGen, results were largely similar for both the GIP/GIPR variants and the polygenic BMI variants and for alle three endpoints and with no indication of pleiotropy (all P for the MR Egger intercept ≥.18) (see Supplementary data online, Figure S5).

Potential confounders and mediators

The associations between BMI, the GIP/GIPR genetic score, the polygenic BMI score, MACE, and heart failure, and potential confounders and mediators of the BMI–outcome association are shown in Supplementary data online, Figure S6. There was no association between the potential confounders sex, age, physical activity, smoking, alcohol intake, and menopause for women and the GIP/GIPR score and with largely similar results for the polygenic BMI score, indicating a low risk of pleiotropic effects through these variables. The GIP/GIPR genetic score and the polygenic BMI score were both associated with hypertension and concentrations of HbA1c, triglycerides, and C-reactive protein, indicating that the effect of lower BMI on risk of MACE and heart failure is partly mediated through changes in these variables (see Supplementary data online, Figures S6 and S7).

Absolute 10 year risk reduction

The absolute 10 year risk reduction of 3-point MACE was 4.3% per 5 kg/m² lower BMI instrumented by the GIP/GIPR allele score and 1.3% instrumented by the polygenic BMI score (Figure 6). The corresponding 10 year absolute risk reductions were 5.5% and 1.5% for 4-point MACE and 6.0% and 4.0% for heart failure, respectively.

Discussion

In this observational and one- and two-sample MR study of 408 056 individuals from the UK Biobank and 419 821 from FinnGen, genetic proxies for body weight reduction via GIP receptor targeting reduced the risk of MACE and heart failure, mediated partly through lower BMI and partly through lower HbA1c (see Structured Graphical Abstract). Notably, the risk reductions observed with the GIP/GIPR score were greater than those observed with a polygenic BMI score representing general weight loss, suggesting that pharmacological targeting of the GIP receptor may confer cardiometabolic benefits beyond those attributable to weight reduction alone. These findings are novel.

Mechanistically, GIP is an incretin hormone, secreted from enteroendocrine K-cells in the proximal gut, promoting postprandial insulin secretion by activating GIP receptors in the pancreas. Beyond the pancreas, GIP receptors are also present in other tissues, including the gut, adipose tissue, heart and vasculature, and brain.32 Various mechanisms by which GIP receptor signalling may influence body weight have been suggested. These include reduced energy intake by GIP receptor signalling in the central nervous system and increased energy expenditure through increased thermogenesis in brown adipose tissue.33–35 GIP signalling has effects in adipose tissue and in lipid metabolism, enhancing insulin sensitivity and improving glucose homeostasis. It has also been shown to stimulate lipoprotein lipase activity in mice and promote the clearance of circulating triglycerides in humans.36,37

Beyond weight reduction and improved glycaemic control, GIP may also improve endothelial function and reduce vascular inflammation, leading to improved vascular and renal function, which may contribute to the overall cardiovascular benefits.38–40 Unrelated to the present findings, previous genetic studies investigated the role of GIP levels and GIP signalling in cardiovascular disease and mortality and found shared causal variants for Type 2 diabetes and cardiometabolic outcomes in the GIP and GIPR genes in publicly available consortia data.41,42

We found that a lifelong 1 kg/m² lower BMI by genetic proxies for the GIP/GIP receptor pathway, is associated with up to 29% lower risk of MACE, and 43% lower risk of heart failure, within a general population setting. In comparison, in the SELECT trial, weight management with the GLP-1 receptor agonist semaglutide reduced the risk of MACE by 20%, corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 1.5%, and the risk of worsening heart failure by 18%, in individuals with overweight or obesity and a history of cardiovascular disease over 3 years of treatment.3 In that study, the mean reduction in body weight was 8.5%, corresponding to a 2.9 kg/m² decrease in BMI (from 33.3 to 30.4 kg/m²). More recently, the dual GLP-1 and GIP receptor agonist tirzepatide was shown to reduce body weight by 11.6%, equating an average BMI reduction of 4.4 kg/m2 (from 38.3 to 33.9 kg/m²) after 2 years of treatment and to reduce the risk of worsening heart failure by 46%.43 In contrast, weight loss by lifestyle interventions have shown less efficacy in reducing risk of cardiovascular events; however, the average weight reduction in these studies has been considerably smaller.44,45 Notably, when analyses were restricted to individuals who achieved a ≥10% weight loss, a 21% reduction in cardiovascular risk was observed, which is comparable with the findings of the SELECT trial.3,46 In the Swedish Obesity Study, bariatric surgery was shown to reduce body weight by 16% (corresponding to an average reduction from 42.4 to 35.6 kg/m2 in BMI) and the risk of cardiovascular events by 33% after 15 years of follow-up.47

Mechanistically, the cardiovascular risk reduction is likely mediated by a decrease in total body fat, followed by improvements in multiple cardiometabolic risk factors.48–51 Specifically, reductions in visceral adipose tissue induce several metabolic improvements, including a decrease in the secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, reduced insulin resistance, enhanced β-cell function, and improved lipid metabolism.52,53 Additionally, reductions in perivascular adipose tissue may attenuate atherosclerosis through improved vascular endothelial function, and reductions in pericardial adipose tissue may enhance cardiac pump function due to decreased mechanical pressure, which may partly explain the major effect of weight loss on heart failure outcomes.48 The reduction in intraabdominal fat mass may also alleviate mechanical pressure on the kidneys, thereby lowering blood pressure via both mechanical and hormonal pathways. In line with this, both observational, genetic, and clinical intervention studies have demonstrated that lower body weight and body weight reduction is associated with favourable changes in blood pressure, lipid profile, glycaemic control, and markers of systemic inflammation.3,8,54–58 In clinical studies, reductions in blood pressure, hyperglycaemia, and atherogenic lipid and lipoprotein concentrations reduce the risk of cardiovascular events.59–61

We used genetic variants in the GIP and GIPR genes as proxies to investigate the effects of glucose and body weight-lowering drugs targeting the GIP receptor. These variants were selected based on their association with lower BMI and glucose/HbA1c levels in GWAS. This approach was intended to replicate the downstream phenotypic effects of such drugs. However, the approach does not account for the functional effects of the variants (or haplotypes) on a molecular level, which is a limitation of our study. Previous studies have explored genetic variation in GIPR, and functionally characterized these variants in vitro and in rodent models.62,63 These studies have shown that carriers of rare loss-of-function variants in GIPR have lower BMI, body fat percentage, and waist circumference compared with non-carriers, suggesting that impaired GIP receptor signalling is associated with reduced body weight. Both GIP receptor agonism and antagonism have been shown to reduce body weight in obese mice.64–66 Further studies are needed to elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which GIP receptor signalling, as well as agonism and antagonism of this pathway, impacts body weight, and energy homeostasis.

The only GIP-targeting drug approved to date is a dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist, activating both pathways. Thus, combining genetic proxies for GIP and GLP-1 may provide a more comprehensive representation of the therapeutic implications of GIP receptor targeting. However, neither we nor others has been able to identify genetic variants in the GLP1R gene that are significantly associated with BMI.67 Therefore, the present study could not mimic a dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist. A limitation of our study is that our analyses were restricted to individuals of European including UK and Finnish ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations. However, we are not aware of data to suggest that our results of genetic lower BMI per se would not apply to individuals of other ethnicities. The risk of horizontal pleiotropy—where the genetic variants may influence cardiovascular outcomes through pathways unrelated to BMI reduction or its downstream effects—represents an inherent limitation in Mendelian randomization studies.68 However, we found no evidence of association between the GIP/GIPR genetic score and potential confounders such as sex, age, physical activity, smoking, alcohol intake, or menopause in women. In the two-sample MR analysis, the MR Egger regression did not suggest pleiotropy. However, we cannot exclude that the analyses are biased by unmeasured confounders or horizontal pleiotropy. The strengths of our study include the large sample size of phenotypically well-characterized and genetically homogeneous individuals in the one-sample MR analysis, as well as the consistent findings in the two-sample analysis using the FinnGen cohort.

In conclusion, genetic proxies for body weight reduction via GIP receptor targeting lowers risk of MACE and heart failure. This effect is mediated partly through lower BMI and partly through lower HbA1c. Whether the reduction in cardiovascular risk by proxied GIP receptor targeting can be translated into clinical practise needs to be confirmed in randomized controlled trials of GIP receptor targeting drugs.
