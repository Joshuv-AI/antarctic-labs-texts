Controlling the structural, optical, and dielectric properties of PVP/NaAlg-MWCNT flexible nanocomposites for next-generation electronics
Authors: A. A. Al-Muntaser, Eman Alzahrani, Turki Alkathiri, Sultan Albarakati, Ahmed F. Almutairi, Saleh Aldwais, G. M. Asnag, A. Y. Yassin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, nanocomposites based on polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and sodium alginate (NaAlg) in a 30/70 wt% ratio were successfully prepared using the solution casting method and reinforced with multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs). The effect of MWCNT incorporation on the structural, optical, dielectric, and electrical properties of the pristine PVP/NaAlg blends was comprehensively investigated. XRD patterns showed that the incorporation of MWCNTs enhanced the amorphous nature of the polymeric blend. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra showed the characteristic vibrational bands of PVP and NaAlg, with noticeable shifts and intensity changes upon MWCNT loading, suggesting strong interfacial interactions. These interactions are attributed to hydrogen bonding between PVP (C = O) and NaAlg (–OH, –COOH) groups, in addition to π–π stacking and dipole–π interactions with MWCNTs. UV–vis spectroscopy demonstrated a gradual increase in optical absorption accompanied by a redshift, consistent with improved light-harvesting capability. The optical band gap decreased from 4.25 to 2.73 eV (indirect) and from 5.01 to 4.68 eV (direct). Moreover, both AC and DC conductivities, as well as dielectric properties, increased with higher MWCNT concentrations, evidencing enhanced charge transport and polarization behavior. The DC conductivity rose from 2.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ to 1.15 × 10⁻⁵ S·cm⁻¹, while the dielectric constant increased from 10 to 1.15 × 10⁵, reflecting the remarkable energy storage capability of the prepared films. The electrical conduction process follows the correlated hopping mechanism. These findings highlight the potential of PVP/NaAlg–MWCNT nanocomposite films as promising candidates for next-generation optoelectronics and energy storage applications.

Introduction

Blending polymers and embedding nanoscale fillers are two complementary strategies that have transformed materials design: blends allow tuning of mechanical, thermal, and processing behavior by combining distinct polymer attributes, while nanofillers (e.g., carbon nanotubes, metal oxides) introduce large changes in electrical, optical, and dielectric properties at low loadings1–3. Together, polymer-blend nanocomposites provide a flexible platform to produce multifunctional films and coatings for sensors, bioelectronics, energy storage, and biomedical devices4–7.

The anionic polysaccharide known as sodium alginate is a naturally generated substance that is made of β-D-mannuronic (M) and α-L-guluronic (G) acid blocks. It forms hydrogels through ionic crosslinking (e.g., Ca²⁺) and is prized for biocompatibility, film-forming ability, and biodegradability, with broad use in food, biomedical, and packaging applications8. Its hydrophilicity and ionic character make NaAlg useful in drug delivery, wound dressings, and environmentally friendly coatings, but they also limit intrinsic electrical conductivity and optical clarity in some applications9.

Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), in contrast, is a synthetic, non-ionic, water-soluble vinyl polymer bearing a polar lactam side group that promotes hydrogen bonding and miscibility with many polymers and fillers. PVP is widely used in pharmaceuticals, adhesives, and membrane/fiber formation because of its excellent film-forming capability, solubility in aqueous and organic media, and ability to stabilize nanoparticles10. Unlike NaAlg, PVP is not inherently ionically cross-linkable or biodegradable (without modification), but it imparts mechanical flexibility and improved film integrity when blended with biopolymers.

Blending PVP with NaAlg seeks a balance, where NaAlg supplies biocompatibility, gelation behavior, and ionic functionality, while PVP enhances film formation, interfacial adhesion, and processability. Blending PVP with NaAlg can guarantee improved mechanical homogeneity, tunable swelling/hydration, and enhanced capacity to host dispersed nanofillers; but their blends include potential phase separation at certain ratios, reduced thermal stability compared with some synthetic polymers, and sensitivity of ionic properties to humidity and ionic content, which can complicate dielectric measurements. Therefore, crosslinking strategy and filler dispersion play a critical role in achieving stable and reproducible properties11,12. These limitations motivate adding conductive nanofillers, such as multi-walled carbon nanotube (MWCNT), to extend functionality.

MWCNTs are well known for their high specific surface area, outstanding conductivity, and structural properties, which make them useful for applications in energy storage and flexible sensors, where high dielectric permeability and AC conductivity are required, especially upon dispersion within polymers such as PVP1,13. The polymer nanocomposites doped with MWCNTs have received great attention owing to the significant electrical conductivities, dielectric stability, and mechanical strength of MWCNTs14,15. Iqbal et al.16 reported that functionalized MWCNTs in PVP/NaAlg composite membranes enhanced reverse-osmosis performance and improved thermal stability but also increased surface roughness and required functionalization to avoid aggregation. For PANi, Sharma et al.17 observed that a lower optical bandgap was achieved with increasing MWCNT concentration in the MWCNTs/PANi matrix. Some previous studies on PVP/NaAlg systems employed hybrid inorganic fillers such as CoFe₂O₄/MWCNT composites, where electrical and optical enhancements originated from the synergistic contribution of ferrite nanoparticles and carbon nanotubes rather than from MWCNT networks alone18. In such hybrid systems, the intrinsic roles of MWCNT-induced percolation and charge transport cannot be distinguished independently. Moreover, studies focusing on MWCNT reinforcement in PVP/NaAlg have mainly investigated membrane performance (e.g., reverse osmosis, antibacterial behavior, or thermal stability) without systematically addressing electrical percolation thresholds, frequency-dependent dielectric relaxation, impedance spectroscopy, or optical band-gap evolution. Compared with those works, the current manuscript focuses explicitly on electrical/dielectric percolation, optical band-gap shifts, and structure–property correlations in a single controlled blend ratio (30/70 PVP/NaAlg), allowing a clearer mapping between MWCNT dispersion, interfacial chemistry, and multifunctional outputs.

To the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive study has yet systematically examined a controlled 30/70 wt% PVP/NaAlg matrix that correlates the evolution of dielectric, structural, and optical properties with the formation of MWCNT networks. This study therefore contributes in two ways: (1) it provides a systematic electrical and dielectric characterization (frequency-dependent permittivity, AC conductivity, and impedance spectroscopy) across a range of MWCNT loadings to identify percolation behavior in a 30/70 PVP/NaAlg host; and (2) it correlates those electrical changes to structural (XRD/FTIR), optical (UV–Vis/optical band-gap), and (DC/AC) electrical conductivity and dielectric response across frequencies to reveal how nanotube networks alter polymer blend microstructure and charge-transport pathways. Taken together, these comparisons clarify design rules for biodegradable, conductive films and coatings that must reconcile alginate’s hydrophilicity and biocompatibility with the stringent dispersion requirements of conductive carbon fillers. Therefore, this manuscript investigates MWCNT reinforcement of a PVP/NaAlg blend (30/70 wt%) to systematically evaluate enhancements in electrical, structural, optical, and dielectric performance. We hope that this work will help pave the way for more adaptable and efficient nanocomposites, which will be essential in flexible optoelectronic and energy-storage devices by providing deep insights into the relationships between MWCNT concentration and nanocomposite performance.

Materials and methods

Materials

High-viscosity sodium alginate (NaAlg), MW=350,000 g/mol, was obtained from Biochemika (Germany), while polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), MW=72,000 g/mol, was acquired from Chemical Ltd., England. Functionalized carboxylated multi-walled carbon nanotubes (MWCNTs–COOH) with a purity of 91% were sourced from Nanothinx, Greece. Double-distilled water was employed as the solvent in all preparations. All chemicals were of analytical grade and used directly without additional purification.

Preparation of nanocomposite samples

Predetermined quantities of PVP (0.36 gm) and NaAlg (0.84 gm) were separately dissolved in 40 mL and 80 mL of double distilled water (DDW), respectively. The two polymer solutions were then mixed under continuous magnetic stirring at 40 °C to form a homogeneous PVP (30%wt.)/NaAlg (70%wt.) blend solution.

In parallel, the required amount of MWCNTs was dispersed in 35 mL of DDW and stirred magnetically at room temperature to obtain a preliminary suspension. The resulting dispersion was further subjected to high-power ultrasonication using a probe sonicator (Model UIP500hdT, QSONICA, USA; 20 kHz, 500 W) for 10 min to ensure uniform dispersion of the nanotubes.

Characterization techniques

Thickness measurement

The thickness of the prepared nanocomposite films was measured using a Digimatic QuantuMike micrometer from DML Co., Ltd., Sheffield, United Kingdom (Model 293-185-30, Mitutoyo) with an accuracy of ± 0.001 mm. Each film was measured at three different points, and the average value was reported.

FTIR spectroscopy

FTIR spectra of the PVP/NaAlg–MWCNTs films were recorded at room temperature from 400 to 4000 cm⁻¹ using a Nicolet iS10 spectrometer (JASCO, USA) with 1 cm⁻¹ resolution. Air was utilized as the reference background. The MWCNTs powder spectrum was measured with the KBr pellet method for comparison. Each sample was scanned five times to ensure reliable and reproducible data.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

XRD patterns were obtained using a PANalytical X’Pert PRO diffractometer (Malvern, United Kingdom; Malvern Panalytical Co., Ltd.) with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.1541 nm). The measurements were performed over a 2θ range of 5–75° at a scanning speed of 5°/min. The instrument was operated at 40 kV and 10 mA.

UV–visible spectroscopy

The prepared sample absorbance with dimensions of 10 mm × 20 mm was recorded using a double-beam UV–Visible spectrophotometer (JASCO V-570, Japan). Measurements were taken over a wavelength range of 190–1000 nm with a 0.5 nm step. Air served as the reference, and the films were positioned in quartz cuvettes during the analysis.

Electrical and dielectric measurements

The dielectric behavior and AC conductivity for the prepared nanocomposite films were examined at room temperature in the range of frequency between 10⁻¹ and 10⁷ Hz using a broadband dielectric spectrometer (Concept-40 system, Novocontrol Turnkey). Measurements were carried out in the parallel plate configuration using copper electrodes. The applied AC voltage was 0.1 V. Each film was tested three times, and the mean values were recorded.

TEM spectroscopy

TEM analysis of the prepared nanocomposite samples was conducted using a JEOL 1200 EX microscope operating at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV.

Results and discussion

FTIR spectral analysis of PVP/NaAlg/MWCNTs nanocomposites

The analysis of the PVP/NaAlg mixture and its nanocomposites, which incorporate different amounts of MWCNTs, was conducted through FT-IR spectroscopy to investigate structural interactions and chemical modifications. The spectra in Fig. 1, recorded in the range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹, revealed the characteristic vibrational bands associated with the functional groups of both polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) and sodium alginate (NaAlg) and provided insight into the interfacial interactions induced by the MWCNTs. Also, FTIR peak positions and assignments were summarized in Table 2 for PVP/NaAlg host polymer and PVP/NaAlg/MWCNTs composites. The following discussion interprets the major spectral regions and their evolution as a function of MWCNT concentration.

For pure MWCNTs, the stretching vibrations of O-H groups were observed as a broad absorption peak at 3440 cm⁻¹. In the pure PVP and NaAlg polymers, the O-H stretching band is typically centered at 3455 cm⁻¹19. In the PVP/NaAlg blend (sample M0), this band was observed at 3460 cm⁻¹, indicating strong hydrogen bonding between the hydroxyl groups of alginate and the carbonyl groups of PVP. Upon the addition of MWCNTs (samples M1–M3), the band became broader and shifted to lower wavenumbers (3450, 3447, and 3444 cm⁻¹, respectively). This shift towards the characteristic O-H peak of MWCNTs (3440 cm⁻¹) suggests that the carboxyl groups (–COOH) on the functionalized CNT surface actively participate in interfacial hydrogen bonding with the polymer matrix, thereby strengthening the polymer–filler interaction20,21.

The pure MWCNTs exhibit characteristic carboxyl peaks at 1636 cm⁻¹ and 1715 cm⁻¹, along with a peak at 1600 cm⁻¹ attributed to the C = C bond in the graphitic structure. For pure PVP, the C = O stretching vibration is located at 1636 cm⁻¹20, which overlaps with the asymmetric carboxylate (COO⁻) vibration of NaAlg at 1636 cm⁻¹. In the nanocomposites, this region underwent significant modification; the original band at 1636 cm⁻¹ split into two distinct peaks: one at approximately 1661 cm⁻¹ (M1) and another at approximately 1601–1605 cm⁻¹. The shift to 1661 cm⁻¹ reflects the interaction between the PVP carbonyl and the carboxyl groups of MWCNTs (originally at 1650 cm⁻¹). The emergence of the peak at 1601–1605 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the asymmetric stretching of the carboxylate (COO⁻) groups, which are influenced by the presence of the functionalized filler. These spectral changes confirm the formation of strong non-covalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and dipole–dipole interactions, between the –COOH groups of the filler and the functional groups of the polymer matrix.

The pure PVP shows a characteristic vibration of the CN (pyridine ring) at 1495 cm⁻¹ and CH₂ scissoring vibrations at 1425 cm⁻¹. For NaAlg, the symmetric carboxylate (COO⁻) vibration is also located at 1425 cm⁻¹3. In the blend (M0), this band was observed at 1420 cm⁻¹. Upon adding MWCNTs, this band showed subtle shifts to 1418–1419 cm⁻¹. This confirms that the symmetric carboxylate groups of the alginate are involved in the interaction with the functionalized filler. Additionally, the CH₂ twisting or wagging vibrations of PVP at 1283 cm⁻¹ were observed to shift slightly to 1287–1291 cm⁻¹ in the nanocomposites, reflecting local polarity changes and structural perturbation in the PVP environment due to the incorporation of MWCNTs.

The C–O stretching vibrations of NaAlg at 1095 and 1040 cm⁻¹ and the C-C bond of MWCNTs at 1128 cm⁻¹ contribute to the complex absorption in the 1000–1200 cm⁻¹ region. In the blend, the prominent band at 1092 cm⁻¹ is attributed to the C–O–C stretching and glycosidic linkages of the alginate backbone. The uronic acid and mannuronic acid characteristic peaks of NaAlg at 945 cm⁻¹ and 818 cm⁻¹ were observed at 947 and 821 cm⁻¹ in the blend, respectively3,22–24. In the nanocomposites, these peaks showed minor shifts (948–952 cm⁻¹ and 816–817 cm⁻¹), indicating changes in the polysaccharide chain conformation and increased structural disorder induced by the functionalized filler25. The peak at 602 cm⁻¹ remains stable across all samples, likely related to the CN bending of PVP (originally at 631 cm⁻¹).

Overall, the FTIR spectra demonstrate the existence of substantial interfacial interactions between the polymer blend and the MWCNTs. The detected shifts in the O-H and C = O bands, along with the splitting of the carbonyl/carboxylate peak and the shifts in the symmetric carboxylate and skeletal vibrations, confirm the formation of robust hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions between the –COOH groups of the functionalized CNTs and the functional groups of PVP/NaAlg, as shown in the proposed schematic diagram in Fig. 2. These interactions facilitate effective load transfer and electron delocalization pathways, which correlate with the improved electrical, structural, and dielectric performance observed in the nanocomposites. Thus, FTIR analysis provides compelling evidence for successful blending and homogeneous dispersion of MWCNTs within the PVP/NaAlg matrix.

XRD analysis of PVP/NaAlg/MWCNT nanocomposites

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the PVP/NaAlg blend and its nanocomposites containing varying concentrations of MWCNTs in the 2θ range of 5°–75° are presented in Fig. 3. Both the (002) and (100) planes of graphitic carbon are responsible for the two distinctive diffraction peaks that are seen in the pristine MWCNTs. These peaks are located at roughly 26.0° and 43.5°26. The sharp (002) reflection corresponds to an interlayer spacing (d002) of around 3.42 Å, confirming the well-ordered graphitic structure of the nanotubes. In contrast, the XRD pattern of the pure PVP/NaAlg blend shows a broad, low-intensity halo centered around 24.45°, representing the predominantly amorphous nature of the polymeric matrix. This broad hump arises from disordered chain packing and the absence of long-range crystallinity typical of amorphous polymer systems.

Upon incorporating MWCNTs into the PVP/NaAlg matrix, a gradual evolution in the diffraction profiles is observed. The characteristic (002) reflection of MWCNTs becomes progressively more pronounced as the filler percent increases from 0.10 wt% to 0.30 wt%, signifying the successful integration and increasing presence of crystalline graphitic domains within the polymer matrix. The relative enhancement of this reflection intensity suggests an increase in the apparent degree of crystallinity of the composite, primarily as a result of the contribution of the highly ordered carbon nanotube phase. Additionally, this observation implies good dispersion and partial alignment of MWCNTs within the host polymeric network, enhancing interfacial interactions27. This increase in the crystalline structure confirms the compatibility and efficacy of the MWCNTs in the PVP/NaAlg host matrix. The addition of MWCNTs also influences the broad amorphous halo of the PVP/NaAlg blend. As the MWCNT content increases, a reduction in the intensity of the peak at 13.33° is evident, coupled with slight changes in the peak position and shape. These changes can be attributed to two key phenomena: (i) the contrast effect introduced by the crystalline MWCNTs, which enhances the relative crystalline contribution in the diffraction pattern, and (ii) interfacial ordering of polymer chains around the nanotube surfaces, where the π–π interactions and hydrogen bonding between the functional groups of PVP/NaAlg and the graphitic walls of MWCNTs induce partial alignment or densification of polymer chains. This local ordering near the nanotube–polymer interface can result in reduced interchain spacing and modified chain packing, as reflected by subtle shifts in the amorphous halo toward higher 2θ values.

The broadness of the (002) reflection in the nanocomposites compared to that of pristine MWCNTs indicates the presence of lattice strain, curvature, and partial disorder within the graphitic domains due to polymer–nanotube interactions. Such broadening can also be associated with reduced coherent scattering domain sizes, confirming that the MWCNTs are well distributed within the polymer matrix without significant agglomeration27. At higher loadings (0.30 wt%), the (002) peak becomes sharper and more intense, demonstrating improved stacking and reorganization of MWCNTs, possibly resulting from nanotube–nanotube interactions at higher filler content28.

Overall, the XRD results confirm that the PVP/NaAlg blend remains largely amorphous, while the introduction of MWCNTs enhances the overall structural ordering of the system. The progressive development of the (002) graphitic peak and the corresponding reduction in the amorphous halo (13.33° peak) intensity signify an increase in the crystalline fraction and improved structural regularity within the nanocomposite. This structural modification is expected to contribute positively to the enhancement of the electrical, optical, and dielectric properties of the nanocomposites.

UV/Vis investigation

In Fig. 4, UV–Vis spectra (190–1000 nm) were recorded for a neat PVP/NaAlg film and nanocomposites containing MWCNTs at 0.10, 0.20, and 0.30 wt%. The spectra show a strong rise in absorbance toward the UV, with systematic intensity growth upon adding MWCNTs across the entire range, consistent with π–π* transitions of the polymer matrix and additional sub‑gap absorption from carbon nanotube tails and interfacial charge‑transfer states.

Maximum absorption has been identified for the nanocomposite with 0.3% MWCNTs, which agrees with the findings of the similar study based on the MWCNTs/PVA nanocomposite films29. With increasing MWCNT concentration, the absorption edge shifts toward longer wavelengths. This shift reflects enhanced localized states, tail-state formation, and increased structural disorder due to CNT incorporation and the development of charge-transfer complexes between polymer chains and dispersed MWCNTs30. Generally, absorbance reduces by increasing the wavelength of incident light until it stabilizes at longer wavelengths31. These changes indicate (i) enhanced density of localized states near the band edges and (ii) electronic coupling between the polymer and MWCNTs that facilitates charge‑transfer transitions. It also points out the great potential of PNCs as one of the most promising materials in developing flexible devices based on spectral tuning because of their wavelength-sensitive behaviors.

The acquired data indicate that the incorporation of MWCNTs resulted in a reduction of both the direct and indirect optical transition energies. Direct Tauc band gaps decrease from ~ 5.01 ± 0.03 eV (pure blend) to ~ 4.68 ± 0.02 eV (0.30 wt%), evidencing strong polymer–CNT electronic interactions and tail-state formation. Indirect band gaps also exhibited a significant decrease from ~ 4.25 ± 0.07 eV (pure blend) to ~ 2.73 ± 0.06 eV (0.30 wt%). The reduction in the energies of the optical bandgap, which occurs in direct proportion to the increasing concentration of MWCNTs, indicates that these carbon nanotubes enhance the electronic structure of the PVP/NaAlg blend by adding polaronic levels and defects. The alteration of the energy gap is attributed to the localized states inherent in these nanocomposites, which arise from the conjugated charge transfer complexes formed between the dopant and the PVP/NaAlg blend, as well as the defects present in the polymeric chains. This phenomenon influences the absorption onset in relation to the energy of photons27. Consequently, as the content of MWCNTs rises, the emergence of localized states and their intermingling has heightened the disorder within the host framework, leading to a reduction in the extracted optical threshold of the PVP/NaAlg-based nanocomposites due to localized states, tail-state formation, and increased structural disorder.

The newly established energy levels facilitate the movement of electrons from the valence band to localized states, thereby improving the semiconducting properties of the compound and enabling seamless transitions of electrons from the valence band to the conduction band. The XRD results corroborate these findings. Similar findings were reported by Abdelghany et al.4 in their study on PVP/SA blend films loaded with Au NPs and in our previous work32 based on PS/PMMA: NiFe2O4 blends containing CuO nanoparticles. Optical images and TEM analysis

Figure 6 shows the optical micrographs of the prepared nanocomposite films, and Fig. 7 displays the TEM analysis of the PVP/NaAlg nanocomposite containing 0.30 wt% MWCNTs, which give complementary information on both the dispersion state and nanoscale dimensions of the incorporated nanotubes. As expected, the optical micrographs cannot resolve individual MWCNTs due to the diffraction limit of visible light; however, they clearly show the formation of continuous networks and bundled structures throughout the polymer matrix. Such features reflect the mesoscale (micrometer-level) aggregation of MWCNTs rather than their intrinsic nanoscale diameters.

In contrast, TEM imaging allows for direct observation of the nanotubes and hence gives a more accurate measure of the nanotube dimensions. In the 0.30% MWCNT composite, the nanotubes have an average diameter of 83.03 nm, which reflects bundles or aggregates of nanotubes rather than individual MWCNTs. This nanoscale dimension is important in that while optical microscopy shows only the macroscopic distribution and partial agglomeration of nanotubes, the TEM results confirm that the filler phase is genuinely nanoscale, verifying the material’s classification as a true polymer nanocomposite. These observations are consistent with previous studies on CNT-based polymer nanocomposites, where uniform dispersion and strong interfacial adhesion are repeatedly identified as key factors governing mechanical, electrical, and dielectric performance. For example, earlier reports emphasize that the overall properties of CNT–polymer systems are highly dependent on nanotube dispersion quality, interfacial bonding, and tube alignment38. Achieving homogeneous dispersion remains challenging because of the strong van der Waals forces that promote CNT bundling and aggregation39.

In the present work, the networked bundles seen in the optical micrographs align with such expected aggregation behavior. Nevertheless, the TEM-measured nanotube diameter in the nanometer range demonstrates that despite some clustering, the nanotubes retain their nanometric characteristics, a situation widely observed in practical nanocomposite systems. There are similar studies that reported comparable findings. As an example, the preparation of a PVA–chitosan/MWCNT nanocomposite was performed by Zohora et al., and characterization of CNT dispersion was carried out using optical microscopy, SEM, and other techniques40. Their optical images also showed regions that were aggregated; similarly, nanotube morphology could be resolved more clearly at higher resolution imaging, such as SEM/TEM. Interconnected MWCNT networks, partially aggregated or not, are known to drastically enhance electrical, mechanical, and dielectric properties from a materials-performance point of view by means of the percolation of conductive pathways. The percolation threshold, electrical conductivity, and dielectric response in many polymer/MWCNT systems have been found to strongly depend on nanotube loading and dispersion quality41. Therefore, in the present nanocomposite system, such network-like morphology is expected to favor percolation and interfacial polarization, hence contributing to the enhancement in dielectric performance, energy-storage behavior, and conductivity.

AC conductivity (σac) study

Figure 8 shows the logarithmic AC conductivity spectra at room temperature for the pure PVP/NaAlg blend (M0) and for nanocomposites containing 0.10–0.30 wt% MWCNTs (M1–M3). The conventional outline of a disordered conductor, with a plateau at low frequencies and a power-law distribution at high frequencies, is present in all of the compositions. This behavior is captured by Jonscher’s universal dielectric response, σ(ω) = σdc + Aωs, (0 < s < 1), where the frequency-independent term σdc reflects long-range transport through percolated pathways and the dispersive term arises from localized/hopping motion in disordered regions42,43.

Introducing MWCNTs produces decade-scale increases in σdc and shifts the knee (crossover) to a higher frequency. Even at 0.10 wt% (M1), σdc rises by roughly three orders of magnitude compared with M0. At 0.20–0.30 wt% (M2-M3), further increments are observed and the slope in the dispersive regime becomes slightly smaller, both signatures of a denser, better-connected nanotube network and of a greater fraction of long-range transport.

Thus, the room temperature DC conductivities are summarized in Table 3, where their value for M0 ≈ 2 × 10–10 S·cm⁻¹; M1 ≈ 1 × 10⁻⁷ S·cm⁻¹; M2 ≈ 8.51 × 10− 7 S·cm⁻¹; and M3 ≈ 1.15 × 10− 5 S·cm⁻¹. Further, the AC conductivity (σac) recorded 9.93 × 10− 6 S·cm⁻¹ for M0 and reached 1.22 × 10− 4 S·cm⁻¹ for M3. The frequency exponent (s) lies between about 0.55 and 0.89 as exhibited in Table 3, which provides further evidence that CBH is the main conduction mechanism present in the studied samples44–46. Consequently, the relationship among charge carriers diminishes, enabling a greater number of localized states to engage in the conduction process6,7. The interaction between MWCNTs and the functional groups present in the PVP/NaAlg blend facilitates the formation of a charge-transfer complex (CTC). This interaction is the catalyst for the observed enhancement in AC conductivity. This composite concurrently alters the chemical composition and electronic band structure of the polymer chains, while enhancing their flexibility.

Therefore, the entire conductivity of the system exhibits an enhancement with the rising content of MWCNTs, likely attributable to the augmented number of conducting channels facilitated by the dispersed MWCNTs within the PVP/NaAlg matrix. These findings agree with results reported in the literature47–50. For example, polystyrene/MWCNTs show pronounced growth in σdc across the percolation region with s ≈ 0.6–0.9, while PVA/alginate/CNT films report monotonic increases in σac (10− 7–10− 5 S·cm⁻¹) and similar exponents—magnitudes comparable to the present M1-M3 values. These results give a preference to our nanocomposite over the other MWCNT-based composites. The electrical conductivity is dependent on the kind, morphology, form, dimension, and distribution of the nanofiller material. The improvement in conductivity may also be reasoned to be influenced by the enhanced amorphous form of the polymer composite, which would lower the energy barrier and enhance ionic mobility49.

Dielectric spectra study

Studying the dielectric characteristics of polymer electrolytes is a very useful way to learn about the interfacial mechanisms that control the buildup of charge carriers and polarization at the electrode-electrolyte interface. Improving the efficiency and functionality of electrochemical devices relies heavily on this information. It also makes it possible to check whether the conductivity trend is true. The dielectric constant εʹ and the dielectric loss εʹʹ are the two parameters that are evaluated in dielectric research. These are considered to be the most essential parameters. Figures 9a,b depict the dielectric constant εʹ and dielectric loss εʹʹ for PVP/NaAlg incorporating varying weight percentages of MWCNTs. Both plots demonstrate a notable increase in the values of εʹ and εʹʹ, particularly for the M3 system. This behavior may be attributed to the increased MWCNT concentration (0.3 wt%), which likely enhances the number of charge carriers retained within the electrolyte. Accordingly, there has been an increase in the number and density of mobile ions within the M3 system, which contributes to the enhancement of conductivity51. The plots of εʹ and εʹʹ exhibit an absence of relaxation peaks, suggesting that the observed increase in conductivity can be attributed to a rise in the density of mobile ions.

With the increase in frequency, there is a gradual decline in conductivity over time, ultimately stabilizing at a plateau within the high-frequency region following a notable crossover point. The heightened dielectric constant observed in the lower frequency spectrum can be ascribed to the polarizing influence of the electrodes in conjunction with space charge, which accounts for the non-Debye behavior exhibited. Polymer composites’ dielectric characteristics are, hence, strongly dependent on the activity and dispersion of the fillers’ surfaces, as shown by the observed behavior3,12,50.

Dielectric loss represents a meticulous assessment of energy dissipation, frequently incorporating factors from electrode materials alongside charge or dipole polarization. In Fig. 9b, the dependency of εʹʹ on frequency of the current systems at room temperature showed that the dielectric loss has the greatest values at lower frequencies and subsequently decreased with increasing frequency. The change of polarization at the interface of the electrode and electrolyte serves as a means to evaluate dielectric loss within the low-frequency spectrum. The intricate dynamics of charge mobility within the host matrix, coupled with sufficient temporal allowance for charge organization at functionality before any alteration in electric potential, culminate in a notable observable magnitude of dielectric loss52. No charge accumulation was observed during the interaction in the high-frequency domain.

The charge carriers or dipoles at lower frequencies are capable of readily aligning with the electric field, thereby enhancing the mobility of polymer segments. However, at elevated frequencies, their ability to respond rapidly to the variations in the applied field diminishes, leading to a notable reduction in ε′ and ε″. Furthermore, the improvement noted in the dielectric characteristics of PVP/NaAlg/MWCNTs nanocomposites can be attributed to the potential formation of micro-capacitor structures, a consequence of the dielectric properties of PVP/NaAlg that effectively isolate the MWCNTs53.

Electric modulus

The real (M′) and imaginary (M″) parts of the electric modulus were employed to suppress electrode polarization and highlight bulk charge-transport processes in PVP/NaAlg/MWCNTs nanocomposites. The diminished values of M′ and M″ at the lower frequency suggest minimal electrode polarization21. In the M′ spectra shown in Fig. 10a, all films show negligible values at low frequencies, confirming that interfacial space–charge contributions are largely screened in the modulus formalism32. Upon increasing frequency, M′ rises toward a high‑frequency plateau. Incorporation of MWCNTs shifts this dispersion region to higher frequencies and reduces the magnitude of the low‑frequency upturn, demonstrating a shortening of the relaxation time (τr) and a mitigation of interfacial charge build‑up via the Maxwell-Wagner-Sillars (MWS) process54.

Complex impedance, Z*, can be expressed by the equation58:

Complex impedance (Nyquist) plots were used to assess charge-transport processes in the current system. Figure 11 presents the Nyquist plots (Z′ versus Z″) of PVP/NaAlg/MWCNTs nanocomposites measured at room temperature for a series of MWCNT loadings. For all compositions, the Z″–Z′ curves form single, depressed semicircles, indicating that the electrical response is governed primarily by bulk resistance (Rb) in parallel with a dielectric element (CPE). In the corresponding equivalent circuit, the presence of a single low-frequency semicircular arc in the low frequency region can be attributed to the bulk resistance connected in series with capacitance via conductive grain boundaries59. In such a representation, the diameter of the semicircle equals Rb, and the high‑ and low‑frequency limits correspond to the left and right intercepts on the Z′ axis, respectively. It is evident that the Rb value for the pure blend was\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:4.8\times\:{10}^{6}\:{\Omega\:}$$\end{document}, and it reached \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:5\times\:{10}^{3}\:{\Omega\:}$$\end{document} after incorporating 0.3 wt% of MWCNTs into the PVP/NaAlg host matrix. The depression of the arcs reflects a distribution of relaxation times typical of heterogeneous polymer blend–filler microstructures (i.e., non‑Debye behavior)57,58.

Introducing MWCNTs systematically contracts the semicircle diameter and shifts the entire response to lower Z′ values. This trend evidences a large decrease in Rb as conductive MWCNT pathways emerge and percolate through the PVP/NaAlg matrix, thereby facilitating long‑range charge transport58. Concomitantly, the apex of the semicircle moves toward smaller Z′ and Z″ values, consistent with a reduction in both the resistive contribution and the characteristic relaxation time τ of the equivalent R–C element.

As above-mentioned, the increase in MWCN content reduces Rb, indicating enhanced conductivity and semiconductor behavior. Higher MWCNT levels in PVP/NaAlg/MWCNTs composites improve capacitive properties, as shown by reduced semicircle diameter in Nyquist plots, suggesting greater charge mobility and lower interfacial impedance. The characteristics of these composites render them highly promising for advanced dielectric capacitors, enhancing both charge storage capabilities and their ecological sustainability60,61.

Conclusion

PVP/NaAlg–MWCNTs nanocomposite films were successfully prepared using the solution casting method. FTIR analysis confirmed strong interactions between the polymer blend and MWCNTs. Red shifts in C = O and O–H bands indicate hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. These interactions improved compatibility and dispersion of MWCNTs within the polymer matrix.

XRD results showed that the blend remained mostly amorphous, while MWCNTs increased structural ordering. The appearance of the (002) graphitic peak confirmed improved crystallinity.

Optical studies revealed a decrease in both direct and indirect band gaps with higher MWCNT content. This reduction suggests enhanced electronic coupling and charge delocalization. Electrical conductivity increased markedly with MWCNT loading, confirming network formation. The DC conductivity rose from 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ to 1.15 × 10⁻⁵ S·cm⁻¹. The frequency exponent (0.55–0.89) indicated a correlated barrier hopping (CBH) conduction mechanism. Relaxation time decreased with increasing filler content, showing faster charge mobility. Impedance analysis showed single depressed semicircles, indicating bulk-dominated non-Debye behavior. Bulk resistance dropped from 4.8 × 10⁶ Ω to 5 × 10³ Ω after MWCNT incorporation. MWCNTs enhanced the structural, optical, and electrical performance of the PVP/NaAlg blend. These nanocomposites show great potential for use in flexible optoelectronic and energy-storage devices.
