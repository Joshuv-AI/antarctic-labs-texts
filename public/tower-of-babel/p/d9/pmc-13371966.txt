The experiential self-state as affective salience: a dual-self states framework for consciousness and self-transcendence
Authors: Valerie van Mulukom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis article proposes a dual-self states framework in which reflective and experiential modes of consciousness are understood as dynamic configurations along a processing continuum. The reflective self-state corresponds to narrative, denotational, and self-referential consciousness underpinned by default mode network activity. The experiential self-state is characterized as an affective-salience mode of perspectival awareness, anchored in salience network hubs (particularly the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex), which integrate interoceptive and exteroceptive signals into a coherent, non-propositional sense of being-here-as-subject, accompanied by sustained, non-reflective meta-awareness. Within a predictive processing framework, the experiential self-state corresponds to a configuration in which the precision-weighting of high-level self-referential priors is reduced and a broader range of affective and bodily signals shapes conscious experience, a shift governed by dopaminergic, noradrenergic, and serotonergic mechanisms. This characterization carries direct implications for individual differences in traits such as mindfulness and absorption, and for conditions involving dysregulated salience processing. The framework further proposes that self-transcendent experiences, including absorption, meditation, awe, and mystical or religious experience, are best understood as expressions of the experiential self-state, and serve as theoretical and empirical testing grounds for the framework’s core claims.

Introduction

How should we understand the sense of self that underlies conscious experience? This article proposes a dual self-state framework, in which reflective and experiential modes of consciousness are understood as configurations along a processing continuum, characterized as a relative dominance of prior-driven, narratively structured self-processing versus bottom-up affective and bodily signal integration, respectively. This builds on, but differs from, accounts that largely treat these modes as co-present structural layers of self, such as Gallagher’s (2000) minimal/narrative distinction or Damasio’s (2010) proto/core/autobiographical hierarchy.

Central to this framework is the proposal that the experiential self-state is best characterized as an affective-salience mode of consciousness. Building on interoceptive and predictive accounts of bodily awareness (Craig 2009, Seth et al. 2012, Seth 2013) and enactive and neurophenomenological accounts of consciousness (Varela et al. 2017), the experiential self-state is anchored in salience network (SN) hubs [particularly the anterior insula (AI) and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC)], which integrate interoceptive and exteroceptive signals into a coherent, non-propositional sense of being-here-as-subject, accompanied by sustained, non-reflective meta-awareness. Within a predictive processing framework (Friston 2010, Carhart-Harris and Friston 2019), this corresponds to a configuration in which the precision-weighting of high-level self-referential priors is reduced and a broader range of affective and bodily signals shapes conscious experience. This characterization generates a specific neurobiological account of the experiential self-state, encompassing dopaminergic, noradrenergic, and serotonergic mechanisms, and has direct implications for individual differences in traits such as mindfulness and absorption, and for conditions involving dysregulated salience processing.

This framework offers a lens onto self-transcendent experiences: States of absorption, contemplative depth, awe, and mystical or religious experience that are phenomenologically characterized by self-diminishment, porosity of self-boundaries, and felt connectedness to something beyond oneself are understood as expressions of the experiential self-state. In these configurations, reflective prior-dominance is loosened and salience-guided, non-propositional awareness becomes more prominent, thus not considering these experiences anomalous per se. Meditation, absorption, and related altered states serve as both theoretical and empirical testing grounds for the framework’s core claims about how reflective and experiential modes are dissociable, and how shifts between them can be induced and cultivated.

Dual-self states framework

The dual-self states framework I propose extends previous work, starting with William James (1890, 1892), who distinguished two understandings of the self: the self as an object of experience or ‘Me’ and the self as subject of experience or ‘I’. This has been picked up by the philosophy (Gallagher 2000, Letheby and Gerrans 2017), psychedelics (Millière et al. 2018), and meditation (Farb et al. 2007, Britton et al. 2021, Gibson 2024) literatures, where the self-as-object has been called ‘reflective self’ or ‘narrative self’, and the self-as-subject ‘experiential self’ or ‘embodied self’; see Table 1. Here, I will use reflective and experiential self. In brief, the reflective is the self we reflectively construct using autobiographical memory and narrative, and which is associated with the kind of analytical awareness which we often equate with ‘conscious awareness’, while the experiential self is the self that ‘is’; it is our personal sensing and feeling without ‘conscious’ reflection.

Gallagher defines ‘minimal self’ ‘a consciousness of oneself as an immediate subject of experience, unextended in time’ (Gallagher 2000, p. 15), characterized phenomenologically by pre-reflective ownership and agency, and the ‘narrative self’ ‘a more or less coherent self or self-image that is constituted with a past and a future in the various stories that we and others tell about ourselves’ (Gallagher 2000, p. 15), which is linked explicitly to Neisser’s ‘extended self’ and Dennett’s ‘nonminimal’ self. Damasio (2010) proposes a model grounded in neurobiology, which contains three selves: the ‘proto-self’, which is a non-conscious collection of neural patterns that continuously map the current state of the organism (e.g. homeostatic and body-state representations), the ‘core self’, which is the transient sense of self that arises when the organism-object interaction modifies the body maps of the proto-self, leading to momentary ‘pulses’ of core consciousness, an in-the-moment ‘I’ that knows it exists now, and the ‘autobiographical self’, which is the extended self-constructed from stored autobiographical memories and related knowledge, which can be activated to elaborate core consciousness and provide a sense of self across time. Thus, Damasio’s hierarchy runs from basic bodily regulation (proto-self) to momentary subjectivity (core self) to temporally extended identity (autobiographical self).

Both Gallagher and Damasio focus on bodily grounding, such as through ownership, agency, and interoception, to support the minimal/core self, which is in line with the present proposal. Like Damasio, the present framework assumes a proto-self in the sense of a bodily state that encompasses homeostatic states which the experiential self operates on, but we will avoid calling it a ‘self’, since it lacks any kind of consciousness, whether reflective or experiential. However, in Gallagher and Damasio’s models, less is known about the experiential ownership that remains when bodily ownership is changed or attenuated, a gap the present framework aims to fill.

Gallagher’s (2000) and Damasio’s (2010) conceptualizations are taxonomic constructs about layers or aspects of self; they treat the selves as structural aspects of the self that are typically co-present. The present framework, by contrast, the reflective and experiential selves are treated here as state-level modes of consciousness that can alternate and be cultivated (e.g. in contemplative practice or self-transcendent episodes). These two modes should be understood as poles of a continuum of processing in which reflective, prior-heavy and experiential, sensory-heavy configurations can coexist to varying degrees, rather than mutually exclusive, all-or-nothing states. Treating these modes as configurations that vary in dominance over time, rather than as structural aspects that are typically co-present, allows the experiential self-state to be tracked in ways that structural taxonomies do not readily accommodate, and generates testable neurocomputational predictions.

In what follows, discussions of ‘reflective’ versus ‘experiential’ self states should be read as a shorthand for dominant configurations along this continuum. A configuration counts as reflectively dominated when self-referential, temporally extended, and working-memory-dependent processing is prevalent, typically accompanied by increased default mode network (DMN) engagement and explicit self-as-object judgement. A configuration counts as experientially dominated when embodied, affective-salience processing and non-propositional awareness of being-here-as-subject are prevalent, typically accompanied by increased SN engagement and reduced narrative elaboration. Intermediate cases are expected and accommodated; what matters for the present framework is which pattern dominates at a given time.

Consciousness in the dual-self states framework

The reflective self-state is what we ordinarily think of as conscious awareness: it is when we think about ourselves, reflect on what we are doing or have done, how we are perceived, and so on. It is an integration of self-referential processing, including reflecting on representations of the self, whether in the past, future, or imagination (Gallagher 2000), supporting self-image and identity, self-esteem, and self-evaluation (be it self-criticism or self-compassion). The reflective self can be further divided into components, typically including the private/individual versus public/social/relational selves distinction (e.g. Neisser 1988), which may be supported by different subsystems of the DMN (Andrews-Hanna et al. 2014).

The experiential self-state is free of ‘self-conscious’ self-referential processing that the reflective self-state comprises, and can be understood as is the self that ‘is’, in the here and now, as a sensing entity (Gallagher 2000). The state is nonetheless one of awareness and consciousness: This type of sensing awareness, which we will call ‘experiential awareness’, does not require cognitive reflection, but it is not non-consciousness. As Cappuccio (2017) points out: there are many cognitive processes that are accessed by the system at a subpersonal level, and only if they do not explicitly inform phenomenal experience, they should be considered the ‘nonconscious’. Experiential consciousness is consciousness on an affective, embodied level, and as such still informs phenomenal experience. Philosopher John Searle put it plainly: ‘By consciousness I simply mean those subjective states of awareness or sentience that begin when one wakes in the morning and continue throughout the period that one is awake until one falls into a dreamless sleep, into a coma, or dies or is otherwise, as they say, unconscious.’ (Searle 1990).

The reflective self-state is characteristically denotational and propositional, that is to say, explicit and describable in language, whereas the experiential self-state is predominantly connotational and non-propositional, or ‘not’ straightforwardly describable in language (Block 1995). This distinction roughly tracks Block’s contrast between access-conscious states, whose contents are available for reasoning, report, and rational control of action, and phenomenally conscious states, whose primary feature is the qualitative ‘what-it-is-like’ character of experience rather than their articulation in linguistically structured, self-referential propositions. We can, of course, describe the experiential self-state, but whenever we do so, we are working from a reflective self-state; we have moved from the self-as-subject to self-as-object. Moreover, verbalization can lead to significant changes in processing or recall of the experiences (Schooler 2002).

However, the experiential self-state should not be conflated with ‘bodily self-consciousness’ (Dary and Lopez 2023) when it is described as a form of ‘embodied awareness’. Bodily self-consciousness includes the multisensory integration of bodily experiences. The bodily experiences encapsulated by this conceptualization are ‘self-location’, the experience of where one is in space, ‘body ownership’, the experience of identifying with a body, ‘agency’, the experience of being in control of one’s movements, and ‘first-person perspective’, the experience of an egocentric perspective on the world (Blanke 2012). Monti et al. (2022) describe this as the awareness ‘of having a body (sense of body ownership), of controlling its movements (sense of body agency) and of dwelling in it (sense of body location)’ (p. 2469). Experiential awareness is based on the interoceptive and exteroceptive information that the body receives, and as such shares much of the information that bodily consciousness relies on too. Importantly however, experiential awareness is nonetheless not the same as bodily awareness.

Liang et al. (2021) demonstrate the distinction between experiential and bodily awareness with their experiments using body-part (hand) and the full-body illusions, with participants being touched either in sync or out of sync with their own body (part). In their studies, they distinguish between body ownership, the feeling that a particular hand or body is mine, and experiential ownership, the sense that I am the one having the experiences happening to me (more closely aligned with William James’ self-as-subject or ‘I’). The authors found they could manipulate these two experiences independently. Their experiments showed that under asynchronous tactile stimulation (when touch on the rubber hand or fake body was out of sync with visual cues), participants reported a diminished sense of body ownership, yet they still experienced a strong sense of experiential ownership, i.e. feeling that they were the ones having the tactile sensations. Conversely, in conditions where tactile stimulation was present but was no longer perceived as self-owned (such as after a delay), participants maintained their sense of experiential ownership even when their sense of body ownership was weakened or absent. Physiological measurements using skin conductance responses (SCR) similarly supported these findings: SCRs associated with body ownership were sensitive to timing and synchronization of stimuli, while SCRs linked to experiential ownership were not. These findings support a clear distinction between experiential and bodily ownership.

Although bodily consciousness is not the same as experiential awareness, it is at the foundation of the experiential self-state. The reduction of reflective thought is often induced or accompanied by embodiment, typically involving an increase in bodily consciousness that provides a pathway toward meta-awareness. In many contemplative and therapeutic practices, attention is first deliberately redirected toward interoceptive and proprioceptive sensations (e.g. the breath or one’s posture), which function as ‘attention anchors’ (Dunne et al. 2019). This process has been described in Bernstein et al.’s (2019) theory of ‘decentring’ in meditation, which involves (i) an increase in bodily consciousness, (ii) a reduction of reflective thought, which may occur in either order of each other or simultaneously (though there are also suggestions that the temporal sequence is as presented here, see Letheby and Gerrans 2017), and in the final step, (iii) a reduction of interoceptive focus. While there is an overlap in engagement with interoception between bodily consciousness and experiential consciousness, for the latter to take place, a switch needs to be made from top-down engagement (as in often the case in bodily consciousness) to a bottom-up one (see also the section ‘Meditation as experiential self-state practice’ below).

Millière et al. (2018) describe these stages as separate processes. On the one hand there is ‘narrative self-loss’, which is the reduction of reflective thought coupled with an increase in bodily consciousness, and on the other there is ‘multisensory self-loss’, which is a perceived loss of self-boundaries, which includes bodily consciousness. Here I suggest that for full experiential self-states, these processes likely follow each other, where engagement with interoception, combined with a withdrawal from top-down attentional control over it, allows for an embodied, immersive state in which a different kind of awareness can emerge, characterized by sustained, non-propositional meta-awareness (Dunne et al. 2019). The idea is that bodily sensations become ‘almost invisible’, instead changing into a subtle and indistinct background presence, such as most clearly evident in meditative states (Ataria et al. 2015). This self-detached meta-awareness has also been called the ‘experiential perspectivalness of phenomenal consciousness’ (Zahavi 2014) or ‘perspectival ownership’ for short (Lindström et al. 2023, 2025). Notably, while such perspectival ownership may be considered like a first-person perspective, it is not the same as a sense of spatial self-location, which is part of bodily consciousness (Blanke 2012). Instead, it can be thought of as a ‘locationless centrality’ (Ataria et al. 2015, Millière et al. 2018).

Dunne et al. (2019) argue, based on the Buddhist literature, particularly that of Tibetan Mahamudra, that ‘meta-awareness’ is the ability to observe and recognize one’s own mental processes and states, such as thoughts, feelings, or sensations, without becoming entirely absorbed in them. This form of awareness can be sustained over time and may operate non-propositionally, meaning it is not related to verbal or explicit judgments, but rather pertains to a continuous, implicit monitoring of how one is attending to mental states and processes. There may be an intermittent, propositional form of meta-awareness (for instance, the judgment ‘I am not paying attention’), but the meta-awareness operative in the experiential self-state is of a different kind: It is the non-reflective perspectival quality of being immersed in affective and bodily experience, in the sense of awareness of the self-as-subject rather than a detached observer of it. Following enactive accounts, awareness and its affective contents are co-constituted in the lived body rather than standing in a hierarchical observer–observed relation, yet a sustained, non-propositional sensitivity to the quality of ongoing experience is retained (Varela et al. 2017, Dunne et al. 2019).

Although the experiential self-state shares an emphasis on non-propositional, present-centred awareness with some contemplative traditions, it should not be conflated with what is often termed ‘nondual awareness’ or ‘consciousness-as-such’. In contemporary accounts, nondual awareness is generally characterized as an intrinsically reflexive, non-representational awareness; that is, independent of specific contents and subject-object structure (Josipovic and Miskovic 2020). Some accounts go even further, proposing as a fundamental, irrelational ‘space’ within which all phenomenal contents arise (Metzinger 2020). By contrast, the experiential self-state conceptualized here is explicitly contentful and affectively structured: it is constituted by ongoing, salience-guided integration of interoceptive and exteroceptive signals into a coherent, perspectival field of experience for a situated subject, underwritten by predictive, bodily and affective processes rather than by a sui generis mode of awareness (Dunne et al. 2019, Josipovic 2021). In other words, the experiential self state is positioned within an embodied, predictive, and perspectival framework in which meta-awareness is a sustained modulation of how affective and bodily signals shape experience, rather than an ontologically distinct, contentless, or subjectless awareness.

The experiential self-state also differs from the ‘flow’ state (Csikszentmihalyi 1990, Jackson and Marsh 1996). Flow is typically described as a state of optimal performance in which attention is tightly focused on the task at hand, action feels fluent and effortless, and self-reflective awareness of oneself as the agent largely recedes into the background (Nakamura and Csikszentmihalyi 2014). In flow states, attention and awareness are narrowly absorbed in skilled action with heightened bodily engagement but relatively low explicit meta-awareness of experience. During the experiential self-state, by contrast, the shift away from reflective self-processing is accompanied by a sustained meta-awareness of ongoing sensory and affective experience.

Cognitive neuroscience of the dual-self states

Neuronal representations of the dual-self states

What the processes of the reflective self share is their conscious self-referential nature. Correspondingly, the self-referential processes of the reflective self are underpinned by the DMN. The DMN is a large-scale brain network comprising the anterior medial prefrontal cortex (aMPFC), posterior cingulate cortex (PCC), ventral medial prefrontal cortex, precuneus, inferior parietal lobule, and middle temporal gyrus (Johnson et al. 2002, Andrews-Hanna et al. 2014). The DMN is most active during rest, mind-wandering, and internally focused cognition such as autobiographical memory retrieval, self-reflection, imagining future scenarios, considering the perspectives of others, and constructing an internal narrative (Menon 2023).

The core of the DMN which engages in self-reflection has been well mapped out, with main activation taking place in the aMPFC and PCC (Johnson et al. 2002, Andrews-Hanna et al. 2014, Davey et al. 2016). The aMPFC is engaged during explicit self-reflection, evaluation of personal traits, and introspection tasks (e.g. judging how well adjectives describe oneself, imagining future self-related events) and the PCC functions as a central hub for integration of internal (autobiographical) and external (sensory) information, self-monitoring, and maintenance of a continuous sense of personal identity (Johnson et al. 2002, Andrews-Hanna et al. 2014).

The experiential self on the other hand is the self state engaged in sensing the world and feeling. As such, representations of the self in the world are required, such as those processed within bodily consciousness (Blanke 2012). Bodily consciousness is primarily supported by the temporoparietal junction (Ionta et al. 2011), the supplementary motor area, ventral premotor cortex, intraparietal sulcus and occipitotemporal cortex, as well as the posterior insula (Ionta et al. 2014). Together, these brain regions support self-location, body ownership, agency, and the first-person perspective of one’s body.

However, experiential awareness goes beyond bodily consciousness. Experiential awareness involves the integration of bottom-up interoceptive and exteroceptive signals, combined with top-down predictions to generate a current awareness state that provides predictions back down to visceral systems (Gu et al. 2013). Experiential awareness also involves the integration of bottom-up interoceptive and exteroceptive signals with top-down monitoring for salience (Menon and Uddin 2010). The body receives many signals through autonomic functioning outside of conscious awareness; to engage efficiently with the world, the system must determine which environmental or internal signals are sufficiently important to reach awareness. To support these processes of awareness and salience detection, the experiential self-state relies on the ACC and AI, key regions of the SN (Seeley et al. 2007). The SN serves as a ‘gatekeeper’ or ‘switch’, highlighting what is most relevant in the environment or internal state and coordinating neural resources to allow flexible and appropriate responses (Menon and Uddin 2010).

The SN’s core functions are (i) integrating information from the body, the senses, and affect, supporting awareness, motivation, and rapid behavioural responses (Critchley et al. 2004); (ii) monitoring both external (sensory) and internal (bodily) signals, identifying which events are most relevant or important at any given time (the bottom-up detection of salient events); (iii) modulating autonomic reactivity to salient stimuli and facilitating rapid access to the motor system (Menon and Uddin 2010); (iv) allocating attention and executive resources by switching the brain’s activity between the internally focused DMN and the externally focused central executive network (CEN) (Sridharan et al. 2008); and (v) modulating and switching between other networks—when a salient event is detected, the SN initiates, coordinates, and sustains activity in networks involved in attention and working memory.

Specifically, within the SN, the AI supports integration of interoceptive signals (internal physiological cues) and external sensory cues and prediction of bodily states to generate conscious access to interoceptive awareness (Craig 2009, Gu et al. 2013). The ACC is involved in linking emotional, motivational, attentional, and interoceptive processing relevant to bodily self-consciousness and self-regulation (Etkin et al. 2011), and is often co-activated with the AI for monitoring, error detection, and processing emotional relevance to the self. The AI and ACC together integrate signals about the body’s internal states (interoceptive processing) and help generate the experience of affect (Seth 2013, Barrett 2017). It should be noted that Craig’s (2009) proposal for the insula’s hierarchical cytoarchitecture, a strict tripartite division of the insula into posterior-bodily, mid-affective, and anterior-cognitive zones is now recognized as an oversimplification, with large-scale coactivation analyses indicating substantial functional overlap across subdivisions (Uddin et al. 2014). The present framework follows Craig’s broad account of the AI supporting self-aware interoceptive experience, but treats the insula’s integrative role as embedded within a predictive processing framework rather than as primarily structural and descriptive (see below).

Neuroimaging evidence supports the continuum proposed for the dual self states: the DMN and SN exhibit anticorrelated activity (Fox et al. 2005), such that shifts between reflective and experiential processing are best understood as graded changes in the relative dominance of each network, rather than discrete switches. Carhart-Harris et al. (2014) extend this, proposing that conscious states vary along a continuum of neural entropy, with altered states characterized by decreased DMN dominance and increased bottom-up signal complexity. In line with this, decreases in DMN activation have been noted in meditation experiences (Brewer et al. 2011, Tomasino et al. 2013) as well as other experiences of altered states of consciousness, including psychedelic (Gattuso et al. 2023) and awe (van Elk et al. 2019) experiences. Similarly, differential activation of the AI and ACC, and changes in their connectivity, have been observed across meditation (Fox et al. 2016), psychedelic (Yu et al. 2024), and religious experiences (Ferguson et al. 2018, McNamara and Grafman 2024).

While the present account centres on the SN as the cortical hub coordinating affective salience, the SN operates within a broader subcortical scaffold that generates the foundational conditions for awareness. Damasio and Carvalho (2013) identify brainstem nuclei, including the periaqueductal grey (PAG), parabrachial nucleus, and nucleus tractus solitarius, as the most caudal sites at which integrated maps of whole-body states are assembled, constituting central substrates for feeling states, and Panksepp (2004) similarly locates primary-process affective consciousness in PAG dynamics and their ascending projections to intralaminar thalamic nuclei. The thalamus likely plays a particularly pivotal role in this vertical architecture spanning the brainstem, thalamus, and cortex. Ward (2011) argues that thalamic activity is a necessary condition for conscious awareness (beyond a modulatory function), gating what cortical computations enter experience through thalamocortical synchrony. The experiential self state may therefore best be understood as emerging from a dynamically coupled system spanning brainstem affective nuclei, thalamic integrative hubs, and cortical SNs, with the SN serving as the principal cortical coordinator of affective salience within this broader architecture.

It should also be noted that this account presupposes a particular theoretical position on the relationship between affective salience and meta-awareness. In higher-order theories of consciousness (Rosenthal 2005), affective tone constitutes the content of experience, while meta-awareness is a distinct, second-order function that takes that content as its object, in which case the SN might support the former without explaining the latter. The present framework does not adopt this view; in line with enactive and neurophenomenological accounts (Varela et al. 2017, Dunne et al. 2019), meta-awareness here is not a second-order representation of affective states but a non-propositional, perspectival sensitivity, i.e. co-constituted with affective processing itself. The SN’s integration of interoceptive and exteroceptive signals is thus not understood as generating content that a separate awareness mechanism subsequently monitors; rather, the perspectival, felt quality of experience and the integration of bodily signals are two aspects of the same process.

Neurobiology of affective salience

Building on research situating dopamine as central in reward-based learning, research over the past two decades has increasingly examined the role of dopaminergic signalling in salience (Ungless 2004). The ‘motivational salience’ hypothesis proposes that activity in the dopamine system transforms neural representations of stimuli from neutral pieces of information into attractive or aversive entities that capture attention and motivate behaviour (Berridge and Robinson 1998). Dopamine release has been implicated in the reinforcement or ‘stamping-in’ of memories (particularly stimulus–stimulus and stimulus–response associations) which confers motivational importance or salience to otherwise neutral environmental stimuli (Wise 2004). This in turn biases attention, approach tendencies, and other goal-directed behaviour. In psychological terms, this pattern of dopaminergic ‘stamping-in’ can be understood as shaping ongoing affective processing, by calibrating which bodily sensations and environmental cues are experienced as motivationally charged and therefore prioritized in awareness and action.

Dopaminergic signalling is involved in detecting and assigning significance to prediction errors within a broader cortico-striatal network architecture [including the AI and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC)] (Schultz 2016). The midbrain dopamine neurons, particularly those projecting to the ventral striatum, show a reward prediction error signal, increasing when outcomes are better than expected, pausing when they are worse than expected, and returning to baseline when rewards are fully predicted (Schultz 2016). The SN interacts closely with dopaminergic midbrain and striatal targets during reward anticipation and outcome processing, with AI and dACC responding to valenced or ‘signed’ reward prediction error signals and to the motivational salience of unexpected events across reward, pain, and affective domains (Seeley et al. 2007, Menon and Uddin 2010).

There is individual variation in the amount of dopamine individuals can synthesize in the striatum, which has been associated with stronger resting state and task-evoked connectivity within the SN (McCutcheon et al. 2019b). Theories building on this research have suggested that dopamine-mediated reward prediction error signals are integrated by SN hubs to recalibrate large scale network configurations (for example, switching between default mode and CENs) in line with changing predictions about the organism’s internal state and environmental contingencies (Sridharan et al. 2008, Menon 2015).

While the primary focus here has been on dopamine, it should be noted that the neuromodulatory architecture underlying precision-weighting involves a broader division of labour. Within active inference frameworks, acetylcholine is associated with encoding sensory precision, modulating the signal-to-noise ratio of bottom-up prediction errors arising from expected uncertainty, while noradrenaline via the locus coeruleus (LC-NE) encodes unexpected uncertainty in the form of volatility in environmental contingencies, triggering rapid cortical gain adjustments and network reconfiguration (Dayan and Yu 2005, Parr and Friston 2017). The LC-NE system operates in phasic and tonic modes: Phasic bursts facilitate task-relevant behavioural responses by increasing cortical gain, while elevated tonic noradrenaline promotes disengagement from current goal-directed routines and exploration of alternative states (Aston-Jones and Cohen 2005). The LC-NE system may therefore be particularly critical for the rapid network-level reconfiguration underlying shifts toward the experiential self-state, with tonic LC activity supporting withdrawal from reflective processing, and phasic activity signalling the salience of interoceptive and affective events that anchor embodied awareness. In line with this suggestion, activity in the cingulo-insular-thalamic network, including AI and dACC, has been shown to correlate positively with upper alpha-band EEG power as a marker of sustained, non-selective perceptual readiness (Sadaghiani et al. 2010), a neural signature congruent with the experiential self-state as conceptualized here.

Dopamine modulates the affective weight and salience of bottom-up signals within the experiential self-state, calibrating which bodily and environmental cues are experienced as motivationally significant. The shift into that state is a separate process, and appears to be primarily a ‘serotonergic’ process: Evidence from pharmacological challenge studies shows that selective 5-HT2A antagonism attenuates the subjective and neural effects of psychedelics (Preller et al. 2018), pointing to 5-HT2A receptor agonism as the key mechanism underlying this shift in these states. Under non-pharmacological conditions, such as meditation, flow, absorption, a comparable shift may be achieved through attentional and regulatory means rather than pharmacological ones.

Predictive processing account

The predictive processing framework proposes that the brain continuously generates hierarchical generative models of the world and the body, using top-down predictions to suppress or contextualize incoming sensory signals (Friston 2010). Perception and experience are thus not a passive registration of inputs but an active inference process, in which prediction errors, which are mismatches between what is expected and what arrives, reach higher levels of the hierarchy only when they carry sufficient precision, or signal-to-noise weight, to warrant updating the model (Friston 2010). Within this framework, the reflective self-state corresponds to a high-prior regime: self-referential generative models, anchored in autobiographical memory and narrative, dominate processing and effectively suppress a wide range of bottom-up interoceptive and exteroceptive signals that do not fit the prevailing model of self and world. The experiential self-state, by contrast, corresponds to a recalibration of this regime, in which the precision of high-level priors is reduced and a broader range of bodily and sensory signals is granted sufficient weight to shape conscious experience, thus enabling informationally rich, matched engagement between body and brain which characterizes the feeling of presence.

Within this framework, the AI has been characterized as a comparator that suppresses informative interoceptive prediction errors via top-down predictions arising from a generative model of bodily states (Seth et al. 2012). In this framework, presence arises from the successful matching of informative interoceptive signals by top-down predictions—a dynamic brain–body-world loop in which the AI operates as a comparator, and its calibrated activity constitutes the felt sense of being here (Seth et al. 2012). Fermin et al. (2022) extend this architecture, proposing that the insula’s parallel networks with prefrontal cortex and striatum instantiate hierarchically organized interoceptive inference, from habitual visceral predictions in the posterior granular insula to exploratory higher-order representations in the agranular AI, with dopaminergic signals encoding the precision, or confidence, of each level. The experiential self-state maps onto this structure: It is the mode in which interoceptive predictions are precise and informative enough to sustain a coherent, affectively grounded sense of being here, without the high prediction-error load associated with disrupted presence or the hyperprior dominance associated with the reflective mode.

The REBUS model (Carhart-Harris and Friston 2019) provides a specific neurobiological account of how the shift toward the experiential self state is achieved pharmacologically, via a loosening of high-level priors. It proposes that 5-HT2A receptor activation on deep-layer pyramidal neurons in high-level cortical regions reduces the precision-weighting of top-down beliefs, freeing ascending bottom-up signals that would ordinarily be suppressed. This mechanism is what allows the experiential self-state to emerge in psychedelic conditions. Note that this is pharmacologically dissociable from dopaminergic function: Selective 5-HT2A antagonism attenuates the subjective and neural effects of psychedelics (Preller et al. 2018), pointing to serotonergic rather than dopaminergic signalling as the driver of prior relaxation in these states.

Taking a similar framework but focused on contemplative practice, Farb et al. (2015) propose that by means of meditative practice, predictive models of the self can be recalibrated by shifting the relative weighting of interoceptive signals and reflective interpretations. They suggest that the shift from reflective to experiential weakens rigid priors, allowing a wider range of bodily signals to be treated as acceptable rather than error signals that must be fixed. Van Elk and Aleman (2017) extend this to spiritual and mystical phenomena more broadly, suggesting that many spiritual or mystical phenomena involve altered weighting of interoceptive and exteroceptive prediction errors, changes in multisensory/DMN dynamics, and top-down interpretive models of ‘self’ and ‘other’ (e.g. divine agents). In these states, priors about the bodily self and the structure of experience are transiently relaxed or reweighted, such that interoceptive and multisensory signals may be accorded atypical precision, or normally dominant narrative models of self-world boundaries may be down-weighted, allowing experiences of unity, presence, or transcendence to emerge as alternative best-fitting interpretations of the incoming signal (van Elk and Aleman 2017).

Experiential self-states

The experiential self-state as affective salience

On a cognitive level, the functions typically ascribed to the SN can be characterized as involving the ongoing integration of interoceptive and exteroceptive information into a coherent, consciously accessible experiential state, shaped by top-down expectations and task demands (Craig 2009, Menon and Uddin 2010, Gu et al. 2013). Heightened awareness of bodily sensations and environmental cues is continuously evaluated for its personal and contextual relevance, such that only a subset of signals is prioritized for entry into awareness, with the remainder remaining at an autonomic, non-reportable level (Craig 2002, 2009, Garfinkel et al. 2015). In this sense, the SN serves as a filter and coordinator, selecting events of current importance and reallocating attentional and executive resources by shifting between internally oriented (DMN) and externally oriented (CEN) modes of processing (Menon and Uddin 2010, Goulden et al. 2014). Within this framework, the AI can be understood as supporting the integration of internal bodily cues, external sensory input, and predictive models to yield a felt sense of one’s current bodily-affective state, whereas the anterior cingulate contributes to attributing motivational significance, action readiness, and self-regulatory adjustments to this state (Seeley et al. 2007, Craig 2009).

In this framework, the psychological level description of neuronal salience processing will be referred to as ‘affective processing’. Affective processing should be understood in the understanding of ‘affect’ as the sensations and interoceptions situated between positive and negative valence and high and low arousal (Russell 1980). It should not be equated to feelings or emotions: Feelings are understood as the (reflectively) conscious experience of affect (i.e. the feeling of interoceptions and sensations) and emotions are understood as ‘appraisals’ of what you have just experienced or thought of, as a culturally defined label for routinely experienced feelings, which helps a person categorize and make sense of what they are feeling (Barrett 2017). Thus this framework takes a view of affect as a combination of valence and arousal, likely present across sentient animals, which function is to motivate the experiencer to approach or avoid (Corr 2013). Affective processing can therefore be considered the psychology version of salience processing as described in the neuroscience literature which involves the consideration of bodily signals, the allocation of attention, and the motivation to action. This affective processing is supported by the dopaminergic system, where signed reward prediction errors signal motivational salience of events and which contribute to the SN and its modulation of other brain networks.

The present paper adopts Barrett’s dimensional characterization of core affect (valence and arousal as the fundamental axes of affective experience; Barrett 2006) as a useful descriptive taxonomy, while disagreeing with her constructivist conclusion that no brain region plays a dedicated or privileged role in generating affective experience. The neurobiological framework proposed here aligns more closely with Craig’s (2009) account of hierarchical interoceptive processing, but extends it by situating the AI within a broader architecture spanning brainstem affective nuclei, thalamic integrative hubs, and cortical SNs, and by specifying the computational mechanisms (e.g. precision-weighting, hierarchical inference, and dopaminergic signalling) by which affective salience constitutes the experiential self-state.

Meditation as experiential self-state practice

Meditation is a particularly effective context to investigate consciousness during the experiential self-state (Gibson 2024), because it is a group of practices which moves its practitioners away from the reflective self in its content or philosophy, has attention regulation as its foundation (Lutz et al. 2008, Hölzel et al. 2011), improves interoception and meta-awareness (Kok and Singer 2017), and alters key brain circuits associated with both the narrative and experiential selves, specifically the DMN and SN, and their connectivity (Farb et al. 2007, 2015, Brewer et al. 2011). For mindfulness and open-monitoring practices particularly, it may be argued that meditation training systematically trains the shift from narrative, reflective self-processing to an embodied, affective-salience-guided experiential self-state (Farb et al. 2007, Parkinson et al. 2019).

Though meditation encompasses a broad spectrum of techniques, at its core are emotional and attentional regulatory strategies (Lutz et al. 2008). A further distinction can be made between ‘focused attention meditations’, which involve a narrow window of attention, whereby practitioners focus on a chosen object (e.g. their breath or a body sensation), and ‘open monitoring meditations’, which involve a broad, open, receptive window of attention (Lutz et al. 2008, Gibson 2024).

This distinction between the two main meditation techniques is further demonstrated in a large-scale longitudinal study with 229 adults conducted by Kok and Singer (2017), in which they compare four different meditation techniques, including breathing and body scan meditations (both focused attention techniques), open monitoring, and loving kindness. They found that while all techniques improved interoceptive awareness and focus, certain techniques improved certain processes more than others: the body-scan meditation, one of the focused attention techniques, led to the greatest increase in interoceptive awareness, whereas observing thought meditation, the used open-monitoring technique, was associated with the greatest increase in meta-awareness. Importantly, they found that once participants improved in interoception, improvements in meta-awareness followed.

This hierarchy of processes is reflected in the order in which meditators tend to learn meditation: Many novice meditators start with focused attention techniques before moving to open monitoring (Lutz et al. 2008, Hölzel et al. 2011, Tang et al. 2012, Farb et al. 2015). Meditation practice can be understood as a ‘staged’ transition from an effortful, top-down conceptually driven ‘doing’ mode to a more effortless, interoceptively grounded ‘being’ mode (Tang et al. 2012, Chiesa et al. 2013, Farb et al. 2013, 2015). Tang et al. (2012) suggest that in early practice, maintaining the intended mental state requires deliberate top-down control to stabilize attention on chosen objects and to counteract mind-wandering, drawing heavily on executive networks and conflict-monitoring processes. With ongoing training, however, attention is increasingly reallocated; away from abstract, narrative self-processing towards moment-by-moment interoceptive sensations such as a breath and body, associated with increased involvement of the anterior cingulate, insula, and striatum, which reduce the conflict between competing states and support state switching (Tang et al. 2012).

Chiesa et al. (2013) likewise argue that mindfulness training initially relies on top-down emotion regulation mechanisms, but with continued practice comes to engage predominantly bottom-up processes characterized by reduced reactivity in emotion-generative and interoceptive systems, without the need for active cognitive reappraisal. Consistent with this, Farb et al. (2007) demonstrate that an 8-week intensive mindfulness course sees participants activating the ventromedial prefrontal cortex and amygdala less, combined with increased activation in the insula and somatosensory regions, reflecting increased interoceptive and exteroceptive awareness, when asked to engage in an experiential focus task. Farb et al. (2015) describe early practice as dominated by conceptually structured self-processing and later stages as emphasizing an experiential focus on present-moment interoceptive sensation, and related work shows that mindfulness training increases interoception-related recruitment of AI and strengthens functional coupling between prefrontal regions and posterior insula during interoceptive attention (Farb et al. 2013).

Traits associated with affective salience processing

The ability to shift from the reflective to the experiential self-state is influenced by meditation. As such, it comes as no surprise that trait mindfulness is associated with decreased functional connectivity with neural regions related to self-referential processing and mind wandering (i.e. DMN regions) and increased functional connectivity with neural regions related to attentional control, interoception, and executive function (including the ACC and insula) (Parkinson et al. 2019). In this, trait mindfulness is considered stable personality-like characteristic (though it can be trained and developed), and is typically measuring by one of two self-report scales, namely the Mindful Attention Awareness Scale (Brown and Ryan 2003) or the Five-Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (Baer et al. 2006).

A trait associated with the ability to sustain an experiential self-state - though perhaps with less voluntary control over switching to said state than the mindfulness trait - is ‘absorption’. Trait absorption is considered capacity for strong attentional commitment and imaginative involvement (Lifshitz et al. 2019) and to involve an experiential mindset rather than an instrumental mindset, or ‘a readiness for effortless experiencing’ (Tellegen 1981, p. 223). According to Tellegen (1981), people who score high on trait absorption, are drawn towards immersion into experiences as an end in itself, involving total attentional engagement. While we may all have such experiences, such as awe experiences when viewing a sunset, some people appear to have a particular willingness and ability to become deeply engrossed in sensory and imaginative experiences. Furthermore, such experiences involving absorption tend to be spontaneous or effortless and moreover feel very ‘real’ (Lifshitz et al. 2019).

Like trait mindfulness, trait absorption is considered a relatively stable personality-like characteristic, though anthropologist Tanya Luhrmann has suggested that it may be developed through prayer and frequent engagement in other religious rituals (Luhrmann et al. 2010, Luhrmann 2012, 2020), which involve focused attention and immersion into one’s own mind. Moreover, it has been found that greater mindfulness practice and experience in actors is associated with greater trait absorption (van Mulukom 2024); an association which may go in either direction, or both, but which further supports the link with the experiential self-state.

Trait absorption is typically measured by the Tellegen Absorption scale (Tellegen and Atkinson 1974, Jamieson 2005) which shows strong internal and test–retest reliability, though the construct validity of the scale remains disputed (Terhune and Jamieson 2021), such as whether it is a unified psychological construct, or a set of distinct capacities that have a strong tendency to co-occur (Girn et al. 2023). Moreover, it may be a context-dependent measure despite being a trait measure (Tellegen 1981), which may influence the reliability with which associations are found (Girn et al. 2023).

At the neurobiological level, absorption has been associated with the intensity of mystical-type experiences under psychedelics (Studerus et al. 2012, Lebedev et al. 2015), suggesting that a trait-level readiness for experiential immersion may predispose individuals to shifts away from the reflective self-state under conditions that pharmacologically relax high-level priors (Carhart-Harris and Friston 2019). Preliminary evidence further suggests a serotonergic substrate for this individual difference: Stenbaek et al. (2021) found that lower pre-drug neocortical 5-HT2A receptor binding potential predicted both a more prolonged peak of psilocybin-induced subjective intensity and higher mystical-type experience scores, raising the possibility that trait absorption and serotonergic receptor availability may reflect overlapping neurobiological substrates underlying individual differences in experiential immersion. However, at present, the evidence linking trait absorption specifically to 5-HT2A receptor density remains correlational, and the precise nature of this relationship still needs to be established. Similarly, trait absorption was not reliably associated with grey matter density in brain regions, nor static resting-state functional connectivity (Girn et al. 2023)—in part possibly due to the absorption scale’s psychometric issues.

Another trait associated with the experiential self-state is Openness to Experience, one of the Big Five personality dimensions, characterized by intellectual curiosity, aesthetic sensitivity, and receptivity to novel inner and outer experiences (McCrae and Costa 1987). Openness is the strongest Big Five correlate of trait absorption (Tellegen and Atkinson 1974), and is also reliably associated with self-reported awe and mystical-type experiences (Griffiths et al. 2011), suggesting that individuals high in this trait may have a greater general propensity for immersive experiential engagement. Openness also appears to be responsive to experiential interventions: MacLean et al. (2011) found significant increases in Openness following psilocybin-occasioned mystical experiences, with effects persisting at 14-month follow-up. This finding has since been replicated in a clinical sample, where psilocybin therapy for treatment-resistant depression was associated with pronounced increases in Openness at 3-month follow-up (Erritzoe et al. 2018). More recently, Weiss et al. (2024) found that psilocybin therapy was associated with increases in both Openness and trait absorption relative to baseline, with some effects persisting at 6-month follow-up. These findings suggest that Openness functions both as a trait-level predictor of experiential depth and as an outcome of transformative experiential states.

Deficits associated with affective salience processing

Disordered functioning of the SN is also implicated in maladaptive traits and affective disorders (Schimmelpfennig et al. 2023), including the association of trait anxiety with overactivation of the AI-dACC pathway (Massullo et al. 2020), and elevated neuroticism with heightened AI responses even during objectively certain decisions, consistent with a tendency to experience relatively safe situations as uncertain or potentially threatening (Feinstein et al. 2006). Taking stock of several studies on deficits associated with an overactive SN, Schimmelpfennig et al. (2023) conclude that AI overactivation is associated with excessive sensitivity and anxiety arousal. This is in line with the understanding of AI as the hub in the SN which integrates interoceptive and exteroceptive prediction errors into a unified ‘best guess’ about bodily threat or safety, and which, when hyper-responsive, is likely to overweight threat-related prediction errors and underweight safety-confirming evidence. In predictive-processing terms, such overactivation can be seen as an aberrant assignment of precision to threat priors and bodily warning signals, biasing affective processing toward chronic vigilance, uncertainty, and avoidance even in relatively benign contexts.

Extending this predictive-processing view from cortical to subcortical levels, disruptions of dopamine signalling can likewise be understood as altering how much precision is assigned to particular prediction errors and salience tags. Disruptions of dopamine signalling have been understood as altering the precision and attribution of salience to prediction errors, which in turn may contribute to aberrant motivational and psychotic experiences, such as in schizophrenia (Howes et al. 2012, Howes and Nour 2016, McCutcheon et al. 2019a). The ‘aberrant salience hypothesis of schizophrenia’ (Kapur 2003) suggests that disordered mesostriatal dopamine release leads to an over-attribution of meaning and motivational value (or ‘incentive salience’) to otherwise irrelevant environment events (Howes and Nour 2016). This in turn can be associated with the feeling that everything in the world holds meaning and may have changed in an unknown way. When this experience is turned inwards, then salience may be misattributed to internal states and representations, which in turn can then be perceived as externally generated—i.e. auditory verbal hallucinations.

Within the experiential self-state, such disturbances in dopamine and salience processing are expected to distort the basic felt configuration of bodily-affective signals and their contextual relevance. Insofar as mesostriatal dopamine and SN hubs help determine which interoceptive and exteroceptive cues are treated as signal rather than noise, their dysregulation may lead to a mode of experience in which ordinary sensations, thoughts, and perceptions are accorded atypical motivational weight and are therefore experienced as unusually significant, intrusive, or self-relevant. Under these conditions, the experiential self may become pervaded with a sense of heightened meaningfulness or threat, with aberrant affective tagging of stimuli shaping moment-to-moment awareness in ways that depart from the more balanced, context-sensitive integration of bodily state, environment, and action readiness described in healthy experiential self-states. At the same time, evidence that higher trait self-transcendence is associated with greater dopamine D2/3 receptor availability in the left insula (Kim et al. 2019) suggests that trait-like variation in dopaminergic signalling within this SN hub may also support experiential self-states in which bodily and contextual signals are tagged as meaningful in non-pathological ways, facilitating transcendent modes of experience.

So far we have concentrated on conditions involving insufficient or misdirected salience signalling. A complementary pattern emerges in hyperactive salience states where the problem is a failure to down-regulate threat-related precision signals, such as in anxiety disorders and PTSD. In these conditions, chronic locus coeruleus (LC) hyperactivity and noradrenergic overactivation amplify the gain of threat-related interoceptive and exteroceptive signals, sustaining pathological hypervigilance in which anticipatory bodily threat dominates the experiential self rather than affectively balanced presence (Krystal and Neumeister 2009, Morris et al. 2020). In predictive processing terms, this reflects aberrant upweighting of threat priors and of precision assigned to ascending bodily warning signals, alongside diminished top-down prefrontal regulation. This pattern is consistent with the inverted-U model of noradrenergic function, in which excessive LC-NE activity impairs prefrontal cognitive control and shifts processing toward bottom-up, salience-driven responses (Aston-Jones and Cohen 2005, Bouras et al. 2023). Neuroimaging evidence supports this framework: Acute-stress-induced changes in SN connectivity prospectively predict PTSD symptom development, with hyperconnectivity between core SN regions (including the amygdala, AI, and dACC) and hypoconnectivity of the DMN characterizing vulnerability to post-traumatic symptoms (Zhang et al. 2022).

This hypervigilant configuration raises an important caveat for the proposal that meditation and other contemplative practices cultivate the experiential self-state. If such practices function by increasing the precision accorded to interoceptive signals and relaxing hyperprior dominance, they may be contraindicated, or at minimum require modification, for individuals whose baseline state is already characterized by excessive bottom-up interoceptive precision. Lindahl et al. (2017) document a substantial range of adverse contemplative phenomena, including heightened anxiety, derealization, and involuntary hyperarousal, particularly among practitioners engaged in intensive retreat-based practice; these effects were more prevalent in individuals with prior trauma exposure or anxiety vulnerability. Antonova et al. (2015) found that experienced meditators showed attenuated startle habituation relative to controls, suggesting that sustained meditation practice may reduce the normal dampening of threat-related interoceptive responses. The relationship between meditation and the experiential self-state therefore appears to be moderated by baseline noradrenergic tone. For individuals in hypervigilant states, therapeutic approaches that first recalibrate LC-NE and SN hyperreactivity (e.g. trauma-sensitive interventions or top-down emotion regulation approaches) may be necessary preconditions for contemplative practice to facilitate, rather than exacerbate, the experiential self-state.

Discussion

The dual-self states framework proposed here argues that consciousness is best understood as consisting of reflective and experiential self-states, which are dynamic modes rather than fixed structural ‘selves’. The experiential self-state is characterized as an affective-salience mode of awareness, in which interoceptive and exteroceptive signals are integrated by the SN into a coherent, non-propositional sense of being-here-as-subject, accompanied by meta-awareness. Self-transcendent and related altered states serve as a particularly productive empirical context for investigating these modes and the shifts between them, with meditation research providing the most developed evidence base.

The present account extends classic distinctions of selves which are largely static (James 1890, Gallagher 2000, Damasio 2010) and neurophenomenological work on the embodied self, by foregrounding the phenomenology of non-reflective, meta-aware subjectivity. The account distinguishes experiential awareness from nondual awareness (Josipovic and Miskovic 2020) and bodily self-consciousness (Liang et al. 2021), and anchors this distinction in specific neurocomputational mechanisms. The present framework also has affinities with Gerrans’ (2024) proposal that the self is best understood as an ‘avatar’: a generative model constructed by the brain to coordinate interoceptive and exteroceptive signals in service of homeostatic regulation. The present account extends this by characterizing the experiential self-state as an actively cultivable mode of consciousness, one that can be trained through for instance contemplative practice and that provides a theoretical and empirical framework for investigating shifts in self-awareness more broadly.

Conceptualizing distinct types of consciousness is challenging given the difficulty of identifying non-reflective yet aware cognitive processes, and much of what is known reflects a longstanding bias toward language-mediated, reflective accounts: Research on consciousness typically proceeds through verbal report, which has historically privileged reflective awareness over the experiential self-state. Experiential awareness may be expressed through art and symbolization (van Mulukom et al. 2025b), but such expressions are not generally treated as valid sources of knowledge within the scientific disciplines that study consciousness.

The present framework holds that perspectival ownership is necessary for consciousness, whereas bodily ownership, while facilitating the experiential self-state, does not carry this status. A detailed account of how interoceptive focus recedes (through decentring or a shift from top-down to bottom-up processing, as in meditative practice) remains an important target for future work, as do temporal models of how these processes unfold in real time. Such work would address the question of how far the experiential self-state can be sustained without a body-centred spatial perspective, and what this implies for the minimal conditions of conscious subjectivity. The framework also points to direct links between interoception, the AI, and the self-as-subject as requiring further investigation (Babo-Rebelo, Richter, et al. 2016a, Babo-Rebelo, Wolpert, et al. 2016b), alongside connections to the autonomic nervous system (Berntson et al. 2019).

The present account takes the experiential self-state to be an SN-based, meta-aware mode of subjectivity, thus integrating phenomenological descriptions of experiential awareness and self-transcendence with predictive-processing and dopamine-based accounts of salience, proposing that shifts from reflective to experiential self-states reflect changes in the precision-weighting of interoceptive, exteroceptive, and conceptual priors mediated by SN and mesolimbic mechanisms (Menon and Uddin 2010, van Elk and Aleman 2017). The proposed role of dopamine and SNs is anchored in established findings on reward prediction error, motivational salience, and their network-level consequences (Menon 2015, Schultz 2016), but the specific mapping onto reflective and experiential self-states remains to be tested directly.

Nonetheless, this account allows for testable predictions. The dual self-states continuum model predicts that shifts toward the experiential self-state should be accompanied by graded reductions in DMN engagement and increases in SN dominance. The magnitude of change in SN connectivity between reflective and experiential tasks should correlate with subjective ratings of experiential and perspectival awareness and with trait mindfulness or self-transcendence. This is testable longitudinally using meditation training paradigms with repeated neuroimaging, predicting practice-dependent changes in DMN-SN anticorrelation that track self-report measures of experiential depth. Under pharmacological manipulations, individual differences in baseline serotonergic and dopaminergic function should further modulate these shifts: serotonergic prior-relaxation should predict the degree of shift toward experiential self-reports, while baseline striatal dopamine synthesis capacity should predict the affective intensity and salience of the resulting experiential content, consistent with the distinct roles of serotonergic and dopaminergic mechanisms proposed here.

If the experiential self-state is constitutively dependent on body-world coupling rather than neural activity alone, then conditions that selectively decouple bodily engagement from environmental responsiveness, while leaving SN activity intact, should preferentially disrupt the experiential self-state without equivalently affecting reflective self-processing. Tasks that selectively increase interoceptive prediction-error salience (e.g. unpredictable heartbeat or breathing perturbations) should enhance experiential self phenomenology and SN activity more in trained meditators than controls, reflecting altered precision-weighting learned through practice. Paradigms in which interoceptive feedback is decoupled from action consequences, such as cardiac feedback paradigms with real-time delay or pharmacological manipulation of peripheral autonomic signals, combined with experience sampling measures targeting experiential versus reflective dimensions, would provide a direct test of this prediction.

Evidence from meditation neuroscience, research on altered states of consciousness, and studies of individual differences in traits and disorders all support the framework. Meditative training shifts activation and connectivity away from DMN activity toward interoceptive and SN regions, developing present-centred, non-propositional awareness consistent with the experiential self-state (e.g. Farb et al. 2007, Fox et al. 2016). Meditation, and likely other mind–body practices such as yoga, can therefore be understood as systematic training in the shift from reflective to experiential self states, recalibrating predictive models of self and world through changed SN dynamics. Further research may extend causal findings of longitudinal research (which do, it should be noted, often involve confounding issues with proficiency in meditation studies) with neuromodulation studies (Abellaneda-Pérez et al. 2024), targeting specific brain regions to elucidate subcomponents of the processes.

Psychedelic, awe, and religious experiences like meditative experiences down-regulate default mode activity and alter SN dynamics (Mograbi et al. 2024), in parallel with reports of ego dissolution and changed boundaries between the self and other (Millière et al. 2018). Further research could elucidate distinctions and overlaps between experiences in meditation and in other altered states experiences, like psychedelic, awe, religious, spiritual, and mystical experiences (Yaden and van Elk 2026). A shared experience of self-transcendence, by which the self-boundaries and self-salience are reduced and by which consequently a person becomes connected or unified with something greater than oneself (Yaden et al. 2017), has been argued to unify these experiences, by reflecting a shift from the reflective self to the experiential self via ego dissolution and immersion (van Mulukom 2026). Building on philosopher Charles Taylor’s theory of a Western shift from a ‘porous’ engaged pre-modern self to a ‘buffered’ or mind-centred, disengaged self (Taylor 2007), it has been proposed that the reflective self can be considered a boundary (e.g. between self and others, or self and the world), which is much reduced in the experiential self-state (van Mulukom 2026). Further research, including anthropological research (Luhrmann et al. 2021, Luhrmann and Weisman 2022), will likely show the usefulness of this conceptualization; for some preliminary current work on self-transcendence and self-boundaries, see research by Dambrun and colleagues (Dambrun 2016, Hanley et al. 2020) and Lindström and colleagues (Lindström et al. 2023, 2025).

Trait-level findings that mindfulness, absorption, self-transcendence, anxiety, and neuroticism are associated with interoceptive awareness, SN connectivity, and dopaminergic function, provide further evidence for trait modulations of the experiential self-states. However, any insights into traits remain limited by the current psychometric and accompanying neurobiological status of some constructs, particularly absorption. A validated psychological scale measuring reflective and experiential self-states would also strengthen the empirical programme (cf. van Mulukom 2026). Finally, while disorders like schizophrenia are briefly mentioned here, an investigation of a wider range of relevant deficits would be insightful, also including psychosis more broadly (Kesby et al. 2023) and disorders of the self, like derealization/depersonalization (Seth et al. 2012, Gerrans 2019). For instance, Deane et al. (2020) found, when comparing the loss of allostatic control in depersonalization with that of the selfless experience of meditation practitioners, that depersonalization corresponds to a breakdown of the experiential self-state, in which affective-salience signalling and meta-aware grip on the world are lost along with the sense of perspectival ownership, whereas meditative selflessness shows a experiential self-state in which boundaries are loosened but affective attunement, allostatic control, and meta-awareness are preserved or even enhanced.

Limitations sketched here show where further research is most needed. For instance, neurocomputational models should instantiate reflective and experiential self-states as distinct patterns of priors and precision-weighting over interoceptive, exteroceptive, and top-down channels, and derive predictions about how contemplative practice or pharmacological interventions should move systems between these configurations (Farb et al. 2015, van Elk and Aleman 2017). Second, experimental work should systematically probe experiential ownership across meditative, psychedelic, awe, and religious contexts, combining multisensory illusions, interoceptive tasks, network-level measures, and fine-grained phenomenology to determine how far experiential self states can be sustained when bodily self-representations are altered (e.g. Lindström et al. 2023, 2025, van Mulukom et al. 2025a). Third, longitudinal studies should examine how trait mindfulness, absorption, self-transcendence, and anxiety shape the trajectory and quality of experiential self-states during training or therapy, with the idea to potentially develop interventions according to SN and neurobiological profiles (Parkinson et al. 2019, Schimmelpfennig et al. 2023). Finally, extending this research onto comparative (Panksepp 2005), developmental (Rochat 2019), and cross-cultural research (Luo et al. 2022), would allow both for a better understanding of the constructs, but also explore its possible extensions and generalizations as well as specific limitations (e.g. in terms of applicability across ages, cultures, and species).

In conclusion, this dual-self states framework places experiential self states as central to conscious life, and encourages consciousness research to take seriously modes of awareness that have been under-theorized, including non-propositional, embodied, and meta-aware or perspectival forms of subjectivity that often emerge most clearly in self-transcendent and contemplative contexts. These self-transcendent and related altered states are treated as productive venues for investigating how the experiential mode of consciousness is embodied and affectively structured, not as uncommon outliers. By bringing together phenomenology, neuroscience, predictive processing, and traits, this framework offers a programme for future consciousness research that takes seriously embodiment, diverse experiences, and individual differences. This dual-self states framework is intended as an invitation to investigate the full extent of human consciousness with embodied and phenomenological perspectives, to better understand how brain, body, and environment come together to produce multiple states of being a self in the world, self-transcendent experiences among them.
