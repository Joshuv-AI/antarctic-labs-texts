A comparative analysis of preoperative single-dose antimicrobial therapy versus extended postoperative antimicrobial regimen in elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy
Authors: S M Gurutham Prahalad Reddy, P V Raghuveer Pratap, A Ajay Raja, P Nikhil Bharadwaj
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGallbladder stones are the most prevalent condition affecting the biliary system, with varying incidence rates worldwide. Given the low occurrence of post-operative infections following laparoscopic cholecystectomy, the necessity of prophylactic antibiotic administration is often debated. Several clinical trials have demonstrated that a single pre-operative dose of antibiotics is just as effective as multiple doses. This study aims to compare the effects of single-dose versus multiple-dose antibiotics in laparoscopic cholecystectomy, specifically regarding post-operative infection-related complications. This prospective comparative study was conducted in a tertiary care hospital, Tamil-Nadu for a period of 18 months. Patients were randomly allocated into the study and control groups using a computer-generated simple randomization sequence in a 1:1 allocation ratio. No block randomization or stratification based on age, ASA grade, or other baseline variables was applied, and single dose of cefoperazone and sulbactam given at the time of induction to one group and in the other group cefoperazone + sulbactum 1 gm 1-0-1 was continued post-operatively in the other group for 5 days and Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE), which were opened only after patient enrolment. Patients were followed up for four weeks to study the post-operative infections and related complications. Due to the nature of the intervention (single-dose vs. extended antibiotic regimen), blinding was not feasible. The study was therefore conducted as an open-label trial. The majority of participants were between 31 and 40 years old, with a slightly higher proportion of males compared to females. Baseline characteristics were largely comparable between the two groups, except for a significantly higher proportion of ASA Grade II patients in the single-dose group compared to the extended-regimen group (77.8% vs. 57.8%, p = 0.034). The growth of organisms showed no variation between the two groups, and the incidence of surgical site infections (SSIs) was comparable, with no statistically significant difference (p > 0.05). Surgical site infection occurred in 3 of 45 patients (6.7%) in the single-dose group and 2 of 45 patients (4.4%) in the extended postoperative antibiotic group. This difference was not statistically significant p value 0.900. Single-dose prophylactic antibiotic administered at the induction of anesthesia is just as effective as multiple-dose antibiotic therapy continued post-operatively in preventing post-operative infections among patients undergoing laparoscopic cholecystectomy.Keywords: Gallstones, Prophylaxis, AnesthesiaSubject terms: Diseases, Gastroenterology, Medical research

Introduction

Gallbladder stones are the most common pathology affecting the biliary system, with incidence rates varying globally. Cholesterol stones are the most frequently found in a functioning gallbladder. In India, gallstone prevalence is higher among females than males and is more common in Northern India compared to Southern India1. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy is considered the gold standard treatment for gallstones due to its advantages, including less post-operative pain, shorter hospital stays, minimal analgesic requirements, and improved cosmetic outcomes2. Additionally, it offers a lower complication rate and better post-operative outcomes compared to conventional open procedures3. Prophylactic antibiotic use in laparoscopic cholecystectomy involves the administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics targeting the most common organisms responsible for surgical site infections (SSIs). This ensures a higher tissue concentration of the antibiotic, preventing bacterial colonization4. In clean cases with no bacterial contamination, antibiotics may not be necessary5. However, antibiotic prophylaxis is mandatory in open cholecystectomy, as wound infections are a common complication due to the differing behaviour of skin in open procedures compared to laparoscopic ones6. Despite this, the actual benefit of antibiotic prophylaxis in infection prevention remains unclear7. Surgical site infections (SSIs) remain a significant concern, contributing to morbidity, mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and increased out-of-pocket expenses. Many surgeons continue post-operative antibiotic administration for 7–10 days, even in clean and clean-contaminated cases, due to the fear of SSIs. However, this practice adds to the financial burden, increases the risk of hospital-acquired infections due to extended hospitalization, and promotes antibiotic resistance. Several clinical trials have demonstrated that a single pre-operative dose of antibiotics is as effective as multiple doses in preventing post-operative infections. Cephalosporins, known for their superior tissue penetration, maximal antimicrobial activity, safety, effectiveness, and non-toxicity, are globally recommended as prophylactic antibiotics8,9. This study examines the effects of single-dose versus multiple-dose antibiotic regimens on post-operative infection-related complications following laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Study design and setting

This prospective comparative study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Tamil Nadu from May 2023 to December 2024 (18 months). The study protocol was approved by the SRM Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No: SRMIEC-ST0922-186). All procedures followed relevant clinical and ethical guidelines. Written and oral informed consent was obtained from all participants in English or Tamil. Patient confidentiality and data safety were strictly maintained.

A total of 90 patients were enrolled and randomly allocated into two equal groups of 45.

The sample size was calculated based on previously reported SSI rates of approximately 5% in laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Assuming a non-inferiority margin of 5%, with a confidence level of 95% and a power of 80%, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 86 patients. To account for possible dropouts, 90 patients were included (45 in each group).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis was undertaken. The continuous variables were analyzed and presented as mean and standard deviation. Statistical significance was found using student t test for continuous variables and by Chi-square test for categorial variables.

Results

Demographic characteristics

Both the study and control groups had equal numbers of participants across all age categories. The majority of participants were aged 31–40 years, accounting for 42.2% in the study group and 35.6% in the control group. Males comprised a larger proportion of the total sample (56.7%) compared to females (43.3%), with similar distributions in both groups: males represented 55.6% in the study group and 57.8% in the control group, while females accounted for 44.4% and 42.2%, respectively.

Participants classified as ASA Grade 2 were more prevalent than those in ASA Grade 1, with 77.8% in the study group and 57.8% in the control group (Chi-square test, p = 0.034). Type 2 diabetes mellitus was present in 66.6% of the study group and 51.1% of the control group (Chi-square test, p = 0.125), while systemic hypertension was observed in 60% of study participants and 46.6% of control participants (Chi-square test, p = 0.187). Upper abdominal pain was the most commonly reported symptom, affecting 75.5% of the study group and 78% of the control group (Chi-square test, p = 0.780) (Table 1). The study group had a significantly higher proportion of ASA Grade II patients compared to the control group (77.8% vs. 57.8%, p = 0.034). Type 2 diabetes mellitus was present in 30/45 (66.6%) of the study group and 23/45 (51.1%) of the control group (p = 0.125). Hypertension was observed in 27/45 (60%) and 21/45 (46.6%) respectively (p = 0.187). The incidence of SSIs did not differ significantly between the two groups (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The principal finding of the present study is that a single prophylactic dose of antibiotic administered at induction is as effective as continued postoperative antibiotic therapy in preventing Surgical Site Infections (SSIs) following elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. No statistically significant difference was observed in SSI rates between the two groups, supporting the non-inferiority of single-dose prophylaxis (Table 5).

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy has become the standard treatment for symptomatic cholelithiasis due to its advantages of reduced tissue trauma, shorter hospital stays, and lower postoperative infection rates compared to open surgery. Consequently, the necessity of prolonged antibiotic use in uncomplicated laparoscopic procedures has been increasingly questioned. Current recommendations suggest that antibiotic prophylaxis should be reserved for selected high-risk situations, including acute cholecystitis, bile spillage, jaundice, pregnancy, immunocompromised states, and conversion to open surgery10,1111.

The findings of the present study are consistent with those reported by Shah et al., who demonstrated comparable SSI rates between single-dose and multiple-dose antibiotic regimens in elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy12. Their study emphasized that single-dose prophylaxis improves patient compliance, reduces treatment cost, minimizes adverse drug reactions, and limits the emergence of antimicrobial resistance. Similarly, Basant et al., and Naser et al., reported no significant difference in SSI rates between patients receiving a single pre-operative dose of ciprofloxacin and those receiving postoperative antibiotics for two to three days13,14. Their study further highlighted the economic advantage of single-dose therapy, concluding that a single 500 mg dose of ciprofloxacin administered at induction is sufficient for SSI prevention in uncomplicated cases.

Comparable results have also been reported by Sutariya et al., who observed postoperative infection rates of 4.4% in the single-dose group and 3.3% in the multiple-dose group, with no statistically significant difference between the two17. They concluded that extended postoperative antibiotic administration does not confer additional benefit in elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy. Likewise, Koirala et al. and Chand et al. reported similar SSI rates between single-dose and prolonged antibiotic groups, with differences that were not statistically significant15,16. These findings collectively reinforce the growing body of evidence against routine postoperative antibiotic use in low-risk laparoscopic cholecystectomy.

Microbiological analysis in the present study identified Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus species, and skin commensals as the most commonly isolated organisms, consistent with organisms reported in previous studies. Importantly, the spectrum and incidence of SSIs were similar in both study groups, further supporting the adequacy of single-dose antibiotic prophylaxis.

From a broader perspective, the implications of these findings are clinically and economically significant. Routine continuation of postoperative antibiotics increases healthcare costs, exposes patients to unnecessary drug-related adverse effects, and contributes to the growing problem of antimicrobial resistance. Adoption of a single-dose prophylactic regimen aligns with principles of antibiotic stewardship, ensuring effective infection prevention while minimizing inappropriate antibiotic exposure.

An important observation in this study was the significantly higher proportion of ASA Grade II patients in the single-dose group. Since higher ASA grade is associated with increased risk of surgical site infections, this baseline imbalance could have potentially biased the results against the single-dose group. However, despite this higher risk profile, the incidence of SSIs remained comparable between the two groups. Although the single-dose group had a significantly higher proportion of ASA Grade II patients, who may carry a relatively increased perioperative risk profile, SSI rates remained comparable between groups. This suggests that the absence of a significant difference is unlikely to be solely attributable to baseline imbalance; however, residual confounding cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

The rate of post-operative surgical site infections (SSIs) in elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy is similar between patients receiving a single intravenous dose of cefoperazone and sulbactam at anesthesia induction and those receiving multiple intravenous doses for five days post-procedure, in addition to the peri-operative dosage. Moreover, a single-dose antibiotic regimen can help reduce medical expenses without compromising effectiveness. Therefore, in clean surgical procedures such as laparoscopic cholecystectomy, administering a single dose of cefoperazone and sulbactam is a safe and effective approach. This approach supports antibiotic stewardship by reducing unnecessary antibiotic exposure and limiting the development of antimicrobial resistance.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the open-label design may have introduced observer or performance bias, as neither participants nor investigators were blinded to treatment allocation. Second, this was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability of the findings to broader clinical settings. Third, the relatively small sample size of 90 patients may have limited the statistical power to detect small differences in SSI rates between groups. Finally, the conservative postoperative oral intake protocol beginning on postoperative day 3 differs from current enhanced recovery pathways favoring earlier feeding and may affect external applicability.
