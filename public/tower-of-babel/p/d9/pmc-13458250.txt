Closing the gap – lung cancer evaluation and treatment among indigenous and non‐indigenous patients in the Top End, Northern Territory, Australia
Authors: Angus Lloyd, Paul DeKlerk, Subash Heraganahally, Lin Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundLung cancer outcomes have been reported to be poorer in the indigenous population of the Northern Territory (NT). We examine whether longer diagnostic and treatment time frames contribute to the observed survival disparity.AimsAssess the time frames to diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer in the Top End, NT, Australia.MethodsDemographic, clinical and survival data were assessed and compared between indigenous and non‐indigenous patients diagnosed with lung cancer between January 2015 and December 2020.ResultsOut of 534 medical records assessed, 388 patients with complete data were included, of which 91 (23%, males 55%) were indigenous and 297 (77%, mles 65%) non‐indigenous, with a mean age of 60 and 66 years, respectively. Median time from initial computed tomography (CT) scan to diagnosis (22 vs 23 days, P = 0.94) and initial CT to treatment were similar (61 vs 60 days, P = 0.61) in indigenous versus non‐indigenous patients. Diagnostic and treatment time frames were shorter in remote compared to urban patients (initial CT to diagnosis 18 vs 27 days, P = 0.002; initial CT to treatment 46 vs 65 days, P = 0.15). Indigenous patients had lower 1‐year median survival, and remote patients had lower 1‐ and 3‐year median survival. Higher rates of inpatient evaluation were observed in indigenous and remote residing patients (indigenous 54% vs 46%, P = 0.2; remote 58% vs 45%, P = 0.038).ConclusionLung cancer care pathways are similar in indigenous and non‐indigenous populations, even by remoteness. Lower survival rates among indigenous and remote patients highlight a need for more research.Keywords: Aboriginal, diagnosis, equity, health disparities, malignancy, time‐to‐treatment

Introduction

Lung cancer is reported to be the leading cause of cancer‐related death in Australia, including in the Northern Territory (NT). 1 Approximately one quarter of the NT population identify as indigenous, with the majority residing in remote/very remote areas. 2 A previous study in the NT population demonstrated that indigenous patients with lung cancer have poorer survival outcomes than non‐indigenous patients, with 1‐ and 5‐year survival rates of 25% and 9.4% for indigenous patients versus 42% and 16.2% for non‐indigenous patients respectively. 3 Poorer outcomes may in part be explained by the higher prevalence of risk factors for lung cancer alongside the presence of multiple morbidity among the indigenous population, more specifically those residing in remote and rural locations. 4 , 5 The 2018–2019 National Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Health Survey found that smoking rates were as high as 58% in those residing in very remote areas of Australia. 6 Furthermore, 46% of indigenous Australians have one or more chronic conditions such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), bronchiectasis and/or cardiovascular disease. 6 , 7 Smoking and COPD have consistently been shown to not only be risk factors for the development of lung cancer but also to contribute to poorer lung cancer survival. 8 , 9 , 10 Limited access to diagnostic testing and specialist services is reported as a contributor to poorer outcomes, particularly for indigenous patients and those living in remote locations. 3 , 11 Despite this conclusion, studies evaluating lung cancer diagnostic pathways and treatment commencement in these populations are lacking.

National Optimal Care Pathways (OCP) recommend a 6‐week timeline from specialist referral to treatment, with 2‐week intervals for both diagnosis and treatment initiation in lung cancer patients. 12 While meeting these benchmarks has been demonstrated in an Australian cohort to improve 1‐ and 5‐year survival in non‐small‐cell lung cancer (NSCLC), national compliance with these time frames remains low. 13 Delays may be attributed to the increasing complexity of diagnostic testing and prolonged intervals between initial computed tomography (CT) scan and tissue biopsies. 14 , 15 , 16

Despite previously reported survival disparities for indigenous Australians with lung cancer, the impact of diagnostic and treatment delays remains unclear. This retrospective study investigates healthcare timelines and access for indigenous and remote patients in the NT's Top End region over a 5‐year period to identify potential contributors to poor survival.

Methods

Setting

The NT is an Australian federal territory occupying the central‐northern region of Australia. The Top End/Top End Health Service (TEHS) region within the NT covers approximately 176 500 km2 of the total area of the NT and consists of an estimated population of 179 705 people, and approximately 26%–30% self‐identity as indigenous, the highest proportion in comparison to any other Australian state or territory. 17 Moreover, the vast majority, up to 80% of indigenous people in the NT reside in remote and rural communities. 17 Notably, the largest geographic centres in TEHS, NT (Darwin and Palmerston) represent outer regional centres or Australian Statistical Geography Standard (ASGS) category RA3, with remaining areas classified as remote/very remote with ASGS category RA4 and 5. 18 This study was conducted at the respiratory and sleep service based at the Royal Darwin Hospital (RDH), in the TEHS region of the NT of Australia.

Study participants

This study included indigenous and non‐indigenous adult patients between January 2015 and December 2020 undergoing evaluation for lung cancer. Patients were identified for the presence of lung cancer through the NT Cancer Registry database as well as hospital electronic medical records (EMRs) at the RDH with coded diagnoses of lung cancer via International Classification of Diseases (ICD‐10). Patients were excluded in whom lung cancer workup occurred outside of the TEHS, NT region, had an incomplete diagnostic evaluation, did not have a primary lung malignancy or were diagnosed outside of the specified study period. Clinical data assessed

For all the included study patients, baseline demographics, including age at diagnosis, smoking status, date of death and presence of COPD, were collected. Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance score when documented in the medical record was used to measure performance status. 19 ASGS was used to determine remoteness based on patient EMR postcodes with RA3 or less designated as urban/Darwin and RA4 or above as rural/remote. 18 The date of the diagnostic CT scan was used instead of the specialist referral date due to complexity in accurately identifying referral dates from the EMR or inability to account for verbal/inpatient referrals.

The date of diagnosis was defined as the date of a procedure that resulted in histological confirmation of primary lung malignancy. Tumour characteristics, including histological subtype and tumour, node, metastasis stage (8th edition) were recorded with early and late‐stage disease defined as less than stage 2B and Stage 3A disease or higher respectively. 20

Lung cancer management and survival data

Definitive treatment date was defined as the date in which the patient had either surgical resection, chemotherapy and/or radiotherapy or commenced on palliative care (hospice or community‐based care). In cases where multiple treatment modalities occurred, the earliest occurrence of treatment was used as date of definitive treatment. Treatment intent was deemed curative in those receiving surgical resection, stereotactic ablative body radiotherapy (SABR) or chemoradiotherapy with curative treatment intent. All‐cause mortality was determined using hospital EMRs through 31 December 2023, providing a minimum of 3 years of follow‐up for the entire cohort to facilitate 1‐ and 3‐year survival analyses.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was to assess for differences in the time from initial CT imaging to both histological confirmation and the initiation of treatment, compared between indigenous and non‐indigenous cohorts. Further analysis of timelines adjusted for remote versus urban/Darwin status was done as a secondary outcome. Secondary outcomes evaluated differences between indigenous and non‐indigenous patients in referral source (inpatient vs outpatient), disease stage at presentation (early vs late) and treatment intent. Statistical analysis utilised the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, chi‐squared test and Fisher's exact test where appropriate for comparison of baseline demographics and clinical characteristics. Time‐to‐event analysis (in this case, time to diagnosis and time to treatment, 1‐ and 3‐year survival analyses) was completed using Kaplan‐Meier curves, and univariate comparison between groups was conducted using the log‐rank test. For time to treatment, the log‐rank tests were censored by survival. No censoring was conducted for time to diagnosis as diagnosis of lung cancer was an inclusion criterion for the study. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.1, and alpha of 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance. 21

Ethics

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics governance/committee of the TEHS, NT and Menzies School of Health Research (Reference: HREC;2022–4258).

Results

Demographic and clinical data

A total of 534 patients had a diagnosis of lung cancer between 2015 and 2020, with 388 patients having complete data for inclusion in the final analysis, of which 91 (23%) self‐identified as indigenous and 297 (77%) were non‐indigenous.

Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics for indigenous and non‐indigenous patients are presented in Table 1. Median age at diagnosis was younger among indigenous patients at 60 years compared to 66 years for non‐indigenous patients (P‐value <0.001). Other notable significant differences between groups included higher rates of geographic remoteness in indigenous patients, with 68% residing in remote areas compared with 13% of non‐indigenous patients (P‐value <0.001). Furthermore, indigenous patients were more likely to be smokers (current smoker 71% vs 43%, P‐value <0.001) and to have COPD (49% vs 28%, P‐value <0.001). Poorer performance status was more prevalent in indigenous patients at diagnosis, with 24% classified as ECOG 2 and 12% classified as ECOG 3 compared to 11% and 9.7% respectively in non‐indigenous patients (overall P‐value = 0.012). Most lung cancer cases diagnosed were NSCLC in 338 patients, while SCLC was less common, occurring in 45 patients. Adenocarcinoma was the most common histological subtype in both groups, making up 56% of cases in non‐indigenous and 46% in indigenous patients.

Diagnostic time frames

From 2015 through to 2020, there was no significant difference between indigenous and non‐indigenous patients in the median time from initial CT scan to diagnosis or treatment. Median time between diagnostic CT to tissue diagnosis was 22 days in indigenous versus 23 days in non‐indigenous patients (Table 2). Furthermore, there was also no significant difference in the time between diagnostic CT to definitive treatment between indigenous and non‐indigenous patients at 61 days and 60 days respectively (Table 2). Adjusting for place of residence demonstrated significant differences in remote versus urban/Darwin patients with shorter time frames from diagnostic CT to diagnosis (18 vs 27 days, Table 2) and to treatment (46 vs 65 days, Table 2).

Disease stage, treatment and survival data

The rates of early‐ and late‐stage disease were comparable between indigenous and non‐indigenous patients, with late‐stage disease observed in 68% and 67% of cases respectively and early‐stage disease in 21% and 22% (overall P‐value = 0.9, Table 3). Treatment rates were comparable among patients, with rates of curative intent being 29% in indigenous versus 33% in the non‐indigenous patients (P‐value = 0.4, Table 3). Figure 3 shows Kaplan–Meier curves of 1‐ and 3‐year survival by indigenous status and remoteness. Indigenous patients had lower 1‐year survival rates than non‐indigenous patients (P = 0.029, Fig. 3A), with the survival between patients at 3 years not achieving statistical significance (P = 0.088, Fig. 3B). Remote residents had significantly lower survival at 1 year (P = 0.00025, Fig. 3C) and 3 years (P = 0.0034, Fig. 3D) compared to urban/Darwin residents.

Discussion

This study found no significant differences in the time to lung cancer diagnosis or treatment initiation between indigenous and non‐indigenous patients. Geographical remoteness was a key determinant of diagnostic evaluation and treatment; individuals residing in remote areas experienced shorter diagnostic intervals than those in urban patients, independent of indigenous status. Despite this finding, mortality disparities persisted, with indigenous patients exhibiting lower short‐term survival rates than their non‐indigenous counterparts. Furthermore, patients in geographically remote areas demonstrated poorer survival outcomes at both 1 and 3 years compared to urban residents. The comparable or even reduced lung cancer evaluation times observed in our study may be explained by the use of diagnostic methods specifically tailored to the NT patient population. Our study found that a higher number of indigenous and remote patients underwent evaluation for lung cancer while they were inpatients. A prior study of an Australian lung cancer population reported 25% of patients had a diagnosis of lung cancer made during hospitalisation. 22 In comparison, over 50% of the indigenous patients in this cohort underwent their lung cancer diagnostic work‐up while admitted to the hospital. Indigenous patients are more likely to experience geographic remoteness with less outpatient access to cancer diagnostics, specialist and multi‐disciplinary cancer care. 23 , 24 Inpatient evaluation likely provides an expedited diagnostic pathway, albeit at a higher cost to the healthcare system. While this conclusion is supported by a prior study in a large lymphoma cohort, robust evidence that this can be extrapolated to lung cancer cohorts is limited. 25

We suggest that higher rates of inpatient diagnoses in indigenous and remote patients are likely attributable to the transfer from remote communities, where limited access to specialist care necessitates hospital admission for the comprehensive management of suspected lung cancer. Additionally, some patients may undergo opportunistic lung cancer evaluation as an incidental finding while admitted for unrelated reasons. 26 Inpatient admission offers an effective model of care by ensuring timely, comprehensive evaluation and reducing loss to follow‐up, albeit at a higher healthcare cost.

The survival analysis showed significant differences in 1‐year mortality among indigenous patients and in 1‐ and 3‐year mortality among remote patients. Our study's observed survival aligns with previously reported disparities among indigenous patients. 3 However, although a survival gap persists, 1‐year mortality rates have improved compared to earlier studies. 1 , 3 , 6 , 11 An analysis of NT cancer registry data (1991–2020) reported better overall survival for indigenous patients and a reduced survival gap with non‐indigenous patients over a 30‐year period. 27 Our study supports this trend, which likely reflects, in part, advancements in lung cancer care in the TEHS region. Expansion of local capabilities, including positron emission tomography (PET) and, most recently, endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS), has enabled the evaluation and management of lung cancer to be performed locally in the NT. 27 Despite these improvements, our study identified the greatest survival gap among remote patients. This suggests pronounced disparities between remote and urban/Darwin residing Indigenous patients, given that most residents in the remote Top End are indigenous. Similar diagnosis‐to‐treatment intervals among indigenous patients, along with faster diagnoses for remote patients, suggest that other factors explain the poorer survival seen especially in remote groups. Our study population was younger than the national average, a finding consistent with the NT having the youngest median age in Australia. 28 , 29 Prior studies have demonstrated that older age at diagnosis is a significant poor prognostic factor in lung cancer survival. 30 , 31 The poorer survival outcomes in our study are unexpected given the younger median age of the cohort. This discrepancy may be attributed to poorer performance status and a higher burden of underlying comorbidities. For instance, over one‐third of the indigenous patients had an ECOG status greater than 2 with high rates of smoking and COPD. Additionally, higher rates of inpatient evaluation, particularly in remote patients, may also contribute to poorer survival. Previous studies have shown that lung cancer patients who are first evaluated as inpatients by medical oncology tend to be in poorer health and are less likely to receive systemic treatment. 32 , 33 Additionally, because indigenous patients often have a higher prevalence of comorbidities, overall mortality in this study may not be solely attributable to lung cancer.

This study has several limitations which need to be acknowledged. As this was a retrospective study utilising EMR records and registry data, we cannot account for all possible confounders and sources of bias. Moreover, given the inaccuracies and incomplete nature of specialist referral data, the date of the initial diagnostic CT scan served as a more reliable indicator for determining the commencement of the lung cancer work‐up. A limitation in the use of the date of the diagnostic CT scan is that it has most likely resulted in an overestimate of the diagnostic work‐up time frame as referral would be expected to occur after the diagnostic CT scan.

Limitations regarding the reported survival data must be acknowledged. Many patients were excluded due to incomplete work‐up or failure to complete the work‐up, and these exclusions were mostly indigenous/remote patients. Therefore, a more pronounced survival difference likely exists between indigenous and remote patients compared with their respective counterparts for this reason. Furthermore, assessment of lung cancer‐specific mortality was outside the scope of this analysis, presenting a clear limitation due to reliance on all‐cause mortality data rather than survival outcomes specific to lung cancer. The 1‐year mortality data showed significant differences among both indigenous and remote patients, most likely reflecting lung cancer‐related deaths. However, the observed significance in 3‐year survival rates – particularly among patients residing in remote areas – may be affected by mortality from comorbidities/unrelated causes, resulting in an underestimation of true lung cancer survival rates.

Despite being the largest study of a NT lung cancer population, the relatively small sample size may have limited the statistical power to detect significance in certain observed trends. Applicability of this study's disease classification and treatment intent reflected institutional practice at the time of data collection, though advances in lung cancer care may reduce relevance to current practice. Furthermore, the population studied is unique to NT, and thus conclusions drawn may not be generalisable to other health services within Australia.

Contrary to previous studies, and despite these limitations, we did not observe a significant difference in time to diagnosis or treatment commencement for indigenous patients, with remote patients actually demonstrating shorter time frames. 3 , 34 , 35 This work provides insight into lung cancer management in regional Australia and includes a substantial cohort of indigenous patients – a population underrepresented in the literature. These findings may serve as a benchmark for future research, particularly when evaluating the impact of lung cancer screening programmes.

Conclusion

Indigenous patients have lower lung cancer survival rates, and this study suggests these are not attributable to delays in diagnostic evaluation or treatment commencement. Future research should investigate other factors affecting care and allocate resources to improve outcomes. As national lung cancer screening expands, urgent measures are needed to close the survival gap, focusing on evaluating care quality and treatment completion.
