Background

The complex interplay between idiopathic sensory ganglionopathy and achalasia, two rare medical conditions, remains poorly understood. We present the case of a 44-year-old Thai male who presented with progressive sensory disturbances and dysphagia for 3 years without known autoimmune comorbidities and was ultimately diagnosed with both idiopathic sensory ganglionopathy and achalasia.

Case presentation

A 44-year-old Thai male with hypertension and atrial fibrillation managed with bisoprolol and edoxaban presented to the neuromuscular center of King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital owing to abnormal sensations in both legs that had persisted for 3 years. These sensations were particularly noticeable when he dried his body after a shower, which led him to switch to a softer towel. He also reported numbness and an abnormal temperature perception on his right arm, specifically from the elbow to the little and ring fingers. He experienced similar symptoms on his left hand, particularly in the thumb, index finger, and on the back of the hand. Despite these sensory issues, he had normal urination and bowel movements and did not experience significant dry eyes or mouth.

A year prior to hospital presentation, he had increased pain and difficulty using his right hand, to the extent that writing became impossible. In addition, he developed difficulty swallowing solid food. After swallowing, he would experience chest discomfort, often leading to induced vomiting. Notably, he had no accompanying symptoms of heartburn or regurgitation. Physical examination revealed reduced proprioception in both feet and hands, being more pronounced on the right side. No weakness was observed, and deep tendon reflexes were absent in all extremities. In addition, neither the Babinski sign nor ankle clonus were elicited.

Initial laboratory tests, including blood count, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, electrolyte, liver, and thyroid function tests, all yielded normal results. Serology tests yielded negative results for anti-double-stranded (ds)DNA, anti-Smith (anti-Sm), anti-nuclear ribonucleoprotein/Smith (anti-nRNP/Sm), anti-Sjögren’s-syndrome-related antigen A (anti-SS-A)/anti-Sjögren’s-syndrome-related antigen B (anti-SS-B), anti-Ro-52, anti-topoisomerase I (anti-Scl70), anti-histidyl-transfer ribonucleic acid synthetase (anti-Jo-1), anti-centromere protein B (anti-CENP B), anti-nucleosomes, anti-histones, anti-ribosomal P-protein, perinuclear antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (p-ANCA), and cytoplasmic antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (c-ANCA). The cerebrospinal fluid analysis showed normal glucose levels, mildly elevated protein levels, and no evidence of pleocytosis. A cytology examination revealed no inflammatory or malignant cells.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the whole spine showed a normal cord size and signal intensity, including the dorsal column. A sensory nerve conduction study revealed no response in the right ulnar nerves and reduced amplitudes in the bilateral sural and bilateral superficial radial nerves. A motor nerve conduction study of bilateral tibial, peroneal, median, and ulnar nerves showed normal findings, including the F response. The results of the nerve conduction study are presented in Additional File 1.

The patient was diagnosed with idiopathic sensory ganglionopathy and began treatment with a 3-day course of 500 mg pulse intravenous methylprednisolone, followed by daily 40 mg oral prednisolone. However, steroid-induced Cushing syndrome prompted a modification of therapy. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was administered at 2 g/kg (total 150 g) over 3 days and repeated at monthly intervals for 3 months.

While undergoing treatment for idiopathic sensory ganglionopathy, the patient underwent gastroenterological evaluation. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) revealed a normal esophagus and no masses at the cardia. A barium esophagogram demonstrated significant stasis of barium in the distal esophagus, with no contrast leakage or outpouchings. Esophageal manometry (Fig. 1) confirmed achalasia type II per the Chicago Classification (version 4.0). Although his painful paresthesia improved under immunotherapy, his dysphagia persisted. He subsequently underwent pneumatic dilation, resulting in complete relief of his swallowing difficulties. The timeline of events is provided in Additional File 2.

Discussion and conclusion

Sensory ganglionopathy is a rare condition, characterized by the degeneration of dorsal root ganglia (DRG) neurons. It can result from paraneoplastic syndromes, autoimmune diseases, or toxic exposures (for example, pyridoxine overdose or chemotherapy), or it can be classified as idiopathic [1–3]. Symptoms can vary significantly, but commonly involve various sensory disturbances. If sensory ganglionopathy is linked to a treatable condition, including autoimmune disease, treatment can sometimes halt or slow progression. However, in paraneoplastic cases—those linked to cancer—the course of sensory ganglionopathy often mirrors the course of the cancer, progressing despite treatment [1, 3].

Notably, the course of idiopathic sensory ganglionopathy, accounting for approximately half of all cases, can differ dramatically between patients. Some patients experience a slow progression, whereas others face a rapid onset or a relapsing–remitting course. Regardless, early diagnosis and treatment can improve the prognosis, although treatment responses vary [1, 3]. Supportive treatments, including physical therapy, are also beneficial for symptom management. Current research underscores a predominant immune-mediated mechanism in idiopathic sensory ganglionopathy, often involving T-cell infiltration and autoantibody production (for example, anti-fibroblast growth factor receptor 3 (anti-FGFR3) and anti-argonaute [anti-AGO]) [2, 4]. In addition, the frequent association between idiopathic sensory ganglionopathy and autoimmune diseases, such as Sjögren’s syndrome, further suggests a potential shared immune predisposition [1, 2, 4, 5]. In our patient, no evidence of systemic autoimmune disease was found, but the co-occurrence of achalasia raises the possibility of overlapping autoimmune pathways.

Achalasia, an esophageal motility disorder, is due to impaired lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxation. Symptoms typically involve dysphagia, heartburn, and chest pain [6, 7]. It arises from the loss or dysfunction of inhibitory neurons in the myenteric plexus of the distal esophagus [6]. Although the exact cause is unclear, autoimmune responses or viral infections are hypothesized to trigger this nerve damage. Achalasia is classified into three types, all characterized by absent peristalsis and impaired LES relaxation. Types I and II develop from autoimmune attacks leading to neuronal death. Myenteric plexus immune attacks involve antineuronal antibodies and T-cell infiltration. In type II achalasia, pan-esophageal pressurization and incomplete LES relaxation predominate; T-cell infiltration may contribute to neuronal destruction, consistent with an autoimmune etiology [6–8]. Type III achalasia is considered distinct from types I and II, featuring inflammation-induced neuronal dysfunction without outright destruction and resulting in inhibitory and excitatory signal imbalance. Treatments, ranging from botulinum toxin injection to myotomy, generally aim to alleviate obstruction and improve esophageal motility [7, 8]. Although an autoimmune connection is plausible, achalasia treatment rarely involves immunotherapy [9, 10].

Although a report of sensory neuropathy with epineurial inflammation and achalasia co-occurring in people with underlying Sjogren’s disease exists, their relationship in the absence of these autoimmune disorders remains unclear. In our case, the patient was diagnosed with both idiopathic sensory ganglionopathy and achalasia, without any additional autoimmune diseases [5]. This aligns with earlier studies showing T-cell infiltration in the pathophysiology of both diseases, providing stronger evidence of shared autoimmune-driven pathogenesis. Notably, our patient’s use of high-dose steroids led to exogenous Cushing syndrome, requiring the transition to IVIG for ongoing immunomodulatory therapy. Despite improved sensory symptoms, no improvement was noted in his swallowing dysfunction until pneumatic dilation was performed.

Another differential diagnosis to consider is Triple A syndrome (Allgrove syndrome). However, in this patient, presenting with sensory ganglionopathy and achalasia appears less likely owing to several factors. The age of symptom onset is atypical for Triple A syndrome, which typically presents in early childhood or adolescence. In addition, the absence of two key components of the syndrome—adrenal insufficiency and alacrima—further reduces the likelihood of this diagnosis. Moreover, genetic testing for mutations in the AAAS gene, which are responsible for Triple A syndrome, returned negative results in this patient. Furthermore, the patient’s neurological presentation and response to treatment did not align with the typical progressive neurological course seen in Triple A syndrome.

In conclusion, we present a patient with coexisting idiopathic sensory ganglionopathy and achalasia without additional autoimmune diseases, raising the possibility of shared immunologic pathways targeting both dorsal root ganglia and the myenteric plexus. However, the lack of response of achalasia to immunotherapy in this case underscores the complexity of treating such overlapping conditions. Mechanical interventions (for example, pneumatic dilation) may be essential once irreversible changes occur in the esophagus. Our findings emphasize the need for comprehensive evaluations and further research into the interplay between autoimmune neuropathies and esophageal dysmotility.
