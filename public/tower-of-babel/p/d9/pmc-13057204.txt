Multi-cohort analysis of metagenome for type 2 diabetes identified universal gut microbiota signatures across populations
Authors: Ying Dong, Minjie Wang, Xinyu Zhou, Pan Wang, Kaixin Yan, Siyuan Wang, Jiu-Chang Zhong, Hong Li, Lin Zhao, Bo Li, Jing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeveral studies have investigated the association between the gut microbiota and type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) in various populations. Nonetheless, noises specific to individual cohorts might distort the microbial dysbiosis characteristics and result in inconsistent findings across studies. Thus, we aimed to identify the universal features of perturbed gut microbiota across diverse populations.MethodsA total of 433 fecal shotgun metagenomic sequences were analyzed to profile and compare the gut microbiome shifts between patients with T2D and healthy controls from cohorts in Europe and Asia.ResultsBased on cross-cohort integrative analysis, patients with T2D showed significantly higher microbial alpha diversity, and distinctive microbial structures compared to healthy individuals. By excluding bacteria exhibiting divergent directional changes, consistent characteristics with ten T2D-enriched bacteria, such as Clostridium bolteae and Clostridium citroniae and eight T2D-depleted bacteria, including Streptococcus thermophiles and Haemophilus parainfluenzae were revealed across populations. Particularly, these reliable bacterial markers, which were robust against demographic variation, distinguished patients with T2D from healthy controls with high accuracy (AUCs > 0.8) in both European and Asian cohorts. Correlation analysis demonstrated that T2D-enriched and T2D-depleted bacteria, respectively, formed their own mutualistic networks that were negatively linked to each other. Moreover, T2D-enriched bacteria were dramatically positively associated with fasting blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin. Functionally, 10 KEGG pathways with consistent directional changes across European, Asian, and combined cohorts were identified. Specifically, the Nucleotide excision repair pathway was markedly downregulated in patients with T2D, while the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications was consistently enriched in patients with T2D across cohorts.ConclusionsOur results elucidated reproducible profiles of gut commensal bacteria in patients with T2D, which are robust across populations. Identifying the universal gut microbiome signatures of T2D in heterogeneous cohorts offers valuable insights for understanding disease development and is crucial for prevention and diagnosis across diverse populations.Subject terms: Type 2 diabetes, Type 2 diabetes

Introduction

Over the past decades, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) has become a major public health issue due to the increasing prevalence, high disability and mortality [1]. According to the global estimates, investigators indicated that T2D has affected ~382 million populations worldwide, with a speculation to reach 592 million by 2035 [2]. Both genetic and environmental risk factors have been recognized to be implicated in T2D, and the later includes dietary and a sedentary lifestyle relevant to the human gut microbiome [3].

Emerging studies have continuously revealed that gut microbiota involvement plays a pivotal role in T2D disease [4, 5], although findings diverge among different populations. For instance, Qin et al. demonstrated that several Clostridium species were overabundant in patients with T2D [6], whereas Karlsson FH and colleagues instead suggested the expansions of multiple Lactobacilli species [3]. Moreover, studies based on disparate ethnic groups in Chinese populations illustrated that forty-four T2D-related bacterial markers were the most closely related to diabetes in the Han subjects, of which there were Flavonifractor, Alistipes, Prevotella, Oscillibacter, Clostridium XlVa, and Lachnospiracea incertae_sedis, etc. While in the Mongolian subjects, twenty microbiome biomarkers, such as Fastidiosipila and Barnesiella were identified to be T2D-related [7].

Additionally, the microbial communities colonized in our intestinal tract are highly dynamic due to susceptibility to various factors, such as diet, host genetics and other factors, implying different populations might cause cohort-specific results [8]. By utilizing larger sample size, meta-analysis enables pinpoint subtle differences that are too small to be detected through single-cohort studies, while also taking into account the population-specific characteristics [9]. And fecal metagenomics provides a powerful approach to identify disease-associated species and understand interplay between the host and microbiota at a higher taxonomic resolution [10]. Previous research has demonstrated that the trans-ethnic gut microbiota and inflammatory signatures were linked to pre-diabetes in individuals from India and Denmark through the 16S rRNA gene sequencing [11]. However, the 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing method has inherent limitations, including potential skewing of microbial composition induced by amplification bias [12], inadequate resolution for species-level characterization of most microorganisms [13], and reliance on predictive tools for microbial gene quantification. To date, questions remain exist about the replicability of gut metagenomic features shared by patients with T2D across different ethnicities.

Thus, in the present study, we comprehensively profiled the intestinal microbiota using multiple fecal shotgun metagenomics datasets of patients with T2D and healthy controls acquired from European and Asian cohorts, to enhance the statistical power in exploring the association between gut microbiota and T2D, and form the basis for clinical prognostic tests and mechanistic studies.

Methods

Study population and metagenomic sequencing

Datasets of raw fecal shotgun metagenomic sequences by similar sequencing platform (Illumina platform), insert size and read lengths, but geographically diverse metagenomic studies (Europe and Asia), were retrieved from publicly available NCBI or EBI databases under the accession code ERP002469, SRA045646 and SRA050230, and ERP008729 [3, 6, 14]. Considering age matching, subjects <35 years old or >80 years old were excluded, and individuals with deficient lipid indices and impaired glucose tolerance were also excluded. Finally, a total of 433 individuals comprising 218 patients with T2D and 215 healthy control individuals were retained for final analysis in present study. Among these individuals, 156 subjects were from Europe, including 92 healthy controls and 64 patients with T2D; and 277 people were from Asia, including 123 healthy individuals and 154 patients with T2D.

Gene catalogue construction

To ensure consistency in the bioinformatic analyses, all raw sequencing data were reprocessed using the same method. Gene catalog construction was carried out as we described in the previous study [15]. Briefly, the sequencing reads were quality controlled and de novo assembled into contigs using SOAP2 v2.21 (default parameters except for–m200 –×400 –s119), and the remaining high-quality reads were utilized for further analysis. Gene prediction from the assembled contigs was performed using MetaGeneMark (version 2.10). Taxonomic annotation of predicted genes was conducted according to NR database using DIAMOND (Version 0.7.9.58, default parameters except that –k 50-sensitive –e 0.00001) with e-values ≤ 10× e−value of the top hit. The taxonomic level and abundance of annotated genes were determined using the lowest common ancestor-based algorithm and implemented by MEGAN analyzer (Tübingen, Germany). The abundance of taxonomy was defined as the sum of the abundances of matching genes [15].

Microbial diversity

To estimate the bacteria richness of patients with T2D and healthy controls, within-sample (α) diversity using indices viz. Shannon diversity, Chao1 richness and Pielou evenness based on the species profiles were calculated with R (version 3.6.3) vegan packagev2.6.4. Moreover, the differences in microbial communities were assessed by analyzing the β-diversity. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was conducted with both Bray–Curtis and Jaccard distance across samples to visualize β-diversity distinctions by vegan and ape packages (version 5.7.1) in R software. We calculated p values using permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) implemented by the adonis function, with the analysis performed on species-level microbial abundance profiles. Wilcoxon tests were conducted to evaluate significance of differences between the α diversity metrics of samples belonging to different health status or geographies. Additionally, considering that confounding factors such as age and gender might affect the results of gut microbial composition [16, 17], stratified analysis of α diversity parameters by age, gender and body mass index (BMI) was conducted, respectively.

Functional analysis

Functional annotation was carried out by DIAMOND (Version 2.0.11, default parameters except that -k 25 -sensitive -e 0.001) against the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) (Release 73.1, with animal and plant gene removed) database. Each protein was assigned to KEGG modules by the highest-scoring annotated hit (s) containing at least one high-scoring segment pair scoring >60 bits.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs) or median (first quartile, third quartile) and comparison was conducted using the Student’s t test or Wilcoxon rank-test. Categorical variables were presented as numbers (percentages) and compared using Chi-square test. The data analyses were performed using SAS version 9.3 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA). All p values were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To estimate the statistical power of single and combined cohorts, we calculated Mann Whitney Wilcoxon tests (simulation) with PASS (version 15.0.5). Moreover, the statistical powers were compared under the fold change of 10% and 20%. Combined analysis on bacteria showing accordant changes in the individual study cohort was performed. If candidate bacteria exhibited significant contradictory directions of abundance change in different cohorts or had low relative abundance (present in over 20% of the samples), we excluded them from the analysis. Consistent with analytical strategies reported in previous multi-cohort microbiome studies [8], statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 based on the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Furthermore, analysis of compositions of microbiomes (ANCOM-II) method was also applied to validate the differentially abundant bacteria, with the false discovery rate (FDR) controlled at 0.05.

Correlations among species were calculated with the Spearman’s correlation analysis. The cutoff for correlation coefficient was ≥0.2 or ≤ −0.2, and p values were <0.001. Subsequently, associations between the top eight differential species in abundance and clinical indicators were also estimated with Spearman’s correlation analysis. The visualization of correlation networks and heatmap were performed using the OmicStudio tools (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool) with igraph package (version 1.2.6) in R software.

The random forest classifier was trained on 75% of the data and tested on the remaining 25% data using the random forest package in Python (version 3.10.10) scikit-learn package. In test set, the random forest model was used to calculate the mean decrease of Gini index in differentially expressed bacteria and assess the variable importance. Furthermore, the predictive model was constructed using the differentially expressed bacteria, which were further applied for receiver operation curve (ROC) analysis. The performance of the differentially abundant bacteria model, clinical indicators (age, gender and BMI) model, and the combination of differentially abundant bacteria and clinical indicators was measured as area under the ROC curve (AUC). And higher AUC value indicates better diagnostic efficacy to distinguish the individuals with T2D from healthy controls. Meanwhile, the diagnostic efficacy of region-specific differential bacteria models for T2D was also calculated in test cohorts.

Results

Intestinal microbiota composition across cohorts

In this study, we analyzed the fecal shotgun metagenomic sequences of two cohorts from different regions (Europe and Asia), including 215 healthy controls and 218 patients with T2D, to investigate alterations in the gut microbiota of T2D. The demographic and clinical details of the individuals from Europe and Asia are shown in Supplementary Table S1. Among Asian cohort, patients with T2D were significantly older, had higher triglyceride, and higher low-density lipoprotein cholesterol compared to control participants. While in individuals from Europe, patients with T2D exhibited higher BMI, triglyceride, but lower total cholesterol and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol.

We first evaluated whether species richness and microbial diversity differed between T2D samples and controls by measuring the α diversity. The Shannon and Pielou indices were significantly higher in patients with T2D compared to healthy controls across European, Asian as well as the combined cohorts, with the exception of the Shannon index in the European cohort (Fig. 1A–F). Unlike the above indices, Chao1 richness exhibited no notable difference between patients with T2D and healthy controls (Supplementary Fig. S1). Moreover, it’s noted that with the dissimilarities in beta diversity assessed by PCoA and calculated using Bray–Curtis and Jaccard distance metric, we detected significant bacterial difference between patients with T2D and healthy controls in European cohort (p = 0.001 for Bray–Curtis distance, Fig. 1G; p = 0.001 for Jaccard distance, Fig. 1H), Asian cohort (p = 0.004 for Bray–Curtis distance, Fig. 1I; p = 0.003 for Jaccard distance, Fig. 1J) and combined cohort (p = 0.001 for Bray–Curtis distance, Fig. 1K; p = 0.001 for Jaccard distance, Fig. 1L), respectively. Thus, varied microbial diversity observed in patients with T2D may play a significant role in disrupting the delicate balance of the gut ecosystem.

Furthermore, as several confounding factors might potentially influence the gut microbiota, stratified analysis of age, gender and BMI was performed to control these factors and minimize biases in the combined cohorts. Our analysis revealed similar and stable variation patterns of alpha diversity indices across age subgroups, sex (male vs. female), and BMI-stratified groups (Supplementary Figs. S1B, S2A–F). For instance, Pielou evenness was consistently higher in patients with T2D than in healthy controls across both sexes (p = 0.05 for male and p = 0.03 for female, Supplementary Fig. S2C, D); the Shannon index showed a marginally significant difference between the two groups (p = 0.09 for male and p = 0.07 for female, Supplementary Fig. S2C, D), while Chao1 richness exhibited no significant differences (p = 0.67 for male and p = 0.89 for female, Supplementary Fig. S1B).

Notably, there were obvious differences of microbial biodiversity between European and Asian cohorts, indicating that the countries or regions of origin significantly affect the intestinal microbiota species composition (Supplementary Fig. S3). Across both healthy controls and patients with T2D, alpha diversity indexes (i.e., Shannon and Pielou) in the Asian cohort were dramatically lower than those in European cohort (Supplementary Fig. S3A–D), whereas the Chao1 index showed no significant intercohort difference (Supplementary Fig. S3E, F). In addition, beta diversity analysis based on Bray–Curtis and Jaccard distance metrics revealed a clear separation between individuals from the Asian and European cohorts, indicating substantial differences in their bacterial communities (Supplementary Fig. S3G–J). These evidences support the heterogeneity of intestinal flora across cohorts, and it was therefore imperative to explore the reproducibility microbial signatures in T2D.

Bacteria differing in abundance between patients with T2D and healthy controls

Taxonomic annotation and abundance profiles were assessed to evaluate the gut microbial composition among healthy controls and patients with T2D across cohorts (Supplementary Fig. S4). The global microbial taxonomy at phylum, genus and species level were relative similar between healthy controls and patients with T2D from European or Asian cohorts, respectively. However, the gut microbiota were differentially abundant between individuals of European and Asian, further illustrating the influence of national origins on gut microbiota profiles. Gut microbes including Bacillota (66.6% ± 16.8%), Bacteroidota (12.3% ± 11.3%), Actinomycetota (11.4% ± 13.6%) and Pseudomonadota (3.3% ± 7.9%) were identified as the top four phyla in European cohort. Whereas in Asian cohort, the most predominant phyla were Bacteroidota (54.0% ± 22.1%), Bacillota (32.7% ± 17.3%) and Pseudomonadota (7.1% ± 11.3%) and Actinomycetota (3.9% ± 8.8%), with absolutely disparate proportion from individuals from Europe (Supplementary Fig. S4A and Supplementary Table S2). While considering the genera and species, the top20 most abundant genera was identified in the combined cohort, such as Bacteroides, Eubacterium, and Ruminococcus, etc., and certain species including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Prevotella copri, and Ruminococcus bromii (Supplementary Fig. S4B, C).

As supported by previous study [18], when two statistical tests are applied to the same research question, the test with higher power is generally preferable, as it reduces the risk of false negative results. Subsequently, Mann–Whitney Wilcoxon tests (simulation) analysis was conducted to compare the statistical power between combined-cohort design (integrating European and Asian cohorts) and the single-cohort design (European cohort and Asian cohort alone). For a detected 20% change in bacterial abundance, the European single cohort had an estimated power of 0.682, the Asian single cohort 0.922, and the combined cohort reach 0.990. Notably, the statistical power of the combined cohort was higher than that of either the European or Asian single cohort, confirming the advantage of integrating multiple cohorts (Fig. 2A). This finding is consistent with prior research, where simulation-based power analysis also demonstrated that combined-cohort design yield higher statistical power for detecting microbial abundance changes compared to individual single-cohort design [8]. Therefore, given the strong ‘population’ effect on the taxonomic signature of the gut microbiome, bacteria species exhibiting differential abundance in patients with T2D and healthy controls across all the cohorts were investigated (Fig. 2B). After excluding the low abundant species (present in <20% of the samples) and bacteria showing ‘divergent directional changes’ among European and Asian cohorts, 45 species were retained for further analysis. We next discovered particular gut microbial species that exhibited consistent alterations in abundance in metagenomes of individuals with T2D. Using the rank sum method, eighteen differentially abundant species with ten enriched and eight depleted, were identified to have significantly disparate abundances in the gut microbiome of patients with T2D compared to healthy controls across cohorts (Fig. 2B).

Among the T2D-associated species, those with the lower enrichment in the patients were Anaerostipes hadrus, Streptococcus thermophiles, Lachnospiraceae bacterium_5_1_63FAA, Haemophilus parainfluenzae, Escherichia unclassified, Clostridium bartlettii, Peptostreptococcaceae noname_unclassified, and Ruminococcus lactaris (Fig. 2C). Notably, Clostridium bolteae, Clostridium asparagiforme, and Clostridium citroniae were observed to be and dramatically enriched in patients with T2D across European, Asian and the combined cohort synchronously. Additionally, microbial taxa with differential abundances in the combined cohort were also characterized using the ANCOM-II approach. Significant abundance variations were observed in 18 species between patients with T2D and healthy controls, among which 4 species were enriched and 14 species were depleted in T2D group (Supplementary Fig. S5A). For instance, Desulfovibrio piger was consistently enriched in patients with T2D across European, Asian, and combined cohorts (Supplementary Fig. S5B). Conversely, Bacteroides clarus, Haemophilus parainfluenzae and Granulicatella adiacens exhibited depletion in subjects with T2D from these cohorts (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Our results suggested that despite interregional discrepancies in dietary habits and genetic backgrounds of patients with T2D, a conserved pattern of gut microbiota variation can be identified.

Relationship between T2D-associated intestinal microbiota species

In order to gain a deep understanding of interactions between bacteria and bacteria from an ecological perspective, correlations between differentially abundant bacteria in patients with T2D was revealed based on Spearman’s correlation (Fig. 4A). We observed close and positive linkages among the T2D-enriched species, such as Clostridium bolteae, Clostridium citroniae and Clostridium hathewayi, etc. In addition, the T2D-enriched and T2D-depleted species were identified to respectively formed their own mutualistic networks, which were negatively related with each other.

Furthermore, by KEGG Orthology annotation, we identified differentially abundant functional pathways between healthy controls and patients with T2D. Our analyses revealed 10 KEGG pathways that exhibited consistent directional changes across European, Asian, and combined cohorts (Supplementary Fig. S7). For instance, the Nucleotide excision repair pathway was significantly downregulated in patients with T2D relative to healthy controls across all the three cohorts. In contrast, the AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications was consistently enriched in patients with T2D across the European, Asian, and combined cohorts.

Identification of gut bacterial species-based biomarkers of T2D through cross-cohorts

To distinguish patients with T2D from healthy controls on the basis of bacterial profiles, and identify potential microbial biomarkers for the diagnosis of T2D, the differential species we observed across cohorts were fitted into a random forest model utilizing a machine learning method. Firstly, we ranked the lists of microbial features crucial for T2D according to the feature importance for random forest model, as measured with the mean decrease Gini. Bilophila unclassified, Bacteroides ovatus, Clostridium bartlettii, Anaerostipes hadrus, and Haemophilus parainfluenzae were regarded as the top five most critical microbial biomarkers for predicting T2D in the training model (Fig. 6A). In the validation phase, the random forest model consisting 18 differentially abundant species in T2D as variables, achieved a high accuracy with AUC at 0.836 on the test samples (Fig. 6B). And the performance was slightly improved with AUC increased to 0.871 after the inclusion of clinical phenotype information (age, gender and BMI), which demonstrated strong ability to detect T2D. Considering the population diversity, to further assess the diagnostic potentials of the bacterial markers in single cohort, we considered the prediction performance in European and Asian separately. It was observed that the differential intestinal microbiota features gained accuracy at AUC of 0.815 and 0.832, using random forest with the test sets from Asia and Europe, respectively (Fig. 6C, D). Overall, our findings demonstrate that the established model has excellent specificity and can accurately identify T2D-specific microbiome signatures.

Discussion

Analysis of T2D cohorts that are heterogeneous for geography and ethnicity is quite necessary for studying the T2D-associated microbiome. In the current study, we performed a trans-ethnic metagenomic study of intestinal flora datasets from populations in different continents, and aimed at comprehensively assessing the universal microbiome signatures of T2D in Europe and Asia. Based on the analyses of Shannon and Pielou indices, as well as PCoA dissimilarity, patients with T2D were identified to exhibit significantly higher microbial alpha diversity, and specific microbial community from healthy individuals, especially in the combined cohort of European and Asian. We also pinpointed the crucial bacterial markers at species level, which were enriched or depleted in patients with T2D consistently across different cohorts, using model-free statistical method (Wilcoxon tests). Further analysis yield insights into the close association and strong interplay between bacterial markers and clinical indicators in patients with T2D. Additionally, a machine learning classifier based on random forest model was established with differential species-level bacteria, achieving good diagnostic performance in distinguishing subjects with T2D from healthy subjects.

To our knowledge, the richness and diversity of the gut microbes have been extensively assessed in patients suffering from diabetes mellitus, with varying results reported previously. Earlier findings indicated that, as compared to the healthy controls, microbial diversity and taxonomy richness were notably lower in subjects with type 1 diabetes [19]. Meanwhile, Pinna et al. demonstrated that no significant difference was observed in α-diversity between normoglycemic and prediabetes groups [11]. Regarding to intestinal microbiota diversity among patients with T2D, there was previous study having revealed a decreasing trends in the richness along with the progressive deterioration of glucose tolerance [20]. However, some other studies failed to replicate notable difference in α-diversity between patients with T2D and normoglycemic controls [4]. It is noteworthy that in present study, the intestinal microbiota diversity among patients with T2D was significantly higher than that observed in healthy controls within the combined cohort. One possible explanation for the discordance could be that the metagenomic sequencing datasets utilize in current study exerts advantages than 16S rRNA sequencing in previous studies [4, 11, 19, 20] in terms of sequencing depth, annotation level and taxonomic resolution, etc. And the increased diversity of gut bacteria observed in patients with T2D of the current cohort might reflect the imbalanced intestinal environment, characterized by overabundance of various harmful bacteria and a scarcity of commensal or beneficial bacteria [21]. Stratified analysis has been commonly used by investigators to mitigate the impact of confounding variables in evaluating differences at both design and analysis stages [22]. Thus, by taking into account potential confounding that might affect the gut microbiome, we conducted stratified analyses and discovered a stable alteration of α-diversity in T2D across different age, gender and BMI ranges.

Most importantly, the taxonomic composition at species level was focused, and we specifically explored those bacteria exhibiting consistent variation in T2D compared to controls across European and Asian cohorts, after excluding bacteria showing divergent directional changes among cohorts. Finally, we obtained 10 enriched and 8 depleted bacterial species in cases with T2D compared to the controls, as reproducible microbial signatures across populations. The diabetes-related species we observed have been previously reported to be linked with various diseases as risk factors. For instance, Bacteroides ovatus was associated with an increased risk of colorectal cancer diagnosis compared to patients with negative blood cultures [23].

Particularly, our results suggest that Clostridium citroniae and Clostridium bolteae are universally associated with T2D across the European and Asian cohorts. Previous study targeting new-onset T2D has elucidated Clostridium citroniae (Hazard ratio: 1.21, 95% CI: 1.04–1.41) and Clostridium bolteae (Hazard ratio: 1.20, 95% CI: 1.04–1.39) to significantly aggravate the risk of disease, which is in line with our findings in the present study [24]. In addition, Clostridium citroniae was recently reported to have prominent positive correlation with the production of trimethylamine N-oxide, a gut-derived metabolite that related to red meat intake [25]. Notably, the direct relationship between red meat consumption and T2D risk has been well-recognized for years [26]. Moreover, trimethylamine N-oxide was demonstrated to be linked to adipose tissue inflammation and the impairment of hepatic insulin signaling, both of which are closely associated with increased insulin resistance, high blood glucose levels, coupled with the occurrence of T2D [27].

And the abundance of Clostridium bolteae was observed to be enhanced in both progressive multiple sclerosis and relapsing remitting multiple sclerosis patients [28]. Remarkably, Clostridium bolteae was also illustrated to be more abundant among patients suffering from T2D, along with several opportunistic pathogens [6]. And a notable reduction in the relative abundance of Clostridium bolteae was observed in patients with T2D following treatment with antidiabetic drug acarbose [29]. Here in the current study, we further confirmed that the enrichment of both Clostridium citroniae and Clostridium bolteae was significantly positively correlated with fasting blood glucose and glycated hemoglobin, highlighting Clostridium citroniae and Clostridium bolteae may play a role in the pathogenesis of T2D, while in-depth mechanistic studies are necessary to explore the specific underlying mechanisms in future research.

Streptococcus thermophiles, one of the most common dairy starter strains, was known to be effective in deactivating etiologic risk factors of colon carcinogenesis [30]. By combining analysis of multiple cohorts, we identified Streptococcus thermophiles to be significantly depleted in patients with T2D across geographies, and robustly negative relationship with glycated hemoglobin was detected. These findings support previous epidemiological studies having shown that the consumption of fermented foods is associated with reduced risks of T2D, along with improved weight management [31]. Besides, Haemophilus parainfluenzae was previously found to be enriched in helicobacter pylori-negative gastritis [32], and Crohn’s disease [33]. In contrast, compared with pregnant women with pregestational T2D, Haemophilus parainfluenzae has been observed to be overrepresented in the healthy pregnancies [34]. Meanwhile, negative correlation between Haemophilus parainfluenzae and glycemic traits, such as oral glucose tolerance test (oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) 0-h glucose levels, and OGTT 2-h glucose levels, etc.) [34] was illuminated, which was in agreement with our findings.

Previous studies have elucidated the potential capacity of gut microbiome as biomarkers for distinguishing colorectal cancer [8], inflammatory bowel disease [35] across cohorts. Thus, by including these highly reproducible microbial biomarkers for T2D across cohorts, we developed machine learning models to distinguish patients from controls. Our diagnostic models achieved good performance to identify individuals with T2D from the healthy controls among cohorts, when validated on datasets excluded from the training of the model. And the combination of a microbiome model with other clinical factors substantially improved the diagnostic accuracy. Importantly, we found the set of bacterial biomarkers remained robust against demographic variations, as demonstrated by the performance of bacterial markers achieving AUCs of >0.8 in European and Asian cohorts.

Although we have taken into account potential confounding factors, including age, gender, and BMI in the stratified analysis, other potential confounders such as comorbidities, and duration of T2D were not available in the present study. Metformin was previously revealed to markedly affect the gut microflora [36], whereas, because of the unavailability of medication information from public mategenomic datasets, we failed to assess the effect of antidiabetic drugs on gut microbiome. Utilizing filtering pipeline to exclude bacteria exhibiting divergent abundance changes between groups, might possible result in the loss of certain intriguing species for T2D, however, this approach has been demonstrated to effectively minimize the false positive discovery rates [8]. Despite these limitations, our study has pooled and uniformly analyzed deep-sequence fecal shotgun T2D datasets of populations from distinct geography, and discovered bacterial markers that are consistently enriched and depleted in patients with T2D across various cohorts. The shotgun metagenomic sequencing datasets used in the current study enable species-level microbial resolution in understanding T2D-associated variants, and allow further analyses of bacterial interactions. Importantly, our findings point to a direction for further studies on validating the potentials of microbial diagnostic tests targeting T2D across populations.

Conclusions

For complicated metabolic diseases, identifying the universal biological signatures across populations is particularly crucial to advance the understanding of pathophysiological characteristics. Our study revealed the alterations of gut microbes in patients with T2D that are robust and highly reproducible in different ethnicities. Furthermore, we elucidated the valuable potential of intestinal microbial markers in diagnosing T2D across diverse populations with biological disparities.
