Noninvasive coronary imaging: Technological breakthroughs and the integrated application of coronary magnetic resonance angiography☆
Authors: Yiming Sang, Shuang Li, Hui Wang, Hongkai Zhang, Baiyan Zhuang, Lei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the growing demand for coronary artery visualization and the diagnosis of coronary artery disease, noninvasive coronary imaging modalities have gained significance. Coronary magnetic resonance angiography (CMRA) is a promising technique as it is noninvasive and does not involve ionizing radiation or iodinated contrast agents. Although certain limitations in this technology have prevented its application as a first-line clinical imaging modality, its immense developmental potential has positioned it as a focal point of research. This review discusses the advancements in CMRA across multiple technical domains—including motion correction, imaging acceleration, and integration with artificial intelligence—as well as its current clinical status and future prospects.Keywords: Coronary magnetic resonance angiography, 5.0T, Navigator, Compressed sensing, Fat suppression

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of global disease-related mortality, with coronary artery disease (CAD) being a significant component of cardiovascular diseases. In 2021, the global number of CAD cases reached 254.20 million, with 9.21 million deaths attributed to CAD. The average annual direct and indirect costs in the United States alone amounted to $252.2 billion [1]. Given these staggering statistics, there is a critical need for effective strategies to mitigate the impact of CAD. Early diagnosis emerges as a key solution, offering the potential to identify the disease in its early stages and implement timely interventions to improve patient outcomes, reduce complications, and ultimately, decrease mortality and healthcare expenditures. Currently, there are many imaging methods commonly used in clinical practice to diagnose coronary heart disease [2].Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) is a widely accepted first-line tool for evaluating CAD, offering a comprehensive assessment of coronary artery calcium, non-calcified plaque, and vascular remodeling. It also effectively evaluates bypass grafts and detects non-atherosclerotic conditions. But CCTA also has certain limitations: In addition to ionizing radiation and the use of iodinated contrast agents, blooming artifacts caused by calcification can exaggerate stenosis severity or result in false-positive diagnoses, and its relatively low temporal resolution may lead to motion artifacts (requiring lower heart rates, may necessitating β-blocker) [3].Invasive Coronary Angiography (ICA) is the gold standard imaging modality for the clinical assessment of CAD, offering unparalleled insights into distal coronary artery anatomy. However, the risks of invasive complications, ionizing radiation, and iodinated contrast agents limit its utility as a screening or monitoring tool for patients with suspected or stable CAD.

Coronary magnetic resonance angiography (CMRA) has gradually gained attention due to its advantages of being non-invasive, safe, and free from iodine contrast agents.

The clinical utility of CMRA has traditionally been constrained by image degradation resulting from cardiac and respiratory motion. However, with recent technological advancements, CMRA has made significant progress. Studies have shown that CMRA outperforms CCTA in delineating the lumen of heavily calcified coronary arteries without blooming artifacts [4]. Furthermore, CMRA utilizes patient-specific acquisition windows during the cardiac cycle to achieve optimal temporal resolution, enabling diagnostic coronary magnetic resonance images without the need for β-blockers [5,6]. The limitations of CMRA include low spatial resolution and prolonged imaging time, primarily due to interference from cardiac and respiratory motions, the complexity of coronary artery dimensions and anatomical features, and their proximity to fat and ventricular structures. Nonetheless, advancements in CMRA technology, such as accelerated image acquisition, motion correction, reconstruction frameworks, and integration with artificial intelligence, have aided in achieving higher image quality, faster acquisition times, and superior diagnostic accuracy (Fig. 2).

Methods

The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis statement (PRISMA) criteria were followed in reporting this systematic review. Since the information was available to the general public, institutional review board approval was unnecessary.

Search Strategy: A systematic search of databases (PubMed) was conducted from inception to August 2025. No filters were applied, such as language, publication country, or type of article, including abstracts and posters. The bibliographic references of the publications were manually examined to find any additional acceptable studies that might be included. An EndNote library was created by downloading the available results. The entire search strategy is displayed in the PRISMA diagram (Supplementary material 1).

Study Selection and Data Extraction: Two reviewers (SYM and ZBY) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all articles and selected potentially eligible studies. Articles considered eligible were then evaluated in full length with the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were solved through discussion, or by consulting a third reviewer (XL).

A standardized data collection form that followed the PRISMA for systematic reviews was used to obtain the following information from each study: title, name of authors, year of publication, country of origin, study characteristics, target outcome, aims, study and comparative groups, key findings and limitations. We selected articles relevant to all aspects of CMRA's technical advancements and clinical utility, excluding those with limited development potential, small sample sizes, or limited applicability.

The imaging basis of CMRA (field strength, sequences, and contrast enhancement)

In current clinical practice, the most commonly used magnetic field strengths for CMRA are 1.5 T and 3.0 T. Due to its inherent high blood–myocardium contrast, the steady-state free precession (SSFP) sequence is the preferred method for 1.5 T coronary imaging [5,7]. Nevertheless, increased B1 field inhomogeneity, frequency shifts caused by tissue susceptibility variations, and specific absorption rate (SAR) limitations are some of the challenges that hinder the acquisition of high-quality coronary images using SSFP at 3.0 T [8]. Compared with SSFP, gradient echo (GRE) sequences are less sensitive to magnetic field inhomogeneity and have been adopted for 3.0 T CMRA. 3.0 T GRE CMRA utilizes nonselective inversion pulses for background signal suppression, necessitating the use of T1-weighted contrast agents to augment the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of intracoronary blood and improve the blood–myocardium contrast [9]. Currently, gadolinium-enhanced methods are the preferred approach for boosting CMRA, offering greater safety and lower nephrotoxicity than conventional iodine-based contrast agents. Conventional contrast agents include extracellular contrast agents (e.g., gadobenate dimeglumine) and blood-pool agents (e.g., gadofosveset trisodium) [10,11]. Newly developed ferumoxytol-enhanced CMRA demonstrates good diagnostic performance and safety, with a long imaging window that benefits pediatric patients with small structures and fast heart rates, and is also friendly to patients with renal insufficiency or contraindications to gadolinium-based agents [12,13].Using GRE sequences in 3.0 T contrast-enhanced whole-heart CMRA substantially shortens the acquisition time while delivering higher SNR and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR). However, in clinical practice, CMRA is predominantly performed at 1.5 T, partly because noncontrast methods are relatively easier to implement at lower field strengths. Ultra-high magnetic field strengths (e.g., 5.0 T) hold promise for achieving higher SNR and spatial resolution without contrast(Fig. 3). Although higher field strength does not universally guarantee better image quality, Lu et al. [14] reported satisfactory results in a 5.0 T CMRA study. They proved that 5.0 T noncontrast CMRA could simultaneously assess coronary calcification and stenosis, showing higher specificity, superior image quality, and better diagnostic performance than 3.0 T nonenhanced CMRA for detecting significant stenoses (≥50 %). A key limitation, was the omission of T2 preparation pulses due to SAR limitations, which consequently lowered the contrast between the coronary arteries and the myocardium. Hence, the incomplete suppression of myocardial and venous blood signals compromised coronary visualization and image quality to some extent.

CMRA's technical breakthroughs

Motion correction

Cardiac and respiratory motions are the major obstacles in obtaining high-quality CMRA images and are closely linked to artifact generation. For reducing cardiac motion artifacts, determining the cardiac quiescent phase is critical. Prospective electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated triggering can identify the relative quiescent phase of the heart (typically mid-to-late diastole). However, its efficacy is considerably affected by arrhythmias and heart rate instabilities. An alternative approach involves continuous data acquisition, followed by the retrospective reconstruction of cardiac phases to select the most motion-free phase [15,16]. Moreover, an investigation proposed a clinical strategy combining diastolic and systolic phase imaging, which successfully enhanced the diagnostic accuracy of 3.0 T nonenhanced CMRA [17].

Respiratory motion suppression techniques extend beyond breath-holding and include respiratory gating and navigator technologies. The most common method to address respiratory motion during free breathing is diaphragmatic navigator (DNAV) acquisition. This method uses a fixed correlation factor (typically 0.6) to estimate and correct for craniocaudal cardiac motion, accepting data within a predefined acquisition window [18]. Applying an abdominal belt reduces the diaphragmatic displacement amplitude, improving the scan efficiency for DNAV-based whole-heart CMRA [19].However, the lag between diaphragmatic and cardiac motion [15], and irregular breathing patterns or diaphragmatic drift during data acquisition can severely compromise DNAV efficiency. A navigator efficiency of <20 % indicates unsuccessful data capture. The self-navigation (self-NAV) technology tracks cardiac motion directly instead of relying on the diaphragmatic position, eliminating indirect measurement and reducing the sensitivity to respiratory variability. This method does not require any external navigators or additional hardware, accepts free-breathing data, and achieves 100 % gating efficiency via retrospective data binning and image-based motion correction. This approach shortens the scan time, minimizes patient discomfort, and enhances CMRA robustness [15,20,21]. Nonetheless, its difficulty in separating moving tissues from static structures is a limitation [22]. Image-based navigation (INAV) acquires low-resolution 2D/3D images during each heartbeat to isolate moving and static tissues, facilitating multidirectional respiratory motion estimation. The field-of-view and orientation of INAV are aligned with those of high-resolution 3D coronary CMR, eliminating extra planning while achieving 100 % respiratory efficiency and increased scan speeds [23,24]. (Fig. 4) Fat navigator gating corrects the cardiac position on a per-heartbeat basis using full 3D images of pericardiac fat, offering effective motion suppression and acceptable SNR/CNR [18,25]. Focused navigation, an emerging advancement derived from self-NAV, estimates 3D nonrigid motion from 1D navigator signals [26]. Besides these methods, image registration algorithms can combine images from various respiratory phases by modeling the cardiac motion as translational, affine, or nonrigid transformations, augmenting the scan efficiency [27]. Hybrid approaches integrate self-NAV and INAV with affine/nonlinear corrections to account for all respiratory motions, permitting CMRA data acquisition across the entire respiratory cycle with shorter scan times [24].

Accelerated imaging techniques

For both CMRA and other magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) modalities, long scan times and low imaging speeds remain persistent challenges, making accelerated imaging techniques critically important. Conventional approaches, such as parallel imaging, allow faster acquisition but are limited to acceleration factors of 2–3. Higher factors introduce excessive noise, thus necessitating prolonged scan times for CMRA. The compressed sensing (CS) technology, based on reconstructing images from incompletely filled k-spaces, substantially decreases the scan duration and achieves improved acceleration. However, this method is associated with potential staircasing artifacts. On 3 T scanners, CS sequences with an undersampling factor of 4 are commonly accepted to balance image quality and acquisition time [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]].More efficient k-space sampling trajectories enable accelerated imaging, including non-Cartesian trajectories such as spiral, 3D radial, 3D variable-density spiral Cartesian, and golden-angle rotations [6,23,33]. Extra-dimensional golden-angle radial sparse parallel MRI (XD-GRASP) has been recently proposed, introducing a novel application of motion-compensated sparsity. Instead of discarding or correcting motion, XD-GRASP reconstructs an additional motion dimension: continuously acquired k-space data are grouped into multiple undersampled datasets representing distinct motion states. These are subsequently reconstructed into a full-fidelity multidimensional image series using motion signals extracted directly from the data. This strategy offers high scan efficiency while providing clinically relevant physiological information [27,34].

4D/5D CMRA

4D cardiac MRI techniques have been developed over the past decade to acquire time-resolved 3D volumes covering the entire cardiac cycle. These methods permit motion-resolved reconstruction without predefined ECG trigger delays. Furthermore, these 4D techniques have been successfully applied to CMRA, confirming the feasibility of free-breathing 4D CMRA [16,35]. A 5D motion-resolved imaging framework has been established by extending XD-GRASP principles to 4D whole-heart imaging. This framework employs multidimensional CS to reconstruct 5D whole-heart images that separate cardiac and respiratory motion dimensions, rather than discarding or correcting the motion. Therefore, myocardial function can be evaluated in any orientation, and high-resolution coronary anatomy can be visualized across multiple cardiac phases. Recent advancements include the development of free-running frameworks, eliminating the need for slice-specific planning, respiratory gating, cardiac triggering, or even ECG lead placement. When applied to 5D whole-heart imaging with self-gated cardiac and respiratory motion resolution, these frameworks achieve high isotropic spatial and temporal resolutions [27,36,37].(Fig. 5)

Fat suppression techniques

Most coronary arteries course through epicardial fat, and suppressing fat signals can enhance the contrast between coronary arteries and surrounding tissues, yielding clearer vascular images [31].Although several robust methods (e.g., SPIR, STIR, and PROSET) are available, effective fat signal suppression remains challenging, especially in regions with high magnetic field strengths and increased field inhomogeneity. Lipid insensitive binomial off-resonant excitation (LIBRE), a recently developed broadband fat suppression technique, exhibits improved tolerance to main field (B0) and radiofrequency field (B1) inhomogeneity and achieves enhanced fat suppression [[38], [39], [40]]. The Dixon water–fat separation technique is based on MRI chemical shift principles. This method exploits the precession frequency differences between water and fat and the B0 field distribution information to deliver exceptional water–fat separation and highly robust fat suppression [9,31]. Clinical applications of CMRA

Detection of CAD is a principal clinical application of CMRA. A study comparing high-resolution free-breathing CMRA with the gold standard ICA revealed that high-resolution noncontrast CMRA achieved high sensitivity and negative predictive value for diagnosing CAD within a predictable scan time of approximately 10 min [4]. Comparative studies evaluating the diagnostic accuracy of CMRA and CCTA for detecting coronary artery stenosis have demonstrated largely comparable performance. While some analyses suggest a marginal advantage for CCTA in specific arterial segments, overall evidence indicates that both modalities are effective in excluding significant disease and providing prognostic information related to future cardiovascular events [41,42]. A prospective comparative study by Sun et al. [43] evaluated the diagnostic performance of 3.0 T contrast-enhanced CMRA against dual-source computed tomography angiography (DS-CCTA) for detecting coronary artery stenosis of ≥50 %. The findings indicated that neither CE-CCMRA nor DS-CCTA demonstrated a statistically significant difference in diagnostic accuracy at the patient or vessel level. A 2023 meta-analysis summarizing 34 studies on the diagnostic accuracy of CMRA for CAD reported AUC values of 0.88 in patient-based analysis, 0.90 in vessel-based analysis, and 0.92 in segment-based analysis for 1.5 T CMRA. For 3.0 T CMRA, the AUC values were 0.94, 0.95, and 0.96, respectively. Within these patient-based analyses, contrast-enhanced 3.0 T CMRA demonstrated significantly higher specificity compared to non-enhanced 1.5 T CMRA [44].In addition, CMRA exhibited prognostic utility in patients with CAD. A study with a median follow-up of 25 months [45] showed that patients with considerable stenosis on CMRA had markedly higher rates of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) and all cardiac events than those without stenosis. This study emphasized the ability of CMRA to exclude CAD in suspected patients, reducing unnecessary ICA referrals. Another long-term follow-up study (median 5.6 years) [46]established that free-breathing whole-heart CMRA enabled noninvasive risk stratification for MACE and cardiac mortality. Patients without obstructive CAD on CMRA displayed a low risk of future cardiac events, highlighting its prognostic value.

Furthermore, CMRA allows for the identification of high-risk coronary plaques, both with and without the use of contrast agents, by exploiting the T1-shortening effect associated with plaque characteristics. The identification of high-risk plaques holds prognostic significance, independent of coronary luminal stenosis [47]. Multiple studies have shown that high signal intensity plaques on T1-weighted imaging are closely associated with vulnerable plaques [48,49]. Xie et al. [50] developed coronary atherosclerosis T1-weighted characterization with integrated anatomical reference (CATCH). CATCH can simultaneously acquire dark-blood plaque and bright-blood anatomical reference images to detect high-risk plaques. This approach enables the identification of high-risk plaques that correlate with features characteristic of high-risk plaques detected by optical coherence tomography (OCT). Subsequently, Shunya et al. [51] utilized the CATCH technique and found that, in patients with stable CAD, the primary component of high-signal-intensity plaques on T1-weighted MRI was intraplaque hemorrhage, rather than lipid. Other clinical applications of CMRA include Kawasaki disease (KD) and coronary artery anomalies. KD is the leading cause of acquired heart disease in children and is characterized by coronary artery aneurysms that may evolve over time. This condition is correlated with thrombosis, stenosis, and myocardial ischemia risks. Serial assessment of aneurysm size is vital for risk stratification and management. Nonetheless, the invasiveness of ICA and radiation exposure from CCTA limit their use in pediatric patients with KD, who often require long-term follow-up. Although ultrasonography is the standard method for evaluating coronary lesions in KD, its subjectivity and limited visualization of mid-to-distal coronary segments and small branches restrict its utility. Noncontrast CMRA offers a noninvasive, radiation-free alternative, facilitating the visualization of proximal, mid, and distal coronary branches. In addition, this method provides reliable imaging of coronary dilation or stenosis, and its diagnostic performance and clinical relevance have been validated [52,53]. Furthermore, CMRAdemonstrates superior clinical value in evaluating anomalous coronary artery disease and outperforms ICA in delineating abnormal vessel courses [54]. Although CMRA is not currently integrated into a one-stop cardiac MRI protocol, incorporating it into a comprehensive one-stop cardiac MRI protocol enables the addition of coronary anatomical information to multiparametric evaluations of cardiac structure and function.This approach can provide the requisite diagnostic, therapeutic decision-making, and planning data for CAD in a single session. A thorough CMR protocol includes cine imaging, myocardial perfusion imaging (MPI), and late gadolinium enhancement (LGE). Following contrast agent administration, a waiting period of approximately 10 min is needed before LGE imaging, which can be utilized for contrast-enhanced CMRA and is positioned between MPI and LGE. This workflow enhances the sensitivity and accuracy in the diagnosis of coronary artery disease by identifying patients with morphologically significant stenosis that lacks hemodynamic relevance, thereby reducing false-negative results [29,[55], [56], [57]].

Frontier explorations of CMRA combined with AI

In recent years, the integration of CMRA with artificial intelligence (AI) has led to significant technological breakthroughs. In their investigation, Qi et al. [58] proposed a rapid 3D nonrigid respiratory motion estimation method using unsupervised convolutional neural networks. The researchers achieved subpixel motion estimation accuracy and high image quality while achieving an approximate 20-fold decrease in the computational time. Yang et al. [59] proposed an unrolling-based technique to determine 3D nonrigid respiratory displacement fields from artifact-corrupted CMRA images. This approach permits accurate motion estimation even under high acceleration factors, improving the precision of CMRA image reconstruction. Recent advancements in deep learning (DL) have significantly enhanced the capabilities of CMRA. Tabo et al. [60] leveraged DL reconstruction (DLR) techniques to optimize compressed sensing (CS) for CMRA, resulting in substantially reduced imaging times while concurrently improving or maintaining image quality. This optimization led to enhanced signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR), with a notable improvement in vascular sharpness. Wu et al. [61] asserted that deep learning-based CS reconstruction accelerates 3D whole-heart nonenhanced mDIXON CMRA, attaining clinically acceptable scan durations and superior diagnostic performance compared with CCTA, the reference method. Further demonstrating DL's impact on efficiency, Wood et al. [6]introduced a deep learning-derived automated detection of optimal cardiac trigger delay and automated acquisition planning for motion-corrected CMRA based on the INAV technology. This method selects the optimal motion-free acquisition window, minimizes the time required for trigger delay calculation and window placement, and improves CMRA acquisition efficiency. Takafuji et al. [62]developed a deep convolutional neural network (DCNN) specifically designed for CMRA image analysis, enabling the automated detection of significant coronary artery stenosis.In another study, Lin et al. [63] developed an AI auto-segmentation system that effectively facilitates CMRA vascular reconstruction, considerably reducing the workflow time of radiologists. Collectively, these studies highlight the transformative potential of deep learning in automating analysis, improving efficiency, and enhancing the diagnostic utility of CMRA.

Future directions for advancements in CMRA

CMRA demonstrates significant potential in non-invasive coronary artery assessment. However, its clinical implementation remains unrealized, and several challenges require resolution. Firstly, low spatial resolution and motion artifacts are significant contributors to diminished CMRA image quality and represent primary obstacles to clinical adoption, severely impacting diagnostic accuracy for cardiovascular disease. Enhancing CMRA image quality critically relies on maximizing signal, minimizing interference, and precisely compensating for motion. Recent advancements in navigation and correction techniques have substantially mitigated artifacts caused by cardiac and respiratory motion. Concurrently, temporal resolution reconstruction frameworks have further optimized image clarity and contrast. Despite these improvements, current solutions remain insufficient. Future strategies may involve combining AI-driven motion prediction algorithms with integrated multiple compensation strategies, alongside leveraging ultra-high field strengths and optimized contrast agents to augment coronary artery signal.

Secondly, the relatively prolonged scanning time, while a concern, has seen encouraging progress in accelerated CMRA imaging, rendering the acquisition time generally acceptable. Integration with AI is expected to further reduce imaging durations. Unfortunately, as a technology still under development, a consensus on CMRA scanning protocols has not been reached. The emergence of various alternative techniques leaves the optimal choices for navigation, accelerated imaging, and fat suppression methods undetermined. Variations in CMRA protocols across different institutions and imaging vendors may also compromise the reproducibility and broader applicability of the results. Therefore, there is a present need not only for technological development and AI integration but also for more multi-center trials to validate CMRA accuracy and elucidate the differences between various techniques, with the ultimate goal of achieving standardized CMRA scanning.

Regarding AI integration, several potential challenges persist. These include data privacy concerns, algorithmic bias, and the necessity of validation across diverse populations. All such research must adhere to evolving guidelines and regulations for AI in medical devices being developed and updated by regulatory agencies. Furthermore, the application of CMRA in distinct patient populations, such as pediatric patients, the elderly, and individuals with contrast agent allergies, necessitates tailored adjustments to optimize patient-specific protocols, alongside considerations of cost-effectiveness.

Finally, the advancement of CMRA hinges upon interdisciplinary collaboration between engineers, clinicians, and computational scientists. Such collaboration is crucial for overcoming technical limitations and ensuring successful clinical implementation. The objective is to transition CMRA from a theoretical research technology to a clinically applicable diagnostic tool and a cornerstone of non-invasive cardiovascular imaging.

Conclusion

CMRA has achieved significant advancements in image acquisition, motion correction, and AI integration, leading to shorter scan times and improved image quality, thereby enhancing its clinical applicability. However, compared with ICA and CCTA, the clinical adoption of this technique is still limited. Ongoing technological developments are expected to broaden the clinical utilization of CMRA, leveraging its inherent advantages of being noninvasive and radiation-free and ultimately integrating it into one-stop cardiac MRI examinations.
