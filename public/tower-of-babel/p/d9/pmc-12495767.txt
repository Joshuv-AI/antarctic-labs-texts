A multidimensional analysis-based risk prediction model for stress urinary incontinence in middle-aged and elderly women
Authors: Zhen Wang, Yong Shi, Zongle Xu, Junhua Xi, Yanbin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUrinary incontinence (UI) is a common condition in middle-aged and elderly women, characterized by involuntary urine leakage. It significantly affects quality of life, causing embarrassment, depression, social isolation, and economic burdens. Although the prevalence of UI among adult Chinese women has declined to approximately 16.0% from 30.9% fifteen years ago, the rate of medical consultation remains low, around 10%.ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop a multidimensional predictive model for stress urinary incontinence (SUI) to assist in the elderly women identification of high-risk individuals.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective review of clinical data from female patients undergoing urodynamic evaluations at the [BLINDED FOR REVIEW] from September 2020 to March 2025. After excluding incomplete records, 202 participants were included and randomly allocated into a training set (n = 151) and a validation set (n = 51) at a ratio of 7:3. Clinical variables including demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, obstetric history, and relevant comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, urethral prolapse, etc.) were analyzed. Predictive variables were selected using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression in the training set. Subsequently, multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to construct the prediction model and nomogram. The nomogram provides an intuitive tool for individualized risk assessment, enabling early identification and targeted interventions. The discrimination ability was evaluated by the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC), calibration was assessed via the Hosmer–Lemeshow test and calibration curves, and clinical utility was measured through decision curve analysis (DCA).ResultsBaseline characteristics revealed significant differences between SUI and non-SUI groups regarding BMI (P = 0.003), smoking (P = 0.001), alcohol consumption (P = 0.001), urinary tract symptoms (P < 0.001), and diabetes mellitus (P = 0.007). LASSO regression identified six critical predictors from 20 candidate variables: BMI ≥ 28 kg/m² (OR = 15.18, P = 0.007), urethral prolapse (OR = 12.57, P = 0.009), parity ≥ 3 (OR = 21.92, P = 0.033), diabetes mellitus (OR = 16.37, P = 0.011), history of urinary tract diseases (OR = 9.01, P = 0.004), and heavy physical labor (OR = 6.90, P = 0.05). These variables were confirmed as independent predictors by multivariate logistic regression (all P ≤ 0.05). The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.94 (95% CI: 0.89–0.99) in the training set and 0.77 (95% CI: 0.63–0.92) in the validation set, indicating robust discrimination ability. Calibration curves showed high consistency between predicted and observed outcomes (Hosmer–Lemeshow test, P = 0.618). DCA demonstrated substantial net clinical benefits within the threshold probability range of 10–60%. The nomogram provided intuitive individualized risk assessment.ConclusionThis study successfully developed a multidimensional predictive model for SUI with favorable discrimination, calibration, and clinical applicability. The model serves as a valuable tool for early identification and targeted interventions in women at high risk of SUI. Further prospective studies and external validations are warranted to assess its applicability across diverse populations.Brief summaryA nomogram-based multidimensional model accurately predicts the risk of stress urinary incontinence, aiding early identification and targeted intervention in middle-aged and elderly women.

Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI) is prevalent among middle-aged and elderly populations, significantly impairing quality of life and mental health. Globally, more than 200 million individuals suffer from UI [1]. UI primarily includes stress urinary incontinence (SUI) and urgency urinary incontinence (UUI). SUI is most common among female UI patients and is characterized by involuntary urine leakage due to increased abdominal pressure from sneezing, coughing, laughing, or physical activity. Urodynamic studies show involuntary urine leakage with elevated abdominal pressure and absence of detrusor contractions during bladder filling [2]. In China, the prevalence of SUI among women is approximately 18.9%, peaking at 28.0% among those aged 50–59 years. Common risk factors include advanced age, vaginal delivery, multiple pregnancies, and obesity [3], potentially leading to pelvic floor dysfunction or detrusor hyperactivity. Early identification of high-risk individuals and targeted interventions are crucial for reducing UI incidence and its associated burdens. However, previous studies primarily focused on postpartum UI or epidemiological surveys in elderly populations, with limited predictive studies for individual patient risk of UI. Moreover, significant regional differences exist in SUI prevalence due to varying lifestyle habits.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the prevalence of SUI among middle-aged and elderly female outpatients at The Second People’s Hospital of Hefei and construct a nomogram-based prediction model. The study sought to enhance the accuracy and specificity of early SUI screening and provide valuable references for clinical diagnosis and prognostic risk assessment.

Data and methods

Participants and Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

This study retrospectively analyzed the data of female patients who underwent standardized urodynamics for lower urinary tract symptoms(LUTS) in the urology department from September 2020 to March 2025 under blind review. The diagnosis of stress urinary incontinence (SUI) or its absence was confirmed based on the urodynamic findings, adhering to the International Continence Society (ICS) definitions. A total of 202 women with complete data were enrolled after obtaining informed consent. Among these, 51 were urodynamically confirmed to have SUI, and 151 were urodynamically confirmed to have no evidence of SUI or other forms of urinary incontinence meeting ICS criteria.

All participants underwent urodynamic studies (UDS) based on clinical indications for evaluating lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), including urinary frequency (> 8 voids/day), urgency, voiding difficulty, or inconclusive non-invasive testing. The non-SUI control group (n = 151) consisted of women with LUTS but no urodynamic evidence of SUI or other incontinence subtypes per ICS criteria. Common diagnoses in this group included overactive bladder (34%), bladder outlet obstruction (22%), and sensory urgency (18%), all requiring UDS for differential diagnosis and treatment planning.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) Female patients aged 45–80 years; (2) Complete clinical data; (3) Mild neurological disorders were permitted; and(4) No history of tension-free vaginal tape obturator (TVT-O) surgery prior to admission.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) Incomplete data; (2) Previous history of any anti-incontinence surgery (e.g., tension-free vaginal tape obturator [TVT-O], colposuspension, autologous fascial slings, bladder neck injections, etc.). or pelvic floor reconstructive surgery (e.g.,anterior/posterior colporrhaphy); (3) Moderate to severe neurological diseases (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, hemiplegia, stroke); (4) Diagnosis of SUI and history of prior TVT-O surgery before admission; (5) Acute urinary tract infection, urinary tumors, or other urinary emergencies; (6) Pregnancy or breastfeeding; and (7) Neurological disorders (including multiple sclerosis and peripheral neuropathy).

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of [Blind for review]. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data Collection and Processing

The key diagnostic variable, stress urinary incontinence (SUI), was defined and determined solely based on the results of the standardized urodynamic evaluation conducted at our institution, which assessed for urine leakage coinciding with increased abdominal pressure in the absence of a detrusor contraction.

Variable descriptions and coding

Continuous variables such as BMI and age were categorized based on clinically established thresholds to enhance model interpretability and clinical utility. Specifically: BMI classifications followed Chinese standards [4]: 0 = normal(18.0-23.9), 1 = overweight(24.0-27.9), 2 = obese(≥ 28.0), as these cutoffs align with nationally recognized cardiometabolic risk categories. Age was dichotomized at 55 years (0: <55;1: ≥55) to reflect the established increase in SUI risk post-menopause [3]. Parity was binarized as < 3 or ≥ 3 deliveries based on obstetric studies indicating significantly increased pelvic floor injury risk beyond two vaginal births [5]. While continuous variables or spline functions may capture nonlinear effects, these categorizations were prioritized for three reasons: (1) clinical relevance of established thresholds, (2) simplicity for nomogram implementation in routine practice, and (3) mitigation of overfitting given our sample size(n = 202). Future studies with larger cohorts could explore continuous parameterizations.Age (AGE) was categorized as 0 (< 55 years) or 1 (≥ 55 years). BMI classifications followed Chinese standards: 0 = normal (18.0–23.9), 1 = overweight (24.0–27.9), 2 = obese (≥ 28.0) [4]. Heavy physical labor (JOB) was defined as a history of occupations or daily activities involving sustained heavy lifting (≥ 20 kg), prolonged standing (> 6 h/day), or high-intensity manual labor (e.g., farming, construction, factory work) for ≥ 5 years, coded as 1 (present) or 0 (absent). Physical exercise (PE) was defined as Structured moderate-to-vigorous exercise (e.g., brisk walking, swimming, cycling, pelvic floor training) performed ≥ 3 times/week for ≥ 30 min/session over the past year. Coded as 1 (present) or 0 (absent).Binary variables such as obstetric history (DG), smoking (Smoke), alcohol consumption (Alcohol), uterine surgery (US), physical exercise (PE), habitual consumption of tea or coffee (DL), heavy physical labor (JOB), menopause (MP), macrosomia delivery (HB), chronic constipation (CC), urethral prolapse (UP), urinary frequency (UF), diabetes (DM), hypertension (HBP), stress urinary incontinence (SUI), respiratory diseases (BD), and urinary system diseases (UD) were coded as 0 (absent) or 1 (present). Stress provocative test (SP) was recorded as 0 (negative) or 1 (positive), delivery mode (NDM) as 0 (vaginal) or 1 (cesarean), and number of deliveries (DT) as 0 (< 3) or 1 (≥ 3). The primary outcome variable was the presence or absence of SUI.

Initial data preprocessing involved removing records with incomplete data, resulting in a final dataset of 202 patients. The cleaned dataset was randomly divided into training (approximately 151 patients) and validation sets (approximately 51 patients) at a 7:3 ratio. Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using R software. Initially, Missing data (< 5% for all variables) were imputed using the MICE package in R, applying multiple imputation with chained equations under the MAR assumption. A priori sample size estimation was performed using the method for comparing the AUC of a predictive model against a null model (AUC = 0.5). Assuming an expected AUC of 0.75, a significance level of 0.05, power of 80%, and a case-control ratio of approximately 2:1, a minimum of 180 participants was required. The final sample size of 202 met this requirement.

Candidate predictor variables were screened using Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) binary logistic regression in the training dataset. LASSO introduces a penalty term to shrink certain regression coefficients toward zero, facilitating feature selection and mitigating multicollinearity, thus identifying the most predictive variables for the outcome [6, 7]. Five-fold cross-validation was employed to select optimal variables at the λ value minimizing mean squared error (Fig. 1A illustrates the LASSO regression coefficient paths, and Fig. 1B depicts model parameter selection). Subsequently, selected variables entered a multivariable logistic regression model using a forward stepwise method to determine final predictors and their regression coefficients. Based on the multivariate logistic regression model, a nomogram was developed to provide an intuitive tool for individualized SUI risk assessment (Fig. 2). The nomogram integrates six key predictors: BMI, urethral prolapse (UP), diabetes (DM), history of urinary tract diseases (UD), heavy physical labor (JOB), and number of deliveries ≥ 3 (DT). Each predictor is assigned a score on a point scale (0–100). The total points are converted to a predicted SUI probability (0–100%) on the bottom axis. Clinicians may use this tool by summing the points for a patient’s specific risk profile and mapping the total to the SUI risk probability [7].

Model Validation and Evaluation: Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted for the training set (Table 3) [8], and areas under the ROC curve (AUC) values were calculated to assess model discrimination capability (Fig. 3). AUC values range between 0.5 and 1.0, with values closer to 1.0 indicating superior discriminative ability. The model trained on the training set was subsequently applied to the validation dataset to evaluate generalizability. Calibration was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test [4, 6, 7], grouping validation samples into ten risk strata and comparing observed and predicted positive case numbers (P > 0.05 indicated good calibration). Calibration curves (Fig. 4) compared predicted risks and observed outcomes, visualizing model accuracy; ideally, the curve approaches the diagonal line. Decision curve analysis (DCA) evaluated clinical utility by calculating net benefit across various threshold probabilities [8], comparing intervention decisions using the model against strategies of “treating all” or “treating none.” If the model’s net benefit curve surpassed both extremes over a broad threshold range, it demonstrated clinical usefulness. Although this study provides a promising model, further external validation is required to confirm its applicability to different populations. In future studies, we plan to conduct multi-center, prospective cohort studies with larger sample sizes to evaluate the generalizability and clinical utility of the model across diverse settings and healthcare systems. Results

A total of 202 women were included, comprising 51 in the urinary incontinence group and 151 controls. No significant age differences were observed between groups (P > 0.05). According to BMI classification, the incontinence group showed higher proportions of overweight (24.0 ≤ BMI < 28.0; 65.3%) and obesity (BMI ≥ 28.0; 14.3%) compared to controls (combined overweight/obesity: 37.1%; P < 0.01). Nearly all participants had histories of vaginal delivery, limiting discriminative ability for this variable. Smoking (47%) and alcohol use (41%) were significantly more common in the urinary incontinence group compared to controls (21% and 15%, respectively; P < 0.01). Diabetes prevalence was significantly higher in the SUI group (12.2%) versus controls (5.7%) (P = 0.007). Hypertension rates were similarly higher but nonsignificant (20.4% vs. 11.4%, P = 0.27). Mild urethral prolapse occurred exclusively in the urinary incontinence group (6.1%). History of uterine surgery was more frequent in the incontinence group (14.3%) compared to controls (5.7%), yet differences remained nonsignificant (P = 0.21). Urinary frequency symptoms occurred in 26.5% of incontinence cases.

LASSO regression analysis included eight initial variables (age, BMI, obstetric history, urethral prolapse, diabetes, hypertension, urinary frequency, and uterine surgery). Optimal λ (approximately 0.04) was identified through five-fold cross-validation, simplifying the model while retaining significant predictive variables (Fig. 1A). The variable screening path (Fig. 1B) demonstrated shrinking coefficients as λ increased. Ultimately, six predictors (age, BMI, urethral prolapse, diabetes, history of urinary system diseases, and heavy physical labor) were selected for multivariable logistic regression; other variables were eliminated due to zero coefficients.

The final logistic regression model identified BMI, UP, and DM as significant predictors positively associated with SUI. Age showed a non-significant negative association. Overweight or obese individuals had a significantly elevated risk (OR = 3.75, 95% CI: 1.20–11.7, P = 0.023), as did those with UP (OR = 4.10, 95% CI: 1.05–16.2, P = 0.043). Although DM was associated with an increased risk (OR = 2.50, 95% CI: 0.77–8.13), the result was not statistically significant (P = 0.12). The final nomogram (Fig. 2) demonstrates high clinical practicality. For example, women with obesity (BMI ≥ 28), urethral prolapse, diabetes and a history of recurrent labor will accumulate more than 240 total scores, which will translate into more than 80% of the risk of SUI. On the contrary, low-risk features (such as normal BMI, no comorbidities) generate a total score of < 70 and a predicted risk of < 20%.

Discussion

This study constructed and validated a predictive model for stress urinary incontinence (SUI) among middle-aged and elderly women, integrating multidimensional clinical variables. Six predictors were identified by LASSO regression, of which five—including BMI ≥ 28, urethral prolapse, diabetes mellitus, history of urinary tract disease, and heavy physical labor—were confirmed as independent predictors by multivariate logistic regression. The model exhibited strong predictive accuracy (AUC of 0.94 in the training set, and 0.77 in the validation set), excellent calibration, and significant clinical utility, as demonstrated through decision curve analysis.

Interpretation of Risk Factors

The multivariate logistic regression analysis identified six independent predictors for stress urinary incontinence (SUI), with key variables demonstrating statistical significance: BMI ≥ 28 kg/m² (P = 0.007, OR = 15.18), urethral prolapse (P = 0.009, OR = 12.57), parity ≥ 3 (P = 0.033, OR = 21.92), diabetes mellitus (P = 0.011, OR = 16.37), and history of urinary tract diseases (P = 0.004, OR = 9.01). These factors were robustly associated with increased SUI risk, aligning with existing literature; for instance, parity ≥ 3 emerged as a major predictor, corroborating evidence on cumulative childbirth’s detrimental impact on pelvic floor integrity [5, 9]. In contrast, heavy physical labor showed only a borderline significant trend (P = 0.05, OR = 6.90), but its substantial effect size warranted inclusion in the model, suggesting a potential clinical relevance that merits further investigation. Variables such as age (P = 0.751) and alcohol consumption (P = 0.639) did not reach statistical significance, indicating their effects may be confounded by other factors in this cohort. This distinction underscores the importance of prioritizing statistically validated predictors for clinical decision-making, while acknowledging trends that could inform future research.

Our results highlighted that elevated BMI is a critical independent predictor for SUI. Obesity may increase intra-abdominal pressure, causing chronic stress on pelvic floor support structures and subsequently inducing urinary incontinence, consistent with previous findings [7, 8, 10]. Women with urethral prolapse also showed a markedly higher SUI risk, aligning with previous reports suggesting pelvic organ prolapse as a significant risk factor for urinary incontinence [11–13]. Although diabetes mellitus did not achieve statistical significance due to limited sample size, its elevated odds ratio implies clinical relevance, warranting increased vigilance among diabetic patients [14, 15]. Additionally, a history of urinary tract diseases significantly correlated with increased SUI risk [16, 17], reinforcing the need for regular pelvic health assessments in these patients.

Contrary to expectations, age was not a significant independent predictor after adjusting for other factors. While large epidemiological studies often demonstrate a positive association between advancing age and increased SUI risk [3, 4, 6–8], our study suggests age effects may diminish after controlling for confounding variables like obesity and pelvic floor disorders. Additionally, parity ≥ 3 emerged as a major predictor, corroborating existing literature regarding cumulative childbirth’s detrimental impact on pelvic floor integrity [5, 9]. Heavy physical labor, despite borderline statistical significance (P = 0.05), showed a strong effect size and was thus included in the model.

Model Comparison and Clinical Application

The developed predictive model incorporated six readily accessible clinical variables, demonstrating substantial clinical utility, particularly within threshold probabilities of 10–60%. The moderate validation AUC (0.77) indicated good generalizability, substantially improving upon single-variable predictors (AUC < 0.65). Calibration, assessed by the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, indicated excellent concordance between predicted and actual risks (P = 0.726). Decision curve analysis (DCA) confirmed clinical utility within common decision thresholds (10–60%), where the model provided superior net benefits compared to “treat all” or “treat none” strategies. For instance, implementing pelvic floor training interventions based on a 20% risk threshold would optimize clinical outcomes and resource allocation.

Model Strengths and Limitations

This predictive model integrates easily accessible clinical factors, making it suitable for clinical practice. The nomogram provides a fast and intuitive method for stratifying SUI risk in clinical environments. By converting complex regression coefficients into actionable scores, it facilitates shared decision-making on preventive interventions (such as pelvic floor training for high-risk populations) [18, 19]. Future implementations can integrate this tool into electronic health records for real-time risk calculation. However, the model has some limitations.

The relatively small sample size, particularly in the validation set (n = 51), may affect the reliability and stability of the model’s performance metrics. Although the training set (n = 151) enabled robust model development, the small validation set limits the model’s stability and generalizability. While the sample size was sufficient for the intended statistical analyses, further external validation is necessary to confirm the model’s applicability across different populations.

Although the current model mainly serves clinical patients who have already developed LUTS symptoms, it assists doctors in identifying high-risk SUI individuals and prioritizing intervention. All participants underwent UDS testing due to LUTS symptoms, which may make the model more suitable for symptomatic populations rather than general screening of asymptomatic high-risk individuals. In the future, the screening effectiveness of the model needs to be validated among asymptomatic populations in the community.

What’s more, self-reported definitions of heavy physical labor (JOB) and physical exercise (PE)may introduce recall bias.Future studies should use standardized questionnaires.

Multi-center, prospective studies with larger sample sizes are essential to assess the model’s generalizability and clinical utility. Additionally, data collection from a single center introduces potential selection bias, and missing information on certain variables could impact the model’s accuracy [19–21]. Moreover, the model only addresses SUI and does not consider other types of urinary incontinence, which may limit its broader applicability.

Conclusion

This study identified BMI ≥ 28, urethral prolapse, diabetes mellitus, history of urinary tract disease, parity, and heavy physical labor as significant independent predictors of SUI in middle-aged and elderly women.The model provides good calibration, moderate discrimination, and significant clinical utility within practical decision thresholds. Clinical professionals can utilize this nomogram for early identification and intervention planning, thereby potentially reducing the incidence and burden of SUI [22, 23]. Further large-scale studies are necessary to validate and enhance the generalizability of the model before broader clinical implementation.
