Severity and progression of visual impairment and risk of dementia
Authors: Allen T C Lee, Owen N W Leung, Zhaohua Huo, Jessie O T Kwok, Kelvin K L Chong, Wai C Chan, Ada W T Fung, Suk L Ma, Ruby S Y Lee, Marcus Richards, Sheung‐Tak Cheng, Linda C W Lam

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONVisual impairment is associated with dementia risk, but the role of severity and longitudinal change remains unclear. BACKGROUND

Visual impairment, which is prevalent in late life yet often underdiagnosed and undertreated, 1 , 2 , 3 is increasingly recognized as a potentially modifiable risk factor for dementia. 4 Although systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have supported an association between visual impairment and dementia risk, 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 several important gaps remain. First, most prior studies were cross‐sectional or retrospective in design and treated visual impairment as a binary exposure, limiting understanding of potential dose–response relationships and whether dementia risk differs between mild or stable visual impairment and severe or progressive visual loss. Clarifying the roles of severity and progression of visual impairment in relation to dementia risk is of great clinical and public health importance, particularly for informing targeted screening and intervention strategies. Second, visual impairment may compromise performance on vision‑dependent cognitive testing, increasing the risk of misclassifying visual deficits as dementia. Although vision‑adapted cognitive screening tools exist, 9 , 10 they do not fully account for confounding factors such as depression. These limitations underscore the importance of comprehensive clinical assessments to accurately distinguish dementia from visual impairment and minimize misclassification. Third, dementia has a long preclinical phase – often lasting a decade or more – during which neuropathological changes precede clinical symptoms. For instance, visual disturbances and retinal nerve fiber layer thinning may occur before cognitive decline becomes apparent. 11 , 12 Consequently, associations observed close to the time of dementia diagnosis may reflect reverse causation rather than a true antecedent relationship. To address this concern, the Lancet Commission on Dementia Prevention recommends excluding dementia cases occurring within the first 5 years of follow‐up in analyses of putative dementia risk factors. 4 Long‐term cohort studies incorporating this approach are therefore essential to more clearly delineate the temporal relationship between visual impairment and dementia.

By combining prospective longitudinal assessments of visual acuity with clinical ascertainment of dementia and exclusion of early incident dementia cases, this study aimed to clarify whether the severity and progression of visual impairment were independently associated with long‐term dementia risk while minimizing potential biases from reverse causation, confounding, and outcome misclassification. The findings may strengthen the evidence for visual impairment as a risk factor for dementia and highlight the need to incorporate longitudinal visual assessments into dementia risk stratification.

METHODS

Study design, setting, and participants

This study was based on our longitudinal, territory‐wide community cohort of Chinese adults aged 65 years or older in Hong Kong. 13 The cohort comprised 18,298 individuals who attended any of the 18 Elderly Health Centers (EHCs) – government‐run centers providing primary health care for older adults through health assessments and disease prevention – between January 1 and June 30, 2005. For the present study, individuals with clinically diagnosed dementia at baseline were excluded. To minimize residual confounding from suspected dementia, those living in care homes or with a diagnosis of Parkinson's disease or stroke were also excluded. Additionally, those with incomplete baseline data were excluded. After exclusions, 15,576 participants were included in this study (Figure 1).

Flow diagram of inclusion and follow‐up of participants in this 16‐year follow‐up study. EHCs, Elderly Health Centers.

Participants were followed for 16 years. Standardized clinical assessments of cognitive status, together with comprehensive evaluations of lifestyle behaviors, physical and psychiatric comorbidities, and demographic characteristics, were conducted at baseline and during regular follow‐ups at the EHCs. To minimize loss to follow‐up, participants who missed scheduled visits were actively traced and assessed by geriatric psychiatrists from the research team. For participants who could not be traced, their names were cross‐checked with the Death Registry to identify dementia recorded as cause of death. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or from their family members or legal guardians if they became mentally incapable of giving consent before the follow‐up assessment. Ethics approval was granted by the Ethics Committees of the Department of Health of the Government of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region (SAR) and the Chinese University of Hong Kong. This study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

Assessment of visual acuity

Habitual visual acuity was assessed using a standard 6‐m Snellen E chart. Participants were tested while wearing their usual optical corrections. Snellen acuity was converted to LogMAR units for analysis, with higher values indicating poorer visual acuity.

Ascertainment of dementia

The study outcome was incident dementia over 16 years of follow‐up. Dementia ascertainment was based on standardized clinical assessments conducted uniformly by trained physicians at baseline and follow‐up, including detailed history‐taking and non‐visual cognitive tests (delayed recall and the Abbreviated Mental Test 14 ) to minimize misclassification related to vision‐dependent testing. Participants who were traced and interviewed by geriatric psychiatrists underwent additional clinical evaluation and assessment using the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale. 15 Dementia was diagnosed according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) criteria or a CDR score of 1 to 3. 16 All dementia diagnoses were independently reviewed by a panel of geriatric psychiatrists, with final adjudication by the principal investigator in case of disagreement.

Assessment of covariates

Demographic characteristics (age, sex, and educational attainment), common ophthalmological conditions (cataract and glaucoma), physical and psychiatric comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, heart diseases, obesity, hearing impairment, poor mobility, and depression), and lifestyle factors (physical, intellectual, and social activities, fruit and vegetable intake, and smoking) were examined. Cataract and glaucoma were diagnosed by ophthalmologists. For the present analysis, cataract and glaucoma were examined as binary variables; data on laterality, disease subtype, severity, treatment, and onset were not collected. Other health problems were diagnosed by physicians using ICD‐10 criteria. Obesity was defined as a body mass index of 25 kg/m2 or greater, according to Asian standards. 17 Hearing impairment was defined as hearing loss of ≥40 dB at 1 to 2 kHz in the better ear during audiometric testing (Audioscope, Welch Allyn 23300). Poor mobility was defined as requiring walking aids or being chairbound. Lifestyle behaviors during the preceding month were assessed using a standardized self‐reported questionnaire and categorized according to a locally validated leisure activity classification system. 18 Adequate fruit and vegetable intake and regular participation in physical, intellectual, and social activities were defined as previously reported. 13 , 19 , 20

Statistical analyses

A sample size of 1000 would have provided ≥80% power (two‐sided α = 0.05) to detect an association between moderate to severe visual impairment and dementia, assuming an annual dementia incidence of 1% and a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.0 or greater. 21

Follow‐up time was calculated in person‐years from baseline until dementia diagnosis, death, loss to follow‐up, or end of follow‐up. Baseline characteristics were compared using independent‐sample t‐tests, one‐way ANOVA, or chi‐squared tests, as appropriate.

Cox proportional hazards models were conducted to examine the associations between baseline visual acuity and incident dementia. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for demographic characteristics, ophthalmological conditions, physical and psychiatric comorbidities, and lifestyle factors. Participants were censored at death, dropout, or end of follow‐up, and HRs with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated. Given the higher mortality risk associated with poor visual acuity, competing‐risk analyses were additionally performed using Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard models, with death treated as a competing event. Model 3 comprised Model 2 with additional exclusion of participants who developed dementia within the first 6 years of follow‐up or whose dementia‐free status by Year 6 could not be confirmed because of dropout, to minimize potential reverse causation in accordance with the Lancet Commission recommendations for dementia risk‐factor studies. 4 HRs were then estimated for incident dementia occurring during the subsequent 10 years (Years 7 to 16).

Dementia risk was further examined across baseline visual impairment severity, classified using ICD‐11 LogMAR thresholds as normal (<0.3), mild (0.3 to 0.47), moderate (0.48 to 0.99), and severe (≥1.00), applying the same adjusted models and the 6‐year exclusion window. Dementia‐free survival probabilities and corresponding standard errors (SEs) by baseline visual impairment severity were estimated using Kaplan‐Meier methods. The missing data mechanism was assessed using Little's test for missing completely at random. Differences in the timing of dementia onset were quantified using restricted mean survival time (RMST) up to 16 years, calculated as the area under the Kaplan‐Meier survival curve, with RMST differences relative to normal vision used to estimate average differences in dementia‐free years.

To assess whether the observed association remained consistent across age groups and diabetes status, we conducted sensitivity analyses using fully adjusted models stratified by baseline age (65 to 74 years vs ≥75 years) and diabetes status (with vs without diabetes). Continuous age was retained as a covariate in the age‐stratified models, whereas diabetes was omitted from the diabetes‐stratified models because it was constant within each stratum. Effect modification was further assessed by including interaction terms between age group or diabetes status and baseline visual acuity.

To evaluate whether visual acuity level at any given time and its subsequent rate of change were associated with dementia risk, joint models of longitudinal visual acuity and time to dementia onset were applied. These models combined a linear mixed‐effects model for LogMAR visual acuity trajectories (incorporating random intercepts and slopes) and a Cox proportional hazards model for incident dementia, linking the two processes through both the current LogMAR value and its rates of change, including their interaction. The models were adjusted for potential confounders, and only visual acuity measurements obtained prior to dementia onset were included in the analyses. Joint models were fitted using Bayesian estimation, with 10,000 iterations and 2000 burn‐in samples, implemented in R (version 4.4.2) with the JMbayes2 package (version 0.5–0).

Baseline visual acuity level and risk of dementia

Poorer baseline visual acuity level (expressed on the original LogMAR scale) was associated with a higher risk of incident dementia in unadjusted analyses (Model 1, Table 2: unadjusted HR = 3.81; 95% CI = 3.16 to 4.60; p < 0.001). Baseline visual impairment severity and risk of dementia

The proportion of participants who developed incident dementia increased with greater severity of visual impairment at baseline, being highest among those with severe visual impairment (28.6%), followed by moderate (26.5%) and mild visual impairment (22.6%), and lowest among those with normal vision (16.6%) (p < 0.001; Table 1).

In unadjusted analyses, mild, moderate, and severe visual impairments were each associated with higher dementia risk compared with normal vision, with unadjusted HRs of 1.54 (95% CI = 1.35 to 1.76), 2.16 (95% CI = 1.88 to 2.47), and 2.64 (95% CI = 2.14 to 3.26), respectively (all p < 0.001; Model 1, Table 2). However, after adjusting for demographic factors, ophthalmological conditions, physical and psychiatric comorbidities, and lifestyle behaviors and excluding dementia cases occurring within the first 6 years of follow‐up, only moderate to severe visual impairment remained associated with increased dementia risk (Model 3 in Table 2: moderate visual impairment: adjusted HR = 1.42, 95% CI = 1.16 to 1.74, p = 0.001; severe visual impairment: adjusted HR = 1.57, 95% CI = 1.12 to 2.21, p = 0.009).

Dementia‐free survival over 16 years declined progressively with increasing baseline visual impairment severity (Figure 2). Missing data were consistent with being missing completely at random (Little's test, p = 0.36). By Year 16, Kaplan‐Meier‐estimated dementia‐free survival probability decreased from 0.684 (SE = 0.020) among participants with normal vision to 0.594 (SE = 0.009), 0.472 (SE = 0.014), and 0.382 (SE = 0.051) among those with mild, moderate, and severe visual impairment, respectively.

Sensitivity analyses by age and diabetes status

In sensitivity analyses, the associations varied by baseline age group (eTable 2). Among participants aged 65 to 74 years, poorer visual acuity was associated with a higher risk of dementia (adjusted HR = 2.19, 95% CI = 1.62 to 2.95), as were moderate (adjusted HR = 1.42, 95% CI = 1.19 to 1.68) and severe visual impairment (adjusted HR 1.49, 95% CI = 1.07 to 2.08). Corresponding estimates were attenuated and no longer statistically significant among participants aged ≥75 years. Formal interaction tests indicated significant effect modification by age for both continuous LogMAR visual acuity (p for interaction < 0.001) and visual impairment severity (p for interaction = 0.008).

Among participants without diabetes, poorer visual acuity, moderate visual impairment, and severe visual impairment were associated with a higher risk of dementia, with adjusted HRs of 1.69 (95% CI = 1.35 to 2.11), 1.35 (95% CI = 1.16 to 1.57), and 1.41 (95% CI = 1.12 to 1.78), respectively. Although these associations were not statistically significant among participants with diabetes, formal interaction tests showed no evidence of effect modification by diabetes status for either LogMAR visual acuity (p for interaction = 0.432) or visual impairment severity (p for interaction = 0.826).

Joint modeling analyses (Figure 3) illustrated how dementia risk varied according to visual acuity level (x‐axis) and its subsequent rate of change (y‐axis).

Among participants with stable visual trajectories (slope = 0 LogMAR per year), poorer visual acuity level (expressed per 0.1‐unit increase in LogMAR) was associated with a higher risk of dementia (unadjusted HR = 1.17; 95% CI = 1.13 to 1.21; p < 0.001). The association remained significant after adjusting for confounders (adjusted HR = 1.13; 95% CI = 1.10 to 1.17; p < 0.001). Notably, HRs of 2.0 or greater were observed at visual acuity levels of LogMAR 0.55 or worse.

In contrast, among participants with worsening visual trajectories (slope > 0 LogMAR per year), the association with dementia risk varied by visual acuity level. Worsening trajectories were not significantly associated with a higher risk of dementia until visual acuity levels approached LogMAR 0.61 or worse, beyond which dementia risk increased more sharply in the upper range of the heatmap (Figure 3).

Associations for improving visual trajectories were also examined. Compared with a worsening visual trajectory (slope = 0.02 LogMAR per year; 97.5th percentile), which was associated with a 24% increase in dementia risk per 0.1‐unit increase in LogMAR, an improving visual trajectory (slope = −0.01 LogMAR per year; 2.5th percentile) was associated with a more modest 14% increase in dementia risk per 0.1‐unit increase in LogMAR.

DISCUSSION

In this 16‐year, territory‐wide cohort study of more than 15,000 community‐dwelling older adults, poorer visual acuity level was associated with a higher risk of incident dementia, independently of demographic, health, and lifestyle factors and after exclusion of early dementia cases. Notably, moderate to severe, but not mild, visual impairment was independently associated with increased dementia risk. Participants with moderate and severe visual impairment developed dementia approximately 1.5 and 2.1 years earlier, respectively, than those with normal vision. Moreover, among these individuals, their risk of dementia was further amplified in the presence of a rapid decline in visual acuity. Improvements in visual acuity were associated with a dementia risk that was not as high as that seen with continued visual deterioration, although the risk was not fully eliminated. Taken together, these findings strengthen the evidence supporting visual impairment as a dementia risk factor and highlight the importance of incorporating visual assessment with longitudinal monitoring into dementia risk stratification.

Comparison with previous studies

Compared with prior longitudinal studies, 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 our study benefits from extended follow‐up, rigorous clinical ascertainment of dementia, and comprehensive control of confounding factors, thereby reducing the likelihood of reverse directionality, misclassification error, and confounding effects. To our knowledge, this is among the largest and longest community‐based cohort studies to demonstrate an independent association between visual acuity and dementia risk. The long follow‐up period strengthens the temporal link between visual impairment and dementia onset. Although causality cannot be inferred from this observational design, the observation that visual impairment precedes dementia onset by a significant margin supports its role as a risk factor rather than solely a prodromal manifestation. Together with the graded associations across severity levels observed in the present study, where moderate to severe, but not mild, visual impairment is independently associated with higher dementia incidence, these results provide additional evidence for a possible dose‐dependent relationship between visual impairment and dementia risk.

Our results also highlight a more complex relationship between visual impairment and dementia. In contrast to our earlier 6‐year analysis, in which poorer baseline visual acuity, but not the rate of decline, was associated with increased dementia risk, 21 the extended follow‐up revealed associations with both acuity level and trajectory. This pattern is unlikely to be attributable to the large sample size alone, as similar associations with trajectories were not observed at 6 years. Instead, the emergence of these associations over extended follow‐up likely reflects a greater number of dementia events occurred and longer exposure to divergence in visual decline. Importantly, two clinically relevant thresholds were identified: Dementia risk approximately doubled at a visual acuity of LogMAR 0.55 even among individuals with stable vision, and the impact of visual decline on dementia risk became more pronounced at LogMAR levels of approximately 0.61 or worse. These findings suggest that moderate to severe visual impairment confers higher dementia risk, which may be further exacerbated by rapid visual decline. Our results highlight the importance of healthcare professionals recognizing and addressing increased dementia risk in older adults with impaired and worsening vision. Regular eye assessments may therefore be warranted, as monitoring both visual acuity and its longitudinal changes could help identify individuals at increased risk.

Contrary to some previous reports, 28 , 29 we did not observe independent associations between cataract or glaucoma and dementia risk after accounting for visual acuity. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Although ophthalmologist‐confirmed diagnoses were available, information was limited to the presence or absence of each condition, without details on laterality, subtype, severity, duration, or treatment. As these characteristics may differentially affect visual function and dementia risk, we were unable to assess potential heterogeneity across disease subgroups. Consequently, associations specific to particular forms or stages of cataract or glaucoma may have been attenuated and therefore not detected in the present analyses. Prior studies reported improvements in cognitive outcomes and structural brain changes following cataract surgery. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 In this context, our observation that improving visual acuity was associated with attenuated dementia risk is consistent with the hypothesis that addressing visual impairment may moderate, though not fully reverse, dementia risk. Long‐term intervention studies will be required to determine whether correcting visual impairment, even in late life, can delay or prevent the clinical manifestation of dementia.

Realizing any potential benefit from earlier identification and treatment of visual impairment will depend on access to and uptake of appropriate eye care, including regular eye examinations, timely spectacle correction, and low‐vision rehabilitation. Studies among older adults have identified low perceived need, limited understanding of preventive eye care, and practical and service‐related barriers to preventive service utilization, including routine eye examinations. 35 , 36 These findings suggest that screening alone may be insufficient without accessible services, effective communication, and support for follow‐up care. A comprehensive approach spanning early detection, access to eye care, and ongoing management of visual impairment may be required to maximize any potential benefits for brain health in later life.

Although the mechanisms underlying these associations remain to be elucidated, our findings provide several insights. The persistence of the association after adjustment for age suggests that visual impairment is not merely a surrogate for chronological aging. At the same time, the significant interaction by age indicates that the association was stronger among younger‐old adults (65 to 74 years) than among older‐old adults (≥75 years). The attenuated association in the latter group may reflect survivor effects, greater heterogeneity, reduced statistical power, or the increasing influence of competing age‐related disease burden in advanced age. From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the potential importance of timely identification and management of visual impairment in later life, particularly among younger‐old adults.

Although the associations were more evident among participants without diabetes, formal interaction testing provided no evidence that the association differed by diabetes status. This finding suggests that the observed relationship is not solely attributable to diabetes‐related vascular or metabolic disease burden and may be relevant across different metabolic risk profiles. Nevertheless, diabetes was classified only according to its presence or absence at baseline in the present analysis. Future studies incorporating information on diabetes onset, duration, complications, glycemic control, and treatment may help further clarify these relationships.

Several biological and behavioral mechanisms may underlie the association between visual impairment and dementia development. Diminished visual input and sensory deprivation may contribute to downstream neurodegeneration through reduced cortical stimulation. Consistent with this hypothesis, neuroimaging studies have demonstrated associations between visual impairment and structural and functional changes beyond the visual cortex, including the hippocampus and frontal networks. 37 , 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 Visual impairment may also influence dementia risk indirectly through increased falls, reduced mobility and physical activity, social isolation, loneliness, depression, and reduced participation in cognitively stimulating activities, many of which are established risk factors for dementia. Although some of these factors were adjusted for at baseline, such adjustment does not preclude the possibility that visual impairment contributes to dementia risk through subsequent changes in mobility, activity levels, social engagement, or psychological well‐being over time.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It followed a large, territory‐wide community cohort for over a decade, with a moderate but acceptable attrition rate given the age of participants and the extended follow‐up. The clinical diagnosis of dementia was established through comprehensive and detailed clinical assessments conducted independently of visual function, minimizing misclassification. Individuals who developed dementia within the first 6 years were excluded from the analysis, minimizing the possibility of reverse directionality. Additionally, this study accounted for a wide range of potential confounders, including physical health, sociodemographic factors, and health‐related behaviors.

Nevertheless, this study has some limitations. First, the observational design precludes causal inference. Although the extended temporal separation between visual impairment and dementia onset, the observed dose–response relationship, and the persistence of associations after exclusion of early dementia cases reduce the likelihood of reverse causation, residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Visual impairment may partly reflect accelerated biological aging, frailty, vascular burden, or shared neurodegenerative processes that increase the likelihood of both visual decline and dementia. Second, despite active tracing and registry linkage, some under‐ascertainment of dementia among participants lost to follow‐up may have occurred. Third, habitual, rather than best‐corrected, high‐contrast distance visual acuity was assessed. While this approach may better reflect real‐world risk exposure, it does not fully distinguish visual impairment attributable to uncorrected or undercorrected refractive error from that caused by ocular disease and captures only one dimension of visual function. Future studies incorporating a broader range of visual function measures, including near visual acuity, contrast sensitivity, and visual fields, may provide additional insights into the relationship between visual impairment and dementia risk. Fourth, data on earlier‐life cognition and detailed information on the onset, severity, and treatment of cataract and glaucoma were unavailable. Other age‐related eye diseases, including age‐related macular degeneration and diabetic retinopathy, were also not examined. These conditions may affect visual function through different pathophysiological mechanisms and may have distinct associations with cognitive outcomes. Fifth, genetic factors (e.g., ApoE4) and blood‐based or neuroimaging biomarkers were not available, precluding subtype‐specific analyses. Sixth, fewer than 10% of participants had normal vision at baseline, and fewer than 17% showed improvement in visual acuity, limiting precision at the extremes of visual change. Finally, generalizability to other populations should be approached with caution, as the cohort comprised exclusively older, community‐dwelling ethnic Chinese adults who had a relatively low burden of comorbidities.

Conclusions

In this large, long‐term cohort study, poorer visual acuity was independently associated with an increased risk of dementia. Moderate to severe visual impairment was linked to earlier dementia onset, with risk further amplified by rapid subsequent visual decline. The substantial temporal separation between visual impairment and dementia onset strengthens the case for visual impairment as a risk factor rather than solely a prodromal symptom. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating visual assessments into dementia risk stratification and highlight the need for future studies to determine whether timely identification and correction of visual impairment can contribute to dementia prevention.
