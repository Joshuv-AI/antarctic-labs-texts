Methodological innovations in perfusable vascular networks: Advancing high-density tissue models
Authors: Rinki Singh, Nobuhito Mori, Yasuyuki S Kida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of physiologically relevant in vitro tissue models has long been constrained by the absence of a perfusable vasculature. Native organs and tumors are sustained not only by their cellular components but also by vascular networks that deliver oxygen and nutrients, remove waste, and mediate immune and pharmacological interactions. To date, most engineered tissues have been generated at low densities (LDs; 106–107 cells/mL) at the cellular level, where diffusion alone sustains viability. These LD constructs are experimentally tractable but physiologically simplistic because they systematically overestimate drug penetration and fail to capture metabolic gradients. By contrast, high-density (HD; 108–109 cells/mL) tissues replicate the diffusion-limited transport, heterogeneous drug exposure, and hypoxia-driven phenotypes observed in vivo. However, HD constructs rapidly undergo necrosis in the absence of vascularization, which highlights the fact that perfusable vasculature is not an enhancement but an enabling factor. This review summarizes methodological innovations that address this challenge. First, we discuss endothelial cell-based strategies including self-assembly, angiogenic stimulation, and co-culture with stromal or pericyte cells, which recapitulate biological morphogenesis but often lack hierarchical fidelity. Then, we survey microfluidic platforms that provide patterned reproducible conduits under flow and enable systematic investigation of angiogenesis, barrier function, and drug transport. Bioprinting and sacrificial templating approaches are highlighted for their scalability and ability to create branching perfusable networks within millimeter-scale tissues. Synthetic microvascular scaffolds generated by two-photon polymerization and related methods offer notable precision at capillary resolution, although biocompatibility and scalability remain challenging. Moreover, hybrid and emerging approaches integrate organoids, bioprinting, and microfluidics into unified platforms to yield physiologically adaptable yet structurally controlled vascularized constructs. Finally, we consider the functional consequences of vascularization in HD tissues. Perfusable networks sustain metabolic activity, preserve tissue-specific phenotypes, and enable physiologically relevant drug penetration and metabolism. In oncology, vascularized HD tumor models reproduce drug gradients, angiogenesis, and vessel-guided invasion, which provide platforms for evaluating therapeutic resistance. In regenerative contexts, vascularization of liver or cardiac tissues preserves cytochrome P450 activity, electrophysiology, and contractility, which makes these systems suitable for toxicity prediction and pharmacokinetics. Furthermore, a perfusable vasculature transforms cell delivery into a dynamic process that enables immune, stromal, and progenitor cells to infiltrate tissues under flow. These advances enable the modeling of immune infiltration, immunotherapy response, and metastatic spread in manners not achievable with static LD models. Thus, immune-integrated vascularized systems constitute a frontier with future directions that include modeling physiological immune trafficking, probing endothelial contributions to therapy-associated toxicities, and developing standardized readouts of perfusion stability and vascular function. By combining HD tissue architecture with perfusable vasculature, these emerging systems narrow the gap between in vitro constructs and in vivo physiology. We propose that vascularized HD tissues constitute the next generation of translational models with the capacity to improve drug safety prediction, advance immunotherapy testing, and accelerate effective treatment development.Keywords: Perfusable vasculature, Cellular density, Endothelial cell, Drug safety, Tissue models

Introduction

From the earliest tumor spheroids to current organ-on-chip systems, in vitro models have steadily progressed towards one central goal, which is to capture the life, adaptation, and response of real tissues under the governance of a vasculature. In vivo, vessels do not exist merely as a passive plumbing system; instead, they choreograph oxygen and nutrient delivery, waste removal, paracrine signaling, immune cell trafficking, and the entry route for virtually every systemically administered therapy [1,2]. This vascular interface establishes spatial and temporal gradients that shape phenotype and therapeutic response, which are features that simple diffusion-sustained cultures cannot reproduce.

Historically, engineered constructs on the cellular level were maintained at low density (LD; approximately 106–107 cells/mL) and sub-millimeter thickness to ensure sufficient passive diffusion to preserve viability. These LD systems have proved experimentally tractable and invaluable for discovery; however, they inevitably flattened the very gradients that define tissue behavior, leading to overestimation of drug penetration, underrepresentation of hypoxia and metabolic zonation, and omission of barrier-limited transport [3]. By contrast, native organs and tumors exhibit a high density (HD; approximately 108–109 cells/mL) and millimeter-to-centimeter-scale thickness. At >200–500 μm distance from a vessel, diffusion alone fails, and necrosis ensues [4]. This mismatch triggered a field-defining realization that a perfusable vasculature is not an embellishment for HD models but an enabling architecture that reconciles realistic density and size with long-term function.

Therefore, the focus in the past decade has been less on “whether” to vascularize and more on how to do so with fidelity and control. One branch used endothelial self-organization and stromal co-culture to trigger angiogenic morphogenesis inside permissive matrices. This approach is biologically faithful and adaptive with the drawback of limited hierarchical control [5,6]. Alternatively, microfluidic organ-on-chip systems impose geometry and flow from the outset, which creates endothelial/epithelialized conduits with tunable shear for barrier, transport, and transmigration studies [4,7]. Through bioprinting and sacrificial templating, these ideas were extended to thick tissues by casting interconnected channels that could be endothelialized and perfused over weeks, even at the centimeter scale. The most recent hybrid platforms fuse organoids, microfluidics, and printing to combine biological adaptability with structural determinism to achieve vascular networks that both form and function under physiological constraints.

Achieving perfusion leads to several changes. First, drug delivery becomes vascular and not just diffusive, where compounds enter via lumens; encounter endothelial junctions, efflux pumps, and perivascular resistance; and establish spatially heterogeneous exposures that mirror in vivo conditions rather than those in vitro [8,9]. Second, distribution in HD tissues shifts from uniform diffusion to advection–diffusion regimes with steep gradients and perivascular “hot zones,” which expose failure modes and resistance niches that LD models miss [10]. Third, metabolism stabilizes under flow. That is, hepatic constructs retain albumin secretion and phase I/II activity; polarized transporters and zonation emerge; and clinically relevant hepatotoxicity and clearance become measurable over weeks [11]. Finally, perfusable beds enable dynamic cell delivery, rolling, adhesion, and extravasation. Thus, immune and progenitor cells may be introduced intraluminally and interrogated by shear, which enables quantitative studies of immune infiltration and immunotherapy response in dense vascularized tissues [12,13].

In this review (Fig. 1), we present methodological innovations that feature advanced vascular integration across multiple fronts, such as endothelial self-assembly, microfluidics, bioprinting/sacrificial templating, and synthetic microvascular scaffolds, along with emerging hybrid strategies. We highlight how the convergence of these approaches overcome the size-density barrier. Additionally, we examine their functional impact on drug delivery, distribution, and metabolism and discuss future directions such as immune–vascular coupling and multi-organ integration to support our primary hypothesis that vascularized HD tissues represent the next generation of translational models with potential to improve drug safety prediction, accelerate immunotherapy testing, and narrow the gap between preclinical experiments and clinical outcomes.

Size-cell density barrier in engineered tissues

The physiological relevance of engineered tissues depends on their ability to reproduce the size and density of native organs and tumors. In vivo, tissues are organized at millimeter to centimeter scales and packed at cellular densities in the order of 108–109 cells/mL [[14], [15], [16]]. These conditions drive high metabolic demand and tightly regulate microenvironments. Replicating these parameters in vitro is critical for capturing realistic nutrient exchange, drug penetration, and metabolic activity. However, these same features impose stringent biophysical barriers. Oxygen and nutrient diffusion rarely occur past 200–500 μm. This implies that spheroids and dense constructs with diameters or thicknesses larger than this threshold develop hypoxic or necrotic cores [17]. LD constructs (106–107 cells/mL) avoid these limitations but sacrifice physiological accuracy (Fig. 2a), whereas HD constructs (Fig. 2b) (108–109 cells/mL) reproduce the in vivo metabolic constraints but rapidly lose viability in the absence of vascularization [18,19].

Comparison of engineered tissue densities. (a) Low-density construct (106–107 cells/mL) is maintained by diffusion but shows low physiological representation. (b) High-density construct (108–109 cells/mL) reflects native tissue and density but requires vascularization to overcome diffusion limits.

Thus, the combination of millimeter-scale size and physiological cell density creates a dual constraint that cannot be resolved by diffusion alone. Therefore, perfusable vascular networks are not an optional structural feature but a design requirement for sustaining HD engineered tissues to faithfully reflect native physiology.

Methods for in vitro vascular integration in three-dimensional (3D) tissues

Building blocks of vascularization

The successful fabrication of perfusable vascular networks in engineered tissues relies on a coordinated interplay among cells, matrices, and molecular cues. These elements act as the foundational “ingredients” that enable the self-assembly, stabilization, and functional integration of vascular systems.

Endothelial cells (ECs) are the central building blocks of all vascular systems. Among them, human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs) are most widely used because of their availability and robust angiogenic potential. However, they lack tissue specificity. Therefore, iPSC-derived ECs and organ-specific ECs (e.g., brain microvascular ECs and liver sinusoidal ECs) are used instead because they better reproduce barrier and metabolic functions [20,21]. Mural support cells, including pericytes, fibroblasts, and mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), are indispensable for stabilizing networks, promoting lumen formation, and maintaining barrier function through paracrine signaling and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [22].

The ECM provides both structural and biochemical guidance for endothelial morphogenesis. Natural hydrogels such as fibrin and collagen facilitate sprouting angiogenesis by offering degradable wound-healing-like environments [5]. Semi-synthetic systems such as gelatin methacryloyl combine cell-adhesive motifs with photo-crosslinkability to support patterned architecture [23]. Synthetic polymers such as polyethylene glycol provide tunable stiffness, degradability, and functionalization, which provide precise control over vessel morphology and stability [24]. Furthermore, vascular morphogenesis is directed by biochemical and physical signals such as VEGF (which is the dominant angiogenic stimulus), Ang1 (which stabilizes nascent vessels), and S1P (which strengthens barrier integrity). In addition to molecular factors, shear stress generated by perfusion aligns ECs, reinforces junctions, and prolongs vessel functionality. Together, these three components (ECM, molecular factors, and shear stress) establish the foundation for vascularization strategies. Despite the importance of each individual component, the synergistic integration of ECs supported by mural cells embedded in responsive matrices and guided by angiogenic cues ultimately determines whether engineered tissues achieve stable and functional perfusion.

Extracellular matrix as determinant of perfusion and transport in HD tissues

Although endothelial cells and supporting stromal populations provide the cellular framework for vascularization, the ECM is an equally critical factor in perfusion, diffusion, and long-term tissue stability. In engineered HD tissues, the cause of transport limitations is not restricted to the cellular packing density but may additionally be attributed to structural and mechanical properties of the surrounding matrix. Thus, physiological relevance in HD constructs may be attributed to the coupled interactions among cell density, ECM architecture, and perfusion dynamics rather than to cellular concentration alone [25,26].

ECM composition and degradability

Commonly used matrices such as collagen, fibrin, and hyaluronic acid differ substantially in fibrillar organization, degradability, and biochemical signaling capacity. Collagen type I provides structural rigidity and defined fibrillar architecture, which supports endothelial sprouting and lumen formation; however, its density-dependent fiber packing restricts interstitial transport [27]. By contrast, fibrin is highly permissive to angiogenesis and is readily remodeled by matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), which enables dynamic vascular expansion and integration with perfused channels [28]. Hyaluronic acid-based matrices influence tissue hydration and interstitial pressure, which modulate convective transport and immune cell migration. Furthermore, semi-synthetic systems such as gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) and polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based hydrogels enable stiffness, remodeling, and ligand density, using which investigators decouple biochemical and mechanical cues from purely structural effects. Thus, these material-specific properties directly shape vascular morphogenesis, permeability, and long-term perfusion stability.

Matrix density, porosity, and diffusion limits

In HD tissues (108–109 cells/mL), oxygen and nutrient transport are diffusion-limited at distances >200–500 μm from a vessel [18]. Furthermore, the effective diffusion coefficient of engineered tissues is constrained by ECM density, pore size, and tortuosity. Increased collagen concentration or hydrogel crosslinking reduces pore diameter and increases matrix resistance, which compounds hypoxia in thick constructs even in the presence of perfusable channels. In contrast, although solute penetration is enhanced in highly porous or degradable matrices, their mechanical stability is compromised [29]. Thus, diffusion limitation in HD constructs reflects a combined effect of cellular consumption and matrix-imposed transport resistance. This interplay is particularly relevant in tumor models, where dense ECM deposition and matrix compaction further restrict nanoparticle and drug penetration and reinforce heterogeneous exposure gradients observed under perfusion [30].

Mechanical stiffness and vascular morphogenesis

Matrix stiffness influences diffusion and regulates endothelial phenotype, vessel stability, and barrier integrity. Excessively stiff matrices may impair lumen formation and promote aberrant vascular architectures, whereas overly compliant matrices may collapse under perfusion or fail to maintain long-term channel patency [31]. Furthermore, ECM mechanics are involved in initial vessel formation in addition to strongly influencing vascular remodeling, which affects endothelial alignment, junctional stability, and vessel maturation. In tumor-mimetic systems, increased matrix stiffness is associated with abnormal angiogenesis, elevated interstitial pressure, and impaired drug delivery. Furthermore, immune cell migration and infiltration are stiffness-sensitive processes, which suggests that ECM mechanics directly influence immune trafficking and immunotherapy modeling in vascularized high-density tissues [32].

Matrix remodeling and engineered ECM strategies

Engineered extracellular matrices provide strategic control over matrix remodeling. Consequently, this impacts perfusion and transport in vascularized tissues. MMP-sensitive hydrogels and degradable fibrin matrices that have been designed to permit controlled endothelial invasion, promote capillary interconnection with preformed channels, and restore perfusion in thick constructs [33]. Tunable GelMA and PEG-based systems enable stiffness modulation without altering biochemical composition, which enables the systematic investigation of the manner in which mechanical constraints regulate vessel diameter, permeability, and hypoxia gradients [29]. Moreover, engineered ECMs carefully tune degradability and mechanical stability to support vascular remodeling while maintaining channel patency and stable flow distribution. In tumor-on-chip platforms, these matrix design strategies have been used to modulate immune cell infiltration and recreate desmoplastic-like barriers that restrict therapeutic penetration, which indicates that engineered ECM remodeling actively shapes vascular–immune–tumor interactions [34].

Collectively, these findings highlight the fact that ECM properties are not passive background parameters but active regulators of perfusion efficiency, diffusion gradients, and vascular stability in HD tissues. Therefore, the physiological behavior of vascularized constructions depends on coordinated tuning of cell density, matrix architecture, and flow conditions. Thus, integrating matrix design with vascular engineering strategies is essential for achieving sustained perfusion, realistic drug penetration profiles, and faithful modeling of immune and metabolic phenomena in next-generation translational tissue platforms.

Endothelial self-assembly and organoid vascularization

ECs have been integral to vascularization strategies owing to their intrinsic ability to self-organize into microvascular networks in supportive ECMs. Early study of EC self-assembly showed that, HUVECs embedded in fibrin or collagen gels spontaneously formed branching capillary-like structures. Zheng et al. [4] established one of the first stable models by co-culturing HUVECs with fibroblasts in fibrin gels and showed that paracrine signaling from stromal cells was necessary to maintain network stability under perfusion conditions. Similarly, Kolesky et al. [35] have engineered thick vascularized tissues by embedding HUVECs within fibrin hydrogels containing patterned microchannels. Over time, the self-assembled capillary networks connected with the perfused conduits, which enabled nutrient transport across multi-millimeter constructs.

These EC-based strategies have since been extended to diverse regenerative and disease models. In regenerative contexts, vascularized cardiac patches incorporating ECs with cardiomyocytes and stromal cells improved graft survival and synchronized contractility [36]. Similarly, vascularized liver organoids generated by combining iPSC-derived hepatocytes with HUVECs and MSCs formed sinusoid-like networks that supported hepatic maturation and drug metabolism, as observed in a representative endothelial cell-based vascularized liver organoid model (Fig. 3a) [37,38]. In the nervous system (Fig. 3b), co-culture with ECs has enabled cerebral organoids to acquire perfusable microvessels, which has enhanced neuronal survival and barrier properties [20,39]. Comparable approaches have been applied to kidney organoids, where flow-supported EC networks improved nephron maturation and functionality [40]. Similarly, in pancreatic islet models, EC integration restored insulin secretion and enhanced islet viability [41]. Additionally, EC-based vascularization (Fig. 3c) has been extended to tumor models, where the incorporation of ECs into tumor spheroids or engineered tumor tissues generated vascular-like networks that sustained tumor viability and altered therapeutic response [42,43].

Taken together, these studies have shown that EC-based vascularization achieved through self-assembly or co-culture with support cells or angiogenic stimulation remains one of the most biologically faithful strategies for in vitro vascular integration. However, the generated networks often lack hierarchical organization and long-term stability, which highlights the need for further innovations that combine the self-organizing potential of ECs with engineering approaches that impose structural control.

Biological maturation and cellular completeness of engineered vasculature

Endothelial cells alone are often insufficient to achieve stable and physiologically relevant vascular networks. In native tissues, vascular maturation depends on interactions with supporting mural and stromal cells, which include pericytes, smooth muscle cells, and fibroblasts. They contribute to vessel stabilization, barrier regulation, and long term perfusion stability [44]. Endothelial heterogeneity is another important consideration because endothelial cells exhibit organ specific phenotypes and distinct arterial, venous, and microvascular identities that influence transport properties and responses to drugs or inflammatory signals [45]. Additionally, vascularized tissue models enable the investigation of immune cell trafficking and infiltration under flow conditions, although interpretation of these experimental results requires careful consideration of transport effects and flow dynamics [12]. Finally, most current engineered vascular systems lack lymphatic-like drainage and do not fully reproduce interstitial fluid handling, which is an important limitation and potential direction for future development.

Engineered perfusion platforms with defined channels

Microfluidic platforms have transformed vascular tissue engineering by enabling precise control over fluid flow, shear stress, and biochemical gradients to closely mimic physiological conditions. Early studies have established the fact that microfabricated channels lined with ECs reproduce aspects of microcirculation, which provides the foundation for perfusable vascular systems [46]. Currently, advances in soft lithography and hydrogel engineering have enabled the integration of these channels into 3D constructs to support sustained perfusion and organized endothelial morphogenesis. Unlike spontaneous endothelial self-assembly, which yields heterogeneous and unstable networks, microfluidic platforms allow patterned, reproducible, and scalable vascular integration, which is suitable for studying dynamic vascular–tissue interactions in vitro.

One of the earliest engineered vascular channels was reported by Chrobak et al. [5], who fabricated cylindrical conduits in collagen gels using microneedles and seeded them with HUVECs to create lumenized microvessels that remained perfusable for up to 2 weeks. Building on this foundation, organ-on-chip platforms have incorporated endothelialized channels to better mimic physiological blood flow and organ function. Particularly, liver-on-chip models have advanced notably owing to the critical role of the liver in drug metabolism. Mori et al. [47] (Fig. 4a) have reported a perfusable 3D liver tissue containing human hepatocytes and endothelialized channels that maintained long-term function and supported drug toxicity testing. To date, this is one of the most robust vascularized liver models created. Recent systems have integrated microfluidic vascular networks with primary or iPSC-derived hepatocytes to recapitulate zonation and metabolic gradients, which has further improved physiological relevance [49].

Additionally, cardiac tissues have benefited from vascular integration. Zhang et al. [50] have engineered perfusable vascularized cardiac microtissues by embedding ECs and cardiomyocytes in hydrogel scaffolds, which enabled synchronous beating and drug response testing under flow. Newly vascularized heart-on-chip (Fig. 4b) platforms have achieved functional microvascularization interfaced with human myocardium, which supports electromechanical coupling, tissue maturation, and disease modeling under physiologically relevant flow conditions [48,51].

Microfluidic blood–brain barrier (BBB) models have increasingly advanced beyond simple monolayers towards vascularized perfusable microvessel systems. Hajal et al. [52] have described a microfluidic human BBB model that self-assembles endothelial, astrocyte, and pericyte components into lumenized perfusable structures, which enabled that quantitative assessment of permeability and molecular transport. Recent systems show substantial advancements. For example, iPSC-derived BBB chips currently support true microvascular architecture and receptor-mediated transcytosis [53] and T-cell extravasation across perfusable vessels [54]. These advances have directed the field toward increasingly physiologically accurate BBB models with vasculature under flow.

The same principles have been used to create vascularized tumor models, where perfusable microvessels provide physiologically relevant flow, nutrient delivery, and routes for immune or tumor cell trafficking. Lee et al. [13] have reported multilayered blood vessel/tumor tissue chips that integrate endothelialized channels and tumor compartments to study T-cell extravasation and influence of tumor-secreted VEGF. These chips function as platforms for preclinical immunotherapy testing. Similarly, Geyer et al. [55] have reported a pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) model, which combines pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells with perfusable vasculature to capture tumor–vessel crosstalk and drug response dynamics in a physiologically relevant setting. With recent advances, these systems have been extended toward patient-derived and immune-competent tumor chips, where vascularized networks both support cytotoxic cell infiltration and enable immunotherapy evaluation under flow [56,57].

Collectively, these studies show that microfluidic vascular platforms are powerful tools for integrating perfusable vasculature into engineered tissues. They have been widely applied to tumor biology, regenerative medicine, and organ-specific models and have enabled systematic investigations of angiogenesis, barrier function, and drug transport. Nevertheless, most microfabricated channels remain larger than physiological capillaries; hence, long-term stability of endothelialized conduits remains a challenge and highlights the need for hybrid approaches that merge microfluidic patterning with biologically driven vascularization.

Bioprinting and sacrificial templating

Currently, bioprinting is a powerful approach for constructing perfusable vascular networks as it enables direct deposition of cells and biomaterials in predefined architectures. Sacrificial templating is the most pivotal of these strategies because it is conducive for fabricating hollow conduits that may later be endothelialized and perfused. Unlike microfluidic platforms that rely on channel molding, bioprinting provides greater flexibility in spatial patterning, scalability, and integration with heterogeneous tissue components [58]. A seminal contribution was made in this area by Miller et al. [6]. They introduced the use of carbohydrate glass as a sacrificial ink for fabricating vascular channels within engineered tissues. After printing filamentous networks of carbohydrate glass and embedding them in cell-laden hydrogels, the ink was dissolved, which left behind perfusable hollow conduits that were subsequently lined with ECs. Refinements in sacrificial inks have increased the range of vascularization strategies. For example, Kolesky et al. [59] (Fig. 5a) used fugitive (Pluronic) inks to co-print multicomponent tissues and maintain >1 cm-thick vascularized constructs under long-term perfusion. In organ-scale and high-cell-density tissues, SWIFT (Skylar-Scott et al., 2019) was used to embed vascular channels within dense matrices (Fig. 5b) [60]. Recently, Maggiotto et al. [61] (Fig. 5c) have applied sacrificial bioprinting to generate a vascularized neuroblastoma model. They embedded endothelialized perfusion channels adjacent to tumor aggregates and achieved stable perfusion, which showed vascular invasion into the tumor microenvironment. This model enabled functional studies of angiogenesis and drug penetration in a disease-relevant context.

Taken together, bioprinting and sacrificial templating is a versatile and scalable toolkit for engineering perfusable vasculature. From carbohydrate glass pioneers to modern multi-material approaches, these methods have shown the ability to create branching interconnected vascular systems that sustain HD tissues and enable disease modeling. Future directions include coupling bioprinting precision with endothelial self-organization to generate hierarchical vascular networks that recapitulate in vivo microcirculation with increased fidelity.

Synthetic microvascular scaffolds and advanced fabrication

High-resolution synthetic fabrication methods, particularly two-photon polymerization (2 PP) and multiphoton laser writing, have provided new opportunities to engineer perfusable vascular scaffolds with capillary-scale precision. Unlike sacrificial templating or self-assembly, which rely on degradable inks or biological remodeling, these photonic approaches enable deterministic patterning of channels with micrometer features and reproducible architectures.

A landmark proof-of-concept was reported by Hsieh et al. [62]. They used two-photon laser scanning photolithography to generate 3D polymeric scaffolds with interconnected microchannels that supported hepatocyte culture in lumenized environments. Based on this, Limongi et al. [63] have fabricated vessel-like scaffolds via 2 PP using a biocompatible photoresist, which were successfully endothelialized to form lumenized microvascular channels. In fact, 2 PP yielded precise structures in addition to biological microvasculature. A major limitation of early 2 PP scaffolds was their reliance on rigid photopolymerized resins. To address this, Buchroithner et al. [66] have developed new photopolymers optimized for multiphoton lithography that achieved submicron resolution while maintaining compatibility with ECs. Similarly, Cantoni et al. [64] (Fig. 6a) have reported a hybrid approach where multiphoton-written channels were combined with hydrogel molding to produce a multi-hydrogel perfusable vasculature, which enabled the integration of synthetic scaffolds into softer tissue-like environments. These innovations have advanced the field towards structural fidelity without sacrificing biocompatibility, which is an essential step for translation into thick tissues. An alternative trajectory has been the use of multiphoton laser ablation directly within cell-laden matrices. Rayner et al. [65] (Fig. 6b) have applied this concept by ablating vascular beds in collagen gels and guiding EC migration into the channels, which created endothelialized microvascular networks capable of sustaining perfusion. These studies show that multiphoton techniques could be used to pattern vasculature in situ within soft biomaterials and thereby advance towards physiological tissue contexts.

Collectively, these studies highlight the unique advantages of synthetic microvascular scaffolds such as excellent spatial resolution, reproducibility, and ability to design hierarchical networks that approach the scale of arterioles and capillaries. However, the translation of these techniques to large tissue volumes remains constrained by fabrication throughput and photopolymer biocompatibility. Future prospects likely rely on hybrid strategies that merge the architectural precision of laser fabrication with the self-organizing capacity of ECs to generate physiologically adaptive vascular systems.

Hybrid and emerging approaches

Recent innovations increasingly involve hybrid strategies that combine the structural precision of engineering with the adaptability of biological self-organization. These approaches merge microfluidics, organoids, and bioprinting with the aim of overcoming the limitations of individual techniques and achieving perfusable vascular systems with both reproducibility and physiological relevance.

A significant contribution was the integration of microfluidic vasculature with organoids by Cakir et al. [67]. They showed that human brain organoids vascularized by endothelial sprouts within perfusion channels exhibited enhanced survival and maturation. Hachey et al. applied this principle to oncology and established vascularized colorectal tumor models coupled to perfusion. First, they developed a vascularized micro-tumor platform that recapitulated in vivo drug responses [68]. Later, they created a patient-derived colorectal cancer vascularized microtumor (VMT) that mirrored clinical disease [69]. Additionally, high-throughput perfusable colon organoids have been developed using the IFlowPlate system [70]. Related tumor-on-chip studies integrated patient-derived breast cancer organoids with a quiescent perfused microvascular network [71]. Others studies have elaborated on the impact of intraluminal flow on tumor growth and drug delivery [72].

Moreover, hybrid vascular platforms have been extended to multi-organ models, where organotypic tissues are linked by perfusable endothelial networks to capture systemic interactions. Skardal et al. [73] have reported a tumor–liver chip with colorectal spheroids and hepatic constructs that shared an endothelialized circulation, which enabled the study of metastatic colonization and liver-specific drug metabolism. Furthermore, Arslan et al. [48] constructed a vascularized cardiac microtissue on a chip by integrating pre-vascularized endothelial networks with cardiac tissue, which enabled functional perfusion of beating-heart constructs. Other designs include organ-on-chip systems that have linked liver and heart units to examine drug safety—particularly cardiotoxicity and hepatic metabolism—under flow [74]. Adriani et al. [75,76] have developed a brain–vascular chip by integrating perfused endothelial networks with neural tissues. This chip showed the ability to model BBB function and neurovascular coupling. These examples highlight the ability of multi-organ vascularized platforms to capture inter-tissue pharmacokinetics and systemic disease processes in a manner that static cultures cannot.

Together, these hybrid and emerging platforms show that vascularized organoids and multi-organ chips are reshaping in vitro modeling. They enable perfusable vasculature in both single-tissue and systemic contexts. Thus, they act as powerful tools for studying inter-organ pharmacokinetics, metastatic progression, and patient-specific therapy responses. Their ability to bridge biological complexity with engineered control positions them as promising platforms for translational oncology, regenerative medicine, and drug development. Experimental criteria for defining perfusable vasculature

The term perfusable vasculature is often used qualitatively across engineered tissue platforms, and the criteria used to express functional perfusion vary between studies [76]. In general, a minimal set of experimental observations must be considered when assessing whether engineered vascular networks are truly perfusable. These include evidence of continuous lumen connectivity across the network, detection of barrier function or controlled permeability using tracer molecules or junctional markers, and confirmation that flow is distributed throughout the network rather than through isolated shunt pathways [77]. Additionally, temporal stability is important because channels that initially support flow may collapse or become occluded over time. In some systems, additional evaluation using red blood cell perfusion or thrombosis assays may further validate physiological compatibility [78]. Establishing these minimal criteria is essential to improve cross-study comparison and help distinguish between structures that merely contain lumens and those that support sustained functional perfusion.

HD vs LD tissues

Why cell density matters

Cell density is a critical determinant of tissue physiology because it governs the balance between nutrient consumption and diffusion-mediated supply. Most native organs, including the liver, brain, and solid tumors, exhibit cell packing densities in the order of 108–109 cells/mL, and these levels cannot be sustained without perfusable vasculature [17,79]. By contrast, many early engineered constructs operated at lower cell densities (e.g., 106–107 cells/mL) in a range that was manageable using passive diffusion alone. These LD tissues are easier to maintain and manipulate, but they cannot recapitulate the full metabolic demand, mechanical stiffness, or realistic drug responses observed in HD tissues. Engineering reviews discuss the effect of lower density in limiting functional maturation and phenotype under mass-transport constraints [80]. Diffusion modeling studies highlight the limiting of diffusion with increase in cell density [79].

Diffusion limitations in LD vs HD tissues

The role of diffusion becomes apparent on comparing LD and HD models. Sutherland et al. [17] first described the diffusion barrier in multicellular tumor spheroids. They reported necrotic core formation due to oxygen depletion in spheroids that exceeded 500 μm in diameter. Later studies confirmed that denser spheroids restricted drug and nanoparticle penetration. Tchoryk et al. [81] showed that larger tightly packed tumor spheroids exhibited substantially lowered nanoparticle uptake compared with that of smaller less dense counterparts. Similarly, Ahmed-Cox et al. [82] have reported slowed penetration kinetics in dense spheroids based on live imaging of nanoparticle transport. Collectively, these studies indicate that although LD constructs permit uniform diffusion and exposure, HD models naturally develop steep concentration gradients, hypoxia, and heterogeneous drug accumulation, which are features essential for faithfully reproducing in vivo pharmacokinetics and therapeutic resistance.

Metabolic and phenotypic differences

In addition to diffusion limitation, cell density drives distinct metabolic and phenotypic states. In the liver, HD cultures preserve hepatic identity and drug metabolism. For example, primary human hepatocytes maintained at high plating density showed stable albumin production and CYP3A activity, whereas those at lower density favored loss of function and dedifferentiation [83]. In neural systems, increase in tissue mass and maturity are coupled to metabolic reprogramming. For example, cerebral organoids undergo a shift toward oxidative phosphorylation as they develop complex neuronal networks, which is an adaptation linked to the higher metabolic demand of the organoid and dense cytoarchitecture [84]. In tumors, dense 3D architectures promote hypoxia-driven metabolic changes and therapy resistance. For example, multicellular tumor spheroids that develop hypoxic/necrotic cores exhibit gene-expression profiles that are closer to those of in vivo tumors with higher chemoresistance [3], and metabolic-flux measurements showed that 3D spheroids rewire glycolysis/OXPHOS relative to dilute 2D cultures [85]. For example, hypoxia in pancreatic cancer is directly linked to enhanced glycolysis and treatment resistance [86]. Together, these findings indicate that HD models elicit more effects in addition to recreating diffusion gradients such as triggering metabolic shifts, stabilizing organ-specific functions, and inducing adaptive stress responses. These features are central to mimicking the complex physiology of native tissues and are rarely captured in LD constructs.

Necessity of vascularization for HD tissue viability

HD engineered tissues approach native cell packing and thickness; hence, their survival and function are rapidly diffusion-limited unless oxygen and nutrients are delivered by perfusable vascular networks. In vivo, most cells occur within approximately 200 μm of a capillary. Similarly, large in-vitro tissues require vascularization and flow in tandem to avoid hypoxia and necrosis and sustain growth and maturation over time [87].

In the liver, transitioning from static culture to perfusion enables long-term functioning at high cell loads. Human hepatocyte spheroids in an automated perfusion bioreactor maintained albumin/urea output and phase I/II enzyme expression for 3–4 weeks, whereas comparable static formats typically decompensate far earlier. Similarly, earlier microphysiological liver platforms showed that constant perfusion preserves polarized transport and function in 3D hepatic tissues compared with those in static culture [88].

In the heart, diffusion alone constrains engineered myocardium thickness. However, this limitation is directly addressed by introducing endothelialized perfused channels into engineered heart tissue to enable intratissue perfusion in vitro. Recently, vascularized hiPSC-derived cardiac microtissues on-chip established perfusable microvessel networks that support tissue function under flow, which highlights the need for vascular integration in dense cardiac constructs [48]. Similar studies showed that perfusable culture systems improved maturation and functional readouts in human cardiac tissues relative to that observed in non-perfused settings [89]. In tumor models, the addition of perfusable microvessels is essential for more than mitigating core death. In fact, intraluminal flow through an engineered vascular network modulates growth, invasion, and drug transport into dense tumor regions. These phenomena are poorly observed in avascular constructs [72].

Finally, in all tissues, embedded vascular conduits make true large-scale constructs feasible. Multimaterial bioprinting of thick (>1 cm) vascularized tissues allowed on-chip perfusion for >6 weeks, which showed that perfusable vasculature is the enabling feature for centimeter-scale high-cell-density tissues [35]. Similarly, soft-microfluidic capillary-scale networks kept multi-mm3 neural and liver constructs viable while preventing hypoxia/necrosis and accelerating differentiation, which provides direct evidence that perfusion shifts HD tissues from failure to long-term function [87].

LD constructs are easy to sustain but physiologically simplistic. In contrast, HD constructs capture in vivo-like metabolism, mechanics, and pharmacokinetics but fail without perfusion. Hence, perfusable vascularization is the common resolution across organs as it restores mass transport, removes waste, and enables flow-dependent signaling, which are conditions required for organ-level fidelity in long-term HD models.

Functional implications of perfusable vasculature: flow direction, scalability, and cell delivery

The integration of perfusable vascular networks into HD constructs is necessary not just for viability but additionally for transforming the functional properties of engineered tissues. Two critical parameters are flow directionality and shear stress, which regulate endothelial phenotype, barrier integrity, and tissue-vascular exchange. This principle has been observed in the following: early liver-on-chip systems designed to replicate sinusoidal shear [88] in advanced microfluidic recirculation platforms, such as the UniChip [90]; vascularized liver constructs using controlled unidirectional perfusion [47]; and pump-less microfluidic devices engineered for directional flow [91]. Collectively, these studies emphasize that organ-specific shear ranges (such as 0.1–0.5 dyn cm−2 in hepatic sinusoids) are essential benchmarks while designing perfusion for HD engineered tissues.

Additionally, vascularization enables the scale-up of engineered constructs to the millimeter–centimeter range. Without perfusion, HD constructs larger than approximately 500 μm typically develop necrotic cores; however, bioprinting and sacrificial templating approaches have produced centimeter-thick tissues with embedded vascular conduits that remain viable and functional for weeks under perfusion [35,92,93]. These studies show that perfusable vasculature is the key enabler of thick high-cell-density tissues and bridge the gap between microscale models and organ-scale constructs.

Another unique advantage is the ability to achieve dynamic cell delivery and trafficking. Perfusable vascular beds allow stromal, immune, and progenitor cells to be introduced intraluminally and for these cells to adhere under shear and transmigrate into the parenchyma, which mimics the physiological processes of rolling, adhesion, and extravasation. This principle has been observed in diverse systems. For example, immune cells perfused through vascularized tumor chips infiltrated dense tumor masses. Stromal and progenitor cells delivered via flow stabilized microvessels and integrated into organoids. Pluripotent-derived precursors migrated along vascular networks to promote tissue maturation [13,71,94]. Collectively, these studies highlight the capacity of perfusable constructs to transform cell delivery into a controlled, repeatable, and physiologically relevant process, which enables the quantitative analysis of immune infiltration, therapeutic cell engraftment, and interstitial distribution in mm-scale engineered tissues.

Drug delivery, distribution, and metabolism in HD tissues

In engineered HD tissues, the first barrier for pharmacology is drug delivery into the construct. Unlike LD models, where passive diffusion ensures nearly uniform exposure, HD constructs reproduce the clinical reality of often restricted drug entry to perivascular zones with poor access to deeper regions. The introduction of perfusable endothelialized channels has been central in addressing this limitation. Now, compounds may be introduced intraluminally under controlled flow and shear such that delivery is governed by both advection–diffusion dynamics and endothelial barrier properties. This enables physiologically relevant dosing regimens, continuous perfusion, bolus injection, or repeated pulses that mirror patient treatment protocols. Across applications, vascularized systems show that intravascular delivery induces spatial gradients of penetration. They highlight vessel-specific barriers such as junction tightness or efflux activity and support the controlled co-delivery of therapeutic cells, biologics, or nanoparticles. In tumors [10,71,72,95,96], perfused chips show localized drug accumulation near vessels with heterogeneous uptake in compact spheroids, which helps identify treatment resistance mechanisms that are not captured by static cultures. In liver models, perfusion ensures directional delivery across polarized epithelia, which sustains metabolic activity and transporter function that are essential for realistic drug handling [97,98]. BBB systems further highlight the fact that luminal delivery under flow recapitulates selective CNS access and transporter-driven restriction [52,53,99,100].

Drug distribution within engineered tissues is highly dependent on cell density and vascularization. In HD constructs, diffusion alone is insufficient to ensure uniform penetration, which results in steep concentration gradients and hypoxic cores. This phenomenon was first observed in multicellular tumor spheroids. Recent vascularized tumor models have confirmed that perfusable microvessels alter transport from pure diffusion to an advection–diffusion regime, which leads to heterogeneous intratissue exposure with perivascular accumulation and underdosing of distal zones [71].

Metabolism is equally density- and perfusion-dependent. In hepatic constructs, static LD cultures rapidly lose cytochrome P450 activity, whereas HD liver spheroids and liver-on-chip systems under perfusion sustain phase I/II enzyme expression, albumin secretion, and clinically relevant metabolism for several weeks [[8], [9], [10]]. Perfused platforms have reproduced acetaminophen hepatotoxicity [11] and donor-specific drug clearance, which has provided predictive insight into drug-induced liver injury. In kidney-on-chip systems, perfusion maintains transporter-mediated handling (OAT1/3, OCT2, P-gp) and vectorial secretion, which enabled nephrotoxicity assessment under physiological flow [12,13]. Similarly, vascularized BBB models sustain efflux transporter activity and selective permeability. This enables mechanistic studies of CNS drug penetration, which are not possible with LD constructs [14,15].

Together, these findings show that drug distribution in HD constructs is inherently heterogeneous and that drug metabolism is only faithfully maintained under perfusion. Therefore, vascularized HD models provide the necessary physiological fidelity to evaluate efficacy, resistance, and toxicity in a manner that bridges the gap between conventional organoids and in vivo pharmacology.

Discussion

The development of perfusable vascular networks is a central milestone in the evolution of engineered tissue models. As presented in this review, the transition from LD constructs to physiologically relevant HD tissues fundamentally changes the requirements for tissue viability and function. Although LD models may be sustained by passive diffusion, HD tissues replicate the metabolic demand and spatial gradients of native organs and tumors, which necessitates active transport through perfusable vascular systems. The various strategies described here indicate that vascularization is not merely an architectural feature but a critical determinant that enables the generation of large metabolically active engineered tissues.

Different vascularization platforms address this challenge through complementary design principles. Endothelial self-assembly and organoid vascularization approaches reproduce biologically driven angiogenic processes and enable capillary-scale networks that closely resemble native microvasculature. These systems are particularly valuable for studying developmental vascular morphogenesis, tumor–vascular interactions, and organoid maturation. However, the emergent nature of these networks limits the precise control of vascular geometry and perfusion pathways. In contrast, microfluidic organ-on-chip systems impose defined channel architectures and controlled flow environments, which enable reproducible studies of endothelial barrier function, immune cell trafficking, and drug transport under physiologically relevant shear stress. Bioprinting and sacrificial templating approaches extend these capabilities to thicker tissues, which enables the fabrication of millimeter-to-centimeter-scale constructs with embedded perfusable channels. Finally, advanced microfabrication methods such as two-photon polymerization and multiphoton ablation provide notable spatial precision at near-capillary dimensions. However, these technologies currently remain limited in throughput and scalability. Apart from the engineering platform itself, the biological microenvironment strongly influences the performance of the engineered vasculature. Vascular maturation depends on interactions between endothelial cells and supporting mural or stromal cell populations such as pericytes, fibroblasts, and smooth muscle cells, which regulate vessel stability and barrier integrity. Additionally, ECM composition and mechanical properties modulate endothelial morphogenesis, diffusion gradients, and tissue perfusion. These factors become particularly critical in HD tissues, where transport is already diffusion-limited and matrix architecture significantly influences oxygen delivery, drug penetration, and immune cell infiltration.

Nevertheless, despite the significant progress achieved, several common technical challenges remain across vascularized tissue platforms. Channel occlusion or clogging may occur because of cell aggregation or matrix deformation, particularly in narrow microvessels. Endothelial delamination from channel surfaces may compromise barrier integrity and long-term perfusion stability. Operational factors such as bubble formation within perfusion circuits may disrupt flow and damage the endothelial layers. In hydrogel-based constructs, matrix contraction driven by stromal or tumor cells may distort vascular channels and generate uneven perfusion, which would produce localized hypoxia or heterogeneous drug exposure. Additionally, prolonged culture may lead to endothelial phenotype drift or loss of specialized vascular characteristics. Addressing these challenges typically requires coordinated optimization of matrix mechanics, perfusion stability, and supporting cellular components.

Taken together, the diverse vascularization strategies reviewed here should be viewed as complementary rather than competing approaches. Self-organized vascular networks provide biological fidelity. Microfluidic systems offer precise control of flow and transport. Bioprinting enables scalable tissue fabrication. High-resolution microfabrication mediates deterministic vascular patterning. Integrating these approaches with HD tissue architecture is a key direction for future prospects in this field. Continued advances in vascular maturation, perfusion stability, and standardized functional benchmarks are essential for translating vascularized HD tissues into reliable platforms for disease modeling, drug testing, and regenerative medicine.

Conclusion

The integration of perfusable vascular networks into engineered tissues has fundamentally reshaped the trajectory of in vitro model development. Among diverse strategies including endothelial self-assembly, microfluidic patterning, bioprinting, sacrificial templating, and high-resolution synthetic scaffolds, vascularization has emerged as not just a supplementary feature but the central determinant of achieving long-term viability and functional fidelity in HD constructs. In this context, resolving the size–density barrier would enable perfused systems to restore physiological mass transport, sustain metabolic activity, and reproduce drug penetration gradients to bridge critical gaps between LD constructs and native tissue physiology. In both regenerative and oncological contexts, vascularized HD models have shown the ability to preserve organ-specific phenotypes, capture heterogeneous therapeutic responses, and support dynamic immune and stromal cell trafficking, which elevates them to a new class of translational platforms.

However, key challenges remain despite these advances. Most current models are limited in their ability to reproduce the full hierarchical organization of native vasculature, and long-term stability under continuous perfusion has not been consistently achieved. The lack of standardized benchmarks for vascular function such as metrics for barrier integrity, shear responsiveness, and perfusion longevity continues to hinder reproducibility and cross-platform comparison. Moreover, although proof-of-concept studies have validated the use of vascularized HD tissues in drug testing, translating these systems into routine preclinical pipelines will require improvements in scalability, throughput, and integration with existing pharmacological workflows.

Future development in this field may occur in three promising directions. First, the incorporation of immune–vascular interfaces will enable physiologically relevant modeling of immune cell trafficking, extravasation, and immunotherapy response and create opportunities to study both therapeutic efficacy and vascular-mediated toxicities. Second, the development of multi-organ perfused systems will provide a means to investigate drug distribution, metabolism, and systemic toxicity across interconnected tissues, which would offer more predictive insights into pharmacokinetics and adverse events. Third, advances in standardization and scalability, including the establishment of functional readouts for vascular health and the adoption of bioprinting and hybrid fabrication strategies, will be essential for ensuring reproducibility and facilitating regulatory acceptance.

Collectively, these innovations indicate a future in which vascularized HD tissues will evolve from specialized experimental platforms into widely adopted tools for translational research. Perfusable constructs have increased the accurate prediction of drug safety and efficacy, captured immune–tumor–vasculature interactions, and supported the development of patient-specific models; thus, they exhibit the potential to significantly narrow the gap between preclinical testing and clinical outcomes. Consequently, vascularized HD tissues are primed to define the next generation of engineered models in the context of basic science and therapeutic development.
