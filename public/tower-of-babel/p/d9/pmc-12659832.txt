Long-term results of surgery for retinal detachment due to retinopathy of prematurity
Authors: Aditi Joshi, Aashul Chawla, Henna Valakkadavil, Pramod Bhende, Parveen Sen, Pradeep Susvar, Chetan Rao, Lingam Gopal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Surgery for stages 4 and 5 of retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) is challenging. The long-term results even in initially successful cases are not satisfactory. Data on long-term results are sparse.Methods:Retrospective analysis of patients with stages 4 and 5 of ROP who underwent surgery at a tertiary care center and had a minimum follow-up of 5 years. Most stage 4 a and 4b were managed by scleral buckling or lens sparing vitrectomy, while most stage 5 needed lens sacrificing vitrectomy. The anatomical success was assessed at 6 weeks postsurgery and again at the last follow-up, while the visual success was assessed only at final follow-up visit.Results:84 eyes of 60 patients with a mean follow-up of 9.27 years were analyzed. The mean age at presentation was more in stage 5 ROP compared to stage 4a and 4b (P < 0.001). Overall anatomical success was seen in 94.2% of stage 4a, 82.35% of stage 4b, and 63.6% of stage 5 at 6 weeks, which deteriorated to 88.2% in stage 4a, 70.59% in stage 4b, and 30.3% in stage 5 at the end of last follow-up. At last visit, the highest level of corrected vision attained was 6/9 in stage 4a, 6/18 in stage 4b, and 6/60 in stage 5 ROP.Conclusions:Anatomical and visual results are better with stages 4a and 4b compared to stage 5 ROP. On long-term follow-up, the incidence of recurrent retinal detachment is highest in stage 5 ROP.

Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) is an important cause of childhood blindness.[1] While the primary trigger factor is prematurity itself, there are several known and unknown factors that finally dictate the course of events in a given child. The disease prevalence changes from one geographical region to another depending upon the level of neonatal care and the ability for very small infants to survive. In a developing nation like India, there is a wide variation in the level of neonatal care as well as awareness of ROP. This results in several children presenting with stage 5 ROP without prior screening for ROP or laser treatment for the early stages of the disease. Stage 4 and 5 ROP signify end stage disease with retinal detachment (RD) and hence have severe effect on the visual function. Eyes treated with laser or anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) treatment can still progress to these severe stages of the disease. Treatment for stage 4 and 5 ROP is only by surgery. The relatively less severe disease can be managed with scleral buckling[23] or lens sparing vitrectomy, while most eyes with stage 5 ROP necessitate lens sacrifice with vitrectomy.[45]

Surgery for retinal detachment in ROP is considered difficult with relatively low success rates in published literature.[67] Considering the developing retina and the occurrence of detachment of retina due to fibrosis at a young age, the effect on the structure of the retina is significant and considerably different compared to adult retina. Even in eyes with initial success after surgery, recurrence of retinal detachment is not uncommon due to factors such as recurrent fibrosis and stretching of the thin retina that occurs with growth of the eye resulting in retinal breaks in the already thin retina. In this series, we present the long-term results of surgery for retinal detachment due to ROP (stages 4 and 5) after a minimum follow-up period of 5 years.

Methods

This retrospective study involved long-term follow-up data of infants diagnosed with retinal detachments secondary to ROP (stages 4 and 5) and who were surgically managed at a tertiary care center in South India. Considering a minimum follow-up period of 5 years, we selected patients operated between 2006 and 2019. This study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki, with Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval obtained prior to initiation of the study.

The medical records of these patients were reviewed, and data were entered in an excel sheet and analyzed with appropriate statistical tools using Stata 14.0. Of 156 patients who were operated for stages 4 and 5 of ROP between 2006 and 2019, there were 60 patients (84 eyes) who had longer than 5 years of follow-up postsurgery and form the subjects of this study.

Most patients were referred to us with a diagnosis of ROP-related retinal detachments (with or without prior treatment for preretinal detachment stages of ROP). A few of these were referred with stage 2/3/aggressive posterior ROP and progressed to retinal detachment despite lasers or anti-VEGF injection or both and necessitated surgery.

Surgical interventions consisted of lens-sparing vitrectomy (LSV), vitrectomy with lensectomy, scleral buckling, or a combination. We collected data, including gender, birth weight, gestational age, major risk factors for ROP, age at presentation to us, specifics of initial surgical intervention, any secondary intervention, complications, and anatomical and visual results. While anatomical results were assessed at 6 week visit and again at final follow-up visit, visual results were only assessed at the final follow-up visit.

There were six surgeons involved in the management of these cases, all of whom had experience in managing retinal detachments secondary to ROP.

Surgical technique

Scleral buckling

Scleral buckling for stage 4 ROP was with no. 240 silicone band (Labtician Ophthalmics, Canada) placed 360° circumferentially and secured in each quadrant using partial-thickness scleral tunnels. The band ends were joined with clove hitch knot using 5-0 polyester suture or a Watzke sleeve (Mira, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Anterior chamber paracentesis was performed as needed to facilitate band tightening. Cryopexy was done to the avascular retina as needed.

Lens sparing vitrectomy

Surgery was done with a 25-gauge system. Sclerotomies were made with a trocar cannula system or direct entry with a microvitreoretinal blade and use of a sutured 25-gauge infusion cannula. The sclerotomies were placed 1.5 mm posterior to the limbus. The circumferential location (clock hours) of the sclerotomies was decided based on location of the traction retinal detachment (TRD). In a few cases, all the three sclerotomies were placed in the nasal half the eye ball with an infusion cannula placed at the horizontal meridian and the two active sclerotomies on either side closer to the vertical meridian. This permitted good access to temporal peripheral TRD without accidental trauma to the elevated retina. With indentation, the lens could be preserved in a majority of cases. After the central vitreous was removed, the anatomy was assessed to decide whether induction of posterior vitreous detachment (PVD) is feasible or not. In a few cases, PVD induction was possible. Otherwise, the surgery was aimed to be minimalistic without being too aggressive for fear of creating retinal breaks.

Lensectomy and vitrectomy

For stage 5 ROP where lensectomy and vitrectomy was the standard approach, the entry sclerotomies were either at limbus or 0.5 to 0.75 mm posterior to limbus- aimed to exit behind the iris root. After lensectomy, the residual capsule was mobilized with forceps and carefully removed. Bimanual surgery was done with intraocular forceps and scissors under viscoelastic fill of the eye or using an anterior chamber maintainer. A nick was made in the center of the retrolental membrane and carefully deepened with scissors till the retinal surface was reached. Subsequent dissection varied depending on the anatomy and ease of separation of the membrane from underlying retina. The aim was mostly to create radial cuts toward the periphery and use them as handles for circumferential opening of the peripheral trough. Once the trough was opened, the dissection was done across the plateau of the elevated retina. Finally, the funnel opening was identified and dissection was done into the funnel and posterior traction was relieved to the extent possible. Very often, the tissue could be pulled off the optic disc area, suggesting complete relief of posterior traction. No attempt was made to drain subretinal fluid unless there was a significant exudative component. In these cases with significant exudative component, the retina was tense and tended to crowd forward, not permitting proper dissection. Subretinal fluid drainage with an externally placed needle created space and permitted a more comfortable dissection. Utmost care was taken to avoid iatrogenic retinal breaks. Minor bleeds were allowed to stop spontaneously. Major bleeds were stopped with light cautery avoiding excessive retinal coagulation. When bilateral surgery was indicated, it was either performed simultaneously or sequentially, with a 5–7 day interval.

Postoperative examinations were conducted on day 1 and periodically thereafter. Anatomical success was assessed at 6–8 weeks and the last follow-up. Additional evaluations included visual behavior assessment, cycloplegic refraction, intraocular pressure measurement, and so on. Aphakic rehabilitation when required was with spectacles. Anatomical reattachment was labeled ‘type 1 success’ if the entire retina was attached with or without some folds. If, however, part of the retina was detached but the posterior pole was attached, it was labeled ‘type 2 success.’ The eyes were labeled ‘failure’ if there was total or posterior pole detachment of retina. Repeat surgery was considered for eyes with residual traction and visual potential. Most cases of partial RD where scleral buckling was done had the buckle removed or cut at approximately 1 year of age. In addition, second surgical intervention was also required for glaucoma and cataract.

Results

Between 2006 and 2019, a total of 157 patients underwent surgery for stage 4 or 5 ROP in a tertiary care center in South India. The mean follow-up postsurgery was 4.67 years (SD 4.31). Of these, 60 patients had more than 5 years of follow-up after surgery (mean: 9.27 yrs ± 3.48; range 5 to 22.4 yrs). This study focused on this subgroup of 60 patients. 24 patients had bilateral surgery accounting for a total of 84 eyes that were included in this study.

The mean birth weight (grams) was 1220.87 ± 308.69 (range 600–2200), and the mean gestation age (weeks) was 29.13 ± 2.40 (range 25–34). The mean post menstrual age (PMA) (weeks) at presentation was 49.9 ± 17.03 (range 34–134.6) with no significant difference between males and females. The PMA at presentation (weeks) was higher for the stage 5 ROP (60.62 ± 19.14) vs 43.93 ± 8.26 for stage 4B and 43.26 ± 9.82 weeks for stage 4a. This difference was found to be statistically significant (P = <0.001, Anova test).

Of the 84 eyes, 42.9% did not have any prior treatment. The rest had laser (40.5%), anti-VEGF (1.19%), or a combination, before referral to us with retinal detachment. 34 eyes (40.48%) presented with stage 4A; 17 eyes (20.24%) presented with stage 4B, and the rest 33 (39.29%) presented with stage 5 ROP. Stage 4 ROP: Even in eyes with partial retinal detachment (stage 4), information on the zonal distribution of the initial stages of disease was not available to us for most cases since the patients were mostly referred with retinal detachment. However, from the location and configuration of the retinal detachment, one could approximately judge whether the disease was in zone 2 or zone 1. Thus, 9 eyes presumably had zone 1 disease in this series. In all these eyes, the TRD was in the posterior pole. In the rest (zone 2), the traction detachment was predominantly temporal in a majority of cases. In 30 of the 38 eyes with temporal retinal detachments, the macula was clinically not elevated, although subclinical detachment could not be ruled out. Optical coherence tomography was not done in this series to ascertain the macular status.

Stage 5 ROP: All except one eye had closed funnel configuration of retinal detachment with no visibility of optic disc.

The mean PMA (weeks) at surgery was 48.54 + SD10.53 for stage 4a, 46.95/SD 7.31 for stage 4b, and 62.99/SD19.17 for stage 5 ROP (P = <0.001, Anova test). 17 of the 84 eyes needed preoperative anti-VEGF injection to reduce the vascularity before surgery. 10 of these were stage 4a, and the rest were cases of stage 4b ROP.

Details of the primary surgical intervention are given in Table 1. Most eyes with stage 4 (a and b) could be managed with lens sparing vitrectomy or scleral buckling procedure. It is interesting to note that none of the eyes presenting with predominantly nasal TRD could be managed with scleral buckling alone and also more often needed lens sacrifice (2 out of 4 eyes) compared to those with predominantly temporal TRD (scleral buckle 17 out of 38 eyes; lens sacrifice 4 out of 38 eyes). There was one case of stage 5 ROP that could be managed by buckling procedure with subretinal fluid drainage. Although the retina was totally detached in this case, there was more of exudative component and less of tractional component.

70 of the 84 eyes did not have any intraoperative complication. Among the rest, the complications included retinal breaks in 4 eyes, lens touch in 3 eyes (in eyes that were planned for lens sparing vitrectomy), and a macular hole in one eye. Postoperative complications included glaucoma in 11 eyes, vitreous hemorrhage in 5 eyes, and cataract in 3 eyes.

Second intervention: The second surgery included 10 eyes for repeat vitreo retinal surgery (2 with silicone oil injection), 4 eyes for anti-glaucoma surgery (trabeculectomy or diode cyclophotocoagulation), 2 eyes for lens removal (one with IOL implantation), and 17 eyes for buckle removal. Both eyes that had silicone injection had subsequent removal of the oil. Of the 5 eyes with vitreous hemorrhage postoperatively, one underwent revision surgery. This was a case of stage 4a ROP with attached retina on ultrasound and also had glaucoma. The second surgery was in the form of trabeculectomy, vitreous washout, and additional membrane dissection. In the rest (4 eyes), the vitreous hemorrhage cleared, but retina was persistently detached and further surgery was felt to be not useful.

Anatomical success: Table 2 details the relationship of stage of ROP at surgery to the anatomical success at 6 weeks postoperatively and at final follow-up visit. As alluded to in the methods section, for the purpose of this study, we labeled type 1 success as total reattachment of retina and type 2 success as partial reattachment of retina with the posterior pole attached. There was a gradational decrease in overall anatomical success from stage 4a to 4b and 5 both at 6 weeks postsurgery (P = 0.015) and final follow-up (P = <0.001). The ratio of type 1: type 2 success was 1.46 for stage 4a, 0.74 for stage 4b, and 0.61 for stage 5 ROP at 6 weeks post op visit, indicating greater chances of residual retinal detachment for stages 4b and 5 compared to stage 4a. The overall reduction in the percentage of success between 6 weeks and final follow-up visit was the highest for stage 5 ROP (52.3%) vs 6.25% for stage 4a and 14.23% for stage 4b. Both type 1 and type 2 reattachment at 6 weeks visit had similar drop in percentage of final success for stage 4a and 4b. However, for stage 5 ROP, the drop in final percentage of success compared to 6-week visit was 76.9% for type 2 reattachment vs 12.5% for type 1 reattachment.

Stage 4 in general and 4a in particular had good visual results. Discussion

Surgery for ROP is indicated in eyes with stages 4 or 5 of the disease. However, the outcome of surgery is widely varied with stage 4 having better success than stage 5.[89] The retina in stage 5 ROP is detached for a much longer time and often under goes contracture, leading to mismatch between the retinal surface area and eye wall even after all preretinal traction is relieved. The later in life the child presents with stage 5 ROP, the greater is this mismatch. In stage 4 ROP on the other hand, the chances of reattaching the retina are higher since the surgery is often done early in life with very little contracture of retina.

In this series, stage 4 ROP was seen in two clinical scenarios: 1. Untreated eyes developing relatively peripheral fibrosis and the retina being pulled toward ora serrata; 2 Post laser photocoagulation retinal detachment developing predominantly in the posterior pole. In the first situation, management was by scleral buckling or lens sparing vitrectomy depending on the relative location of traction. Post scleral buckling, the posterior pole often remains distorted, but there was functionality and these children could be helped subsequently by low vision aids later in life. In eyes with posterior pole detachments, lens sparing vitrectomy was the treatment of choice. In these cases, a minimalistic surgical approach was taken to limit the dissection to the fibrotic areas and clearing the vitreous in front. Although attempts were made to induce posterior vitreous detachment (PVD), this was not persisted with if the PVD induction was difficult or the retina was being pulled up with the vitreous. In one case, a large preretinal hemorrhage served as an initiating point for inducing PVD. In eyes with posterior pole detachments located around the optic disc, residual traction on the macula remained in most cases since it was never possible to dissect or peel the tissue completely.

We described the anatomical success in terms of type 1 and type 2 since posterior pole reattachment (with persistent peripheral retinal detachment) can be considered a success in the milieu of ROP-induced retinal detachments. Table 2 reveals the gradational decrease in the anatomical success from stage 4a to stage 4b and to stage 5 (P = <0.001). This experience is similar to reported literature with stage 5 ROP having low success rates.[89101112]

This article concentrates on the long-term results of these eyes since the success in the immediate postoperative period does not always sustain later in life. Growth of the eye along with the growth of the child has differing effect on the retina depending on the extent of retinal thinning and reattachment of the retina following the surgery. The longer the duration of retinal detachment, greater is the retinal contracture and correspondingly greater is the stretch the retina has to undergo to configure to the underlying RPE. Hence, the retina is under perpetual tension and this tension worsens with growth of the eye ball. Not uncommonly, this can result in retina giving way at its weakest points, especially the macula. Table 2 gives information on this conversion from success to failure over time for each of the stages of ROP. A totally reattached retina (type 1 success) at 6 weeks postoperatively is likely to have better maintenance of attached status of retina for all stages of ROP over time, while the long-term stability of reattachment is very poor for a partially reattached retina (type 2 success) in stage 5 ROP. 10 out of the 13 eyes with stage 5 ROP and type 2 success in early post op period had failure at final follow-up visit. Rhegmatogenous retinal detachment in this scenario has very little chance of success even with repeat surgery. Stage 4 ROP has better long-term stability postsurgery, mainly due to the fact that the surgery was done earlier in life and the retinal thinning was not as much as in stage 5 ROP. In general, breaks occurring during surgery or in the postoperative period spell failure since it is very difficult to smoothen and reattach the retina unlike in adult retinal detachments with proliferative vitreo retinopathy. However on occasions, stage 4 eyes with rhegmatogenous retinal detachment may provide opportunity to relieve all traction and reattach the retina with silicone oil injection. Two cases had such surgery with silicone oil injection and a good result post oil removal.

There were 10 eyes wherein revision vitreo retinal surgery was done including the two eyes with silicone oil injection discussed above. Of these, 6 eyes had type 1 success, 3 type 2 success, and only 1 had failure. However, we have been selective in recommending revision surgery for failed first attempt, and hence, the results were skewed. None of the eyes with buckle removal had any recurrences. Of the 4 eyes that had glaucoma intervention, only 1 had attached retina at final follow-up. In the milieu of type 1 success, management of cataract later in life was found unremarkable. Minimal vitreous hemorrhage postoperatively is not uncommon but clears rapidly. The management of nonclearing postoperative hemorrhage is largely decided based on the posterior segment condition (assessed based on operation notes of first intervention and ultrasound). More than the hemorrhage, it is the operability or otherwise of the retinal condition that dictates the decision to reoperate. In this series, only one of the five eyes with vitreous hemorrhage was offered second intervention and this case did well surgically and recovered useful vision.

Labeling the disease as zone 1 or zone 2 was admittedly less than accurate in this series due to reasons given in methods section. However, the anatomical success was not significantly different between the groups (P = 0.223 by Chi-square test).

Since eyes with failure have perception of light or worse vision, Table 3 details only the log Mar visual acuity for eyes that had success at final follow-up. Overall, stage 4a had the best results. Stage 5 eyes had in general poor visual recovery despite anatomical success compared to stage 4. However, there were eyes with stage 5 ROP that recovered 6/60 vision and retained it even on a long follow-up of more than 5 years.

Despite the generally poor results in stage 5 ROP, it may be still worth trying the surgical option in these cases since 1) at least a few eyes could recover useful vision and 2) children can adapt to even the very low levels of vision and it is surprising to see them running around without stumbling into objects even when visual acuity is not recordable on Snellen’s chart.

The results of stage 5 ROP vary widely from one series to another due to the socioeconomic factors that are unique to that area. These differences pertain to age at presentation (and surgery), configuration of the funnel (open vs closed), and so on. In this series, the mean post menstrual age at surgery for stage 5 ROP was 62.99 weeks, while eyes with stages 4 A and B had surgery between 46 and 48 weeks post menstrual age. The late presentation was associated with mostly closed funnel configuration (only one of the 33 eyes with stage 5 ROP had open funnel configuration with disc visibility) which in turn affected the surgical success.

Conclusion

This study highlights the results of long-term follow-up (minimum of 5 years) after surgery for sequelae of ROP. In general, stage 4 ROP was found to have better success of reattachment of retina compared to stage 5 ROP. Over time, recurrence of retinal detachment was a significant problem with stage 5 ROP, especially when the initial reattachment was partial (type 2 success). A totally reattached retina in the early postoperative period (type 1 success) has high chances of retaining this status over a long follow-up for both stage 4 and 5 ROP. Visual results are poor in stage 5 compared to stage 4 even with type 1 success. The study highlights the importance of early detection of retinal detachment and management for best results.
