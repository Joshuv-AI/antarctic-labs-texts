A presentation of recurrent cerebral ischemic stroke in eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis: a case report
Authors: Bryan Gregory, Ahmed Dawood Al Mahrizi, Harman Gill, Fatima Mossolem, Song Ang Peng, Randy Rueda, Eunseuk Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis is characterized as a small vessel vasculitis with the propensity to have a myriad of presentations due to the targeting of several different organ systems. More often than not, it is the pulmonary system that is affected. However, other reported presentations have included involvement of the skin, cardiovascular system, neurological system, renal, musculoskeletal, and even gastrointestinal tract in sequential order of increasing rarity. In most of these reported cases, only one or a couple of systems are involved. Here, the authors present a case of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis that has peculiar and ominous involvement of both the neurological and gastrointestinal systems, lending to its extreme rarity as well as severity.Case presentationA 69-year-old Eastern European female patient was diagnosed with eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis exhibiting extensive multisystem involvement. Her case included a rare documented instance of hemorrhagic shock secondary to ileal gastrointestinal bleeding, occurring concurrently with multiple cerebral vascular infarctions.ConclusionThis case highlights the complexity and severity of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis when it involves multiple organ systems simultaneously. It emphasizes the importance of recognizing eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis as a differential diagnosis in patients presenting with multisystem disease and unexplained eosinophilia. The report also underscores the need for aggressive management strategies and vigilance in diagnosing rare manifestations such as gastrointestinal hemorrhage and recurrent cerebral ischemic strokes.Keywords: Churg–Strauss, Vasculitis, Autoimmune ischemic infarct, Eosinophilia, ANCA positive, Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis

Introduction

Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is characterized as a small vessel vasculitis with the propensity to have a myriad of presentations due to the targeting of several different organ systems. In order of increasing rarer presentation, these organ systems include pulmonary, dermatological, cardiovascular, neurologic, kidney, musculoskeletal, and gastrointestinal tract. The typical manifestation starts with a prodromal phase of atopy in a middle-aged adult, such as asthma, rhinosinusitis, and allergic rhinitis that eventually leads to an eosinophilic phase with increased eosinophilia in the blood and infiltration of various organs [1]. Later on, patients might go on to develop small vessel vasculitis lending to the possibility of life-threatening complications of the various organs stated above.

The pathophysiology of EGPA is not fully understood, however, there is a foundational basis. It has been hypothesized that antineutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibodies activate neutrophils, causing them to infiltrate the endothelium of the vessel walls. The neutrophils then degranulate, causing an oxidative burst of toxic oxygen free radicals, as well as lytic enzymes that induce tissue damage. There is also evidence that the neutrophil activation is potentiated by cytokines upregulated in the disease process, in addition to antibodies to proteinase 3 inhibiting alpha 1 antitrypsin [2].

It has been well documented that other small vessel vasculitis, such as granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA), has abdominal manifestations with abdominal pain, gut ulceration, or even infarction [3]. However, only two cases of small intestine ulceration have been reported at this time, with one noted to have incidence in the small intestine [4, 5]. In this case, the patient was noted to have extensive superficial ulceration, with profuse bleeding, in the ileum. There still is debate, however, as to the exact etiology of the ulceration in this case, as the patient did complete a round of pulse dose steroids with tapering before presenting for hemorrhagic shock.

In regards to management, there have been many different treatment modalities for EGPA that have been considered and studied. Glucocorticoids have been the gold standard, but the intensity of the therapy depends on the severity of the illness with weight-based oral prednisone for systemic vasculitis and pulse dose therapy with a slow taper to oral prednisone for more severe involvement. In the presence of life-threatening complications, an additional agent has typically been required. Cyclophosphamide has historically been the agent of choice, however, it has recently fallen out of favor for Rituximab in terms of treating ANCA-positive vasculitis.

In general, overall prognosis is relatively optimistic, providing the patient receives adequate and appropriate treatment. As long as the patient is treated, 1-year survival rates are approximately 90%, and 5-year survival rates are about 62%. Without treatment, prognosis is extremely poor, with 5-year survival rates hovering around 25%. The most notable etiologies causing death include myocarditis and myocardial infarction induced by coronary arteritis. However, it has been documented that other patients have died from cerebral bleeding, renal failure, and gastrointestinal hemorrhage [6].

Case presentation

We present the case of a 69-year-old Eastern European female patient with past medical history of asthma diagnosed in childhood, hypertension, hypothyroidism, recent cerebrovascular accident (CVA) on aspirin, and myocardial infarction (MI), as well as a family history of asthma, presented to the ED for dizziness, left-sided visual symptoms, right-sided facial droop, and right lower extremity weakness. Initial computed tomography (CT) scan of the head showed small foci of hyperintensity along both the right and left parietal cortices likely related to petechial hemorrhage from recent ischemia and extensive pansinusitis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain showed restricted diffusion within bilateral cerebral and cerebral hemispheres in a watershed distribution. Initial labs showed leukocytosis with eosinophilic predominance (33.1, eosinophil% 52.0), anemia (hemoglobin 10.1), and elevated alkaline phosphatase (129). Computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the chest was performed to assess lymphadenopathy due to concerns for lymphoma, and showed inflammatory nodules in the anterior upper lobes of the lungs bilaterally. Echocardiogram with bubble study to assess for patent foramen ovale (PFO) in the setting of the acute strokes revealed moderate circumferential pericardial effusion (1–2 cm) and right atrial collapse consistent with early tamponade physiology for which a pericardial window was created. Flow cytometry showed eosinophilia comprising 61% of the total cell count without evidence of an aberrant phenotype of myeloid population, and blasts were not increased. Extensive vasculitis workup was done as presented in Table 1. Bone marrow biopsy showed mildly hypercellular bone marrow with eosinophilia comprising 40–45% of the total marrow cellularity. Cytoplasmic antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (C-ANCA) titers were positive in 1:160 ratio and immunoglobulin (Ig)E was elevated at 1102. In addition, a kidney biopsy was performed, confirming EGPA. The patient was started on 1 g of intravenous methylprednisolone for 2 days and then 1 mg/kg thereafter, and 1 g of rituximab. The patient’s symptoms appeared to go into remission shortly afterwards, and they were subsequently discharged.

The patient was once again admitted, however, this time for maroon-colored stools, and was found to be positive for Clostridium difficile infection, and colonoscopy was deferred due to this acute infection. Patient was placed on intravenous steroids during admission, and diarrhea resolved with plans for outpatient follow up and colonoscopy. However, the patient was readmitted several days later for gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding. Colonoscopy, performed emergently, revealed shallow colonic ulcers in the ascending colon with no active bleed. Nuclear medicine (NM) bleeding scan, however, did reveal active bleeding in the right lower quadrant. Interventional radiology was able to cauterize several foci of hemorrhage in the right hepatic flexure involving distal branches of the right colonic arteries, including the right, middle, and ileocolic arteries. A second bleeding scan was performed, and showed evidence of active hemorrhage in the region of the cecum, for which interventional radiology embolized three separate branches of the superior mesenteric artery. Due to persistent bleeding, an exploratory laparotomy was performed, which revealed active hemorrhage in the terminal ileum. This led to a partial right colectomy with small bowel resection of the terminal ileum, and ileostomy. The procedure halted further hematochezia for the rest of the admission. Spontaneous breathing trials were attempted, however, the patient failed every attempt due to diminished mental status where she was unable to wake up. Due to the change in mental status, brain MRI was performed. The study revealed multiple acute infarcts in several regions of the brain, including the left frontal lobe and left cerebellar tonsil. Due to the inability to wean mechanical ventilation, and a new neurologic baseline of being comatose, a collective decision was made by the family to place the patient on comfort care. She passed shortly afterwards.

Discussion

Distinct characteristics of the case

The unique case of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) presented here is characterized by widespread multisystem involvement and the development of serious, even life-threatening complications. The patient presented a combination of gastrointestinal hemorrhage that required surgical procedures and multiple cerebral infarctions, a rare presentation of EGPA. Although EGPA is known to affect many organ systems, simultaneous involvement of multiple systems, such as the gastrointestinal system and the central nervous system, is uncommon and poses serious challenges in managing the disease. The gastrointestinal hemorrhage caused hemorrhagic shock, necessitating a sequence of procedures, such as embolization and subtotal colectomy with ileostomy. Concurrently, the patient developed recurring cerebral ischemic strokes, complicating treatment even more. The coexistence of serious presentations is a reflection of EGPA’s unpredictable nature and stresses the need to recognize its capability to lead to serious multisystem disease [7].

The etiology of gastrointestinal ulceration and hemorrhage in EGPA is not fully understood; yet, ischemia caused by vascular injury secondary to vasculitis or eosinophilic mucosal invasion of the gastrointestinal mucosa is suspected to be responsible [2]. In this case, it is likely that mucosal compromise was caused by earlier corticosteroid use or that it masked signs of gastrointestinal disease until a massive hemorrhage appeared. This case highlights a key concern for healthcare providers caring for EGPA: though corticosteroids effectively modulate inflammation, they also increase the risk of complications, such as gastrointestinal hemorrhage or perforation [8].

The rarity of such presentations makes this case highly relevant to improving our knowledge of EGPA’s varied presentations. Most cases presented in literature generally affect a single or two organ systems, with respiratory symptoms dominating as the most prevalent one [9]. However, this case serves to illustrate that EGPA can manifest in a life-threatening way, involving uncommon systems such as the gastrointestinal system and the brain. By reporting this case, healthcare practitioners gain useful knowledge of EGPA’s potential to manifest in a serious manner, hence reinforcing the need for more vigilance in diagnosing and managing such uncommon presentations.

Diagnostic challenges

The diagnostic process in this case was fraught with challenges, largely owing to the initial presentation of neurological symptoms, which risked misdiagnosis. The patient presented with dizziness, visual impairments, drooping of the face, and weakness in the limbs—symptoms more classically linked to cerebrovascular events rather than to vasculitis [10]. Initial imaging was shown to demonstrate multiple cerebral infarcts in a watershed distribution, raising concern for embolic or thrombotic etiology over autoimmune etiology. In addition, laboratory results presented with leukocytosis in eosinophilia, where eosinophils accounted for 52% of total white blood cell count and a high IgE count. These act as key indicators and can be associated with other disease processes such as parasitic infections or malignancy [11].

The diagnosis of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) was arrived at via a confluence of a positive C-ANCA titer, high IgE, and results of a kidney biopsy that confirmed the existence of vasculitis. The fact that this patient had a notably high ratio of C-ANCA titers is somewhat of a novelty in this case. This is due to the fact that EGPA is typically P-ANCA positive. However, of the various small vessel vasculitides, EGPA has been documented to have the greatest degree of variability of testing positive for either marker. In fact, recent literature suggests that greater than 20% of cases of classical EGPA have tested positive for C-ANCA, and up to 50% of cases do not test positive for either marker [12]. In retrospect, this case highlights the paramount importance of keeping a wide differential diagnosis in sight when presented with cases of unexplained eosinophilia in conjunction with multisystem disease. Neurologic presentations are not uncommon in EGPA; nevertheless, these can cloud the diagnosis if health practitioners fail to take note of the potential for vasculitis. This case highlights again the need for heightened clinician suspicion to keep EGPA in their differential diagnosis when presented with unexplained eosinophilia and multisystem disease—even when initial presentations deviate from classic pulmonary or cutaneous presentations [13].

Gastrointestinal involvement in EGPA

The gastrointestinal symptoms presented in this case were unusually severe, adding to its uniqueness. Gastrointestinal presentations in eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) occur in around 12–78% of cases, however, life-threatening hemorrhage that can only be surgically corrected is relatively uncommon [7]. The patient suffered recurring gastrointestinal hemorrhage after procedures, such as embolization of branch vessels of the superior mesenteric artery and an exploratory laparotomy with partial resection of the bowel. Adverse events of this level of severity are not often encountered in similar cases, even in times of confirmed gastrointestinal pathology [14].

Treatment challenges and outcomes

The treatment course for this patient illustrates the challenges associated with managing severe multisystem EGPA. Initial therapy with high-dose corticosteroids and rituximab appeared effective in inducing transient remission; however, the patient’s clinical course was marked by recurrent gastrointestinal hemorrhages and progressive neurological deterioration. This suggests that conventional immunosuppressive therapies may be insufficient for refractory cases or those involving severe multisystem disease [15].

Rituximab has emerged as a preferred agent over cyclophosphamide for ANCA-positive vasculitis due to its favorable side effect profile and efficacy [16]. However, this case highlights that even rituximab may not be adequate for managing aggressive disease manifestations such as those seen here, as the patient received 1 g. The patient’s inability to achieve sustained remission despite aggressive therapy raises questions about whether additional or alternative immunomodulatory agents could have been beneficial. Other therapies such as mepolizumab have shown promise in targeting interleukin (IL)-5-mediated pathways specifically involved in eosinophilic inflammation [17]. The authors hypothesize that while rituximab may have been sufficient for induction therapy, the patient likely would have benefited from remission maintenance therapy. The American College of Rheumatology and Vasculitis Foundation even recommend in their 2021 guidelines that severe cases of EGPA warrant methotrexate, azathioprine, or mycophenolate mofetil, with tapering of glucocorticoids as tolerated [18].

Another notable challenge was balancing immunosuppression against the risk of infection—a common complication in patients receiving high-dose steroids or biologics [19]. The patient developed Clostridium difficile infection during hospitalization, which delayed diagnostic procedures such as colonoscopy and may have exacerbated her clinical deterioration [20].

Ultimately, despite aggressive treatment efforts, including surgical interventions and intensive care support, the patient’s condition deteriorated due to recurrent strokes and failure to be weaned from mechanical ventilation. This underscores the need for innovative therapeutic approaches tailored to refractory cases of EGPA.

Prognostic factors

The adverse course in this example is attributed to a confluence of circumstances: recurrent cerebral infarcts, chronic gastrointestinal hemorrhage that required surgery, and a failure to be weaned from mechanical ventilation that occurred secondary to altered mental status. Once confirmatory diagnosis is achieved via biopsy, it is paramount to assess the severity of the illness with recognition of certain prognostic factors. These prognostic factors can be succinctly addressed with what is known as the Five Factor Score (FFS). The FFS uses five points, which include: (1) proteinuria greater than 1 g in 24 h; (2) creatininemia greater than 140 micro moles per liter; (3) specific gastrointestinal involvement; (4) specific cardiomyopathy; and (5) specific CNS involvement. A score of 2 or greater, as in this patient’s case, denotes a 5-year survival rate of 54.1% with a relative risk of 2.40 [21].

Recent evidence shows that patients with eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) having gastrointestinal disease have lower cumulative survival rates, lower rates of long-term remission, and higher relapse rates compared to patients without gastrointestinal disease [22]. This observation suggests that gastrointestinal disease is predictive of a more aggressive disease course.[22]. Moreover, neurologic complications—repeated strokes—are a complicating factor in management and a primary cause of morbid complications and mortality [23]. EGPA-associated cerebral infarcts are believed to be caused by vasculitis-associated vessel obstruction or embolization secondary to cardiac disease (such as pericardial effusion) [24]. The observations underscore the paramount importance of urgent diagnosis and aggressive treatment in patients presenting with widespread multisystem disease.

The case adds to the growing literature detailing uncommon yet serious gastrointestinal complications of EGPA. The case highlights the need for close monitoring of patients suspected of or confirmed to harbor gastrointestinal disease, reaffirming that timely intervention is key in preventing potentially life-threatening consequences [7, 25].

Future directions

The case highlights a number of areas that need to be explored to better manage patients afflicted with refractory or severe EGPA. First, there is a great need for new drugs that specifically target EGPA pathophysiology. As one example, biologic drugs that block interleukin-5 (IL-5) or other cytokines that play a role in eosinophilic inflammation would provide new options for patients that fail initial treatment [26]. Second, this case highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to managing complex cases of vasculitis such as EGPA. Multidisciplinary input from various specialists, such as neurologists, gastroenterologists, rheumatologists, and intensivists, would facilitate better care by more effectively managing organ-specific complications [27]. Lastly, future studies should focus on developing biomarkers of disease severity or response to treatment. Such biomarkers would allow patients to be risk-stratified, enabling more accurate clinical decision-making [28, 29].

Conclusion

When encountering a patient with increased eosinophilia, we encourage providers to keep EGPA as a differential in mind beyond the typical diagnoses of malignancy and parasitemia. In addition, clinicians must be aware that not all strokes occur secondary to thrombosis or hemorrhage, and that an autoimmune pathophysiology is one of many possible etiologies. This also applies to gastrointestinal bleeding in the setting of superficial or absent ulcerations in the small intestine, and as a new onset pericardial effusion or heart failure. A past medical history of atopy including asthma and allergic rhinitis should raise suspicion for providers, but biopsy remains the only definitive diagnosis. Glucocorticoids remain as the best defense. However, in the setting of increasing severity and confirmed ANCA-vasculitis, agents such as rituximab and cyclophosphamide bolster the patient’s response and increase their chances of remission.
