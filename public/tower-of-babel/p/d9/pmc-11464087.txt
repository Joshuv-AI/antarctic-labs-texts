Detection of damage caused by Nezara viridula on soybean using novel imaging approaches based on computed tomography and image color analysis
Authors: Szilvia Gibicsár, Tamás Donkó, Dániel Fajtai, Sándor Keszthelyi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoybean ( Glycine max L.) is an important leguminous plant, in which pests trigger significant damage every year. Important members of this community are insects with piercing‐sucking mouthpart, especially the southern green stinkbug, Nezara viridula L.. This insect with its extraoral digestion causes visible alterations (morphological and color changes) in the seeds. We aimed to obtain precise information about the extent and nature of damage in soybeans caused by N. viridula using nondestructive imaging methods. Two infestation conditions were applied: one with controlled numbers of pests (six insects/15 pods) and another with naturally occurring pests (samples collected from the apical part of the plant and samples from whole plants). An intact control group was also included, resulting in four treatment groups. Seed samples were analyzed by computed tomography (CT) and image color analysis under laboratory conditions. According to our CT findings, the damage caused by N. viridula changed the radiodensity, volume, and shape (Solidity) of the soybean seeds during the pod‐filling and maturing period. Radiodensity was significantly reduced in all three damaged categories compared to the intact sample; the mean radiodensity reduction range was 49–412 HU. The seed volume also decreased significantly (25%–80% decrease), with a threefold reduction for samples exposed to regulated damage compared to natural ones. The samples exposed to natural damage showed significant but minor reduction in solidity, while samples exposed to regulated damage showed a prominent decrease (~12%). Image color analysis showed that the damaged samples were well distinguishable, and the differences were statistically verifiable. The achieved data derived from our external and internal imaging approaches contribute to a better understanding of the internal chemical processes, and CT analysis helps to understand the alteration trends of the hidden structure of seeds caused by a pest. Our results can contribute to the development of a practically applicable system based on image analysis, which can identify lots damaged by insects.Keywords: CT‐imaging, nondestructive, southern green stinkbug, yield

INTRODUCTION

Soybean (Glycine max L.) is a major crop grown in arable land, and its production will be in increasing demand in the coming years. Assessing the quality of soybean seeds is essential to ensure maximum yield and yield quality. Therefore, efficient methods are needed to assess the different traits affecting the quality of the crop, related to physiological and seed health changes. The presence of insect pests threatens the safety of soybean production worldwide, among them pentatomid heteropterans are of great importance (Gaur & Mogalapu, 2018).

The southern green stinkbug, Nezara viridula L., 1758 (Hemiptera: Pentatomidae) is a cosmopolitan species native to Africa and widely distributed in Europe, Asia, the United States, and Australia (Todd, 1989). It is a highly polyphagous bug, with soybean and many other economically important crops among its host plants (Panizzi, 2000; Panizzi et al., 2000; Todd, 1989). Regarding the dicots, legumes are favored host plants; in general, preference varies with plant phenology and maturity (Panizzi, 2000; Panizzi et al., 2000; Todd, 1989). Plants are most attractive during fruit or pod formation (Bundy & McPherson, 2000; Lye & Story, 1988; Todd, 1989).

Piercing‐sucking insects feed by inserting their stylets in plant tissues and releasing enzyme‐containing saliva into plants before feeding. Digestion begins extra‐orally, the predigested plant material serves as food (Labandeira & Phillips, 1996). Pentatomids damage to plant tissues is manifested as deformation, thinning, and tissue death (Schaefer & Panizzi, 2000). In leguminous plants, damage caused during the period of pod‐filling results in inadequate seed development, with shriveled, smaller, deformed seeds (Brier & Rogers, 1991). After pod‐filling, the damage appears on seeds as dark spots in small depressions (Todd & Herzog, 1980). On mature dissected seeds, the damage is visible as a chalky texture in the tissues under the puncture. Depending on the time of damage, the quantity and quality of the harvested crop, seed viability, and germination can be reduced (Jensen & Newsom, 1972).

The quality assessment of the harvested crop can be based on the examination of external and internal characteristics. External characteristics that can be detected include color, shape, surface texture, and size, while internal characteristics include insect infestation, moisture content, microstructure, and density (Du et al., 2019). Several methods are available to test the quality of the crop seeds to be sold. Traditional methods, although standardized, are time and labor‐intensive, require sample preparation, and are invasive.

The use of digital imaging techniques, such as computed tomography (CT), has many advantages for crop qualification. Firstly, structural and physiological changes in plant tissues that would be difficult to detect by other means can be monitored, and secondly, they can be investigated noninvasively without damaging the plant. Additionally, CT imaging has a wide range of applications for the certification of agricultural products. It can be used for quality control, microstructure observation, detection of pest infestation, and other physical property measurements (Du et al., 2019; Toews et al., 2006). In the case of soybean seeds, CT has great potential, for the characterization and identification of mechanical and insect damage (França‐Silva et al., 2023). In 2004, the International Seed Testing Association (ISTA) formally recommended the use of low‐energy X‐ray testing to assess the physical quality of seeds, which has proven to be a rapid and nondestructive method for analyzing seed structure (ISTA, 2004). The method allowed the detection of seeds that were filled or empty, malformed, mechanically damaged, or damaged by insects or fungi (França‐Silva et al., 2023).

Nowadays, agriculture faces a number of challenges that have a significant impact on the productivity and profitability of the sector. Global soybean production increases, primarily due to expanding soybean cultivated area but also improved yields. The expansion of soy cultivation and monocropping to meet growing demand has led to the destruction of vital ecosystems, particularly in the Brazilian Amazon and Cerrado (Bicudo Da Silva et al., 2020; IPBES, 2019). To increase the harvestable crop in a sustainable way, we need to focus on crop protection, considering that global climate change could increase the range of certain pests important to agriculture and the damage they cause could rise dramatically. N. viridula is already a pest of major importance in soybean and its significance may increase in the future. The well‐known symptoms of heteropteran damage on harvested soybeans are loss of size, discoloration, patches, and wrinkling. Accompanying the appearance of these symptoms, the internal composition of soybean seeds also changes (Miner, 1961; Thomas et al., 1974).

The color of the harvested crop is an important external marker of quality. The difference in color of insect‐damaged soybean seeds compared to undamaged ones can be objectively compared by image color analysis. Color spaces like HSV (Hue, Saturation, Value) and RGB (Red, Green, Blue) are a virtual spatial coordinate system used to represent colors, where the properties of each color are expressed by its coordinates (Ajmal et al., 2018; Schwarz et al., 1987). Decisions based on the similarity of color should generally be made in the HSV color space rather than the commonly known RGB. The “Hue” channel gives the hue, “Saturation” the saturation, and “Value” the brightness. In HSV color space, similar colors, at least as perceived by human vision, appear at close HSV values (Ajmal et al., 2018).

We aimed to characterize the damage of N. viridula on fully matured soybean seeds using nondestructive CT imaging and image color analyzing by using the HSV color space. Using these techniques, we intended to identify and evaluate the damage‐related external color changes, changes in seed radiodensity, size reduction, and seed distortion. In addition, our goal was to investigate whether these parameters, which are generally accepted to characterize the quality of seeds, really reflect the destructive effect of the saliva injected by the stinkbug and whether they can be used for quick and simple detection of stinkbug damage.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sampling and experimental settings

A field experiment was set up in Kozármisleny, Hungary, (GPS:46.044780,18.276796) in 2023 to characterize the damage caused to soybean by N. viridula. “Annika” soybean (medium maturity variety, with cycle of 145–148 days) was planted with .75 m row spacing. The N. viridulas (adults and fifth instar larvae in mixed gender) used in the experiment were collected by hand from a soybean field just before the start of the experiment. No pesticides were applied in the fields (neither on the field on which the experiment was conducted nor on the one used to collect the stinkbugs). Two infestation conditions were applied in a field experiment, one with controlled numbers of pests and another with naturally occurring pests. Controlled‐damage part of the experiments was set up as follows: Twelve soybean plants were infested with adult and 5th instar N. viridula. Six stinkbugs per plant were placed in isolation bags for 44 days from the pod‐filling period (R5–R6) to full maturity (R8). Isolation bags (.4 × .4 × .8 m, isolating only the upper 15 pods) were randomly distributed but with a minimum of 1.5 m spacing. The plants were inspected every other day, and dead stinkbugs were replaced. After the infestation period, the insects were eliminated from the cages. To characterize the natural insect infestation, soybean seed samples were collected from the field; the top 15 pods of 12 non‐isolated randomly chosen plants were harvested, and 12 whole plants were also sampled. Twelve control plants were kept pest‐free with isolation bags of the same design and placement as described above.

This resulted in four treatment groups as follows:

Harvesting was done by hand at full maturity (R8), and seeds were removed from the pods. Yield was assessed at harvest, with seed weight standardized to 13% humidity. The average seed weight of each treatment group was determined. The samples were stored in dry conditions (45%–50% RH) at 10°C until the analyses. For the CT imaging, 100 randomly selected seeds (representative of the sample) were prepared per treatment. Each single seed was placed in a separate hole of a special sample holder plate. A sample holder was used to secure and hold the seeds in a nonoverlapping position. For the image analysis, an appropriate amount of random seeds were selected to gaplessly cover a predefined area (5 × 5 cm) for each treatment group.

CT‐imaging and post‐processing

CT data were acquired on a Siemens Somatom Definition AS+ (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) scanner using the following settings: tube voltage 120 kV, current 200 mAs, spiral data collection with pitch factor .7, slice thickness .6 mm, reconstruction kernel V80u. The groups of samples were arranged in four grid‐like sample holders made from low radiodensity material and stacked on each other on the vertical axis. The maximum capacity of a sample holder was 108 seeds (9 × 12); thus, all the samples (100 seeds) from a given treatment group could be placed in a single sample holder. The reconstructed image containing all the samples has resulted in an image of 512 × 512 × 700 voxels with an isometric voxel size of .2 × 0.2 × 0.2 mm using Syngo CT software (version “VA48A”). Figure 2. shows the CT‐based 3D‐rendered images of the N. viridula damaged and intact soybean seeds.

Computer tomography‐based 3D‐rendered images of N. viridula damaged (left) and intact (right) soybean seeds L: left; R: right; I: interior; S: superior.

The image was processed using custom‐made software implemented in Python using publicly available and in‐house packages and algorithms. Firstly, the four stacked grids were segmented using automatic thresholding and morphological operators. Afterwards, a hard threshold at −700 Hounsfield Unit (HU) was applied on each grid of samples followed by morphological cleaning and labelling. Finally, convex hulls were calculated using the “N‐dimensional Quickhull algorithm” (Barber et al., 1996). Binary mask used later on called as “convex mask” denotes the set of the voxels inside the convex hull.

Using the “original” masks, the approximated volume in mm3 and HU scaled radiodensity of each sample was calculated.

The structural changes and internal integrity of each individual sample were assessed by the calculation of “solidity.” Solidity is a dimensionless measure, calculated by the volume ratio of the original mask's volume and the volume of its convex mask (Noor et al., 2012).

In this sense, a lower value of solidity indicates more concave structures on the seed (e.g., wrinkles and dimples). Method of calculating solidity shown in Figure 3.

Image color analysis

HSV is a cylindrical‐coordinate color model, which can be considered as a transformation of the Cartesian RGB color model into a cylindrical space. In this new space, the color information is encoded by the angular dimension of the Hue value, where 0° represents the red primary, 120° the green primary, and 240° the blue one, while at 360° the space is wrapped back to the red primary. Hue is a well‐established term used in perceptual vision physiology and represents the degree of sensation that an object appears to be similar to one of the perceived colors (red, yellow, green, blue) or a certain combination of any consecutive pairs (Fairchild, 2013). This way the fundamental color information can be represented by a single value, which simplifies the color analysis and permits the use of conventional statistical models.

Seeds harvested from the four groups were photographed on a single image under standardized lighting conditions (Mars Hydro TS1000, LG‐LED Solutions Limited, Shenzhen, China) with a Nikon DSLR camera (Nikon D3400, Nikon AF‐P DX 18–55 mm lens, NIKON Inc., Tokyo, Japan). Four new images were extracted from the image encompassing the seeds of the four groups as separate images with identical dimensions using GIMP (version 2.10.28). Images were fed to a custom software implemented in Jupyter notebook utilizing numpy (version 1.24.3) and opencv‐python (version 4.8.1) packages. For each of the input images, the software extracted 100 equally sized image parts and calculated the average H, S, and V values of all subparts resulting in 100 Hue values per input image. These Hue values were fed into statistical analysis. Additionally, a representative figure was prepared in RGB color model (cube) to aid the inter‐group visualization and interpretation of color differences.

Statistical analysis

The yield was expressed as the percentage of the calculated yield in the intact samples group. Relative yield was estimated from 20 observations/sample, using 100 randomly selected seeds in each observation.

All analyses were performed with SPSS (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). A Kruskal–Wallis H test was used to determine if there were differences in radiodensity, kernel volume, or solidity between four groups of kernels with different treatments: the nat.rand., nat.apic., regul.and intact samples. Distributions were similar for all groups, as assessed by visual inspection of a boxplot.

A Kruskal–Wallis H test was conducted to determine if there were differences in Hue values between samples that differed in their treatment: the nat.rand., nat.apic., regul., and intact samples. Distributions of Hue values were similar for all samples, as assessed by visual inspection of a boxplot.

DISCUSSION

Soybean has a major role in human nutrition, animal feed, and industrial uses, as such, its quality characteristics are obviously important. The quality of the harvested crop depends, among many other factors, on the degree of stinkbug damage, which in our experience can be detected by noninvasive methods such as CT imaging and image color analysis. The advantages of these methods are being fast, easy to automate, labor efficient and provide information without destroying the sample. In our study, we observed differences in harvestable yield, parameters measured by CT imaging, and harvested crop color due to stinkbug damage.

In case of soybean exposed to natural field damage, samples taken from the top 15 pods show a greater yield reduction than samples taken from the whole plant (39% and 24%, respectively). The reason for the different observed yield loss is probably due to the fact that the development of the lower pods starts earlier. During the period when the lower pods are susceptible to damage—the period of pod development and filling—the natural stinkbug populations are small. Pod‐filling of the upper pods, on the other hand, occurs during the period when the stinkbug population builds up so that the damage caused by a larger number of pests results in greater yield loss in the upper pods.

The 68% yield loss in case of seeds collected from isolated plants after regulated (six pests/15 pods) damage is exceptionally high and can be explained by the extremely high number of pests. This is well above the economic threshold (regardless of geographic location), and such pest numbers should not occur in soybean fields with adequate crop protection (Bueno et al., 2015; Musser et al., 2011). Yield loss caused by N. viridula can be highly variable, depending on several factors such as the number of pests and the time of damage. As Brier and Rogers reported, yield loss caused by N. vidula in soy during pod fill is 3.74–10.10 g/bug (Brier & Rogers, 1991).

The median radiodensity was significantly reduced in all three damaged samples (nat.apic, nat.rand., and regul.) compared to the intact sample, as shown in Table 1. The decrease was similar for samples collected from plants exposed to natural damage (nat.apic and nat.rand., 49.48HU, and 64.82 HU, respectively), while samples exposed to regulated damage showed a more pronounced reduction (412.78 HU). Soybean seeds have suffered significant damage from stinkbugs, since the damage occurred during pod‐filling; the seeds were unable to develop properly, resulting in less dense tissues, in accordance with earlier reports (Miner, 1966; Todd & Turnipseed, 1974). We found that the increase in damage was accompanied by a decrease in seed radiodensity and its standard deviation, as shown in Figure 5. It can be said that the undamaged seeds have higher radiodensity values and higher standard deviation of radiodensity. During proper development of the seeds, they develop characteristic structures, which, due to their tissue diversity, are characterized by different radiodensity values. The lowest values are found in severely damaged, undeveloped, and poorly structured, flat seeds.

Volume decreased similarly to seed radiodensity (25%–80% decrease), with a threefold reduction for samples exposed to regulated damage (6 N. viridula/15 pods) compared to natural damages (nat.apic. and nat.rand.), as shown in Table 1. Seeds did not reach their potential mature size, because the bug damage occurred in early endosperm formation. As a result of stinkbug damage during the pod‐filling stage, seeds usually fail to grow to full size and become deformed and shriveled (Brier & Rogers, 1991; Nair et al., 2018).

Our scientific approach should be highlighted in case of solidity. The use of this parameter has proved to be very useful in classifying damaged seeds. It effectively complements the conventional quantitative parameters measured by CT (e.g. volume, radiodensity, minor, and major axis lengths) with a value characterizing the distortion of the seeds. The samples exposed to natural damage (nat.apic and nat.rand.) showed a significant but minor reduction in solidity, while samples exposed to regulated damage showed a prominent decrease (~12%), as shown in Table 1. The shape changes, deformation and tissue death are the consequences of stinkbug damage as described earlier (Schaefer & Panizzi, 2000). By looking at the distribution of the radiodensity and solidity, we can see that besides the low radiodensity values—typical of damaged samples—the solidity values are also low, as shown in Figure 6. A decrease in solidity values reflects a higher degree of distortion of the seeds. The intact sample has the highest solidity values, with a value close to 1, which would be characteristic of a mostly convex soybean seed.

Regarding the image color analysis, there was a significant difference in Hue values between the samples. The subsequent posttests revealed that most of the samples differed in median Hue values, as shown in Figure 7. The higher median Hue for the nat.rand. sample is probably due to the inclusion of “older” pods from the base of the plants, while the other three samples (nat.apic., regul., intact) were all gathered from the “younger,” apical 15 pods. The results are consistent with the literature, namely, that despite the pronounced differences between most of the examined groups, color analysis alone is not sufficient to achieve 100% discrimination accuracy (Ahmad et al., 1999). The relationship between the degree of color change and the severity of stinkbug damage has not been investigated yet. Prior data in the literature suggest that severe damage is accompanied by severe discoloration (Todd & Turnipseed, 1974). However, even mild damage can appear as minor discoloration or spots on soybeans. In late damage—on fully developed soybean seeds—staining and discoloration are the main (and sometimes the only) external manifestations of bug damage (Sosa‐Gómez et al., 2020; Todd & Turnipseed, 1974).

The identification of these external symptoms of stinkbug damage is highly important, as they indicate internal, compositional, and therefore qualitative changes in the seeds. Although our experimental work does not expose compositional changes in seeds caused by stinkbug damage, it is important to highlight that changes in nutrient content occur in most cases. Based on the available literature regarding the effects of stinkbug damage, their feeding on soybeans can reduce the average weight of the seeds and thus the amount of harvestable crop (Daugherty et al., 1964). Some sources report a reduction in oil content and an increase in protein content (Miner, 1961; Thomas et al., 1974; Todd, 1981; Todd & Turnipseed, 1974). Other sources report no significant relationship between stinkbug damage and protein or oil content (Miner, 1961). Changes in fatty acid composition due to stinkbug damage have also been detected in soy (Todd, 1981; Todd et al., 1973). In the case of pods punctured during early endosperm formation, the loss of quality is clear, the amount of stored material is radically reduced, which may result in unfilled pods with severely atrophied, flat seeds (Todd & Turnipseed, 1974). However, less severe forms of damage also show a change in the internal contents, according to the literature (Daugherty et al., 1964).

The results obtained in our experiment may be directly applied in practice. Color analysis of images taken under standardized conditions using our methods can be a simple, rapid method for detecting damaged lots. It is also suitable for identifying crops severely damaged by stinkbugs and for detecting damage with a milder degree of color change. Our finding that it is possible to isolate different levels of stinkbug damage by analyzing the colors could provide a basis for the future development of intelligent automated grain grading systems. It is important to add that proper sampling can be crucial for the success of the method. Indeed, comparing samples from different parts of the plant can be misleading (as observed in our study).

The phytosanitary situation (degree of yield loss, discoloration, etc.) studied only reflects the given injuries of our experimental field circumstances. Of course, the extent of the phytosanitary consequences in the plant is largely influenced by the phenological stage of the host plant and the time of infestation.

CT‐based diagnostics for soybean stinkbug damage is an advanced and accurate method for identifying damage with changes in shape, size, and radiodensity. It can thus be used for postharvest damage detection with high efficiency. With further testing, it may also offer a perspective in the detection of damage associated with loss of germination capacity.
