Preliminary effects of intermittent theta burst stimulation combined with virtual reality-based cycling training on upper extremity function in children with cerebral palsy: a double-blind randomized crossover trial
Authors: I-Chun Chen, Chia-Ling Chen, Hsieh‑Ching Chen, I-Jun Chou, Rou-Shayn Chen, Chia-Ying Chung, Katie Pei-Hsuan Wu, Keh-Chung Lin, Ching-Yi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral palsy is a permanent disorder that affects motor and posture control, significantly impacting the motor function, daily activities, and participation of affected individuals. Virtual reality-based training and repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation have each demonstrated benefits for cerebral palsy rehabilitation, but their combined effects remain largely unexplored. This randomized controlled crossover trial examined the potential effects of intermittent theta burst stimulation, a patterned form of repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, on virtual reality-based cycling training designed to improve upper extremity function in children with spastic cerebral palsy.MethodsTwelve participants were enrolled and randomized, with eight completing two sequential intervention phases in a counterbalanced order: real stimulation combined with virtual cycling training (n = 10 conditions) and sham stimulation combined with virtual cycling training (n = 10 conditions). The virtual cycling training program included twelve sixty-minute sessions delivered two to three times per week, and stimulation focused on the hand motor region of the involved hemisphere. The outcome measures included the Box and Block Test, the Bruininks-Oseretsky Test of Motor Proficiency, Pediatric Motor Activity Log, Caregiver Functional Use Survey, and Goal Attainment Scales to assess manual dexterity, activity performance, caregiver-reported functional use, and personalized goal attainment, both before and after the intervention.ResultsLinear mixed-effects models revealed a significant treatment effect for Goal Attainment Scales scores only (F = 7.474, p = 0.019), with intermittent theta burst stimulation yielding superior results. No significant period, sequence or treatment effects were observed for other outcomes. Between-condition comparisons confirmed a significant difference in Goal Attainment Scales scores (mean difference = 12.63, 95% CI [3.26, 22.08], p < 0.05, Cohen’s d = 1.30), while all other measures showed non-significant differences with small effect sizes.ConclusionsThese preliminary findings suggest that a combination of intermittent theta burst stimulation and virtual reality-based cycling training may modestly improve goal-directed upper-extremity function in children with spastic cerebral palsy. However, given the exploratory design and limited sample size, larger studies are required to confirm these findings and establish clinical efficacy.Trial registrationThis trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04101994) on 22 September 2019. Participant recruitment began on 29 March 2019. Background

Cerebral palsy (CP) refers to a neurodevelopmental syndrome characterized by persistent dysfunction in motor and posture control, arising from a static insult in the evolving fetal or infant brain. The motor impairment associated with CP frequently accompanies sensory-perception, cognitive, communicative, and behavioral disturbances, as well as seizure disorders and comorbid musculoskeletal conditions [1, 2]. Notably, upper extremity (UE) dysfunction in individuals with CP is particularly common and is often attributed to inadequate trunk and shoulder coordination, along with disparities in muscle equilibrium between spastic and paralytic muscles. These impairments hinder the ability to perform UE-specific tasks, such as approaching, catching, holding, gripping and various forms of handling [3, 4], thereby imposing substantial restrictions on daily living activities [5]. Given the lifelong nature of CP, interventions that not only target motor function but also promote daily activities and participation are essential.

Recently, conventional neurorehabilitation techniques have been complemented by the advent of advanced assistive technologies, leading to novel approaches aimed at enhancing motor and functional outcomes in individuals with CP [6]. Virtual reality (VR) is described as digitally simulated circumstances that allow real-time interaction through computer-generated content. VR can be delivered in two formats: immersive, involving a head-wearable display, and nonimmersive, involving a desktop display. The impact of VR on individuals with CP is encouraging, as it has been demonstrated to increase interest and engagement in rehabilitation programs, thereby promoting active participation in treatment programs. Emerging evidence indicates that VR has potential as an adjunct therapy in CP management [7, 8]. For example, two well-designed randomized controlled trials employing a novel home-based virtual cycling training (VCT) program for lower limbs reported a significant effect on muscle strengthening and enhancing bone density in children with spastic CP [9, 10]. However, few studies have applied the VCT program for UE in children with CP.

In the clinical management of CP, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has emerged as a promising noninvasive neuromodulation technique [11]. By modulating cortical excitability, inhibition, and neuroplasticity [12], rTMS reflects a shift from pharmacological to circuit-based interventions [13]. Although more longitudinal research is needed [14], recent studies have shown that rTMS enhances motor function in children with CP and can be successfully combined with treadmill, gait, or virtual reality training [11, 12, 15, 16].

Theta burst stimulation (TBS), a rapid and efficient rTMS protocol, exerts both facilitatory and inhibitory effects on synaptic transmission [17]. Whereas continuous TBS (cTBS) tends toward inhibition with prolonged use, intermittent TBS (iTBS) promotes rapid facilitation [17]. According to the “interhemispheric competition” model, stroke-induced transcallosal inhibitory signals from the contralesional (nonaffected) hemisphere impede the restoration of motor function [18], whereas cortical hyperexcitability in the ipsilesional hemisphere gradually diminishes as time passes [19]. As a result, the effectiveness of cTBS during the chronic phase may be restricted. Conversely, during the chronic stroke stage, the activation of the ipsilesional motor cortex has been demonstrated to occur concurrently with the process of motor reconditioning [20], which justifies the stimulation of the ipsilesional primary motor cortex (M1). Given the similarities between CP and stroke as chronic neurological disorders, this study explored the underinvestigated potential of iTBS in treating UE dysfunction in CP patients.

According to earlier studies, iTBS can improve actual use of the afflicted limb and increase daily life involvement in stroke patients, hence augmenting the effects of VCT on UE function [21]. Nevertheless, this combined regimen has not yet been examined among children with CP. By delving into the enhanced effects of iTBS on VCT in children with spastic CP, the current study filled this knowledge gap. We hypothesized that, compared with the control, iTBS may enhance the efficacy of the VCT program on UE function. The rationale underlying this phenomenon is grounded in the complementary mechanisms of the two interventions: VCT targets peripheral motor recovery, whereas iTBS modulates cortical excitability at the central level.

Methods

Participants

The participants in the present study were recruited from the Department of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation at a tertiary medical center. All participants were children diagnosed with spastic CP and classified according to the Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS) levels I-II [22]. Eligible participants were aged 4–20 years, in the prepubertal phase, capable of independent ambulation, able to complete motor function assessments, and had sufficient cognitive ability to understand and follow instructions throughout the examination procedure. Children with certain health issues were excluded from the study. These issues included chromosomal abnormalities, progressive neurological disorders, and significant comorbidities unrelated to CP. Additional exclusion criteria included active clinical conditions, such as infection, major surgery, nerve blocks, hormonal dysregulation, or an inability to perform the task or cooperate during the assessment. This investigation was carried out in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Board of the Chang Gung Medical Foundation approved this study. (No: 201701518A0). This trial is registered under ClinicalTrials.gov ID No. NCT04101994. The parents or legal guardians of all participants, and the participants themselves when appropriate, provided written informed consent prior to participation in this study.

Experimental design and procedure

This study was designed as a prospective, double-blind, randomized controlled crossover pilot trial. As illustrated in Fig. 1, participant recruitment and allocation followed a structured process detailed in a CONSORT-style flow diagram. Among the 223 screened children with CP, 211 were excluded on the basis of predefined eligibility criteria, and 12 participants were enrolled. The participants were randomly allocated to one of two treatment sequences, with randomization conducted via an online tool (https://www.randomizer.org/). Sequence A involved active iTBS coupled with VCT in the first phase, followed by sham iTBS combined with VCT in the second phase. Sequence B began with sham iTBS and VCT in Phase 1, followed by active iTBS and VCT in Phase 2.

The experimental design comprised 12 treatment sessions per intervention phase, with a three-month washout period implemented before switching to the alternative treatment. Outcome evaluations were performed at four predefined time points: T0 (baseline prior to Phase 1), T1 (immediately after Phase 1), T2 (Phase 2 pre-intervention baseline following the washout interval) and T3 (immediately after Phase 2). Notably, assessments at T2 served as the pre-intervention reference point for Phase 2, and the potential for lingering residual effects from the Phase 1 intervention should be considered.

This study adopted a crossover design comprising two intervention phases separated by a three-month washout period. At baseline, 12 children were enrolled and equally allocated to the two treatment sequences (n = 6 per sequence). However, after the first phase, two participants from each sequence withdrew, resulting in four individuals completing the crossover transition in each sequence. Ultimately, a total of 20 evaluable intervention courses were obtained from the 12 participants who were randomized (10 for active iTBS + VCT and 10 for sham iTBS + VCT), which included 16 courses from the 8 participants who completed both phases and 4 courses from the 4 participants who withdrew after Phase 1. All participants continued their usual rehabilitation programs during the washout period.

Participants, caregivers, and outcome assessors were blinded to treatment allocation throughout the study. The iTBS operator was not blinded due to the technical requirements of intervention delivery; however, the operator was not involved in outcome assessment, the Goal Attainment Scale (GAS) goal setting, or data analysis. No formal evaluation of the integrity of the blind was conducted.

All the participating children underwent outcome measures both before and after the intervention. The project leader (C.L. Chen) conducted the baseline diagnosis and evaluated motor severity, including the GMFCS and Manual Ability Classification System (MACS) [23], but was not involved in group allocation. The demographic and clinical data of the participating children were meticulously documented.

Figure 2 shows a schematic overview of the experimental procedures. Each child received either real iTBS or sham stimulation on the same day, preceding the commencement of the VCT program for 12 consecutive sessions. To mitigate the possible interference of physical activity on the effects of iTBS, children with CP and their parents were explicitly instructed to abstain from any physical activity five minutes prior to, during, and five minutes subsequent to brain stimulation. In an effort to reinforce the effects of iTBS, we made every attempt to minimize physical activity during the time interval between iTBS and VCT. The participating children were subsequently relocated from the iTBS stimulation site to the VCT site, with an approximate distance of 2 m separating the two locations. The VCT training program was initiated immediately after the VCT setup was completed and the patients’ vital signs were verified. On average, the time elapsed between the end of iTBS and the start of VCT training was approximately 10 min. Upon the completion of the therapy, all the participating children were evaluated.

Standardization of assessments and interventions

To ensure consistency and minimize bias, the intervention was administered by trained researchers who were not involved in the outcome assessments. The assessment of outcome measures was conducted by physical or occupational therapists who had not previously interacted with the participants and were unaware of the group allocation. These evaluators interacted with participants exclusively during the course of the assessments. Prior to the study, all the evaluators underwent a structured training program. This program included a written examination and a reliability assessment. The intra- and interrater reliability of the scale were evaluated on the basis of data from 10 patients who were assessed at two time points, seven days apart. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated for all outcome measures, which demonstrated excellent reliability (ICCs > 0.98).

Both evaluators and intervention operators received comprehensive training and competency testing. The training of the evaluators focused on ensuring the precision of the assessments. This training was overseen by the principal investigator, in conjunction with a senior physical or occupational therapist who had obtained certification. Similarly, intervention operators were trained in standardized device operation and stimulation protocols under the supervision of the principal investigator and a senior neurologist. This rigorous training protocol ensured the standardization and fidelity of both assessment and intervention procedures across all participants.

Determination of the active and resting motor thresholds

The participants were instructed to sit comfortably with their forearms resting on the desk in a pronated position, and they were told to keep their bodies relaxed throughout the entire procedure. The TMS was utilized to determine the resting motor threshold (RMT) and active motor threshold (AMT) via a MagPro X100 stimulator (MagVenture, USA) fitted with a figure-eight coil (outer diameter of each wing: 7.5 cm). Surface silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) disc electrodes were strategically positioned over the first dorsal interosseous (FDI) muscle of the involved UE. The positioning of the TMS coil was tangential to the scalp over the primary motor cortex corresponding to the affected limb. The handle was set at a 45° angle posteriorly with respect to the sagittal plane to generate posterior-to-anterior magnetic field flow. The motor “hot spot” was identified as the scalp location that elicited the maximum motor evoked potential (MEP) amplitude in the FDI. If no MEP could be elicited on the affected hemisphere, the hot spot was identified from the mirror site of the motor hot spot on the contralateral (unaffected) hemisphere as a reference [21, 24]. RMT was defined as the lowest stimulation intensity capable of eliciting an MEP with a peak-to-peak amplitude ≥ 50 µV in at least 5 out of 10 consecutive trials with the FDI at rest. The application of AMT was delineated as the lowest intensity required to elicit an MEP of ≥ 200 µV in a minimum of 5 out of 10 successive trials, contingent upon the occurrence of a minor voluntary contraction of the FDI (10–20% of maximal effort).

The motor hotspot localization procedure was conducted over the primary motor cortex, which corresponds to the affected upper limb. In cases where a MEP could be elicited from the affected hemisphere, the location of the hotspot and the intensity of the stimulation were determined using standard procedures based either on the RMT or AMT.

For participants with undetectable MEPs in the affected hemisphere, the stimulation site was determined by mirroring the motor hotspot identified in the contralateral hemisphere. Because AMT and RMT require measurable MEP responses, these thresholds could not be directly determined in MEP-negative participants. In the participant in whom no MEP could be elicited even at maximum stimulator output, the RMT was operationally defined as 100% maximum stimulator output for dosing purposes. Therefore, iTBS was delivered at 70% of this operational RMT, corresponding to 70% maximum stimulator output. This dosing approach was used only for the MEP-negative participant and was adjusted within safety and tolerability limits. In the present study, the number of children who exhibited a recordable affected-hemisphere MEP at baseline was 11 out of 12, and only one single MEP-negative case was identified.

This approach is frequently used when direct motor mapping is not a valid option However, it may not accurately reflect the functional motor representation in children with CP, particularly given the heterogeneity of corticospinal organization in this population.

rTMS with an iTBS protocol

The iTBS, a variant of rTMS, has the potential to offer equivalent or even greater efficacy [17]. The iTBS is applied to the affected primary motor cortex. A figure-eight coil was positioned over the motor hot spot corresponding to the FDI muscle, as identified through standard motor mapping procedures. True iTBS was delivered to the primary motor cortex of the affected hemisphere at an intensity of 80% of the AMT or 70% of the RMT, which is consistent with established safety and efficacy guidelines. These stimulation parameters were selected to optimize cortical excitability while maintaining participant comfort and safety. As demonstrated in a preceding study [21, 24], sham stimulation was delivered at the same location using an identical flip coil. This resulted in an output of 78% of that elicited by the non-flip side at a lower intensity (60% of the AMT), equal to 46.8% of the AMT. It is evident from the preceding study [25] that the sham stimulation, with an intensity lower than 70% AMT, exerts no influence on MEPs. However, it has been shown that this type of stimulation generates sensations and sounds that are indistinguishable from those produced by real stimulation. A number of preceding studies have employed a comparable sham stimulation method [26, 27].

Each iTBS cycle comprised a 2-second burst at 5 Hz, followed by an 8-second interval of rest. Each burst comprised three pulses at a frequency of 50 Hz, yielding a total of 600 pulses per session, which spanned a duration of 200 s. Studies on spaced TBS [28, 29] have produced results that align with this protocol. The patients received instructions for ongoing plasticity processes [30]. The intervention program consisted of 12 sessions, during which participants received either real or sham iTBS prior to VCT training.

Virtual cycling training program

This study is the first to implement a VCT program targeting the UE in children with CP, although VCT targeting the lower limb has been shown to increase lower limb muscle strength [9]. Research has focused on UE VCT in stroke patients and has supported its therapeutic benefits [21]. On the basis of this rationale, the current study applied a VCT program specifically designed for bilateral UE rehabilitation.

The VR-based UE cycling system was developed by Dr. Hsieh-Ching Chen through the innovative integration of VR with an arm cycle (BK0010, X - BIKE Fitness Technology Company Limited). The setup of the VCT program is presented in Fig. 3. During the training period, the participants were immersed in VR in a virtual environment designed to simulate real-world cycling experiences, creating the perception of navigating various road terrains while actively controlling the movement of a virtual bicycle through arm cycling. The visual speed of the virtual environment was dynamically synchronized with real-time input from the cycling speed sensor, allowing the onscreen motion to reflect the participant’s effort. This interactive feedback mechanism not only heightened engagement but also significantly enhanced the motivation of the participating children.

The VCT program involves 60-minute daily sessions, which are administered 2–3 days per week, resulting in a total of 12 training sessions. The training process comprises a 5-minute stretching warm-up, 5 min of resistance training (2 × 10 repetitions, 2-min rest), and 40 min of core virtual arm cycling. The core virtual arm cycling encompassed 5 min of warm-up, 10 min intervals with high-resistance cycling, 20 min of continuous high-cadence cycling (low to moderate resistance), and 5 min of cool-down.

The primary objective of resistance adjustment in endurance training is to ensure that subjects can exert effort during training and reach their target heart rate (THR). The THR was established at 60–80% of the heart rate reserve, as calculated by the Karvonen formula [31], with resistance adjusted individually to maintain cadence and THR. The Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE, 1–10) [32] was recorded, and heart rate, oxygen saturation, and blood pressure were continuously monitored to ensure safety. During the training period, the subjects received verbal encouragement to increase their cadence, with the objective of reaching the THR. They are also instructed to maintain both cadence and heart rate within a specific range.

Outcome measures

Outcome measures were implemented at the pretreatment and posttreatment phases. The measures in evidence were grounded in the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health framework. The assessments encompassed key domains, including body function, activity and participation. All outcome measures used in this study were previously developed and published by other researchers and were adopted for use in the present study, with appropriate references provided.

The outcome measures for body function were the Box and Block Test (BBT) [33], and one item (making dots in circles) of the Bruininks-Oseretsky Test of Motor Proficiency (BOTMP) [34], and a grip strength measurement. BBT [33] serves as a functional assessment tool developed to measure unilateral gross manual dexterity. The item “formation of dots in a circle task,” selected from the BOTMP [34], is used to assess fine motor speed and hand dexterity. Grip strength was measured as an additional indicator of UE muscular function.

The Pediatric Motor Activity Log (PMAL) [35] is a structured, parent-reported interview assessing UE function in children with CP across 22 daily tasks. Two subscales, amount of use and quality of movement, capture the frequency and effectiveness of affected UE use in daily life. Similarly, the Caregiver Functional Use Survey (CFUS) [36] is also a parent-rated tool, that focuses on the frequency of use and quality of movement of the most affected UE, particularly during bimanual tasks. In essence, the PMAL emphasizes unimanual tasks, whereas the CFUS prioritizes bimanual tasks. Higher scores on both scales indicate better functional ability. The GAS [37–39] was used to evaluate individualized treatment outcomes and has demonstrated high responsiveness in pediatric rehabilitation, including children with CP. GAS goals were established prior to randomization through a collaborative process involving the evaluators, participants, and caregivers, all of whom were blinded to treatment allocation at the time of goal setting. The individualized goals primarily targeted daily UE functional activities, such as writing, feeding, or object manipulation. To ensure consistency across the crossover design, the same goals were retained for each participant throughout both intervention phases.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses described herein were executed in SPSS version 21 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants. Continuous variables (e.g., age, motor thresholds, and clinical outcomes) were expressed as means ± standard deviations (SD). Categorical variables (e.g., gender, CP subtype, and functional levels) were presented as frequencies and percentages (%). To ensure baseline comparability, a crossover design was employed, whereby each participant acted as their own control. The statistical analyses pertaining to the outcome measures were executed through the implementation of linear mixed-effects models, thereby facilitating the adequate accounting for the crossover design and the repeated measures within participants. The model was specified as follows for each outcome variable: The fixed effects incorporated in the analysis included the treatment condition (iTBS versus sham), the intervention period (Phase 1 versus Phase 2), and the treatment sequence. A random intercept was incorporated as a participant-level random effect. The baseline values of the corresponding outcome measure were incorporated as a covariate in the model. The estimated marginal means between-condition differences, 95% confidence intervals, and effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were reported. The evaluation of model assumptions was conducted through a meticulous examination of residual plots. A p-value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study initially included a total of twelve patients diagnosed with spastic CP who were selected to undergo the treatment protocol. However, due to hospital policy restrictions implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic, four participants were lost to follow-up (2 from each sequence), resulting in 8 participants who proceeded to the second crossover treatment phase (4 in the iTBS + VCT sequence and 4 in the sham iTBS + VCT sequence). All 8 participants completed the second phase intervention.

The cohort was balanced by gender, with six males and six females included in the study. The average age of the participants was 9.6 ± 4.7 years. Outcome measures

Linear mixed-effects models were employed to analyze the impact of treatment (iTBS + VCT vs. sham iTBS + VCT), period (Phase 1 vs. Phase 2), and sequence (treatment order) on the outcomes, while accounting for repeated measurements within participants. As demonstrated in Table 3, a substantial treatment effect was identified exclusively for GAS scores (F = 7.474, p = 0.019). This suggests that the iTBS condition produced a statistically significant improvement in GAS scores compared to the sham condition. No significant treatment effects were identified for BBT, BOTMP, grip strength, CFUS, or PMAL measures (all p > 0.05). No significant period effects or sequence effects (i.e. carryover effects) were detected across outcomes (all p > 0.05). However, due to the small sample size, the study may not have had sufficient power to detect subtle carryover effects.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this exploratory clinical trial is the first to investigate the effects of iTBS combined with VCT on UE motor function in children with spastic CP. In the present study, active iTBS was found to be associated with greater improvements in GAS scores in comparison to sham stimulation. However, no significant between-condition differences were observed in changes to other outcome measures, including the BBT, the BOTMP, grip strength, the PMAL, and the CFUS. These preliminary findings suggest that iTBS is a feasible treatment for pediatric CP, and that it may enhance the effects of VCT on personalized goal-directed function to a modest degree. The findings indicate that there is potential for iTBS to support the practical use of the affected UE in real-life contexts. However, these results should be interpreted with caution, particularly given the limited sample size.

The results suggest that iTBS may have a modest additive effect when combined with VCT, particularly for improving individualized goal-directed participation in children with CP. In this study, we observed a significant treatment effect for GAS scores, with significantly higher scores following iTBS + VCT compared with sham iTBS + VCT. Therefore, the iTBS + VCT condition yielded greater improvements in GAS than the sham condition. The GAS has been employed as a metric to evaluate specific interventions for patients with CP [40–42]. Individuals with CP frequently have a wide range of rehabilitation objectives that extend beyond the medical conception of disability, underscoring the necessity of considering a multitude of priorities for each individual [42]. Another study [41] demonstrated an efficacious method to assess and enhance motor capacity in children with CP during daily task participation, as reflected in goal attainment, and the GAS reliably detected changes in functional goals with personalized, meaningful goals for each child and family. This phenomenon may be attributed to the fact that, in addition to facilitating the training of the UE movement function through VCT exercises, iTBS may play a role in central neural modulation. This is achieved through targeted stimulation of the affected cerebral hemisphere, which may contribute to motor function in children with CP [43]. By applying repeated and precisely regulated stimulation to representative areas within the motor cortex, iTBS may generate a cumulative effect, which could potentially influence both local and distant brain activity. This mechanism may enable the fine-tuning of cortical excitability, facilitates reorganization of cortical functional regions, and modulate neuroplasticity of the cerebral cortex [16, 43]. Despite the utilization of disparate methodologies and instruments to assess efficacy, the current study demonstrated that this integrated approach, iTBS + VCT intervention, may hold promise for enhancing individualized goal-directed participation in neurorehabilitation for children with CP, although its impact on broader clinical participation requires further validation in larger cohorts.

Notably, no significant between-condition differences were observed for standardized motor outcomes other than GAS. One possible explanation is that the VCT paradigm primarily involved bilateral, repetitive, symmetric gross motor movements, which may preferentially enhance proximal UE stability, interlimb coordination, and general motor activation rather than directly improving distal hand dexterity or assisting-hand performance. Consequently, the transfer effect to fine motor outcomes such as BBT, BOTMP, grip strength, PMAL, and CFUS may have been limited. These findings suggest that the lack of significant standardized motor improvements may reflect limited task transfer between the intervention and the selected outcome measures, rather than simply absence of an iTBS effect. In unilateral CP, task-specific interventions directly targeting grasp, release, manipulation, and bimanual hand use may be more closely aligned with these functional outcomes. These observations align with evidence from existing systematic reviews, which has reported minimal effects of rTMS on unilateral hand dexterity when standard assessment tools are used [44, 45].

Regarding the washout period, we acknowledge that treatment effects in pediatric UE rehabilitation may persist for several months and that a three-month washout period may not eliminate all prior effects, particularly given that participants continued their usual therapies. However, linear mixed-effects model analysis revealed no significant period or sequence (carryover) effects. This suggests that any residual effects were minimal and did not systematically bias comparisons between conditions. Therefore, while we did not assume complete washout, we considered the crossover design acceptable for an exploratory comparison of the iTBS + VCT and sham conditions.

The sham stimulation protocol used in this study was adapted from previously validated adult TBS and stroke studies [21, 24] rather than from established pediatric CP protocols. We selected this approach because it provides comparable auditory and somatosensory stimulation while minimizing effective cortical modulation. However, it is important to note that the neurophysiological responses to sham stimulation can vary between adult and pediatric populations. This aspect should be taken into consideration when interpreting the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the present findings. Firstly, recruiting, retaining and adhering to the intervention was substantially more challenging during the pandemic, largely due to strict hospital policies, rigorous enrolment criteria and pandemic-related disruptions. The eligible sample was further restricted by participants’ limited cooperation or functional capacity, and the substantial time and financial commitments impacted parental motivation and study continuity. Secondly, this study lacked standardized, objective assessments of assisting-hand function that are specifically validated for pediatric CP, which may have reduced sensitivity for detecting subtle changes in motor performance. Thirdly, while no significant period or sequence effects were observed, suggesting minimal carryover, the three-month washout period was relatively brief, given the known persistence of motor gains in UE training for children with CP. Fourthly, the sham stimulation protocol was adapted from adult stroke studies rather than from established pediatric CP protocols. Finally, the relatively small final sample size led to a limited statistical power, particularly in the detection of small-to-moderate changes in standardized motor outcomes. Moreover, in view of the restricted sample size, corrections for multiple testing were not implemented across the various outcome measures. This may have led to an elevated risk of Type I error. Given these constraints, the present findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger samples, standardized pediatric-specific assessments, and appropriate washout intervals are needed to confirm these preliminary results.

Conclusion

In summary, this exploratory study highlights that rTMS—specifically iTBS—could be a potential adjunctive strategy for the management of CP. iTBS has the potential to enhance the effects of VCT in improving personalized, goal-directed UE function for children with spastic CP. Moreover, the proposed novel iTBS + VCT protocol is both feasible and safe, and practical for application in clinical settings among children affected by CP. iTBS holds potential as an integral component of future CP rehabilitation strategies. A comprehensive evaluation is imperative to ascertain the extent to which iTBS can be incorporated with therapeutic programs to optimize its efficacy. Future studies should prioritize the long-term effects of iTBS, and determine optimal treatment parameters, including factors such as intensity, frequency, duration, and patient population characteristics.
