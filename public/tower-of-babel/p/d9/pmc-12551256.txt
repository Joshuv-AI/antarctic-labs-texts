Fork from camping utensils as a wounded shell caused severe vascular injury in a combat patient injured in the war in Ukraine: a case report
Authors: Igor Lurin, Vitalii Makarov, Volodymyr Nehoduiko, Ivan Veryovkin, Maksym Malimonenko, Victor Orlov, Andrii Dinets
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe war in Ukraine is associated with severe injuries and challenges in the management of patients. There are various kinds of high-energy ballistic projectiles, but kitchen cutlery or camping utensils parts, such as forks, knives, or spoons, are not usually expected to be found in the gunshot wound, but might serve as a ballistic projectile causing severe damage to the human body. Vascular injury is also a challenge in war surgery, and the application of temporary arterial shunts is a good approach, as well as the application of damage control tactics. The aim of this study was to demonstrate the rare case of management of the combat patient who received a severe vascular injury to the femoral artery by fork from camping utensils after the strike by the KAB precision-guided munition, who was treated with temporary arterial shunts with application of DCR and DCS.Case presentationA 34-year-old Ukrainian soldier was wounded by a precision-guided munition (KAB) in East Ukraine. The injury resulted in severe trauma to the left lower extremity, including superficial femoral artery damage. Due to the persistent threat of drone attacks, the rapid evacuation to advanced medical facilities was delayed for 12 h. The Forward Surgical Team diagnosed the patient with arterial thrombosis of superficial femoral artery and a foreign metal mass, identified as a fork, which was a part of a camping utensil, embedded in the hip. Surgical intervention involved the removal of the foreign bodies and amputation below the upper third of the hip due to critical arterial ischemia. Background

Severe vascular injury is a very common trauma in armed conflicts and warfare, including the ongoing war in Ukraine [1]. The latter is associated with application of all kinds of low- and high-velocity weapons such as armed FPV-drones, multiple-launch rocket systems, as well as air precision-guided munition KAB, carrying 500–3000 kg of munition [2–7]. Taken together, these kinds of lethal weapons cause severe trauma, which includes amputations, thoraco-abdominal injuries, neurosurgical injuries, as well as severe vascular trauma [8–11]. Frequently, the ballistic and gunshot injuries are associated with multiple damage to various parts of the body, despite the body armor or other protective features. According to our observations, the frequency of the vascular injury in combat trauma constitutes 7.5-9%, which is high and associated with a high rate of lethal outcomes and a higher incidence of post-vascular injury, such as extremity amputation in case of critical limb ischemia. The distribution of the vascular injuries in the ongoing war in Ukraine, the following frequencies of the vascular trauma (unpublished data): 40–50% to the femoro-popliteal segment, 20–30% the tibial and upper arm segments. The ballistic and gunshot injuries to the femoral arteries are the most frequent vascular injuries, constituting up to 28% of all injuries to the vessels in combat settings in Ukraine. Other studies also report a high incidence of vascular injuries and a higher risk of critical limb ischemia [1, 8, 12–14].

The bullets or metal fragments from ballistic projectiles are a common cause of damage to the human body. Uncommonly, other objects might be involved in the damage process after the application of the gunshot weapons, FPV, or KAB. As shown mainly in the civil trauma reports, such traumatic objects might be kitchen cutlery (forks, knives, etc.), gardening tools, as well as camping utensils. The latter is frequently in use by military personnel in Ukraine in the area of the battlefield for food intake in tactical conditions. The camping utensils usually consisted of stainless steel folding detachable cutlery, a knife, a spoon, and a fork. These parts from the utensils might receive kinetic energy from high-energy weapons, breaking away the part of the camping utensil, which may serve as a ballistic projectile, causing severe damage to the human body. Also, ballistic damage to the human body by unusual objects such as kitchen cutlery or camping utensils is a rare event in both civil and combat conditions [7, 15]. The aim of this study was to demonstrate the rare case of management of the combat patient who received a severe vascular injury to the femoral artery by fork from camping utensils after the strike by the KAB precision-guided munition.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old male Caucasian soldier of the Armed Forces of Ukraine received a ballistic shelling injury from a precision-guided munition KAB deployed from an aircraft in the battlefield area in East Ukraine. The patient was admitted to a medical stabilization point (approx. 1–3 km from the frontline) 30 min after the injury, which is in line with the “golden hour” principle. At the stabilization point, the patient was diagnosed with severe damage to the left lower extremity with superficial femoral artery injury. The patient received temporary arterial shunting by using a soft polyvinylchloride tube for the left superficial femoral artery, followed by permanent heparinization by delivering a continuous and controlled dose of heparin using a volumetric infusion pump system (B. Braun Infusomat, Germany). Taking into consideration the permanent presence of russian armed FPV drones, hunting on military and sanitary transport, it was a high risk for the lives of patients and medics; therefore, it was not possible to arrange a rapid evacuation of the patient to the higher Role of medical care to prevent critical ischemia of the left lower extremity. The tactical conditions became appropriate 12 h after the injury, and the patient was evacuated to the Forward Surgical Team (FST) facility. FST performed examinations of the patient, including X-rays of the chest, abdomen, and extremities, as well as an ultrasound of the vessels in the lower extremities. During the wound revision by FST, a deep wounds were identified on the upper ⅓ of the left hip, measured 60 × 30 mm without signs of bleeding and 100 × 50 mm encompassing non-functioning arterial shunt. An acute arterial insufficiency was diagnosed in the left lower extremity and thrombosis of the arterial shunt.

To further evaluate the status of the patient, the blood tests were performed for clinical chemistry, coagulation; the clinical urinary test was also done by using laboratory machines Respons 920 (Germany), Lab Analyt (China), HumaClot Duo Plus (Germany), Labline 40, and Sunrise (Austria), equipped with Biorad і Biosan. Arterial ultrasound was performed using portable devices “Sonosite Micromaxx, 2017” with SonoCalc software to calculate the volume of injury. X-ray examination was performed using the machine “OPERA RT20, 2018”, which is designed to perform examinations in various patient positions, with direct contact or at a distance of up to 180 cm from the patient. This is an important feature of this X-ray machine, allowing it to minimize the need to move the patient’s body, which is important for severe trauma cases.

At physical examination, the ⅔ of the left lower extremity was cyanotic and cold. Ultrasound showed the absence of arterial blood supply below the level of the upper part of the ⅓ left hip, and the artery shunt was not functioning.

To our surprise, a preoperative X-ray of the pelvis revealed a foreign mass of metal density with the shape of a fork embedded with a round-shaped foreign body in the area of the upper 1/3 of the left hip (Fig. 1). Prior the surgery, the patient reported he kept the stainless steel folding camping utensils (camping detachable cutlery knife, spoon, and fork) in his left back pocket at the moment of the KAB strike. Therefore, the KAB fragment detached the fork from the cutlery tool, and these two served as wounding projectiles. The patient underwent surgery under general anesthesia. At revision, there were identified and removed a massive metal fragment from the KAB measuring 60 × 30 mm, and a metal camping fork of 130 х 30 mm (Fig. 2). The temporary arterial shunt was thrombosed, and the critical arterial ischemia of the left lower extremity was diagnosed. The amputation of the left lower extremity below the level of the upper ⅓ of the hip was performed. The left leg stump was not sutured because of the ballistic nature of trauma, which is in line with the current war surgery guideline in Ukraine. The patient was evacuated for further management from the FST (Role 2) to the deployed field hospital of the Role 4 (higher level of medical provision) for medical care after 2 days following the amputation and removal of the ballistic fragment and fork. The stitching of the left leg stump was performed on the 6th day after the amputation, followed by its removal on the 14th day. 21 days after the injury, the patient was discharged from the Role 4 deployed hospital for rehabilitation and prosthesis of the lower left extremity.

Discussion

In this publication, we demonstrated a case of a combat patient who was under strike by a high-velocity metal fragment from a KAB (a Russian precision-guided missile), whose trajectory was through the camping cutlery tool in the back pocket of the patient, resulting in the detachment of the fork from the tool, followed by injury of the left superficial femoral artery.

This case report is also demonstrated specific features of the medical provision in the modern conflict with wide presence of the tactical FPV drones, interfering with normal timing for evacuation of the patients to the appropriate Role of medical care, which was resulting in thrombosis of the temporary arterial shunt in our patient followed by critical ischemia and need to perform amputation of the lower left extremity.

The wounds from such unusual metal projectiles as cutlery knives, forks, stainless steel rods, etc., are unusual in combat settings, but are described in civilian patients. For instance, Mittal et al. showed a case with a fatal outcome of a factory worker who was injured by a stainless steel fragment while doing his work on a cutting machine [16]. In contrast, Marrackchi et al. presented a case of a civilian patient who survived after the attack by a kitchen knife to his skull, followed by the retained tip of the knife within his head for 15 days after the injury [17].

As we have shown previously, high-energy weapons are associated with severe injuries, including arterial trauma. Temporary arterial shunting is an effective approach to stabilize the combat patients with ballistic injuries, which is associated with good treatment outcomes in case of normal evacuation to the appropriate Role of deployed medical facility [18]. In contrast to Rogovskyi et al., and despite installing the temporary arterial shunt, we were not able to save the injured extremity from amputation because of the high risk for patient transportation due to frequent enemy attacks on sanitary transport by tactical FVP-drones [1]. Because of such tactical circumstances, we were not able to avoid leg amputation; however, it was possible to save the patient’s life, which is also a great achievement. As shown in other combat-related studies and cases from Ukraine, we also applied a damage control surgery approach, which is an excellent war medicine approach associated with good treatment outcomes, and we believe it also played an important role for our patient in this case report [2, 19–23].

It is also worth mentioning that unusual injuries from various garden or kitchen items are described in the head area. For example, a case of a patient with a retained garden fork to the head was published by Gonya et al. In that case report, an injury of the frontal area of the brain followed by craniectomy and removal of the fork by its trajectory and discharge of the patients in normal condition on the 6th day after the operation [24]. The unexpected features of the ballistic trauma were previously reported in our previous reports as well as by others in the studies of in civil and combat patients. For instance, some studies reported bullet embolism, unusual localization of the projectile in the body as well as unusual localization of inlet/outlet holes [3, 8, 10, 25, 26]. Also, one should be aware of possible non-firearm projectiles such as metal shards from explosive devices, or shrapnel as well as such secondary projectiles as parts of camping utensils, kitchen cutlery or even fragments from the body or other tissue that become projectiles, such as a bone fragment causing a fracture [27, 28]. Basically, any object at the trajectory of the high-energy ballistic wave may serve as a wounding shell.

Other publications and our experiment suggest surgical magnetic tools as a useful instrumental approach to fix and remove metal fragments from the artillery shelling, gunshot, or other ballistic injuries in both combat and civil settings [2, 29]. However, the large wound area the size of the fork was taken into consideration, and the latter was removed under the naked-eye control of the surgical team.

This case report and other studies support the overall opinion about the unusual and severe injuries of the combat patients, involving multiple sites of the body and frequently associated with problematic medical provision due to tactical or organizational issues, such as problematic planning of the healthcare system [3, 6, 9, 29, 30]. Our report is also in line with Prat et al., showing the higher frequency of lower extremity injuries was also reported in the epidemiological study evaluating data from the recent Israel-Gaza conflict [31].

Conclusions

The trajectory of a high-energy ballistic projectile might go through the various objects (e.g., cutlery forks, etc.), followed by their involvement in the injury process, and increase the severity of the wounds. Ongoing war in Ukraine is associated with the impossibility of wide use of the aeromedical evacuation due to tactical obstacles, resulting in prolongation of the time for patients’ evacuation to higher level of medical care and higher risk for extremity amputations in patients with vascular injury.
