Pathology Testing for Patients With Low Back Pain in Australian Emergency Departments
Authors: Claudia Côté‐Picard, Qiuzhe Chen, Hugo Massé‐Alarie, Peter Youssef, Michael Spies, Chris G Maher, Gustavo C Machado
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveWe aimed to describe the profile of patients with low back pain who received pathology testing in emergency departments or after inpatient admission, and to describe the ordered tests.MethodsA retrospective study of electronic medical records from three emergency departments in Sydney, New South Wales, Australia, from January 2016 to October 2021, was undertaken. We included patients diagnosed with a lumbar spine condition at discharge from the emergency department and extracted their demographic and episode of care characteristics.ResultsPathology tests were ordered in 23.8% of 15,300 episodes of care. Patients who received pathology testing were typically older, were female, had a non‐English preferred language, required an interpreter, arrived by ambulance during working hours, had their condition triaged as urgent, had a diagnosis of a serious low back pain pathology, were admitted to inpatient wards, and had an increased length of stay. Full blood count, electrolytes, urea, creatinine (EUC) and liver function tests were the most ordered tests.ConclusionsThe characteristics of patients with low back pain receiving pathology testing in emergency departments as well as those of the tests were described. The results suggest that guideline recommendations were partly followed, but an investigation into the appropriateness of pathology testing is needed to confirm this hypothesis and ensure the relevance of low back pain care in emergency departments.Keywords: blood tests, emergency departments, low back pain, urinalysis

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is a common reason for emergency department (ED) visits. A meta‐analysis of 21 studies from 12 countries estimated a 4.4% prevalence of LBP in the ED [1]. About 45% of ED presentations for LBP have diagnoses of a lumbar spine condition, and of those, most are non‐urgent [2]. The majority (85%) are non‐specific, 10% are radicular syndromes and only 5% are serious spinal pathologies (e.g., epidural abscess) [2].

Diagnostic work‐up including pathology testing and imaging should be reserved to patients for whom a serious pathology is suspected or when considering surgery [3]. Most LBP guidelines provide advice on imaging, but guidance on pathology testing is less common [4]. In Australia, the Colleges for Emergency Medicine and Pathology have recently released a joint guideline [5] and recommend ‘electrolytes, urea, creatinine’ (EUC), glucose, full blood count and dipstick urinalysis for admitted patients with non‐traumatic LBP. The Emergency Care Institute, Australia, recommends pathology testing when a specific lumbar spine pathology or a non‐spinal cause is suspected in acute LBP [6].

Lumbar imaging in patients with LBP has been well documented. A meta‐analysis including 16,552 LBP episodes estimated a 36% imaging rate in ED [7]. An Australian study of 649 patients with LBP in ED reported imaging overuse and underuse prevalences of ~9% and ~4%, respectively [8], whilst another with 1459 patients reported a 4.8% overuse rate [9]. In contrast, only a few studies described pathology testing in LBP management, and whether it is usually appropriate. The order rate for pathology testing in ED care of LBP was ~20% in studies from Canada and the US [10, 11], 44% in patients ≥ 65 years in Australian EDs [12] and 62.2% in a private Australian ED [13]. There remains a lack of evidence on the profile of patients with LBP who receive pathology testing in EDs, and on the characteristics of the tests. The aim of this study was to describe the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients with LBP who were ordered pathology tests in EDs and after inpatient admission in Australian hospitals and the types of pathology tests ordered.

Methods

Study Design, Setting and Population

This is a retrospective analysis of ED electronic medical record data of patients aged ≥ 18 years who presented to one of three public hospitals within the Sydney Local Health District, New South Wales, Australia (Royal Prince Alfred Hospital, Concord Repatriation General Hospital and Canterbury Hospital) from January 2016 to October 2021 and who received a SNOMED‐CT diagnosis related to a lumbar spine condition at ED discharge [14]. Patients who presented with LBP but received a non‐lumbar spine diagnosis at ED discharge were not included. The Sydney Local Health District (Royal Prince Alfred Hospital zone) Ethics Committee granted approval for this study (protocol no. X17‐0419). We followed the Reporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely‐collected Data (RECORD) guideline [15, 16].

Study Procedures

Electronic medical record data were obtained by the Sydney Local Health District Targeted Activity and Reporting System (STARS) [16].

Demographic and Episode of Care Characteristics

Demographic characteristics were age, sex, postcode, country of birth (Australia or overseas), preferred language (English or other) and whether the patient required an interpreter. Episode of care characteristics were the mode of arrival to ED (ambulance or others), presentation hour and day, ED triage category, diagnosis at ED discharge, whether there was a hospitalisation or a re‐presentation to the same ED within 28 days and ED length of stay (LOS).

Socioeconomic status was derived from the patient's postcode using the Australian Bureau of Statistics' Index of Relative Socioeconomic Advantage and Disadvantage (IRSAD) for Areas 2016 [17]. IRSAD were extracted as deciles and dichotomised to disadvantaged (1–5) or advantaged (6–10). ED visits from Monday to Friday between 8:00 and 17:00 were considered as being during working hours. ED clinicians triaged the patients using the Australasian Triage Scale [18], that ranges from 1 to 5, and we dichotomised it to urgent (1–3) and less urgent (4, 5). Lumbar spine diagnoses were grouped into two categories: non‐serious LBP, including non‐specific and radicular LBP, and LBP due to a serious spinal pathology (serious LBP). ED LOS was the time from ED arrival to discharge/hospital admission. When the LOS was > 4 h, it was categorised as extended. For 1926 LBP episodes, an admission was recorded but data was missing for hospital ward and admission date. We verified those who were also recorded in another data set that collected the same outcomes at the same hospitals, but from 2019 to 2024 (one third of the 1926 episodes), and noticed that they were solely admitted to ED short stay units (stays < 24 h). We thus considered all 1926 episodes as non‐admitted.

Pathology Tests

We extracted all pathology tests ordered at least once in the ED or after inpatient admission, and two rheumatologists from the team sorted them into categories. We however did not have the information on the tests that were performed during the screening by the nursing staff unless they were sent to the laboratory. We excluded variables that were groups of tests as the tests included in the groups were also recorded individually. We excluded variables recorded as pathology tests but that represented non‐test processes (e.g., ‘_POC RPA Billing’, ‘Haem Comment’, ‘Coagulation Comment’). The classification of pathology tests into categories and the excluded tests are reported in Table S1.

Outcomes of Interest

We wanted to examine (i) the proportion of LBP episodes where at least one pathology test was ordered, (ii) the demographic and clinical characteristics of LBP episodes who received pathology testing and of those who did not, (iii) the proportion of LBP episodes who were ordered the different pathology test categories, (iv) the 10 most frequently ordered pathology tests and (v) the distribution of the number of ordered pathology tests.

Data Analysis

We calculated the number of LBP episodes where a pathology test was ordered, with results stratified by demographic/clinical characteristics. Categorical variables are presented as frequency (%) and continuous variables as mean (SD). Multiple presentations for LBP by the same patient over the six‐year study period were analysed independently as different presentations. The analyses were conducted in Stata/BE 18.0.

Results

Patient Characteristics and Pathology Test Orders

This study included 15,300 LBP episodes of 11,521 patients who presented to one of three EDs and who received a discharge diagnosis related to the lumbar spine from January 2016 to October 2021. Patient and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. The sample included 7775 (50.8%) females, and the mean age was 52.9 years ± SD 20.1. Most diagnoses at ED discharge were non‐serious LBP (14,642 episodes; 95.7%).

At least one pathology test was ordered for 3634 of 15,300 LBP episodes (23.8%) (Table 1). The number of individual tests recorded was 261, and 38 were excluded (9 non‐tests and 29 groups of tests), leaving 223 tests included in the analyses. Patients who received a pathology test were older (65.6 years ± SD 19.3) and stayed longer in ED (6.1 h ± SD 4.1) than those who did not. Females were more likely to receive a pathology test than males (26.8% vs. 20.6%), as were patients with a preferred language other than English (29.3% vs. 21.9%) and those requiring an interpreter (38.0% vs. 22.4%). Those arriving by ambulance (40.1% vs. 15.7%) and those arriving during working hours (26.9% vs. 21.1%) were more likely to receive pathology testing. Those triaged as urgent (34.0% vs. 16.3%) and those who received a serious LBP diagnosis at ED discharge (45.3% vs. 22.8%) were more likely to receive pathology testing. Those admitted to hospital were almost always ordered pathology testing (89.7%). Pathology testing order rates were similar across socioeconomically advantaged (25.2%) and disadvantaged (24.2%) patients, and between patients whose country of birth was Australia (22.6%) and those who were born overseas (24.9%).

Pathology Test Characteristics

Biochemistry, haematology, immunology, coagulation and blood gas were the five most ordered test categories (Table 2). The 10 most ordered pathology tests were full blood count, EUC, liver function tests, C‐Reactive Protein, ‘calcium, magnesium and phosphate’ (CMP), glucose random, international normalised ratio (INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), coagulation studies (INR & APTT) and blood film review (Table 3). Figure 1 shows the percentage of LBP episodes per number of ordered tests for admitted and non‐admitted LBP episodes. For admitted episodes the mode was six tests (12.5% order rate) and the mode for non‐admitted episodes was 5 (1.7% order rate). Figure 2 shows the percentage of LBP episodes per number of ordered tests for serious and non‐serious LBP episodes. The mode was six for both serious and non‐serious LBP episodes (5.5% order rate for serious and 3.4% for non‐serious LBP episodes).

Discussion

Key Results

Nearly a quarter of LBP episodes in participating EDs resulted in pathology testing. Typically, patients who were ordered at least one pathology test were older, were female, had a non‐English preferred language, required an interpreter, arrived by ambulance during working hours, had their condition triaged as urgent, had a diagnosis of a serious LBP pathology at ED discharge, were admitted to inpatient wards and had an increased LOS. The most ordered tests were full blood count, EUC and liver function tests.

Strengths and Limitations

This study included real‐world data of a large patients' sample over a 6‐year period. It is, to our knowledge, the first study to comprehensively describe the profile of adult patients with LBP for whom pathology tests are ordered in EDs or after inpatient admission, as well as the types of tests ordered. This study presents some limitations. Notably, the data set did not comprise the competing provisional diagnoses or comorbidities a clinician may have been considering when ordering the tests. Also, we could not determine whether the tests for admitted episodes were ordered in the ED or after inpatient admission, as the data were pooled. These limitations impede on discussing the appropriateness of test ordering. Given the study's retrospective nature, misclassification bias might have occurred for some variables (e.g., admission status). Also, there is uncertainty regarding the generalisability of our findings to settings such as primary care, where serious LBP pathologies have lower prevalences [19]. Finally, we did not have the information on the tests performed during the screening by the nursing staff unless they were sent to the laboratory, so certain tests might have been missed [20].

Comparison With Existing Literature

Characteristics of Patients Receiving Pathology Test Orders

Our findings align with those of de Luca et al. [12]. They found older age, inpatient admission, serious pathology and ambulance arrival to increase the odds of pathology testing and the male sex and conditions triaged as less urgent to decrease the odds of testing in older patients with LBP in EDs [12]. Characteristics of patients who were ordered pathology testing in our study were also observed in patients with LBP who were ordered lumbar imaging in EDs in a study by Ferreira et al. [2]. Older age (≥ 65 years), ambulance arrival, presentation during working hours, urgent triage categories and a longer stay in ED increased the odds of any lumbar imaging, whereas the male sex and a diagnosis of a non‐serious LBP decreased the odds of lumbar imaging [2]. Chen et al. [21] examined healthcare use for culturally and linguistically diverse patients with LBP in Australian EDs. They found that patients requiring an interpreter and those having a non‐English preferred language were more likely to receive lumbar imaging [21], but after secondary analyses, language did not influence care delivery in patients with similar age [22]. It is possible that age also drove pathology testing in people with poorer English proficiency in our data, but further analyses are needed to confirm it.

Older adults are more likely to present to ED with a serious illness [23], and have generally more comorbidities and polypharmacy complicating their management [24]. This could explain that they were more likely to receive pathology testing in our study, and potentially reflects concordance with guidelines [6]. A large 7‐year study by Squire et al. [25] revealed that the majority of critically‐ill patients use ambulance transport to ED. It is possible that in patients using ambulance in our study, a higher proportion had a serious spinal pathology, explaining why it was a driver for diagnostic work‐up. The very high rate of pathology testing in admitted LBP episodes (89.6%), almost ninefolds that of their counterparts (10.0%), suggests guideline adherence [5] and is consistent with studies of both public and private Australian EDs [12, 13]. The higher proportion of pathology testing in serious (45.3%) compared to non‐serious (22.8%) LBP episodes suggests alignment with guidelines [6], but this should be confirmed with studies using provisional diagnoses at ED arrival.

Pathology Tests Characteristics

Our overall 23.8% rate of pathology test ordering closely resembles that of Nunn et al. (22.5%) [11]. Full blood count and EUC were each ordered for 23.6% and 23.5% of our included LBP episodes, respectively, which is higher than in Friedman et al. (9.7% and 5.2%, respectively) [10]. Both studies solely examined the tests ordered to patients in the ED, whereas we included pathology tests ordered in the ED and after inpatient admission [10, 11]. This could explain the differences with Friedman et al. [10], and might suggest that a lower overall proportion of pathology testing was observed in our study compared to that of Nunn et al. [11] A notable difference in our findings is that urinalysis was not in the top 10 most ordered tests, indicating that it was ordered for less than 3% of LBP episodes, whereas it was ordered for 21.9% of patients in Nunn et al. [11] and 18.8% in Friedman et al. [10]. A potential explanation is that urine dipstick might have been performed by nursing staff as a screening test at ED arrival and only recorded if the result was positive and a formal urinalysis was performed.

Full blood count and EUC were both ordered for 89%, random glucose for 37% and urinalysis for less than 13% of admitted episodes, partly following the guidelines as these tests are recommended for admitted patients with non‐traumatic LBP [5]. INR, APTT and coagulation studies were ordered for ~30% of admitted episodes, and liver function tests for 72%. This diverges from guidelines as they are generally not indicated [5]. Serious LBP episodes were ordered full blood count, INR, APTT, coagulation studies, EUC and liver function tests at higher rates than those with a non‐serious diagnosis. This suggests concordance with guidelines as these tests are recommended when a serious pathology is suspected [6]. Admitted or serious LBP episodes received a higher number of distinct pathology tests than their counterparts, also aligning with guidelines [3, 5, 6].

Conclusions

We described the profile of patients with LBP who received pathology testing in the ED and after inpatient admission. The increased proportion of pathology testing amongst admitted patients and those with a serious LBP pathology suggests concordance with guidelines. We, however, question the high ordering rates of liver function tests and coagulation studies and the very low rates of urinalysis for admitted patients, as these do not follow the recommendations [5]. Future studies should examine the appropriateness of pathology testing for patients with LBP in ED and other settings to ensure relevant management of LBP.
