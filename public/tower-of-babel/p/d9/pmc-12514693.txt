Intracranial vessel wall MRI in ischemic stroke in systemic lupus erythematosus: radiological characteristics and clinical associations
Authors: Xue Bai, Suqin Jin, Mingjun Xu, Runtong Zhang, Yan Li, Lijun Song, Qiang Shu, Dexin Yu, Qi Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe morphology of intracranial vessel wall lesions (VWLs) in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) has not yet been fully elucidated. This study aimed to explore the radiological characteristics of intracranial VWLs in SLE ischemic stroke patients and the relationship between these characteristics and clinical features using high-resolution vessel wall imaging (HRVWI).MethodsThis cross-sectional study evaluated the distribution and patterns of intracranial VWLs in SLE ischemic stroke patients, and individually age- and sex-matched control ischemic stroke patients without autoimmune diseases. A univariate analysis was performed to compare the clinical and radiological characteristics between the different patients and the VWL patterns. A follow-up study of HRVWI was carried out where data were available.ResultsA total of 30 SLE patients (9 males) with a mean [± standard deviation (SD)] age of 42.0±13.1 years were enrolled in the study. In total, 68 segment VWLs in 25 SLE patients were found. Concentric thickening and enhancement (CTE) (n=50) and eccentric thickening and enhancement (ETE) (n=18) were the two main VWL patterns. In total, 76 VWLs were found in the control group, all of which were ETE lesions. The percentage of culprit VWLs (90.0% vs. 88.9% vs. 69.7%, P=0.013) and the distribution of the VWLs in the internal carotid artery (ICA; 60.0% vs. 5.6% vs. 19.7%, P<0.001), middle cerebral artery (MCA; 18.0% vs. 61.1% vs. 52.6%, P<0.001), and vertebral artery (VA) (0% vs. 11.1% vs. 13.2%, P=0.013) differed significantly among the SLE-CTE, SLE-ETE, and control VWLs, mostly due to the difference between the SLE-CTE and control VWLs. The SLE patients with CTE patterns had a higher systemic lupus erythematosus disease activity index (SLEDAI) score (P=0.003), a lower percentage of lupus nephritis (LN) (P=0.015), and more VWL segments (P=0.010) than the SLE patients with ETE patterns. In total, 11 patients were followed up for 8.36±5.0 months. No new VWLs were found. Most VWLs (28/37) remained stable without significant improvement.ConclusionsHRVWI highlights the frequent occurrence of VWLs in large- and medium-sized intracranial arteries in SLE ischemic stroke patients. The two main VWL patterns were CTE and ETE lesions, which represented vasculitis and atherosclerosis, respectively, and which were associated with disease activity and LN. The VWLs rarely disappeared even in patients who received combined therapy for several months.Keywords: Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), ischemic stroke, magnetic resonance angiography (MRA)

Introduction

Ischemic stroke is a significant manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), resulting in a sustained reduction in health-related quality of life (1). Individuals with SLE have an approximately twofold higher risk of ischemic stroke than the general population (2,3). The etiology of SLE ischemic stroke is complicated and includes high disease activity, antiphospholipid antibodies (APLs), heart valve disease, systemic hypertension, and the early onset of atherosclerosis (4,5).

Vasculopathy plays a vital role in the pathogenesis of SLE ischemic stroke, which may involve vasculitis, atherosclerosis, and antiphospholipid syndrome (6,7). Previous neuropathologic studies have reported various small vessel changes in patients with SLE (8,9). However, the pathology of culprit stroke vessels, many of which are intracranial large and medium arteries (10,11), has rarely been examined due to the relative inaccessibility of these specimens.

With the development of radiologic techniques, high-resolution vessel wall imaging (HRVWI) has enabled the evaluation of large and medium intracranial vessel wall pathology (12), but it has rarely been applied to SLE cerebrovascular events (13). We hypothesized that the size of the lesioned intracranial vessels in SLE ischemic stroke patients would differ from the vessels of extracranial organs, which are always small (14,15). We also speculated that the patterns of the lesions may be diverse due to the heterogeneity of SLE. This study sought to describe the intracranial vessel wall in a consecutive cohort of SLE ischemic stroke patients using HRVWI to investigate the association between HRVWI patterns and clinical features, and to provide evidence for the management of SLE ischemic stroke. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2024-2541/rc).

Methods

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Committee of Qilu Hospital, Shandong University (approval No. KYLL-202011-072) and informed consent was obtained from all individual participants.

Study population

We prospectively and consecutively enrolled SLE patients with ischemic stroke admitted to the Qilu Hospital of Shandong University from January 2021 to August 2023. Patients were included in the study if they met the following inclusion criteria: (I) met the 1997 American College of Rheumatology criteria for the classification of SLE (16); and (II) had been diagnosed with ischemic stroke with current focal neurologic deficits presenting as an acute ischemic lesion on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in the corresponding vascular territory. Patients with unstable vital signs or creatinine clearance lower than 90 mL/min were excluded from the study.

The SLE patients were individually matched to control subjects based on age (within 5 years) and sex. The control subjects were drawn from patients with ischemic stroke but without autoimmune diseases who were admitted to the Qilu Hospital of Shandong University and underwent intracranial HRVWI examination.

Demographic, laboratory, and radiological characteristics

Standard data collection procedures were used to collect patient data, including data on any clinical manifestations of SLE, concomitant medical conditions (e.g., hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and hyperlipemia), laboratory test results (e.g., antinuclear antibodies, anti-ds DNA, and APLs), the administration of drugs, and radiological information (including MRI sequences and extracranial vessel ultrasound). The systemic lupus erythematosus disease activity index (SLEDAI) (17) was used to describe the activity of lupus. The Systemic Lupus International Collaborating Clinics/American College of Rheumatology Damage Index (SDI) (18) and the modified Rankin scale (mRS) were used to assess disability (19).

MRI protocol

All examinations were performed on a 3T MRI scanner (Megnetom Verio, Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) with a standard eight-channel head coil. The MRI protocol on enrollment and follow up comprised pre-gadolinium (Gd) and post-Gd MRI sequences performed in the following order: (I) brain T1-weighted imaging, T2-weighted imaging, T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery, and diffuse-weighted imaging following routine protocols for the clinical evaluation of stroke patients; (II) three dimensional (3D) time-of-flight (TOF) magnetic resonance angiography [MRA; repetition time (TR)/echo time (TE) =22 ms/3.6 ms; flip angle =18°; field of view (FOV) =220×220 mm2; acquired resolution =0.8×0.8×0.8 mm3]; (III) the 3D T1-weighted Sampling Perfection with Application-optimized Contrasts using different flip angle Evolution (SPACE) sequence (FOV read =206 mm; TR =700 ms; TE =29 ms; voxel size =0.8×0.8×0.8 mm3; matrix =256×256); (IV) the 3D T2-SPACE sequence (FOV read =206 mm; TR =2,000 ms; TE =140 ms; voxel size =0.8×0.8×0.8 mm3; matrix =256×256); and (V) the post-Gd 3D T1-SPACE sequence (which required the intravenous administration of 0.1 mmol/kg of gadobenate dimeglumine; the post-Gd images were acquired 5 minutes after gadobenate dimeglumine injection).

The TOF-MRA sequence centered in the Circle of Willis was mainly used to identify the location of stenotic arteries. All the HRVWI sequences were axially acquired centered on the Circle of Willis. This study evaluated C2 to C7 of the internal carotid artery (ICA) according to Bouthillier’s classification (20), the A1 and A2 segments of the anterior cerebral artery (ACA), the M1 to M3 segments of the middle cerebral artery (MCA), the V4 and V5 segments of the vertebral arteries (VAs), the basilar artery (BA), and the P1 and P2 segments of the posterior cerebral artery (PCA).

HRVWI analysis

Image reconstruction was conducted using the offline workstation (MMWP, Siemens) with 3D multiplanar reformation, and the images were analyzed by two experienced radiologists (D.Y. and X.B.), who were blinded to the clinical information of the participants. Any differences between the two readers were resolved by consensus. For the apparent vessel wall thickening identified on the axial source images, the pre- and post-contrast 3D T1-SPACE was reconstructed orthogonal to the short and long axis relative to the vessel flow direction, and the slice thickness was 0.8 mm throughout each thickened vessel segment. The following properties shown on the images were recorded: the location and characteristics of vessel wall thickening and enhancement, which were defined as concentric or eccentric according to the criteria described by Swartz et al. (21). Concentric thickening and enhancement (CTE) was defined as the width of the thinnest part at least 50% or more of the thickest part of the vessel wall segment. Eccentric thickening and enhancement (ETE) was defined as either clearly limited to one side of the vessel wall, or the thinnest part of the wall enhancement was estimated to be less than 50% of the thickest part. Every possible lesion was analyzed with caution to exclude false positive lesions caused by artifacts.

Vessel wall intensity was graded using a previously established grading scheme (22), referencing normal vessel wall and pituitary infundibulum on post-contrast HRVWI. Grade 0 indicated that the enhancement resembled or was less than that of the intracranial arterial walls without plaque in the same patient; grade 1 indicated that the enhancement was greater than that of grade 0 but less than that of the pituitary infundibulum; and grade 2 indicated that the enhancement was similar to or greater than that of the pituitary infundibulum.

A culprit VWL was defined as a lesion arising upstream to an infarction, while a non-culprit VWL was defined as a lesion occurring without an infarction in the corresponding territory (23). Lesions that emerged consecutively across ≥3 segments were defined as longitudinally extensive vessel wall lesions (LEVWLs).

Large-, medium-, and small-sized vessels were differentiated as de Boysson et al. described in a primary angiitis of central nervous system (PACNS) study (24). According to this definition, vessels detected by HRVWI were large and medium sized. Vessel-MASS software (Research Edition version 2014, Leiden University Medical Center, the Netherlands) was used to assess the degree of stenosis of each VWL. The vessel segment containing the entire VWL was analyzed.

3D post-Gd SPACE images were first reconstructed orthogonally to the vessel axis at 1.0-mm thick slices throughout each vessel segment with the VWL. The quantitative MRI measurements included the lumen area (LA) and outer wall area (OWA). The thickest part of the vessel wall of each VWL was selected, and measured successively for three slices (avoiding the lumen thrombus section), and the mean value was taken. The percentage of luminal area stenosis (LAS%) was calculated as follows: LAS% = (OWA − LA) / LA ×100%.

Follow-up

A follow-up HRVWI scan was performed at least 3 months after the first HRVWI examination if the SLE patient agreed. The image protocol was consistent with the first examination. The neurological symptoms, extracranial disease activity, treatment, and radiological information of these patients were documented.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The quantitative variables are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), or the median and interquartile range (IQR). The categorical variables are described as the percentage. Comparisons between groups were conducted using the t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, or one way analysis of variance for the quantitative data, and the Chi-squared test, Fisher exact test, or Mann-Whitney U test for the categorical data. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for comparisons between two groups. A P value <0.0125 was considered statistically significant for comparisons among three groups.

Results

Demographic and HRVWI characteristics of the SLE and control groups

A total of 30 SLE ischemic stroke patients of Han nationality (9 males) with a mean [± standard deviation (SD)] age of 42.0±13.1 years were enrolled in the study. No evidence of cardiogenic embolisms was found in any of the patients. One patient (Patient 4) was diagnosed with cryptococcus neoformans meningitis, which was considered the cause of the bilateral basal ganglia infarction. Three (10%) patients were diagnosed with SLE within 7 months of stroke, 11 (36.7%) were diagnosed with SLE and stroke simultaneously, and 16 (53.3%) suffered stroke 1–24 years after the diagnosis of SLE. Among the 16 patients who had previously had SLE, 5 (16.7%) had not taken any medicine for several months to years, 5 (16.7%) were only taking glucocorticoids before stroke, and 6 (20%) were taking both glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants before stroke. All the patients had already started the treatment for SLE and ischemic stroke (including glucocorticoids, hydroxychloroquine, antithrombotic drugs, with or without immunosuppressants, or statins, or antihypertensive drugs) before HRVWI imaging, and none of the patients had undergone cerebrovascular surgery.

Among the 30 patients in the control group, other than atherosclerosis, no other causes of ischemic stroke were found. Before the HRVWI examination, all the control patients were undergoing antiplatelet and lipid-lowering treatment with or without antihypertensive drugs, and no patient had undergone cerebrovascular surgery.

The SLE patients had a significantly lower fasting blood glucose (FBG) level than the controls (4.70±0.78 vs. 5.25±0.83 mmol/L, P=0.011). The percentage of hyperlipemia and the total cholesterol (TC) level were also lower in the SLE patients than the controls (20% vs. 50%, P=0.015; 3.82±1.23 vs. 4.44±0.95 mmol/L, P=0.034). There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of the other traditional risk factors (Table 1).

A total of 68 segments of vessel wall lesions (VWLs) in 25 SLE patients (50 CTE VWLs in 13 patients, and 18 ETE VWLs in 12 patients, accounting for 73.5% and 26.5% of the total VWLs, respectively), and 76 ETE VWLs in 30 control patients were found by HRVWI. The percentage of MCA VWLs was significantly higher in the controls than the SLE patients (83.3% vs. 46.7%, P=0.003). The VWL patterns also differed between the two groups (P<0.001). Meanwhile, as a specific vessel wall finding, dissection was observed in one SLE patient with CTE lesions of the ICA. LEVWLs were only observed in the SLE patients with CTE lesions (5/13, 38.5%), all of which were located at the ICA. However, none of the ETE lesions in either group extended more than two segments. No VWLs were found by HRVWI for the SLE patient who had cryptococcus neoformans meningitis. No signs of aneurysm, moyamoya, or reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome were found in either group. Comparison of VWL characteristics in the SLE and control groups

The VWLs in the SLE group were further allocated to the SLE-CTE and SLE-ETE groups based on the HRVWI patterns. The stenosis degrees of the VWLs in the three groups were 77.22%±10.07%, 76.70%±5.44%, and 74.58%±12.13%, respectively, and did not differ significantly between the three groups (Table 2). The percentage of culprit VWLs (90.0% vs. 88.9% vs. 69.7%, P=0.013) and the distribution of the VWLs in the ICA (60.0% vs. 5.6% vs. 19.7%, P<0.001), MCA (18.0% vs. 61.1% vs. 52.6%, P<0.001), and VA (0% vs. 11.1% vs. 13.2%, P=0.013) differed significantly among the SLE-CTE, SLE-ETE, and control groups, mostly due to the difference between the SLE-CTE and control groups. The distribution of VWLs in the ICA and MCA also differed significantly between the SLE-CTE and SLE-ETE groups. The difference between the VWLs in the SLE-ETE and the control groups was not statistically significant.

Comparison of clinical and radiological characteristics between the SLE patients with different HRVWI patterns

Table S2 shows the clinical and radiological characteristics of the SLE patients with CTE (n=13) and ETE (n=12) patterns. The percentage of lupus nephritis (LN) was significantly lower in the CTE patients (2/13, 15.4%) than the ETE patients (8/12, 66.7%, P=0.015). The mean (± SD) SLEDAI score was 19.1±8.5 in the CTE patients and 9.3±5.8 in the ETE patients (P=0.003). In relation to the distribution of the VWLs, the most vulnerable locations were the ICA and MCA (5/13, 38.5%) in the CTE patients, and the MCA (9/12, 75.0%) in the ETE patients. The number of segments of VWLs was significantly greater in the CTE patients than the ETE patients (median, 3.0 vs. 1.0, P=0.010). No statistically significant differences were observed between the CTE and ETE groups in terms of gender, age at SLE or ischemic stroke diagnosis, interval between diagnosis of stroke and SLE, atherosclerosis risk factors, SDI, mRS, laboratory factors related to the activity of SLE, APLs, or the HRVWI enhancement grade.

Follow-up

Of the SLE patients, 11 underwent follow-up MRI and HRVWI studies. The mean time from the first study to the follow-up HRVWI study was 8.36±5.0 months. Apart from antithrombotic drugs and lipid-lowering drugs, all the patients were treated with glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants due to moderate to severe disease activity. None of the patients experienced a lupus flare. Neither new infarctions nor new VWLs were found. None of the baseline 37 VWLs worsened, but three VWLs disappeared, and three VWLs showed a reduced extent and luminal stenosis improvement, and the enhancement grade of the VWLs decreased from two to one (Table S3).

Discussion

This study showed the distribution and patterns of intracranial VWLs in SLE ischemic stroke patients using HRVWI, suggesting the heterogenetic pathogenesis of intracranial vascular lesions in patients with SLE. The association between the clinical and VWL patterns was also explored.

Most (25/30, 83.3%) of the SLE ischemic stroke patients had large- to medium-sized intracranial VWLs. In a retrospective study of SLE patients, Ide et al. found that a substantial proportion of VWLs in intracranial arteries were visible by HRVWI (25), the severity of which was associated with brain infarctions. Case reports have found large and medium arteries to be culprits of ischemic stroke in SLE patients (26,27). Conversely, large and medium arteries are seldom affected in extracranial organs in SLE patients (14). According to a histologic and arteriographic study of SLE patients by Ramos-Casals et al., 86% of the patients had small vessel vasculitis and 14% had medium-sized vessel vasculitis in their extracranial organs (15). Kang et al. summarized the anatomical, embryological, and histological differences between extracranial and intracranial arteries, and suggested that these factors played a role in the different atherogenic process in the two areas (28). We thus presumed that the difference of the embryological origin and anatomical structure was one of the reasons why the location of the lesioned arteries was different in the extracranial and intracranial arteries of the SLE patients.

Unexpectedly, the VWLs in most (21/30, 70%) of the SLE patients appeared unilaterally, and the follow-up study did not reveal any new lesions in the other hemisphere. Unilateral VWLs are rare in PACNS. Salvarani et al. reported that bilateral vasculitis was found in 96.2% of PACNS patients by angiogram (29). AbdelRazek et al. hypothesized that there was a functional difference in immune responses, rather than a structural antigenic difference, between the two hemispheres for unilateral PACNS patients (30). As a disease with high heterogeneity, SLE may also lead to a different immune status in the bilateral hemisphere.

The special LEVWL subtype was detected at the ICA in five SLE patients (three of whom had APLs) with CTE lesions, which has never been reported in PACNS or atherosclerosis before. A similar radiological sign was described by Yeo et al. as long segmental stenosis with CTE lesions of the ICA without intraluminal thrombus on HRVWI in three patients with APLs, none of whom had a comorbidity of SLE (31). VWLs longer than 5 mm in length were also found in giant cell arteritis (GCA), distributed to the ICA, VA, and BA with extension into the P1 segment of the left PCA (32).

CTE and ETE lesions were the main patterns of the VWLs in the SLE patients, while the ETE lesions were the only pattern in the control patients in which the cause of ischemic stroke was considered to be atherosclerosis. According to previous studies, CTE lesions represent vasculitis and ETE lesions represent atherosclerosis (33,34). Our research was consistent with this view. The SLE-CTE VWLs had a higher percentage of culprit VWLs, greater ICA distribution, and less MCA and VA distribution than the control VWLs. Meanwhile, compared with the control VWLs, there was no difference in the percentage of culprit VWLs and the distribution of VWLs in the SLE-ETE VWLs, indicating that these radiological characteristics of atherosclerosis in patients with SLE are consistent with those in patients without SLE. A few studies have reported a close connection between SLE and atherosclerosis (35,36), but these studies have mainly focused on cardiovascular disease. Conversely, very little is known about the relationship between SLE and cerebrovascular atherosclerosis due to a lack of effective inspection methods. With the support of HRVWI, this was the first study to suggest that both vasculitis and atherosclerosis are the main causes of intracranial VWLs in SLE ischemic stroke patients.

Further, we hypothesize that high disease activity promotes vasculitic ischemic stroke, and cerebral atherosclerosis can be accelerated by LN, for which the MCA is the most vulnerable vessel. To our knowledge, this was the first study to examine the relationship between LN and cerebral vascular lesions. Previous studies have focused on the extracranial arteries of LN patients. Garg et al. reported that renal arteriosclerosis was prevalent two decades earlier in LN patients than healthy peers (37). Sharma et al. measured the flow-mediated dilation of the brachial artery, and found that the LN patients were at a higher risk of developing premature atherosclerosis than the SLE patients without nephritis (38).

Our follow-up study showed that the majority of VWLs remained stable for at least several months despite the patients receiving a combination therapy of glucocorticoids, immunosuppressants, antithrombotic drugs (aspirin or warfarin), and statins. This condition is much more like PACNS than GCA. Studies on PACNS have shown that vascular wall enhancement persists for several months after immunotherapy (39) and decreases approximately 1 year later (40), while the enhancement of the vessel wall of the GCA decreases rapidly under corticosteroid treatment in the first few days (41). A larger sample-sized, longer-term follow-up study needs to be conducted to summarize the characteristics of the imaging evolution of intracranial VWLs in SLE.

This study had several limitations. First, the sample size was small, and the follow-up study of HRVWI was difficult to carry out due to the relatively low prevalence of SLE and safety concerns related to contrast-enhancement MRI. Second, due to the FOV limitation, we could only obtain images of the intracranial segment of the ICA and VA. In the future, we intend to add an extracranial segment examination when the proximal part of the intracranial ICA or VA is involved.

Despite the small sample size, HRVWI showed that intracranial VWLs are common and could be a promising tool for exploring the mechanism of SLE ischemic stroke. However, a meticulously designed prospective multicenter study based on a larger population and fundamental experiments needs to be conducted to validate our results. Treatment targets should be further clarified among the options of secondary stroke prevention, improvement of functional end organ perfusion, and structural angiographic amelioration.

Conclusions

We found that most SLE ischemic stroke patients had VWLs in large- to medium-sized intracranial arteries with two main patterns: CTE and ETE lesions, representing vasculitis and atherosclerosis, respectively. LEVWLs and dissection were also observed. Disease activity and LN were found to be associated with different VWL patterns. These findings may extend understandings of the pathogenesis of ischemic stroke in SLE patients.
