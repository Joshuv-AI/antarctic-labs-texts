Biomechanical advances in oblique lateral lumbar interbody fusion using different interbody cages
Authors: Yermike Bieshanbieke, Fei Xie, Baofeng Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOblique lateral lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF) is a minimally invasive spinal fusion technique that has gained increasing popularity in the treatment of lumbar degenerative diseases. The interbody cage is a critical implant in OLIF procedures, and its material properties, structural design, dimensions, placement location, and combination with supplemental fixation strategies play a decisive role in determining immediate postoperative stability, fusion rate, restoration of disc height and lumbar lordosis, stress distribution, and the risk of adjacent segment degeneration (ASD). This review aims to summarize recent biomechanical research advances on OLIF combined with different interbody cages, to critically evaluate the advantages and limitations of various cage designs, and to analyze the effects of different fixation strategies on postoperative biomechanical performance. By comparing the biomechanical characteristics of polyetheretherketone, titanium alloy, novel biodegradable, and composite cages, and integrating evidence from finite element analyses, in vitro biomechanical experiments, and clinical studies, this review elucidates their differences in providing spinal stability, promoting osseous fusion, and reducing postoperative complications. In addition, this article highlights the biomechanical impact of cage placement, size, and geometry, and explores how topological optimization and patient-specific design may further enhance cage performance. Drawing on successful experiences with interbody cage designs in cervical fusion surgery, future directions for lumbar interbody cages are discussed, including integrated structural design, material innovation, and personalized customization, with the goal of providing optimized surgical strategies and implant selection for clinical practice.Keywords: Oblique lateral lumbar interbody fusion, Interbody cage, Biomechanics, Research advances

Introduction

Lumbar degenerative diseases are a leading cause of low back pain and neurological dysfunction, substantially impairing patient quality of life. When conservative treatment fails, lumbar fusion is an effective strategy to restore spinal stability and alleviate symptoms [1, 2]. With the continued evolution of minimally invasive spine surgery, oblique lateral lumbar interbody fusion (OLIF) has gained widespread adoption. Its retroperitoneal approach avoids direct injury to intra-abdominal organs and the psoas major muscle, while permitting placement of a large-footprint cage to effectively restore disc height and lumbar lordosis, thereby achieving indirect decompression [3, 4].

In OLIF procedures, the interbody cage is the critical implant for achieving stable intervertebral fusion. Cage selection and placement strategies directly influence immediate postoperative stability, fusion rate, stress distribution, and the occurrence of long-term complications—particularly cage subsidence and adjacent segment degeneration (ASD) [5, 6]. Biomechanical studies, including in vitro experiments and finite element analysis (FEA), have systematically examined the effects of cage material, design, dimensions, placement position, and supplemental fixation on segmental spinal biomechanics, providing essential theoretical support for optimizing surgical strategies and guiding development of novel cage designs [7, 8].

In recent years, advances in materials science and three-dimensional (3D) printing have led to the development of novel interbody cages, including 3D-printed porous titanium cages, biodegradable cages, and composite constructs. These innovations aim to overcome the limitations of conventional implants, optimize biomechanical performance, enhance fusion rates, and reduce complication risks. Furthermore, the choice of fixation strategy—whether stand-alone cages, anterior plate fixation, or posterior pedicle-screw fixation—has a significant impact on postoperative biomechanical stability following OLIF [9, 10].

This review focuses on recent biomechanical research advances in OLIF combined with different interbody cages. We describe the biomechanical basis of OLIF, analyze the effects of different cage materials and designs on biomechanical performance while comparing their respective advantages and limitations, and discuss the biomechanical roles of various fixation strategies in OLIF. Furthermore, we emphasize the importance of cage placement and size, summarize strengths and weaknesses of existing cage designs, and draw on the developmental experience of interbody cages in cervical fusion to outline future directions for lumbar cage design. To ensure transparency and reproducibility, this review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. The detailed search strategy and evidence appraisal framework are described in the Methods section.

Methods

Search strategy

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement. A comprehensive literature search was performed across the following electronic databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and Scopus (English-language databases); and CNKI, Wanfang Data, and SinoMed (Chinese-language databases). The search covered publications from January 2015 to December 2025. This timeframe was selected to capture the period during which OLIF gained widespread clinical adoption and biomechanical research proliferated. Additional studies were identified through reference list screening of included articles and forward citation tracking of seminal publications.

The search strategy was developed using the PICO framework: Population—patients or biomechanical models undergoing lumbar interbody fusion; Intervention—OLIF using different interbody cage types; Comparison—different cage designs, materials, or fixation configurations; Outcome—biomechanical outcomes including range of motion (ROM), cage stress, endplate stress, subsidence, construct stability, and adjacent segment biomechanics. A combination of controlled vocabulary (MeSH terms) and free-text keywords was employed, organized into three concept groups: (1) surgical technique (e.g., “oblique lumbar interbody fusion,” “OLIF,” “lateral lumbar interbody fusion,” “anterior to psoas”); (2) interbody device (e.g., “cage,” “interbody,” “PEEK,” “titanium,” “3D-printed,” “porous”); and (3) biomechanical outcomes (e.g., “finite element,” “biomechanical,” “ROM,” “subsidence,” “stress,” “fusion”). Boolean operators were used to combine terms both within and across concept groups. No language restrictions were applied to the Chinese-language databases. The detailed search strings for PubMed/MEDLINE are provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they: (1) reported original biomechanical data (FEA, in vitro mechanical testing, or clinical studies reporting biomechanical outcomes such as subsidence, fusion, or implant-related complications); (2) evaluated OLIF or related lateral/anterior-to-psoas approaches; (3) compared different interbody cage types (materials, designs, or parameters) or fixation configurations; and (4) reported at least one biomechanical outcome. Studies were excluded if they: exclusively evaluated posterior approaches (PLIF, TLIF) without an OLIF or lateral component; were review articles, editorials, case reports, or conference abstracts without original data; reported only clinical outcomes without any biomechanical assessment; or were non-peer-reviewed sources including preprints and dissertations.

Study selection and data extraction

Two independent reviewers screened all identified records. After duplicate removal using reference management software, titles and abstracts were screened against eligibility criteria, followed by full-text assessment of potentially relevant articles. Reasons for exclusion were documented at the full-text review stage. Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third senior reviewer.

Data were extracted using a pilot-tested standardized form including: study characteristics (first author, year, country, study design), methodological details (FEA model type, in vitro specimen characteristics, or clinical cohort description), cage specifications (material, design, dimensions, porosity where applicable), fixation configuration, and biomechanical outcomes (ROM, stress distribution parameters, subsidence rates, fusion rates, and adjacent segment biomechanical parameters). Where reported, sample size, statistical methods, and key quantitative findings were also recorded.

Evidence appraisal

Given the heterogeneity of included studies, a customized evidence hierarchy was developed for three study categories: FEA studies, in vitro biomechanical studies, and clinical studies. Evidence levels were classified from Level I (highest) to Level V (lowest) within each category (Table 3). For FEA studies, Level I was assigned to validated models with comprehensive sensitivity analysis and experimental verification; Level II to well-described models with partial validation; and Level III to models with limited or no validation. For in vitro studies, Level I was assigned to large-sample controlled biomechanical experiments with power analysis; Level II to moderate-sample comparative studies; and Level III to small-sample or single-specimen studies. For clinical studies, Level I was assigned to RCTs or large prospective cohorts (> 100 patients); Level II to retrospective comparative studies; and Level III to case series without controls. Quality was graded using a modified GRADE approach: High (+++), Moderate (++), Low (+), and Very Low (–). Risk of bias was assessed using validated tools: Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) for randomized controlled trials (RCTs), the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for non-randomized clinical studies, and adapted ASME V&V 10-2019 criteria for FEA studies. Two reviewers independently performed the quality assessment; disagreements were resolved through consensus.

Data synthesis

Due to anticipated heterogeneity in study designs, loading conditions, outcome measures, and cage specifications, a quantitative meta-analysis was not performed. Instead, a structured narrative synthesis was organized by study category (FEA, in vitro, clinical), cage comparison type (material, design, dimension, placement), and biomechanical outcome domain (stability, stress distribution, subsidence, fusion, adjacent segment effects). Key findings were summarized with explicit reference to the evidence level and quality grade of supporting studies. Where multiple studies reported comparable outcomes, patterns of consistency or divergence were highlighted to enable readers to assess the strength of conclusions.

Development and biomechanical basis of OLIF

OLIF accesses the disc space through a retroperitoneal corridor, avoiding direct injury to intra-abdominal organs and the psoas major. Compared with conventional posterior approaches, it reduces surgical trauma, intraoperative blood loss, and postoperative pain [3, 4], supporting its expanding application in lumbar degenerative conditions including discogenic low back pain, degenerative disc disease, mild degenerative spondylolisthesis, and scoliosis [9–11].

From a biomechanical perspective, the primary goal of OLIF is to restore segmental physiological alignment, disc height, and stability, thereby creating a favorable mechanical milieu for fusion and enabling indirect decompression. By inserting a large-footprint cage into the disc space, OLIF effectively distracts the intervertebral space and restores disc height, indirectly enlarging the foraminal and central canal dimensions to relieve neural compression [3, 4]. A large-footprint cage provides a greater endplate contact area, which is critical for load sharing, reducing focal stress concentration, and lowering the risk of cage subsidence [6, 12].

Recent meta-analytic evidence has further substantiated the clinical advantages of OLIF. Shi et al. [13] (2024, Journal of Orthopaedic Surgery and Research) conducted a meta-analysis of 14 studies comprising 877 patients and found that OLIF was associated with significantly shorter operative time, less intraoperative blood loss, and superior restoration of intervertebral height compared with TLIF. Similarly, a meta-analysis of MIS-TLIF versus OLIF published in the same journal (2024), encompassing 24 studies with 1785 patients, demonstrated that OLIF outperformed MIS-TLIF across multiple perioperative and radiographic parameters [14]. Regarding sagittal alignment restoration, Lechtholz-Zey et al. [15] (2025, Clinical Spine Surgery) performed a comprehensive meta-analysis of 88 papers and established a hierarchical ranking for lumbar lordosis restoration: ALIF > OLIF > TLIF, highlighting OLIF’s intermediate position in achieving sagittal correction while retaining the advantages of a lateral approach.

The biomechanical advantages of OLIF are primarily reflected in three aspects: (1) Restoration of disc height and lumbar lordosis: The OLIF corridor permits the use of larger cages that better conform to anatomical morphology, including wedge-shaped designs, which facilitate restoration and maintenance of disc height and lumbar lordosis [16, 17]. Some studies suggest that ALIF and OLIF generally outperform TLIF and PLIF in restoring disc height and lumbar lordosis [3, 18], improvements that may reduce adjacent-segment loading and thereby lower the risk of ASD [18]. (2) Immediate stability: After cage insertion, sufficient immediate stability is required to prevent migration or subsidence and to establish a stable microenvironment for fusion. Although the large-footprint cage used in OLIF provides robust initial support, stand-alone OLIF may be insufficiently stable in certain motion planes—particularly flexion–extension and axial rotation—necessitating supplemental fixation [11, 19–21]. (3) Stress distribution: An optimal cage should distribute loads evenly and avoid stress concentration on the endplate or within the implant itself, thereby reducing the risks of endplate injury, cage subsidence, and fixation failure [22, 23]. Cage material and design directly influence stress distribution patterns. For example, PEEK’s elastic modulus (~ 3.6 GPa) approximates that of cortical bone, facilitating more physiological load transfer, whereas conventional titanium alloys exhibit substantially higher stiffness that may cause stress shielding [24, 25]. 3D-printed porous titanium cages address this limitation by enabling elastic modulus tuning through controlled porosity, achieving more favorable stress distribution [26, 27].

Adjacent segment degeneration is another important biomechanical consideration in OLIF. Spinal fusion alters the physiological motion and load distribution of the spine, potentially increasing mechanical stress on adjacent segments and accelerating their degeneration [28, 29]. The magnitude of this effect is influenced by cage design, fixation strategy, and patient-specific factors [9, 10]. Overly rigid fixation may increase adjacent-segment stress, whereas cage materials with an elastic modulus closer to that of bone or semi-rigid fixation systems may help mitigate ASD [23, 30].

Overall, the biomechanical rationale of OLIF lies in effective disc-space distraction via a minimally invasive corridor, restoration of disc height and lumbar lordosis, and stable support provided by a large-footprint cage. Nevertheless, to fully realize these advantages while minimizing complications, further biomechanical research and optimization remain essential with respect to cage materials, design, placement strategy, and supplemental fixation selection.

Influence of interbody cage materials and designs on biomechanical performance

The material composition and structural design of an interbody cage are key determinants of its biomechanical performance, fusion potential, and complication profile. With advances in biomaterials science and manufacturing technologies, cage materials have evolved from autograft and allograft bone to polyetheretherketone and titanium alloys, and more recently to 3D-printed porous titanium, biodegradable materials, and composite constructs. Each material-design combination exhibits distinct biomechanical characteristics and potential clinical advantages.

Conventional PEEK and titanium-alloy cages: trade-offs and limitations

Polyetheretherketone (PEEK) and conventional titanium alloys are the most widely used materials for spinal interbody cages; their markedly different biomechanical properties produce distinct clinical performance profiles. PEEK cages are favored because of their good biocompatibility, chemical inertness, and an elastic modulus (~ 3.6 GPa) substantially lower than that of titanium alloys (~ 110 GPa) and closer to cortical bone (~ 13.8 GPa), facilitating physiological load transfer and potentially stimulating bone formation while reducing stress shielding [24, 25]. The radiolucency of PEEK minimizes imaging artifacts on postoperative radiography and computed tomography (CT), enabling reliable assessment of cage position and fusion progression [31, 32]. However, PEEK’s relatively smooth surface exhibits limited osteoinductive and osteoconductive capacity, which may compromise the rate and quality of fusion; graft materials are typically required [31, 32]. Despite the modulus-matching advantage, PEEK cages may still exhibit a relatively high subsidence risk in patients with osteoporosis. Park et al. reported cage subsidence in 8.3% of patients using a large-footprint PEEK cage in BELIF [33]. In BE-TLIF, You et al. reported a significantly lower subsidence rate with large-footprint 3D-printed titanium cages than with PEEK cages [34], suggesting that cage footprint and design may outweigh material choice alone in determining subsidence risk.

Conventional titanium-alloy cages (e.g., Ti-6Al-4 V) offer high strength, high stiffness, good biocompatibility, and favorable osseointegration. The titanium surface can achieve direct bone-implant bonding, promoting osseointegration that may enhance cage stability and facilitate fusion. However, their elastic modulus far exceeds that of bone, resulting in pronounced stress shielding that may inhibit bone remodeling and contribute to peri-implant bone loss [25]. Fan et al. reported that semi-rigid posterior fixation may increase stresses on the endplate and cage, underscoring the importance of stiffness matching [23]. In addition, titanium alloys generate pronounced artifacts on radiography and CT that may compromise postoperative fusion assessment. In a CT-based evaluation after L5-S1 ALIF, Farber et al. found higher fusion rates with 3D-printed and solid titanium cages, whereas subsidence was more common in older patients [35].

Critical analysis: resolving the PEEK-titanium paradox

The comparative biomechanical performance of PEEK and titanium cages presents a nuanced picture that challenges simplistic material-based recommendations. Conventional titanium alloys, despite their superior strength and osseointegration capacity, exhibit an elastic modulus (~ 110 GPa) far exceeding that of cortical bone (~ 13.8 GPa), resulting in pronounced stress shielding that may inhibit bone remodeling. Conversely, PEEK’s modulus (~ 3.6 GPa) more closely approximates bone, facilitating physiological load transfer, yet its smooth surface offers limited osteoinductive capacity.

Recent evidence has further complicated this dichotomy. Large-footprint 3D-printed porous titanium cages have demonstrated significantly lower subsidence rates than both conventional titanium and PEEK constructs. Duan et al. [36] (World Neurosurgery, 2024, Meta-analysis—Level II) reported that 3D-printed porous titanium cages achieved lower subsidence rates at 1-year follow-up and higher fusion rates at 6 months compared with PEEK, suggesting that cage architecture may play an important role in clinical outcomes.

This interpretation is supported by conflicting evidence regarding PEEK-titanium composite cages. One retrospective self-controlled study [37] (Cureus, 2024) reported that PEEK-titanium composite cages achieved significantly lower subsidence rates than 3D-printed titanium cages at 6 months (4.8% versus 27.9%), suggesting that multilayer designs integrating titanium’s osseointegration with PEEK’s modulus-matching properties may offer optimal biomechanical performance. However, another study by Makino et al. [38] found higher subsidence rates with PEEK-titanium than with 3D-printed titanium, indicating that cage geometry and surgical technique may mediate material effects.

Collectively, these findings suggest that the PEEK-titanium debate has evolved beyond material selection toward an integrated design paradigm in which material properties, surface architecture, footprint dimensions, and patient-specific factors collectively determine outcomes.

3D-printed porous titanium-alloy cages: performance innovations and remaining challenges

3D printing has fundamentally reshaped the design and manufacture of interbody cages, enabling porous titanium-alloy constructs with biomimetic trabecular architectures that improve upon both conventional titanium and PEEK implants.

The proposed advantages of 3D-printed porous titanium-alloy cages include: (1) Optimized elastic modulus and reduced stress shielding: By precisely controlling porous architecture and porosity, 3D-printed titanium-alloy cages can tune the effective elastic modulus toward a range closer to human bone, substantially reducing stress shielding [26, 27]. Shuai et al. reported that a 3D-printed porous, ultra-low-modulus Ti-24Nb-4Zr-8Sn lumbar cage demonstrated favorable mechanical stability and reduced cage-endplate interface stress [26]. A comparative study by Zhu et al. indicated that porous designs can minimize micro-motion, improve stability, and reduce subsidence risk [27]. (2) Superior osseointegration and bone ingrowth: A porous structure provides a three-dimensional scaffold conducive to osteoblast attachment, proliferation, and differentiation, and optimization of porosity, pore size, and interconnectivity can promote bone ingrowth and vascularization, potentially accelerating fusion [39–41]. In a randomized controlled trial, Ham et al. [42] (Journal of Bone and Joint Surgery, 2024) reported a fusion rate of 96.6% with a non-windowed 3D-printed titanium cage in a multicenter RCT, demonstrating excellent osseointegration capacity. (3) Reduced subsidence risk: By increasing endplate contact area, improving stress distribution, and facilitating bone ingrowth, 3D-printed porous titanium-alloy cages may reduce subsidence incidence. Donaldson et al. reported an overall subsidence rate of 3.03% in patients undergoing lumbar fusion with 3D-printed titanium cages, lower than rates reported for other cage types [39]. Studies by You et al. [34] and Hudson et al. [31] similarly suggested that large-footprint 3D-printed titanium cages substantially reduce both the incidence and severity of subsidence.

Recent large-scale clinical evidence has further strengthened the case for 3D-printed porous titanium cages. Burkhard et al. [43] (Global Spine Journal, 2025) reported outcomes from 233 patients with a minimum 2-year follow-up, finding that 3D-printed titanium cages were associated with a significantly lower reoperation rate (13% versus 26.3% for PEEK, P = 0.017). At the meta-analytic level, Duan et al. [36] (World Neurosurgery, 2024) demonstrated that 3D-printed porous titanium cages achieved lower 1-year subsidence rates and higher 6-month fusion rates compared with PEEK cages. A network meta-analysis [37] (World Neurosurgery: X, 2024) ranked 3D-printed titanium highest for fusion rate, further establishing its clinical superiority.

The optimal porous architecture remains an active area of investigation. A pore size range of approximately 300–600 μm has been suggested for promoting osteoblast attachment and bone ingrowth while maintaining adequate mechanical strength, though this requires further clinical validation. Porosity, pore interconnectivity, and strut thickness must be carefully balanced to achieve favorable bone ingrowth without compromising structural integrity.

3D printing also enables patient-specific customization according to individual anatomy and biomechanical demands. When combined with topology optimization, it may minimize material usage while meeting strength requirements and further improving stress distribution [27]. However, key challenges remain: (1) Cost: Manufacturing and material costs associated with 3D printing remain relatively high. (2) Long-term clinical evidence: Although short- and mid-term outcomes are encouraging, long-term efficacy and complication rates require validation in large-scale prospective studies. (3) Optimization of porous architecture: The optimal combination of porosity, pore size, and interconnectivity remains to be established, and design trade-offs must balance bone ingrowth, mechanical strength, and stress shielding.

Emerging biodegradable and composite cages: future directions and challenges

To overcome the limitations of conventional cages and address potential long-term issues of 3D-printed titanium cages, such as imaging artifacts and stress shielding, increasing attention has been directed toward biodegradable and composite materials. Ideally, these materials should provide sufficient initial mechanical support and gradually degrade after fusion is achieved, allowing progressive load transfer to newly formed bone and minimizing stress shielding.

Biodegradable cages have emerged as a major research focus, with materials such as magnesium alloys and polylactic acid receiving particular attention. Magnesium alloys exhibit favorable biocompatibility and biodegradability, and their elastic modulus lies between that of PEEK and titanium alloys, closer to that of bone. Chi et al. evaluated a novel Mg-Zn-Nd-Zr alloy lumbar interbody cage and demonstrated superior osteoinductive properties compared with PEEK in both in vitro and in vivo settings; however, early implantation may be accompanied by hydrogen gas formation, which requires further optimization. Han Zhenchuan et al. further reported that a novel biodegradable lumbar interbody cage exhibited significantly superior height retention under compressive failure, load-bearing capacity, and stiffness compared with PEEK and iliac bone graft cages. Poly(amino acid)/nano-hydroxyapatite/calcium sulfate cages have demonstrated favorable mid-term clinical outcomes, effectively restoring segmental height and lordosis and promoting graft fusion, with fusion rates comparable to those of PEEK cages [31, 32]. The principal advantage of biodegradable cages lies in their potential to eliminate stress shielding while promoting bone fusion. However, critical challenges remain, including precise control of degradation kinetics in relation to mechanical strength—degradation that outpaces fusion may lead to catastrophic loss of structural support, while insufficiently rapid degradation perpetuates stress shielding—ensuring sufficient load-bearing capacity before fusion is complete, and thorough validation of the biocompatibility and safety of degradation by-products.

Composite cages combine different materials to overcome the limitations of single-material constructs. Jin et al. designed a novel topology-optimized multilayer titanium-PEEK cervical cage featuring an outer titanium layer and an inner PEEK core, integrating the osseointegration capability of titanium with the low elastic modulus of PEEK [44]. Similarly, Ma Lei et al. demonstrated reduced stress shielding with titanium-PEEK composite cages in PE-PLIF [45]. This design concept is equally applicable to lumbar interbody cages.

However, the clinical evidence for PEEK-titanium composite cages remains inconsistent. As noted above, a retrospective self-controlled study [37] reported that PEEK-titanium composite cages achieved significantly lower subsidence rates than 3D-printed titanium cages at 6 months (4.8% versus 27.9%), suggesting that multilayer designs may offer optimal biomechanical performance. Conversely, Makino et al. [38] reported higher subsidence rates with PEEK-titanium composites than with 3D-printed titanium, suggesting that cage geometry and surgical technique may mediate material effects. These contradictory findings indicate that composite cage performance may be highly dependent on specific design parameters, including layer thickness distribution, surface treatment, and overall cage geometry, precluding generalized conclusions at this stage.

While composite cages offer tunable mechanical properties and synergistic advantages, challenges remain regarding interfacial compatibility between dissimilar materials, manufacturing complexity, cost-effectiveness, and long-term performance validation.

Topology optimization and patient-specific design: toward precision customization

With continued advances in computer-aided design, finite element analysis, and additive manufacturing, interbody cage design has progressively shifted toward topology optimization and patient-specific customization. This approach enables the development of cages that better accommodate individual anatomical characteristics and biomechanical demands, achieving superior mechanical performance and physiological compatibility.

Topology optimization is a structural design methodology that identifies the optimal material distribution under predefined loading conditions, constraints, and performance requirements. In cage design, it is primarily applied to optimize stress distribution by generating complex internal architectures with rational material allocation, promoting more uniform stress transfer under physiological loading and reducing the risks of endplate damage and cage subsidence [27]. He et al. developed a topology-optimized posterior lumbar interbody cage incorporating oblique fixation, which reduced peak von Mises stress and cage displacement, improving load transfer and structural stability [46]. Topology optimization can also mitigate stress shielding by enabling porous or lattice architectures that reduce material usage and overall stiffness while maintaining sufficient mechanical strength, yielding an elastic modulus more closely matched to bone [26, 47]. Bozyiğit et al. proposed a patient-specific lattice cage with graded stiffness, demonstrating stress shielding reduction of approximately 30–50% [47].

Recent advances have expanded the scope of topology optimization from localized structural features to full-size cage architectures. Full-size topology optimization for patient-specific cages represents a promising computational approach, though current clinical evidence remains limited and further biomechanical validation is warranted.

Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted cage design represents a further frontier. Machine learning algorithms can accelerate the iterative optimization process by predicting biomechanical performance from design parameters, reducing computational cost and time. AI-driven approaches may also enable multi-objective optimization simultaneously targeting stress distribution, osseointegration potential, and manufacturing constraints, potentially yielding designs that are difficult to achieve through conventional methods.

Patient-specific design is based on reconstructing individual spinal anatomy from CT or magnetic resonance imaging data and subsequently designing customized interbody cages tailored to the patient. The primary advantages include precise anatomical matching, maximization of cage-endplate contact area, enhanced initial stability, and optimized load transfer [27, 47]. Experimental work by Manuel et al. demonstrated that ALIF cages positioned along the apophyseal ring exhibited increased structural strength, and cages maximizing apophyseal contact tolerated higher subsidence loads, underscoring the importance of patient-specific design in optimizing endplate contact [48]. Tailoring cage wedge angles and dimensions also allows more precise restoration of optimal sagittal alignment, which is critical for reducing ASD risk and improving clinical outcomes [16].

The integration of topology optimization with patient-specific design enables the development of truly personalized, optimized cages and holds considerable promise for maximizing fusion rates and minimizing complication risks. However, this approach currently faces challenges related to computational cost and time, material property constraints, regulatory approval pathways for patient-specific implants, and the need for robust clinical validation (Table 1).

Effects of different fixation strategies on the biomechanical stability of OLIF

In OLIF procedures, the interbody cage is commonly supplemented with additional fixation to provide adequate immediate stability, prevent cage migration or subsidence, and create a stable microenvironment for fusion. Notably, different fixation strategies differ substantially in biomechanical performance, surgical invasiveness, and complication risk profiles.

Stand-alone OLIF: balancing minimal invasiveness and stability

Stand-alone OLIF involves placement of an interbody cage without additional supplemental fixation. The main advantages include minimal surgical invasiveness, shorter operative time, reduced blood loss, and avoidance of fixation-related complications. However, immediate stability relies primarily on friction at the cage-endplate interface and the intrinsic support provided by the cage itself. Multiple biomechanical studies have demonstrated that SA-OLIF exhibits inferior stability in certain motion planes, particularly during flexion–extension and axial rotation, characterized by greater range of motion (ROM) and higher cage stress, thereby increasing the risk of cage subsidence and migration [11, 19, 20].

Finite element analysis by Li et al. showed that SA-OLIF had a greater ROM in flexion–extension than models with bilateral posterior pedicle-screw (BPS) fixation, and that the maximum cage stress in the SA configuration exceeded that of BPS under all loading conditions, indicating compromised stability and an increased risk of cage subsidence [20]. Consistently, Yu et al. reported that the SA group exhibited the highest ROM and cage stress among the fixation strategies evaluated [19].

Clinically, SA-OLIF may be appropriate for younger patients with good bone quality, no evident spinal instability, single-level disease, and no requirement for major deformity correction. In contrast, for patients with osteoporosis, multilevel fusion, or overt instability, SA-OLIF provides insufficient stability and is associated with higher risks of subsidence and nonunion [9, 59].

Lateral plate fixation: a compromise between minimal invasiveness and stability

To enhance the stability of stand-alone OLIF while avoiding additional trauma associated with posterior instrumentation, lateral plate fixation (LPF) has emerged as an important supplemental fixation strategy. LPF involves placement of a low-profile plate and screws anterior or lateral to the cage to secure the implant to the adjacent vertebral bodies. Biomechanical studies have shown that LPF can improve immediate postoperative stability after OLIF and reduce the risks of cage subsidence and migration, particularly during flexion–extension and lateral bending [59–61].

In an in vitro biomechanical evaluation, Wang et al. demonstrated that LPF significantly reduced ROM in both normal-bone and osteoporotic models, achieving stability comparable to that of unilateral posterior pedicle-screw (UPS) fixation [59]. In a retrospective study, Hudson et al. found that lateral lumbar interbody fusion (LLIF) combined with lateral plate-and-screw fixation was associated with a lower radiographic subsidence rate [60].

Integrated cage design represents a key developmental direction for LPF. Cai et al. designed an OLIF cage incorporating a low-profile plate and crossed screws; compared with conventional OLIF combined with anterior plate-and-screw fixation, the novel LPCS-OLIF cage demonstrated intrinsic biomechanical advantages in establishing immediate postoperative stability and reducing stress concentration-related complications [62]. This integrated configuration reduces implant prominence and simplifies surgical procedures, potentially decreasing soft-tissue irritation and fixation-related failure. Nevertheless, under extreme loading conditions or in patients with severe osteoporosis, plate-screw loosening or pullout may still occur, and plate placement may pose risks to adjacent vascular and neural structures.

Posterior pedicle screw fixation: the “gold standard” and the challenge of adjacent segment stress

Posterior pedicle screw fixation (PPSF) is widely considered the “gold standard” in spinal fusion because it provides the greatest immediate stability. In OLIF, PPSF is commonly used as supplemental instrumentation to enhance stability at the fused segment, particularly in multilevel fusion, marked instability, deformity correction, or patients with osteoporosis. By substantially reducing ROM in all planes and providing high construct stiffness, PPSF can minimize the risks of cage subsidence and migration and create an optimal mechanical environment for osseous fusion [9, 10].

Despite its superior stability, the high rigidity of PPSF nearly abolishes motion at the fused segment, resulting in stress redistribution to adjacent non-fused segments and accelerating the development of ASD [9, 10, 23]. Finite element analysis by Zhang et al. demonstrated that bilateral pedicle screw fixation generates higher intradiscal pressure and facet cartilage stress than unilateral pedicle screw fixation, suggesting that unilateral fixation exerts a less pronounced biomechanical impact on adjacent segments [63]. To mitigate ASD, investigators have explored the use of rods with lower elastic moduli or semi-rigid fixation systems [23, 30]. Chou et al. [30] conducted a finite element analysis of OLIF with polycarbonate urethane (PCU) rods and reported that this semi-rigid construct minimized adjacent-segment stress increases while maintaining adequate stability at the fused segment. These findings highlight the potential of stiffness-matching strategies to reduce ASD risk. However, the use of semi-rigid or dynamic systems requires careful patient selection; excessive flexibility may increase endplate and cage stress, particularly in osteoporotic patients, potentially elevating subsidence risk [23]. The optimal balance between fixation stiffness and adjacent-segment protection remains an area of active investigation (see also “Biomechanical considerations in adjacent segment degeneration” section for a comprehensive discussion of ASD prevention strategies). Additional disadvantages of PPSF include increased surgical invasiveness, greater blood loss, higher infection risk, and instrumentation-related complications such as screw loosening or breakage.

Cortical bone trajectory fixation: an optimized posterior instrumentation strategy

Cortical bone trajectory (CBT) screw fixation is a relatively novel pedicle screw insertion technique in which the screw path follows the cortical bone of the pedicle. This trajectory is designed to enhance screw purchase while minimizing violation of the medial pedicle cortex, potentially reducing surgical invasiveness. Numerous biomechanical studies have demonstrated that CBT screws can provide stability comparable to, or even exceeding, that of traditional pedicle screws [9, 11, 64].

Finite element analysis conducted by Guo et al. [9] showed that both OLIF combined with bilateral pedicle screw fixation (OLIF-BPSF) and OLIF combined with unilateral pedicle screw fixation (OLIF-UPSF) reduced peak von Mises stress, with OLIF-BPSF demonstrating more favorable performance in osteoporotic models. Similarly, Tao et al. [11] reported that the bilateral CBT (BCBT) model exhibited the lowest cage stress and the highest construct stability among the fixation configurations evaluated. Finite element analysis by Tuoheti et al. [65] suggested that modified CBT techniques may reduce the incidence of adjacent segment degeneration after TLIF by decreasing range of motion and stress at adjacent segments. A recent meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials comparing CBT with traditional pedicle screw fixation demonstrated comparable fusion rates between the two techniques, with CBT showing a potential advantage in reducing operative time and blood loss [54]. An additional advantage of CBT screws is their relatively shallow insertion angle, which limits paraspinal muscle dissection and reduces surgical trauma. However, CBT fixation requires greater technical precision, and its long-term clinical outcomes and complication rates in OLIF specifically warrant further investigation (Table 2).

Effects of cage positioning and size on biomechanical performance

Cage position, size, and geometry are key determinants of postoperative biomechanics, fusion potential, and complication risk after OLIF. Precise cage placement and appropriate sizing can maximize restoration of disc height and lumbar lordosis, optimize load transfer, and reduce the risks of cage subsidence and migration.

Positioning: optimizing endplate contact, stress distribution, and lordosis restoration

The location of the cage within the disc space is critical for endplate contact area, stress distribution, lordosis restoration, and immediate stability. In principle, cage placement should aim to maximize contact with the vertebral endplate and position the cage as close as possible to the supportive cortical ring (apophyseal ring), where bone density and strength exceed those of the central cancellous bone, thereby providing stronger support and more effective resistance to subsidence [22, 48]. In an in vitro analysis, Yu et al. [22] reported that vertebral bone mineral density, as well as cage position and orientation, had pronounced effects on cage-endplate contact, offering biomechanical guidance for cage selection and design. Consistently, experimental work by Manuel et al. demonstrated that placing ALIF cages along the anterior apophyseal ring increased structural strength; cages maximizing apophyseal contact tolerated higher subsidence loads, and cortical bone thickness was strongly correlated with subsidence load capacity [48].

Cage positioning also significantly influences postoperative segmental lordosis. A recent clinical study demonstrated that cage position is an important independent predictor of lordosis restoration in anterior lumbar interbody fusion (beta = − 0.356, P < 0.001), with more anterior placement associated with greater lordosis restoration [51]. This finding has important implications for sagittal balance optimization in OLIF, where the anterior corridor facilitates more anterior cage placement than posterior approaches. Finite element analysis combined with in vitro experimentation by Li et al. [50] systematically evaluated 12 cage positions, 16 inclination angles, and 4 cage heights in a comprehensive parametric study. Their findings recommended central-anterior positioning with a height not exceeding 12 mm as the optimal configuration for balancing disc height restoration, endplate stress reduction, and construct stability [50]. Using a three-dimensional finite element model, Nan et al. [21] analyzed the biomechanical effects of different cage positions in lateral lumbar interbody fusion and found that a centrally positioned cage produced the greatest ROM during flexion–extension, lateral bending, and axial rotation, while also yielding the lowest peak stress values, suggesting that medial placement may reduce the risks of adjacent segment degeneration and cage subsidence. Similarly, finite element analysis by Morimoto et al. [66] indicated that placing the cage centrally or slightly anteriorly increased construct stiffness and stability and reduced endplate stress as well as the risk of collapse or subsidence. In a retrospective clinical analysis, Tang et al. [67] found that transverse cage placement resulted in a larger grafting area within the disc space, higher fusion rates, and better maintenance of disc height compared with oblique placement.

Critical analysis: lordosis restoration versus subsidence risk–a biomechanical trade-off

The relationship between cage position and biomechanical outcomes presents a nuanced trade-off that requires careful surgical planning. While more anterior placement is associated with superior lordosis restoration [52], it simultaneously alters the biomechanical loading profile of the vertebral endplate. Recent evidence indicates that wider cages combined with more anterior positioning effectively reduce postoperative ROM but concurrently increase subsidence risk [53]. This apparent paradox arises because anterior vertebral endplates, despite their cortical reinforcement, are subjected to complex asymmetric loading patterns when cages are placed too far anteriorly, particularly in the presence of osteoporosis or suboptimal bone quality.

The parametric study by Li et al. [50] provides critical guidance for navigating this trade-off: central-anterior (rather than extreme anterior) positioning optimizes the balance between lordosis gain and endplate protection. Furthermore, the 12 mm height threshold identified in their analysis represents a practical upper limit; exceeding this height significantly increases endplate stress regardless of positioning [50]. These findings suggest that surgeons should not pursue maximal lordosis restoration through extreme anterior placement without considering the attendant subsidence risk, particularly in patients with compromised endplate quality. A patient-specific approach incorporating preoperative assessment of endplate morphology and bone mineral density (see “Endplate quality and cage subsidence: the challenge of osteoporosis” section) is essential for optimal cage positioning.

Cage size and geometry: determinants of height restoration, lordosis, and subsidence

Cage size and geometry are critical determinants of disc height restoration, lumbar lordosis, construct stability, and resistance to subsidence.

Effect of size on subsidence

Larger cages typically provide a greater endplate contact area, allowing load dispersion and reducing stress per unit area, thereby lowering the risk of cage subsidence [6, 12, 34]. Experimental biomechanical work by Yuan et al. demonstrated a positive correlation between cage size and subsidence load, indicating that larger cages require higher forces to induce subsidence [6]. Using finite element analysis, Wu et al. similarly found that 3D-printed cages with larger axial footprints reduced stresses on both instrumentation and endplates, which may help prevent subsidence [12]. In a BE-TLIF cohort, You et al. reported that larger cages substantially reduced both the incidence and severity of subsidence [34].

Effect of size and shape on disc height and lumbar lordosis

Selection of an appropriately sized cage is essential for effective restoration of disc height. Wedge-shaped cages can facilitate restoration and maintenance of segmental lumbar lordosis, thereby improving overall sagittal alignment [16, 17]. Pennington et al. reported that, compared with bullet-shaped cages, banana-shaped interbody cages achieved greater postoperative disc height and segmental lordosis [16]. A recent meta-analysis by Lin et al. (2024) comparing banana-shaped versus straight cages in lumbar interbody fusion found that banana-shaped cages were associated with significantly greater restoration of segmental lordosis and disc height, although differences in fusion rates and subsidence incidence did not reach statistical significance [56].

Cage height: optimization and upper limits

While greater cage height facilitates more extensive disc height restoration and indirect decompression, recent biomechanical evidence has identified important upper limits. Chayer et al. [55] (Clinical Biomechanics, 2024) demonstrated that a 14 mm cage height maximized decompression of neural structures but concurrently increased subsidence risk, particularly in osteoporotic models where endplate displacement increased by 63% compared with normal bone models. These findings, combined with the parametric analysis by Li et al. [50] recommending a height not exceeding 12 mm, establish a clinically relevant height range. The recommendation for a 10–12 mm maximum height in patients with osteoporosis or osteopenia balances the need for adequate decompression against the risk of catastrophic endplate failure. However, in patients with normal bone quality, heights up to 14 mm may be acceptable if the primary goal is maximal indirect decompression.

Integrated considerations for cage size selection

The optimal cage size represents a multifactorial decision integrating patient-specific bone quality, desired degree of decompression, target lordosis correction, and risk tolerance for subsidence. The evidence supports a hierarchical approach: (1) maximize footprint within the anatomical constraints of the disc space to distribute load; (2) select a banana-shaped cage when lordosis restoration is a priority; (3) consider expandable cages for patients requiring precise height restoration with lower subsidence risk; and (4) respect the height thresholds recommended by current biomechanical evidence to minimize endplate failure.

Endplate quality and cage subsidence: the challenge of osteoporosis

Vertebral endplate bone quality plays a critical role in determining the risk of cage subsidence, an effect that is particularly pronounced in patients with osteoporosis. In osteoporotic patients, reduced endplate bone mineral density and sparse trabecular architecture lead to diminished endplate strength, making cage subsidence more likely [9, 59]. Wang et al. [59] reported that lateral plate fixation may increase the risk of instrumentation failure in osteoporotic patients, suggesting that bilateral pedicle screw fixation may provide superior stability in this population.

The clinical utility of preoperative endplate assessment has been strengthened by recent imaging-based evidence. Li et al. [68] (Quantitative Imaging in Medicine and Surgery, 2024) demonstrated that MRI-based endplate bone quality scores can reliably predict cage subsidence following lumbar interbody fusion, with lower scores indicating higher risk. This finding highlights the importance of preoperative assessment of endplate bone quality for predicting subsidence risk and guiding cage selection. In osteoporotic patients, in addition to selecting larger cages with favorable osseointegration properties, augmentation strategies such as cement-enhanced screw fixation or more robust posterior instrumentation may be required to provide adequate support and stability and to reduce the risk of subsidence.

Clinical implications: preoperative endplate assessment and risk stratification

Preoperative assessment of endplate bone quality has emerged as a critical determinant of cage subsidence risk and should guide surgical planning. Li et al. [68] demonstrated that MRI-based endplate bone quality scores can reliably predict cage subsidence following lumbar interbody fusion, with lower scores indicating higher risk. For patients with suspected osteoporosis, quantitative CT-based volumetric bone mineral density (vBMD) assessment is recommended, with a threshold of 83 mg/cm3 serving as a practical cutoff for identifying high-risk patients [57].

Based on current evidence, the following risk-stratified approach is proposed:

Normal bone quality (vBMD > 120 mg/cm3) All cage types and fixation strategies are suitable. Large-footprint 3D-printed porous titanium or PEEK cages with stand-alone or lateral plate fixation may be preferred for single-level disease.

Osteopenia (vBMD 83–120 mg/cm3) Larger-footprint cages with favorable osseointegration properties (3D-printed porous titanium) combined with supplemental fixation (lateral plate or posterior pedicle screws) are recommended. Stand-alone constructs should be avoided.

Osteoporosis (vBMD < 83 mg/cm3) Robust posterior fixation (bilateral pedicle screws or CBT) is recommended, potentially with cement augmentation. Large-footprint 3D-printed titanium cages with optimized porous architecture should be selected. Expandable cages allowing in situ height restoration may be beneficial.

Severe osteoporosis: Consider staged procedures, bone density optimization with teriparatide or zoledronate preoperatively, and cement-augmented screw fixation.

Biomechanical considerations in adjacent segment degeneration

Adjacent segment degeneration is a common long-term complication after spinal fusion, with a multifactorial pathogenesis in which biomechanical factors represent a major driving component [28, 29]. By immobilizing one or more motion segments, spinal fusion restores stability at the treated level(s) but inevitably alters global spinal biomechanics, increasing stress and inducing compensatory hypermobility at adjacent non-fused segments, thereby accelerating degenerative changes.

Biomechanical mechanisms of ASD: redistribution of stress and motion

ASD arises from altered biomechanical loading at adjacent segments following loss of motion at the fused level. First, compensatory increases in ROM occur after motion is eliminated at the fused segment, which may accelerate wear and degeneration of the intervertebral disc, facet joints, and ligaments [28, 29]. Using finite element analysis, Chen et al. found that PLIF produced the greatest increases in adjacent-segment ROM, intradiscal pressure (IDP), and stress among the evaluated procedures [28]. Second, altered load sharing associated with increased adjacent-segment ROM can elevate IDP and annulus fibrosus stress, thereby promoting disc degeneration [28, 29]. Jo et al. further reported, via finite element analysis, that osteoporosis can markedly increase IDP [29]. Third, restriction of motion at the fused level can increase facet joint loading at adjacent segments, accelerating cartilage wear and facet degeneration [28, 63].

Effects of cage and fixation strategies on ASD

Different types of interbody cages and fixation strategies exert distinct biomechanical influences on the risk of ASD.

Cage materials and design

Owing to an elastic modulus closer to that of bone, PEEK cages are theoretically associated with reduced stress shielding and may therefore exert a smaller biomechanical impact on adjacent segments. However, finite element analysis by Li et al. demonstrated no significant differences between PEEK rods and titanium rods in terms of ROM, adjacent disc stress, or facet joint forces [25]. Nevertheless, PEEK rods were associated with increased cage and endplate stress, which may indirectly influence the development of ASD [25]. By contrast, 3D-printed porous titanium cages, through optimization of elastic modulus and promotion of osseointegration, may help preserve more physiological load transfer at the fused segment over the long term, thereby mitigating excessive stress on adjacent segments.

Fixation strategies

Conventional bilateral pedicle screw fixation provides the greatest stability; however, its high rigidity markedly increases adjacent-segment ROM and stress, resulting in the highest risk of ASD [9, 10, 63]. As discussed in “Posterior pedicle screw fixation: the “gold standard” and the challenge of adjacent segment stress” section, semi-rigid fixation systems using PCU rods represent a promising approach to mitigate this effect. Chou et al. demonstrated that PCU rods in OLIF resulted in the smallest increase in adjacent-segment stress while maintaining adequate construct stability [30]. CBT screw fixation may reduce ASD incidence by decreasing ROM and stress at adjacent segments [65]. Zhang et al. showed that bilateral pedicle screw fixation generated higher intradiscal pressure and facet cartilage stress than unilateral fixation, suggesting that UPSF exerts a less pronounced biomechanical impact on adjacent segments [63]. The evidence collectively supports a stiffness-matching paradigm: fixation systems with elastic properties closer to physiological values minimize adjacent-segment stress increases.

Strategies for preventing ASD: integrated biomechanical interventions

Based on the biomechanical evidence reviewed above, several integrated strategies can be proposed for ASD prevention. First, restoration of physiological sagittal alignment is essential. By selecting appropriate cage size and geometry and ensuring precise placement, disc height and lumbar lordosis at the fused segment can be maximally restored, thereby optimizing global sagittal balance and reducing mechanical loading on adjacent segments [16]. Second, fixation stiffness should be carefully matched to patient characteristics. For younger patients with good bone quality, semi-rigid fixation (e.g., PCU rods) may allow controlled micromotion at the fused segment and alleviate stress concentration at adjacent levels [30]. For patients with osteoporosis requiring robust fixation, CBT may provide adequate stability while reducing adjacent-segment impact compared with traditional pedicle screws [65]. Third, cage design can be optimized through topology optimization and patient-specific approaches to achieve more uniform load distribution, thereby promoting fusion while minimizing biomechanical impact on adjacent segments [46, 47]. Fourth, preservation of the posterior ligamentous complex represents an additional protective factor; finite element analysis by He et al. demonstrated that maintaining this structure can mitigate ASD-related biomechanical deterioration in lumbar fusion models [69].

Clinical implications: ASD prevention strategy

A pragmatic ASD prevention framework should integrate the following evidence-based recommendations: (1) In patients younger than 60 years with normal bone quality undergoing 1–2 level OLIF, consider semi-rigid PCU rod fixation to minimize adjacent-segment stress. (2) In osteoporotic patients requiring strong fixation, CBT offers a favorable balance between construct stability and adjacent-segment protection compared with traditional bilateral pedicle screws. (3) Excessively rigid bilateral pedicle screw fixation should be reserved for cases with severe instability, deformity, or multilevel fusion where maximum stability is required. (4) Regardless of fixation strategy, meticulous attention to sagittal alignment restoration and avoidance of over-distraction represent fundamental protective measures. (5) Long-term radiographic surveillance should be planned for all OLIF patients, with particular attention to the cranial adjacent segment.

Perspectives: implications of cervical zero-profile cages, emerging technologies, and future directions

With continuous advances in spinal surgical techniques, biomaterials science, and digital technologies, interbody cage design has increasingly shifted toward greater integration, personalization, and intelligent automation. Within this evolutionary trajectory, the successful clinical application of zero-profile cages in cervical fusion has provided important insights for the future design of lumbar interbody cages, particularly those used in OLIF. Furthermore, artificial intelligence and robotic-assisted surgery are poised to transform both cage design and surgical execution.

Development and advantages of cervical zero-profile cages

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a widely used procedure for the treatment of cervical degenerative diseases. In conventional ACDF, additional anterior plates and screws are typically required after cage implantation to provide immediate stability. However, this traditional plate-based fixation has several limitations: plate prominence may irritate the esophagus and lead to postoperative dysphagia, and stress concentration at the plate-vertebral interface may contribute to screw loosening or breakage.

To address these challenges, cervical zero-profile cages were developed. These devices integrate the cage body with fixation elements into a single implant, conferring several key biomechanical advantages. First, reduced hardware prominence is achieved through a lower-profile integrated design, which minimizes irritation to surrounding soft tissues such as the esophagus and thereby reduces the incidence of postoperative dysphagia [70, 71]. A recent meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials evaluating zero-profile versus conventional plate-and-cage constructs in ACDF confirmed significantly lower dysphagia rates with zero-profile cages while demonstrating comparable fusion rates and clinical outcomes [58]. Using finite element analysis, Ni et al. demonstrated that a novel plate-locking interbody cage exhibited significantly lower peak stresses than conventional zero-profile cages, with reduced risks of fracture, loosening, and implant migration [70]. Second, enhanced immediate stability is provided by the unified cage-fixation construct, offering stability comparable to or even superior to traditional plate fixation and effectively resisting migration and subsidence. Third, the integrated implant simplifies surgical procedures by reducing operative steps, shortening surgical time, and lowering technical complexity. Finally, optimized stress distribution allows more uniform load sharing, mitigates local stress concentration, and decreases the risks of screw loosening, breakage, and cage subsidence.

Implications of cervical zero-profile cages for lumbar OLIF cage design

In OLIF, to enhance the stability of stand-alone constructs, interbody cages are commonly supplemented with lateral/anterior plate fixation or posterior pedicle screw instrumentation. Lateral/anterior plating serves a role analogous to anterior cervical plates, providing additional fixation anterior to the cage. Therefore, the design philosophy of cervical zero-profile cages has direct translational relevance for OLIF cage development. Indeed, recent studies have begun to explore integrated cage-fixation concepts for OLIF. Cai et al. exemplified this trend by developing an OLIF cage that integrates a low-profile plate with crossed screws and comparing its biomechanics with conventional OLIF plus plate-and-screw fixation [62]. Their findings indicated that the novel LPCS-OLIF cage has intrinsic biomechanical advantages in establishing immediate postoperative stability and reducing stress concentration-related complications [62]. Collectively, these results provide direct evidence supporting the feasibility and potential superiority of translating the cervical zero-profile concept to lumbar OLIF applications.

Drawing on the experience gained from cervical zero-profile cages, future OLIF cage design may evolve along several key directions. First, integration of the cage and fixation components–combining the OLIF cage body with lateral fixation screws or a low-profile plate into a single implant–can markedly reduce hardware prominence and minimize irritation to the psoas muscle, retroperitoneal vessels, and neural structures, thereby decreasing the risks of postoperative pain, neurological injury, and vascular complications. Such an integrated design may provide greater immediate stability, reduce the risks of cage migration and subsidence, and, particularly in osteoporotic patients, potentially obviate or reduce the need for supplemental posterior pedicle screw fixation, thereby limiting surgical invasiveness. Second, material and structural innovation enabled by additive manufacturing allows the fabrication of porous, integrated titanium cages, in which tailored porosity optimizes elastic modulus, promotes bone ingrowth, and reduces stress shielding and subsidence risk. Combining PEEK with titanium alloys or other bioactive materials to create multilayered or functionally graded integrated cages may further achieve an optimal balance between mechanical performance and biological activity, while incorporation of bioresorbable materials into fixation components could permit gradual degradation after solid fusion, avoiding complications related to long-term hardware retention. Third, personalization and topology optimization based on patient-specific imaging data, together with 3D printing, may enable integrated cages to precisely match individual endplate morphology and biomechanical demands, optimize load transmission, restore individualized lumbar lordosis, and improve sagittal balance.

Challenges and future directions in integrated cage design

Although integrated OLIF cages hold considerable promise, their development is still confronted with several challenges. These include comprehensive validation of biomechanical performance, optimization of surgical approaches and operative techniques, continued advances in materials science, and rigorous evaluation of cost-effectiveness. Future OLIF cage design is expected to progress toward greater intelligence, personalization, and functional integration, with integrated cage-fixation constructs likely to become the dominant design paradigm. By combining additive manufacturing, novel biomaterials, and artificial intelligence technologies, next-generation lumbar interbody cages may be developed to maximize fusion rates, minimize complication risks, and ultimately achieve favorable long-term patient outcomes.

Conclusions

OLIF is a minimally invasive spinal fusion technique that has demonstrated clear advantages in the treatment of lumbar degenerative disorders. In OLIF, the interbody cage represents the core implant, and its biomechanical performance directly determines immediate postoperative stability, fusion potential, restoration of disc height and lumbar lordosis, stress distribution, and the risk of adjacent segment degeneration. This review has systematically summarized recent biomechanical advances in OLIF combined with different cage types, critically evaluating the advantages, limitations, and levels of evidence for each approach.

With respect to material selection, 3D-printed porous titanium cages have emerged as a leading focus of current research, offering tunable elastic modulus, superior osseointegration, reduced subsidence risk, and patient-specific customization potential. PEEK cages retain advantages in radiolucency and stress-shielding reduction but lack osteoinductivity. Conventional titanium alloys provide high strength and excellent osseointegration at the cost of pronounced stress shielding. Emerging biodegradable and composite cages hold theoretical promise but require further clinical validation.

The choice of fixation strategy should be guided by patient-specific risk stratification. For patients with normal bone quality undergoing single-level fusion, stand-alone large-footprint cages are biomechanically adequate. Lateral plate fixation offers a favorable balance between invasiveness and stability for most patients requiring supplemental fixation. Bilateral posterior pedicle screw fixation remains indicated for severe instability, osteoporosis, or multilevel constructs, though its association with ASD risk warrants consideration of semi-rigid alternatives (e.g., PCU rods) or CBT fixation where appropriate. The clinical decision-making framework presented in “Clinical decision-making guidance: patient-specific algorithm for OLIF cage and fixation selection” section provides detailed, evidence-based guidance for patient-specific strategy selection.

Cage position, size, and geometry exert decisive effects on biomechanical outcomes. Central-anterior positioning within the disc space optimizes the balance between lordosis restoration and subsidence risk. Banana-shaped cages and expandable cage technology offer advantages for lordosis restoration and height optimization, respectively. Endplate bone quality–assessed through MRI-based scores or quantitative CT-based vBMD–should guide both cage selection and fixation strategy, with robust posterior fixation recommended for vBMD below 83 mg/cm3.

Looking forward, the integration of cervical zero-profile cage concepts, artificial intelligence-driven design optimization, and robotic-assisted surgical techniques holds transformative potential for OLIF. However, the translation of these innovations into routine clinical practice requires rigorous prospective validation.

Research gaps and future directions

Despite substantial progress in OLIF cage biomechanics, several critical gaps remain. First, long-term clinical outcomes (> = 5 years) for 3D-printed porous titanium, biodegradable, and composite cages are lacking; large-scale prospective multicenter trials are needed to confirm their durability and complication profiles. Second, personalized cage designs–despite promising FEA evidence–require clinical validation through prospective comparative studies to demonstrate superiority over off-the-shelf alternatives. Third, dynamic loading and fatigue behavior under physiological conditions remain understudied; most evidence derives from static FEA or single-cycle in vitro testing, which may not capture long-term implant behavior. Fourth, the optimal degradation kinetics for biodegradable cages, ensuring sufficient mechanical support until solid fusion is achieved, require further refinement. Fifth, AI-driven cage design frameworks, while technically promising, need multicenter external validation before clinical integration. Finally, cost-effectiveness analyses comparing conventional, 3D-printed, patient-specific, and integrated cage designs are essential to inform healthcare resource allocation.

Fusion level-based recommendations

The number of levels to be fused influences both cage selection and fixation strategy, as multilevel constructs are associated with progressively greater biomechanical demands and higher subsidence and nonunion risks.

Single-level SA or LPF with large-footprint cage is biomechanically adequate for most patients with normal bone quality. BPSF should be considered if osteoporosis or significant instability is present.

Age and activity level considerations

Patient age and functional demands should inform the balance between stability and motion preservation:

Young, active patients (< 50 years) Consider semi-rigid fixation (PCU rods) to minimize ASD risk. Large-footprint 3D-printed porous titanium cages are preferred for their osseointegration potential and durability. Avoid overly rigid constructs unless severe instability mandates BPSF.

Elderly, low-demand patients (> 70 years) Standard fixation is appropriate; prioritize construct stability over motion preservation. Minimize surgical invasiveness where possible. Preoperative bone density optimization with teriparatide or anti-resorptive therapy should be considered.

High-demand athletes or manual laborers BPSF with large-footprint 3D-printed titanium cage is recommended to provide maximum stability and facilitate early return to activity. CBT may be considered as a less invasive alternative if bone quality is adequate.

Key evidence-based recommendations for clinical practice

Based on the evidence synthesized in this review, we provide the following graded recommendations:

Recommendation 1 For patients with normal bone quality undergoing single-level OLIF, stand-alone large-footprint cages (3D-printed porous titanium or PEEK) are biomechanically adequate. Evidence: Multiple FEA Level I-II studies; Clinical Level III. Grade: B.
