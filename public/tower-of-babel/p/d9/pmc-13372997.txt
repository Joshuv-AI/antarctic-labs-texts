Integrated Genetic and Biochemical Approach to Patients with Bone Disorders Exhibiting Low Alkaline Phosphatase
Authors: Naoko Hidaka, Soichiro Kimura, So Watanabe, Tomoyuki Akiyama, Kanako Tachikawa, Toshimi Michigami, Natsuho Adachi, Koki Irie, Yoshitomo Hoshino, Hajime Kato, Masaomi Nangaku, Sakae Tanaka, Noriko Makita, Taku Saito, Nobuaki Ito
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypophosphatasia (HPP) is a rare metabolic disorder caused by loss-of-function ALPL variants and characterized by heterogeneous skeletal and extra-skeletal manifestations. Although recent diagnostic criteria include ALPL variants, the diagnosis can be made based solely on clinical features. In adult-onset HPP, however, symptoms are often mild or nonspecific, including musculoskeletal symptoms and fatigue, making it difficult to differentiate from other conditions. This retrospective, cross-sectional study aimed to integrate genetic and biochemical data to enhance the clinical interpretation of low alkaline phosphatase (ALP) in patients with bone disorders. Among 865 consecutive adults visiting a bone-specialized outpatient department, patients with persistent hypophosphatasemia underwent ALPL sequencing. Clinical and biochemical characteristics were compared among three groups: biallelic or monoallelic variants with dominant-negative effect (DNE), monoallelic variants without DNE, and variant-negative individuals. Fifty-six patients (6.5%) showed persistent hypophosphatasemia. Two (0.2%) were compound heterozygotes, and 29 (3.4%) carried monoallelic variants without DNE. Clinical symptoms and secondary causes of hypophosphatasemia were similar between monoallelic without DNE and variant-negative patients, whereas serum ALP, urinary phosphoethanolamine (PEA), and plasma inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi) differed significantly. A model using thresholds of ALP < 35 IU/L, urinary PEA > 91 µmol/gCr, and plasma PPi > 1.96 µmol/L demonstrated high diagnostic performance for identifying patients with monoallelic variants without DNE. This study elucidated the complex clinical spectrum of patients with low ALP. Although clinical manifestations were comparable between monoallelic individuals without DNE and variant-negative individuals, distinct biochemical profiles were observed. Introduction

Hypophosphatasia (HPP) is a rare metabolic bone disorder caused by loss-of-function variants in the ALPL gene. Tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNSALP), encoded by ALPL, is ubiquitously expressed and plays a key role in bone mineralization by hydrolyzing inorganic pyrophosphate (PPi) within bone matrix vesicles to supply inorganic phosphate. Conventionally, HPP has been classified by age of onset and whether symptoms are limited to the teeth: perinatal (lethal), perinatal benign, infantile, childhood, adult-onset, and odontohypophosphatasia. These categories largely reflect disease severity; however, substantial phenotypic heterogeneity exists, especially among patients with milder forms such as childhood- and adult-onset HPP [1, 2]. For example, manifestations of mild adult-onset HPP include chronic musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, and impaired mobility in addition to skeletal manifestations, and recent studies have also suggested neurological symptoms, such as headache, sleep disturbances, and depression [3]. It has been recognized that the diagnosis of adult-onset HPP requires a multidisciplinary approach. To address this diversity, international diagnostic criteria for adult-onset HPP were recently proposed [4]. The new criteria comprise four major and five minor items, and the diagnosis can be made when an individual fulfills either two major criteria or one major and two minor criteria. Although this framework is sophisticated and clinically useful, potential pitfalls remain. The definition of ALPL variants as a major criterion is not stringent, and thus, diagnosis can be made solely on clinical manifestations. This approach might have two advantages. One is that patients with characteristic manifestations of HPP can be diagnosed even when genetic confirmation is unavailable. Another is that patients harboring deep intronic variants that may be missed by conventional genetic testing can still be diagnosed based on clinical manifestations and biochemical findings. Conversely, reliance on clinical manifestations alone may complicate diagnostic interpretation, since many of these features are nonspecific and overlap with other conditions. Considering that HPP fundamentally arises from loss-of-function ALPL variants, both the presence of the variant and its residual enzymatic activity should be emphasized in diagnosis.

A recent study proposed a novel genetic nosology based on in vitro enzymatic activity, the presence of a dominant negative effect (DNE), and zygosity of the variant [5]. ALPL variants were categorized into three functional types: variants with severe loss of activity (< 20% of wild type, s), variants with severe loss and DNE (< 40% of wild type, Sd) [6], and variants with mild loss (≧20% of wild type, m). By combining these functional classes with zygosity, three clinical categories of HPP were proposed: severe (homozygous s/Sd/m or compound heterozygous s/Sd), moderate (compound heterozygous m/any or heterozygous Sd), and mild (heterozygous s or m). Patients diagnosed as HPP for the first time in adulthood may fall into the moderate or mild category, traditionally classified as childhood- or adult-onset HPP. This genetic nosology, however, raises questions regarding the clinical interpretation of heterozygous individuals carrying non-DNE variants. Although these individuals are classified as having “mild” HPP, their clinical manifestations are highly variable, ranging from asymptomatic carriers to patients with skeletal and/or extra-skeletal symptoms. The clinical significance of this category and its implications for patient management remain incompletely understood.

The increasing availability of enzyme replacement therapy (ERT) with asfotase alfa (AA) has further emphasized the importance of accurate patient stratification. Although AA, a bone-targeted recombinant alkaline phosphatase (ALP), has proven efficacy for skeletal symptoms, evidence supporting its benefit in extra-skeletal manifestations, such as muscle pain, headache, depression, and calcium pyrophosphate deposition (CPPD) disease, remains limited [7]. Therefore, improved understanding of the biological and clinical significance of ALPL variants may contribute to more individualized therapeutic decision-making.

To clarify the clinical implications of ALPL genotypes and guide their appropriate interpretation in real-world practice, we performed ALPL exon sequencing in all patients with hypophosphatasemia visiting a bone-specialized outpatient department. Based on the genetic nosology, patients were categorized into three groups: biallelic or monoallelic with DNE variants, monoallelic without DNE variants, and variant-negative, and their clinical and biochemical characteristics were described and compared.

Patients and Methods

Study Design

This was a retrospective, single-center, cross-sectional observational study conducted at the Osteoporosis Center of the University of Tokyo Hospital, a tertiary referral institution. The Osteoporosis Center provides consultation services for patients with bone disorders, including severe/early-onset/male osteoporosis, hereditary and acquired bone metabolic diseases, skeletal dysplasia, and other complex pathophysiological conditions. The multidisciplinary team at the center comprises endocrinologists, orthopedic surgeons, geriatricians, gynecologists, and rheumatologists. Patients who visited the center between December 2018 and December 2024 were consecutively included in the study. Among them, patients with two or more serum ALP measurements below the age-specific reference range at the referral clinics or our institution were classified as having recurrent hypophosphatasemia, irrespective of the interval between measurements. The reference range for serum ALP was 38 to113 IU/L for adults (≥ 18 years), whereas age- and sex-specific reference ranges were applied to patients younger than 18 years. These patients underwent baseline laboratory examinations and genetic testing for ALPL variants, including the exons and exon–intron boundaries, after appropriate genetic counselling. The pathogenicity of identified variants was assessed using the ALPL gene variant database (https://alplmutationdatabase.jku.at/) [8], which provides ACMG/AMP-based classifications together with curated functional data, including residual enzymatic activity and DNE, derived from validated in vitro assays and linked to the corresponding literature references. Functional characteristics, including residual enzymatic activity and DNE status, were extracted from the database and used for variant categorization. The single variant of uncertain significance (VUS) identified in this study was independently evaluated using in silico prediction tools (MutationTaster2021 [9] and CADD [10]), population allele frequency (gnomAD database v 4.1.0 [11] and jMorp [12]), and the original publication [13, 14].

Data Collection

Patients with hypophosphatasemia underwent blood and urine testing in a clinical setting, including measurements of serum ALP activity, albumin-corrected calcium, inorganic phosphate, intact parathyroid hormone (PTH), 25-hydroxyvitamin D, bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BAP), tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase 5b, zinc, magnesium, copper, adrenocorticotropic hormone, cortisol, thyroid-stimulating hormone, and ALP substrates (urinary phosphoethanolamine [PEA], plasma PPi, and pyridoxal 5′-phosphate [PLP]). Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scans of the lumbar spine (L2–L4) and femur were also performed. Medication history regarding glucocorticoids and antiresorptive agents (bisphosphonates and denosumab), nutritional supplementation, symptoms, and past medical history were routinely collected through questionnaires and medical interviews at the Osteoporosis Center. For the present study, manifestations potentially associated with adult HPP were systematically assessed using a structured questionnaire. These included fracture history (timing, anatomical site, and circumstances), dental abnormalities (adult tooth loss and/or dental caries), joint pain, muscle pain lasting ≥ 3 months, headache lasting ≥ 3 months, CPPD disease, and depression. For muscle pain and headache, symptom frequency was categorized as daily, ≥ 15 days per month, or < 15 days per month. Gene sequencing analysis of the ALPL exon/exon–intron boundaries was available at the Kazusa DNA Research Institute within routine clinical practice, covered by insurance. Plasma PPi and serum PLP were measured for research purposes. Plasma PPi was quantified using the ATP sulfurylase method, as previously described in detail [15]. As no established adult reference interval is currently available for plasma PPi measured by this method, the reference range used in this study (1.47–2.97 µM) was based on the interval reported in 10 healthy adults after overnight fasting [16]. PLP measurement was performed by Dr. Akiyama at Okayama University Hospital, following a previously published protocol [17]. Serum PLP values obtained during vitamin B6 supplementation were excluded from group comparisons and statistical analyses. These baseline laboratory examinations were performed using standardized assays.

Assessment of the Enzymatic Activity of the Novel ALPL Variant

Using ALP cDNA, the expression plasmid for green fluorescent protein (GFP)-tagged TNSALP (pcDNA-GFP-ALP) was constructed [18], and the identified ALPL variant (c.77C > A [p.Pro26His]) was introduced with the QuikChange Lightning Site-Directed Mutagenesis Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). The expression plasmids of the wild type, a variant TNSALP (p.Pro26His identified in Patient 56), and vitamin D receptor (pSG5-VDR-GFP) as a negative control were introduced into COS-7 cells using FuGENE HD Reagent (Roche, Indianapolis, IN). Three days after transfection, cell lysates were harvested in 10 mM Tris–Cl and 0.05% Tris-Triton X-100. The enzymatic activity of ALP in the cell lysates was measured using p-nitrophenylphosphate as a substrate. The expression of GFP-tagged protein (TNSALP or VDR) was confirmed by Western blotting using aliquots of the cell lysates and the anti-GFP antibody. Densitometry of the signals was performed using ImageJ software, and the enzymatic activity of the cell lysates was normalized based on the signal intensity.

In Vitro Validation of Compound Heterozygosity

To evaluate whether the two ALPL pathogenic variants identified in each of the two patients (Patients 55 and 56) were located on the same allele or on the opposite allele, direct cloning of the genomic DNA fragment harboring both variant positions was performed. In the first PCR, genomic DNA was amplified using primers flanking the region containing the two variant positions (Supplementary Table 1). The PCR products were then used as templates for a second PCR, which generated fragments compatible with ligation into the linearized pcDNA3.1( +) vector using In-Fusion® Snap Assembly Master Mix (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan), with primers designed for this purpose (Supplementary Table 1). The resulting expression plasmids were introduced into DH5α high Champion™ competent E. coli cells (SMOBIO Technology Inc., Taiwan) and cultured on LB agar plates containing ampicillin. Colonies (approximately 10 per sample) were picked, and plasmids were isolated using a standard miniprep protocol. Sanger sequencing was performed on purified plasmids to determine the allele status (wild-type or variant) at both variant positions within each cloned fragment.

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables were primarily treated as continuous variables and summarized using medians and interquartile ranges. Clinical and biochemical characteristics were compared between variant-negative individuals (n = 25) and monoallelic without DNE patients (n = 29). Categorical variables were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test, while continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. To distinguish between monoallelic without DNE patients from variant-negative individuals, diagnostic models were developed using ALP, urinary PEA, and plasma PPi, which demonstrated significant between-group differences. This analysis included 49 patients (variant-negative: n = 21; monoallelic without DNE: n = 28) with complete data for urinary PEA and plasma PPi. Optimal cut-off values for each variable were determined using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. Four diagnostic models were then constructed: (1) ALP alone, (2) ALP + urinary PEA, (3) ALP + plasma PPi, and (4) all three variables combined. Model performance was evaluated by calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio (PLR), and negative likelihood ratio (NLR), each with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). These metrics, as well as the area under the ROC curve (AUC) were estimated using the bootstrap method with 1,000 resamplings. Because antiresorptive agents may secondarily reduce ALP levels by suppressing bone turnover, a sensitivity analysis was performed in a subset of 36 patients who had not received these agents. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.0).

Study Approval

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Tokyo Hospital (reference numbers 2879 and 11221). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment in the study.

Results

Genetic Classification Based on ALPL Variants

Among 865 patients who visited the Osteoporosis Center over six years (from December 2018 to December 2024), 56 patients (6.5%, 56/865, Patients 1–56 in Supplementary Table 2) exhibited persistently low serum ALP levels. All 56 patients consented to ALPL sequencing. Pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants, as well as VUS, were identified in 31 patients (3.6%, 31/865). Two of these patients (0.2%, 2/865) harbored two pathogenic variants, while the remaining 29 patients (3.4%, 29/865) carried a single variant consistent with heterozygosity (Fig. 1).

Patient 55 had c.979 T > C (p.Phe327Leu) and c.1559delT (p.Leu520ArgfsTer86), whereas patient 56 had c.77C > A (p.Pro26His) and c.299C > T (p.Thr100Met) (Table 1). Cloning and Sanger sequencing of fragments flanking both variant sites confirmed that the variants were located on different alleles, establishing compound heterozygosity in both cases (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Excluding 25 patients without ALPL variants (classified as “variant-negative”) and the 2 patients with biallelic variants (classified as “biallelic”), the remaining 29 patients were classified as “monoallelic without DNE”, since the variants they carried lacked evidence of DNE (Fig. 1). The most frequent variant among monoallelic without DNE patients was c.1559delT (p.Leu520ArgfsTer86) (n = 14), a well-established severe loss-of-function variant prevalent in Japanese patients with the perinatal severe HPP [19]. This variant exhibits approximately 5% residual enzymatic activity in vitro [6]. The second most common variant was c.979 T > C (p.Phe327Leu) (n = 7), which retains approximately 70% of wild-type ALP activity [20] (Table 1). One variant, c.613G > A (p.Ala205Thr) (Patient 47), was classified as a VUS, but may represent a mild loss-of-function allele based on in silico prediction (MutationTaster2021; CADD [score: 27.9]) and population frequency [13, 14].

Accordingly, the 56 patients were categorized into three groups: variant-negative (n = 25), monoallelic without DNE (n = 29), and biallelic (n = 2). No patients with a monoallelic DNE variant were identified in our cohort. Their clinical and biochemical characteristics were summarized in Table 2 and Supplementary Table 2.

Case Description of Biallelic Patients (Patients 55 and 56)

Patient 55

A 17-year-old female with persistent hypophosphatasemia over the past two years was referred to the Osteoporosis Center for evaluation of possible HPP. She was born small for gestational age (2010 g at 40 weeks and 3 days) and exhibited decreased suckling in the neonatal period. During her school age, she was diagnosed with a learning disorder, mild intellectual disability, and autism spectrum disorder. At age 15, routine blood testing conducted by her pediatric psychiatrist for iron deficiency anemia incidentally revealed persistently low serum ALP. At her initial visit, laboratory findings confirmed low ALP (9 IU/L; reference range at 17-year-old: 42–200 IU/L), elevated urinary PEA (270.1 µmol/gCr; reference range: 31.0–110.0 µmol/gCr), elevated serum PLP (419.9 nmol/L; reference range: 19.8–200.0 nmol/L [21]), and serum PLP-to-pyridoxal (PL) ratio (35.6; reference range: 2.3–13.7 [21]). Genetic analysis, supplemented by cloning of the relevant ALPL region, identified compound heterozygous variants c.979 T > C (p.Phe327Leu) and c.1559delT (p.Leu520ArgfsTer86), confirming the diagnosis of HPP (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 1A). Despite these biochemical abnormalities, she had no history of fractures, musculoskeletal pain, dental abnormalities, or rickets-like deformities; therefore, ERT was not initiated. After her diagnosis, her mother—who had mild but persistent hypophosphatasemia—was also evaluated as part of the present study (Patient 40) and was found to carry a monoallelic c.1559delT (p.Leu520ArgfsTer86) variant, consistent with the daughter’s compound heterozygosity.

Patient 56

A 48-year-old female presented to the Osteoporosis Center with a history of recurrent polyarticular pain and swelling. She had a premature loss of permanent teeth in childhood, and since her twenties, had experienced repeated knee, shoulder, and hip pain. Joint calcifications on imaging led to a clinical impression of CPPD disease, and she received symptomatic management for many years. At age 47, routine blood tests revealed persistent hypophosphatasemia, prompting evaluation for HPP. At her initial visit, serum ALP was low (8 IU/L; reference range: 38–113 IU/L), with mild hypercalcemia (10.3 mg/dL; reference range: 8.8–10.1 mg/dL) and hyperphosphatemia (5.7 mg/dL; reference range: 2.7–4.6 mg/dL). Urinary PEA was elevated (521.9 µmol/gCr), while plasma PPi was 4.40 µmol/L (reference range: 1.47–2.97 µmol/L). Although serum PLP was markedly elevated (11,783.5 nmol/L) due to vitamin B6 supplementation, the PLP-to-PL ratio (20.1) remained consistent with reduced ALP activity. Genetic analysis and subsequent cloning-based verification identified compound heterozygous ALPL variants: c.77C > A (p.Pro26His) and c.299C > T (p.Thr100Met) (Table 1; Supplementary Fig. 1B). Functional analysis revealed that the newly identified p.Pro26His variant retained approximately 70% of the wild-type ALP activity, indicating a mildly reduced enzymatic function (Fig. 2), while p.Thr100Met has been shown to possess DNE (28.2% of wild type) previously [6]. Technetium-99 m hydroxymethylene diphosphonate (99mTc-HMDP) bone scintigraphy demonstrated multiple areas of increased uptake in the thoracic vertebrae, bilateral femoral heads, and metatarsal bones, consistent with pseudofractures (Fig. 3A). Based on these findings, ERT with AA was initiated to improve her skeletal manifestations. Her joint pain resolved within one month (Brief Pain Inventory score: 5.7 to 0.0), although a self-limited episode of left shoulder pain occurred six months later, consistent with CPPD. Radiographic joint calcifications persisted (Fig. 3B), but serial scintigraphy demonstrated progressive healing of pseudofractures, with one new lesion detected at the right metatarsal at one year (Fig. 3A). Her functional capacity improved markedly, with her 6-min walk distance increasing from 430 to 718 m (reference mean distance in Japanese healthy adults aged 40 to 49 years: 703 m [22]).

Biallelic Compared to Monoallelic Without DNE and Variant-Negative Patients

Serum ALP levels were markedly low in the two biallelic patients (9 IU/L in Patient 55 and 8 IU/L in Patient 56), whereas all monoallelic patients without DNE and variant-negative individuals had levels above 10 IU/L (Fig. 4). Urinary PEA and plasma PPi concentrations were also higher in the biallelic patients than in the other groups. Although the extremely elevated PLP level in Patient 56 was attributable to vitamin B6 supplementation, Patient 55 also showed higher PLP than the monoallelic without DNE and variant-negative groups (Fig. 4). Mild increases in serum calcium and inorganic phosphate were observed in both biallelic patients, although statistical comparison was limited due to the small number of cases. Of the 56 patients in the cohort, ERT with AA was initiated only in Patient 56.

Comparison Between Monoallelic Without DNE and Variant-Negative Patients

At baseline, serum ALP levels were within the reference range in 16 variant-negative individuals (16/25, 64.0%) and in 5 patients with a monoallelic variant without DNE (5/29, 17.2%), suggesting that some cases represented temporal hypophosphatasemia. Clinical and biochemical characteristics stratified by ALP levels were shown in Supplementary Table 3. Secondary causes of low ALP, including zinc or magnesium deficiency, profound hypothyroidism, hypercortisolism, and use of antiresorptives [23], were present in both variant-negative and monoallelic without DNE groups at similar frequencies (Supplementary Table 4). Exposure to antiresorptives (bisphosphonates or denosumab) was comparable between groups. Findings suggestive of atypical fracture were observed in two variant-negative individuals (Patients 7 and 20) and one patient with a monoallelic variant without DNE (Patient 38). None of the seven glucocorticoid users received doses consistent with iatrogenic Cushing’s syndrome (Supplementary Table 2). One variant-negative patient (Patient 24) showed markedly elevated serum copper, but the concurrently high ceruloplasmin level (55.7 mg/dL; reference range: 21.0–37.0 mg/dL) made Wilson disease unlikely [23].

Serum ALP and BAP activities were significantly lower in monoallelic without DNE than in variant-negative patients (p-value: < 0.0001 and < 0.001, respectively). Consistently, ALP substrates (urinary PEA, plasma PPi, and serum PLP) were significantly higher in the monoallelic without DNE group (p-value: < 0.001, < 0.01, and 0.036, respectively) (Fig. 4). Clinical features (pathological fractures, dental abnormalities, musculoskeletal pain, headache, and history of CPPD disease) and laboratory parameters did not differ between groups (Table 2). Femoral bone mineral density tended to be lower than lumbar spine (L2–L4) bone mineral density across the groups, whereas median Z-scores were modestly reduced. Referral indications could be broadly categorized into two groups: hypophosphatasemia identified during the evaluation or treatment of bone-related conditions (e.g., osteoporosis) and hypophosphatasemia detected incidentally during health checkups or evaluation of unrelated medical conditions. Underlying bone-related conditions prior to the initial visit were summarized in Supplementary Table 5. Bone-related conditions were present before the initial visit in 12 of 25 variant-negative individuals (48.0%) and 12 of 29 patients with a monoallelic variant without DNE (41.4%). More than half of the patients in both groups had no prior diagnosis of a bone-related condition. Among these individuals (variant-negative, n = 13; monoallelic without DNE, n = 17), hypophosphatasemia was identified during evaluation of joint pain in three variant-negative individuals (3/13, 23.1%) and six patients with a monoallelic variant without DNE (6/17, 35.3%).

Discussion

This study examined patients with bone disorders presenting with low serum ALP, aiming to contextualize the genetic nosology of HPP within real-world clinical practice. While recent diagnostic criteria for HPP do not require genetic confirmation, low serum ALP remains a practical and readily available clue for raising clinical suspicion of HPP in routine outpatient practice. In our cohort, 56 of 865 patients (6.5%) underwent ALPL sequencing, resulting in the identification of two patients with biallelic pathogenic variants (0.2%), 29 with monoallelic variants without DNE (3.4%), and 25 variant-negative individuals (2.9%). Only one biallelic patient (0.1% of the total cohort) was considered eligible for ERT. The frequency of biallelic (i.e. genetically-confirmed HPP) (0.2%) was comparable to previous reports from osteoporosis clinics in the UK (0.3%) and Australia (0.4%) [24, 25], underscoring the rarity of HPP among adults. A key strength of this study is that genetic interpretation incorporated both zygosity and functional evidence, allowing meaningful stratification. Notably, clinical features and general biochemical parameters, aside from ALP and its substrates, did not differ between monoallelic without DNE and variant-negative patients (Table 2). Bone mineral density was mildly reduced and generally comparable across the groups, suggesting that densitometric findings alone may have limited value in distinguishing these patient categories. Moreover, serum ALP levels often fluctuated, particularly among variant-negative individuals, and secondary causes of hypophosphatasemia were observed at similar frequencies in both groups (Supplementary Table 4). Therefore, careful interpretation of genetic findings in the context of zygosity, DNE status, and clinical manifestations is essential for appropriate clinical management.

The clinical value of genetic testing may be particularly relevant in several patient populations. First, accurate diagnosis can be challenging in patients receiving antiresorptive therapy. As demonstrated in the present cohort, osteoporosis was prevalent in both variant-negative individuals and patients with monoallelic variants without DNE. Furthermore, hypophosphatasemia and atypical fractures may occur during antiresorptive treatment, potentially mimicking HPP. Whether antiresorptives increase the risk of atypical fractures in patients with monoallelic variants without DNE remains unknown, however, genetic testing may contribute to future risk stratification and individualized therapeutic decisions. Second, genetic testing may identify individuals with moderate or severe forms of HPP before the development of overt skeletal complications. Such patients may benefit from the avoidance of antiresorptive agents and closer longitudinal monitoring. Third, some patients carrying monoallelic variants without DNE may present predominantly with extra-skeletal manifestations despite the absence of overt skeletal disease. The clinical relevance of such manifestations has increasingly been recognized in adult HPP. For example, a study in Belgium reported that approximately half of the patients with HPP presenting with fatigue (3/7) had previously been misdiagnosed with chronic fatigue syndrome and/or fibromyalgia [26]. Although the pathophysiological relationship between the extra-skeletal manifestations and impaired ALPL function remains incompletely understood, genetic diagnosis may facilitate recognition of HPP in patients with otherwise unexplained symptoms and provide a framework for future studies investigating therapeutic approaches targeting non-skeletal aspects of the disease.

Despite the availability of ERT with AA, its indication in adult patients remains uncertain. In our cohort, one biallelic patient (Patient 56) presented with pseudofractures identified by bone scintigraphy, which prompted initiation of ERT with AA. While partial healing of pseudofractures was observed within 6 months, no apparent effect on CPPD disease was noted: joint calcifications remained unchanged (Fig. 3B), and a CPPD flare occurred during AA treatment. AA is currently the only available recombinant ALP with high affinity for bone. Although its efficacy in improving osteomalacia is established, its impact on the extra-skeletal manifestations of adult-onset HPP, such as CPPD disease, muscle pain, headache, and psychological symptoms, remains unclear. When considering AA therapy for patients without fractures or pseudofractures, careful consideration should be given to objective indicators that can assess treatment effectiveness. Patient 56 has continued AA treatment for approximately two years and reports satisfaction with the therapeutic outcomes. However, clear criteria regarding when to discontinue AA have not been established. The most common adverse event associated with AA is injection site reactions. In addition, a previous case report [27] raised concerns about the potential exacerbation of spinal ligament ossification leading to spinal cord compression. Besides, the CPPD flare observed during AA treatment in Patient 56 raises the possibility that transient changes in pyrophosphate metabolism may trigger acute CPPD arthritis, analogous to gout flares observed during initiation of urate-lowering therapy. Further studies are required to better characterize the long-term benefits and adverse effects of AA in adult patients.

This study clarified the clinical complexity surrounding hypophosphatasemia. Serum ALP levels fluctuated considerably in both monoallelic without DNE and variant-negative groups, and secondary causes of hypophosphatasemia were not limited to variant-negative individuals. Moreover, clinical features alone did not differentiate monoallelic without DNE patients from variant-negative individuals (Table 2). In contrast, ALP, BAP, and their substrates (urinary PEA, plasma PPi, and serum PLP) showed significant differences between groups (Fig. 4), which motivated the development of biochemical differentiation models. A previous study from Spain proposed a threshold ALP value of 25 IU/L to distinguish heterozygotes from variant-negative individuals with hypophosphatasemia [28]. In our cohort, however, the optimal threshold was higher at 35 IU/L. One explanation is that the Spanish cohort likely included monoallelic patients with severe ALPL variants exerting a DNE (categorized as “moderate HPP”), whereas such variants are rare in Japan. Consequently, all heterozygotes in our study carried variants without a DNE and were classified as having “mild HPP”, resulting in comparatively higher ALP activity. To address the potential influence of prior antiresorptive therapy on serum ALP activity, we additionally performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients without antiresorptive exposure. The results were comparable to those of the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of the proposed biochemical differentiation model (Supplementary Table 7).

The clinical implications of such differentiation model remain uncertain, given the absence of clear differences in clinical symptoms between monoallelic without DNE and variant-negative individuals. Although clinical manifestations were similar between groups in the present study, the pathophysiological significance of chronic low ALP activity and its potential contribution to extra-skeletal symptoms remain incompletely understood. Therefore, whether patients with monoallelic variants without DNE would benefit from specific clinical interventions remains unresolved. Furthermore, the long-term impact of sustained low ALP levels on bone metabolism is unknown. If prolonged hypophosphatasemia proves deleterious, even variant-negative individuals with secondary hypophosphatasemia may require clinical follow-up. However, in our study, patients with low ALP (< 20 IU/L) did not exhibit more severe clinical or biochemical features (Supplementary Table 3). Future population-based studies are needed to clarify the long-term consequences of hypophosphatasemia, irrespective of ALPL variant status.

This study has several limitations. First, the timing of serum ALP measurement prior to study inclusion varied among patients. Because the Osteoporosis Center is a specialized department within a tertiary care hospital, many participants had been evaluated at multiple institutions before presentation, making it difficult to determine the longitudinal pattern of ALP levels. Second, the genetic analysis in this study was limited to the ALPL exons and exon–intron boundaries; pathogenic variants in deep intronic regions may therefore have been missed. Furthermore, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification analysis for detecting large deletions or duplications was not performed. Some secondary causes of hypophosphatasemia in variant-negative individuals also remained unresolved (Supplementary Table 4), and undetected ALPL variants cannot be excluded in this group. Third, the differentiation models between monoallelic without DNE and variant-negative patients were not validated in external cohorts. As noted above, the higher optimal ALP threshold in our study compared with a European cohort may reflect differences in the prevalence of DNE variants across populations; thus, model interpretation should account for genetic background. Lastly, clinical symptoms were assessed by patient questionnaires, which may be subject to recall bias.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluated patients with hypophosphatasemia in a bone-specialized outpatient setting, demonstrating that stratification based on ALPL variant zygosity and functional evidence provides clinically meaningful differentiation. The frequency of genetically confirmed HPP was 0.2% (2/865), and ERT with AA was initiated in one patient. Although serum ALP and its substrates differed between monoallelic individuals without DNE and variant-negative individuals, clinical symptoms and the prevalence of secondary causes of hypophosphatasemia were comparable across groups. Nevertheless, the distinct biochemical profile observed in monoallelic individuals without DNE suggests that they may represent a biologically distinct subgroup, although the clinical significance of this distinction remains to be fully elucidated. Genetic classification incorporating zygosity, residual enzymatic activity, and DNE corresponded well with ALP activity, clarifying the impact of ALPL variants in real-world clinical practice. These findings support a diagnostic approach that integrates genetic interpretation with biochemical assessment, rather than relying solely on ALP levels or clinical symptoms. Further studies are warranted to determine the long-term effects of ERT in adults, including its impact on extra-skeletal manifestations of HPP, and to clarify the clinical consequences of persistent hypophosphatasemia in monoallelic patients without DNE and variant-negative individuals.
