Improving neonatal total parenteral nutrition (TPN) safety and standardisation in a tertiary NICU: a multicomponent QI initiative
Authors: Priyadarshini Virupaxi Chougula, Pardha Ramineni, Vikrant Rajendra Deshmukh, Pradeep Suryawanshi, Suprabha Kumari Patnaik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTotal parenteral nutrition (TPN) is essential for newborns admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) who are unable to meet their nutritional requirements through enteral feeding. However, practices often vary due to resource constraints, lack of standardisation and inadequate training—leading to contamination risks, suboptimal nutrition and increased infections. At our 60-bed tertiary NICU (≈1500 admissions/year), adherence to TPN standards was only 10%. This quality improvement project aimed to increase adherence to >80% through a structured, multidisciplinary protocol.MethodsA stepwise quality improvement initiative using planning, implementation, evaluation and action cycles was implemented. Interventions included shifting TPN preparation to trained residents, introducing a laminar flow surveillance system, deploying an Excel-based TPN calculator, modifying TPN composition to reduce contamination and establishing a rotation schedule. Ongoing training, competency checks and real-time monitoring were incorporated to support sustainability.ResultsAdherence to standardised TPN practices improved from a baseline of ~10% to sustained levels, with an overall mean adherence of 84.2% . The Statistical Process Control (SPC) analysis demonstrated a centreline shift from 66.0% in the early unstable phase to 94.1% in the stable phase, confirming sustained process improvement. As a balancing outcome, the overall sepsis incidence among inborn neonates on TPN was 3.2%.ConclusionThis quality improvement initiative successfully enhanced adherence to standardised TPN protocols in the NICU through structured training, workflow optimisation and process surveillance. The structured, practical and team-based approach offers a scalable model for improving the quality and consistency of TPN practices in resource-limited settings, with sustained monitoring and regular training remaining essential for long-term success.Keywords: Intensive Care Units, Neonatal; Neonatology

Introduction

Total parenteral nutrition (TPN) is essential for neonates who cannot meet their nutritional needs enterally due to gastrointestinal immaturity or dysfunction. The primary goal is to prevent growth faltering while ensuring safe, effective nutritional support through careful monitoring and adjustments based on clinical status.1 Despite its necessity, TPN is complex and requiring expertise at every stage. Variability in TPN practices is driven by limited high-quality evidence, resource constraints and outdated protocols, often resulting in inadequate nutrition and poor growth outcomes.1,6 Neonates are particularly vulnerable to adverse events due to both the therapy itself and its intricate multistep process.1 7 Variations in healthcare settings and stakeholder practices contribute to inconsistencies in parenteral nutrition (PN) prescription, preparation and administration.8,10

Local problem

Despite the benefits of standardised TPN practices, our neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) struggled with adherence to optimal preparation and monitoring protocols. In our 60-bed tertiary NICU, admitting ~1500 neonates annually, TPN preparation was inconsistent due to variability in technique, sterility and adherence to guidelines. Initially, suboptimal formulations were prepared by nursing staff. TPN components were often prepared separately at the bedside and at different times for each baby, rather than in one coordinated session. This unmonitored process led to inconsistent preparation, unnecessary wastage of ingredients and increased infection risk. Instead of a single compounded solution, amino acids, lipids and dextrose-electrolyte solutions were often administered separately, increasing contamination risk due to multiple connections. During a sepsis outbreak, review of infection control practices revealed the absence of a formal laminar flow surveillance system. Although the outbreak was not linked to TPN, this gap highlighted the need for routine monitoring. Additionally, compliance with TPN preparation protocols was only ~10% in the 4 months preceding the quality improvement (QI) project (January–April 2024), underscoring the need for structured improvements.

Available knowledge

PN practices for newborns and preterm infants vary globally due to differences in prescription, preparation and administration. Despite established guidelines, adherence remains inconsistent, especially in resource-limited settings where cost, availability and lack of trained personnel are major barriers.11 In developed countries, PN is prepared by trained pharmacists under strict aseptic conditions, while in India and other developing nations, it is often manually compounded by physicians and nurses, increasing risks of contamination and errors.12 13 Structured training, improved monitoring and proactive PN strategies have been shown to enhance outcomes in preterm infants.14 In South Africa, standardised ‘all-in-one’ PN bags have been proposed for facilities without pharmacy units,15 while Egypt’s educational programmes improved PN practices.16 Kuwait highlighted the role of multidisciplinary teams, regular audits and standard protocols.17 PN prescribing errors, often due to non-standardised orders and miscommunication, can be reduced by standardised forms, multidisciplinary review and computerised order entry.18 Compounding under laminar flow hoods and using automated compounding devices improve safety and accuracy, though verification remains crucial.10 These findings stress the need for structured PN programmes, enhanced training and improved implementation in resource-limited settings.

Rationale

We applied the WHO Point of Care QI framework to address the problem of low adherence to existing TPN protocols in our NICU. Although standardised protocols were available, daily practice showed wide variation due to unclear responsibilities, lack of structured training and absence of routine monitoring. We believed that simple, practical interventions—such as defining roles, providing targeted training and regularly reviewing practice—would improve adherence. The approach focused on making changes that were feasible, sustainable and easily integrated into the existing workflow.

SMART aim (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound aim)

To improve compliance with standardised TPN preparation and administration protocols in the NICU from a baseline of approximately 10% to at least 80% within an 8-month period, by implementing structured interventions including centralised preparation, staff training, protocol standardisation and routine process monitoring—ensuring measurable and sustainable improvements in safety and quality.

Setting

The study was conducted in a 60-bed, Level IIIB accredited NICU of a tertiary care teaching hospital, with~1500 annual admissions. Of these, 20 beds cater to Level IIIB care. The unit is staffed by five consultants and a total of 15 neonatology residents, with per-shift coverage including 5–7 nurses and 3–5 residents, varying depending on census and acuity. The unit has a designated TPN preparation room with a laminar airflow workstation to enable aseptic compounding. Although standardised TPN protocols (eg, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) 2020 guidelines and the European Society for Paediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology and Nutrition (ESPGHAN) 2018 recommendations) existed, adherence was low, leading to inconsistencies in preparation and administration.

Intervention

Patients were assessed for TPN eligibility based on standard clinical indications such as prematurity, surgical conditions, feed intolerance or critical illness. Eligibility was determined according to unit protocols, in line with international guidelines including NICE (2020), ESPGHAN (2018) and the National Neonatology Forum recommendations. A QI initiative using planning, implementation, evaluation and action (PDSA) cycles was implemented to improve adherence to standardised TPN protocols. Gap analysis (figure 1) identified key issues: inconsistent preparation techniques, inadequate training and contamination risks. Interventions were implemented sequentially through PDSA cycles, targeting preparation practices, calculation errors, sterility and workflow (details in the PDSA section).

The project was planned for months, allowing for ongoing assessment and continuous refinement of interventions.

Patient and public involvement

No patients were involved. TPN administration to eligible neonates is standard of care, and this project focused on improving internal NICU processes for its prescribing, compounding and administration.

Study of the intervention

A pre-intervention and post-intervention analysis assessed adherence to protocols, formulation accuracy and preparation compliance. Data from audits and observations were analysed using PDSA cycles, with feedback sessions to address confounders. Comparisons with past NICU data ensured improvements were intervention-driven.

PDSA cycles

For each PDSA cycle, we explicitly describe the planning, implementation, evaluation and actions taken for sustainability, summarised in online supplemental table 1.

PDSA cycle 1: transitioning TPN preparation to residents and fellows

Though standard guidelines for TPN existed (NICE 2020, ESPGHAN 2018), they did not specifically address preparation and administration practices. In our unit, considerable inconsistencies were observed in these processes, and baseline adherence was only 10%. The first step was to formulate a structured unit protocol for TPN preparation and administration that detailed who would prepare TPN (trained residents and fellows instead of nurses), how it would be prepared (under a UV-exposed laminar flow hood using a standardised 11-step checklist and individualised formulations, with amino acids mixed with dextrose-electrolyte solutions and lipids administered separately) and when it would be prepared (once daily in a coordinated session). A structured training programme was delivered through hands-on demonstrations, video tutorials and written guides; this was conducted in two batches to accommodate clinical duties, with monthly reinforcement sessions for residents and Level III nurses. Adherence was assessed using the 11-step checklist (online supplemental file 2), which covered eligibility, preparation, labelling and administration steps and was completed through direct observation with real-time feedback. Within 2 weeks, adherence improved to 79%, accompanied by better workflow efficiency, reduced preparation time and greater acceptance of the protocol. The intervention was subsequently adopted into routine practice, supported by ongoing education and continuous monitoring.

PDSA cycle 2: introduction of TPN calculator

Manual TPN calculations often led to inconsistencies and potential errors. To standardise formulations and improve accuracy, an Excel-based TPN calculator was developed. Following structured training, residents and fellows used the tool during a 2-week testing phase for daily TPN preparation. Protocol adherence improved from 76.7% in week 3 to 85.7% in week 4. The calculator enhanced dosage accuracy, minimised calculation errors and increased user confidence. Based on these positive outcomes, it was integrated into routine practice, with periodic updates planned based on user feedback.

PDSA cycle 3: laminar flow surveillance and contamination prevention

A sepsis outbreak during the early phase of the project (week 5 and 6) led to a review of infection control practices, revealing the absence of a structured laminar flow surveillance system. Although unrelated to TPN, this prompted a temporary 2-week halt in TPN preparation. A comprehensive surveillance protocol was introduced, including routine cleaning, biweekly surface cultures, monthly air quality monitoring and biannual High-Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) filter integrity checks (online supplemental file 3). Staff were trained on environmental hygiene, and adherence improved within a week, reaching 100% in week 7. The protocol was integrated into routine practice with regular audits and ongoing education to minimise contamination risks.

PDSA cycle 4: implementing a computerised TPN prescription template

Handwritten TPN prescriptions often led to transcription errors, resulting in delays as parents were repeatedly sent back to the NICU for corrections; this disrupted workflow and contributed to a decline in adherence to 78.9% in week 11. To address these issues, a standardised computerised prescription template was introduced, aiming to enhance accuracy, streamline workflow and ensure documentation consistency. After a 2-week testing phase and refinements based on user feedback, adherence improved significantly, reaching 100% by week 13. The intervention was successfully integrated into routine practice, with provisions for ongoing evaluation and updates.

PDSA cycle 5: TPN preparation rotation schedule

Although adherence had improved significantly—exceeding 90% in weeks 14 and 19, and reaching 100% in weeks 16–18—a drop to 86.2% in week 20, though within the SMART aim, highlighted workflow inefficiencies from preparation delays and lack of designated personnel. To address this and ensure reliability, a structured rotation schedule was introduced, assigning clear roles and incorporating backup personnel for uninterrupted workflow. Over 2 weeks, this intervention improved coordination, minimised delays and stabilised processes, with adherence rebounding to 100% in week 23. Owing to its success, the schedule was adopted into routine practice with ongoing monitoring.

A financial concern was raised by the nursing in-charge about the daily preparation of fresh TPN, involving individual prescriptions and potentially higher patient costs. To address this, we undertook a crude comparison of the prices of key consumables (drip sets, electrolytes, multivitamins, amino acids, dextrose, lipids, syringes, needles, etc) before and after the intervention, per patient, which did not show any appreciable difference from previous practices. In addition, TPN for all eligible babies was prepared together, enabling shared use of components and rotating prescriptions. Tested over 2 weeks, this approach improved efficiency and managed costs effectively. Based on positive feedback, the strategy was integrated into routine practice to reduce financial strain while maintaining TPN quality.

PDSA cycle 6: inventory management system

To prevent TPN preparation delays due to shortage of supply, an inventory management system was introduced to track stock levels and automate reordering. A 2-week trial assessed its effectiveness, with real-time inventory tracking and team training. However, technical difficulties, stock discrepancies and increased workload disrupted workflow, leading to overordering and shortages. Due to these challenges, the system was discontinued, and manual tracking resumed while alternative management solutions were explored.

PDSA cycle 7: TPN adjustments for extreme low birth weight infants

In week 25, adherence declined to 86.7%, and 2 out of 15 opportunities halted within 6 hours due to metabolic instability. To address this, TPN formulations were proactively modified using prior patient data to anticipate common derangements and improve tolerance in this vulnerable group. Within 2 weeks, adherence improved and remained consistently above the SMART aim, including 100% adherence in weeks 28 and 30. The revised strategy was integrated into routine practice, with ongoing data-driven refinements to ensure continued safety and efficacy in ELBW infants.

Measures

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was adherence to standardised TPN preparation and administration, defined as the percentage of TPN preparations meeting protocol requirements. Adherence was assessed using an 11-step process checklist covering eligibility assessment, order calculation, prescription, preparation, aseptic precautions, labelling, administration and waste disposal (online supplemental file 2). Each step was scored as ‘done’ (yes) or ‘not done’ (no). Each TPN preparation was considered ‘adherent’ only if all 11 steps were performed correctly (all scored as ‘yes’). Weekly adherence was then calculated as the proportion of fully adherent TPN preparations among all eligible preparations. The goal was to improve adherence to TPN protocols from a baseline of 10% to over 80%, aiming to bring more consistency to the preparation process and strengthen overall quality of care. Data were collected through direct observation, periodic audits and documentation reviews. Historical data were analysed to confirm that improvements were due to interventions rather than other NICU practice changes.

The primary focus of this initiative was on process improvement. While overall sepsis incidence among inborn neonates on TPN was reviewed as a balancing measure, specific Central Line–Associated Bloodstream Infection (CLABSI) rates could not be calculated since central line tip cultures are not routinely performed. Electrolyte disturbances were also not included, as daily weight, fluid balance and serum electrolytes were incorporated before each TPN preparation, and any dyselectrolytaemia could equally be attributed to underlying disease or prematurity rather than TPN itself.

Process measures

Two key process measures were tracked. First, the transition of TPN preparation from neonatal nursing staff to trained residents and fellows was assessed by monitoring the percentage of TPN preparations performed by trained personnel and the number of completed training sessions. Competency assessments and audits ensured accuracy and adherence to protocols. Second, laminar flow surveillance consistency was evaluated through routine sterility audits and microbiological testing. The rate of detected contamination in TPN preparations was used to measure the impact of enhanced surveillance.

Ambiguous steps identified in the baseline process map (figure 2) included the review of appropriateness of calculations (later standardised by incorporating routine discussion of eligibility and composition during clinical rounds), placement of components on the laminar hood surface after disinfection (addressed through structured cleaning protocols and checklist steps) and switching on of the fan filter unit (made reliable through checklist integration and direct observation).

Assessment approach

A structured evaluation framework led by the senior resident involved regular audits and competency assessments to ensure adherence to TPN protocols. Real-time feedback enabled prompt correction of procedural lapses. Residents and fellows received standardised training from a neonatologist using evidence-based protocols, reading material and instructional videos. Laminar flow integrity was tracked via a dedicated log reviewed by the infection control nurse, sister-in-charge, and senior resident, enabling early breach detection. Periodic review meetings discussed findings, challenges, and action plans, promoting accountability and continuous improvement. This approach strengthened protocol compliance and enhanced the safety and reliability of TPN administration in the NICU.

Analysis

Statistical analysis used run and p charts to track adherence to standardised TPN protocols over time. Laminar flow surveillance, a key process measure, was evaluated using run charts (performed vs not performed). P chart analysis in R software (V.4.3.1, R Core Team, 2023) confirmed all points remained within 3-sigma control limits, indicating a stable, controlled process.

Results

Outcome indicator

Over the 32-week TPN QI initiative, the run chart with PDSA overlays (figure 3) showed progressive improvement in adherence from a baseline of ~10%, with a temporary drop to 0% in weeks 5 and 6 falling below the lower control limit, indicating special-cause variation related to a sepsis outbreak. Following corrective actions, adherence returned within 3-sigma limits and stabilised, with a mean of 84.2% across all weeks and 90.7% when weeks 5 and 6 were excluded. The p chart (figure 4) demonstrated a centreline shift from 66.0% in the early unstable phase to 94.1% in the stable phase, with values consistently trending towards the upper control limit and reduced common-cause variation, confirming sustained process improvement through sequential PDSA cycles.

As a balancing outcome, the overall sepsis incidence among inborn neonates receiving TPN during the study period was 3.2%.

Process indicators

Environmental surveillance measures, including surface swabs, air quality monitoring with settle plates and periodic fumigation, were introduced from June 2024 following a sepsis outbreak. Although there were intermittent lapses, routine audits ensured that adherence to the surveillance protocol was maintained throughout the study period. Compliance with the QI intervention for TPN preparation and administration was 100% in all the months, with all preparations performed by trained residents/fellows using the standardised checklist under direct observation. Training participation (figure 5) improved progressively, with attendance rising from below 60% in May–June 2024 to 100% in later months, reflecting the effectiveness of structured, recurring training sessions in promoting protocol adherence.

Discussion

This QI project enhanced adherence to standardised TPN protocols in a high-volume Level III NICU by addressing preparation and administration gaps. Interventions included transitioning TPN preparation to trained residents and fellows, structured training, use of a TPN calculator, customised formulations and laminar flow surveillance, which became particularly critical following a sepsis outbreak. The systematic application of PDSA cycles enabled refinements, while the multidisciplinary approach supported integration into routine workflow. Several interventions were institutionalised as sustainable system-level changes, including adoption of a computerised prescription template to reduce transcription errors, daily use of the excel-based calculator, implementation of a structured laminar flow surveillance protocol with inclusion of this data in monthly departmental audit to ensure sustainability. Embedding these practices into standard operating procedures ensures continuity and reduces reliance on individual motivation, offering a scalable model for other NICU settings.

The administration of PN is a multifaceted process requiring collaboration among various healthcare professionals, including dietitians, physicians, residents, pharmacists, nurses and compounding technicians. It involves a series of essential steps, starting from evaluating the patient’s nutritional needs to prescribing, transcribing, compounding, labelling and dispensing the formulation. Additionally, safe administration and continuous patient monitoring are crucial to ensure efficacy and prevent complications (figure 6).19 Low adherence to standardised TPN protocols is known to increase the risk of infection, metabolic instability, growth faltering and inadequate nutrient delivery, as highlighted in previous studies.3,57 In our project, we did not directly measure clinical outcomes such as CLABSI, electrolyte disturbances or growth; instead, our primary focus was protocol adherence. However, adherence is a well-recognised process measure and a validated surrogate for patient safety in TPN administration.6 8 9 By improving adherence through PDSA cycles, we aimed to reduce potential risks associated with inconsistent practices, even though outcome measures were not specifically tracked in this initiative. Over the past decades, various organisations have introduced guidelines and recommendations aimed at enhancing best practices, promoting standardisation and improving both safety and efficiency in PN administration.120,22

Sommer et al found that 14% of nurse-prepared PN formulations had dosing inaccuracies due to inadequate training and lack of aseptic protocols, advocating for centralised pharmacy preparation.23 In our initiative, improvement in adherence after transitioning TPN preparation to residents and fellows was not attributable to differences in discipline between cadres. Rather, the change reflected system modifications—structured training under neonatologist supervision, preparation in a dedicated laminar flow hood and use of an 11-step checklist with real-time monitoring. These measures reduced variability and errors that were common when preparation occurred ad hoc at the bedside. Standardised protocols and specialised training are crucial for ensuring safe and precise PN administration in neonatal care.

The introduction of an Excel-based TPN calculator and a computerised prescription template led to automated complex calculations, reducing transcription and interpretation errors. Guenter et al6 emphasised that standardised competencies lower prescribing errors, while Kraft et al10 highlighted the role of pharmacist-led reviews in enhancing TPN safety. Despite established guidelines, errors persist—Krzyzaniak and Bajorek noted that administration errors account for 31%–63%, while dispensing errors (11.9%–25%) stem from labelling and formulation mistakes.24 A 2009 study reported PN prescription errors in 1.6% of cases, with transcription (39%), administration (35%) and preparation (24%) being the most common sources of error.25 Incident reports in 2010 identified PN and replacement preparations as major contributors to adverse drug events in paediatric patients (37% of cases),26 and a 2012 survey found that over 40% of institutions had not fully implemented safety safeguards.27 Even in a tertiary NICU, PN order errors were 3.9% in 201728 and in 2021, 0.6% of prescriptions still required intervention.10 These findings underscore the critical need for robust safety measures, pharmacist verification and institutional strategies to mitigate errors in neonatal TPN administration.

Structured laminar flow surveillance played a vital role in preventing contamination-related complications, particularly following the sepsis outbreak. Stucki et al reported microbial contamination rates of 0% in cleanrooms, 6% in operating rooms and 16% in general wards, highlighting the necessity of controlled environments for PN preparation.29 These findings align with safety consensus recommendations, such as those by Ayers et al, emphasising rigorous infection control measures to ensure patient safety during PN administration.8

The training component was central to this QI initiative, evolving from comprehensive theoretical sessions, hands-on practical training and audio modules to refresher sessions and reading materials over time. The importance of sustained education is well supported in the literature—Mistry et al7 found that ongoing training significantly reduces patient safety incidents, while Ramakrishnan et al13 demonstrated that structured quality control minimises PN errors.

Several of our PDSA interventions are also supported by published guidelines and evidence-based practices, making them potentially generalisable to other NICU settings. Standardised checklists and protocols are recommended by international PN safety guidelines.120,22 Transitioning preparation to trained residents and fellows aligns with evidence that inadequate training contributes to dosing inaccuracies.23 The use of computerised TPN calculators and order templates reflects established recommendations for standardised competencies and pharmacist-led reviews to reduce prescribing errors.6 10 24 25 Rigorous laminar flow surveillance and aseptic preparation protocols are endorsed by PN safety consensus statements and infection-control studies.8 29 Finally, structured and ongoing training is widely recognised as an effective strategy for sustaining safety and reducing PN-related incidents.7 13 These evidence-based practices highlight which interventions from our QI project may be translated to other NICU contexts.

Impact and future direction

This QI project improved adherence to TPN protocols, streamlined preparation and enhanced safety practices in the NICU. While objective outcomes such as growth, infection rates and metabolic derangements were not assessed, these remain important areas for future evaluation to better understand the clinical impact of the intervention.

Limitations

The generalisability of this project is limited to NICUs with similar infrastructure, staffing and resource availability. Replication may be affected by variations in institutional protocols, staff training and infection control practices. Internal validity was constrained by the absence of a control group and potential confounders such as infection outbreaks and staffing changes. Data collection was based on observational audits and documentation, which may introduce reporting bias. Clinical outcomes such as CLABSI or dyselectrolytaemia were not systematically assessed, as the project was designed to target process improvement; overall sepsis incidence was reviewed only as a broad balancing measure. Financial evaluation was restricted to a crude comparison of consumable costs rather than formal cost-effectiveness analysis. Finally, no formal statistical testing was applied due to the real-time, iterative nature of QI cycles. Despite these limitations, structured audits, standardised protocols and continuous feedback contributed to the reliability and sustainability of the observed improvements.

Conclusion

This QI initiative effectively enhanced adherence to standardised TPN practices in a high-volume NICU through structured protocols, targeted training and process optimisation. The interventions improved preparation accuracy, minimised errors and promoted safer, more consistent TPN administration. These strategies offer a practical, replicable model for improving PN practices in similar neonatal care settings.
