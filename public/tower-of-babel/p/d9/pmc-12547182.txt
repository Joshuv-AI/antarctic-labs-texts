Delayed pneumocephalus following craniofacial trauma in a child: successful management with combined endoscopic endonasal and external osteoplastic skull base repair
Authors: Utku Kubilay, Fatih Yılmaz, Mehmet Türkalp Yıldız, Selen Eryetli Polat, Yeliz Pekcevik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDelayed pneumocephalus is exceptionally rare in children and may result from unrecognized skull base fractures with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak. Prompt recognition is essential to prevent life-threatening complications.Case presentationA 13-year-old boy developed delayed pneumocephalus and meningoencephalocele after high-impact facial trauma with multifocal skull base defects (posterior frontal sinus wall, sphenoid, and anterior ethmoid). He underwent combined endoscopic endonasal and external osteoplastic repair with multilayer closure and a temporary lumbar drain. Recovery was uneventful with complete clinical and radiological resolution within four months.DiscussionIn the absence of pediatric-specific guidelines, management is often extrapolated from adult algorithms that favor a short conservative trial followed by CSF diversion or surgery for persistent leaks or high-risk features. In multifocal, complex defects, a hybrid endoscopic–osteoplastic strategy can provide precise localization and secure multilayer closure, and adjunctive lumbar drainage has been shown to further improve repair success.ConclusionClinicians should suspect delayed pneumocephalus when new neurological or rhinologic symptoms arise after pediatric craniofacial trauma. A combined endoscopic–osteoplastic approach is a safe and effective option for complex skull base defects in children.

Introduction

Pneumocephalus denotes the presence of intracranial air and most commonly follows cranial trauma or surgery. Delayed presentations are extremely uncommon in children and can be easily overlooked [1]. Mechanistically, air may enter through an occult skull base fracture and associated CSF leak via a one-way “ball-valve” effect or negative-pressure “inverted soda bottle” dynamics, leading to progressive intracranial air accumulation [2].

Pediatric CSF leaks, though uncommon, are clinically significant—complicating approximately 2 % of pediatric traumatic brain injuries and up to 12–30 % of skull base fractures [3]. Despite this burden, pediatric-specific diagnostic and therapeutic guidelines are lacking, and practice is largely extrapolated from adult data. We present a child with delayed pneumocephalus due to multifocal skull base defects successfully treated with a hybrid endoscopic–osteoplastic repair, and we discuss practical implications for management.

Case presentation

This case report was prepared in accordance with the Surgical CAse REport (SCARE) 2025 guidelines [4].

A 13-year-old previously healthy boy fell from a fourth-floor balcony on February 28, 2025, sustaining Le Fort II and nasomaxillary–zygomatic complex fractures, posterior frontal sinus wall fracture, and additional skull base fractures on initial CT. There was no intracranial hemorrhage. He was intubated at the scene, stabilized, and transferred the next day to a university hospital where open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of facial fractures was performed by plastic surgery. He was discharged on postoperative day 3 in stable condition.

Over the subsequent two weeks he developed intermittent clear rhinorrhea initially attributed to mucosal irritation. On March 20, 2025, he returned with worsening headache, bradyphrenia, and confusion. Neurologic examination revealed slowed cognition without focal deficits. Repeat CT demonstrated bifrontal intracranial air with midline shift, and a meningoencephalocele through the posterior wall defect of the frontal sinus. Given the multifocal nature of the defects and the presence of encephalocele, a combined repair was undertaken on April 9, 2025. Endoscopic endonasal stage: right sphenoidotomy and ethmoidectomy were performed. The sphenoid defect was obliterated with autologous fat and reinforced with fibrin sealant; the anterior ethmoid defect was covered with fascia lata and supported using a posteriorly and superiorly based nasoseptal flap. A Draf IIb frontal sinusotomy was added to secure drainage. External osteoplastic stage: via a supraciliary incision, a frontal bone flap was elevated to expose the posterior frontal sinus wall. Multilayer closure was achieved using fascia lata, septal cartilage, an additional fascia layer, and fibrin sealant; the bone flap was repositioned and fixed with titanium mini-plates.

A lumbar drain was maintained for 10 days postoperatively to reduce CSF pressure across the reconstruction; drainage was titrated to maintain low intracranial pressure while avoiding over-drainage, and the drain was removed once leakage ceased and the patient remained clinically stable.

Postoperative CT on May 8, 2025, confirmed resolution (Fig. 3, Fig. 4). At four months, the patient was neurologically intact with no recurrence of CSF leak. Discussion

Delayed pneumocephalus in children is exceptionally rare. Reported pediatric cases most often describe single-site defects managed conservatively or with isolated endoscopic repair; multifocal defects requiring a hybrid approach appear to be uncommon in the literature. To our knowledge, very few pediatric reports have detailed combined endoscopic and osteoplastic reconstruction in this context.

In the absence of pediatric-specific guidelines, adult algorithms are commonly applied. These typically recommend (i) conservative measures for 3–7 days (bed rest, head elevation, stool softeners, cough suppression), (ii) CSF diversion for persistent leakage, and (iii) surgery for unfavorable locations or complications such as pneumocephalus, meningitis, encephalocele, or failure of conservative therapy [[5], [6], [7]]. Our patient presented with high-risk features—multifocal defects, a meningoencephalocele, and intracranial air with mass effect—rendering conservative management inappropriate and favoring early definitive repair.

Endoscopic endonasal techniques with autologous grafts and fibrin sealant achieve high primary success rates (≈90 %) in adults, with the nasoseptal flap particularly valuable for higher-flow leaks [8,9]. Nevertheless, pediatric skull base anatomy poses unique challenges: smaller sinus volumes, narrower corridors, and immature landmarks may limit exposure and graft stability.

Involvement of the posterior frontal sinus wall is particularly challenging, and osteoplastic approaches remain essential when endoscopic access is limited [10].

In our case, the endonasal stage allowed minimally invasive treatment of sphenoid and ethmoid defects, while the osteoplastic stage provided direct visualization and secure multilayer closure of the large posterior frontal sinus wall defect—an area where endonasal access alone would likely be insufficient.

Durable skull base closure often requires multilayered reconstruction, and adjunctive lumbar drainage has been shown to improve repair success in complex intradural cases [11]. This literature supports our strategy of combining multilayer closure with temporary lumbar drainage, which resulted in an uncomplicated recovery.

Limitations include the single-patient design and relatively short follow-up; however, complete radiological and clinical resolution at four months supports the effectiveness of a multidisciplinary, individualized approach when multifocal pediatric skull base defects coexist with delayed pneumocephalus.

Conclusion

In pediatric craniofacial trauma, delayed pneumocephalus should be considered when new neurological or rhinologic symptoms emerge post-injury. When multifocal or high-risk skull base defects are present, a hybrid endoscopic–osteoplastic approach offers precise localization and secure multilayer closure, enabling safe and effective management.
