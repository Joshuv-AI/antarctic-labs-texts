A Golgi‐localized N‐methyltransferase and reversible aldo–keto reductases coordinate dual terminal routes in galanthamine biosynthesis
Authors: Basanta Lamichhane, Archana Niraula, Natacha Merindol, Sarah‐Eve Gélinas, Patrick Lagüe, Simon Ricard, Hugo Germain, Isabel Desgagné‐Penix
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYGalanthamine, a therapeutic Amaryllidaceae alkaloid produced exclusively by species within the Amaryllidoideae subfamily, is a key treatment for early‐stage symptoms of Alzheimer's disease. Elucidating its biosynthetic pathway is essential for strategies aimed at enhancing production through metabolic engineering. Galanthamine derives from the metabolic precursor 4′‐O‐methylnorbelladine, which undergoes cytochrome P450‐mediated para–ortho′ C‐C phenol coupling to yield nornarwedine. Two competing terminal routes have been proposed: (i) reduction of nornarwedine to norgalanthamine, followed by N‐methylation, or (ii) N‐methylation of nornarwedine to narwedine prior to reduction. Here, we identify three aldo–keto reductase (AKR) candidates (LaAKR1, LaAKR2, and LaAKR3) and three N‐methyltransferase (NMT) candidates from Leucojum aestivum: LaNMT, homologous to coclaurine N‐methyltransferase‐like (NMT‐like), and two γ‐tocopherol methyltransferases (TMT) homologs, LaTMT1 and LaTMT2. Subcellular localization studies revealed distinct compartmentalization, with LaNMT targeted to the ER‐cytosol, LaTMT1 to plastids, and LaTMT2 to the Golgi apparatus. In vitro, LaTMT2 methylated both nornarwedine and norgalanthamine, with a kinetic preference for nornarwedine. LaTMT1 methylated γ‐tocopherol to α‐tocopherol (vitamin E). All three AKRs catalyzed reversible interconversions between nornarwedine and norgalanthamine, and between narwedine and galanthamine, with LaAKR3 favoring the reduction reaction whereas LaAKR1 the oxidation reaction. These findings identify LaTMT2 and LaAKRs as key branch‐enabling enzymes, reconcile long‐standing models of galanthamine biosynthesis, and provide a strategic target for metabolic engineering strategies to enhance galanthamine production.

INTRODUCTION

Amaryllidaceae alkaloids (AAs) are specialized isoquinoline‐derived metabolites produced predominantly within the Amaryllidoideae subfamily (Desgagné‐Penix, 2021). AAs exhibit diverse biological activities (Cimmino et al., 2017), including antitumor, antiviral, antibacterial, antifungal, antimalarial, analgesic, acetylcholinesterase inhibitory, and cytotoxic effects (Jayawardena et al., 2024; Ka et al., 2021; Paiva et al., 2023; Sokhna et al., 2024; Tallini et al., 2024). Among AAs, galanthamine is of particular importance as an FDA‐approved therapeutic used for symptomatic management of early‐stage Alzheimer's disease. Like other alkaloids, the structural and pharmacological diversity of AAs is shaped by tailoring enzymes such as methyltransferases (MTs), which modulate a molecule's stereo‐electronic profile, encourage or inhibit specific conformations, enhance steric bulk, increase overall hydrophobicity, and invert the polarity of an electronegative moiety (Wessjohann et al., 2014). Despite galanthamine's importance, the enzymatic steps responsible for efficient terminal N‐methylation and reduction remain ambiguous (Liyanage et al., 2025; Mehta et al., 2024), highlighting key outstanding questions in AA biosynthesis.

Aldo–keto reductases (AKRs) are a large protein superfamily of NAD(P)H‐dependent oxidoreductases that catalyze the reduction of various carbonyl substrates into their corresponding primary and secondary alcohols (Jez et al., 1997a). The superfamily is organized into 16 families and subfamilies based on amino acid sequence identity: a family is defined by less than 40% identity, while a subfamily shares greater than 60% identity (Jez et al., 1997b; Penning, 2015). In plants, AKRs are predominantly members of the AKR4 family and function as metabolically versatile enzymes that participate in diverse biochemical pathways and stress responses, serving as key catalysts in specialized metabolism, including the biosynthesis of pharmaceutically important alkaloids (de Sousa et al., 2025; Unterlinner et al., 1999). Structurally, AKRs are primarily 34–37 kDa monomeric soluble proteins with a highly conserved (α/β)8‐barrel (triose‐phosphate isomerase) motif, and a catalytic tetrad, typically consisting of aspartate, tyrosine, lysine, and histidine (Kratzer et al., 2006; Penning, 2015).

Plant MTs are classified based on the atom they methylate (e.g., O, N, C, or S), and typically use S‐adenosyl‐L‐methionine (SAM) as a methyl donor (Liscombe et al., 2012). Structural studies of benzylisoquinoline alkaloids (BIA) N‐MTs provide mechanistic insight into substrate activation and specificity. Coclaurine N‐MT (CNMT) from Coptis japonica and pavine N‐MT (PNMT) from Thalictrum flavum are canonical SAM‐dependent enzymes that share a Rossmann‐fold SAM‐binding domain and an α‐helical substrate‐binding region. Both utilize conserved acidic and histidine residues, specifically the E204/E207/H208 triad in CNMT and the E205/H206 dyad in PNMT, for general base catalysis (Bennett et al. 2018; Torres et al., 2016), reflecting convergent strategies for substrate activation and specificity across structurally homologous N‐MTs.

Within plant specialized metabolism pathways, AKRs and MTs are particularly prominent, catalyzing essential transformations in diverse alkaloid pathways, including tropane, monoterpenoid indole, purine, isoquinoline, and benzylisoquinoline alkaloids. In Papaver somniferum, the BIA pathway, which shares L‐tyrosine as a common precursor with AA, leading to morphinan, protoberberine, and benzophenanthridine alkaloids, relies on enzymes such as coclaurine N‐MT (CNMT) (Choi et al., 2002), reticuline N‐MT (Morris & Facchini, 2016), tetrahydroprotoberberine N‐MT (Liscombe & Facchini, 2007), 1,2‐dehydroreticuline reductase, and codeinone reductase (Carr et al., 2021). Similar orthologs and functional homologs have been reported across Ranunculales, including Papaver bracteatum, Glaucium flavum, T. flavum, C. japonica, C. chinensis, C. teeta, and Eschscholzia californica (Morris & Facchini, 2019). In monoterpenoid indole alkaloids (MIA), N‐MTs evolved from γ‐tocopherol MTs (TMTs) catalyze the methylation of intermediates, while AKRs such as perakine reductase from Rauvolfia serpentina contribute to redox transformations essential for the formation of alkaloids, including ajmaline and ervincine (Bennett et al., 2018; Torres et al., 2016). In Catharanthus roseus, Cr2270, a homolog of the TMT family from Arabidopsis thaliana, exhibits high specificity for N‐methylation of substrates in the vindoline pathway (Liscombe et al., 2010), and AKR cathenamine reductase converts intermediates like cathenamine to ajmalicine (Hasnain, 2010).

The terminal architecture of AA biosynthesis remains incompletely resolved, including the role of AKRs in shaping redox flux at the branch‐point intermediate nornarwedine. Following cytochrome P450–mediated para–ortho′ phenol coupling of 4′‐O‐methylnorbelladine, nornarwedine occupies a central metabolic node subject to reduction (Figure 1). In our previous study, we detected norgalanthamine in some of the replicates in the in vivo system upon agroinfiltration of CYP96T and norcraugsodine/noroxomaritidine short‐chain dehydrogenase/reductase (Lamichhane et al., 2025) (Figure 1), whereas AKRs in Narcissus and Lycoris have been shown to catalyze the reduction of narwedine (Mehta et al., 2024; Xiang et al., 2025). Consequently, the contribution of AKRs to terminal pathway directionality remains unresolved. Similarly, the type of N‐MTs involved in the AA biosynthesis remains unclear, particularly regarding the final N‐methylation step leading to galanthamine, for which recent studies reached divergent conclusions (Liyanage et al., 2025; Mehta et al., 2024). Two alternative routes have been proposed: (i) reduction to norgalanthamine by an unidentified reductase, followed by N‐methylation to yield galanthamine, (ii) N‐methylation of nornarwedine to narwedine followed by reduction by AKR to galanthamine (Figure 1). Critically, direct enzymatic evidence for N‐methylation of nornarwedine in planta is lacking, leaving the order of the terminal reactions unresolved. While our previous work supported the first route by identifying a CNMT‐like enzyme capable of converting norgalanthamine to galanthamine (Liyanage et al., 2025), Mehta et al. (2024) reported evidence for a TMT acting prior to reduction.

To clarify the enzymology underlying the final steps of galanthamine biosynthesis, we systematically compared CNMT‐like and TMT along with AKRs and NR candidate enzymes from Leucojum aestivum using nornarwedine, narwedine, and norgalanthamine as substrates (Figure 1). By combining subcellular localization, in vitro biochemical assays, and transient expression in Nicotiana benthamiana, we identified a Golgi‐localized TMT homolog that performs both proposed terminal N‐methylation reactions. These methylation events are dynamically interconnected by reversible AKR‐mediated redox interconversions, thereby reconciling competing terminal pathway models. These findings resolve galanthamine biosynthesis and provide a strategic enzymatic target for metabolic engineering efforts aimed at enhancing galanthamine production.

RESULTS

Identification and phylogeny of N‐methyltransferase candidates

To identify enzymes responsible for the terminal N‐methylation steps in galanthamine biosynthesis, we mined the Leucojum aestivum transcriptome using three TMT reference sequences: Arabidopsis thaliana (Uniprot Q9ZSK1), Catharanthus roseus Cr2270 (vindoline N‐methyltransferase; Uniprot W5U2K2), and Cr7756 (picrinine N‐methyltransferase; Uniprot A0A8X8M501). This search yielded 26, 28, and 29 candidate transcripts per query, respectively. Among them, two TMT‐like transcripts with the highest sequence similarity with baits were selected for further analysis, together with the previously characterized CNMT‐like LaNMT (Liyanage et al., 2025). Phylogenetic reconstruction using ClustalW alignments within a previously established NMT framework (Liscombe et al., 2010) placed LaTMT1 and LaTMT2 in the TMT clade, while LaNMT clustered with BIA‐related CNMTs (Figure 2a, Table S1). Notably, LaTMT2 subclustered with a previously proposed N‐methylating TMT candidate (NtNMT1) from Narcissus cv. Tête‐à‐Tête (Liscombe et al., 2010; Mehta et al., 2024). LaTMT2 shares 44% and 16% sequence similarity with LaTMT1 and LaNMT, respectively, but exhibits 82% similarity with NtNMT1 (Table S2), supporting its identification as a homolog of NtNMT1. Likewise, LaTMT1 displays 71% and 61% similarity to canonical vitamin E–synthesizing methyltransferases from Canna indica and Arabidopsis thaliana, respectively (Table S2). Coding sequences of LaTMT1, LaTMT2, and LaNMT were 1044, 876, and 1065 bp, encoding 348, 292, and 355 amino acids, respectively. Conserved domain analyses (InterProScan, the NCBI Conserved Domain Database, and ScanProsite/ExPASy) confirmed their assignment to the SAM‐dependent methyltransferase superfamily. Multiple sequence alignment revealed that all three candidates retained the canonical Rossmann‐fold motifs II, III, and IV, and conserved catalytic residues Glu204, Glu207, and His208 (Figure 2b). AlphaFold3 structural predictions further revealed that LaTMT1 and LaTMT2 were structurally similar (Root mean square deviation (RMSD) = 1.273 Å), while LaNMT diverged more substantially (RMSD = 3.485 and 3.067 Å, respectively). The N‐terminal regions were especially divergent between LaTMTs and LaNMT. As expected, LaNMT superposed well with C. japonica CNMT (RMSD = 0.498 Å), while LaTMT1 and LaTMT2 were more distant (RMSD = 2.839 Å; 2.895 Å, respectively) (Figure S1). The folding of SAM and ligand binding pockets was, however, similar, following the class I methyltransferase α/β Rossmann‐fold, with an active site composed mainly of α‐helices, and four of the seven conserved β‐sheets pointing toward SAM (Figure S1). The catalytic residues Glu204, Glu207, and His208, as well as the active site Tyr81 (Tyr72 for LaTMT1, Tyr17 for LaTMT2), were oriented toward the superposed CNMT substrate nitrogen. Overall, the predicted structures highlighted divergence in the N‐terminal region among the candidate MT enzymes, together with pocket compatible with isoquinoline‐like alkaloids as substrates for SAM‐dependent N‐methylation. Despite the lack of prior mechanistic studies on plant TMTs, the conservation of the sequence and conformation of the putative catalytic residues' Glu‐Glu‐His motif in NMTs suggests a possible shared mechanism.

Identification and phylogeny of aldo–keto reductase candidates

Using Narcissus cv. Tête‐à‐Tête NtAKR1 as bait (Mehta et al., 2024), four sequences with more than 75% sequence identity with the bait were identified in our transcriptome database. The two longest sequences were selected and named LaAKR1 and LaAKR2. While amplifying from cDNA, a third sequence, LaAKR3, with 8 amino acid differences relative to AKR2, was identified (Figure S2). Sequences of LaAKR1, LaAKR2, and LaAKR3 were 963, 975, and 975 bp, respectively, encoding proteins with approximate molecular weights of 36.27, 37.09, and 36.97 kDa, respectively. The NCBI Conserved Domain Database confirmed their assignment as NAD(P)H‐dependent AKR4A and 4B family. Phylogenetic analysis by maximum likelihood using 1000 bootstraps revealed class 4B as a distinct clade, grouping enzymes from plants that produce specialized metabolites (Figure S3a, Table S3). Multiple sequence alignment with crystallized and previously characterized AKR sequences of Papaver somniferum and Zea mays confirmed the presence of the conserved catalytic tetrad Asp50, Tyr55, Lys84, and His117 in the candidates (Figure S3b). LaAKR3 shares 86% and 98% sequence similarity with LaAKR1 and LaAKR2, respectively, and 92% and 81% similarity with the characterized AKRs from Lycoris aurea (Xiang et al., 2025) and Narcissus cv. Tête‐à‐Tête (Mehta et al., 2024), respectively (Table S4). The AlphaFold3 structural predictions of AKR candidates superposed with the reference crystal structure EcAKR from Echinochloa colona (PDB:7F7L) (Li et al., 2022) revealed that LaAKR1 was more similar to 7F7L (RMSD = 0.669 Å) than were LaAKR2 and LaAKR3, deviating by 2.422 Å and 2.387 Å from the reference, respectively, with a longer and more exposed C‐terminal α‐helix for LaAKR2 (Figure S4a). Nonetheless, superimposition confirmed the similarity between the structures (Figure S4a), with NADPH and the catalytic tetrad clearly superimposed, in a configuration consistent with aldo–keto substrate reduction (Figure S4b).

Leaf‐specific AA biosynthesis and relative expression of candidate genes in L. aestivum

To assess the biosynthetic capacity of L. aestivum leaves, targeted LC–MS/MS metabolite profiling detected N‐methylated alkaloids lycoramine and galanthamine, along with AA intermediates, including tyramine, phenylalanine, haemanthamine, norgalanthamine, dopamine, vittatine (or crinine), 4′‐O‐methylnorbelladine, L‐tyrosine, 3,4‐dihydroxybenzoic acid, 4‐hydroxybenzoic acid, and norbelladine (Figure S5). Notably, several metabolites predicted in the pathway, including 3,4‐dihydroxybenzaldehyde, 3′,4′‐O‐dimethylnorbelladine, 3′‐O‐methylnorbelladine, 9‐O‐demethylhomolycorine, and tazettine, previously reported (Ptak et al., 2020; Szlávik et al., 2004) in the genus, were not detected in any replicate of this organ. A broader absence was noted for additional biosynthetically or structurally related phenolic intermediates such as caffeic acid, p‐coumaric acid, ferulic acid, isoferulic acid, vanillin, and levodopa, suggesting either abundance below detection limits, tissue‐specific biosynthesis, or pathway divergence. 11‐Hydroxyvittatine, lycorine, and sanguinine were detected at low signal intensity in all samples; however, confirmation was not possible due to insufficient spectral quality. Untargeted metabolite screening was also performed using GC–MS, resulting in the detection of four previously unreported masses (m/z 207, 420, 476, and 490) in the non‐polar fraction (Figure S6a–g). A complete list of both detected and undetected metabolites is provided in Table S5 along with the documented metabolites in either leaves or shoots cultures or bulbs of L. aestivum (Table S6). Altogether, these results confirmed that leaves are active tissues for galanthamine and precursors accumulation and provided a metabolic context for enzyme function.

To determine whether the metabolite accumulation observed in leaves is supported by transcriptional regulation of pathway genes, we analyzed the tissue‐specific expression of candidate genes encoding enzymes involved in galanthamine biosynthesis in L. aestivum organs using RT‐qPCR (Figure 3). Distinct expression patterns were observed across bulb, leaf, and flower tissues. Genes associated with the early step of the galanthamine pathway, like LaCYP96T2, LaOMT, and LaNR, showed higher transcript abundance in bulbs (e.g., the mean relative expression of LaCYP96T2 in bulb is 0.5040 vs. 0.1932 in leaf, **P < 0.0078). In contrast, several downstream tailoring enzymes exhibited elevated expressions in leaves, including LaTMT1, LaTMT2, LaNMT, LaAKR2, and LaAKR3 (*P < 0.0232 and **P < 0.0082). In combination with the expression patterns, the metabolite profiling results support the leaf tissue as a site of late‐stage galanthamine biosynthesis, where reductive and methylation reactions are transcriptionally enriched.

To further examine whether the transcriptional and metabolite profiles observed in leaves correspond to the occurrence of active enzymatic conversions, crude protein extracts from L. aestivum leaves were subjected to in vitro assays following extensive dialysis to remove endogenous metabolites and cofactors. When norgalanthamine was supplied as substrate in the presence of SAM, the dialyzed crude extract catalyzed the N‐methylation to form galanthamine (Figure S7a). When nornarwedine was used as a substrate in the presence of SAM and NADPH, the crude extract catalyzed N‐methylation and/or reduction to form narwedine, norgalanthamine, and galanthamine, indicating that these reactions are catalyzed by enzymes present in the leaf extract (Figure S7b). Together with tissue‐specific transcript accumulation and metabolite profiling data, these results provide functional evidence that L. aestivum leaf tissue harbors the enzymatic machinery required to support late‐stage galanthamine biosynthesis.

LaTMT1, LaTMT2, and LaNMT localize in distinct compartments

Signal peptide prediction using TargetP‐2.0 indicated a high likelihood of chloroplast transit peptide for LaTMT1 (score: 0.8761), whereas LaTMT2 and LaNMT were predicted as “other” with organelle‐targeting signals (scores: 0.9986 and 0.9979, respectively) with the remaining likelihood out of 1 extremely low for subcellular destinations (Table S7). To experimentally determine their subcellular localization, LaNMT, LaTMT1 and LaTMT2 coding sequences were cloned into the pB7FWG2 expression vector to generate C‐terminal EGFP‐fusion constructs. These constructs were transiently co‐expressed in Nicotiana benthamiana leaves alongside organelle‐specific fluorescent markers (Nelson et al., 2007): free RFP (nucleus; cytosol), ER‐rk CD3‐959 (endoplasmic reticulum‐ mCherry), and G‐rk CD3‐967 (Golgi bodies‐mCherry). Confocal laser scanning microscopy was performed 48 h post‐infiltration to assess the subcellular distribution of the fusion proteins. Distinct localization patterns were observed for each candidate. The LaTMT1‐EGFP fusion protein displayed a punctate and reticulated fluorescence pattern consistent with chloroplast localization, showing clear overlap with the intrinsic chlorophyll autofluorescence (Figure 4a). In contrast, LaTMT2‐EGFP signal co‐localized with Golgi‐mCherry, indicating Golgi targeting (Figure 4b). As previously reported, LaNMT‐EGFP exhibited a dispersed fluorescence signal throughout the cytosol and a partial overlap with ER‐mCherry (Figure 4c,d). This differential compartmentalization suggests distinct functional niches, potentially minimizing cross‐reactions while supporting pathway efficiency in a distinct metabolic landscape inside the cell.

Docking supports promiscuous biosynthetic machinery from N‐methylation to carbonyl reduction

To investigate the catalytic potential of the candidates, we performed molecular docking. SAM was positioned in the binding pocket based on superimposition with the CjCNMT crystal structure (PDB: 6GKV). Substrate‐binding poses were analyzed for both norgalanthamine and nornarwedine, the two hypothesized intermediates in galanthamine biosynthesis. The two very similar ligands shared comparable docking scores, positioning, and interaction patterns, reflecting a common binding mode across the tested enzymes. All three enzymes yielded docking scores ranging from −6.3 to −6.8 kcal/mol (Table 1, Figure 5). LaTMT2 showed the shortest substrate‐SAM distances (3.2 Å) and the most favorable S–C:N–C dihedral angles (149.1° and 159.5° for norgalanthamine and nornarwedine, respectively), consistent with a catalytically competent SN2 geometry. LaTMT1 showed distances at the upper limit (3.4–3.5 Å) but favorable angles (164–175°). In contrast, LaNMT showed comparable distances (3.3 Å) but dihedral angles far from the optimal 180° (−85.9° and − 72.7°), suggesting suboptimal catalytic geometry toward both substrates despite productive binding. In LaNMT, norgalanthamine binding was stabilized by a conserved hydrophobic core involving Phe234 and Phe257, as well as hydrogen bonds with Tyr98, Glu204, and His208. Notably, Glu204 and Tyr98 formed a direct bond with the substrate amine. Interactions with His208 and Glu204 were lost upon docking nornarwedine. LaTMT1 featured hydrophobic contacts from Leu71, Trp83, and Tyr260, with Tyr72 and Glu206 forming hydrogen bonds with the substrate nitrogen. For LaTMT2, norgalanthamine's hydrophobic interacting residues included Trp27, Leu31, and Phe204. Tyr17, His33, and His151 contributed polar contacts, with Tyr17 and His151 directly interacting with the substrate amine. Nornarwedine was stabilized by two additional hydrophobic bonds with Trp16 and Glu147. Tyr17 interaction with the amine was conserved. These results support the catalytic potential of LaNMT for norgalanthamine, as docking led to interactions with the catalytic Glu204 and His208 (Figure 5). LaTMT1 could accommodate both substrates, although the distance between the amine and SAM was the highest among the three candidates (Table 1). LaTMT2 exhibited the most compact and enclosed binding cavity with a favorable substrate‐SAM geometry. The ability of all three enzymes to stably bind both norgalanthamine and nornarwedine in silico suggests functional promiscuity.

Extending the docking analysis to reductase candidates revealed a similar pattern of promiscuity. The predicted ligand positions were consistent with the reduction of the C=O bond for narwedine and nornarwedine inside LaAKR1, 2, and 3 (Figure S8a–c), although an alternative reduction of the endocyclic C=C bond cannot be excluded. Ligands interacted with catalytic Tyr54 in all cases via both H‐bonds and hydrophobic interactions, suggesting a key role in the reaction. H‐bonds between C=O and Tyr54 were detected for both substrates in LaAKR2 and LaAKR3; in LaAKR1, NADPH engaged preferentially with nornarwedine, and His117 formed a hydrogen bond specifically with narwedine, an interaction absent in LaAKR2 and LaAKR3 (Table S8, Figure S8). To further assess the geometry of hydride transfer, distances between the C4 atom of the NADPH nicotinamide ring and the acceptor carbons were measured (Table S8). C4–C(carbonyl) distances ranged from 3.4 to 3.6 Å across isoforms, while C4–C(olefinic) distances ranged from 3.2 to 3.7 Å, with LaAKR3 showing the shortest distance for narwedine (3.2 Å). Asp49 contributed to NADPH stabilization via salt bridges with Lys261/262 and Arg267/268 in all isoforms, consistent with its established role in cofactor binding.

LaTMT2 exhibits superior catalytic activity in vitro and in vivo

To evaluate the enzymatic activity of LaNMT, LaTMT1, and LaTMT2, we conducted both in vitro and in vivo assays using nornarwedine and norgalanthamine as substrates. Maltose‐binding protein (MBP) and EGFP tags served as negative controls, and papaverine was included as an internal standard for LC–MS/MS analysis. In the in vitro assays, all proteins were expressed as N‐terminal MBP fusion constructs. LaTMT1 (~82 kDa) was found to be insoluble. To overcome this, the predicted signal peptide was removed, yielding a truncated version (ΔLaTMT1) that could be purified as a soluble protein. SDS‐PAGE confirmed expected band sizes of ~75 kDa (ΔLaTMT1), ~76 kDa (LaTMT2), and ~84 kDa (LaNMT) (Figure S9a). When using nornarwedine as a substrate for enzymatic assays, LC–MS/MS analysis showed that crude LaTMT1 and purified ΔLaTMT1, along with LaNMT, exhibited detectable activity above control MBP background, though not statistically significant (LaTMT1 0.0022 ± 0.0016; ΔLaTMT1 = 0.0225 ± 0.0025; LaNMT = 0.0203 ± 0.0026, respectively). By contrast, LaTMT2 catalyzed the formation of narwedine with a 17‐fold increase over MBP background and a 9‐fold increase over LaNMT (p < 0.0001, Figure 6a), indicating higher activity. Similarly, using norgalanthamine as a substrate, galanthamine production was detectable in reactions with ΔLaTMT1 and LaNMT, but LaTMT2 showed the highest catalytic efficiency, producing ~6‐fold more galanthamine than the other enzymes (p < 0.0001, Figure 6b).

We further proceed to fully characterize the LaTMT2 enzyme by testing its activity on norgalanthamine and nornarwedine at various enzyme concentrations, temperatures, incubation times, buffer pH, and substrate concentrations. At 30°C–35°C, the enzyme activity for both substrates was optimal with an incubation time of at least 3 h, reaching a plateau at pH 7.5 (Figure S10a–f). Enzyme kinetic characterization of LaTMT2 was performed with norgalanthamine and nornarwedine as substrates under optimized in vitro conditions. Michaelis–Menten analysis revealed comparable substrate affinities, with km values of 353.2 ± 28.5 μM for norgalanthamine and 332.2 ± 28.7 μM for nornarwedine. The corresponding maximal velocities (Vmax) were 4.5 and 5.5 nmol·min−1·μg−1, respectively. Turnover numbers (kcat) were 0.225 ± 0.008 s−1 for norgalanthamine and 0.284 ± 0.010 s−1 for nornarwedine (Table 2, Figure 6c). We also examined the enzyme's substrate preference in mixed‐substrate assays, in which both substrates were supplied at either similar concentrations or one at half‐concentration in a single‐pot reaction incubated 3 h in 30 °C with buffer at pH 7.5. The amount of product formed indicated a preference for nornarwedine over norgalanthamine of more than 1.5% (Figure 6d).

To confirm these findings in planta, the three candidates were transiently expressed in N. benthamiana. Immunoblotting confirmed expression of EGFP‐3HA (~30.1 kDa), LaTMT2‐3HA (~35.7 kDa), LaTMT1‐3HA (~41.9 kDa), and LaNMT‐3HA (~44.6 kDa) (Figure S9b). ΔLaTMT1 was omitted in the in vivo assay, as it was not necessary to purify the proteins afterwards. LaNMT produced galanthamine in vivo, but was unable to methylate nornarwedine, whereas LaTMT1 catalyzed N‐methylation of nornarwedine, but not its reduced counterpart. In agreement with the in vitro results, LaTMT2 expression led to the highest accumulation of narwedine, with 50‐fold and 120‐fold increases over LaTMT1 and LaNMT, respectively (P < 0.0001, Figure 6e). When infiltrated with norgalanthamine, LaTMT2 yielded at least a 6‐fold higher galanthamine accumulation than all other enzymes (P < 0.0001, Figure 6f), confirming its superior activity across both substrates and systems. Taken together, these results establish LaTMT2 as a robust, substrate‐promiscuous N‐methyltransferase, outperforming both LaTMT1 and LaNMT in vitro and in planta settings. Its consistent activity toward two structurally related intermediates highlights its potential as a key enzyme in galanthamine biosynthesis.

LaTMT1 is a putative classical tocopherol methyltransferase

To further investigate LaTMT1, in vitro enzymatic assays were performed to evaluate the catalytic activity using γ‐tocopherol as substrate in the presence of SAM as the methyl donor (Figure S11a). Docking suggested that γ‐tocopherol fit in the active site of LaTMT1 in a bent conformation comparable to that of 1R5L (Min et al., 2003), an α‐tocopherol transferase from Homo sapiens (Figure S11b; Table S9). The full‐length enzyme, expressed as an N‐terminal MBP fusion, was largely insoluble, consistent with the presence of a predicted N‐terminal chloroplast‐targeting signal peptide. To obtain soluble protein suitable for biochemical analysis, we generated a truncated construct lacking this signal (ΔLaTMT1), which yielded a readily purifiable enzyme. Enzymatic assays were performed using crude extract/inclusion bodies containing full‐length LaTMT1 (c.LaTMT1), purified ΔLaTMT1, and MBP alone together with other negative controls, boiled enzyme and no enzyme with substrate. Reaction mixtures were analyzed by LC–MS/MS using MRM mode, demonstrating that ΔLaTMT1 produced significantly higher α‐tocopherol (~17 μg/mL) compared to crude c.LaTMT1 (~6 μg/mL) whereas the MBP negative control did not show any product (Figure S11c), confirming enzyme‐dependent product formation. To further verify product identity, the reaction mixture was analyzed on LC–MS/MS in product‐ion mode, and the resulting fragmentation spectrum displayed a matching fragmentation profile to that of authentic α‐tocopherol (Figure S11d,e). These results confirm LaTMT1 as a functional γ‐tocopherol methyltransferase, functionally distinct from the alkaloid N‐methyltransferase.

LaAKR isoforms catalyze reversible redox interconversion

To assess AKR candidates' ability to catalyze the reductive tailoring steps of the galanthamine biosynthetic pathway, we measured enzyme activities using purified recombinant proteins under in vitro conditions. Reductive assays conducted in the presence of NADPH confirmed that all three LaAKR isoforms catalyze the reduction of nornarwedine to norgalanthamine and narwedine to galanthamine. However, substantial differences in catalytic efficiency were observed. LaAKR3 exhibited the highest specific activity, producing ~90 ng/mL norgalanthamine from nornarwedine and ~144 ng/mL galanthamine from narwedine per μg of enzyme (P < 0.0001; Figure 7a,c). LaAKR2 displayed intermediate activity, whereas LaAKR1 catalyzed comparatively lower reductive conversion. In contrast, LaNRII demonstrated negligible reductive activity of nornarwedine to norgalanthamine; in one of three biological replicates, yielding 1.8 ng/mL per μg enzyme, not significantly different from negative controls (P < 0.0001; Figure 7a), indicating that LaNRII does not substantially contribute to this reductive step.

When reactions were conducted under oxidative conditions (NADP+), the three AKR isoforms exhibited distinct directional preferences. LaAKR1 showed the highest oxidative activity, catalyzing the reverse conversion of norgalanthamine to nornarwedine and galanthamine to narwedine with significantly greater efficiency than LaAKR2 and LaAKR3 (P < 0.0001; Figure 7b,d). In contrast, LaAKR3 showed a clear bias toward the pathway's reductive direction. To further investigate potential alternative products generated during AKR‐mediated reductions, we performed comprehensive metabolite profiling of both in vivo and in vitro reactions using LC–MS/MS operated in positive electrospray ionization mode with a scan range of m/z 100–400. In reactions supplied with nornarwedine, an additional ion corresponding to m/z 274 [M + H]+ was consistently detected at a retention time of ~14 min in assays containing LaAKR enzymes, clearly distinct from the authentic norgalanthamine peak at ~8.5 min (Figure S12a,b). Product‐ion fragmentation analysis of m/z 274 [M + H]+ revealed a spectrum closely resembling that of nornarwedine, suggesting preservation of the core alkaloid scaffold (Figure S13a,b). Based on the observed mass shift and fragmentation pattern, this metabolite likely represents a reduced derivative arising from C2 to C3 bond reduction, tentatively corresponding to dihydronornarwedine or norlycoraminone (Figure 8). In addition, LaAKR1 produced a second m/z 274 species eluting at 16.72 min whose fragmentation profile closely matched that of norgalanthamine, consistent with formation of the stereoisomer epi‐norgalanthamine (Figure 8; Figures S12a, S13c,d).

Similarly, when narwedine was used as substrate, LC–MS/MS analysis detected an additional ion at m/z 288 [M + H]+ eluting at ~17 min (Figure S14) in reactions catalyzed by LaAKR1 and LaAKR3, in addition to the canonical galanthamine peak at ~11 min. Product‐ion fragmentation of this later‐eluting metabolite closely mirrored that of galanthamine, supporting its assignment as the stereoisomer epi‐galanthamine (Figure S15). These findings indicate that AKR‐catalyzed reductions generate not only the canonical pathway intermediate but also alternative reduced and stereoisomeric derivatives, highlighting previously unrecognized catalytic plasticity in the late stages of galanthamine biosynthesis.

To assess in vivo relevance of LaAKR1‐3 activity, we used Agrobacterium‐mediated transient expression in N. benthamiana. LC–MS/MS metabolite profiling confirmed that all three enzymes catalyzed the interconversion of the pathway intermediates nornarwedine/norgalanthamine and narwedine/galanthamine in planta, although with markedly different efficiencies. Among the tested isoforms, LaAKR3 consistently displayed the highest activity, yielding at least two‐fold greater accumulation of both norgalanthamine and galanthamine compared with LaAKR1 and LaAKR2 (P < 0.001, Figure S16a,b). LaAKR3 was then co‐expressed with the N‐methyltransferase LaTMT2, followed by infiltration of nornarwedine as substrate. This combination resulted in the accumulation of both norgalanthamine and narwedine, together with downstream formation of galanthamine reaching 0.078 ng/mg fresh weight in N. benthamiana leaves (Figure S16c), demonstrating that LaAKR3 efficiently supports reductive flux and operates compatibly with downstream methylation steps.

These results reveal functional divergence among the AKR isoforms: LaAKR1 preferentially catalyzes the “reverse” oxidative reactions, while LaAKR3 is the predominant NADPH‐dependent reductase responsible for the formation of norgalanthamine and galanthamine. Together with LaTMT2, these AKRs coordinate the final reduction and methylation steps that define flux through the galanthamine pathway.

DISCUSSION

Prior to this study, the enzymatic basis for the terminal steps of galanthamine biosynthesis remained unresolved: while a CNMT‐like enzyme was shown to N‐methylate norgalanthamine to galanthamine (Liyanage et al., 2025), and a TMT‐like enzyme was implicated in the N‐methylation of nornarwedine (Mehta et al., 2024), neither the relative contribution nor the substrate scope of these enzymes had been fully established, and the role of AKRs in governing reductive flux and pathway directionality at this terminal node remained largely uncharacterized (Mehta et al., 2024; Xiang et al., 2025). To resolve this ambiguity, we exploited the biosynthetic activity of L. aestivum leaves and identified LaTMT2 as a Golgi‐localized N‐methyltransferase acting on both nornarwedine and norgalanthamine, and three AKR isoforms mediating reversible redox interconversions at the final stage of the pathway, thereby providing enzyme‐level resolution of both competing biosynthetic models.

Phylogenetic analysis placed LaTMT2 within the clade of TMT homologs known to catalyze N‐methylation in specialized metabolism (Koudounas et al., 2022; Levac et al., 2016; Liscombe et al., 2010; Mehta et al., 2024), clustering near the TMT from Narcissus cv. Tête‐à‐Tête previously implicated in narwedine methylation in vivo (Mehta et al., 2024), while LaTMT1 grouped with canonical plastid‐localized γ‐TMTs involved in tocopherol biosynthesis, and LaNMT with BIA‐associated CNMTs. Similarly, the three LaAKR isoforms cluster within the AKR4A/4B subfamilies alongside codeinone reductase from P. somniferum and AKRs from Lycoris aurea (Desgagné‐Penix & Facchini, 2012; Liscombe & Facchini, 2007), consistent with a conserved role in reductive tailoring steps in alkaloid pathways (Gavidia et al., 2002; Unterlinner et al., 1999). Together, these relationships point to the independent recruitment of MT and AKR scaffolds from both primary and specialized metabolism to serve terminal biosynthetic functions in AA pathways.

The choice of leaf tissue as the primary system for candidate characterization is supported by converging lines of evidence. In agreement with previous reports, metabolite profiling confirmed the accumulation of galanthamine and late‐stage intermediates, including norgalanthamine, in leaves, consistent with active late‐stage biosynthesis in this organ. Moreover, four previously unreported masses detected by GC–MS expand the known chemical diversity of L. aestivum and represent potential targets for future structural characterization and bioactivity assessment. Preferential transcript accumulation of LaTMT2, LaAKR2, and LaAKR3 in leaves relative to bulbs further indicates that the terminal methylation and reduction steps are transcriptionally enriched in this tissue. Crucially, dialyzed crude leaf extracts retained the capacity to convert nornarwedine to narwedine, norgalanthamine, and galanthamine in the presence of SAM and NADPH, providing direct enzymatic evidence that the required catalytic machinery is active in leaves. Together, these observations establish leaves as an active site of terminal galanthamine biosynthesis in L. aestivum, consistent with previous studies (Berkov et al., 2011; Karimzadegan et al., 2024).

Despite the lack of prior mechanistic studies on plant TMTs, conservation of the GluxxGluHis catalytic motif, characteristic of CNMTs (Bennett et al., 2018), in both LaTMT1 and LaTMT2 suggests that γ‐TMTs may have been functionally repurposed for alkaloid N‐methylation in L. aestivum. Molecular docking provided a structural basis for the differences in enzymatic activity observed among the three MT candidates. LaTMT2 showed both the shortest substrate‐SAM distances and the most favorable S–C:N–C dihedral angles for both substrates, supporting its superior activity. LaTMT1 showed acceptable dihedral angles (164–175°) but distances at the upper limit for methyl transfer (3.4–3.5 Å), consistent with its detectable but lower activity toward alkaloid substrates. LaNMT, despite comparable distances, showed dihedral angles far from the optimal 180° (−73° to −86°), suggesting that productive substrate binding does not translate into optimal catalytic geometry, which may account for its reduced efficiency relative to LaTMT2. LaNMT interactions with Glu204 and His208 in the presence of norgalanthamine remain consistent with its CNMT‐like identity (Liyanage et al., 2025). Among the AKR candidates, variation within the C‐terminal loop regions of the conserved (α/β)₈‐barrel (Simpson et al., 2009) likely underlies the distinct substrate preferences and directional biases observed among LaAKR1, LaAKR2, and LaAKR3 (Jez et al., 1997a). The predicted binding poses positioned the carbonyl group of both nornarwedine and narwedine in proximity to the catalytic Tyr54 and the NADPH nicotinamide ring, consistent with hydride transfer to the C=O bond, while Asp49 contributed to cofactor stabilization. Together with the RT‐qPCR, these results provided a structural and transcriptional basis for prioritizing LaTMT2 and the three LaAKRs for full biochemical characterization.

The distinct subcellular compartmentalization of the three MT candidates reflects their divergent functions. LaTMT1 localizes to plastids, consistent with its role in tocopherol biosynthesis (Muñoz & Munné‐Bosch, 2019; Zbierzak et al., 2010), while LaNMT distributes between the cytosol and ER, in line with the cytosolic localization reported for alkaloid NMTs in P. somniferum and Coffea arabica (Hagel & Facchini, 2012; Kodama et al., 2008). The Golgi localization of LaTMT2 is more unusual and biologically significant. Several SAM‐dependent MTs have been identified in the Golgi, where they access cytosol‐imported SAM to methylate substrates destined for trafficking (Guirimand, Guihur, Ginis, et al., 2011; Guirimand, Guihur, Poutrain, et al., 2011; Hugueney et al., 2009). Within the AA pathway, early steps occur in the nucleo‐cytosolic compartment (Majhi et al., 2023), O‐methylation is cytosolic, and oxidative coupling by CYP96T2 takes place at the ER (Lamichhane et al., 2025), placing LaTMT2‐mediated N‐methylation at the Golgi as the terminal biosynthetic step, in a compartment consistent with subsequent product sequestration or vacuolar trafficking. Future work should establish whether LaTMT2 resides in the cis‐, medial‐, or trans‐Golgi, and whether alkaloids are subsequently trafficked to the vacuole via vesicular transport. This extends the known subcellular architecture of the AA pathway and positions the Golgi as an unanticipated site of late‐stage alkaloid tailoring in L. aestivum.

LaTMT2 showed markedly higher activity than LaTMT1, ΔLaTMT1, and LaNMT toward both nornarwedine and norgalanthamine, consistent with the proposed catalysis reported for an 80%‐similar TMT from Narcissus cv. Tête‐à‐Tête (Mehta et al., 2024), whose substrate scope had not been verified until now. The comparable Km values for both substrates indicate that LaTMT2 binds nornarwedine and norgalanthamine with similar affinity, while the higher Kcat for nornarwedine demonstrates that substrate discrimination occurs at the catalytic step rather than during binding. This kinetic profile supports a model in which LaTMT2 can serve both terminal routes but preferentially drives N‐methylation prior to reduction. The in planta activity in N. benthamiana, with accumulation of both narwedine and galanthamine upon co‐expression with LaAKR3, confirmed this catalytic function in a cellular context and demonstrated that LaTMT2 and the AKRs operate compatibly within the same biosynthetic system.

The biochemical characterization of LaTMT1 confirmed its identity as a canonical γ‐tocopherol methyltransferase. Detection of α‐tocopherol in the presence of SAM and γ‐tocopherol aligns with the established role of plastid‐localized TMTs in vitamin E biosynthesis (Bergmüller et al., 2003; Shintani & DellaPenna, 1998; Tavva et al., 2007), and is consistent with its phylogenetic placement and plastid localization. Taken together, LaTMT1 appears to function in primary metabolism rather than in AA biosynthesis, narrowing the functionally relevant MT candidates to LaTMT2 and LaNMT. While LaNMT retains activity toward norgalanthamine in vitro and in vivo, its markedly lower efficiency relative to LaTMT2 and its ER/cytosolic localization suggest it may represent an accessory route, potentially active in a different tissue or developmental context rather than the primary methylating enzyme at the terminal step.

All three LaAKR isoforms catalyzed reversible interconversions between nornarwedine/norgalanthamine and narwedine/galanthamine, indicating that the terminal redox steps operate bidirectionally rather than as a strictly committed sequence. Within this system, LaAKR3 functions as the predominant reductive isoform driving forward flux toward galanthamine, while LaAKR1 preferentially catalyzes the “reverse” oxidative reactions. Docking analysis provided partial structural insight into this divergence. While C4(NADPH)–C(carbonyle) distances were comparable across isoforms (3.4–3.6 Å), LaAKR3 showed the shortest C4–C(olefinique) distance for narwedine (3.2 Å), consistent with its superior reductive activity toward this substrate. In contrast, LaAKR1 showed less favorable geometry for both reduction modes, coherent with its oxidative bias. The engagement of His117 with narwedine specifically in LaAKR1, absent in LaAKR2 and LaAKR3, may further contribute to its distinct directional preference. One additional structural basis for this functional divergence may be the substitution of methionine by leucine at position 119, analogous to residue substitutions in codeinone reductase COR1.3 that alter activity and product selectivity (Carr et al., 2021). The detection of norlycoraminone — consistent with C2–C3 bond reduction — alongside epi‐galanthamine points to catalytic plasticity within the LaAKR family, suggesting these enzymes may contribute to structural diversification of AAs beyond galanthamine, and that their reversible activity plays a broader role in shaping pathway flux than previously recognized (Dastmalchi et al., 2018).

These findings provide enzyme‐level support for the dual biosynthetic routes to galanthamine suggested by earlier isotope‐labeling studies (Eichhorn et al., 1998). The dual activity of LaTMT2 toward both nornarwedine and norgalanthamine, combined with the reversible redox activity of the LaAKR isoforms, suggests that the two routes are not competing alternatives but rather dynamically interconnected branches whose relative contributions may shift depending on substrate availability and redox state.

CONCLUSION

This study resolves the long‐standing ambiguity in the terminal steps of galanthamine biosynthesis by identifying LaTMT2 as a Golgi‐localized N‐methyltransferase acting on both nornarwedine and norgalanthamine, and by establishing that three AKR isoforms mediate reversible redox interconversions that dynamically modulate flux through the final stage of the pathway. Together, these enzymes reconcile competing biosynthetic models and reveal an unanticipated level of enzymatic flexibility at the terminal node of galanthamine formation. The Golgi localization of LaTMT2 further redefines the subcellular architecture of AA biosynthesis in L. aestivum. From a broader perspective, the substrate promiscuity of LaTMT2 and the tunable directionality of LaAKRs provide a rational enzymatic basis for metabolic engineering strategies aimed at enhancing galanthamine production and exploring the structural diversity of Amaryllidaceae alkaloids.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Identification of candidates and phylogenetic analysis and multiple sequence alignment

To find putative gamma tocopherol‐like NMT, γ‐TMT from Arabidopsis thaliana and Cr2270 from Catharanthus roseus, and CNMT from P. somniferum were used as bait to pull the candidate transcripts from the Leucojum aestivum transcriptome database. For aldo–keto reductase, sequence from Narcissus species was used as bait (Mehta et al., 2024). Sequences were aligned by ClustalW. Phylogenetic analysis was done using IQ‐TREE with sequences of MTS obtained from UniProt SwissProt or the NCBI database along with published sequences by Liscombe et al. (2012), Liscombe et al. (2010), Tamura et al. (2021). For AKRs, sequences were taken from the database and published sequences. The phylogenetic tree was inferred using the Maximum Likelihood method with the JTT matrix‐based model. As part of the heuristic search, initial tree topologies were generated automatically using the Neighbor‐Joining and BioNJ algorithms, and the best‐scoring topology based on log‐likelihood was selected for ML optimization. The tree was drawn to scale with branch lengths representing substitutions per site, and visualized using iTOL v6 (Jones et al., 1992; Letunic & Bork, 2021).

Molecular modeling of LaNMT, LaTMT1, LaTMT2, LaAKR1, LaAKR2, and LaAKR3

The established models of CNMT bound to N‐ (6GKZ, 6GKV) and S‐adenosyl‐L‐homocysteine from C. japonica were used as reference structures (Bennett et al., 2018). Folding prediction of LaNMT, LaTMT1, and LaTMT2 candidates was performed using AlphaFold Server Beta powered by AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024). Receptor preparation and docking were completed using MOE 2024.0601 software with the AMBER: EHT10 force field (Chemical Computing Group). CjNMT was used as a reference to position the methyl donor SAM in the active site of the candidate following superimposition. The docking site was predicted using Site Finder tools. Ligands (norgalanthamine, nornarwedine) isomeric SMILES codes retrieved from PubChem were submitted to the ZINC20 database to obtain 3D charge data files (Irwin et al., 2020). Two protomers predicted at pH = 8.0 were included as possible ligands. Triangle matcher was used as a placement method for 200 poses generated with tethered induced fit refinement. Ten docking poses for each protomer were compared with the crystallized protein–ligand complex. Coding sequences of LaTMT1 and LaAKR candidates were also submitted to Boltz2 using default parameters with appropriate cofactor (SAM and NADPH retrieved from PUBCHEM, respectively) for structure prediction and docking with γ‐tocopherol (TMT), and with narwedine and nornarwedine (AKRs) (neurosnap.ai; Passaro et al., 2025). The pose with the best docking score, consistent with the catalyzed reaction compared to reference crystal structures, was selected. PLIP was used to detect the interactions between ligands and receptor residues (Adasme et al., 2021). Visualization and figure preparation were performed using Pymol 2.3.4 (Schrödinger, CA, USA).

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

RNA was isolated from 80 mg of snap‐frozen Leucojum aestivum leaves using the RNeasy® Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Mississauga, Canada) according to the manufacturer's protocol. DNAse treatment was performed using Turbo‐DNAse according to the standard protocol from the supplier (Invitrogen, TURBO DNA‐free Kit). cDNA synthesis was carried out using the M‐MLV reverse transcriptase Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Canada) according to the manufacturer's protocol, employing both random hexamers and oligo(dT). The resulting cDNA was stored at −80°C.

PCR amplification of key candidate genes and RT‐qPCR gene expression analysis

Coding sequences of LaNMT, LaTMT1, LaTMT2 from L. aestivum were amplified using primers with attB1 and attB2 overhang along with a 3HA tag in the C‐terminal and 3Flag for LaAKR1, LaAKR2 and LaAKR3 (Table S10) for in vivo enzymatic assays in N. benthamiana. For the in vitro enzymatic assay, coding sequences were amplified using gene‐specific primers with restriction sites overhangs compatible with pMAL‐c2x multiple cloning site, that is, EcoRI and SalI for LaTMT1, EcoRI and XbaI for LaTMT2 as well as LaNMT. For LaAKR1, LaAKR2 and LaAKR3, the restriction sites were embedded after attB1 and stop codon. PCR amplifications were performed using PrimeStar GXL premix (TaKaRa Bio) in a T100 Thermal Cycler (Bio‐Rad). Amplifications were verified by running 2 μL of PCR product on a 0.9% agarose gel, with images captured using the Bio‐Rad ChemiDoc Imaging System. PCR clean‐up and gel purification were carried out following the manufacturer's instructions using the GeneAid PCR & Gel Kit.

Gene expression analysis was performed using reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT‐qPCR). Total RNA was extracted from approximately 150 mg of snap‐frozen plant tissue using a TRIzol‐based protocol. Briefly, samples were homogenized in 500 μL of TRIzol reagent by vortexing, followed by the addition of 200 μL chloroform. After vigorous mixing and incubation on ice for 3 min, the samples were centrifuged at 12000 g for 10 min at 4°C to separate the phases. The aqueous phase containing RNA was carefully transferred to a fresh tube containing 35 μL of 5 M NaCl and 400 μL isopropanol, mixed thoroughly, and incubated at −20°C for 10 min to precipitate RNA. Following centrifugation at 12000 g for 10 min at 4°C, the resulting RNA pellet was retained while the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was washed twice with 1 mL of 75% ethanol, centrifuged at 7500 g at 4°C, and then air‐dried for approximately 10 min. Residual genomic DNA was removed by treatment with TURBO‐DNase according to the manufacturer's instructions (Invitrogen TURBO DNA‐free Kit). RT‐qPCR reactions were carried out using the Luna® Universal One‐Step RT‐qPCR Kit (New England Biolabs) following the supplier's protocol. Gene expression levels were normalized to the reference gene Histone3 using threshold cycle (Ct) values. Relative transcript abundance was calculated using the comparative ΔΔCt method based on the mean Ct values obtained from technical triplicates of pooled biological triplicates. Data analysis and visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism.

Cloning and agrobacterium transformation for in vivo characterization

PCR‐amplified fragments were cloned using the Gateway cloning system according to the manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). In this process, the attP‐flanked pDONR221 vector, which contains a kanamycin resistance gene, was used in a BP recombination reaction to produce attL‐flanked entry clones with attB‐flanked sequences for LaNMT, LaTMT1, LaTMT2, and EGFP. These entry clones were then transformed into E. coli DH5α chemically competent cells, which were cultured on LB agar plates containing 50 μg/mL kanamycin. Positive clones were identified via colony PCR, followed by plasmid extraction using the Presto Mini Plasmid Kit (Geneaid), according to the manufacturer's protocol. The integrity of each positive entry clone was confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Genome Quebec). After verification, selected plasmids were used in an LR recombination reaction, conducted overnight with LR clonase enzyme, to facilitate the recombination between the attL‐flanked entry clone and attR‐flanked destination vector (pK7WG2). The resulting LR reaction products were transformed into E. coli DH5α cells and selected on LB agar plates containing 50 μg/mL spectinomycin. Positive clones were confirmed through colony PCR using Taq polymerase (Taq'Ozyme, France) and, from positive clones, plasmids were extracted using 10 mL LB broth cultures supplemented with spectinomycin. The confirmed expression clones were then transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 by electroporation. For the localization, constructs of LaNMT, LaTMT1, and LaTMT2 cloned were in pB7FWG2 expression vectors to have C‐terminal fusion EGFP protein and were co‐transformed with free RFP or ER‐mCherry (ER‐rk CD3‐959), or Golgi‐mCherry (G‐rk CD3‐967) plasmids into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101 (Nelson et al., 2007). After 48 h of incubation on LB plates at 28°C, the presence of the insert was re‐confirmed via colony PCR like before.

Confocal image acquisition

Laser scanning confocal microscopy was performed using a Leica TCS SP8 system (Leica Microsystems) equipped with a HC PL APO CS2 40×/1.30 oil immersion objective. Fluorescence signals were examined in sequential mode from agroinfiltrated leaf discs. GFP and chlorophyll were excited at 488 nm, while RFP/mCherry was excited at 561 nm. Emission was recorded at 520–540 nm for GFP, 580–620 nm for RFP/mCherry, and 680–700 nm for chlorophyll autofluorescence. Subcellular localization was assessed in three independent experiments, and image processing was carried out using LAS AF Lite software (v.4.0.0.11706).

Cloning, transformation, and protein purification for in vitro characterization

PCR‐amplified fragments were cloned using the restriction digestion/ligation cloning system according to the manufacturer's instructions (NEB). The PCR fragments and pMAL‐c2X plasmids were double digested with restriction enzymes and ligated by T4 DNA ligase. Transformation and plasmid amplification/purification was performed like before using 100 μg/mL ampicillin in growth media. Plasmids were transformed into E. coli Rossetta cells on LB agar plates containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin and chloramphenicol (34 μg/mL). Positive clones were confirmed through colony PCR, and single colony of E. coli Rosetta (BL21) harboring the gene of interest were further grown in 5 mL LB broth cultures (seed culture), supplemented with ampicillin and chloramphenicol overnight. After 14 h, 1 mL of seed culture was transferred in 300 mL LB broth supplemented with antibiotics. As the OD600 reached 0.5, 0.25 mM isopropyl‐β‐D‐thiogalactopyranoside, 50 μM zinc chloride, and 50 μM magnesium chloride were added, and the cultures were incubated overnight at 18°C with 210 rpm in a shaking incubator. Cells were harvested and protein extraction and purification was performed (Lamichhane et al., 2025). Finally, the pure, concentrated, and desalted proteins were obtained using amicon ultra centrifugal filters (Merk, Millipore) and aliquoted in individual tubes and preserved at −80°C.

In vitro enzymatic assay

Recombinant MTs proteins were expressed as N‐terminal maltose‐binding protein (MBP) fusion constructs and purified using affinity chromatography as detailed by New England Biolabs, Inc. For LaTMT1, which remained insoluble, crude protein extracts were used directly at a final concentration of 20 μM. All enzymatic reactions were performed in a total volume of 100 μL under the following standardized conditions: 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5), 2 mM SAM as the methyl donor, 100 μM of substrate, and 5 μM of purified enzyme. Initially reactions were incubated at 35°C for 2 h and 30 min. Following incubation, reactions were quenched by adding an equal volume of mobile phase acidified to pH 2. Papaverine was added as an internal standard to make final 1 ppm in the reaction mix from a stock of 200 ppm. Samples were centrifuged at 10000 rpm for 15 min to remove precipitated proteins, and the resulting supernatants were subjected to downstream metabolite analysis by LC–MS/MS. To comprehensively characterize the LaTMT2 enzyme, we performed enzyme kinetics under systematically varied conditions. Serial dilutions of LaTMT2 (5–0.156 μM, two‐fold dilutions) were assayed at 1, 3, and 5 h to determine optimal enzyme concentration and reaction duration. Temperature dependence was evaluated over a gradient of 20, 30, 40, and 50°C for 1–5 h. Using the optimal enzyme concentration, reaction time, and temperature, the effect of pH on enzymatic activity was assessed across five buffer conditions (pH 5.0, 5.5, 6.5, 7.5, 8.5, and 9.5). In all assays, S‐adenosylmethionine (SAM, 2 mM) served as the methyl donor, and the substrate concentration was maintained at 50 μM. Finally, under the identified optimal conditions (enzyme concentration, reaction time, temperature, and pH), substrate saturation experiments were performed to determine the Michaelis–Menten kinetic parameters, Km and kcat. Similarly, for AKRs 1 mM NADPH in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) with 100 μM of substrate and 2 μL of enzyme was used and incubated for 2 h prior to quenching. Final yield was normalized based on enzyme concentration used.

For the in vitro enzymatic study using total protein extracts from L. aestivum leaves, crude protein extracts were prepared from cryopreserved L. aestivum leaf tissues. Approximately 400 mg of frozen tissue was finely ground and resuspended in 4 mL protein extraction (PE) buffer containing 100 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 10% glycerol, 10 mM β‐mercaptoethanol, and 2% polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP). The homogenate was lysed by sonication at 40% amplitude for 8 min using cycles of 15 s pulse followed by 30 s cooling intervals.

To remove endogenous metabolites and small cofactors, the crude lysate was dialyzed using ‘Spectra/Por 3’ dialysis membranes against 40 mL PE buffer at 4°C with gentle shaking. The dialysis buffer was replaced every 2 h for four cycles. The dialyzed extract was subsequently clarified by centrifugation at 4000 g for 20 min, and the supernatant protein was collected for enzymatic assays following protein quantification. In vitro enzyme reactions were performed using 400 μg protein in 100 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.5) containing 100 μM substrate. Reactions were supplemented with 2 mM SAM for methyltransferase assays or together with 2 mM NADPH for methyltransferase followed by reductase assays. Reaction mixtures were incubated at 30°C for 4 h prior to quenching with mobile phase and run in LC–MS/MS analysis. Authentic standards were used in all the experiments for standardization as well as quantification; however, while quantification was not essential, peak area ratio was calculated by dividing the peak area of the observed metabolite with the peak area of internal standard, that is, papaverine to represent relative abundance. Minor background contributions were accounted for in all analyses. Specifically, narwedine, synthesized from galanthamine, and nornarwedine, derived from narwedine, carryover was subtracted from experimental measurements as background. A similar background elimination was applied to the norgalanthamine standard (MedChemExpress; 96.6% purity), which contained trace galanthamine contamination.

Transient expression of candidate genes in N. benthamiana (in vivo assay)

Individual Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 colonies carrying the desired gene constructs (LaNMT, LaTMT1, LaTMT2, LaAKR1, LaAKR2, LaAKR3, and EGFP) were grown in LB broth containing 50 μg/mL spectinomycin, 50 μg/mL rifampicin, and 30 μg/mL gentamicin and incubated at 28°C for 18–20 h. Culture was centrifuged at 3000 rpm; the pellet was washed in Agrobacterium induction buffer (10 mM MES, pH 5.6, 10 mM MgCl2, and 150 μM acetosyringone) and following incubation for 2 hours at room temperature. 3–4 weeks old N. benthamiana plants, grown in a controlled environment chamber at 22°C with a 16‐h /8‐h dark cycle, were used. The leaves were infiltrated with Agrobacterium suspensions adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.6 units, using a needleless syringe to infiltrate the suspensions to the abaxial side of the leaves. Three leaves from three independently infiltrated plants were used as a triplicate to test the hypothesis. Initially, the Agrobacterium suspensions containing the gene constructs were infiltrated into the leaves and incubated for 48 h, followed by a second infiltration step with 100 μM substrates. After 24 h, leaves were harvested, snap‐frozen, and stored at −80°C for further analysis.

Western blotting

The frozen samples were ground into a fine powder using a mortar and pestle under liquid nitrogen. The expression of the agroinfiltrated genes in N. benthamiana leaves was verified by western blot. For this, 100 mg of tissue was resuspended in 0.5 mL of lysis buffer (100 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, 10% glycerol, 10 mM β‐mercaptoethanol, and 2% polyvinylpolypyrrolidone) and incubated on ice for 30 min with gentle inversion. The extracts were then centrifuged at 12000 g for 30 min at 4°C to pellet the debris, and the supernatants were collected. Protein samples were run in an SDS gel with 6% stacking and 10% resolving phase. A specific primary antibody was used to verify protein expression on PVDF membranes after semi‐dry transfer, followed by blocking with 5% milk for 2 h at room temperature. A rabbit anti‐HA or anti‐flag monoclonal primary antibody raised in rabbit (Cell Signaling) was used overnight at 4°C. Host‐specific secondary antibodies conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP), specifically the Immun‐Star goat anti‐rabbit HRP conjugate from Bio‐Rad, were utilized, and the membrane was illuminated with ECL reagent to detect the bands on the membrane.

Substrates and standards preparation

Narwedine and nornarwedine were synthesized from galanthamine hydrobromide in two steps via a protocol adapted from reported procedures (Hirnschall et al., 2002; Kogure et al., 2011).Scheme 1. Chemical synthesis of narwedine and nornarwedine

Scheme 1. Chemical synthesis of narwedine and nornarwedine

Step A. Galanthamine hydrobromide (200.9 mg, 0.546 mmol) and potassium carbonate (150.7 mg, 1.090 mmol) were added to dry dichloromethane (10 mL), then stirred at room temperature for 1.5 h. The mixture was filtered over glass wool to remove the remaining solid, and the latter was rinsed with dry dichloromethane (3 × 5 mL). The combined dichloromethane solution (25 mL) was brought to 0°C before adding manganese (IV) oxide (94.2 mg, 1.084 mmol), then stirred at 0°C for 5 min. The mixture was then stirred at room temperature for 50 h before adding a second portion of manganese (IV) oxide (47.2 mg, 0.543 mmol). The mixture was stirred at room temperature again for 20 hours before adding a third portion of manganese (IV) oxide (47.7 mg, 0.549 mmol). The solution was finally stirred at room temperature for 30 h. The reaction mixture was then passed through Celite, rinsed with dichloromethane, and the filtrate was evaporated under reduced pressure to afford narwedine (26.1 mg, 17%) without further purification. Spectroscopic data (GCMS, 1H‐NMR, and 13C‐NMR) matched those reported in the literature (Hirnschall et al., 2002; Kogure et al., 2011).

Step B. Narwedine (21.0 mg, 0.0736 mmol) and meta‐chloroperoxybenzoic acid (14.5 mg, 0.0840 mmol) were added to dry dichloromethane (2 mL), then stirred at room temperature for 3.5 h to form narwedine N‐oxide in situ. To the latter crude solution of narwedine N‐oxide was added a solution of ferrous sulfate heptahydrate (12.7 mg, 0.0457 mmol) in methanol (1 mL), and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 30 min. A solution of disodium hydrogen phosphate (15.6 mg, 0.110 mmol) in water (1 mL) was added to the reaction, and the mixture was stirred at room temperature for 10 min. The precipitate was collected by filtration, then washed with dichloromethane. The pH of the biphasic mixture was adjusted to >8.5 with concentrated ammonium hydroxide (5 drops) under vigorous stirring. The resulting phases were then separated, and the aqueous phase was extracted with dichloromethane (2 × 10 mL). The combined organic layers were washed with 0.25 M NaOH (15 mL) and brine (15 mL), then dried with sodium sulfate, filtered by gravity, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude residue was purified by flash chromatography (SiO2) with 10% methanol in chloroform as eluent to afford nornarwedine (8.3 mg, 42%). Spectroscopic data (GCMS, 1H‐NMR and 13C‐NMR) matched those reported in the literature (Hirnschall et al., 2002; Kogure et al., 2011).

Sample preparation for metabolite analysis

Metabolites were extracted from the crushed snap‐frozen samples using a methanol–water extraction solvent (80:2, v/v) at a consistent ratio of 20 μL/mg of the sample. Before extraction, an internal standard of papaverine was added at a concentration of 2 ppm. The samples were then placed on a shaker at 200 rpm overnight, followed by heating at 65°C for 20 min with the lids closed. After centrifugation at 10000 rpm for 10 min, the supernatants were collected and dried using a rotary evaporator. The dried extracts were reconstituted in the mobile phase, consisting of Milli‐Q water and methanol (both containing 0.1% formic acid) at a 90:10 ratio by concentrating 20 times the initial extraction volume. The reconstituted samples were vortexed, filtered through a 0.2 μm PTFE filter, and subsequently analyzed by HPLC–MS/MS.

Metabolite screening in Leucojum aestivum leaf

Leucojum aestivum bulbs purchased from The Seed Company (Canada) were grown indoor in the laboratory plant room. Untargeted metabolite analysis of L. aestivum leaves was performed by GC–MS, while targeted metabolite analysis was performed by LC–MS/MS. Cryopreserved macerated leaves (100 mg) were extracted with 2000 μL of solvent (80:20 methanol: water), heated at 60°C for 20 min, followed by 20 min of vortexing. The extract was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected in another tube. For the remaining biomass, the non‐polar solvent hexane was used for further extraction with an equal volume as before. The supernatant of the extracts collected in new tubes was evaporated under vacuum in a rotary evaporator for the polar fraction and under a stream of nitrogen for the non‐polar fraction. The dried sample was reconstituted with mobile phase for LC–MS/MS and in 100% methanol for GCMS analysis (Baeshen et al., 2023).

Instrumentation and chromatographic conditions

LC–MS/MS

High‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA) system equipped with an Agilent Jet Stream ionization source, a binary pump, an autosampler and a column compartment were used for the analysis. Compounds separation was achieved using a Kinetex EVO C18 column (150 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm, 100 Å; Phenomenex, Torrance, USA). Five microliters of each sample were injected onto the column that was set at 30°C. A gradient method made of (A) formic acid 0.1% v/v in water and (B) formic acid 0.1% v/v in methanol with a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min was used to achieve chromatographic separation. The HPLC elution program is described as follows: 0 min, 10% B; 10.0 min, 10% B; 20.0 min, 100% B; 25.0 min, 100% B; 26.0 min, 10% B. The total run time was 30 min per sample to allow the reconditioning of the column prior to the next injection. The parameters used in the MS/MS source were set as follows: gas flow rate 10 L·min−1, gas temperature 300°C, nebulizer 45 psi, sheath gas flow 11 L·min−1, sheath gas temperature 300°C, capillary voltage 4000 V in ESI+ and 3500 V in ESI− and nozzle voltage 500 V. Agilent MassHunter Data Acquisition (version 1.2) software was used to control the HPLC–MS/MS. MassHunter Qualitative Analysis (version 10.0) and MassHunter Quantitative QQQ Analysis (version 10.0) softwares were used for data processing. Compound identifications were made using authentic reference standards or by using collision‐induced dissociation (CID) mass spectra published in literature (Kilgore, Augustin, et al., 2016; Kilgore, Holland, et al., 2016; Mehta et al.,2024) in the case where reference standards were not available. MRM transitions and instrument parameters used for identifications in in vivo experiments are included in Table S11. Instrument parameters used in MRM mode for metabolite screening in L. aestivum leaves and in SIM mode for in vitro assays are presented in Tables S12 and S13, respectively. Instrument parameters used in Product‐Ion mode to obtain the full CID mass spectra of signals detected in SIM mode are presented in Table S14.

GC–MS

For GC–MS analysis, samples were injected into the GC–MS (Agilent Technologies 6890 N GC coupled with 5973 N inert MSD) in electron ionization mode at 70 eV. The temperature ramp used is described as follows: temperature was set at 100°C for 2 min, followed by 100–180°C at 15°C·min−1, 180–300°C at 5°C·min−1, and a 10 min hold at 300°C. Injector and detector temperatures were set at 250°C and 280°C, respectively, and the flow rate of carrier gas (He) was 1 mL·min−1. A split ratio of 1:10 was applied, and the injection volume was 1 μL.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise stated. Comparisons between groups were evaluated using two‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test to determine statistical significance between group means. A P‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
