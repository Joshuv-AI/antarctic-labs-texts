Exercise-induced fatigue affects lower limb biomechanics in high-risk backhand smash landings among female badminton players
Authors: Binyong Ye, Bin Zhang, Zhanyang He, Chuimei Sheng, Gongju Liu, Houwei Zhu, Guanliang Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThis study used one-dimensional statistical parametric mapping (SPM1d) to examine how exercise-induced fatigue affects lower limb biomechanics during landing from high-risk backhand smashes, including the backhand rear-court jump smash (BRJS) and backhand lateral jump smash (BLJS), in elite female badminton players. After fatigue, athletes showed increased hip coronal motion, knee valgus, and trunk lateral tilt, but reduced hip abduction, indicating weakened coronal control. These biomechanical changes suggest an increased risk of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury. BRJS presented a sagittal plane dominant energy absorption strategy, while BLJS presented a coronal plane dominant balance strategy. Overall, exercise-induced fatigue reshapes landing control strategies and induces biomechanical alterations consistent with an elevated risk of ACL injury. In addition, BRJS exhibits biomechanical patterns potentially associated with ankle sprains, whereas BLJS displays loading patterns linked to ACL injury mechanisms, suggesting movement-specific potential injury risks.

Introduction

Badminton, as a globally widespread competitive and recreational sport, has more than 200 million regular participants.1,2 However, due to risk factors such as high speed, high intensity, variable techniques and tactics, frequent landings, and complex combinations of movements, badminton players are highly susceptible to injury.2,3 Existing epidemiological data indicate that among elite and high-level badminton players, regardless of sex, the overall injury incidence is approximately 1–4 cases per 1,000 h of exposure, with knee and ankle injuries being the most common.4 For example, a large cohort study of Japanese national-level players aged 7–22 years reported injury incidences of 3.24 cases/1,000 h in male athletes aged 15–16 years and 3.52 cases/1,000 h in female athletes aged 17–18 years, when calculated per 1,000 training hours.5

It should be noted, however, that current badminton injury epidemiology has not reached a consensus as to whether female athletes have a higher injury incidence than males.4,5,6 Some studies have reported a greater absolute number of injuries in male players,6 but after adjustment for the number of participants or exposure time, the overall incidence is generally of the same order of magnitude between sexes,4 with only a slight trend toward higher rates in females in a few samples.5 Thus, at the macroscopic level of injury incidence, sex differences in badminton appear unstable. In contrast, sex differences are more consistent and pronounced during the landing phase of smash actions. Studies have shown that, during the preparation and impact phases of landing, female athletes typically exhibit greater knee valgus angles, higher posterior ground reaction forces (GRFs), lower leg and knee joint stiffness, smaller flexion angles, and muscle recruitment and co-contraction patterns that differ from those of males; these unfavorable mechanical and neuromuscular characteristics are thought to be closely associated with the higher risk of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury in females.5 Collectively, these findings suggest that female athletes display biomechanical patterns distinct from those of males, such that their injury mechanisms cannot be directly extrapolated from male samples. Therefore, the present study focused on elite female badminton players to minimize the confounding effect of sex.

Within badminton-specific movements, Kimura et al.7 retrospectively analyzed 21 badminton-related ACL injuries and identified landing from an overhead stroke in the backhand rear court as a typical high-risk scenario strongly associated with ACL injury. Other surveys have similarly reported that more than 40% of lower-limb injuries in badminton occur in the backhand rear-court area, a substantial proportion of which are related to landing from smash actions.8 The backhand rear-court jump smash (BRJS) and backhand lateral jump smash (BLJS) are two of the most commonly used single-leg landing jump-smash techniques in the backhand rear court and can, to some extent, be used interchangeably by players (Figure 1).7 The high risk associated with these techniques primarily arises from the asymmetrical body posture during backhand strokes and the abrupt increase in biomechanical loading at the moment of landing, particularly when the body is supported on a single limb; under such conditions, the knee and ankle must sustain very high impact forces, markedly increasing injury risk.7,8,9 For instance, the Spanish badminton player Carolina Marín sustained an ACL injury when landing from a BLJS, whereas the Chinese player Xuerui Li was injured when landing from a BRJS, both with severe consequences for their professional careers. Despite the widespread use of these techniques in competition and their close association with lower-limb joint injuries, there is still a lack of systematic comparison between BRJS and BLJS in terms of landing biomechanics and potential injury patterns. Fatigue has been increasingly recognized as an important factor influencing lower-limb injury risk, and growing evidence in badminton indicates that a fatigued state specifically increases the likelihood of ankle and knee injuries. Herbaut et al.10 reported that ankle sprains occur predominantly toward the end of matches or training sessions; exercise-induced fatigue increases the magnitude of ankle inversion at initial ground contact and reduces the pre-activation of stabilizing ankle muscles, thereby elevating the risk of inversion sprain. He et al.11 examined ankle biomechanics during single-leg landings in BRJS and BLJS among female players and found that, after fatigue, the ankle inversion angle at initial contact and the peak inversion moment both increased significantly. With respect to the knee joint, Liu et al.12 induced fatigue in the knee musculature and observed significant increases in knee collapse and valgus moments as well as posteriorly directed horizontal GRF at early landing, suggesting an increased load on the ACL. In more recent work, a quadriceps fatigue protocol applied during BRJS revealed that, in a fatigued state, center-of-mass sway, knee flexion power, proximal tibial shear force, and joint reaction forces all increased, thereby exacerbating mechanical loading on the knee joint and heightening the risk of coronal-plane knee injury.13 Taken together, these badminton-specific studies indicate that fatigue often shifts the landing biomechanics of the ankle and knee toward patterns that are more conducive to lower-limb injury. Because BRJS and BLJS are essentially single-leg landing tasks that involve considerable mechanical loading, their landing phases place higher demands on lower-limb stability, impact attenuation, and multi-planar coordination.11 Accordingly, understanding how fatigue alters the landing biomechanics of these actions constitutes an important component of interpreting their potential injury mechanisms. Fatigue protocols in biomechanical research are commonly categorized as local muscle fatigue,14 continuous exercise-induced fatigue,15 or sport-specific fatigue,16 each producing distinct biomechanical responses.17,18 Given that badminton is a non-cyclical sport characterized by unilateral loading and frequent single-leg landings, careful selection of a sport-specific fatigue protocol is required to ensure that the induced fatigue appropriately simulates the actual movement demands of the game.19

Against this background, the present study aimed to apply statistical parametric mapping (SPM) to systematically analyze the single-leg landing biomechanics of elite female badminton players performing BRJS and BLJS in the backhand rear court, and to further examine the effects of fatigue on these biomechanical characteristics. The specific objectives were: (1) to compare lower-limb kinematics, kinetics, and GRF characteristics between BRJS and BLJS, thereby elucidating movement-specific differences in landing control and potential injury mechanisms between the two techniques; (2) to investigate the influence of fatigue on lower-limb biomechanical characteristics during these two actions; and (3) to determine whether fatigue effects are task-dependent, that is, whether fatigue differentially affects landing load patterns in BRJS and BLJS. Previous studies have shown that, in single-leg landing and related tasks, fatigue is commonly associated with a decline in coronal-plane control, such as increased trunk lateral lean toward the support side, reduced hip abduction angles, and greater knee valgus angles.10,11,12,13 From a technical standpoint, BLJS is also characterized by larger mediolateral displacement of the body’s center of mass and more pronounced coronal-plane loading during landing compared with BRJS. Accordingly, we proposed the following hypotheses: (1) fatigue would markedly alter lower-limb kinematics, joint moments, and GRF characteristics during both actions, thereby affecting the distribution of lower-limb loading; (2) the landing biomechanics of BRJS and BLJS differ significantly, reflecting distinct potential injury mechanisms; and (3) fatigue effects would be task-dependent, with more detrimental changes in landing biomechanics during BLJS than during BRJS. Discussion

This study employed the SPM1d method to investigate the effects of exercise-induced fatigue on the lower limb biomechanical characteristics during the landing phase of high-risk smash actions in the backhand area performed by female badminton athletes. The main findings indicate that post-fatigue, both BRJS and BLJS primarily exhibited altered coronal-plane control during landing, with only small sagittal-plane postural adjustments. Simultaneously, BRJS and BLJS demonstrated significant differences in trunk and lower limb joint angles, joint moments, and GRF characteristics. BRJS presented a sagittal plane-dominant energy absorption strategy, while BLJS presented a coronal plane-dominant balance strategy. Overall, the results partially supported the first hypothesis and fully supported the second. Hypothesis 1: fatigue significantly altered trunk and lower limb joint angles, but had no significant effect on joint moments or GRF. Hypothesis 2: BRJS and BLJS indeed exhibited differentiated joint control strategies. However, this study did not identify significant interaction effects, meaning the differences in the effects of fatigue on the two landing actions did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to the limited statistical power associated with the sample size; thus, the third hypothesis was not verified.

Analysis of the effects of fatigue on high-risk smash landing actions

In this study, the fatigue criteria included the following three items: heart rate exceeding 85% of maximum heart rate; RPE level ≥ 17; and vertical jump height below 70% of its maximum value. These comprehensive indicators have been validated in multiple studies and can effectively reflect the fatigue state of the central nervous system and peripheral muscles.20,21,22 During the experimental process, all participants met the aforementioned fatigue criteria, indicating that the adopted fatigue induction protocol possessed good effectiveness.

SPM1d waveform analyses showed that the effects of fatigue on BRJS and BLJS landings were primarily manifested in the coronal plane. Trunk lateral lean continuously increased from 0% to 66.71% of the landing phase, suggesting that fatigue first compromised the dynamic stability of the trunk musculature and reduced postural control, leading to greater trunk lean toward the support side in the early to mid-landing phase as a primary compensatory response to maintain balance.23 Subsequently, hip abduction angle decreased between 59.9% and 100% of the landing phase, accompanied by an increased coronal-plane ROM. This shift brought the GRF vector closer to the center of the supporting hip joint and shortened the coronal-plane moment arm, thereby partially unloading the fatigued hip abductors, but at the same time manifesting as insufficient hip abduction.24,25 Previous studies have shown that reduced hip abduction angle weakens the dynamic support capacity of the knee and predisposes it to valgus motion.26,27 Consistent with this, the increased knee valgus angle observed in our study during 78.8%–100% of the landing phase reflects a distal compensatory response. Taken together, the chain of changes revealed by SPM1d—greater trunk lateral lean, reduced hip abduction, and increased knee valgus—may represent a proximodistal coronal-plane kinetic chain that progressively propagates along the lower limb as landing proceeds, and may characterize a compensatory control pattern adopted under fatigue to preserve overall balance. It should be emphasized, however, that this mechanistic interpretation is based on an integrated inference from existing literature and the present findings, and, given the relatively small sample size, requires further verification in future studies to confirm its universality.

Similar fatigue-related alterations in coronal-plane lower-limb kinematics have been reported by Zhang et al.28 and Wright et al.,29 whereas other studies did not detect such differences.30,31 The primary reason for these inconsistent findings may lie in the heterogeneity of fatigue-induction protocols. Some experiments have employed unidirectional, repetitive tasks (e.g., vertical squats or forward drop jumps) that predominantly load the sagittal-plane musculature and may be insufficient to elicit substantial fatigue in muscles responsible for coronal-plane control.30,31 By contrast, the present study, as well as the aforementioned work by Zhang et al.28 and Wright et al.,29 used multidirectional fatigue protocols that more closely mimic match and training conditions, involving diversified movement patterns and multi-planar muscle coordination. Such protocols likely impose greater demands on the coronal-plane control system and, therefore, are more prone to reveal pronounced coronal-plane kinematic alterations under fatigue.

From the perspective of injury risk, an increase in knee joint valgus angle is a key risk factor for ACL injury.32 Previous studies have shown that when the knee abduction angle reaches 5°, the ACL load can increase by approximately 6 times, and exceeding 8° may lead to structural damage.33 These results suggest that the decline in coronal plane control ability induced by fatigue not only alters movement patterns but may also significantly elevate the risk of knee joint injury. Nevertheless, large-scale prospective studies are needed to explicitly quantify the correlation between these fatigue-induced biomechanical changes and actual injury rates. Additionally, attention should be paid to the effects of fatigue on sagittal plane movement control. This study found that in the later phase of the landing process (76.4%–100%), the trunk forward lean angle significantly decreased post-fatigue. A smaller trunk forward lean angle positions the GRF resultant point closer to the hip joint center, leading to increased recruitment and activation levels of the quadriceps femoris, thereby amplifying the anterior shear force on the tibia and further elevating the ACL loading risk.34,35

It is noteworthy that this study did not observe significant effects of the fatigue factor on GRF or lower limb joint moments. This may be related to the following two factors: first, the study results benefited from the high stringency of the SPM1d analysis employed. SPM1d can effectively control false positives across the entire waveform range, ensuring the robustness of statistical results and, to some extent, reducing the influence of incidental differences on the outcomes. Second, some research has found that as neuromuscular fatigue intensifies, lower limb biomechanical indicators during landing exhibit nonlinear changes from “hard landing” to “soft landing” and back to “hard landing,” with soft landing regarded as a protective strategy that gradually diminishes with deepening fatigue; it is speculated that the body tends to prioritize task completion over self-protection, thereby increasing injury risk.36 The participants in this study were in a relatively high fatigue state, possibly having entered the “hard landing” phase, resulting in no significant ROM changes in the sagittal plane ROM of the lower limb joints. Sagittal plane ROM largely determines GRF, and moments are calculated based on GRF using inverse dynamics methods. This may explain why GRF and lower limb moments showed no significant changes before and after fatigue.

Biomechanical differences analysis of different high-risk smash landing actions

The comparison results revealed significant differences in movement patterns between BRJS and BLJS. In terms of joint ROM, this study found that the ankle and hip joint ROM in BRJS were significantly greater than in BLJS in both the sagittal and horizontal planes. Existing research indicates that different landing directions influence lower limb landing control strategies.37 BRJS tends toward rearward landing, with greater sagittal plane displacement, where primary impact absorption occurs in the sagittal plane. The lower limb joints have higher degrees of freedom in the sagittal plane, allowing for broader movement to effectively buffer energy or reduce GRF.15,28 In contrast, BLJS tends toward lateral landing, requiring more coronal plane control, but the lower limb joints have smaller degrees of freedom in the coronal plane, leading to a more “rigid” landing pattern that directly transmits impact forces to passive tissues, increasing injury risk.38,39 This also explains why the anterior GRF in BRJS was significantly greater than in BLJS, while the lateral and vertical GRF in BLJS were larger. The differences in horizontal plane ROM may stem from the complexity of rearward landing. Studies suggest that landing typically relies on visual cues, but when visual access to the rear landing point is limited, individuals adopt more cautious joint control by increasing rotational range to adjust balance and compensate for perceptual deficits.40,41

Regarding the ankle joint, BRJS exhibited a significantly greater ankle external rotation angle than BLJS during 45.6%–62.0% of the landing process. A larger ankle external rotation angle is considered a protective landing strategy, possibly also due to limited visual access to the rear landing point.42 However, the study results showed that during 0%–9.2% and 0%–100% of the landing phase, BRJS demonstrated greater ankle inversion moment and internal rotation moment compared to BLJS. Existing research confirms that ankle stability significantly decreases when the ankle is in a plantarflexed position.43 Superimposing inversion and internal rotation moments on this posture further exacerbate ankle instability, making it more prone to sprains.44 Therefore, it is noteworthy that in the initial landing phase (0%–9.2%), the superimposition of plantarflexion posture with internal rotation and inversion moments significantly increases the risk of ankle sprains in BRJS.

The knee joint plays a central role in impact absorption and energy transmission during landing. Previous studies have shown that a smaller flexion angle significantly increases ACL shear stress, while a larger flexion angle can reduce anterior tibial shear force through hamstring co-activation.45,46 The results of this study indicated that in the initial landing phase (0%–6.3%), the knee flexion angle in BLJS was significantly smaller than in BRJS. This difference may also arise from variations in landing direction and balance control: BRJS involves rearward landing, allowing for greater flexion angles to absorb sagittal plane momentum, reflecting a “flexible buffering” strategy. In contrast, BLJS involves lateral landing, prioritizing coronal plane balance, which limits energy absorption through increased flexion angles. This difference results in higher shear loads on the ACL in BLJS during the early phase. Additionally, in the later landing phase (67.4%–100%), BLJS exhibited greater knee extension moment, while BRJS showed greater varus and external rotation moments during 0%–68.4%. An increased knee extension moment implies enhanced dominant activation of the quadriceps, generating greater anterior tibial shear force and increasing ACL load.45 In comparison, varus moment is not a direct cause of non-contact ACL injury, while external rotation moment provides some protective effect by dispersing partial stress.45,47 Overall, BLJS presents a “high shear load” kinetic characteristic during landing, making it a landing pattern with higher ACL injury risk.

During 0%–52.34% of the landing phase, BRJS had a smaller hip flexion angle, while BLJS exhibited greater hip extension moment during 0%–48.3%. From a technical action perspective, the smaller hip flexion angle in BRJS positions the support foot behind the center of mass during landing, better counteracting rearward motion inertia and utilizing anterior GRF to quickly return to the center court. In contrast, BLJS with a larger hip flexion angle requires stronger hip extension moment during landing to resist passive flexion caused by GRF and prevent hip collapse.48 Notably, the hip moment in BRJS transitions gradually from flexion to extension, forming an “active flexion—passive extension” temporal pattern. Devita et al.49 also identified this moment pattern in their study, suggesting that this control method facilitates accelerated trunk forward lean, bringing the center of mass closer to the knee level, thereby shortening the moment arm, reducing knee extensor load, and serving as a protective strategy. Meanwhile, BLJS had a smaller hip abduction angle during 0%–83.6%, lower adduction moment during 11.3%–100%, and greater internal rotation moment during 0%–11.6%. As discussed earlier, a smaller hip abduction angle is often accompanied by greater knee valgus, which is a key risk factor for ACL injury.27,50 The smaller adduction moment may be due to the smaller abduction angle in BLJS during landing, which does not require reliance on larger adduction moments to maintain balance and stability during movement. During 0%–11.64% of the landing phase, BLJS had greater internal rotation moment compared to BRJS. Research suggests that greater hip internal rotation moment is associated with higher ACL injury risk.51 Studies by Lawrence52 and Ireland53 further support this view. They found that females with greater hip external rotation strength during single-leg landing experience smaller GRFs and relatively lower knee valgus moments, indicating a significant association between hip external rotation strength and good dynamic lower limb control. Therefore, enhancing hip external rotation strength is crucial for maintaining lower limb stability and preventing ACL injuries.

Regarding the trunk, BRJS exhibited greater trunk forward lean angle in the mid-to-late landing phase (54.1%–100%), which helps lower the center of mass, enhance posterior chain muscle activation, and improve overall support capacity and energy absorption efficiency.54 This postural adjustment couples with the hip joint’s “active flexion—passive extension” temporal pattern to effectively reduce knee extensor load. In contrast, BLJS showed greater lateral tilt angle during 0%–88.6%, reflecting its reliance on coronal plane balance control, altering the GRF resultant direction and increasing ACL load risk.25,34,35 Thus, the two landing actions demonstrate “forward lean energy absorption” and “lateral tilt stability” control differences in trunk posture regulation.

From a time-series perspective, integrating the SPM1d results allows a clearer description of the control characteristics of BRJS and BLJS across different phases of landing. In the early landing phase (approximately 0%–10%), BRJS is characterized by a plantarflexed ankle subjected to relatively large inversion and internal rotation moments, whereas BLJS shows smaller knee flexion angles accompanied by larger hip internal rotation moments. These differences suggest that BRJS is more prone to an ankle sprain mechanism at the onset of impact, while BLJS more closely resembles an ACL high shear-loading pattern. During the subsequent mid-landing phase (approximately 10%–60%), BRJS relies primarily on sagittal-plane mechanisms for momentum dissipation and energy absorption, as reflected by greater ankle, knee, and hip flexion excursions, as well as a hip moment pattern of “active flexion-passive extension” coordinated with increased trunk forward lean. In contrast, BLJS must maintain balance with smaller knee flexion and hip abduction angles, relying more heavily on hip extension moments and trunk lateral lean toward the support side to achieve coronal-plane stability. By the late landing phase (approximately 60%–100%), BLJS still exhibits larger knee extension moments and higher vertical and medial GRFs, whereas BRJS maintains greater trunk forward lean and relatively reduced extensor loading at the knee. These findings indicate that the two techniques diverge further in terms of load-transfer pathways and terminal joint loading patterns during the latter part of landing.

Taken together, exercise-induced fatigue, as elicited by the badminton-specific fatigue protocol, significantly affects the landing control patterns of high-risk smash actions in the backhand court, manifesting as a decrease in coronal-plane control ability and the onset of compensatory mechanisms in the coronal plane. These alterations may potentially heighten the susceptibility to ACL injury in the landing leg.

At the same time, BRJS and BLJS, two commonly used and to some extent interchangeable single-leg jump-smash techniques in the backhand rear court, exhibit distinct movement patterns. BRJS follows a sagittal plane-dominated energy absorption strategy, while BLJS follows a coronal plane-dominated balance strategy. They also display different injury risk profiles: BRJS, in the early landing phase, involves greater ankle inversion/internal rotation moments at the plantarflexed position, suggesting a potential vulnerability to ankle sprains. BLJS, on the other hand, exhibits mechanical characteristics such as limited knee flexion, knee extension dominance, trunk lateral lean, and higher medial GRF and initial vertical GRF, which are associated with elevated ACL injury risk factors.

Limitations of the study

First, this study did not utilize electromyography or motion simulation systems, which limited the ability to deeply analyze the specific effects of fatigue on muscle activity and fully reveal the dynamic mechanisms across different muscle groups. Furthermore, we acknowledge that a comprehensive understanding of injury mechanisms requires a broader kinetic analysis. Future investigations should consider incorporating joint reaction forces, joint power, and impulse. These metrics would provide deeper insights into tissue loading and energy flow characteristics, thereby enhancing the predictive value for injury prevention. Finally, the relatively small sample size remains a limitation of this study. Therefore, caution is advised when generalizing these findings to populations other than elite female athletes, such as males or recreational players. Future studies with larger cohorts are recommended to verify these biomechanical patterns.

Experimental model and study participant details

An a priori power analysis was conducted using G∗Power 3.1 for a 2 (fatigue: pre vs. post) × 2 (action: BRJS vs. BLJS) repeated-measures ANOVA (within-subject factors). Based on effect sizes reported in previous fatigue-related landing biomechanics studies, a medium-to-large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.40), an alpha level of 0.05, and a desired statistical power of 0.80 were specified. Under these assumptions, the required sample size was estimated to be 13 participants. Therefore, this study recruited 13 national first-class female badminton athletes, detailed information see Table 1. Participants had no history of cardiovascular disease in the past year, normal lower limb alignment, and had no lower-limb musculoskeletal injury within the previous 12 months. As the participants were female, they were required to have regular menstrual cycles and not be pregnant, and all participants had no other health conditions or ongoing treatments that could affect their safe participation in the study or influence the results. To ensure consistency and minimize potential confounding variables, the experiments were conducted under uniform conditions at the same venue. All participants used standardized equipment, including rackets, clothing, and shoes of the same brand, to eliminate any external influences. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Sport University (project number: No. 2021179H) and adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were informed of the experimental procedures and provided written informed consent prior to the experiment.

Method details

Data collection

Before the experiment began, participants changed into standardized clothing provided uniformly under the guidance of staff. They first jogged on a treadmill at a speed of 8 km/h for 6 min, followed by 3 min of static stretching. According to the experimental requirements, the Helen Hayes model was employed, with 29 reflective markers placed on anatomical landmarks of the participants to capture segmental kinematics (Figure 2).55 The testing was conducted in two separate sessions (non-fatigued and fatigued conditions) scheduled one week apart to prevent residual fatigue. At the first session, the order of conditions (non-fatigued vs. fatigued) was randomly assigned by drawing lots for each participant; at the second session, the opposite order was used so that the sequence of non-fatigued and fatigued conditions was counterbalanced across the sample. Within each session, after the warm-up, the order of BRJS and BLJS tests was also randomized by drawing lots for each participant. All participants were right-handed, and both BRJS and BLJS were performed on the same limb (racket held in the right hand with single-leg landing on the left leg). All instrumentation, including marker placement and setup of the testing devices, was performed by the same experienced technician for all participants to ensure consistency. Before fatigue induction, participants first performed maximal vertical jumps on a vertical jump platform (Kistler Quattro Jump, 9290BD, Switzerland) to determine their individual maximum jump height, which was used as the reference for the 70% fatigue criterion. Fatigue assessment was conducted immediately after fatigue induction. If the participant met the fatigue criteria, BRJS and BLJS movement tests were performed within a 5-min window; if the criteria were not met, fatigue induction was repeated until the fatigue criteria were satisfied.

The validity of the trials was evaluated by two professional badminton referees and two coaches, using strict criteria including striking a badminton shuttlecock within a predefined overhead hitting zone and directing it to a designated target area, the accuracy of the landing position, full foot contact on the force plate, and correct execution of BRJS and BLJS techniques; for each condition, at least three valid smash trials meeting all criteria were collected per participant.

Fatigue protocol

To closely simulate the intermittent, high-intensity loading characteristics of badminton training and competition, this study developed a badminton-specific fatigue-induction protocol based on the Badminton-Specific Speed Test (BST) proposed and validated by Madsen et al.56 On the basis of the original BST, randomness in stroke selection and multidirectional court movement was introduced to reproduce the on-court decision-making demands and movement uncertainty encountered under match conditions, with the aim of concurrently inducing both peripheral and central fatigue so that the fatigue state achieved by the participants more closely approximated that experienced during actual competition and high-intensity training.10 When passing through the infrared timing gate, participants were required to rapidly execute basic badminton footwork from the center of the court to four predefined target points in response to randomized visual instructions on the display (forecourt: standardized net shot; rear court: standardized high clear to both back corners), and to return immediately to the center of the court after each stroke (Figure 3). Fifteen repetitions constituted one set, with a 15-s rest interval between sets.

Fatigue criteria

During the fatigue protocol, a metronome was first used to determine the fastest movement frequency that each participant could sustain, thereby ensuring that they consistently performed at a relatively high movement speed. When a participant was no longer able to complete the required movements in time with the metronome, they were immediately instructed to perform five maximal vertical jumps on the spot. If, during these five jumps, the height of consecutive jumps failed to exceed 70% of the participant’s individual maximal vertical jump height, their fatigue level was further evaluated by combining subjective ratings of perceived exertion (RPE) and heart rate. The threshold values for RPE and heart rate were based on the high-intensity load ranges reported in previous physiological monitoring studies of badminton competition.57,58 Fatigue induction was considered successful when the participant’s RPE was ≥17 and heart rate was ≥85% of HRmax.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Marker trajectories and ground reaction force data were processed using Visual3D software (version 6, C-Motion, USA) to compute lower-limb kinematic and kinetic variables. Marker coordinate data and ground reaction forces were low-pass filtered using a fourth-order Butterworth filter with a cutoff frequency of 10 Hz. Three-dimensional joint angles of the lower limbs were calculated using a Cardan–Euler rotation sequence.59 All kinetic variables were normalized to body weight. Initial contact was defined as the first frame at which the vertical ground reaction force exceeded 10 N. The analysis window extended from initial contact to the instant of maximum knee flexion angle during landing. For each trial, the kinematic and kinetic waveforms within this time window were time-normalized to 101 data points, representing 0–100% of the landing phase.

Paired-sample t-tests and two-way repeated-measures analyses of variance (2 × 2: fatigue × task) were conducted in SPSS (version 27.0.1, IBM, USA) to analyze scalar variables such as fatigue indices and joint range of motion (ROM). Because joint angles, joint moments, and ground reaction forces change continuously during landing, and one-dimensional time-series data provide a more complete representation of movement patterns than isolated discrete points, statistical parametric mapping (SPM) was used to compare full waveforms across conditions.60,61 Time-series analyses were performed in MATLAB R2019a (MathWorks, USA) using an open-source SPM toolbox.62 Two-way repeated-measures SPM analyses (fatigue × task) were applied to the time-normalized kinematic and kinetic waveforms, with the level of significance set at α = 0.05.

Before conducting SPM analyses, the normality of the residuals was examined. When the assumption of normality was satisfied, parametric SPM1d tests were used; otherwise, the nonparametric SnPM1d approach was adopted. SPM1d implements parametric inference on one-dimensional fields based on random field theory, whereas SnPM1d provides a distribution-free alternative via permutation testing.63 This framework enables the identification of statistically significant differences over the entire landing phase, rather than being restricted to a limited number of preselected discrete time points.63,64
