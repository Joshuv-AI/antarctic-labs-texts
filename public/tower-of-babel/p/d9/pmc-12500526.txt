Glucocorticoids‐Induced Adrenal Insufficiency With Intractable Vomiting: A Case Report
Authors: Yamei Ran, Yongmei Peng, Juan Xiao, Li Zhang, Kangqi Wu, Wenjing Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAn 80‐year‐old male patient was admitted to our hospital with a one‐year history of recurrent reflux and vomiting, accompanied by mild fatigue and decreased appetite. Despite initial treatment with prokinetic agents, his symptoms showed minimal improvement. A comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, including imaging and laboratory tests, ruled out common gastrointestinal disorders (such as peptic ulcer disease, gastroesophageal reflux disease, and functional dyspepsia), intracranial pathologies, and other systemic conditions. Given his clinical presentation and a long‐standing history of chronic glucocorticoid use, glucocorticoid‐induced adrenal insufficiency was suspected as the underlying cause. Notably, after one month of hydrocortisone treatment, the patient exhibited significant clinical improvement, with no other discomfort symptoms. At present, he has been off corticosteroid treatment for six months without experiencing any other symptoms.Keywords: case report, glucocorticoids‐induced adrenal insufficiency, hypoadrenalism, secondary adrenal insufficiency, vomiting

Introduction

Nausea and vomiting are among the most common symptoms prompting patients to seek medical attention. These symptoms may result from a wide range of underlying conditions, including gastrointestinal disorders, intracranial lesions, adrenal insufficiency, and megaloblastic anemia. While gastrointestinal diseases are typically the initial focus in clinical evaluation, it is crucial to recognize that nausea and vomiting can also be manifestations of systemic or extra‐gastrointestinal etiologies.

Adrenal insufficiency, encompassing both primary and secondary forms, is one such condition that may present with nonspecific symptoms such as nausea and vomiting. Primary adrenal insufficiency, or Addison's disease, occurs due to adrenal cortex destruction, commonly caused by infections, malignancies, or autoimmune processes. It is characterized by low cortisol levels and elevated adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) concentrations. In contrast, secondary adrenal insufficiency arises from dysfunction of hypothalamic–pituitary, often due to tumors, medications, or genetic abnormalities, and typically presents with low cortisol levels and normal or suppressed ACTH levels. Clinical manifestations of adrenal insufficiency frequently include fatigue, weight loss, hypoglycemia, and hypotension. However, some patients may present with atypical symptoms, complicating the diagnostic process and potentially delaying treatment.

Herein, we report the case of an 80‐year‐old male with a one‐year history of recurrent reflux and vomiting. A thorough diagnostic evaluation ultimately identified glucocorticoid‐induced adrenal insufficiency as the underlying etiology. This case highlights the importance of considering adrenal insufficiency in the differential diagnosis of chronic nausea and vomiting, particularly in individuals with a history of prolonged glucocorticoid use. Through this report, we aim to review the diagnostic approach to persistent vomiting and share clinical insights into the recognition and management of adrenal insufficiency.

Case History/Examination

An 80‐year‐old male presented to our hospital with a one‐year history of reflux and vomiting, accompanied by mild fatigue and decreased reduced appetite. He experienced gastroesophageal reflux symptoms two to three times per week, typically after meals, without associated heartburn, chest pain, or sleep disturbances. Over the past year, he had multiple intermittent episodes of vomiting, also predominantly postprandial, each lasting approximately 1 to 2 h. Symptoms generally resolved after 2 or 3 vomiting episodes. The vomiting was non‐projectile, non‐bilious, and contained undigested food particles. He denied abdominal pain, bloating, persistent nausea, belching, constipation, or diarrhea, as well as systemic symptoms such as fever, dizziness, lower extremity edema, or headache. Previous evaluations at another hospital included liver and kidney function tests and gastroenteroscopy, all of which yielded normal results. He was initially diagnosed with a functional gastrointestinal disorder and treated with prokinetic agents; however, his response to therapy was unsatisfactory. The patient has no history of alcohol consumption, smoking, or illicit drug use. His medical history included nephrotic syndrome, for which he had received two separate two‐year courses of oral methylprednisolone: the first approximately two decades ago and the second four years ago. He had discontinued methylprednisolone nearly two years prior to presentation. He also had a history of hypertension for over one year, with intermittent use of antihypertensive medications, including furosemide, spironolactone, and candesartan. Recently, he was taking furosemide 20 mg and spironolactone 20 mg once daily, with his blood pressure well controlled.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

The patient was hospitalized for further evaluation and continued management. A targeted differential diagnosis was performed based on vomiting‐specific diagnostic criteria and clinical reasoning. During hospitalization, a thorough physical examination was conducted, revealing unremarkable findings. His complete blood count, renal and liver function tests, serum electrolytes, fasting blood glucose, glycosylated hemoglobin, thyroid function, and lipid profile were all within normal limits. Additionally, fecal occult blood and parasite tests were negative. Brain magnetic resonance imaging, electrocardiogram, and gastrointestinal barium meal examinations showed no abnormalities. Chest and abdominal CT scans revealed the presence of liver and renal cysts.

Given his symptoms of reflux and vomiting, the patient was treated with rabeprazole and mosapride for 2 weeks. However, no significant improvement was observed. Notably, the patient had a history of oral methylprednisolone use. Although he had discontinued the medication 2 years prior, we proceeded with an assessment of his cortisol levels and ACTH rhythm. The patient's daily cortisol rhythm showed consistently low cortisol levels, with the absence of the typical circadian peak. In contrast, his plasma ACTH levels displayed a distinct peak between 8:00 a.m. and 9:00 a.m. (Table 1 and Figure 1). This finding suggested a hormonal profile consistent with adrenal insufficiency, specifically secondary adrenal insufficiency due to impaired cortisol production. Considering the patient's history of oral methylprednisolone use and the evidence of adrenocortical hypofunction, a diagnosis of secondary adrenocortical insufficiency was made.

Treatment with 15 mg of hydrocortisone daily was initiated, and the patient showed a favorable response to therapy. He was discharged after 12 days of hospitalization.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

After one month of hydrocortisone treatment, the patient's condition improved significantly and no further symptoms of discomfort occurred. However, a bone density test indicated osteoporosis, with a T‐score of −2.4 in the neck and −0.5 in L1–L4. Six months after discontinuing corticosteroid treatment, the patient remained symptom‐free. On July 2, 2025, relevant endocrine indicators were reassessed. The patient's cortisol and ACTH rhythms were found to be within normal ranges. Specifically, cortisol levels measured at 8:00 a.m., 4:00 p.m., and midnight were 14.6, 7.58, and 4.99 μg/dL, respectively. Corresponding ACTH levels at the same time points were 18.10, 15.7, and 13.8 pg/mL, respectively.

Discussion

Nausea and vomiting are among the most common symptoms prompting patients to seek care in the Gastroenterology department. These symptoms can occur independently or as manifestations of various underlying conditions. The physiological mechanisms underlying vomiting involve reverse peristalsis in the gastrointestinal tract and coordinated contractions of the abdominal muscles, leading to the expulsion of gastric contents [1]. Nausea and vomiting can present acutely or chronically, with chronic cases being particularly prone to misdiagnosis.

A wide range of factors contributes to chronic nausea and vomiting. These include gastrointestinal diseases, reflex vomiting, cerebral vomiting, systemic diseases, exogenous substances, and psychological conditions [2]. The primary diagnostic approach aims to differentiate gastrointestinal causes, such as gastroparesis or cyclic vomiting syndrome, from non‐gastrointestinal causes, such as medication side effects or disorders affecting the vestibular or nervous systems. Once structural, mechanical, and biochemical causes are ruled out, gastrointestinal conditions can be classified into two categories based on gastric emptying: those with delayed gastric emptying and those with normal gastric emptying. It is also crucial to consider non‐gastrointestinal conditions that may contribute to persistent nausea and vomiting [2, 3] (Figure 2). Special attention should be given to medication‐induced vomiting, as evidenced in this case of chronic nausea and vomiting associated with long‐term oral pharmacotherapy.

Functional gastrointestinal disorders, which present with digestive tract symptoms but cannot be explained by organic lesions or abnormal blood biochemical tests, are diagnosed clinically. Emerging evidence suggests that some patients with functional gastrointestinal disorders also exhibit concurrent endocrine dysregulation [4, 5]. In this case, the patient initially presented with chronic vomiting as the primary symptom, accompanied by reflux. A diagnosis of functional digestive disorder was considered and treated accordingly. However, the symptoms did not improve. According to the Rome IV diagnostic criteria of functional gastrointestinal disorders, thyroid hormone and cortisol levels should be assessed to exclude endocrine‐related causes before such a diagnosis is made [6, 7, 8, 9].

Further testing revealed adrenocortical insufficiency, which may have contributed to the persistent vomiting. However, the patient exhibited atypical clinical features of adrenal insufficiency, which led to an initial misdiagnosis. A Norwegian study on autoimmune adrenalitis found that only 19% of patients with adrenal insufficiency exhibited typical symptoms within one to five years before diagnosis [10].

Another important consideration is the patient's medical history. He had been diagnosed with nephrotic syndrome and had received two separate two‐year courses of oral methylprednisolone, the first 20 years ago and the second five years ago. Unfortunately, the patient could not provide exact dosage information. Although he had discontinued the medication three years ago, the prolonged use of oral methylprednisolone remains relevant in considering its potential role in the development of adrenal insufficiency. Research shows that 15%–67% of patients develop adrenal insufficiency within one year of discontinuing methylprednisolone, with approximately 10% of patients experiencing persistent insufficiency for three or more years post‐cessation [11]. Synthetic glucocorticoids suppress Corticotropin‐Releasing Hormone (CRH) and ACTH via a negative feedback mechanism on the HPA axis. This suppression decreases the synthesis of pro‐melanin and leads to a reduction in the volume and number of pituitary ACTH cells, which in turn reduces ACTH secretion. Over time, the adrenal glands atrophy due to the lack of ACTH stimulation, resulting in adrenal insufficiency [12]. Additionally, a study demonstrated that glucocorticoids could reduce methylation levels in the CRH gene promoter region through epigenetic modifications, potentially explaining the prolonged suppression of the HPA axis over several years [13].

Thus, it is important to explore the risk factors for adrenocortical insufficiency after oral glucocorticoid use. Current evidence suggests that higher doses of glucocorticoid therapy, longer treatment duration, and abrupt discontinuation increase the risk of HPA axis suppression [14, 15, 16, 17]. This patient underwent two courses of glucocorticoid therapy, totaling four years, which aligns with a high‐risk population. In addition, Draoui et al. [18] established a cohort of long‐term glucocorticoid users and found that lower BMI and baseline cortisol levels were strongly associated with the onset of adrenocortical insufficiency. Therefore, it is crucial to develop predictive models to assess the risk of adrenocortical hypofunction in patients undergoing glucocorticoid treatment.

In conclusion, this case not only illustrates the diagnostic strategy for chronic vomiting but also emphasizes the importance of standardized management for patients receiving long‐term oral glucocorticoid therapy, including regular cortisol level monitoring. However, due to the limited sample size, the dose–response relationship between cumulative glucocorticoid exposure and adrenal insufficiency remains unclear. Therefore, future studies are needed to establish predictive models for glucocorticoid‐associated adverse effects, which would help in the early identification of high‐risk populations for adrenal insufficiency.
