Intraoperative and postoperative morbidity in transforaminal lumbar endoscopy: Experience of a Brazilian center with 35 consecutive cases
Authors: Reinaldo Rodrigues Pamplona, Vinicius Santos Santos Baptista
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Transforaminal endoscopic lumbar discectomy (TELD) has emerged as a minimally invasive alternative for treating lumbar degenerative conditions. However, real-world data from middle-income countries remain scarce, particularly regarding intraoperative and postoperative morbidity.Methods: This retrospective study included 35 consecutive patients who underwent lumbar endoscopic discectomy exclusively through the transforaminal approach between 2021 and 2024. Data collected included demographic characteristics, operated levels, operative time, intraoperative complications, postoperative complications, and pain scores using the visual analog scale (VAS). Patients were followed for up to 2 years.Results: The mean patient age was 52.9 years, and 65.7% were female. The most commonly operated levels were L4-L5 and L4-L5-S1. Mean operative time was 117.4 min. No intraoperative complications such as dural tears or nerve root injuries were observed. Four patients (11.4%) developed postoperative paresthesia, which resolved spontaneously; no infections, epidural hematomas, or reoperations occurred. VAS scores significantly improved from a mean of 9.02 preoperatively to 3.2 postoperatively (P < 0.001).Conclusion: TELD is a safe and effective minimally invasive technique, associated with low morbidity and significant pain reduction. These results support the feasibility of its application in healthcare systems with limited resources and add valuable data to the global literature on endoscopic spine surgery.

INTRODUCTION

Endoscopic spine surgery has emerged as an effective minimally invasive alternative for the treatment of degenerative lumbar spine disorders. Among the available approaches, the transforaminal technique has gained prominence due to its ability to access the spinal canal with minimal tissue disruption, shorter recovery time, and low complication rates.[9,6,5] In many cases, this technique can be performed under local anesthesia with mild sedation, making it particularly attractive for high-risk surgical patients, such as the elderly or those with significant comorbidities.[5,8]

Multiple international studies have demonstrated the efficacy of this technique in treating lumbar disc herniations and foraminal stenosis, with success rates exceeding 90%, reduced intraoperative bleeding, short hospital stays, and rapid return to daily activities.[9,4,1] However, despite the robust body of evidence, most available data originate from developed countries, characterized by high surgical volumes and healthcare infrastructures that differ significantly from those in middle-income nations such as Brazil.[2]

In this context, it is essential to understand how this technique performs across different clinical settings and healthcare systems, as factors such as population demographics, time to diagnosis, access to treatment, and hospital infrastructure can directly influence surgical outcomes and complication rates.[3] Moreover, there is a scarcity of studies that systematically analyze intra- and immediate postoperative morbidity parameters, particularly in standardized, consecutive case series focused exclusively on the transforaminal approach.

Reports from lower-volume centers with distinct population profiles are not only important for validating the technique in varied contexts but also serve to inform international guidelines, shape surgical training, and reinforce the safety of its global application. Documenting experiences from countries with differing healthcare realities is essential to ensure that the dissemination of lumbar endoscopy occurs in a safe manner adapted to local conditions.

Against this backdrop, the present study aims to report the experience of a Brazilian center with the exclusively transforaminal lumbar endoscopic technique, through a detailed description of the surgical procedure and analysis of intra- and immediate postoperative morbidity parameters in a consecutive series of 35 cases. By providing nationally sourced data using a standardized methodology, this research contributes to the consolidation of the technique as a viable and safe alternative across different healthcare systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

General information

Patient data were retrospectively collected from the database of the Fundação de Neurologia e Neurocirurgia – Instituto do Cérebro, covering the period from May 2021 to November 2024. The database was populated contemporaneously with the procedures as they were performed, serving solely as a secure record of the surgeries and not initially intended for research purposes. All patients who underwent transforaminal endoscopic lumbar discectomy (TELD) for the indication of lumbar disc herniation were included in the study. The only exclusion criterion was age under 18 years. In a preliminary review of the database, the authors identified 35 patients who met these criteria.

The primary outcomes of this study included postoperative complications such as paresthesia (measured using the Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs [LANNS]), sympathetic alterations, epidural hematoma, infection, as well as the need for reoperation and pain improvement (measured by the visual analog scale [VAS]). Lesions were defined as any injury to these structures, whether caused by accidental incision or improper manipulation, resulting in intraoperative or postoperative consequences for the patient.

Secondary outcomes included dural sac injury, nerve root injury, and operative time. Operative time was defined as the interval from initial skin incision to final closure and was estimated based on recorded time intervals. Pain improvement was evaluated based on the VAS scores completed by the patients, comparing preoperative and postoperative assessments.

Additional data, such as number of patients, sex, mean age, body mass index (BMI), year of surgery, history of prior spine surgery, presenting complaint, and operated levels, were also collected. Furthermore, all patients were retrospectively followed for a minimum of 6 months and up to 2 years. Follow-up was conducted through routine postoperative outpatient visits, during which complications and pain progression were assessed. These visits were scheduled according to institutional protocol at the following intervals: 1 week, 1 month, 6 months, 1 year, and 2 years. Although not all patients completed 1- or 2-year follow-up, all had at least 6 months of follow-up. All data, including intraoperative details, were already documented in our database, eliminating the need for review of hospital medical records.

Surgical technique

All procedures were performed using the full-TELD technique[7] which provides posterolateral access to the intervertebral disc through Kambin’s triangle, a safe working zone located beneath to the nerve root exiting the foramen. This method allows treatment of foraminal, extraforaminal, and selected posterolateral disc herniations without the need for facetectomy or fusion.

Patients were placed in the prone position on a Wilson frame or using thoracic and pelvic rolls to induce slight lumbar kyphosis and optimize fluoroscopic visualization. Preoperative axial magnetic resonance imaging was used to determine the trajectory from the disc pathology through the superior articular process (SAP) to the skin surface, helping to estimate the lateral distance of the skin incision from the midline. Intraoperatively, anteroposterior (AP) and lateral fluoroscopic images were obtained to confirm the level and trajectory. Typical entry points ranged from 8 to 12 cm off the midline, depending on the lumbar level and patient body habitus.

Following local infiltration and under fluoroscopic guidance, an 18-gauge spinal needle was inserted toward the ventral SAP. The needle bevel was oriented cranially to avoid injury to the nerve root exiting the foramen. Once Kambin’s triangle was reached and confirmed on AP and lateral views, a K-wire was introduced into the disc, and the needle was withdrawn. Serial dilators were used to expand the soft-tissue corridor, and foraminoplasty was performed when necessary using reamers to partially resect the ventral SAP, ensuring a safe path for instrumentation without exceeding the medial pedicle line.

A beveled working cannula was inserted with the bevel oriented cranially. The endoscope was introduced, and soft-tissue dissection was performed using a bipolar coagulator and blunt dissector. The target anatomical landmarks included the cranial portion of the caudal pedicle and the SAP. For foraminal herniations, the bevel was rotated 180° to protect the exiting nerve root. For central or posterolateral herniations, the ligamentum flavum was partially removed using a Kerrison rongeur to visualize the traversing nerve root. In these cases, the bevel was rotated 90° to allow medial advancement and safe decompression of the lateral recess.

Disc fragments were mobilized with a dissector or bipolar probe and removed using pituitary rongeurs or grasping forceps. After decompression, the neural elements were assessed with a ball-tip probe to ensure free mobilization of the nerve roots. Hemostasis was achieved using bipolar cautery or hemostatic agents. The endoscope and cannula were then removed. Skin closure was performed using a subcuticular stitch, with or without a deep dermal suture at the surgeon’s discretion.

All procedures were performed using a fully endoscopic transforaminal technique, with access through Kambin’s triangle. In cases of minor bleeding after decompression, a hemostatic agent (Sugicel®) was introduced through the endoscopic working channel at the end of the procedure. At the L5/S1 level, transforaminal access was only attempted when preoperative AP X-ray confirmed that the iliac crest was at or below the height of the L5/S1 disc space, ensuring a feasible path through Kambin’s triangle.

No drains were placed. Patients were discharged on the same day or the following morning with short-term analgesic prescriptions. Lumbar bracing was not required. Patients were informed about the possibility of transient postoperative dysesthesia due to dorsal root ganglion irritation, which typically resolves spontaneously.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted to systematically evaluate the collected data and interpret the study outcomes with scientific rigor. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all collected variables, including means, standard deviations, medians, and interquartile ranges for continuous variables, as well as absolute and relative frequencies for categorical variables. To compare pre- and postoperative VAS scores, a paired t-test was used if the data followed a normal distribution, or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test otherwise. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. A significance level of 5% (P < 0.05) was adopted, and all confidence intervals were presented at the 95% confidence level. All analyses were performed using R statistical software (version 4.3.1), ensuring reproducibility and accuracy of the results.

RESULTS

A total of 35 patients were included in this consecutive case series undergoing lumbar endoscopy through the transforaminal approach between 2021 and 2024. The mean age was 52.9 years, ranging from 38 to 72 years, with a median age of 50 years. DISCUSSION

The results of this series of 35 patients who underwent exclusively transforaminal lumbar endoscopy demonstrate that the technique is safe, reproducible, and effective in reducing pain in patients with degenerative lumbar pathology. A statistically significant improvement was observed in VAS scores, with no major intraoperative complications such as nerve root or dural sac injury. These findings are consistent with current literature, which identifies the transforaminal approach as a reliable minimally invasive technique offering excellent symptomatic relief and a low risk of permanent neurological injury.[6,8,9]

The postoperative complication rate in our study was 11.4%, consisting exclusively of transient paresthesia, with no need for reoperation or additional treatment. This rate is comparable to those reported in other series, where dysesthesias and paresthesias may occur in up to 10–15% of cases and are typically self-limiting.[2,1] These manifestations are believed to be related to nerve root manipulation within confined spaces or to postprocedural neural edema.

The absence of epidural hematoma, infection, or need for reintervention reinforces the safety profile of the technique, particularly when performed in centers with growing familiarity and technical experience. Previous studies have emphasized that with proper patient selection and anatomical proficiency, the rate of serious complications remains below 5%.[4,6] The absence of dural injuries in our series is also consistent with reports highlighting the rarity of such events in well-executed transforaminal approaches.[8,5]

The pain reduction observed in our study (mean VAS decrease from 9.02 to 3.2; P < 0.001) aligns with international findings, which often report significant clinical improvement in the immediate postoperative period, sustained in the medium term.[9,4]

The relevance of this study lies not only in confirming the favorable outcomes of the transforaminal approach but also in contributing data from a Brazilian center. Much of the existing literature originates from developed countries, whose demographic, logistical, and economic contexts differ substantially. Our study offers a realistic perspective on the application of this technique in a developing country setting, where factors such as healthcare access, patient profiles, and hospital resources may significantly influence surgical outcomes.[2,3]

In summary, our findings support the conclusion that transforaminal lumbar endoscopy is a safe technique, associated with low morbidity and sustained clinical efficacy, representing a viable alternative even in resource-limited settings. Careful dissemination and progressive training are recommended to expand its safe implementation.

Strengths and limitations

This study presents several notable strengths. First, it comprises a consecutive series of patients, which reduces selection bias and reflects the real-world clinical practice of a Brazilian center. Second, the surgical protocol was standardized, with exclusive use of the transforaminal approach, allowing for a focused analysis of the technique. In addition, the systematic collection of clinical and complication data – both intraoperative and postoperative – provides an objective assessment of the method’s safety. Finally, the inclusion of data from a middle-income country contributes underrepresented information to the literature, enhancing the international validation of the technique across diverse healthcare settings.

On the other hand, the study has limitations that must be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size and lack of a control group limit direct comparisons with other techniques, such as open microdiscectomy. Furthermore, the follow-up rate declined progressively after the 1st year, which may constrain the evaluation of long-term outcomes. Finally, as a retrospective study, it is subject to documentation bias and limited to descriptive analysis.

CONCLUSION

The results of this consecutive case series support the use of TELD as a safe, effective, and reproducible technique for the treatment of degenerative lumbar disc pathology. The significant reduction in pain, low complication rate, and absence of serious intraoperative events reinforce the clinical viability of this minimally invasive approach. Moreover, the findings offer valuable insights from a middle-income country context, contributing to the global understanding and validation of the technique beyond high-resource settings. Despite inherent limitations such as sample size and retrospective design, this study highlights the relevance and feasibility of implementing transforaminal endoscopic procedures in diverse healthcare environments.
