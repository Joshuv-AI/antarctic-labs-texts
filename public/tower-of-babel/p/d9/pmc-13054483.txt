Next step after endoscopic resection for superficial esophageal squamous cell carcinoma: metastatic risk assessment and management of non-curative resection cases
Authors: Ryu Ishihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndoscopic resection (ER) is an established treatment for early stage esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. However, the management of non-curative resections remains a clinical challenge. This review aimed to evaluate the next steps for patients with non-curative resections based on the existing literature. Relevant studies on ER outcomes in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, including recurrence rates, metastatic risks, and treatment strategies, were reviewed. The analysis focused on pathological findings, including tumor invasion depth and lymphovascular involvement (LVI). Comparative data on surgical resection and chemoradiotherapy (CRT) as adjuvant treatments were analyzed. Patients with pathological epithelial or lamina propria mucosa LVI-negative tumors showed low recurrence and metastasis rates (0.4%–5.6%). In contrast, those with submucosal or positive LVI cancer demonstrated a higher risk (10%–30%). Adjuvant treatment with surgery or CRT significantly reduced recurrence; however, the risk of complications must be considered. CRT showed favorable outcomes in low metastatic-risk cases, whereas surgery was preferred in high-risk patients. Active surveillance may be appropriate in certain low-risk patients with a poor overall health status. The management of non-curative ER cases requires individualized decision-making based on pathological risk factors. Further research is needed to refine the risk assessment and potentially utilize circulating tumor DNA testing to enhance precision.Keywords: Endoscopic submucosal dissection, Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, Neoplasm metastasis, Non-curative, Recurrence

INTRODUCTION

Esophageal cancer is the seventh most frequent cancer and the seventh most common cause of cancer-related deaths globally, with an estimated 511,000 new cases and 445,000 deaths in 2022.1 The prognosis of patients with advanced-stage esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) is generally poor. However, early stage ESCC can be cured via endoscopic resection (ER), esophagectomy, or chemoradiotherapy (CRT).2,3

The treatment strategy for early stage ESCC is primarily determined based on the preoperative assessment of the tumor invasion depth. However, the diagnostic accuracy for determining the tumor invasion depth in ESCC with ambiguous indications for ER remains suboptimal, ranging from 72% to 74%.4 Consequently, in current practice, ER is increasingly performed not only for lesions preoperatively judged as suitable for this approach but also for cases where the indication remains uncertain.5 As ER is applied to a broader range of lesions, the number of cases determined to be non-curative based on postoperative pathological findings has increased. Nevertheless, there has been insufficient investigation into the management of non-curative cases. Therefore, based on previous reports, this review examined how non-curative resection cases should be managed as the next step following ER.

ASSESSMENT OF CURABILITY FOLLOWING ER

Following ER for ESCC, a curability assessment is performed based on histopathological findings. This assessment involves evaluating the risk of metastatic recurrence determined by factors such as tumor invasion depth and lymphovascular involvement (LVI), as well as the risk of local recurrence, which is primarily assessed through the status of the resection margin. This section focuses primarily on the risk of metastatic recurrence. The evaluation of local recurrence risk and subsequent management strategies are addressed in a later section.

First, two reports described patients diagnosed with ESCC with invasion limited to the epithelium (pathological [p]EP) or lamina propria mucosa (pLPM), as well as LVI-negative based on pathological findings after ER.2,6 In one report, 280 patients with ESCC diagnosed with pEP/LPM had a metastatic recurrence rate of 0.4%.2 Another report observed 454 patients diagnosed with pEP/LPM LVI-negative cancers, with a 5-year disease-specific survival rate of 99.7%.6 These findings indicate that while the risk of metastatic recurrence or disease-specific mortality in pEP/LPM LVI-negative cancers is not entirely absent, it remains exceptionally low. Consequently, from the perspective of metastatic recurrence, such cases are likely to be curatively treated with ER alone. Therefore, current guidelines do not advocate additional treatment after ER for these cancers.7-11

For LVI-negative cancers invading the muscularis mucosa (pMM), studies evaluating the risk of metastasis using surgically resected specimens have reported metastatic risks ranging from 6.9% to 15.5%.12-15 In contrast, investigations into the risk of metastatic recurrence following ER for LVI-negative pMM cancers have demonstrated a lower frequency, with rates between 1.7% and 5.6%.9,16-18 The slice width of surgical specimens (5–7 mm) differs from that of ER specimens (2–3 mm).19,20 Histopathological assessment with a wider slice width (5–7 mm) of surgical specimens may have the risk of missing a small area of cancer invasion and a small amount of LVI, which can be detected with a thinner slice width (2–3 mm) of ER specimens. This difference may theoretically lead to different diagnoses between surgical specimens and ER specimens. Considering that the results of this investigation are expected to be applicable to decision-making after ER, an investigation using ER specimens is desirable. Given these findings, investigations using ER specimens are considered more relevant, suggesting a metastatic risk of approximately 1.7% to 5.6% based on such analyses. Current guidelines do not explicitly recommend whether additional treatments should be administered to this subset of patients. However, the past outcomes of adjuvant therapy indicate that recurrence occurs at a notable frequency, even with additional treatment (Table 1). Moreover, surgical resection or CRT, which are commonly employed as adjuvant therapies, carry the risk of treatment-related mortality and severe pulmonary complications.3 Therefore, post-ER surveillance without additional treatment may be a more reasonable approach for patients with pMM LVI-negative cancers who exhibit a lower recurrence risk than for those with submucosal (pSM) invasion or LVI-positive cancers.

According to these findings, the metastatic risk for pSM LVI-negative cancers and pMM/SM LVI-positive cancers was estimated to be 10% and 30%, respectively. Consequently, guidelines recommend additional treatment for these cancers to mitigate the metastatic risk.7-9,11

ASSESSMENT OF METASTATIC RISK

Among pSM- and LVI-positive cancers, distinct subgroups with varying metastatic risks exist (Table 2), necessitating appropriate management based on their respective risk profiles. Numerous studies have investigated the factors associated with metastatic risk in T1 ESCC (Table 3).12,22,28-34 These reports have frequently identified tumor invasion depth, LVI, lesion size, and tumor differentiation as risk factors for metastasis. However, while Japanese studies similarly highlighted tumor invasion depth, LVI, and lesion size as significant risk factors, tumor differentiation has not been consistently identified.12,22,29 This discrepancy is likely attributable to differences in the evaluation of tumor differentiation in Japan, where assessments are conducted based on the degree of keratinization,19 in contrast to the criteria outlined in the World Health Organization classification.

Next, as previously mentioned, studies utilizing surgical resection specimens may yield different assessments of tumor invasion depth than those based on ER specimens. Therefore, we introduced a study that employed ER specimens, which may be more applicable in clinical practice.35 In this study, histopathological findings, such as tumor invasion depth and LVI, were comprehensively evaluated using ER specimens. The presence or absence of metastases was determined through post-ER follow-up analysis. The relationship between the histopathological findings and metastases was subsequently examined. This study identified significant predictors of metastasis, including LVI and tumor invasion depth, using multivariate analysis. Conversely, no association was observed between metastasis and lesion size, differentiation, cytological atypia, or the infiltration pattern. Droplet infiltration (≥4) (Fig. 1), an indicator of the infiltration pattern, was identified as an independent and strong predictor of LVI. A review of previous reports suggests that tumor invasion depth and LVI are likely to be closely associated with metastatic risk. Additionally, infiltration patterns may be strongly correlated with LVI and may indirectly contribute to metastasis. However, the findings related to other pathological factors remain controversial.

ADDITIONAL TREATMENT AFTER ER

In daily clinical practice, decisions regarding the need for additional treatment after ER must be made based on the metastatic risk factors outlined thus far. When additional treatment is deemed necessary, an optimal therapeutic approach should be carefully considered. For T1 ESCC with regional lymph node metastases, surgical resection and CRT are recognized as curative treatment options. Previously, surgical resection was the primary modality used as adjuvant therapy to reduce the risk of metastatic recurrence after ER. However, since the 1990s, CRT has been increasingly used as the definitive treatment for ESCC in Japan. Owing to its less invasive nature compared to surgical resection, CRT has gradually been adopted as an adjuvant treatment following ER. In 2005, the JCOG0508 trial was initiated to evaluate the efficacy of CRT as adjuvant therapy following ER.5,36 This trial demonstrated favorable outcomes, with a 5-year survival rate of 89.7% after ER and CRT. Consequently, CRT was incorporated into clinical guidelines as a viable option for adjuvant treatment after ER.7,9

Several studies have compared surgical resection and CRT after ER. A review summarizing these studies suggested that for patients with high-risk cancers, such as pSM LVI-positive cancers, surgical resection may offer a slight advantage over CRT.37-42 However, it has been well documented that patients receiving CRT tend to be older, have a worse overall health status, and have more comorbidities than do those undergoing surgical resection. These factors introduce a significant bias when comparing the two treatment options. Notably, previous comparative studies have not accounted for these variables. To address this, we conducted a study using patient data from 62 Japanese institutions. We selected patients diagnosed with pMM LVI-positive cancers or SM cancers based on post-ER histopathological findings who subsequently underwent either surgical resection or CRT as additional treatment. Propensity score matching was used to compare the efficacies of the two interventions (Table 1).43

This study found no significant differences in the overall survival (OS) or progression-free survival (PFS) rates between surgical resection and CRT in the overall cohort, suggesting comparable efficacy. However, among patients with high-risk pSM LVI-positive cancers, surgical resection demonstrated a modest advantage in terms of OS and PFS (hazard ratios, 1.41 [0.71–2.83] for OS and 1.50 [0.79–2.83] for PFS). Conversely, in patients with lower metastatic risk, such as those with pSM LVI-negative cancers or pMM LVI-positive cancers, CRT showed a slight advantage (hazard ratios, 0.56 [0.27–1.16] for OS and 0.66 [0.34–1.28] for PFS). Although the differences were not statistically significant, the hazard ratios deviated from 1, indicating a potential clinical relevance that could inform treatment decisions. Based on these results, the authors recommended surgical resection for patients with high-risk pSM LVI-positive cancers, while advising CRT for patients with lower-risk pSM LVI-negative cancers or pMM LVI-positive cancers. However, given the retrospective nature of this study, the findings may be subject to bias. Currently, a randomized trials comparing surgical resection with CRT as an adjuvant treatment after ER, Ad-ESD Trial44 and ASSURE Trial, are underway in China and Korea, and their results are anticipated to offer critical insights for determining the optimal post-endoscopic treatment strategy.

ACTIVE SURVEILLANCE AFTER ER

As previously noted, if additional treatment is not performed in cases of non-curative ER, recurrence is expected in 10% to 30% of cases.27 Therefore, additional treatments were recommended. However, certain cases present challenges for further intervention owing to advanced age or compromised overall health status. Even when additional treatment is viable, concerns regarding a potential decline in the quality of life (QOL) may arise. The prognosis of patients managed with observation alone without subsequent therapy remains a pertinent clinical question. Two studies offered valuable insights into this issue.

The first study analyzed 386 cases of superficial ESCC without additional treatment after ER. The patients were stratified into low-(pMM/SM1 LVI-negative), intermediate-(pMM LVI-positive or pSM2 LVI-negative), and high-risk (pSM LVI-positive) groups. The disease-specific survival rates at 5 years were 99.6%, 94.0%, and 79.7%, respectively.18 The second report examined 64 cases of superficial ESCC, classified as pMM or deeper, that were observed without additional treatment. The OS rate did not differ from that of 48 patients who received additional treatment (p=0.686).6 These findings suggest that observation without additional treatment is a promising therapeutic approach. However, as these results were derived from retrospective studies, the treatment outcomes may be subject to various biases. Well-designed prospective studies are necessary to confirm whether observation after ER with treatment initiated at the time of recurrence yields results comparable to those of immediate additional treatment following ER.

RESECTION MARGIN STATUS

During the era when endoscopic mucosal resection was the primary ER method, larger lesions were often removed piecemeal. The number of piecemeal resections has been reported to correlate with the risk of residual or recurrent disease,45 with residual or recurrent lesions observed in approximately half of the cases involving five or more fragments. However, with the widespread adoption of endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD), piecemeal resection has become rare.

Two studies investigated the relationship between horizontal margins and residual or recurrent disease after en bloc resection via ESD. In one study, 70 lesions with circumferential involvement exceeding half of the esophageal circumference were resected as closely as possible to reduce the risk of post-ESD strictures, and the incidence of residual or recurrent disease was evaluated. Of the 70 patients, 27 had positive or indeterminate lateral margins; however, none exhibited residual or recurrent disease. Conversely, another study examined 39 patients with positive or indeterminate lateral margins for various reasons, revealing residual or recurrent disease in 40.0% (6/15) of the patients with positive margins and 13.3% (2/15) of the patients with indeterminate margins. Among the eight patients with residual lesions, six were treated with additional ER, while the remaining two underwent radiotherapy or CRT. None of the patients developed lymph node or distant metastases. These findings suggest that when the horizontal margin is classified as pathological horizontal margin cannot be assessed (pHMX) or pathological horizontal margin positive (pHM1), careful observation is warranted to monitor local recurrence. However, if the lesion is accurately diagnosed and intentionally resected at the margins, the risk of local recurrence in cases classified as pHMX or pHM1 is low.

Regarding the relationship between vertical margins and local residual or recurrent disease, the following report evaluated cases of pMM and pSM cancers.27 In this study, local residual cancer was detected in 16 of the 222 patients (7.2%). Among the cases classified as pathological vertical margin cannot be assessed (pVMX) or pathological vertical margin positive (pVM1), local recurrence was observed in 10 of the 52 cases (19.2%), with 80% of these involving the submucosal or deeper layers. In cases of VM0, local residual cancer was observed in 6 of 170 cases (3.5%), of which 66.7% were mucosal cancers. However, in two cases classified as pathological vertical margin negative (pVM0), the cancer persisted in the submucosal or deeper layers. The rate of local residual disease was 16.7% (2/12) for pVMX and 20.0% (8/40) for pVM1, with no significant intergroup difference. Post-resection histopathological findings indicated that pMM or pSM cancers with pVMX or pVM1 were associated with a high risk of local recurrence, with the majority occurring in the submucosal or deeper layers, warranting additional prophylactic treatment. Conversely, even in cases classified as pVM0, rare instances of submucosal or deeper residual cancer may occur, necessitating careful observation if no additional treatment is administered.

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

Currently, the treatment strategy after ER is determined based on the histopathological findings of ER specimens. However, this assessment does not provide a direct evaluation of the residual cancer cells and has limited accuracy. As a result, unnecessary additional treatments may be administered to patients who do not experience recurrence, whereas those at a genuine risk of recurrence may not receive appropriate therapeutic intervention, leading to poor outcomes. To improve the accuracy of predicting the risk of metastatic recurrence and ensure that only patients who truly need additional treatment receive it, a direct examination of residual cancer cells is necessary in patients who have undergone ER. Among the available methods for confirming the presence or absence of cancer cells, circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) testing, which has been clinically applied to other cancers, is considered the most promising.46,47 For example, ctDNA has been reported to predict the need for adjuvant chemotherapy after surgical resection of colorectal cancer.48 If a similar approach could be applied to ESCC, additional selective treatments could be performed.

Furthermore, in terms of treatment, studies have demonstrated that DCF therapy (docetaxel, cisplatin, and 5-fluorouracil)49 and immune checkpoint inhibitors50 can significantly improve treatment outcomes. Implementation of more intensive therapies may enhance the likelihood of achieving curative outcomes, even in cases where metastatic recurrence is identified after ER. In the future, there may be a shift toward rigorous surveillance for metastatic recurrence without additional treatment immediately after ER. In this model, curative treatment is initiated only upon detection of recurrence using advanced technology, thereby minimizing overtreatment while ensuring aggressive intervention when warranted.

CONCLUSIONS

The optimal treatment strategy for patients after ER remains an area of ongoing research. Presently, the treatment approach requires precise histopathological evaluation of resected specimens, with risk assessment for metastases predicated on factors such as tumor invasion depth and vascular involvement. However, this approach is not optimal. Future advancements should focus on developing techniques to directly assess the presence of residual cancer cells after ER. By reserving intensive therapeutic interventions for patients with confirmed residual malignancy, treatment efficacy and QOL can be substantially enhanced.
