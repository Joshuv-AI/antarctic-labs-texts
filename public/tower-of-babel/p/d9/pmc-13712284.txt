Lysosomal membrane damage repair and acidification impairment restoration triggered by smart lipid nanoparticles for Alzheimer's disease treatment
Authors: Jiaqing Yin, Wei Lv, Wei Zhao, Zhenwei Ding, Fei Zhu, Hongli Liu, Shengnan Li, Xiang Cao, Linfeng Liu, Kefan Song, Yue Gong, Jiangna Xu, Yuqin Chen, Zhiyuan Zhang, Xiangming Fang, Hongliang Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Alzheimer's disease (AD), characterized by amyloid (Aβ) deposition in neurons, is the most serious neurodegenerative disorder, but lacks effective therapeutic drugs in the clinic. Impaired neuronal lysosomal function drives the pathological accumulation of intracellular Aβ. Targeted rehabilitation of lysosomal dysfunction may offer a potential strategy to restore neuronal homeostasis and mitigate AD progression. In this study, we developed a brain-targeted smart lipid nanoparticle (A-PSL@7BIO) to repair lysosomal membrane damage and restore impaired acidification in the brain for AD therapy. The system was composed of Angiopep-2 peptide-conjugated lipid nanoparticles, which were loaded with a Glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK-3β) inhibitor 7-bromoindirubin-3ʹ-oxime (7BIO) and incorporated with dipalmitoyl-phosphatidylserine (DPPS) and a reactive oxygen species (ROS) responsive phospholipid. Through Angiopep-2-mediated blood–brain barrier (BBB) transcytosis, the smart lipid nanoparticles could enhance brain biodistribution with subsequent neuronal internalization. Pathologically elevated intracellular ROS levels in AD could induce stimulus-responsive payload release, wherein 7BIO selectively inhibited aberrant GSK-3β hyperphosphorylation to restore the physiological acidic lysosomal environment and DPPS structurally repaired compromised lysosomal membranes, synergistically addressing dual aspects of lysosomal pathology. This smart lipid nanoparticles synergistically restored physiological lysosomal homeostasis, reversed neuronal dysfunction, and ameliorated cognitive deficits in AD models, indicating that A-PSL@7BIO could be used as a potential nanomedicine for AD therapy.

Introduction

AD, a neurodegenerative disorder, is characterized by dementia symptoms such as memory loss, aphasia, and executive dysfunction. Currently, over 50 million people worldwide are affected by AD, with projections suggesting that this number will rise to 139 million by 20501,2. In recent years, drugs and therapeutic strategies for the treatment of AD have been continually reported and developed. The introduction of lecanemab and other antibodies has garnered significant attention3,4, while the investigation and application of novel strategies such as proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) offer a promising new direction for the efficient clearance of pathogenic proteins5,6. Recently, mounting evidence has demonstrated that lysosomal dysfunction in neurons is a critical factor in AD pathogenesis7. Characterized by their elaborate dendritic and axonal cytoplasmic extensions, neurons exhibit a profound reliance on the lysosomal‒autophagic system8. As essential intracellular organelles enriched with diverse hydrolases, lysosomes are responsible for degrading key biological macromolecules, including proteins, nucleic acids, and polysaccharides. By executing these catabolic activities, lysosomes orchestrate fundamental physiological processes, including autophagy, antigen presentation, and cellular component recycling, to establish intracellular homeostasis9,10. Lysosomal dysfunction, triggered by disruption of membrane integrity or impairment of the acidic intraluminal environment, plays a critical role in the pathogenesis of cellular senescence and associated age-related pathologies11,12. At present, extensive researches have focused on repairing damaged lysosomes, including enzyme replacement therapy with recombinant enzyme drugs such as Lanzyme and pharmacological chaperones like NB-DNJ for exogenous supplementation of deficient enzymes and promotion of their proper folding13,14. Direct repair of damaged lysosomes via the ESCRT III complex pathway (activating cellular autophagy) and clearance of abnormal components through PROTACs-mediated targeted protein degradation also restore intracellular homeostasis6,15, 16, 17. It has been demonstrated that diminished v-ATPase activity in neurons impairs lysosomal acidification, thereby inhibiting the degradation of toxic Aβ and amyloid precursor protein (APP), which contributes to the pathogenesis of AD18. Compromised lysosomal degradation further exacerbates the accumulation of amyloid precursor protein β-carboxy-terminal fragments (APP-βCTFs) and Aβ due to the continuous delivery of damaged proteins by autophagosomes to dysfunctional lysosomes. Elevated lysosomal membrane permeability (LMP) triggers the cytoplasmic leakage of hydrolytic enzymes, disrupting cellular homeostasis. This cascade culminates in neuronal apoptosis, the release of intracellular components, and the extracellular deposition of Aβ plaques, collectively driving AD pathology progression19,20.

GSK-3, a multifunctional serine/threonine kinase comprising α and β isoforms, is ubiquitously expressed in mammalian cells21. Its hyperactivation is strongly implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of AD. Specifically, hyperphosphorylated GSK-3β reduces lysosomal v-ATPase acetylation, a posttranslational modification essential for its proton-pumping activity22. This disrupts H+ transfer into lysosomes, altering their acidic environment and causing lysosomal acidification failure23. Pharmacological inhibition of GSK-3β restores v-ATPase-dependent proton transport on lysosomal membranes24. Notably, 7BIO, a selective GSK-3β inhibitor, rescued lysosomal acidification by targeting its hyperphosphorylated state and mitigated Aβ-induced neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction through the suppression of GSK-3β, thereby conferring neuroprotective effects25,26. However, strategies for the targeted delivery of 7BIO into the AD brain require further study.

However, restoring lysosomal acidity requires concomitant preservation of lysosomal membrane integrity. Otherwise, proton leakage through damaged membranes negates the benefits of v-ATPase-driven acidification, thereby obstructing functional recovery27, 28, 29. Under stress conditions, lysosomal membranes sustain damage during physiological turnover, disrupting cellular proteostasis. Healthy cells counteract this by activating the phosphoinositide-initiated membrane tethering and lipid transport (PITT) membrane repair pathway. Lysosomal membrane damage induces Ca2+ efflux, which recruits phosphatidylinositol 4-kinase type 2A (PI4K2A). Mechanistically, PI4K2A generates phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate (PI4P) on lysosomal surfaces, facilitating the recruitment of oxysterol-binding protein (ORP) family members and establishing endoplasmic reticulum (ER)–lysosome contact sites. Through these contact sites, phosphatidylserine (PS) is transported from the ER to the lysosomal membrane via the lipid transfer channel established by activated ORP9/10/11. It acts on the carboxyl terminus of autophagy-related 2A (ATG2A-ΔCT) to regulate autophagy-related 2A (ATG2A) membrane binding, thereby promoting lysosomal membrane repair. Therefore, as a key downstream effector molecule of the PITT pathway, PS contributes to the structural and functional restoration of the lysosomal membrane29. Under conditions of lysosomal membrane self-repair mechanism dysfunction in AD, the transport of exogenous PS to the lysosomal membrane may trigger a highly efficacious program to repair damaged membranes and reconstitute their complete structure. Furthermore, PS can act as a pivotal scaffold material for liposomal drug delivery systems. However, PS-targeted delivery strategies for lysosomal repair have not been reported yet, which constitutes a major critical gap in this research field.

BBB acts as a critical protective interface, selectively blocking the entry of neurotoxic plasma components, circulating blood cells, and pathogens into the central nervous system (CNS)30. However, the tight endothelial junctions of the BBB create significant challenges for conventional drug delivery, which results in limited trans-BBB permeability. Angiopep-2 (ANG2), a 19-amino acid peptide derived from the Kunitz domain of aprotinin, serves as a high-affinity ligand for low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP-1) expressed on brain capillary endothelial cells31. This molecular interaction enables ANG2-functionalized drug delivery systems to hijack the endogenous LRP-1-mediated transcytosis pathway, thereby facilitating BBB penetration. Compared with nontargeted systems, ANG2-conjugated drug delivery systems enhance the accumulation of payloads in the brain 3- to 5-fold32,33.

In complex AD pathology, excessive ROS critically impair neuronal function by disrupting synaptic activity, impairing neurotransmission, and exacerbating neuroinflammation34,35. Thioketal (TK)-based ROS-responsive drug delivery systems undergo selective cleavage upon pathological ROS stimulation. This redox stimulus-responsive release profile enables spatiotemporal control of drug delivery at targeted loci36.

Given the key mechanisms of lysosomal membrane damage and acidification impairment in AD pathogenesis, we developed a smart liposomal system (A-PSL@7BIO) loaded with 7BIO and inserted with DPPS for targeted repair of dysfunctional lysosomes. As shown in Scheme 1, the system uses surface-attached ANG2 to target LRP-1 on cerebral capillary endothelial cells, facilitating BBB transmigration into the brain. Upon entering AD neurons, ROS stimulation alters liposome curvature, inducing localized membrane destabilization and drug release. The combination of 7BIO and DPPS is critical for efficient lysosomal repair. Specifically, released 7BIO inhibits GSK-3β overactivation, enhancing v-ATPase proton-pump activity and restoring lysosomal acidification; however, unrepaired damaged lysosomal membranes may cause H+ leakage from v-ATPase, impairing maintenance of the lysosomal acidic microenvironment. Concurrently, PS released from DPPS activates ATG2-mediated lipid transfer pathways to reconstruct damaged lysosomal membranes by reducing permeability. This integrated strategy forms a sequential therapeutic cascade: restored lysosomal proton gradients and membrane integrity reactivate proteolytic enzymes, enhancing degradation of pathogenic Aβ and APP/βCTF aggregates and ultimately preserving neuronal viability by reducing cytotoxic Aβ plaque accumulation. Collectively, the rational design of A-PSL@7BIO, integrating targeted delivery and 7BIO-DPPS synergy, enables effective lysosomal repair in AD, offering a promising strategy for addressing lysosomal dysfunction in AD pathogenesis.Scheme 1Schematic illustration of A-PSL@7BIO construction and diagram of its anti-AD effect.

Methods

Synthesis of DSPE-PEG2000-ANG2

ANG2 (20 mg) and Maleimide-PEG2000-DSPE (8 mg, 3/1 M ratio) were dissolved in 1 mL of PBS and 1 mL of DMF respectively, then added to 8 mL PBS. The reaction proceeded under continuous nitrogen purging (24 h, inert atmosphere). DSPE-PEG2000-ANG2 conjugate was purified via dialysis (MWCO 3.5 kDa cutoff) followed by lyophilization. Structural confirmation was characterized through 1H NMR and FT-IR spectroscopy.

Preparation and characterization of A-PSL@7BIO

Liposomal A-PSL@7BIO was prepared by the thin film dispersion method. Briefly, 8 mg Hydrogenated Soybean Phosphotidylcholine (HSPC), 1 mg DSPE-PEG2000-ANG2, 3 mg DSPE-TK-PEG2000, 1 mg DPPS, 2 mg cholesterol (Chol) were dissolved with 15 mL of methanol–chloroform (1/6, v/v). Dissolve 1 mg of 7BIO in 1 mL of methanol–chloroform (1/6, v/v) to obtain a 1 mg/mL 7BIO/methanol–chloroform stock solution for further use. The prepared lipid solution and the drug stock solution were mixed at a 40/1 (w/w) ratio and formed a uniform film at 45 °C using a rotary evaporator. The film was then ultrasonically dissolved in 5 mL of ultrapure water preheated to 45 °C for 10 min. After being hydrated by water-bath heating at 55 °C for 2 h, the A-PSL@7BIO liposomes were obtained through repeated extrusion by a liposome extruder with 800, 400, and 200 nm, respectively. The excess drugs were removed by centrifugation. Various fluorescent-labeled A-PSL@7BIO were assembled using the same method by adding various fluorescent probes (Coumarin 6, Cy7) to the solvent.

The loading capacity (LC) and encapsulation efficiency (EE) were detected by Ultraviolet (UV) and visible spectra. The ζ-potential and particle size of liposomes were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS), and the morphology of liposomes was observed with transmission electron microscopy (TEM) after uranium acetate negative staining.

Storage and serum stability of A-PSL@7BIO

A-PSL@7BIO was suspended in PBS alone or PBS containing 10% FBS, and stored at 4 °C. The colloidal stability was evaluated through monitoring of hydrodynamic diameter distribution via DLS measurements at standardized intervals over a 7-day storage period.

In vitro release of A-PSL@7BIO

The in vitro release profiles of A-PSL@7BIO liposomes were investigated by dialysis method using PBS (pH 7.4) and 1 mmol/L hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) containing PBS (pH 7.4) as release medium at 37 °C, respectively. Aliquots (1 mL) were collected from the release medium at predetermined intervals (0–48 h), and the medium was replenished with equivalent fresh medium to maintain sink conditions. 7BIO concentration was determined through UV–Vis spectrophotometric detection (λ = 500 nm) using a validated calibration curve (R2 > 0.99).

In vitro cellular cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxic profile of A-PSL@7BIO was evaluated using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. SH-SY5Y cells were plated in 96-well microplates at a density of 5000 cells/well in 200 μL complete medium and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cells were subsequently exposed to escalating concentrations of A-PSL@7BIO (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20 μg/mL) for 24 h. Then, 100 μL 10% CCK-8 reagent (v/v) was supplemented into the culture medium for an additional 2 h incubation. Cellular viability was quantified by the absorbance detected with a microplate reader at 475 nm.

In vitro cellular uptake assay

The bEnd.3 cells (1 × 104 cells) with logarithmic growth were inoculated onto slide of 12-well plates. Cellular systems were then exposed to either Coumarin 6 labeled A-PSL@7BIO or PSL@7BIO for 4 h. After triple PBS rinsing to remove free nanoparticles, nuclei were stained with DAPI and imaged by fluorescence microscope.

In vitro neuroprotective effects of A-PSL@7BIO

The SH-SY5Y cell viability was measured with the CCK-8 assay. SH-SY5Y cells were implanted into 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells/well) and cultured for 24 h. Then, 5 μmol/L Aβ1–42 was added to stimulate the cells. Meanwhile, A-PSL@7BIO at 2, 4, 8, 12, 16 and 20 μg/mL was added for 24 h incubation. And then, 100 μL 10% CCK-8 was added to the cells. The absorbance was detected with a microplate reader at 475 nm after incubation for another 2 h.

Live/dead assay

Live/Dead Assay further tested the viability of SH-SY5Y cells upon Aβ1–42 stimulation. SH-SY5Y neuronal cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. Then, 5 μmol/L Aβ1–42 was added to stimulate the cells. Meanwhile, A-PSL@7BIO at 4, 8 and 20 μg/mL was added for 24 h incubation. After PBS washing, fluorescence imaging was performed using an inverted epifluorescence microscope.

In vitro transwell penetration assay

The trans-endothelial transport capacity of A-PSL@7BIO was quantitatively analyzed using a transwell model simulating the in vitro blood–brain barrier. Briefly, the bEnd.3 cells with a density of 5 × 104/well were planted into the 24-well transwell upper chamber with a pore diameter of 1 μm and cultured for 2 weeks. Then, the medium was replaced every two days. After the transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) of cell monolayers was higher than 200 Ω‧cm2, 200 μL of DMEM medium containing Coumarin 6-labeled liposomes was introduced into the upper chamber of the Transwell system, while 1000 μL of fresh DMEM medium was added to the lower chamber. The fluorescence intensity of the lower chamber sample was measured by a Multiskan Mk3 microplate reader (Ex: 450 nm, Em: 500 nm).

Investigation of lysosome function

SH-SY5Y cells were inoculated into 12-well plates with cell climbing slices and cultured overnight. The cells were transfected with mCherry-eGFP-LC3 adenoviral reporter constructs at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 4, followed by 24 h co-incubation. Viral particles were subsequently removed through triple PBS washes, with transduction efficiency verified via fluorescence microscopy prior to experimental procedures. After pretreatment with 5 μmol/L Aβ1‒42 for 24 h, SH-SY5Y cells were incubated with A-PSL and A-PSL@7BIO, respectively. The slices were rinsed with PBS, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and then co-incubated with anti-CTSD antibody (Abcam, 1/300, v/v), followed by the fluorescence labeling with Goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L DyLight 405 (Abcam, 1/200 v/v). The slices were observed under a fluorescence microscope for the qualitative evaluation of lysosomal pH.

For the quantitative measurement of lysosomal pH, SH-SY5Y cells were incubated with 1 mg/mL Lysosensor Yellow/Blue dextran for 24 h at 37 °C after a 24 h pre-incubation with 5 μmol/L Aβ1‒42 and various formulations. The cells were washed three times with PBS, trypsinized, and transferred into a black 96-well microplate. The standard curve was obtained according to the protocol established previously. In brief, cells were treated with 1% penicillin–streptomycin in MES buffer (5 mmol/L NaCl, 115 mmol/L KCl, 1.3 mmol/L MgSO4, 25 mmol/L Mes), with the pH adjusted to a range from 3.5 to 5.5. The emission at 440 and 535 nm with excitation at 355 nm was read by the Multiskan Mk3 microplate reader. The ratio of emission, 440/535, was calculated and substituted into the standard curve for pH value determination37.

SH-SY5Y cells were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. Then, they were incubated for 24 h with 5 μmol/L of Aβ1–42 and different formulations. SH-SY5Y cells were co-incubated with DMEM/F12 serum-free medium containing the Lysotracker-Red fluorescent probe (1/20,000, v/v) for 30 min. Subsequently, the 12-well plate was placed under a fluorescence microscope to observe the number and fluorescence intensity of the lysosomes within the cells.

Lysosomal integrity dynamics were monitored in SH-SY5Y cells using a standardized fluorescence recovery protocol. Cells cultured in 24-well plates were co-incubated with LysoTracker Red (1/20,000, v/v, in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium) for 30 min incubation. Following initial fluorescence imaging, cellular systems were exposed to 1 mmol/L LLOME for 5 min to induce lysosomal membrane permeabilization. Then, triple PBS rinsing was performed prior to reintroduction of various formulations containing LysoTracker Red in fresh serum-free medium. Time-lapse imaging was conducted at defined intervals: baseline (pre-treatment), post-LLOME exposure (5 min), and at 1, 5 and 10 min post-intervention.

In vivo biodistribution of A-PSL@7BIO

Cy7-labeled A-PSL@7BIO or PSL@7BIO was intravenously injected into 6-month-old APP/PS1-21 mice using PBS as a negative control. The in vivo imaging system (IVIS) was utilized to detect the fluorescence signal in the brain of the mice at 4 and 8 h after injection. The mice were sacrificed 8 h after injection, and the main organs including brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were harvested and observed with the imaging system IVIS. The semiquantitative analysis of the region of interest (ROI) analysis was also measured by IVIS. All animal experiments were in accordance with guidelines evaluated and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Nanjing Medical University (approval number: 2406047)

Open field test (OFT) experiment

The ANY-maze video-tracking system was used to sequentially analyze and record all the behavioral tests. In the OFT, there was a square cavity (50 cm × 50 cm × 50 cm), where the central square occupied half of the total area and was defined as the inner square, while the remaining area was defined as the outer square. Each mouse was placed individually in the chamber and allowed to move freely for 10 min. The distances traveled and the time spent in both the inner and outer squares were recorded.

Morris water maze (MWM) experiment

The cognitive ability of APP/PS1-21 mice was evaluated by the MWM on Day 17 after treatment. The MWM consisted of two parts, spatial navigation task and probe trial test. In the spatial navigation task, mice received a consecutive 5-day training for submerged platform seeking. Each mouse received three-time repeated training according to the randomly-determined training quadrant sequence every day. If the mice successfully found the platform in 60 s with a 5 s stay, the searching time was recorded as escape latency; otherwise, the cut-off time, 60 s, was taken as escape latency, and the mice were manually led for a 10 s dwell on the platform as supplementary training. After the spatial navigation task, the platform was removed, and the probe trial test was conducted. The mice were allowed a 60 s free swim, which started in the farthest quadrant from the original platform location. The swimming trajectories were tracked and analyzed by the ANY maze video-track system.

Nissl staining

The paraffin-embedded tissue sections underwent initial processing through sequential deparaffinization and rehydration procedures. Subsequently, the specimens were subjected to the Nissl staining protocol implementation. Following chromatic visualization, the sections were systematically dehydrated using graded ethanol solutions and chemically cleared through xylene immersion. Final preparation for microscopic analysis was achieved by permanent mounting with neutral balsam under standardized coverslipping conditions.

Western blotting assay

Cell lysate or brain tissue homogenate was harvested as described above. Totally 30 μg of proteins was separated by 5%−12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with BSA (5%, m/v) and then incubated with anti-Amyloid1–40 (1/1000, v/v), anti-CTSD (1/1000, v/v), anti-Zonula occludens-1 (1/1000, v/v), anti-Claudin 5 (1/1000), anti-pTBK1 (1/1000, v/v), anti-pIRF3 (1/1000, v/v), anti-STING (1/1000, v/v), anti-cGAS (1/1000, v/v), and anti-GAPDH (1/5000, v/v) at 4 °C overnight, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. The bands were soaked with ECL luminescent solution and exposed in the Fully Automated Chemiluminescence Image Analysis System (4600 Series, Tanon). The band optical density was semi-quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ.

Immunohistochemistry analysis

The brain tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, dehydrated in an ascending series of ethanol, cleared in xylene, and then embedded in paraffin. The paraffin-embedded brain tissues were sectioned into 8 μm coronal slices. The immunohistochemical staining was performed on the routine method. Briefly, after xylene dewaxing, gradient ethanol hydration, antigen retrieval with citrate buffer, and blocking with QuickBloc blocking buffer, the sections were further incubated with primary antibody, including anti-β-Amyloid1‒42 antibody or anti-Phospho-tau antibody, at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated Goat anti-mouse IgG at 37 °C for 1 h. After being stained with DAB reagent and counterstained with hematoxylin solution, the sections were dehydrated by ethanol gradients, cleared in xylene, and coverslipped with neutral resin. And then, the sections were observed by a microscope (DMi8, Leica, Germany), and the percentage area of amyloid plaque deposit was quantitatively analyzed by ImageJ software.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis of differences between the experimental groups was performed using multiple Student's t-tests and one-way ANOVA. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05 (Data are presented as mean ± SD; ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001 vs. indicated).

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of A-PSL@7BIO

To enhance the ability of the 7BIO and DPPS codelivery system to the AD brain, we modified the 7BIO-loaded and DPPS-containing liposomes (PSL@7BIO) with the brain-targeted peptide ANG2. First, through the Michael addition reaction between the maleimide group on maleimide-PEG2000-DSPE and the sulfhydryl group on ANG2, the brain-targeting functional material DSPE-PEG2000-ANG2 was synthesized and characterized by FT-IR (Supporting Information Fig. S1A) and 1H NMR (Fig. S1B). These results indicated that DSPE-PEG2000-ANG2 was successfully synthesized. And then, A-PSL@7BIO was developed through adding the DSPE-PEG2000-ANG2 polymer during the preparation by film-dispersion extrusion technique using optimized lipid-to-drug ratios. These results indicated that DSPE-PEG2000-ANG2 was successfully synthesized. A-PSL@7BIO was subsequently developed through the addition of the DSPE-PEG2000-ANG2 polymer during preparation via the film-dispersion extrusion technique with optimized lipid-to-drug ratios.

TEM revealed that PSL@7BIO and A-PSL@7BIO were spherical unilamellar liposomes (Fig. 1A), whereas DLS revealed an average hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 180 nm in aqueous solution (Fig. 1B). These structural features increase their potential for drug delivery applications. In terms of surface charge properties, A-PSL@7BIO exhibited a greater ζ-potential than did PSL@7BIO (Fig. 1C), which may be attributed to the fact that a certain amount of charge was shielded upon modification with the ANG2 peptide. Quantitative analysis of 7BIO within the A-PSL@7BIO formulation, along with determination of its EE and drug-LC, was performed via UV spectrophotometry. The results demonstrated that A-PSL@7BIO achieved an EE of approximately 70.16% and an LC of 1.71%, indicating the efficient encapsulation of 7BIO within the lipid-based nanoparticle system (Supporting Information Table S1). In addition, stability analysis of A-PSL@7BIO demonstrated that this formulation exhibited favorable stability within the study time range (Supporting Information Fig. S2).

The in vitro release profile of A-PSL@7BIO was investigated by dialysis. When incubated in a phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) solution at pH 7.4 containing 1 mmol/L H2O2, A-PSL@7BIO exhibited an efficient release of approximately 85.49% of the encapsulated 7BIO over 12 h. In sharp contrast, when incubated in a PBS solution without H2O2, the release rate of 7BIO from A-PSL@7BIO was markedly lower, reaching only approximately 49.42% (Fig. 1D). These results provide compelling evidence for the pronounced ROS-responsive drug release property of A-PSL@7BIO.

In vitro neuroprotective efficacy and BBB permeability of A-PSL@7BIO

To evaluate the neuronal uptake efficiency of A-PSL@7BIO and assess its cytocompatibility in a cellular model, cellular uptake and cytotoxicity assays were conducted in SH-SY5Y cells. The results revealed that A-PSL@7BIO could be efficiently internalized by neurons (Supporting Information Fig. S3). Simultaneously, the results of the CCK-8 cytotoxicity assay demonstrated the excellent in vitro biosafety profile of A-PSL@7BIO, with no significant cytotoxicity observed at concentrations ranging from 1 to 20 μg/mL (Supporting Information Fig. S4). These findings confirmed the favorable biocompatibility of the formulation for neuronal applications.

To investigate the neuroprotective efficacy of A-PSL@7BIO on neurons, Live/dead cell viability and CCK-8 assays were performed using SH-SY5Y cells stimulated with Aβ. The cells exhibited increased propidium iodide (PI) red fluorescence, reflecting increased dead cell populations, and decreased calcein-AM green fluorescence, indicative of viable cell loss. Treatment with A-PSL@7BIO effectively attenuated the PI fluorescence intensity, resulting in a reduction in the number of dead cells, whereas higher drug concentrations further diminished the red fluorescence signals (Fig. 1E and F). The reciprocal modulation of PI signal attenuation with calcein-AM fluorescence restoration across treatment groups provided functional validation of A-PSL@7BIO's neuroprotective efficacy. Moreover, A-PSL@7BIO demonstrated robust neuroprotective effects, promoting SH-SY5Y cell survival, as validated by CCK-8 absorbance quantification. At 4 μg/mL, SH-SY5Y cells in the A-PSL@7BIO group were similar to those in the control group, which demonstrated the strong neuroprotective effect of A-PSL@7BIO against Aβ-induced damage (Fig. 1G).

The ability of A-PSL@7BIO to penetrate the BBB was quantitatively assessed in a bEnd.3 cell monolayer model. Compared with PSL@7BIO without ANG2 modification, A-PSL@7BIO exhibited significantly increased permeability across the cell monolayers at 1, 2 and 4 h post incubation. These results highlighted the essential role of ANG2 in facilitating the transport of A-PSL@7BIO across the in vitro cell monolayer (Fig. 1H).

In vitro lysosomal repair ability of A-PSL@7BIO

To authenticate the in vitro efficacy of A-PSL@7BIO in rectifying lysosomal acidification impairment, Ad-mCherry-GFP-LC3B adenoviral fluorescent probes combined with anti-CTSD antibodies were used for co-labeling. The Ad-mCherry-GFP-LC3B adenoviral fluorescent probe labeled LC3B, a key marker of the autophagy pathway, to visualize autophagosomes. Upon cell infection, the co-expression of mCherry and GFP renders autophagosomes yellow. In normal cells, autophagosome‒lysosome fusion triggers GFP quenching by lysosomal acidity, shifting fluorescence to red; in AD, impaired lysosomal acidification maintains yellow fluorescence. To identify lysosomes, CTSD, a lysosomal marker, is used to stain lysosomes with blue fluorescence. Colocalization of Ad-mCherry-GFP-LC3B and anti-CTSD antibodies labels autolysosomes: normal autolysosomes display purple fluorescence, whereas those with AD-associated acidification defects exhibit white fluorescence, enabling differentiation based on fluorescence color40, 41, 42, 43.

In the pathological milieu of AD, neuronal lysosomes exhibit acidification impairment. The elevated pH of the acidic internal environment impairs the normal activation and function of lysosomal hydrolases. Previous studies have demonstrated that inhibitors of GSK-3β can effectively restore the pH of the lysosomal acidic microenvironment24. In the present study, an Aβ-induced neuronal cell model was established. Co-localization analysis of lysosomes in neurons was performed using the adenoviral probe Ad-mCherry-eGFP-LC3B and the lysosomal marker CTSD. Changes in pH were monitored via alterations in fluorescence signals (Fig. 2A) to evaluate lysosomal damage in neurons under AD pathological conditions20. SH-SY5Y cells co-incubated with Aβ displayed dysregulated lysosomal acidification; in contrast, the empty vector A-PSL exerted a negligible effect on ameliorating such acidification disorders. By sharp contrast, A-PSL@7BIO significantly alleviated lysosomal acidification in neurons and restored their physiological homeostasis (Fig. 2B). Quantitative analysis further demonstrated that after coincubation with Aβ, the intra-lysosomal pH of SH-SY5Y cells increased to approximately 5.0, whereas A-PSL treatment failed to significantly counteract this pH increase. Conversely, A-PSL@7BIO effectively reduced the intra-lysosomal pH in neurons, thereby alleviating lysosomal acidification defects and restoring it to normophysiological levels (Fig. 2C). Additionally, Aβ-induced SH-SY5Y cells were used to evaluate the alleviation of lysosomal membrane impairment in the pathological milieu of AD. In SH-SY5Y cells co-incubated with Aβ, a notable reduction in both the fluorescence intensity of lysosomes and the number of intracellular lysosomes was detected. Treatment with Lipo@7BIO lacking DPPS failed to reverse this decrease in fluorescence. In contrast, treatment with A-PSL@7BIO resulted in a significant recovery of lysosomal-associated fluorescence intensity in SH-SY5Y cells, accompanied by an increase in lysosomal numbers (Fig. 2D and E). Additionally, the lysosomal membrane-disrupting agent LLOMe was utilized to further assess the prompt membrane damage-repair proficiency of A-PSL@7BIO. LLOMe was able to induce changes in the membrane permeability of SH-SY5Y cell lysosomes, and the fluorescence did not recover spontaneously within 10 min. Administration of Lipo@7BIO without DPPS also did not accelerate the recovery of lysosomal fluorescence (Fig. 2F, Supporting Information Fig. S5). In contrast, the A-PSL@7BIO group demonstrated rapid lysosomal membrane repair ability within 5 min, providing further evidence that A-PSL@7BIO could restore lysosomal integrity by quickly fixing lysosomal membrane damage.

Mechanism study of A-PSL@7BIO

Protein expression associated with the repair of lysosomal membrane damage and acidification dysfunction was assessed via Western blotting in Aβ-stimulated SH-SY5Y cells. In normal cells, such damage triggers subsequent upregulation of intracellular ATG2, thereby activating the lysosomal self-repair mechanism. However, the lysosomal self-repair function triggered by the cell-autonomous upregulation of ATG2 fails in the pathological conditions of AD. Therefore, actively upregulating ATG2 through exogenous PS to promote the initiation of this repair pathway is a critical strategy29. In this study, exogenously supplemented DPPS was found to rapidly repair lysosomal membrane damage induced by the lysosomotropic pore-forming agent LLOMe and restore lysosomal function to a certain extent (Fig. 2G, Supporting Information Figs. S6–S8). Therefore, we investigated whether the DPPS-containing nanocarrier A-PSL@7BIO upregulated the protein expression of ATG2. The results revealed obvious upregulation of ATG2 in the A-PSL@7BIO group compared with that in the DPPS-free formulation (Aβ and 7BIO) group and were comparable with those in the PS and PSL@7BIO groups (Fig. 2G and H).

Moreover, in AD pathological conditions, where neuronal lysosomes exhibit acidification dysfunction, increasing v-ATPase activity in lysosomes via the inhibition of abnormally phosphorylated GSK-3β has been shown to effectively restore their acidic microenvironment18,20,44. We therefore investigated whether A-PSL@7BIO could suppress GSK-3β expression. It was shown that it potently inhibited the abnormal phosphorylation of GSK-3β under AD pathological conditions, thereby increasing v-ATPase activity and restoring normal metabolic processing of lysosomal pro-cathepsin D (CatD) to its active mature form mCatD form within lysosomes (Fig. 2G and H), which indicated a significant restoration of the acidic milieu in lysosomes18. These collective findings demonstrated that A-PSL@7BIO restored lysosomal homeostasis by regulating GSK-3β phosphorylation and ATG2 expression, with interactions between GSK-3β, v-ATPase, and ATG2 being essential for maintaining normal lysosomal function.

The results demonstrated that A-PSL@7BIO was efficiently accumulated in the hippocampal region (Fig. 3C–F and G). The OFT was utilized to evaluate the spontaneous locomotor activity and exploratory aptitude of mice within a novel milieu. Compared with WT mice, APP/PS1-21 mice exhibited a greater ability to traverse the margins of the experimental chamber and accrue significantly greater distances and durations in the peripheral squares. Subsequent to A-PSL@7BIO treatment, the distances traveled and durations spent by the mice in the central squares increased substantially (Fig. 4B–D). The MWM test was implemented to evaluate the learning, memory, and cognitive capabilities of AD mice. This experimental approach hinges on the inherent aversion of mice to water and their natural inclination to seek out a concealed platform submerged in water. By capitalizing on this characteristic, the capacity of A-PSL@7BIO to ameliorate spatial memory deficits and cognitive impairments in APP/PS1-21 mice were evaluated. During the initial five-day training, the mice were induced to locate an underwater concealed platform, and the escape latency of each platform was meticulously recorded. As training unfolded, WT mice presented the strongest learning ability, with a marked reduction in escape latency. In contrast, APP/PS1-21 mice presented markedly longer latencies and severely impaired spatial memory, and no latency reduction was observed. These findings indicated that APP/PS1-21 mice presented significant learning and memory deficits. After treatment, the latency in the A-PSL@7BIO group was significantly reduced, with notable differences observed between the A-PSL@7BIO and PSL@7BIO groups (Fig. 4E). On the sixth day of the experiment, the submerged platform was withdrawn. The swimming paths, speeds, percentage of distance traveled and time spent in the platform's previous quadrant and the former platform area crossing times within 60 s were recorded. The absence of significant disparities in swimming speed across all groups implied comparable motor capabilities (Fig. 4F). In terms of quantitative metrics, compared with WT mice, APP/PS1-21 mice demonstrated significantly reduced performance in spatial memory tasks. Specifically, APP/PS1-21 mice exhibited notably lower percentages of time (approximately 17%) and movement distance (approximately 18%) in the platform-containing quadrant, whereas WT mice presented 46% and 40%, respectively, in these parameters. Notably, after treatment with A-PSL@7BIO, these percentages reached 46% and 38%, which closely aligned with the performance levels of the WT group (46% and 40%, respectively) (Fig. 4G and H). The mean number of platform-crossing events for the A-PSL@7BIO group was 3.42, which was significantly different from that of the AD group (Fig. 4I).

An examination of the swimming path maps indicated that the WT mice predominantly swam within the target quadrant, suggesting unimpaired learning and memory functions that enabled them to accurately identify and reach the target area. In contrast, AD mice swam haphazardly along the perimeter of the pool, a characteristic manifestation of impaired spatial memory and cognitive skills. Compared with saline treatment, treatment with 7BIO or PS did not substantially improve the ability of the mice to seek the platform, with no marked difference. Mice in the PSL@7BIO group were able to swim toward the former platform location after a brief exploration of the pool, suggesting a partial restoration of spatial memory. Owing to its superior brain-targeting ability and ability to penetrate the BBB, A-PSL@7BIO-treated mice swam rapidly toward the platform upon entering the water, with their swimming paths concentrated in the target quadrant (Fig. 4J), demonstrating the efficacy of A-PSL@7BIO in enhancing learning capacity and alleviating memory deficits in APP/PS1-21 mice. The nest-building results further supported the cognitive benefits of A-PSL@7BIO, with improved nest-building performance in APP/PS1-21 mice (Supporting Information Fig. S9). Collectively, these findings indicate the significant therapeutic potential of A-PSL@7BIO in addressing AD-related cognitive impairments.

Evaluation of the in vivo neuroprotective effect of A-PSL@7BIO

The neuroprotective effect of A-PSL@7BIO on neurons was evaluated via Nissl staining and immunofluorescence. Nissl bodies, which reflect neuronal activity and functional integrity, enabled the morphological evaluation of the effects of A-PSL@7BIO on neuronal cell bodies through Nissl staining. Damaged neuronal cell bodies appeared dark-colored and atrophied, with interneuronal gaps, whereas normal neurons were light blue stained and arranged in parallel. Compared with the saline group, the A-PSL@7BIO group presented significantly neater arrangements and lighter staining of Nissl bodies in the dentate gyrus (DG), Cornu Ammonis 1 (CA1), Cornu Ammonis 2 (CA2), and Cornu Ammonis 3 (CA3) regions of the hippocampus and cerebral cortex (Fig. 5A–D). These results indicate that A-PSL@7BIO effectively restored neuronal structure and physiologic function. To further validate the neuroprotective effect and assess the Aβ clearance capacity, immunostaining for Aβ and neuronal NeuN in mouse brain slices was performed to explore the dual effects of A-PSL@7BIO in preserving neuronal integrity and reducing the amyloid burden. In the hippocampal and cortical regions, A-PSL@7BIO treatment reduced extracellular Aβ deposition. Moreover, compared with the saline group, the A-PSL@7BIO group presented markedly greater neuron counts and fluorescence intensities (Fig. 6A–C). The hippocampus and cerebral cortex—two key brain regions governing learning, memory, and cognition—were analyzed via Western blotting to detect proteins associated with lysosomal membrane damage repair and acidification restoration in mouse brain neurons. The results revealed that A-PSL@7BIO, with enhanced brain-targeting ability and blood‒brain barrier permeability, synergistically combined the effects of 7BIO and PS on damaged neuronal lysosomes. This combination potently suppressed aberrant GSK-3β phosphorylation, thereby increasing v-ATPase activity, normalizing the phosphorylation levels of both GSK-3β and v-ATPase to physiological ranges, and concurrently augmenting ATG2 expression. Collectively, these effects restored lysosomal homeostasis, enabling the conversion of CatD to mCatD (Fig. 6D–K). Overall, A-PSL@7BIO modulates lysosomal function by attenuating GSK-3β phosphorylation and modulating ATG2 expression, processes that promote the degradation of misfolded APP/β-CTF to mitigate AD pathology and suppress extracellular Aβ deposition. Meanwhile, immunofluorescence staining was used to label the lysosomal damage marker Galectin-3 and the lysosomal marker CTSD to investigate the effect of A-PSL@7BIO on lysosomal function restoration in vivo (Supporting Information Fig. S10). In conclusion, A-PSL@7BIO also exerts a robust effect on repairing lysosomal damage in vivo.

The safety of A-PSL@7BIO

To further assess the in vivo biocompatibility of A-PSL@7BIO, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and blood biochemistry tests were conducted on the mice. A-PSL@7BIO was administered to healthy mice every other day at six consecutive doses, while the control group was given saline. The mice were subsequently euthanized, followed by systematic collection of blood and organ samples for comprehensive safety assessment. Compared with the saline group, the A-PSL@7BIO group exhibited no significant pathological changes (Fig. 7A). Biochemical analyses of blood revealed that the serum ALT, AST, BUN, and CREA levels in the A-PSL@7BIO group were not significantly different from those in the saline group and fell within the normal range, indicating the absence of liver or kidney toxicity (Fig. 7A and B). Conclusions

On the basis of the key role of lysosomal dysfunction in AD pathology, a brain-targeting, stimuli-responsive drug codelivery system (designated A-PSL@7BIO) was developed to address both lysosomal membrane damage and acidification impairment. The system was effectively delivered to the brain via ANG2-mediated brain targeting, thereby restoring neuronal lysosomal function, reducing Aβ deposition, and improving cognitive function in AD model mice. Overall, the A-PSL@7BIO system represents a potential dual-action therapeutic strategy that simultaneously targets lysosomal membrane repair and acidification restoration to mitigate neuronal lysosomal dysfunction in AD.
