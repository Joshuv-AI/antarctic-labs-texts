Structure-informed models for ionic current prediction in nanopore sequencing of expanded dna alphabets
Authors: Ashley Stephenson, Jayson Sumabat, Hinako Kawabe, Sidharth Lakshmanan, Hyo Joong Kim, Yubing Li, Jorge A Marchand, Jeff Nivala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNanopore sequencing enables direct, single-molecule interrogation of biopolymers and shows promise for analyzing not only DNA and RNA but also chemically modified bases, proteins, and other polymers. Expanded DNA alphabets, such as those found in xenonucleic acids (XNAs), open new possibilities for diagnostics, therapeutics, data storage, and engineered biology. However, robust sequencing strategies for these modified molecules remain lacking. While nanopore-based tools exist for some noncanonical bases, they often require extensive experimental calibration by measuring each base across many sequence contexts, which limits scalability and increases cost. In this work, we investigate computational methods for predicting the ionic current signals produced during nanopore sequencing of DNA containing noncanonical XNA bases, aiming to reduce the need for experimental calibration. We compare a sequence-based predictive model with two structure-aware approaches: one using graph-based molecular representations and another adapting a generative language model to molecular SMILES. Our findings show that while sequence context captures much of the signal variability, incorporating structural and chemical information improves predictive accuracy in specific cases. These results highlight the value of structural data representations and model design in scaling XNA sequencing, and suggest this framework could extend to modeling ionic currents from other complex biomolecules, such as proteins.

Introduction

Expanding the four-letter alphabet of DNA beyond the standard nucleotides (A, T, G, C) holds great promise for synthetic biology, biotechnology, and medicine. As DNA synthesis and sequencing technologies have matured, researchers can now design synthetic nucleotides with new chemical functionalities or novel base-pairing properties. Unnatural base-pairing xenonucleic acids (XNAs) are at the forefront of this work, offering the potential to expand the chemical, structural, and binding capabilities of DNA beyond those found in nature. Expanded genetic alphabets could enable applications ranging from novel therapeutics to enhanced data storage systems and engineered organisms with new capabilities [1–5]. Many XNAs have been synthesized with modified sugars, backbones, or nucleobases [6], but compared to natural DNA, there remains a shortage of biomolecular tools and commercial infrastructure to support their widespread use [7, 8].

A major barrier to XNA research is the lack of compatible next-generation sequencing methods. While natural DNA can be read rapidly, cheaply, and at scale, sequencing methods for modified or non-standard nucleotides lag decades behind. Current approaches rely on low-throughput and less generalizable techniques such as mass spectrometry, gel shift assays, or enzymatic conversion of XNAs into standard bases for Sanger sequencing [8]. Adapting sequencing-by-synthesis platforms like Illumina would require major changes to reagents, polymerases, instruments, and analysis pipelines [8]. Some progress has been made, for example, Wang et al. used an enzyme-assisted strategy to sequence a six-letter alphabet (A, T, G, C, Z\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{n}$\end{document}, P) by enzymatically converting synthetic bases into canonical ones [7]. However, this strategy is limited to a single unnatural base pair and requires sequencing both treated and untreated samples, reducing generality and efficiency.

Nanopore sequencing provides a more flexible platform for reading non-standard nucleotides due to its ability to directly sequence single molecules without amplification or labeling [9–11]. Prior work has explored its use for XNAs. Perez et al. combined nanopore sequencing with machine learning to detect hydrophobic bases Ds and Px [12], while Kawabe et al. synthesized and sequenced 12-letter alphabets containing up to six orthogonal base pairs [8]. In these studies, characteristic current signals for each XNA were empirically measured and assembled into models for classification.

Nanopore basecalling relies on decoding ionic current signals as DNA passes through a pore [9]. Existing algorithms are trained on canonical DNA and cannot readily identify XNAs. k-mer–based models have therefore played a key role in detecting modified nucleotides. These models assume that the current is influenced not only by the central base but also by its immediate neighbors, which together form the k-mer. Longer k-mers generally improve accuracy [13, 14], but the number of sequence contexts grows exponentially with k-mer size, driving up experimental cost. Flow cell chemistry also affects the effective k-mer size; for example, ONT’s R10.4.1 pores often require 5–9mers due to double reader-head geometry [13, 15]. Modeling becomes even more challenging when multiple modifications are considered, as the number of possible k-mers grows polynomially. Moreover, empirical k-mer models neglect the chemical and structural features of nucleotides, limiting their ability to generalize to unseen or chemically related variants.

These limitations highlight the need for models that incorporate chemical and structural information. Richer molecular representations could improve robustness and generalizability, reducing dependence on exhaustive empirical calibration and opening the door to de novo predictions. Recent advances in artificial intelligence have spurred progress in molecular representation learning. Approaches range from molecular fingerprints and SMILES notations to graph-based encodings [16], and deep learning models including convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent networks, graph neural networks (GNNs), and transformer-based large language models (LLMs) [17, 18]. GNNs capture molecular connectivity by modeling atoms and bonds as graphs, while LLMs, originally developed for natural language, can be applied to chemical strings such as SMILES, uncovering patterns that inform molecular property prediction.

Here, we evaluate molecular structure–based architectures for predicting nanopore current levels of DNA containing single XNA substitutions. Unlike prior XNA sequencing studies, which relied on fully empirical k-mer measurements [8, 12], our approach aims to reduce experimental burden by modeling the chemical and structural properties of XNA-containing k-mers. As a baseline, we implemented a sequence-only CNN. We then compared two structure-based methods: (i) a graph isomorphism network (GIN) that encodes k-mer molecular graphs with chemical features, and (ii) a GPT-2 model fine-tuned on SMILES representations of k-mers. We benchmarked all three approaches on ten distinct XNA bases and further assessed their utility for basecalling by constructing predicted k-mer tables and testing them in a custom XNA-aware basecaller.

Benchmarking k-mer models

k-mer model performance was evaluated using Xenomorph v1.2.0 with the xenomorph morph and xenomorph stats commands. Sequence-based CNN model

To compare our graph-based model and the GPT-2 model that incorporates k-mer chemical structure information with a baseline model that relies solely on sequence-level information, we trained a simple convolutional neural network (CNN). Each k-mer sequence was represented as a one-hot encoded matrix (k-mer length X number of possible bases), and used as input to the model. The associated mean ionic current level served as the label for each example.

The architecture of the CNN consists of two 1D convolutional layers, each followed by a ReLU activation function and max pooling. The first convolutional layer uses 32 filters with a kernel size of 3, while the second convolutional layer uses 16 filters with the same kernel size. The output of the convolutional layers is flattened and passed through two fully connected layers: the first with 16 units and the second with a single unit to predict the current level for each k-mer.

The model was implemented using PyTorch, and the Adam optimizer was used for gradient descent. Training was conducted for a maximum of 100 epochs with early stopping based on validation loss to prevent overfitting. Mean squared error (MSE) was used as the loss function during training, and R-squared values were computed on the test set to evaluate model performance.

For both the 5mC prediction and XNA experiments, the same architecture and training protocol were applied. Independent training repeats (n = 50 for 5mC experiments; n = 10 for each XNA base-dropout experiment) were performed with different random seeds to evaluate model performance. The average R-squared value and root mean squared error (RMSE) were used as performance metrics.

Graph neural network model

Representing k-mer chemical structures as graphs

Based on the workflow described in [14], each individual nucleotide was first represented as a simplified molecular-input line-entry system (SMILES) string. These individual SMILES strings, shown in Supplementary Table S9, were then combined to represent full k-mer chemical structures depending on each k-mer’s sequence. Specifically, SMILES strings for each nucleotide were concatenated with a “.” character in between each nucleotide. While this character signifies a “non-bond” in SMILES notation, we found that this did not impact model performance compared to following the method described in [14], where an “O” was appended to the end of each concatenation to represent the unbonded hydroxyl group of the sugar phosphate backbone. We chose our approach for convenience in constructing k-mer molecular objects using the rdkit Chem library. We also explored two different sets of SMILES representations, one that includes the sugar-phosphate backbone in the structural representation and one that includes only the base structure. For the 5mC prediction task, we found that SMILES strings including the backbone structure performed better than the base-only representation. This representation was also chosen for this dataset to be consistent with the representation in [14] that our implementation was benchmarked against. For XNA experiments, no significant difference in performance was observed between the two representations, and the base-only representation was used for convenience.

k-mer SMILES strings were used to construct graphs of the form G(A,X). A is an adjacency matrix depicting the connectivity of atoms in each k-mer, where A\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{i,j}$\end{document} equals 1 if and only if the ith and jth atoms are covalently bonded, with atom order determined by each SMILES string. X is a feature matrix where X\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $_{i}$\end{document} is a feature vector storing atom-level chemical properties for each atom in A. Included chemical properties are displayed in Supplementary Table S10 and were chosen based on the approach described in [14]. For 5mC 6-mers, adjacency matrices were padded to the maximum number of atoms of the largest k-mer in the dataset (133x133). XNA 4-mers were padded to a maximum size of (44x44). Before model training, all adjacency matrices are converted to edge index format to be compatible with Pytorch Geometric.

Model architecture

Our graph-based model architecture is a hybrid neural network combining Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) layers and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) layers for processing graph-structured data with additional molecular features. The model begins with four GINConv layers, each equipped with a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) consisting of linear transformations and ReLU activations. These layers capture the graph’s structural information by aggregating neighborhood features.

The parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\epsilon$\end{document} is learnable, allowing the model to balance the importance of a node’s own features and the aggregated features from its neighbors. This mechanism provides flexibility in how much weight is given to a node’s local structure during the feature update process. The GIN framework has demonstrated strong representational power by capturing intricate graph structures through these adaptive aggregation functions, making it well-suited for graph-level discrimination tasks. This feature aggregation and update process is carried out for each graph layer, and by stacking GIN layers, atom feature vectors contain chemical information for all neighboring atoms within a specific distance equal to the number of graph layers applied.

Following the GIN layers, the model applies a series of 1D convolutional layers, with ReLU activations, dropout for regularization, and average pooling to reduce the dimensionality. After the convolutional operations, the output is flattened and concatenated with a flattened one-hot encoded representation of the k-mer sequence. Finally, the model passes this concatenated output through two fully connected layers, leading to a single output prediction for the ionic current level mean. The architecture is designed to leverage both graph-based representations and traditional CNNs, making it suitable for tasks that require processing both topological and feature-based data.

Training

The GIN-based model was implemented using Pytorch Geometric. Mean squared error (MSE) was used as the loss function, and the Adam optimizer was used for gradient descent. Models were trained for a maximum of 500 epochs with early stopping based on validation loss to prevent overfitting. Independent training repeats were performed to evaluate model performance (n = 50 for 5mC experiments; n = 10 for each individual XNA base-dropout experiment). Average R-squared and RMSE values across training runs were calculated as performance metrics.

Fine-tuned GPT-2 model with k-mer structures represented as SMILES strings

Pretrained model and tokenization

Our fine-tuned GPT-2 model was initialized with weights from the “gpt2” configuration provided by the HuggingFace library (https://huggingface.co/openai-community/gpt2). XNA k-mers were converted to SMILES string representations following the same protocol described in “representing k-mer chemical structures as graphs” above.

The SMILES strings were then tokenized using a custom tokenizer built to accommodate the specific alphabet used in the SMILES representation. Each token corresponds to either a single chemical symbol or a group of symbols in the SMILES string. For instance, the SMILES string “Nc1ncnc2[nH]cnc12” would be tokenized into meaningful components like “N,” “c,” “1,” “[nH],” etc. The SMILES tokenization process breaks down the input sequences into smaller units to ensure the model can handle the chemical structure effectively.

Data augmentation

To enhance the diversity of the training dataset, we employed a data augmentation strategy, generating multiple variations of each SMILES string. For each k-mer sequence, 100 different SMILES strings were created by randomizing the atom ordering while preserving the chemical structure. This augmentation significantly expanded the dataset and introduced variation that allowed the model to generalize better to unseen data. For example, from the original SMILES string “Nc1ncnc2[nH]cnc12,” we generated multiple valid structural variations, each of which was tokenized and used during training.

Model fine-tuning

The fine-tuning process involved adapting the pretrained GPT-2 model to perform regression on augmented SMILES data. The GPT-2 model, originally designed for language modeling, was modified by attaching a regression head, enabling it to predict continuous values. We initialized the regression head randomly, while the remainder of the model used the pretrained weights from GPT-2. The model was then trained on the augmented SMILES dataset using mean squared error as the loss function.

Results

Comparing sequence- and structure-based architectures for k-mer-level generalization and DNA 5mC prediction

We first investigated how sequence-based and structure-based learning approaches compare in predicting nanopore signals. In a recent work by Ding et al., the authors developed a graph-based neural network model that predicts ionic currents from DNA k-mer sequences by encoding the molecular structure of each k-mer as a graph with chemical features [14]. Using a graph convolutional network (GCN) to represent atoms and bonds within each k-mer, the model leverages structural information to capture the relationship between nucleotide composition and sequencing currents. The reported model architecture incorporated GCN layers to create embeddings of the molecular graphs, CNN layers to summarize longer-range chemical information above the graph layers, and a final fully connected layer that outputs a predicted ionic current level for each k-mer.

To benchmark this approach, we assessed the predictive advantage of structural information by comparing it against a simpler, structure-agnostic model. Specifically, we implemented a CNN model that relies solely on sequence-based information. Our goal was to assess whether the inclusion of molecular structure yields substantial improvement over traditional sequence-only approaches.

Ding et al. first tested their GCN-based framework using the canonical DNA 6-mer model provided by ONT, which contains all DNA 6-mer sequences (4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{6}$\end{document} = 4096 k-mers). They randomly partitioned the dataset into train-test splits, simulating a scenario where the model must infer missing k-mers from limited data. After 50-fold cross-validation for each split, the authors reported that performance stabilized once more than 40% of 6-mers were included in training, using root mean square error (RMSE) and Pearson’s correlation (r) as metrics. For 6-mers only in the test set, the model achieved an average RMSE of 1 and a Pearson’s correlation of 0.995.

To compare these results to a sequence-only CNN model, we first converted each DNA 6-mer into a one-hot encoded representation of its sequence. Sequence matrices were flattened and fed into the model, which consists of two 1D-convolutional layers followed by two fully connected layers that output an ionic current prediction for each k-mer. We then performed 50-fold cross-validation for each train-test split, dividing the dataset into increments of 5% as in the reported GCN experiment. The average RMSE and Pearson’s correlation across two independent runs were 0.997 and 1, respectively (see Supplementary Table S1), performing comparably to the structure-based GCN model in Ding et al.

Ding et al. also explored the performance of the graph-based model on predicting 5-methylcytosine (5mC)-containing DNA 6-mers. First, they trained the model using all possible canonical DNA 6-mers plus {1%, 5%, 10%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 90%} of randomly selected 5mC-containing 6-mers. They reported decent goodness of fit when 5% of 5mC-containing 6-mers were included in the training data, with an average RMSE and Pearson’s correlation of 1.2 and 0.995, respectively. We replicated this experiment using our CNN model, again performing 50 independent repeats for each training partition (see Supplementary Table S2). When 5% of the 5mC-containing 6-mers were included in our training set, the average RMSE and Pearson’s correlation reached 1.1 and 0.996, respectively. When only 1% of the 5mC data was used for training, the average RMSE and Pearson’s correlation were 1.5 and 0.991. Again, these results were comparable to the GCN performance.

A key area of interest in predictive sequencing models is the de novo prediction of previously unseen modifications (zero-shot learning). The authors explored the GCN’s performance when trained on all canonical DNA 6-mers and tested on 5mC-containing DNA 6-mers. They found that while the goodness of fit for the 5mC-containing 6-mers was significantly worse than for the canonical 6-mers, the model was able to reach an average RMSE and Pearson’s correlation of 1.8 and 0.993, respectively. Despite the comparable performance of our CNN model and the reported GCN model in 5mC prediction when even a small number of 5mC-containing examples are included in the training data (few-shot learning), we found that the CNN model performed significantly worse in de novo zero-shot 5mC prediction cases (average RMSE of 21.2 and Pearson’s correlation of 0.632).

To replicate this, we developed a similar graph-based architecture to the reported model, replacing the GCN layers with Graph Isomorphism Network (GIN) layers. The GIN architecture has demonstrated robust discriminative power for graph-level regression tasks compared to other popular GNN architectures such as GCN and GraphSAGE due to a more expressive aggregation mechanism [19]. k-mers were first converted into SMILES strings by combining the individual base SMILES strings shown in Supplementary Table S9. The full k-mer SMILES string was then used to generate an adjacency matrix showing the connectivity of each atom in the molecule with atoms as nodes and covalent bonds as edges, as well as a feature matrix describing the chemical properties for each atom (see Supplementary Table S10). After several GIN layers, each node’s feature vector contains detailed chemical information within a specific graph distance (equal to the number of graph layers applied). As in the original approach, we subsequently applied CNN layers to summarize longer-range chemical interactions, followed by fully connected layers to predict the k-mer ionic current.

We repeated the de novo 5mC prediction experiment with our GIN model and achieved an average RMSE of 1.3 and a Pearson’s correlation of 0.995. This finding corroborates Ding et al.’s model performance and suggests that while a considerable amount of signal information is contained in the sequence context alone—as evidenced by the CNN model’s performance—incorporating structural and chemical information can significantly enhance model performance and generalization. Models that capture more complex relationships between k-mers and their resultant sequencing current levels have the potential to enable accurate de novo predictions for previously unseen modifications. This capability makes them practically valuable by reducing the need for exhaustive experimental measurements for every new modification or base under study.

Specifically, we evaluated the performance of the baseline CNN model, our GIN model, and a fine-tuned GPT-2 model using an XNA dataset comprising ten different XNA bases, nine from Kawabe et al. [8]. Overview of model architectures. CNN KXmer sequences are first converted into one-hot encoded representations. Standard convolution layers are applied, followed by fully connected layers that output a current level prediction for each KXmer. GIN Sequences are converted into SMILES strings representing KXmer chemical structures. Each SMILES string is used to generate an adjacency matrix showing atom connectivity, along with a feature matrix encoding chemical properties of each atom. GIN layers are applied to aggregate and update the node (atom) features with the guidance of the adjacency matrix. Each atom’s features are summed with its neighboring atoms’ features, and the aggregated features are passed through an MLP layer that learns a transformed representation of the features. The atom features are then updated. Atoms within a graph distance equal to the number of GIN layers are aggregated and updated following this process. Due to the relatively small encoding distance of the GIN layers (1-hop per layer), standard CNN layers are subsequently applied to summarize longer-range chemical information. Finally, fully connected layers output a current level prediction for each KXmer. GPT-2 Sequences are converted into SMILES strings representing KXmer chemical structures. To generate more diverse training data, data augmentation is applied, where for each SMILES string, 100 variations are created by shuffling the atom order while preserving the encoded chemical structure. SMILES strings are tokenized at the atom level to create a vocabulary suitable for the model. A pre-trained GPT-2 model is fine-tuned on the SMILES data, and a regression head is added to output a current level prediction for each KXmer.

For each XNA base, nanopore signal measurements were collected for all 256 possible 4-nt k-mers containing a single XNA substitution (4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{4}$\end{document} = 256 k-mers per XNA). Using k-mers as inputs and their associated normalized mean current levels as target values, we individually evaluated the three models’ ability to predict sequencing current levels. To assess generalization to unseen XNA structures, we conducted base-dropout experiments, where models were trained on all XNA and canonical DNA datasets except one held-out XNA type, which was used for testing. This approach allowed us to evaluate how well each model could infer current levels for previously unseen XNA substitutions based on learned sequence and structural representations.

Each base-dropout experiment generated a model-predicted k-mer table for the held-out XNA k-mer set. Model performance was assessed by computing R2 values for predicted versus true (measured) current levels. The average R2 values for each XNA base and model are shown in Fig. 3A, calculated across ten independent base-dropout runs per XNA base. The sequence-only CNN model achieved an average R2 of 0.85 across all ten XNA bases. Scatter plots for individual XNA bases are shown in Fig. 3B and Supplementary Figs S11–S21.

Next, we examined the effect of incorporating XNA k-mer chemical structures using the GIN model. In this case, the average R2 across all XNA bases was 0.84. Given the strong performance of the CNN model, we hypothesized that incorporating sequence context into the GIN model could improve predictions. To test this, we appended one-hot encoded k-mer sequence representations (as used in the CNN) to the output of the GIN and CNN layers before passing them to the final fully connected layers. This modification allowed the model to leverage both sequence and structural information. Including sequence context improved the GIN model’s average R2 to 0.88.

We also explored representing k-mer structures as 1D SMILES strings rather than 2D molecular graphs. Given the success of pre-trained transformer models in molecular property prediction, we fine-tuned a GPT-2 model to predict XNA sequencing currents. Each k-mer was first converted into a SMILES string encoding its molecular structure. To address the limited dataset size (256 examples per XNA base), we employed data augmentation by generating 100 randomized SMILES variants per k-mer, preserving molecular structure while diversifying textual representations [20]. These SMILES strings were tokenized at the atom level using a custom tokenizer, and the model was fine-tuned on this data to learn a domain-specific vocabulary. Following fine-tuning, we added a regression layer to predict current levels. As with the other models, we conducted base-dropout experiments, training on all but one XNA dataset and testing on the held-out set. This model achieved an average R2 of 0.83 across all XNA bases.

While model performance differences were less pronounced in the XNA task compared to the 5mC prediction task, the available training data for the XNA task was significantly smaller. Nevertheless, the GIN model with sequence context outperformed both the CNN and the sequence-agnostic GIN model on average. Our results highlight the importance of sequence context in determining sequencing current signals while demonstrating that structural and chemical information can enhance predictive power. With larger datasets, we hypothesize that these effects may become more pronounced.

Integrating model-generated k-mer tables in an XNA basecalling model

To assess how the XNA-trained models perform in a sequencing context, we integrated model-generated k-mer tables into a basecaller for single xenonucleotide substitution sequencing. In the work by Kawabe et al., the authors used empirically measured sequencing current levels to generate k-mer tables for basecalling. Using measured 4-nt k-mer models, the authors demonstrate basecalling of heptamer libraries containing single XNA substitutions (NNNNNNN, N = modified nucleotide). During basecalling, heptamer sequences are broken down into their constitutive k-mers (e.g. GTAXCAC \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\rightarrow$\end{document} [\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu _{GTAX}$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu _{TAXC}$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu _{AXCA}$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mu _{XCAC}$\end{document}]) and the level means of each 4-nt k-mer are used to model current transitions. The probability that a given 4-nt k-mer sequence will produce an ionic signal current is modeled as a normal distribution, and for any observed set of signals, the modeled probability density function is used to calculate the likelihood that an observed set of signal levels was emitted from a particular sequence.

For each of our three models, we generated k-mer tables with predicted current level means for each XNA dataset. These k-mer tables were then integrated into the basecalling model in place of the measured k-mer tables to perform benchmarking. It is important to note that the measured k-mer models have an inherent advantage over model-generated k-mer tables, as parts of the heptamer datasets used for sequencing are also used in generating the measured 4-mer models. With this in mind, we evaluated the performance of each model by focusing on three main metrics: root mean square deviation (RMSD) between each model-generated k-mer table and the measured k-mer levels, per-read basecalling accuracy, and area under the curve (AUC) scores calculated from receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Furthermore, to assess the generalization power of each model, we examined two different methods for generating k-mer tables for each model: a de novo prediction case where no examples of the specific XNA base are included in the model training data (zero-shot learning), and a case where 5% of the specific XNA dataset is added during the training process (few-shot learning).

To quantify how closely the predicted current levels matched experimentally determined values, we calculated the RMSD between the predicted and measured current values (Fig. 4A). Lower RMSD values indicate better agreement between predicted and true current levels. Among the three models, the GIN model consistently outperformed the other two in the zero-shot task, exhibiting the lowest RMSD values across most XNA bases (0.37–0.76, avg 0.57). The GPT-2 model showed a wider range of performance, with some bases performing nearly as well as GIN but others displaying higher deviation (0.32–0.83, avg 0.53). The CNN model had the highest RMSD values overall (0.54–0.88, avg 0.70), indicating that it struggled the most to approximate the measured currents. Importantly, few-shot learning improved RMSD values across all models, suggesting that even limited exposure to the target XNA base significantly enhances predictive accuracy.

We next assessed how well each model preserved sequencing accuracy when its k-mer tables were used in place of measured values in the basecalling model (Fig. 4B). While measured k-mer tables unsurprisingly led to the highest per-read sequencing accuracy (0.63–0.90, avg 0.80), the model-generated tables exhibited varying degrees of performance. The GIN and GPT-2 models performed comparably in this metric, with GIN achieving per-read accuracy values between 0.50 and 0.67 (avg 0.57), and GPT-2 showing a slightly broader range from 0.50 to 0.69 (avg 0.59). The CNN model again underperformed relative to the other two, with accuracy values between 0.50 and 0.60 (avg 0.55). This aligns with the RMSD results, where the CNN model’s poorer current predictions translated to reduced sequencing accuracy. As observed with RMSD, few-shot learning led to noticeable improvements across all models, with the GIN performing over 10% higher accuracy in the Zn sequencing task. This reinforces both the idea that partial exposure to the target XNA base can enhance model-generated predictions, and that the structure based models are more capable of generalization.

To evaluate the models’ ability to distinguish correct basecalls from errors, we analyzed ROC curves and calculated AUC scores. Two example ROC curves are shown in Fig. 4C and 4D, with curves for the remaining XNA bases available in Supplementary Figs S1–S10. Measured k-mer tables yielded the highest AUC values, ranging from 0.79 for the worst-performing base (S\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{c}$\end{document}) to 0.98 for the best-performing base (Z\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{n}$\end{document}), with an average of 0.92. Among the models, the CNN exhibited the best AUC scores on average (0.64–0.96, avg 0.82), suggesting that despite its poorer RMSD and per-read accuracy, its predicted k-mer tables still preserved some ability to distinguish correct from incorrect basecalls. The GIN model followed closely behind (0.68–0.90, avg 0.78), while GPT-2 showed the most variability, with AUC scores ranging from 0.41 to 0.86 (avg 0.74). The broader spread in GPT-2’s AUC values suggests that its performance is more dependent on the specific XNA base being tested. As seen in the other metrics, incorporating 5% of the held-out base improved AUC scores across nearly all models, further emphasizing the benefit of even limited k-mer contexts.

Overall, the GIN model exhibited the best balance of performance across all metrics, with the lowest RMSD values and competitive per-read accuracy and AUC scores. The GPT-2 model, while comparable to GIN in some cases, displayed more variability, with some bases performing well and others lagging behind. The CNN model, despite achieving the highest AUC scores in some cases, had the highest RMSD and lowest per-read sequencing accuracy, indicating that while it could still differentiate correct from incorrect calls, its current predictions were the least accurate overall. Across all models, few-shot learning consistently improved performance, highlighting the impact of even partial exposure to the target XNA dataset. This suggests that the need to exhaustively measure every possible k-mer for a given XNA base in every sequencing context is not required to achieve competitive performance across all k-mer contexts. Example current traces showing each model’s predicted mean current levels overlaying measured current levels are shown in Supplementary Figs S27–S29.

In summary, these results demonstrate that while measured k-mer tables still provide the most accurate sequencing performance, model-generated tables represent a viable alternative, particularly when some training data from the target XNA base is available. The relative strengths and weaknesses of each model suggest different trade-offs: GIN offers the most consistent accuracy, CNN provides strong classification performance despite higher deviation, and GPT-2 has potential but is more sensitive to dataset composition.

Discussion

Advancing sequencing technology is essential for unlocking the full potential of expanded genetic alphabets. In this study, we evaluated three machine learning approaches for predicting nanopore ionic currents from DNA sequences containing single XNA substitutions: a sequence-based CNN, a GIN model that encodes k-mer molecular graphs with chemical features, and a GPT-2 model fine-tuned on SMILES representations.

Nanopore signals are influenced by sequence context, molecular structure, and interactions within the pore environment [14, 21]. Traditional k-mer models, built empirically from sequence data alone, capture much of this variability but neglect the underlying chemistry and structure that shape ionic currents. Our results show that sequence information alone explains a large fraction of signal variance, yet adding structural features can improve performance. Hybrid approaches, such as combining sequence and molecular graph features, yielded the best results: the GIN model achieved the highest average R2 across all XNA bases, highlighting the promise of structure-informed modeling.

A key limitation of our study is the dataset size and diversity. The ten XNAs examined represent only a small subset of possible modifications, limiting our ability to fully assess how structural features generalize. Expanding datasets to cover more chemical diversity will be essential. Future work should focus not on ‘sequence vs. structure,’ but on quantifying how structural representations complement sequence features, and under what conditions they most improve performance.

Although our experiments used R9.4.1 flow cells, the modeling strategy is broadly applicable to newer chemistries. Challenges such as predicting currents for nonstandard bases persist across pore generations. Even state-of-the-art pipelines like Remora still use k-mer–segmented labels for modified base training, underscoring the ongoing relevance of k-mer–level modeling [13, 22]. Our framework is therefore complementary to end-to-end approaches like Dorado, which require large, diverse datasets [15]. In contrast, structure-informed k-mer prediction can bootstrap signal expectations for new chemistries, enabling early model development before extensive training data are available.

While nanopore sequencing uniquely enables direct, amplification-free detection of modified bases, it faces challenges such as noisy signals that can obscure subtle modifications, signal overlap between similar modifications and canonical bases, and reliance on training data from known contexts, which can limit generalization [23–25]. Compared to some established methods (e.g. enzymatic transliteration, mass spectrometry), nanopore sequencing can exhibit lower sensitivity or higher false-positive and false-negative rates, particularly in single-read settings.

Sequencing accuracy requirements differ across applications. Consensus accuracy improves with read depth, since random errors average out, but systematic errors can persist even at high coverage [26]. Some applications tolerate noisy single reads, provided coverage is high. Aptamer evolution [2, 27, 28], for example, can rely on consensus sequences from enriched library members. Similarly, consensus-based detection suffices for diagnostics with modest sensitivity [29, 30], monitoring whether engineered organisms retain modified bases [31, 32], or performing QC of synthetic constructs. Other applications require highly accurate single-read calls. Examples include polymerase fidelity quantification at the single-base level [33–37], digital DNA data storage [38, 39] (e.g., ATGC = 2 bits versus ATGCXYWZ = 3 bits), barcode readout in expanded alphabets, and QC of libraries without deep consensus [40, 41].

The models presented here fall into the first, consensus-driven tier. They do not yet reach the per-read accuracy needed for quantitative single-molecule tasks or fully replace measured k-mer tables. However, they performed under stringent conditions: limited datasets, restricted k-mer size (4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $^{4}$\end{document} = 256 per modified base), and zero- or few-shot evaluation. Despite these challenges, the models generalized to unseen bases, indicating that performance is not fundamentally constrained by the method.

A central motivation of this work is scalability. Measuring every k-mer context for each new base is prohibitively time- and cost-intensive. In contrast, predictive modeling dramatically reduces data requirements, enabling rapid prototyping for new chemistries. While accuracy improvements are needed before single-molecule applications are feasible, our results outline a generalizable and resource-efficient path toward that goal.

There are also clear routes for improvement. Transfer learning has proven effective for both GNNs and transformers: models pretrained on large molecular datasets (e.g., ChemBERTa [42, 43], pretrained GNNs [44]) can be fine-tuned to downstream tasks, improving performance in low-data regimes. Applying similar strategies to our models could substantially boost predictive power for poorly represented modifications.

Beyond GPT-2, larger and more advanced LLMs (GPT-3/4, LLaMA, Mistral/Mixtral, Qwen) offer additional promise [43, 45]. These models excel in zero- and few-shot settings, where they generalize with minimal task-specific data, and recent work shows they can learn chemically meaningful embeddings from SMILES [46, 47]. Although originally designed for language tasks, LLMs are increasingly applied to molecular property prediction [48], raising the possibility that they could transfer knowledge across novel nucleotide chemistries. Many questions remain, particularly regarding pretraining transferability to nucleic acid–like polymers, but this is a promising direction.

Our experiments also highlight the practical value of few-shot learning. While true de novo prediction remains a long-term goal, we find that limited exposure to new XNA data substantially improves performance, reducing experimental burden while retaining accuracy. This was evident for both 5mC and XNA tasks, where models trained on small subsets generalized effectively. Such few-shot strategies will be crucial for scaling nanopore sequencing to expanded alphabets.

Finally, this work points to future opportunities. Incorporating higher-order sequence dependencies, leveraging 3D structural information, or developing multimodal models that integrate nanopore traces with sequence and structural features could all further improve performance. As datasets expand in size and modality, we anticipate that machine learning models capable of directly inferring nanopore signals from molecular features will play a central role in enabling sequencing of expanded genetic alphabets and other synthetic biopolymers.
