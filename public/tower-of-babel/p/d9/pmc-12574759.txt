Psychosis-like symptoms and seizure in a case of complicated opioid withdrawal from heroin use
Authors: Ishita Malik, Sourav Khanra, Aniruddha Mukherjee, Sanjay K. Munda
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

Psychotic-like symptoms and seizures are not typical opioid withdrawal symptoms. However, opioid withdrawal has caused psychosis in a few cases, mostly with methadone and buprenorphine.[1] Only one case has reported psychosis in heroin withdrawal.[2] Seizures are another unusual manifestation of opioid withdrawal.[3] A PubMed search through January 2025 with “opioid” AND “withdrawal” AND “seizure” in the title found only two case reports.[34] Herein, we present a case in which a patient experienced psychosis and generalized tonic-clonic seizures whenever he was abstinent from opioids and in opioid withdrawal.

A 27-year-old male presented with a history of episodes of fearfulness, muttering to himself, and wandering behavior for the past 5 days, along with a dependence pattern of brown sugar intake for the previous 3 years. He used an average of 1 g/day by the chasing method. His last intake of brown sugar was 7 days before admission. One day after discontinuing brown sugar, the patient began isolating himself and stopped interacting with others. On the fourth day of abstinence, he experienced a generalized tonic-clonic seizure (GTCS). He complained of body aches and had a reduced appetite. He often muttered and smiled to himself, spoke irrelevantly, and neglected personal hygiene. He wandered around the house and neighborhood, did not sleep at night, and frequently walked around the house. Additionally, he cried without any apparent reason. History revealed that the patient had a similar presentation with behavioral symptoms and GTCS 1 year ago after discontinuing brown sugar. He also smoked one pack of cigarettes daily and occasionally consumed alcohol for the past 8 years. The patient denied using any other psychoactive substances. There were no history of any other psychological or behavioral symptoms. Physical examination was unremarkable except for bilateral fine tremors [Table 1]. On mental status examination, the patient’s attention was aroused but ill-sustained, and his affect was dysphoric, reactive, and communicable. A diagnosis of opioid dependence (F11.21), tobacco dependence (F17.21), and alcohol harmful use (F10.1) was made. Blood investigations were within physiological limits [Table 1].

The relationship between opioids and psychosis is complex. Individuals dependent on heroin have shown reduced gray matter density in the frontal, cingulate, and occipital cortical regions. These brain changes become noticeable after three days of abstinence but return to normal after a month.[5] Endogenous opioids and endorphins function as inhibitory neuromodulators of dopamine activity. In the absence or insufficiency of endorphins, an increase in dopamine levels result in psychosis. Some opioids may have an antipsychotic effect due to their action on dopamine and mu receptors. However, withdrawal-induced psychosis remains uncommon in clinical settings.[6] Opioid withdrawal activates noradrenaline-containing neurons in the locus coeruleus, mediated by excitatory amino acid output. Repeated opiate use alters the activity of medium spiny neurons in the nucleus accumbens, which are primary targets of dopaminergic neurons. These output neurons, containing γ-aminobutyric acid and other opioidergic peptides, project to the ventral pallidum and ventral tegmental area.[3] Homeostatic adaptations due to chronic opioid use may lead to seizures during opioid withdrawal. Another possibility is the presence of impurities or adulterants in the street heroin the patient was using. The consumption of adulterated street heroin and its distribution has been a known global health concern.[7]
