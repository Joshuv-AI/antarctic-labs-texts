ED50 and ED95 of Remimazolam Combined with Sufentanil for Sedation During Ultrasound-Guided High-Intensity Focused Ultrasound Ablation of Uterine Fibroids: A Prospective Dose-Finding Study
Authors: Anna Wei, Lianchun Ou, Yujie Chen, Meihua Chen, Fangyun Wei, Xiaofei Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltrasound-guided high-intensity focused ultrasound (USgHIFU) ablation of uterine fibroids requires conscious sedation to ensure patient comfort while preserving responsiveness for real-time feedback. Remimazolam, an ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine, has faster onset, predictable clearance, and lower respiratory depression risk than midazolam or propofol, making it ideal for conscious sedation. Nevertheless, its optimal maintenance dosage combined with sufentanil remains undetermined. This study was designed to explore the ED50 and ED95 of remimazolam for targeted sedation during the operation.MethodsA total of 40 eligible patients were enrolled in this prospective dose-escalation study. Sedation was induced with remimazolam (0.1 mg/kg) and sufentanil (0.1 μg/kg), both infused over > 30 seconds, followed by continuous infusion of sufentanil (0.3 μg/kg/h) and remimazolam at an initial maintenance dose of 0.1 mg/kg/h. The maintenance dose of remimazolam was adjusted using the Dixon up-and-down sequential allocation method with a 0.02 mg/kg/h stepwise change. Effective sedation was defined as a Ramsay Sedation Scale score ≥ 2 at four or more intraoperative time points without the need for rescue sedation. Probit regression was used to calculate ED50 and ED95, and perioperative safety indicators and satisfaction degrees were recorded.ResultsThe estimated ED50 and ED95 of remimazolam were 0.064 (95% CI: 0.025–0.086) mg/kg/h and 0.102 (95% CI: 0.089–0.128) mg/kg/h, respectively. All operations were accomplished smoothly with fast postoperative recovery. The incidences of postoperative nausea and vomiting were 30.0% and 22.5% respectively. High satisfaction was achieved among patients, surgeons and anesthesiologists, and no severe adverse reactions occurred.ConclusionThe continuous infusion dose of remimazolam ranging from 0.06 to 0.10 mg/kg/h combined with sufentanil can provide satisfactory conscious sedation with quick recovery in USgHIFU treatment. Since single antiemetic prophylaxis fails to effectively reduce postoperative nausea and vomiting, further researches are essential to establish optimized multimodal antiemetic strategies for this sedative regimen.Trial RegistrationChinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400084538), Registration Date: May 20, 2024.Keywords: remimazolam, benzodiazepines, surgical sedation, uterine fibroids, ultrasound-guided high-intensity focused ultrasound

Introduction

Uterine fibroids are the most common benign gynecological tumors, frequently causing menorrhagia and pelvic pain that often require intervention. As a noninvasive thermal ablation technique, HIFU achieves targeted coagulative necrosis of fibroids while preserving adjacent normal tissue, making it a valuable fertility-preserving option.1–5 However, HIFU ablation carries procedure-related risks such as transient skin burns from near-field acoustic energy absorption and neurapraxia when treating fibroids near neural structures. Thus, effective analgesia and sedation are essential to ensure patient comfort and safety while maintaining responsiveness for real-time symptom feedback.

Currently, standard sedation for USgHIFU mainly consists of midazolam combined with opioids.6–8 Midazolam has notable limitations, including a prolonged context-sensitive half-life with extended infusion, variable pharmacodynamic effects, and risk of excessive sedation - all of which are amplified during lengthy procedures. As a new-generation ultra-short-acting benzodiazepine, remimazolam demonstrates rapid onset, predictable clearance, and low risk of respiratory depression, making it highly suitable for procedural sedation.9,10 Remimazolam provides comparable efficacy and superior safety relative to midazolam and propofol in gastrointestinal endoscopy and flexible bronchoscopy.11,12 However, those procedures are short and differ greatly from USgHIFU, which requires prolonged sedation and unique clinical considerations. Patients must remain responsive to report sciatic nerve stimulation during posterior fibroid treatment to avoid permanent neuropathy, promptly alert clinicians to skin burning to prevent full-thickness necrosis, and tolerate the prone position - where sedation-related respiratory depression substantially complicates airway and anesthetic management. Therefore, existing data for remimazolam cannot be directly extrapolated to the HIFU setting.

Conscious sedation for prolonged procedures such as USgHIFU requires synergistic combination of a sedative and an opioid analgesic. Sufentanil was selected for its favorable pharmacologic profile: high analgesic potency, rapid onset, hemodynamic stability, and a well-defined ceiling effect for respiratory depression, all of which improve safety in spontaneously breathing patients. Sufentanil has also been widely and safely used in HIFU sedation protocols, supporting its clinical appropriateness.13,14

To date, the optimal effective dose of remimazolam combined with sufentanil for conscious sedation during USgHIFU has not been established. This study used the Dixon up-and-down sequential allocation method to determine the ED50 and ED95 of remimazolam combined with sufentanil for conscious sedation during USgHIFU ablation of uterine fibroids.

Materials and Methods

Ethical Review

This single-center, dose-finding study obtained written informed consent from all participants enrolled. The study protocol was approved by Yonghong Tan, director of the Ethics Committee of Liuzhou Hospital of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center (Approval No.: Fast Review-Study-2024-158; Approval Date: April 28, 2024). The trial has been prospectively registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No.: ChiCTR2400084538; Registration Date: May 20, 2024).

Study Subjects

We enrolled patients who underwent USgHIFU for uterine fibroids at the HIFU Center, Liuzhou Hospital, Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Center, between June 2024 and September 2024. Inclusion criteria: American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status class I–II; aged 18–55 years; body mass index (BMI) 18.5–28.0 kg/m2. Exclusion criteria: contraindications to HIFU treatment; known allergy or hypersensitivity to study medications; severe dysfunction of major organs including the heart, brain, lung, liver, kidney, and hematopoietic system; uncontrolled hypertension, uncontrolled hyperthyroidism, or severe anemia (hemoglobin ≤ 60 g/L); cognitive impairment or other psychiatric disorders that may compromise patient cooperation.

Interventions

Anesthesia Management and Intervention

All participants were required to fast for 8 hours and withhold oral fluids for 2 hours preoperatively, with no premedication administered. On arrival at the preoperative holding area and following the establishment of intravenous access, baseline clinical and demographic data were collected. The collected variables included the patient’s age, height, weight, BMI, American Society of ASA physical status classification, medical history, surgical history, and anxiety level evaluated using the Chinese version of the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7) scale,15 (Supplement Table 1).

Upon entering the operating room, peripheral intravenous access was established, supplemental oxygen was administered via nasal cannula, vital signs were monitored. Patients received a loading dose regimen consisting of sufentanil 0.1 μg/kg and remimazolam 0.1 mg/kg, both infused over 30 seconds. This was followed by continuous intravenous infusions of sufentanil at 0.3 μg/kg/h and remimazolam. The maintenance dose of remimazolam was determined using the Dixon up-and-down sequential method, with an initial dose of 0.1 mg/kg/h. If sedation was effective, the remimazolam maintenance dose was decreased by 0.02 mg/kg/h for the next patient; if sedation was ineffective, the dose was increased by 0.02 mg/kg/h for the next patient. All sedation and pain assessments were performed by the same two trained and calibrated anesthesiologists, intraoperative adverse events were recorded synchronously, including failure of intraoperative arousal (defined as no response to gentle supraorbital tapping or loud verbal stimulation), respiratory depression (defined as respiratory rate (RR) less than 10 breaths per minute or spontaneous breathing arrest lasting more than 15 seconds requiring arousal stimulation or mask ventilation intervention), hypoxaemia (pulse oxygen saturation, SpO2< 90%), bradycardia (heart rate, HR < 50 beats per minute), tachycardia (HR > 100 beats per minute), hypertension (blood pressure increased by more than 20% relative to baseline), hypotension (blood pressure decreased by more than 20% relative to baseline), and injection pain. Effective sedation was defined as a Ramsay score (Supplement Table 2) ≥ 2 at no fewer than 4 of the 7 intraoperative assessment time points (T2-T8); T1 (pre-induction) and T9 (awakening) were excluded from the assessment as they represent non-sedated states. Ineffective sedation was defined by any of the following criteria: (1) a Ramsay score < 2 at 4 or more intraoperative assessment time points; (2) a Ramsay score < 2 accompanied by restlessness, poor cooperation, and the need for intervention; (3) the requirement for supplemental sedatives to complete the procedure due to an insufficient sedation effect (ie, Ramsay score < 2) or patient request. For patients with ineffective sedation, a rescue dose of remimazolam 0.05 mg/kg was administered intravenously; repeated doses were allowed if necessary, with an interval of at least 5 min between injections. The rescue dose of remimazolam was set at 0.05 mg/kg for two reasons. First, according to the Phase I clinical trial conducted by Antonik et al16 in healthy volunteers, a single intravenous dose of remimazolam at 0.05 mg/kg produced only a mild sedative effect without inducing deep or excessive sedation, which is consistent with the pharmacodynamic characteristics of benzodiazepines. Second, preliminary data from five pilot cases confirmed its efficacy in achieving satisfactory sedation while maintaining stable respiratory function. A maximum of three rescue boluses (total 0.15 mg/kg) were permitted, with a minimum 5-minute interval between doses. If adequate sedation was not achieved after three administrations, patients were switched to midazolam (2 mg per dose, with supplemental doses as needed) along with timely airway support, and such cases were defined as suboptimal sedation for subsequent Dixon analysis. All patients received an intravenous injection of 8 mg ondansetron 30 min prior to surgery as a routine prophylactic antiemetic measure. For patients who developed postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), rescue antiemetic therapy with intravenous dexamethasone 10 mg was administered. Rescue analgesia was administered to patients with a postoperative Visual Analogue Scale (VAS; 0–10; 0 = no pain and 10 = worse imaginable pain) pain score ≥ 4 using intravenous flurbiprofen axetil 50 mg in the anesthesia recovery period or intramuscular tramadol 50 mg in the ward. If SpO2 decreased below 90% during the procedure, patients were encouraged to take deep breaths via verbal or tactile stimulation. If spontaneous breathing and alertness could not be restored, sufentanil infusion was discontinued immediately, and mask-assisted ventilation was initiated. If the HR decreased below 50 bpm, intravenous atropine 0.5 mg was administered as needed. Ramsay Sedation Scale score, Bispectral Index (BIS), VAS pain score, blood pressure, HR, SpO2, and RR were recorded at 9 time points: before anesthesia induction (T1), 5 minutes after drug infusion (T2), immediately after skin incision (T3), 10 minutes after surgery initiation (T4), 20 minutes (T5), 30 minutes (T6), 60 minutes (T7), immediately after procedure completion (T8), and at emergence (T9).

All USgHIFU ablation procedures were performed using the same HIFU device, the JC200 focused ultrasound tumor therapy system (Chongqing Haifu Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Chongqing, China). All procedures were performed by the same two senior operators with at least 5 years of HIFU treatment experience to ensure assessment consistency. Standardized HIFU treatment parameters were applied uniformly across all patients, including a treatment frequency of 0.8–1.0 MHz, focal acoustic intensity of 4000–8000 W/cm2, and focal spot size of 3×3 × 8 mm. A posterior-to-anterior layer-by-layer scanning ablation strategy was performed in accordance with standard clinical guidelines. Real-time ultrasound imaging was used for fibroid localization and intraprocedural treatment monitoring.

At the end of surgery, a designated nurse conducted intraoperative cooperation satisfaction surveys with the surgeons and sedation efficacy satisfaction surveys with the anesthesiologists separately; an overall anaesthesia satisfaction survey was then administered after the patient had fully emerged from anaesthesia. All surveys were conducted by verbal enquiry, with responses dichotomised as either “satisfied” or “dissatisfied”.

Postoperative Management

Following surgery, patients were admitted to the post anesthesia care unit (PACU) for continuous monitoring. Postoperative adverse events were documented, including hypotension, hypertension, bradycardia, tachycardia, PONV, emergence agitation assessed via the Riker Sedation-Agitation Scale (Supplement Table 3), delayed emergence (defined as failure to regain consciousness within 30 minutes after discontinuation of all anaesthetic agents with a Ramsay Sedation Scale score ≤ 2), and severe postoperative pain defined as a VAS score ≥ 4 at any time point. Patients were discharged from the PACU to general wards once they met the discharge criteria with a Steward Recovery Score > 4. Clinical follow-up was conducted 24 hours postoperatively to evaluate adverse events such as postoperative pain, dizziness, nausea, vomiting.

Postoperative Follow-Up at 7 Days

One week postoperatively, telephone follow-up was conducted by specialist anaesthetic nurses who were blinded to group assignments and sedation protocols. The follow-up assessments encompassed GAD-7 scale scores, PONV, and other adverse reactions.

Outcomes

Primary Outcomes

The primary outcomes were the ED50 and ED95 of remimazolam combined with sufentanil for sedation during USgHIFU ablation of uterine fibroids.

Secondary Outcomes

The secondary outcomes included procedural success rate; incidence of adverse events during the procedure, in the recovery room, within 24 hours postoperatively, and at 7 days postoperatively; inadequate intraoperative sedation and rescue medication requirements; abnormal intraoperative vital signs, Ramsay Sedation Scale scores, BIS values, and VAS scores; operative time, anesthesia duration, recovery time (defined as the interval from remimazolam infusion cessation to a Ramsay Sedation Scale score ≤2), and total administered doses of remimazolam and sufentanil; satisfaction scores from the surgeon, anesthesiologist, and patient; and the GAD-7 scale score at 7 days postoperatively.

Sample Size Calculation

For the classic Dixon up-and-down sequential method, sample size determination is typically based on the number of turn-over (crossover) points. Previous studies have suggested that at least 6 crossover points are required to ensure stable parameter estimation.17 Notably, simulation studies have indicated that 10 crossover points are needed to achieve favorable estimation performance.18 In the present study, we comprehensively considered both the number of crossover points and total sample size. We planned to enroll 40 patients to achieve at least 10 crossover points, thereby ensuring the reliability of ED50 estimation.19

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 9.0 software, with a two-sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Measurement data with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), measurement data with a non-normal distribution were expressed by the median Interquartile Range and categorical data were presented as counts and percentages (n, %). The ED50, ED95 and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) of remimazolam for effective procedural sedation were estimated using Probit regression analysis. Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons of hemodynamic and sedation indicators at different time points, and the Bonferroni post hoc test was adopted for pairwise comparisons between key time points (eg, T1 vs T2). The Dixon-Mood up-and-down sequential allocation method was applied to determine the dose of remimazolam, and a sequential trial graph and dose-response curve were generated using GraphPad Prism 9. Baseline characteristics between the effective sedation group and ineffective sedation group were compared using the independent-samples t-test for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Secondary Outcomes

Perioperative Outcomes

Of the 40 enrolled patients, 22 did not require rescue sedation, and the remaining 18 completed surgery successfully after receiving a rescue dose of remimazolam 0.05 mg/kg, with repeated doses allowed when necessary. The overall procedural success rate was 100%. Operation duration, anesthesia duration, and recovery time were 70.0 (60.0, 100.0) min, 85.0 (74.8, 111.5) min, and 1.0 (1.0, 3.25) min, respectively. The total consumption of sufentanil and remimazolam per patient was 29.0 (26.0, 36.5) μg and 13.0 (10.75, 16.0) mg, respectively. Patient, surgeon, and anesthesiologists’ satisfaction rates were 90.0% (36/40), 95.0% (38/40), and 92.5% (37/40), respectively. Rescue analgesia in the ward was administered to one case. Discussion

This prospective dose-finding study using the Dixon up-and-down sequential allocation method demonstrated that the ED50 of remimazolam combined with sufentanil for sedation during USgHIFU ablation of uterine fibroids is 0.064 mg/kg/h (95% CI: 0.025–0.086), and the ED95 is 0.102 mg/kg/h (95% CI: 0.089–0.128). These results provide a evidence-based dosing reference for the clinical application of remimazolam in USgHIFU treatment and fill the gap in evidence regarding optimal dosage in this field.

The ED50 of remimazolam in the present study was 0.064 mg/kg/h, with a relatively wide 95% CI of 0.025–0.086. Exploratory post hoc analyses showed no significant between-group differences in baseline characteristics (age, BMI, preoperative anxiety score), fibroid features (location, type, volume), or procedural parameters (total ultrasound energy deposition, mean power, treatment duration) between patients with effective sedation (n=22) and ineffective sedation (n=18) (all P>0.05), suggesting that these factors may not be the major contributors to the observed sedation response heterogeneity. The wide 95% CI was most likely due to interindividual variability in sedative response, although the small sample size (n=40) may have reduced the stability of the estimate. Given the substantial individual variability, individualized titration starting at 0.06 mg/kg/h guided by real-time Ramsay scores is recommended rather than fixed-dose administration to achieve optimal sedation and minimize overdose risk. Unmeasured factors may also contribute to individual variability, and future larger-sample studies with stratified analyses are warranted to narrow the confidence interval, verify the present findings, and improve the accuracy of ED50 determination.

Remimazolam, a novel reversible benzodiazepine, has been widely used for procedural sedation with favorable outcomes.20–23 Compared with midazolam, which tends to accumulate and shows unpredictable pharmacodynamics during prolonged infusion,24,25 remimazolam exhibits predictable clearance and a wide safety margin between sedation and respiratory depression.26,27 Previous studies have demonstrated that remimazolam has significant advantages over traditional sedatives, including higher procedure success rates, faster recovery, shorter hospital stay, and lower incidences of bradycardia, hypotension, respiratory depression, and hypoxemia.27–29 A clinical trial comparing remimazolam and midazolam for goal-directed sedation in the ICU reported significantly shorter time to achieve adequate sedation and faster awakening in the remimazolam group.28 Similarly, remimazolam caused less respiratory depression than propofol during gastroenteroscopic procedures.22 Although direct comparative data in HIFU remain limited, these findings support our results that the rapid clearance and preserved respiratory function of remimazolam are particularly beneficial for lengthy procedures requiring conscious sedation, in which midazolam accumulation and propofol-related apnea carry considerable safety risks.

This study achieved a surgical success rate of 100%. The median recovery time was only 1.0 (1.0, 3.25) min, indicating rapid restoration of normal physiological function. No serious adverse events occurred. All adverse reactions were transient and self-limiting, resolving spontaneously or recovering with only mild symptomatic treatment. These findings demonstrate that the remimazolam-based sedation regimen adopted in this study is safe and well tolerated.

Regarding sedation depth, the Ramsay score of patients reached 4.35 ± 0.92 within 5 min after drug administration, indicating that the target sedation depth was successfully achieved. However, despite adequate clinical sedation (Ramsay ≥ 2), the BIS values at each intraoperative time point remained at a relatively high level (70.93 ± 7.92–85.98 ± 7.19). The study conducted by Xin et al30 also found that BIS values remained relatively high even when satisfactory sedation depth was achieved with remimazolam, which is consistent with our present findings. Studies31,32 have demonstrated that numerous factors can affect the correlation between BIS and Ramsay Sedation Scale scores, such as patient age, anesthesia modality, type of sedative agent, and clinical setting. As demonstrated by Xin et al33 remimazolam-based sedation is associated with greater electromyographic interference on BIS monitoring compared to propofol, potentially leading to artificially elevated BIS readings. Combined administration of sedatives and opioids also interferes with this correlation.31 Additionally, BIS monitoring is less sensitive to benzodiazepines than to propofol.34 Accumulated evidence reveals that the correlation between BIS and Ramsay Sedation Scale scores gradually declines as sedation or procedural duration extends.32 Therefore, BIS values during remimazolam sedation should be interpreted with caution and should not be used as the sole indicator of sedation depth.

It is worth noting that the incidence of PONV in patients in this study was relatively high (30% nausea, 22.5% vomiting), which was significantly higher than the 16.1% reported in previous studies.35 This may be attributed to several factors: first, the sequential study design with a limited sample size may have failed to accurately reflect the true PONV incidence, highlighting the need for expanded sample sizes to improve result extrapolation in future studies; second, all participants were female, a proven independent risk factor for PONV,36 which may have elevated the observed incidence; third, stratified analysis was performed using the median values of surgical duration, anesthesia duration, sufentanil dosage and remimazolam dosage as cut-off values to explore potential risk factors for postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), the results revealed no significant correlations between these indicators and the incidence of PONV (all P > 0.05), these findings indicated that patient-related risk factors such as female gender exerted a far greater impact on PONV than anesthesia-related parameters in this high-risk population, additionally, the relatively small sample size of this study limited the statistical power of hypothesis testing. Despite preoperative antiemetic prophylaxis with 8 mg ondansetron, PONV incidence remained high, indicating that single-agent antiemetic may be insufficient under this sedation regimen. Future prospective studies should evaluate whether multimodal antiemetic regimens (eg, ondansetron combined with dexamethasone or NK-1 receptor antagonists) can reduce PONV rates.

This study has certain limitations. First, this study is a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, and all study subjects were from the HIFU center of a single hospital, which may have selection bias, and the extrapolation of the results may be affected to a certain extent. Second, this study did not set a control group (such as midazolam combined with sufentanil group), so it is impossible to directly compare the advantages and disadvantages of remimazolam and the traditional sedation regimen. In the future, randomized controlled trials can be used to further verify the effectiveness and safety of the remimazolam sedation regimen. In addition, this study only followed up to 1 week after the operation, and did not conduct long-term follow-up of the patients, so it is impossible to evaluate the impact of the sedation regimen on the long-term recovery and quality of life of the patients. In the follow-up, the follow-up period should be extended to capture long-term outcomes. Furthermore, patient satisfaction in this study was assessed merely by means of a binary yes/no classification, without further quantitative stratified analysis. It is recommended that future studies adopt multi-level rating scales, such as the five-point Likert scale, to conduct more detailed and quantitative evaluation of patient satisfaction.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the ED50 and ED95 of remimazolam combined with sufentanil for sedation during USgHIFU ablation are 0.064 mg/kg/h and 0.102 mg/kg/h respectively. The recommended clinical infusion dose is 0.06–0.10 mg/kg/h, which ensures effective sedation, quick recovery and good safety. Given the unsatisfactory PONV control with single-agent ondansetron, further studies are needed to explore multimodal antiemetic schemes. More large-sample trials are also required to confirm our findings and optimize dosing for special populations.
