From text to translation: using language models to prioritize variants for clinical review
Authors: Weijiang Li, Xiaomin Li, Ethan Lavallee, Alice Saparov, Marinka Zitnik, Christopher Cassa
Published: 2026

Background

As genomic sequencing becomes increasingly integrated into clinical practice, the pace of variant interpretation and biomedical data production has accelerated. From 2019 to 2024, clinical laboratories have submitted 3.68 million variant classifications to ClinVar, a public archive of human genetic variation linked to clinical disorders [1]. However, even in well-studied disease genes such as BRCA1 and LDLR, the majority of variants have only been observed in a few cases or a single individual [2, 3]. Consequently, these variants often lack definitive human genetic evidence to be classified as pathogenic or benign and are instead classified as ‘Variants of Uncertain Significance’ (VUS) [4]. Due to this clinical uncertainty, current practice guidelines do not recommend communicating information about VUS to providers or patients outside of a clinical indication for testing [5].

The sequence variant interpretation (SVI) process involves expert curation of evidence supporting pathogenicity or benignity, following guidelines developed by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) [4]. Information curated during variant assessment includes clinical case evidence, computational predictions of variant effect, and experimental screening measuring protein function, among others. The available evidence is collectively weighted to reach a clinical classification for each variant and is often compiled into a text summary. Since 2019, over 2.1 million of these variant submission text summaries have been deposited to ClinVar [1]. Although they contain a great deal of curated evidence, these reports contain heterogeneous information, lack a consistent structure, and often do not use controlled vocabularies for evidence types or clinical information. These issues are further complicated when multiple diagnostic laboratories review a single variant, often reviewing non-overlapping forms of evidence.

To address this challenge, ClinGen has established Variant Curation Expert Panels (VCEPs), groups of domain experts who systematically review and apply gene- or disease-specific modifications to the ACMG/AMP guidelines. VCEPs play a critical role in improving consistency across laboratories and in resolving variants that would otherwise remain uncertain. However, because VCEPs rely on expert review, their capacity to evaluate variants is limited by human and organizational constraints [6]. To maximize clinical impact, VCEPs follow structured prioritization strategies rather than attempting to curate every VUS [7]. Their highest priority is resolving medically significant conflicts within ClinVar, such as when one laboratory classifies a variant as pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP), and another classifies it as VUS, likely benign, or benign (VUS/LB/B). The second priority includes variants repeatedly submitted as VUS by three or more laboratories, where pooled evidence may enable resolution. The third priority is variants that are likely to be definitively reclassified as pathogenic/likely pathogenic or benign/likely benign, such as variants with clear loss-of-function effects in relevant genes or those observed at a frequency too high to be disease-causing. Finally, VCEPs may curate externally nominated variants that are of special clinical or research importance.

Here, we develop an approach to prioritize variants for VCEP expert review using evidence contained within text summaries developed by clinical diagnostic laboratories. Recent work has highlighted that evidence of pathogenicity may often be available well before a diagnostic classification is made, and that it is highly predictive of final classification [8]. Although ACMG/AMP evidence criteria can be recorded in a structured fashion, in practice this is rare: among approximately 2.3 million ClinVar submission summaries with text, only a small fraction explicitly mention ACMG evidence codes (e.g., PM2, PP3, PS1). Most reports instead describe evidence in free text, making it difficult to systematically identify variants that contain evidence suggestive of pathogenicity or benignity, without manual curation.

Here, we train a language model to discern the evidence patterns that are indicative of pathogenicity, benignity, or uncertainty contained within variant summaries. We then use this model to identify variants that are likely to contain significant evidence of pathogenicity or benignity, to prioritize them for review by expert panels.

Methods

Overview

We parsed and extracted over 2.3 million free-text submission summaries from ClinVar, which laboratories submitted to describe evidence considered during variant interpretation [1]. To minimize label leakage and template-based artifacts, we implemented an evidence-only text processing pipeline that (i) applied a sentence-level classifier to tag each sentence as description, evidence, or conclusion and retained only evidence sentences for downstream modeling, and (ii) used MinHash-based near-duplicate removal at the submission level to prioritize unique text. After additionally filtering short records, this processing yielded approximately 1.2 million evidence-focused summaries linked to variant assertions.

From these summaries, we constructed training, validation, and test sets to fine-tune a BERT-based classifier. For each submission summary, the classifier assigns probabilities that a text summary is pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP), benign or likely benign (B/LB), or uncertain (VUS). We then validated these predictions using held-out ClinVar records using orthogonal deep mutational scanning (DMS) datasets and computational predictions at the variant level. As another form of validation, we calibrate the strength of evidence from our model predictions, using positive likelihood ratios (LR+) derived from variants classified as pathogenic and benign within ClinVar. Finally, we aggregated laboratory submission-level data to the variant level to enable multi-submitter consensus triage and identify variants with consistent model predictions of pathogenic or benign status as candidates for ClinGen Variant Curation Expert Panel (VCEP) prioritization.

This study used two primary data sources: ClinVar submission summaries, deep mutational scanning (DMS) functional scores, and computational scores (REVEL and AlphaMissense scores).

ClinVar dataset processing

Removing short and duplicated text summaries

We filtered short submission summaries by setting a threshold of 100 characters to ensure each comment contained sufficient text and removed duplicate summaries from the dataset.

Removing highly similar submission summaries and standardizing text

Because submission summaries made by the same lab usually follow the same template, we removed highly similar submissions at the submitter-level and gene-level. This is done by grouping submissions from the same lab and by the same gene, then applying MinHash [15] that compares sets of short text fragments (n-grams) to estimate how similar two records are. This method allows for the identification of reports that are nearly identical in content, even if they differ slightly in wording.

To further standardize the extracted ClinVar text, we removed non-English words (including Unicode characters and Greek letters), normalized punctuation and symbols to follow conventional English usage, and excluded highly similar summaries (95% similarity) that often arose from template-based reporting by diagnostic laboratories. Examples of deduplicated text are provided in Section Supplementary Methods.

Training SentenceClassifier to keep evidence-focused text

A ClinVar submission summary is a plaintext clinical report generated by a diagnostic lab that describes the evidence that was evaluated for a single variant related to whether it causes a specific phenotype or disorder. These submission text summaries often include one or more description sentences, for example, detailing the gene, location, and type of variant in the report, rather than specific forms of evidence of pathogenicity or benignity, (e.g., ‘The p.L95P pathogenic mutation (also known as c.284T>C), located in coding exon 3 of the SDHD gene, results from a T to C substitution at nucleotide position 284.’). Furthermore, some sentences serve as a conclusion of the variant classification (e.g., ‘Based on the supporting evidence, this alteration is interpreted as a disease-causing mutation.’), which serves as a clear proxy for a class label. While these sentences can contain evidence of pathogenicity or benignity (such as the functional consequence of the variant), any embedded class labels or specific structural elements can directly encode the final classification or lab-specific templates and therefore may contribute to bias or overfitting in model training; we remove such description and conclusion sentences and retain only evidence sentences for model training.

To reduce label leakage and overfitting to lab-specific templates, we trained a SentenceClassifier to identify these descriptions and conclusions and remove them, retaining only sentences that describe underlying evidence (e.g., case observations, functional studies, or population data). We fine-tuned a BERT [16] model with our labeled data to train a sentence classifier to identify sentences in submission summaries as description, evidence, and conclusion. Examples of our labeled data are provided in Section Supplementary Methods, and the distribution of each type of sentence is included in Additional file 1 Table S1.

We used the NLTK sentence tokenizer [17] to split each submission summary into individual sentences. We employed the SentenceClassifier to label sentences as description, evidence, and conclusion. We then removed description and conclusion sentences from each submission summary, only keeping evidence-labeled sentences (see examples in Section Supplementary Methods) to reduce the likelihood that our dataset contains direct assertions of variant pathogenicity. This approach removes approximately 25% of the text. Detailed statistics on character, word, and sentence level are included in (b) in Fig. 1a.

Sampling training data

Finally, we sampled a subset of the ClinVar dataset to create training and testing datasets. Given the imbalanced distribution of B/LB submission summaries relative to P/LP and VUS summaries (22k B/LB, 216k P/LP, and 682k VUS), we sampled all B/LB summaries and constructed a 1:2:2 ratio for B/LB, P/LP, and VUS summaries. This corpus was split with 80% used for training and 20% for testing the model.

After filtering ClinVar text summaries using each of these three approaches, we created three distinct training sets. We sampled pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP), uncertain (VUS), or benign or likely benign (B/LB) variants, again in proportions of 2:2:1, given the limited number of B/LB variants. All three sets contained the same number of variants; sampling was done by class, submitting lab, and gene.

Fine-tuning BERT models for sequence classification

Using our training and testing data, we defined our approach as a sequence classification task. Specifically, we input a submission summary from ClinVar into a language model and task the model with predicting whether the variant is P/LP (pathogenic or likely pathogenic), B/LB (benign or likely benign), or VUS (Variant of Uncertain Significance). We evaluated multiple BERT-based transformer models [18], including BERT [16], RoBERTa [19], BioBERT [20], ScholarBERT [21], and ClinicalBERT [22]. BERT and RoBERTa are general-domain models, whereas BioBERT, ScholarBERT, and ClinicalBERT are domain-specific models pre-trained on large biomedical or clinical text corpora.

For the fine-tuned model trained from BioBERT-base, we refer to it as ClinVar-BERT in the following texts. Configurations and detailed training setups are discussed in Section Supplementary Methods.

Model validation with experimental and computational data

Experimental and computational dataset construction

To validate the accuracy and generalizability of ClinVar-BERT and other fine-tuned models, we evaluated their performance on separately generated experimental screening data and computational predictions. For experimental screening data, we included estimates of functional impact for genetic variants derived from high-quality assays in genes with established clinical significance: BRCA1, HRAS, LDLR, PTEN, and TP53. All submission summaries for variants in these genes are excluded from the training and testing data. The DMS dataset was constructed by matching ClinVar submission summaries to variants with corresponding functional assay scores. These scores, downloaded from MaveDB [23], were processed using the FUSE optimization pipeline [10] and paired with ClinVar submission summaries at the amino acid substitution level. For validation using computational scores, REVEL scores and AlphaMissense scores are generated using Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor [13] for variants in the DMS dataset that intersect with ClinVar variants that have text summaries.

Benchmark evaluation using experimental functional scores

To evaluate the performance of ClinVar-BERT and other fine-tuned models using DMS data, we first generated ground truth labels. To do so, we assign labels (P/LP, B/LB, and VUS) based on functional scores. For functional data, we include three predicted impact types: ‘LOF’ which we use to include any damaging variants, ‘INT’ or intermediate, and ‘FUNC’ or functional. We acknowledge that there may be other mechanisms, such as gain-of-function, which may differ from loss-of-function mechanism results. We group these within the ’LOF’ group for simplicity of presentation, while understanding that functional assays may not always capture both mechanisms. We then map LOF with our P/LP class label, INT with VUS, and FUNC with B/LB. Ground truth labels were applied to the functional datasets using two strategies: (1) Using the proportions of ClinVar VUS that are expected to truly be P/LP, VUS, or B/LB, which we derived from an early saturation genome editing study in BRCA1 [24], by calculating the frequency of all ClinVar VUS at the time of publication, which was found in the study to be LOF, INT, or FUNC. This resulted in assigning variant scores in the top 21.1% as P/LP, variants in the lower 27.5% as B/LB, and all remaining variants as VUS. (2) We also used an alternative approach with standardized threshold values to assign ground truth labels for each variant in the entire functional validation dataset. Variants in any gene that have a functional score of are labeled as P/LP, functional scores that are are labeled as B/LB, and the remaining variants are labeled VUS.

We calculated performance metrics as follows: We calculated pair-wise AUC values between each class (P/LP vs VUS, B/LB vs VUS, and P/LP vs B/LB) using ROC curve analysis, with the average AUC-ROC reported as the mean of these values. For overall performance metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score), we used a class-weighted metrics calculation method to account for potential imbalances in our dataset. Results for strategy (1) for all models fine-tuned from text-processing methods are included in Table 2, and results for both label-assigning strategies are included in Table S5 in Section Supplementary Results.

Distribution analysis of model predictions with experimental and computational data

We then applied ClinVar-BERT and other fine-tuned models to make predictions on variants with submission summaries in the experimental and computational datasets, where the model produces three probability scores P(B/LB), P(P/LP), and P(VUS) for each prediction. We then normalized prediction scores for B/LB and P/LP by , so that there is no overlap between the prediction scores for each group.

Then we recalibrated our predicted class label frequencies using the expected proportions of impacts from single nucleotide VUS using the same approach as described above, using data from BRCA1 [24]. Finally, with our recalibrated prediction labels, we performed Mann-Whitney U tests [25] to assess the statistical significance and calculated median shifts between groups predicted as P/LP or B/LB based on their functional assay and computational scores. Results for ClinVar-BERT trained with evidence-only data are shown in Fig. 2a, b, and c. Additional results for all settings and models trained from different base models are shown in Additional file 1 Figs. S1, S2, S3, and S4 in Supplementary Results Section (DMS validation results).

Additionally, to ensure the robustness of our findings, we evaluated our model against experimental functional scores, using two alternative choices of priors, which are included in Additional file 1 in Supplementary Results Section (Model validation with experimental and computational data). Results from these alternative threshold methods are presented in Section Supplementary Results, Figs. S6 and S7, demonstrating the consistency of our findings across different class distributions. Furthermore, we validated the model without applying recalibration, as shown in Fig. S5. Overall, we validated the model with recalibration using three substantially different thresholds and without recalibration, and the results are consistent across all three settings.

Clinical calibration of model predictions for ACMG SF Genes and VCEP Genes

ClinVar-BERT predictions span a broad range of evidence types. As a form of validation of our model predictions, we sought to measure the strength of evidence of pathogenicity or benignity that is comprehensively captured within these scores. To do this, we adapted the ACMG/AMP SVI Bayesian framework guidelines, calibrating the strength by measuring the positive likelihood ratio, using previously classified pathogenic and benign variants within ClinVar.

We selected variants within two medically impactful gene sets for our calibration analysis: genes curated by ClinGen Variant Curation Expert Panels (VCEP) [6] and genes included in the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics Secondary Findings (ACMG SF) list [14]. VCEP genes were extracted from the ClinGen VCEP list, while ACMG SF genes were obtained from published supplementary materials.

We used previously classified variants to calibrate this evidence, specifically following the ‘clinical significance’ field within ClinVar, converting to binary labels for calibration. Benign classifications (including “benign,” “likely benign,” and “benign/likely benign”) were assigned label 0, while pathogenic classifications (including “pathogenic,” “likely pathogenic,” and “pathogenic/likely pathogenic”) were assigned label 1. Variants classified as VUS were treated as unlabeled data for threshold calibration.

Within these genes, we calibrated the ‘strength’ of evidence provided by these predictions, using an approach adapted from one used to calibrate computational scores [26]. As our model outputs three probability scores for each variant: P(P/LP), P(B/LB), and P(VUS), we first tried to understand whether a variant was likely to be VUS, to avoid model classification between two small probability values for P(P/LP) and P(B/LB). To do this, we first calculated the strength of evidence that a summary was likely to be a VUS, calibrated using the P(VUS) value. We then excluded any submission from analysis that met the ’supporting’ level of evidence for being a VUS, corresponding to P(VUS) > 0.998665.

We then used a calibration tool [26] to independently measure the strength of evidence for our P(P/LP) and P(B/LB) model outputs. We calculated P(P/LP) and P(B/LB) scores for each variant in these genes, including those that have ClinVar P/LP or B/LP classifications, and provided these scores and labels to the application for calibration. The calibration tool then provides thresholds for different ACMG evidence strength levels, including Very Strong, Strong, Moderate, and Supporting for pathogenic evidence, and Very Strong, Strong, Moderate, and Supporting for benign evidence. With these thresholds, we then assigned ACMG evidence labels by comparing P(P/LP), P(B/LB), and P(VUS) to their corresponding sets of thresholds.

Model interpretability analysis

To analyze whether ClinVar-BERT focuses on clinically relevant evidence patterns, we examined neuron activation using Ecco [27] and compared embeddings from BioBERT [20] and ClinVar-BERT using UMAP visualization [28]. Full methodological details and visualizations are provided in Additional file 1: Supplementary Information for “From text to translation: using language models to prioritize variants for clinical review”.

Results

Model training and evaluation

Our objective is to train a model that learns text representations of evidence of variant pathogenicity, benignity, and uncertainty. With these learned representations of evidence, we then classify clinical text summaries for variants of uncertain significance (VUS), assessing how likely each variant is to contain evidence of being pathogenic, benign, or uncertain. A brief summary of the training strategy is provided in Fig. 1a.

We trained ClinVar-BERT by fine-tuning BioBERT-base [20], a BERT model pre-trained on biomedical literature (PubMed abstracts and PMC full-text articles), using our processed ClinVar training data. The model was evaluated on a held-out test set comprising 20% of variants, achieving an accuracy of 0.9720 (Table 1). This substantial improvement over the pre-trained model without ClinVar fine-tuning, with an accuracy of 0.3760, demonstrates the importance of domain-specific training on variant interpretation text.

During model training, we observed initial overfitting, with the model learning template-based patterns rather than evidence content. Through refinement of our text preprocessing to better remove conclusion statements while preserving evidence, we developed the final ClinVar-BERT model. This iterative development process represents a potential source of information leakage, so to provide conservative performance estimates, we conducted testing for alternative text processing methods, and results are included in Additional file 1 Table S2 in Supplementary Results.

We additionally evaluated multiple pre-trained general-domain BERT language models, including BERT [16] and RoBERTa [19], on the test data without any text processing. Separately, we evaluated BERT models trained on different biomedical text corpora, including BioBERT [20], ClinicalBERT [22], and ScholarBERT [21] (See Methods), including base and large models where available, on the same test data from the ClinVar dataset. Overall, the original pre-trained language models did not perform well on this ClinVar text classification task, with a noticeable performance difference between domain-specific language models and general-domain models (Additional file 1 Table S3 in Section Supplementary Results).

Interestingly, we observed that the performance of domain-specific models, such as BioBERT and ScholarBERT, was not as strong as that of general-domain models, without further training on the ClinVar dataset. However, once these models were fine-tuned with ClinVar data, domain-specific models had slightly better performance than general-domain models, and the performance gap became smaller, with all fine-tuned models achieving comparable results.

Evaluation with expert-curated evidence summaries

To assess ClinVar-BERT’s performance on expert-curated clinical evidence, we evaluated ClinVar-BERT on variant interpretations from the ClinGen Evidence Repository [2]. The ClinGen Evidence Repository contains curated variant assertions and evidence summaries from VCEP expert panels following ACMG/AMP guidelines [4]. We first trained the ClinVar-BERT model excluding variants included in ClinGen, and used each variant’s summary of interpretation as input text for ClinVar-BERT prediction, and the expert assertion (Pathogenic/Likely Pathogenic, variant of uncertain significance, or Benign/Likely Benign) as the ground truth label.

We evaluated the model predictions and performance metrics for each classification category, which are shown in Table 5. Overall, ClinVar-BERT shows strong discrimination for P/LP and B/LB classes, with an AUC of 0.975 and 0.986, respectively. However, performance on VUS classification indicates that even though it achieves an AUC of 0.919, the model tends to predict P/LP and B/LB instead of VUS, achieving an overall accuracy of 82.0% on 11,348 expert-curated variants. Furthermore, a precision of 95.3% for VUS indicates that when the model makes a prediction of VUS, it is usually correct.

Validation using orthogonal functional screening data

We next validated model predictions using orthogonal estimates of variant-level functional impact from deep mutational scanning (DMS) screens as ‘ground truth’ validation data. These experimental screens enable the functional assessment of thousands of coding variants installed in a cell line, typically replacing the native gene sequence. We evaluated model performance using DMS assays for five commonly screened genes: BRCA1, LDLR, TP53, HRAS, and PTEN, using functional score data from MaveDB [9, 23], which was normalized and processed using the FUSE pipeline [10].

We measured our model performance by comparing model predictions of pathogenicity from submission summaries with experimental measurements of variant functional impact from these DMS screening assays. We first developed class labels for ‘ground truth’ functional score data, using thresholds based on the prior expectation of proportions of ClinVar VUS (See Methods). We label loss-of-function scores from experimental assays as P/LP, indeterminate scores as VUS, and functional scores as B/LB for consistency. We then measured the accuracy of ClinVar-BERT model predictions against these ground truth labels.

We found that ClinVar-BERT can effectively separate P/LP from B/LB and VUS classes (Table 2). This indicates its potential in differentiating P/LP submissions among all submissions, which is potentially useful for the use case of prioritizing variant submissions for further evaluation. We evaluated a variety of sentence processing approaches and base models in Additional file 1 Table S4 in Supplementary Results. We found that the model trained from the evidence-only dataset yields the best result, as it achieves the highest pair-wise AUC scores for P/LP vs B/LB and P/LP vs VUS.

We also evaluated a different method to label functional scores that are not based on any single gene, but rather on normalized scores for the entire functional validation dataset, and label variants with functional scores above as P/LP, variants with scores between 0 and as VUS, and variants with as B/LB (see Methods). We find similar performance using this approach, and results for this additional method compared to the aforementioned threshold are shown in Additional file 1 Table S5 in the Supplementary Results Section.

Additionally, we evaluated ClinVar-BERT on different subsets of variants using DMS validation data, grouping by submitting laboratory, year range, and somatic or germline origin. We did not observe major differences in classification performance among different submitters or year ranges, but there are substantial differences for sample origin. There are considerably fewer submissions for variants of somatic origin, so this could potentially be useful to evaluate in future model development. Results are included in Additional file 1 S7, S8, S9, and S10 in Section Supplementary Results (DMS validation results).

ClinVar-BERT significantly separates functional and computational scores

In addition to benchmarking ClinVar-BERT model predictions using labels derived from experimental functional scores, we also analyzed the distributions of functional scores and computational scores for model predictions on VUS submissions. We first analyzed DMS functional scores using existing ClinVar variant classifications (not derived from our models). After processing with the FUSE pipeline, higher DMS functional scores generally indicate larger impacts on protein function, whereas lower scores indicate smaller impacts on protein function. As expected, in most genes, variants originally classified as B or LB had lower functional scores than VUS, which had lower functional scores than variants classified as LP or P (Fig. 2a, top). Notably, this was not the case for HRAS, which had a limited number of variants with classifications in ClinVar with functional scores, and PTEN, which had no B variants and very few LB variants.

We then used our fine-tuned models to make predictions on each VUS-labeled ClinVar submission with a DMS functional score. We recalibrated our VUS output class probabilities to be proportional to the true expected underlying proportions of B/LB, VUS, and P/LP classes. We evaluated several choices of prior expectation for these class proportions and recalibrated model output class labels using these possible choices for evaluation. The first approach classified variant text summaries as B/LB, VUS, and P/LP following the proportion of the number of VUS that were found to retain function, to have intermediate effects, or to result in loss of function, using the number of ClinVar VUS at the time of publication, in an early comprehensive functional screening study [24] (See Methods). Following the same approach as discussed earlier, we also used computational scores to validate model performance, aware that there is potential circularity (see Discussion). We evaluated REVEL scores [11] and AlphaMissense scores [12] for validation, and to reduce the potential of circularity, we only consider submissions with the last update date on or before 2023 specifically for validation with AlphaMissense score, as the scores were released in 2023 (see Fig. 2a, b, and c). Results for models trained with different text processing methods are shown in Fig. S1, and results for different base models are included in Section Supplementary Results (Additional file 1 Figs. S2, S3, and S4).

To ensure robustness of our findings, we also consider two alternative approaches to select these priors. Results for these alternative threshold approaches are presented in Supplementary Results, Additional file 1 Figs. S6 and S7. Furthermore, we validated the model without applying any recalibration for reference, as shown in Additional file 1 Fig. S5.

The DMS functional scores of variants that were classified as P/LP were significantly different from the functional scores for variants that were predicted as B/LB, for nearly all model types and genes. For many models, we found that predicted P/LP and B/LB groups showed highly significant differences, with large separations in their median functional scores (BRCA1 p-value of , TP53 p-value of , Mann-Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction). Notably, DMS data from LDLR were only recently published in [29], so it is highly unlikely that this DMS information was present in any ClinVar text summaries that we evaluated for this gene. These results suggest that these fine-tuned models had been sufficiently trained to understand information useful for variant classification for VUS within ClinVar that did not have sufficient evidence required to be classified as benign or pathogenic.

Performance discussion

We found that the fine-tuned BioBERT-base and BioBERT-large models had the best performance across all language models we evaluated. We found consistent and highly notably differences between the functional scores of variants classified as B/LB and P/LP in all five genes, with large shifts in median functional scores between these two groups. Results for other fine-tuned models were included in Section Supplementary Results (DMS validation result).

Prioritizing variants for expert review within clinically impactful genes

Using ClinVar-BERT, we made predictions for all ClinVar submissions with a text summary available, with 2,391,945 ClinVar submissions in total and 1,856,356 unique variants. Our model prediction for each ClinVar submission includes a predicted label and estimated posterior probabilities for each class label of P/LP, VUS, or B/LB.

Next, we sought to understand the scope of potentially actionable findings within genes frequently reviewed for clinically important findings. We included 85 ’clinically actionable’ genes recommended for review for secondary findings by the ACMG (ACMG Secondary Findings genes v3.3) [14] as well as genes that are included in a ClinGen Variant Curation Expert Panel (VCEP) [6]. ClinGen VCEP genes include 177 genes, including many inherited cancer syndromes, cardiovascular disorders, and inborn errors of metabolism, among others.

Within these genes, we reviewed 153,872 VUS with at least one text submission. Considering a variant could have multiple submissions available and thus could have multiple prediction results from the model, we evaluated the consistency of our model classifications across submissions for each variant. We identified 6,686 variants for which every single submission was predicted by our model to be pathogenic or benign, as shown in Fig. 3. We also identified 14,686 variants where at least two submissions had consistent model predictions of pathogenic or benign, where there are no conflicting model predictions for other submissions related to the same variant. This suggests that a substantial number of VUS are strong candidates to prioritize for those VCEPs, as multiple lab submissions for these variants were predicted by our model to be consistently pathogenic or benign.

Some VCEP expert panels have a substantial number of VUS that could be prioritized for review, Table 4, and detailed results for all VCEPs are included in Additional file 1 Table S15 in the Supplementary Results. We note that not all VCEPs are currently active, and that there is not a complete overlap of ACMG SF genes within ClinGen VCEPs. A table and visualization of the distribution of model predictions of VUS variants are shown in Fig. 3.

Clinical calibration of model predictions for ACMG SF Genes and VCEP Genes

As another form of variant prioritization for VCEP review, we measured the ACMG/AMP framework strength of evidence provided by our model predictions for variants in 177 genes that are reviewed by VCEPs. Our model predictions consider multiple forms of evidence that are described in variant summaries, so they do not map to a single form of evidence of pathogenicity within the ACMG/AMP framework; therefore, we modeled them as numeric scores. We calculated calibration thresholds for our model that map to ACMG/AMP evidence strengths (e.g., Strong, Moderate) under ACMG/AMP guidelines adapted from [26]. Posterior probabilities and associated evidence strength thresholds are also contingent on choice of prior probability [26].

Figure 4 shows the distribution of posterior probabilities for model prediction confidence scores for P/LP and B/LB, and the specific evidence strength thresholds, respectively (See Methods). While we expect a monotonic association between model-predicted scores and posterior probability of disease, Fig. 4a exhibits some stochasticity. This is attributable to the limited number of variants with model-predicted scores, specifically for variants that are known to be pathogenic or benign. Using these thresholds, we then assigned labels to variants and measured the number of VUS being labeled as having pathogenic or benign evidence using the calibrated thresholds. Alignment with VCEP prioritization strategies and clinical implementation

VCEPs follow a structured prioritization strategy to maximize clinical impact [7], and ClinVar-BERT can potentially support these established priorities. For example, to identify medically critical variants, VCEPs focus on variants that have conflicting classifications with at least one lab reporting that the variant is P/LP. For VCEPs to prioritize these for expert review, ClinVar-BERT can identify such variants by grouping model prediction results. Variants with high model probabilities of pathogenicity or benignity could suggest that aggregated evidence supports one classification over another. So if multiple submissions for a variant are predicted to be benign, it is less likely that the variant is medically important.

Furthermore, VCEPs prioritize variants for review that have been submitted as VUS by three or more laboratories, where pooled evidence may enable resolution [7]. Our analysis directly addresses this strategy; we identified 14,686 variants with 2+ consistent model predictions across independent lab submissions (Fig. 3). These represent cases where individual labs lacked sufficient evidence for classification, but the collective evidence patterns suggest the potential for reclassification. These variants could be prioritized in descending order of probability of pathogenicity or probability of benignity. Similarly, combining model predictions across submissions could also be useful, but would take additional calibration and review.

VCEPs or clinical laboratories can also use ClinVar-BERT to prioritize variants for re-review when new evidence emerges (e.g., updated computational scores or a new functional assay for a gene). VCEPs or clinical laboratories can identify VUS with high model confidence scores that make it most likely to re-classify each variant if new evidence can be applied from a novel data source.

Model interpretability analysis

Neuron activation pattern visualization

To understand what evidence patterns ClinVar-BERT learns, we analyzed neuron activation patterns using Ecco [27], which applies non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to identify groups of neurons that consistently co-activate in response to specific text patterns. We examined VUS submissions that ClinVar-BERT classified as B/LB or P/LP with high confidence (probability ).

Additional file 1 Fig. S8 shows neuron activation patterns, where different colors represent distinct neuron groups. While NMF does not automatically label these patterns, we can identify distinct evidence types through inspection. In Additional file 1 Fig. S8a, colored in purple, a VUS submission predicted as B/LB shows distinct clusters highlighting case information (“reported in at least 5 individuals with MODY”), allele frequency data across populations colored in orange, and computational evidence colored in pink (“prediction tools and conservation analyses do not provide strong support”). In Additional file 1 Fig. S8b, a VUS predicted as P/LP shows clusters corresponding to case studies (“multiple unrelated individuals”) colored in blue, functional evidence (“decreased potassium currents with slowed activation”) in pink, and publication citations in purple. We note that these colors represent distinct neuron groups identified through non-negative matrix factorization, but that these colors are arbitrarily assigned rather than having inherent meaning.

These patterns suggest the model has learned to group evidence types aligned with clinical variant interpretation frameworks, including population frequency, computational predictions, functional studies, and case reports consistent with ACMG/AMP guidelines [4].

Submission summary model embedding UMAP visualization

To evaluate how fine-tuning enhanced ClinVar-BERT’s ability to encode variant interpretation information, we compared embeddings from ClinVar-BERT and BioBERT on 25,000 randomly sampled submission summaries using clustering metrics and UMAP visualization.

ClinVar-BERT shows substantially better cluster separation for clinically relevant attributes (Additional file 1 Table S16 in Supplementary Results). For clinical significance and submission classification, ClinVar-BERT achieves higher Silhouette scores (0.4376 and 0.6446 vs. BioBERT’s 0.0991 and 0.1170) and lower Davies-Bouldin indices (1.4352 and 0.5278 vs. 4.4854 and 3.2261), indicating better clustering of pathogenicity-relevant text. BioBERT shows better submitter clustering, suggesting fine-tuning shifted focus from submitter-specific patterns toward variant interpretation features.

UMAP visualization (Additional file 1 Fig. S9 in Supplementary Results) shows that VUS summaries cluster systematically based on evidence content. VUS near P/LP regions contain pathogenic evidence (e.g., population data PM2, computational predictions PP3), while those near B/LB regions lack such evidence. Detailed examples of VUS closest to P/LP clusters, including their REVEL, AlphaMissense scores, and functional consequences, are illustrated in Table A.5.3.

These results demonstrate that fine-tuning adapted the embedding space to better capture clinical evidence patterns, providing a foundation for variant prioritization. Detailed analysis of the UMAP visualization is included in the Supplementary Results.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the potential of training language models to learn generalizable clinical evidence from unstructured variant summary text records in ClinVar. We fine-tuned both general-purpose and domain-specific models using labeled clinical summary text records to discern evidence of variant pathogenicity or benignity. We mitigated model bias and overfitting using a quality control pipeline that identified problematic records and a sentence classifier that filtered sentences that were less likely to contain evidence of pathogenicity or benignity, or more likely to contain class labels. We also validated our models using orthogonal data from ClinGen VCEPs, functional screening assays, and computational scores. We found that VUS classified as pathogenic and benign by ClinVar-BERT had significantly different functional assay scores and computational scores, supporting the hypothesis that this model identified relevant evidence. Finally, we identified many VUS that can be prioritized for classification within genes reviewed by ClinGen VCEPs or for ACMG Secondary Findings genes, using a consensus-based approach that aggregates model predictions across submissions at the variant level.

We found large improvements in variant classification tasks using models fine-tuned with ClinVar training data over general-domain models. Among the models we evaluated, classification performance was also consistently better when fine-tuning models pre-trained on broad biomedical text corpora, such as BioBERT-base, rather than using general-domain models. Given that BioBERT-base was trained on a large set of PubMed abstracts and PubMed Central full-text articles, it appears to be a solid foundation for learning additional generalizable biomedical and clinical evidence within ClinVar text summaries. Training and classification metrics also demonstrate that our sentence classification approach (filtering sentences from text summaries that were unlikely to contain clinical evidence) performed well across validation datasets. This approach reduces overfitting, likely by removing conclusion sentences that contained proxy class labels, as well as removing structurally similar sentences, which were common among description text.

In addition, when applying our model to genes with high clinical relevance, such as those included in the ACMG Secondary Findings list or curated by ClinGen VCEPs, we found that 6,686 VUS had unanimous predictions of pathogenicity or benignity across all submissions, suggesting that a substantial number of VUS could be prioritized for expert review. We further calibrated ClinVar-BERT predictions for P/LP, VUS, and B/LB confidence scores using the ACMG/AMP SVI Bayesian framework, where we obtained evidence strength thresholds that align with ACMG guidelines. This calibration identified thousands of VUS in genes within ACMG SF and ClinGen VCEP genes that reached moderate or strong levels of evidence for pathogenicity or benignity. These results highlight that ClinVar-BERT model prediction scores can be translated into guideline-consistent evidence strengths, though it is unlikely to be useful as a standardized source of classification evidence, given that it includes all forms of evidence of variant pathogenicity or benignity that have been described.

Our findings underscore the utility of adopting language models in processing and interpreting intricate clinical narratives, with potential applications in variant prioritization. ClinVar-BERT has the potential to identify variants whose text summaries contain meaningful clinical evidence, but were not yet sufficient for a pathogenic or benign classification. ClinGen VCEPs often rely on biocurators and interpretation experts, so the ability to prioritize VUS with the strongest model predictions could improve review efficiency, which is important as reviewing all VUS is presently time and resource-prohibitive.

Beyond VCEP prioritization, ClinVar-BERT may have broader applications in variant interpretation. Individual clinical labs could use ClinVar-BERT to reassess existing variant classifications, and potentially highlight cases where other labs have reviewed a variant with substantial evidence. ClinVar-BERT also fills a gap; currently, to our knowledge, there is not yet quantitative data about the likelihood of whether all VUS are more likely to be VUS+ or VUS-, in a machine-readable format. This information could potentially be useful for clinical risk assessment or counseling.

While we have not yet conducted a formal implementation study, we anticipate that employing ClinVar-BERT to pre-prioritize VUS using these evidence strengths could substantially reduce the number of variants requiring full manual review, thereby decreasing biocurator workload. Practical barriers include integrating model outputs into existing curation tools, building user trust in ML-derived evidence summaries, and establishing governance around when and how predictions should influence classification decisions.

Future work could also extract specific forms of evidence present within a clinical summary text and use that to identify evidence gaps. This approach could then be used to integrate information across a set of clinical summaries about the same variant from different diagnostic laboratories. By analyzing information developed by different labs, such an approach could prioritize variants that collectively had sufficient evidence to be reclassified but where the evidence provided by any single lab was insufficient for classification.

Limitations of this work

This study has several limitations. First, some variants have multiple text summaries from different clinical labs, which may need to be harmonized. Because evidence of pathogenicity is often developed over time, it is likely that more information is contained in the latest submission summary, but it may not include all available evidence. In contrast, while a very large number of variants have submission text summaries, many do not.

Second, text summaries are increasingly being generated using lab-standardized templates, which leads to high structural similarity among text summaries. This can lead to bias or overfitting if models learn template-specific features that are correlated with a specific classification, rather than learning about relevant evidence. Although we use SentenceClassifier to mitigate these highly predictive sentence structures, it is still possible that some classification-related terminology appears within evidence sentences, which may introduce residual label information. We consider this a limitation of working with clinical text that was originally written to document classification decisions. These templates are not uniform across labs, but increasingly, VCEPs are recording structured evidence types with reports from expert reviews, which should help mitigate this issue going forward.

ClinVar-BERT makes predictions at the submission level, and each prediction reflects the evidence described in a single laboratory’s submission for a specific phenotype or disease indication. The model does not aggregate information across multiple submissions for the same variant. This reflects ClinVar’s data structure, where each submission represents a single laboratory’s interpretation for a specific clinical context.

Our model cannot resolve variants with conflicting classifications automatically. For example, some variants may be predicted as benign based on the evidence a laboratory gathered, but pathogenic in another, or vice versa. In our analysis, 9,300 (0.5%) of variants in ClinVar have conflicting ClinVar-BERT model predictions across all submissions within a variant (having both pathogenic/likely pathogenic and benign/likely benign classifications for the same variant). When we apply ClinVar-BERT to these variants, some conflicts in model predictions may reflect true phenotype-specific differences in pathogenicity rather than disagreements about evidence interpretation. We have flagged these variants in our data summaries, and future work should analyze predictions for specific phenotypes or disease contexts to better handle these cases.

Furthermore, we acknowledge that there is potential bias and overfitting in our trained model that could affect the generalizability of performance. This could be attributable to multiple factors, including the multi-stage training pipeline, syntactic similarity of text summaries, and previously available functional or computational studies. Moreover, there is a lack of independent test data. To address this as best as possible, we have used three forms of validation: expert-curated clinical summaries, functional assays, and computational scores. Additionally, we also acknowledge there is potential circularity in using functional or computational scores for validation if that information might be included in submission summaries. We have attempted to mitigate this by evaluating a recently published functional dataset in our experimental validation, and by evaluating AlphaMissense scores restricted to variant summaries that were last updated coincident with the publication of those scores. Notwithstanding, these scores are correlated with other existing computational estimates of pathogenicity, which could still potentially confound results.

Given that these models were trained on many forms of clinical evidence, this complicates their calibration and use when following the existing classification guidelines. Model performance is contingent on the quality and representativeness of the clinical reports available within ClinVar. Class imbalance, particularly in the B/LB category, poses a challenge that must continue to be addressed. Continual improvement of model architectures, training strategies, and dataset diversity is needed to enhance model robustness and generalizability.

Conclusions

In conclusion, by training language models to discern evidence of pathogenicity from unstructured clinical text, we have introduced a novel approach to prioritize variants for expert review. This research promises to allow clinicians to leverage expert-curated information that is currently prohibitively complicated to use at scale. This information should enable VCEPs to classify a larger proportion of variants as pathogenic or benign, allowing more patients to learn about clinically actionable findings. This could advance genomic medicine to the large number of patients who collectively harbor a VUS, potentially improving their clinical management.
