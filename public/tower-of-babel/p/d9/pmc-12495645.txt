Development and validation of a predictive nomogram for antenatal depression in China
Authors: Renjie Yu, Luyang Guan, Hongbao Chen, Jun Zhang, Xiaohong Li, Shengying Zhang, Jia Liu, Peiyun Zhang, Linghua Kong, Lanlan Zhang, Tingting Jin, Ju Gao, Xiaobin Zhang, Yong Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntenatal depression is a significant public health concern, with detrimental effects on maternal and infant health. However, there is currently a lack of comprehensive predictive tools specifically tailored for antenatal depression in China. The present study aimed to develop and validate a predictive nomogram for antenatal depression in the Chinese population.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in multiple healthcare settings in Suzhou, Jiangsu Province, China, spanning from March 2017 to December 2019. A total of 3694 pregnant women were included in the study. Demographic and clinical characteristics were collected using structured questionnaires. The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale and various psychological assessments were used to assess antenatal depression and associated factors. LASSO regression and logistic regression analyses were employed to develop the predictive model, and internal validation was performed to assess its performance.ResultsAmong the 3694 participants, 473 (12.8%) pregnant women were positive for antenatal depression. The developed predictive Model incorporated neuroticism, negative coping strategies, and anxiety as significant predictors. The nomogram demonstrated accurate risk assessment capabilities, with an AUC of 0.90 in the validation set. The model exhibited good calibration and clinical utility.LimitationsLimitations of this paper include the cross-sectional design, biases like self-reporting and non-randomized sampling, highlighting the need for future longitudinal studies and diverse population validation to enhance the model's robustness in varied sociocultural contexts.ConclusionThe predictive model for antenatal depression in the Chinese population provides a valuable tool for early detection, intervention, and personalized care for pregnant women.Keywords: Antenatal depression, Edinburgh postpartum depression scale, Nomogram, Prediction models

Introduction

Antenatal depression, characterized by a spectrum of depressive episodes ranging from mild to severe, represents a significant public health concern due to its prevalence and detrimental impact on maternal and infant health. Despite pregnancy being generally viewed as a positive experience, women are at an increased vulnerability to depression during and after pregnancy [1]. Evidence suggests that two-thirds of postnatal depression occurs during the antenatal period [2, 3]. Therefore, targeting the antenatal phase with screening and interventions becomes crucial. The global incidence of antenatal depression is estimated to range between 10 and 20% [4], with substantial variability observed across different socioeconomic contexts, specifically indicating higher prevalence rates in low- and middle-income countries than in high-income countries [5, 6]. In China, the prevalence of antenatal depression varies widely and has shown an upward trend in recent years, influenced by factors such as rapid urbanization, high societal expectations, and gaps in mental health services.

The adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes associated with antenatal depression are multifaceted and far-reaching. Maternal complications encompass an elevated risk of gestational diabetes and preterm birth [7, 8], long-term mental health implications including postpartum depression, and the potential impairment of mother-infant bonding [9]. Neonatal consequences involve a propensity for developmental delays, low birth weight, and compromised cognitive and emotional functioning [10–12]. Moreover, the impact of antenatal depression can persist into childhood, facilitating behavioral issues and an elevated risk of psychiatric disorders [13, 14].

Early detection and intervention for antenatal depression are crucial to mitigate these adverse outcomes. However, the identification of susceptible women remains challenging, as symptoms are often misattributed to typical pregnancy changes, resulting in undiagnosed and untreated cases [15]. A considerable percentage of individuals with antenatal depression, ranging from 32 to 57%, were observed to be inadequately identified within a timely manner [16]. Moreover, the prevalence of limited awareness concerning depression, coupled with social and cultural factors, contributes to the fact that only one-third of women who exhibit positive results in depression screening receive subsequent psychological counseling [17]. Consequently, there is a pressing need for efficacious screening and risk assessment tools to facilitate early intervention. Predictive models, such as nomograms, which graphically represent the multivariate probability of a specific outcome, have recently emerged as a popular tool for disease prediction in various medical fields [18]. Within the context of antenatal depression, such a predictive nomogram could offer a pragmatic approach for healthcare professionals to identify high-risk women and allocate resources for early intervention optimally.

In this study, we aim to develop and validate a predictive nomogram for antenatal depression within the Chinese population. Previous systematic reviews have established a range of risk factors, including lack of social support, history of abuse or domestic violence, lower income levels, personal history of mental illness, specific personality traits, unplanned or unwanted pregnancy, pregnancy complications, adverse life events, and high perceived stress [19, 20]. By incorporating these demographic, clinical, and psychosocial factors into a nomogram tailored for the Chinese population, we aim to provide a practical tool for estimating antenatal depression risk. The study’s findings will contribute to the development of personalized care plans and interventions, enabling early detection, treatment, and support for pregnant women at risk of antenatal depression in China.

Methods

Study design and setting

This research utilized a cross-sectional study design across multiple healthcare settings, including antenatal clinics, Maternity hospitals, and community health centers. The data collection period was from March 2017 to December 2019 in Suzhou, Jiangsu Province, China. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of the Affiliated Guangji Hospital of Soochow University (Approval No. 2016010). Informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study. Confidentiality and privacy of participants’ data were strictly maintained throughout the research process.

In order to estimate the appropriate sample size for our study, we reviewed previous research on the prevalence of our target condition in the relevant population. Although the prevalence of antenatal depression varies across studies worldwide, typically ranging between 10% and 20% [21], there is a lack of precise estimates specific to our target population. Consequently, we conservatively assumed a prevalence of 15% for sample size calculation. Using a desired margin of error (d) of 5% and a confidence level of 95% (Z = 1.96), we calculated that a sample size of approximately 196 participants would be required to achieve sufficient statistical power for our study.

Participant selection and inclusion criteria

Pregnant women from the Chinese population, regardless of gestational age, were eligible for inclusion in the study. The age range for participants was set between 18 and 40 years to ensure a specific demographic representation. Additionally, to enhance the model’s generalizability and relevance in the targeted region, the study exclusively included individuals from the Chinese Han ethnic group. Participants were required to have the ability to comprehend the study’s purpose, procedures, and potential risks, and provide informed consent voluntarily through written documentation before enrollment. Exclusion criteria encompassed individuals with a history of mental illness or current psychotic disorders, as well as those with severe underlying physical illnesses.

Data collection and measures

Data collection was carried out through face-to-face interviews with participants using structured questionnaires. Trained research personnel collected demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, educational level, occupation, gestational age, medical history, and obstetric history. The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), a widely validated and reliable instrument, served as the primary tool for screening antenatal depression. Additionally, various factors associated with antenatal depression, including personality traits, anxiety symptoms, social support, and coping strategies were assessed. These measures were selected to capture a comprehensive range of factors that may influence the risk of antenatal depression. Specifically, the measurement tools used in this study included the EPDS, Eysenck Personality Questionnaire (EPQ), General Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7), Social Support Rating Scale (SSRS), and Trait Coping Style Questionnaire (TCSQ).

EPDS

The EPDS is the Most common and acceptable screening tool for identifying depressive symptoms in the perinatal period worldwide, with a reported sensitivity of 0.68–0.86 and specificity of 0.78–0.96 [22, 23]. It consists of 10 items that evaluate various aspects including mood, optimism, self-blame, anxiety, fear, coping ability, sleep, sadness, crying, and self-harm [24]. In this study, pregnant women were categorized into two groups, antenatal depression groups and non-antenatal depression, based on whether their total score on the EPDS was greater than or equal to 13, a widely accepted cutoff in recent studies focused on antenatal populations. We selected the EPDS over general depression tools like the PHQ-9 because the EPDS was specifically developed for perinatal populations and avoids confounding by somatic symptoms that are common during pregnancy but not necessarily indicative of depression [25]. Several studies have shown that EPDS demonstrates higher sensitivity (up to 86%) and better specificity (up to 78%) in detecting perinatal depression compared to the PHQ-9 [26], especially in distinguishing varying levels of depressive severity. International clinical guidelines also recommend EPDS as the preferred screening tool for antenatal and postnatal depression due to its superior diagnostic accuracy in this population [27]. The Chinese version of the EPDS has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties, with a Cronbach’s α of 0.87–0.91 and an AUC of 0.91 [24].

EPQ

The EPQ is employed to assess the personality traits of pregnant women. This questionnaire primarily examines personality traits based on three dimensions: extraversion (E), neuroticism (N), and psychoticism (P). It assesses factors such as introversion/extroversion, self-control, and adaptability to the environment [28]. Despite the existence of newer personality assessment tools, the EPQ remains one of the most widely used instruments in China due to its strong reliability, concise format, and established clinical relevance. Other personality inventories, such as the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI), the NEO Personality Inventory-Revised, or the Big Five Inventory, either lack cultural adaptation, are too lengthy for clinical settings, or show limited applicability in perinatal populations [29–31]. The Chinese version of the EPQ shows good reliability (split-half reliability = 0.68–0.81; test–retest reliability = 0.83–0.90) and acceptable construct validity (correlation with MMPI subscale: r = 0.52) [32].

GAD-7

The GAD-7 is utilized to evaluate the level of anxiety in pregnant women. This questionnaire employs a 4-point scoring system, with scores ranging from 0 to 4 indicating no anxiety disorder, 5 to 9 indicating mild anxiety, 10 to 14 indicating moderate anxiety, and ≥ 15 indicating severe anxiety [33]. Higher scores indicate more pronounced symptoms of anxiety. The Chinese version of the GAD-7 has been validated in large Chinese populations, demonstrating strong internal consistency (α = 0.908), test–retest reliability (r = 0.856), and good construct validity (CFI = 0.98, RMSEA = 0.07) [34].

SSRS

The SSRS is a widely used instrument designed to assess an individual’s perceived level of social support. It measures the degree of social support individuals perceive when facing difficulties or stressors. The scale encompasses various dimensions, including family support, friend support, and professional support [35]. Each dimension consists of several items that capture different aspects of social support. The Chinese version demonstrates excellent reliability (α = 0.89–0.94) and a well-validated three-factor structure [36].

TCSQ

The TCSQ is employed to assess individual traits and coping styles related to physical and mental health in pregnant women. It consists of 16 items that evaluate positive coping (PC) and negative coping (NC), with each dimension containing 8 items. Higher scores indicate more prominent characteristics of positive or negative coping styles [37]. The Chinese version shows good reliability (α = 0.75–0.82) and validity (CFI = 0.91), and has been applied in Chinese studies on stress and psychological health [38].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using R software (version 4.0.3) in this study. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. In this study, pregnant women were divided into training and validation sets in an 8:2 ratio for subsequent internal validation, following common practice in predictive modeling and prior experience in similar studies [39]. Baseline comparisons confirmed no significant differences in key demographic and clinical variables between the two sets. To identify the most relevant predictors of antenatal depression, we employed the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, a regularization technique that effectively reduces overfitting by penalizing the absolute size of regression coefficients. This method is particularly suitable for high-dimensional data with potentially correlated variables. The optimal value of the regularization parameter (lambda) was selected using 10-fold cross-validation to ensure model stability. The variables selected by LASSO were then entered into a multivariate logistic regression model to construct the predictive nomogram. Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility. Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), while calibration was evaluated through calibration plots and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Finally, clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis (DCA), which quantifies the net benefit of the predictive model across a range of threshold probabilities. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Participant characteristics

In this study, a total of 3833 pregnant women were initially included. However, due to some missing data, the sample loss rate was 3.6%, resulting in a final sample size of 3694 pregnant women. Of these participants, 473 (12.8%; 95% CI: 11.7–14.2) were found to have a total EPDS score above 13. Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of all pregnant women in both the training and validation sets. The training set consisted of 2955 participants, while the validation set included 739 participants. The mean age of participants in the training set was 28.6 ± 3.8 years, and in the validation set, it was 28.5 ± 3.8 years (t = 1.06, P = 0.77). The average education level, gestational weeks, hometown distribution, only child status, comorbidities during pregnancy, and pre-pregnancy and family annual income showed no significant differences between the training and validation sets (all P > 0.05). The psychological and emotional characteristics of participants, as measured by various scales (EPQ-P, EPQ-E, EPQ-N, EPQ-L, SSRS, TCSQ-PC, TCSQ-NC, and GAD-7), did not show significant differences between the training and validation sets (all P > 0.05).

Development of prediction model

The development of the prediction model involved a comprehensive statistical analysis and model-building process. Initially, a univariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify potential predictive factors for antenatal depression, considering variables such as age, education level, occupation, gestational weeks, medical history, and obstetric history. Subsequently, LASSO regression analysis was employed to select the most relevant predictors from Table 2, and a multivariate logistic regression Model was constructed. Among the 19 original variables, three factors—EPQ-N (OR = 1.04, 95% CI: 1.03–1.05), TCSQ-NC (OR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.06–1.11), and GAD-7 (OR = 1.33, 95% CI: 1.27–1.39). These results are shown in Fig. 1(a) and Fig. 1(b) and are detailed in Table 3. To examine the independent predictive value of EPQ-N and TCSQ-NC, a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding GAD-7 from the Model. The ORs of EPQ-N and TCSQ-NC changed by less than 5% (EPQ-N: 1.04 → 1.03; TCSQ-NC: 1.08 → 1.05), indicating their independent associations with antenatal depression. These predictors, along with their corresponding weights, were used to construct the predictive model. The nomogram in Fig. 1(c) visually represents the contribution of each predictor to the probability of developing antenatal depression.

Validation of the predictive model

To validate the performance of the prediction Model, we internally validated it with a validation set of 739 pregnant women, which constituted 20% of the total sample. We assessed the predictive model’s performance in identifying pregnant women at risk for antenatal depression using statistical methods, including sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value. In the validation set, the AUC was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.86–0.93), with a sensitivity of 0.76 and specificity of 0.91. These results demonstrate that the nomogram exhibits diagnostic capability above the moderate level, as showed in Fig. 1(d). The predictive Model was effectively calibrated using a calibration plot and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. The calibration curve indicates a close-to-ideal slope of 1, affirming the model’s excellent fit. Moreover, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test yielded a p-value of 0.165, signifying a high level of agreement between the model’s predictions and actual observations, as shown in Fig. 1(e). The decision curve analysis in Fig. 1(f) provides valuable insight into the threshold probabilities of the predictive Model. This indicates that the Model has a higher net benefit rate and a broader range of applicability within the risk threshold range of 1%−64%, achieving good net benefits.

Discussion

The present study contributes to the understanding of antenatal depression in the Chinese population by providing valuable insights into its prevalence and developing a predictive Model. The high prevalence rate of 12.8% highlights the urgent need for effective strategies to address antenatal depression in China. The developed predictive model exhibited robust performance in accurately identifying pregnant women at risk of developing antenatal depression. Notably, neuroticism, negative coping strategies, and anxiety emerged as significant predictors in the model.

The estimated prevalence of antenatal depression varies between studies. In high-income countries, antenatal depression is estimated to be between 7% and 20% [40–44]. Conversely, low- and middle-income countries have reported rates of 20% or more, although limited research has been conducted in these areas [45–49]. For example, in Bangladesh, a developing country, the prevalence rate is approximately 18% [13], while in Australia, a developed country, it is around 7% [10]. Notably, Gavin et al. found that antenatal depression prevalence in the first trimester is 11.0%, which then drops to 8.5% in the second and third trimesters [16]. In contrast, Bennett et al. found an opposite trend, with a prevalence of 7.4% during the first trimester, 12.8% during the second, and 12% during the third trimester [50]. Understanding the prevalence rate of antenatal depression in China is crucial, as it emphasizes the need for effective interventions and support systems. Previous studies have reported a range of 5.3–28.5% for the prevalence of antenatal depression among Chinese pregnant women [51, 52], indicating a substantial population affected by antenatal depression in China. Moreover, the prevalence of antenatal depression has shown a significant increasing trend in the last decade [53], highlighting the need for increased attention to the mental and psychological state of pregnant women during the antenatal period. Our study revealed that approximately 12.8% of pregnant women in our sample experienced antenatal depression, providing insights into its prevalence in the Chinese population. Although the age difference between the antenatal depression group and the non-depressed group was Modest, it was statistically significant, which aligns with prior studies showing that younger Maternal age is associated with higher risk of antenatal depression. For example, a study found that women under 25 had significantly higher depression risk due to financial stress and lack of parenting experience [54]. Similarly, another report indicated that younger age groups in China faced increased vulnerability to perinatal depression, especially when coupled with low social support [53]. Comparing with other Chinese cities, Suzhou had a relatively lower prevalence attributed to higher socioeconomic status, lower financial pressures, and better education [55].

Our predictive model includes EPQ-N, TCSQ-NC, and GAD-7 as predictors, supported by their relevance to antenatal depression and the existing literature. Several studies have demonstrated that certain personality factors increase the risk of experiencing psychological difficulties during the antenatal period, particularly negative cognitive styles such as pessimism, anger, and rumination. Additionally, individuals with a tendency to be nervous, worried, or shy, along with low self-esteem and self-efficacy, and high levels of neuroticism or psychoticism, are more susceptible to antenatal depression [9, 41, 52]56– [60]. On the contrary, active coping and high self-esteem/self-efficacy have been identified as protective factors [52, 61]. Our findings supported previous research, demonstrating that pregnant women with antenatal depression scored higher on EPQ-N, indicating a significant difference compared to the non-antenatal depression group [52]. This result is consistent with previous studies and suggests that individuals with depression are more likely to exhibit stronger neuroticism personality traits [56]. Neuroticism is a personality trait associated with emotional instability, anxiety, irritability, and emotional sensitivity. Women with higher levels of neuroticism tend to be more susceptible to anxiety and depression [28]. However, we acknowledge that administering a full personality inventory such as the EPQ to all pregnant women may not be feasible in real-world clinical settings due to its length and complexity. Notably, our findings suggest that the EPQ-N subscale alone is the most predictive component in the model. Future studies could explore the development or validation of a brief screening version focusing exclusively on EPQ-N, which may improve the practicality and efficiency of large-scale risk assessment. This may be attributed to the physiological and psychological changes they experience during pregnancy, such as hormonal fluctuations and anxiety about impending parental responsibilities. Furthermore, our study found that pregnant women with antenatal depression tended to adopt negative coping strategies, as evidenced by significantly higher TCSQ scores compared to the non-antenatal depression group. These maladaptive coping strategies may further exacerbate symptoms and discomfort associated with antenatal depression. Previous research has shown that negative coping strategies are unfavorable for pregnant women in managing their physiological conditions, and negative thoughts can increase stress levels during pregnancy [62, 63]. This can contribute to confusion, negative pessimism, and even an increased risk of suicidal ideation among pregnant women [64]. Lastly, our study revealed a significant association between antenatal depression and higher levels of anxiety among women. High anxiety during pregnancy has been identified as one of the strongest risk factors for depression, as supported by previous studies [20, 65]. Women experiencing feelings of anxiety face an increased risk of developing depression during pregnancy [61]. For instance, a study reported that women with antenatal anxiety were approximately three times more likely to experience depression during pregnancy [66]. By incorporating these predictors into the model, we aimed to capture the multifaceted nature of antenatal depression and provide healthcare professionals with a valuable tool for identifying pregnant women at higher risk.

The predictive model developed in this study exhibits robust discriminative ability, precision, and consistency in forecasting the risk of antenatal depression in pregnant women, demonstrating moderate to above-average diagnostic performance. We randomly divided the dataset into training and validation subsets and confirmed that the two sets were comparable in terms of key baseline characteristics. The model demonstrated similarly strong performance in the validation set, supporting its stability and internal generalizability. Previous research has predominantly focused on general demographic factors or limited psychological factors of pregnant women, lacking a comprehensive analysis of multiple determinants [13, 67]. By learning and identifying patterns and characteristics related to antenatal depression from a large dataset, our model offers a comprehensive and accurate risk assessment, facilitating the development of targeted interventions. It is still inconvenient for people in rural China to seek medical treatment for psychological illness, as a result of a severe lack of medical resources [68]. What makes it worse, since the stigma towards people with psychological problems still exists, some depressed pregnant women might hesitate to seek treatment and assistance. Additionally, in order to avoid the risk of medication, some Asian pregnant women are more hesitant to take antidepressants such as SSRIs [69]. Given the challenges of antenatal depression identification, particularly for obstetricians, the establishment of a predictive model empowers healthcare professionals to identify high-risk individuals before the onset of depressive symptoms, enabling timely interventions and support. In real-world clinical settings, the nomogram can serve as a simple and intuitive tool that integrates easily into routine antenatal care workflows. Healthcare providers can calculate individual risk scores using readily available psychological assessments (e.g., GAD-7 and EPQ-N), allowing early recognition of women who may benefit from psychological counseling or monitoring. This alleviates the psychological burden on pregnant women and prevents or mitigates the occurrence of antenatal depression. The construction of a nomogram enables an understanding of the impact of various factors on antenatal depression, serving as a foundation for personalized intervention and treatment plans. In addition, the decision curve analysis demonstrated that the model offers a net clinical benefit across a wide range of risk thresholds (1–64%), supporting its applicability for population-level screening and individualized decision-making. This strengthens its potential for use not only by mental health professionals but also by frontline obstetricians, nurses, and public health workers. However, it is worth noting that a wide range of demographic factors were initially considered during model development but were ultimately excluded from the final predictive model. This exclusion was motivated by their minimal contributions to predicting the risk of antenatal depression and potential correlations with other crucial factors. Incorporating numerous demographic factors could escalate model complexity and potentially lead to overfitting issues. Consequently, these demographic factors were omitted from the final predictive model to ensure simplicity, feasibility of implementation, and generalizability stability. This decision aimed to enhance the model’s predictive performance and provide practical, actionable outcomes for healthcare professionals to guide timely interventions and support measures.

The study has several advantages worth considering. The use of a large sample size enhances the robustness and generalizability of the findings, contributing to more reliable conclusions. Additionally, the inclusion of diverse demographic and clinical characteristics increases the representativeness of the study population, making the results more applicable to a broader context. However, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations in the interpretation of the study findings. First, the cross-sectional design restricts the establishment of causal relationships between predictors and antenatal depression. Longitudinal research would allow for the investigation of how these predictors may change over time and affect the risk of antenatal depression, providing more insight into the dynamic nature of the condition. Second, the sample was recruited exclusively from Suzhou, Jiangsu Province, China, and comprised only Han Chinese pregnant women. While this enhances internal consistency, it limits the generalizability of the findings. Future research should aim to validate the nomogram in more diverse populations across different regions and sociocultural contexts. Third, although the sample size was sufficiently large, it was calculated based on the estimated prevalence of antenatal depression rather than specific effect sizes or risk ratios. Future studies should consider stratified or power-based sampling designs to optimize predictive performance. Fourth, the use of the EPDS as the reference standard may introduce limitations, as it is a screening rather than a diagnostic tool. Incorporating diagnostic interviews conducted by mental health professionals in future studies would improve the precision of outcome classification. Finally, potential sources of bias, such as self-reporting bias and non-randomized sampling methods, should be considered as they may impact the validity of the nomogram. Despite these limitations, the predictive nomogram offers a practical and feasible tool for early identification of antenatal depression risk. Integrating it into routine prenatal care may enable timely psychological support and personalized intervention strategies. Further prospective, multicenter, and cost-effectiveness studies are warranted to confirm its clinical utility and long-term impact.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study developed and validated a predictive model for antenatal depression in a Chinese population. The nomogram demonstrated accurate risk assessment capabilities, providing a valuable tool for early detection, intervention, and personalized care for pregnant women. By identifying the prevalence and predictors of antenatal depression, this study contributes to the understanding of the condition and its management. The predictive model has the potential to improve antenatal depression management in China, leading to better outcomes for both maternal and infant health.
