Widespread ST segment elevation and elevated troponin: unveiling an uncommon cause
Authors: Fei Wang, Ziyi Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This article describes a case involving a patient who presented with chest pain persisting for three days, elevated levels of cardiac troponin, and widespread ST segment elevation in the ECG leads. Subsequent diagnosis revealed esophageal perforation resulting from the accidental ingestion of a fish bone, leading to pericardial effusion and pneumopericardium. This case highlights the importance of healthcare professionals conducting a comprehensive investigation of the patient’s medical history when confronted with widespread ST segment elevation and elevated cardiac troponin levels to avoid misdiagnosis and mitigate potential serious consequences.

Case presentation

An 82-year-old male patient was admitted on January 20, 2021, with a three-day history of intermittent chest pain. The pain was described as stabbing in nature, located retrosternally, and radiating to the shoulder, with exacerbation following meals. The patient also experienced mild diaphoresis during these episodes. His medical history was notable for hypertension, with no documented history of coronary artery disease or diabetes mellitus. Although he was an occasional smoker, he reported no alcohol consumption. Upon admission, his blood pressure was measured at 136/86 mmHg. The patient was alert and oriented, and pulmonary auscultation findings were normal. Cardiovascular examination revealed slight cardiomegaly, a heart rate of 99 beats per minute with a regular rhythm, and no murmurs or additional heart sounds. There was no hepatomegaly or evidence of peripheral edema, and bilateral dorsalis pedis pulses were palpable. The admission electrocardiogram (ECG) showed diffuse ST segment elevation (Fig. 1). The laboratory findings indicated increased cardiac troponin I levels at 2.475 ng/mL (reference range < 0.01 ng/mL) and myoglobin at 56.78 ng/mL (reference range 0–18 ng/mL). The subsequent day’s measurement of cardiac troponin I remained elevated. Given the suspicion of acute myocardial infarction, a cardiology consultation was requested, and a comprehensive medical history was obtained. The patient reported that the onset of chest pain coincided with the consumption of fish and worsened thereafter, despite the administration of sublingual nitroglycerin. A mechanical injury leading to elevated cardiac biomarkers was suspected. An emergency chest CT scan confirmed the presence of a transmural high-density lesion in the esophagus penetrating the pericardium, accompanied by pneumopericardium and pericardial effusion. Contrast enhancement revealed no enhancement of the esophageal wall, corroborating the diagnosis of esophageal foreign body perforation into the pericardium (Fig. 2). Emergency surgical intervention, involving thoracoscopy and open thoracotomy, was conducted to remove two sharp fish bone fragments identified intraoperatively. The pericardial cavity was thoroughly irrigated, and hemostasis was achieved. Continuous pericardial and pleural drainage was maintained postoperatively. Subsequently, the patient’s chest pain resolved, and follow-up examinations demonstrated improvement in pericardial effusion and ECG findings (Fig. 3). Discussion

The patient initially presented with chest pain as the primary complaint, accompanied by diffuse ST segment elevation on the electrocardiogram and elevated cardiac troponin levels, indicative of acute myocardial infarction. However, a comprehensive review of the patient’s medical history revealed that the onset of chest pain coincided with the ingestion of a fish bone. Despite the preliminary suspicion of myocardial infarction, subsequent evaluation via chest computed tomography (CT) scan in the emergency department confirmed that the myocardial injury and abnormal electrocardiographic findings were attributable to pericardial penetration resulting from the ingestion of the fish bone.

Although the patient’s initial presentation resembled the classic symptoms of acute myocardial infarction, several distinguishing features set this case apart. Firstly, the chest pain manifested postprandially and intensified with food intake, which deviated from the typical symptomatology of myocardial infarction. Secondly, while the electrocardiogram demonstrated widespread ST segment elevation, it did not exhibit the characteristic convex upward “tombstone” pattern or the development of Q waves, which are critical in differentiating pericardial effusion-induced ST segment changes from those associated with myocardial infarction. Furthermore, cardiac biomarker levels remained consistently low, in contrast to the expected elevation observed in myocardial infarction cases. Lastly, whereas myocardial infarction commonly presents with lead selectivity and reciprocal ST segment changes, the patient’s electrocardiogram revealed diffuse ST segment changes across multiple leads, providing further distinction from acute myocardial infarction.

Esophageal perforation represents a frequent and severe complication associated with the ingestion of foreign bodies into the esophagus [1]. Foreign bodies lodged in the esophagus, which resist removal and remain within the esophageal tract, are termed esophageal foreign bodies. These instances are frequently encountered in emergency departments. Predominantly, esophageal foreign bodies are situated in the upper and middle segments of the esophagus, with the upper segment being the most prevalent site. Conversely, fewer than 10% of patients present with esophageal foreign bodies located in the lower segment [2]. In this instance, the fish bone became lodged in the lower segment of the esophagus. In China, around 4,000 cases of esophageal foreign bodies are reported annually [3]. However, instances of esophageal foreign bodies perforating the pericardium are infrequent and seldom reported. Esophageal foreign body perforations typically occur in the lower segment of the esophagus, directly penetrating the pericardium and causing pericardial injury. However, the occurrence of pericardial effusion and pneumopericardium is rare. While endoscopic removal is the recommended approach according to guidelines in this case, the foreign body was long and had been retained for over 24 h, significantly heightening the risk of complications. Additionally, the foreign body had already perforated the esophagus, rendering endoscopy less suitable as the initial treatment option [4]. For longer and sharper esophageal foreign bodies prone to migration within the esophagus, endoscopic treatment carries a higher risk and may lead to secondary injury [5]. Following a comprehensive evaluation, the patient underwent thoracoscopic surgery. Initial thoracoscopic exploration revealed a hematoma, indicative of myocardial puncture. The damaged organs, including the esophagus and pericardium, were repaired, and active drainage was initiated. Post-surgical treatment resulted in normalization of the patient’s electrocardiogram and a gradual decrease in cardiac biomarkers, leading to subsequent recovery. This case is both unusual and instructive, particularly for practitioners in emergency medicine, internal medicine, and cardiology. It highlights the importance of considering esophageal foreign bodies as a potential etiology for chest pain temporally related to eating, even in the presence of elevated troponin levels.

Conclusion

In conclusion, although esophageal injuries resulting from foreign bodies are relatively prevalent, instances of direct pericardial penetration are infrequently documented. This case report is of considerable importance, as the patient initially presented with chest pain and elevated cardiac troponin levels, which could have been misinterpreted as indicative of acute myocardial infarction. Consequently, this report highlights the necessity for meticulous evaluation and comprehensive medical history assessment in patients presenting with diffuse ST-segment elevation and chest pain. It is imperative to exercise caution against an over-reliance on auxiliary examination findings for diagnosis, in order to prevent misdiagnosis and reduce the risk of potentially life-threatening outcomes.
