Thyroid disease detection using enhanced extreme learning machine based on drop-connect method
Authors: Aisha Riaz, Fazli Wahid, Sikandar Ali, Salman Jan, It Ee Lee, Amina Salhi, Arij Alfaidi, Ahmed Alkhayyat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe long-term physiologic effects of thyroid problems make them one of the most important endocrine disorders. Even if a lot of machine learning and deep learning techniques have been presented out for the early detection of thyroid disease, it is still difficult to achieve reliable and clinically accurate multi-class diagnostic performance. In this work, we suggest an Enhanced Extreme Learning Machine (EELM) that uses Drop-Connect regularization to enhance generalization and reduce over-fitting that is frequently seen in traditional ELM models. The pipeline for the suggested framework consists of seven steps: data preprocessing, model building, training, and evaluation. To simulate a clinically relevant diagnostic scenario, the model was assessed on a unified four-class thyroid classification task (hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, sick-euthyroid, and normal). The suggested EELM demonstrated steady and reliable multi-class performance with an average accuracy of approximately 82% under 10-fold cross-validation. The model achieved up to 99.89% accuracy in comparative binary classification studies (e.g., hypothyroid vs. normal), indicating the better division of some thyroid diseases. Accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, sensitivity, F1-score, ROC, and AUC measures were used to evaluate performance. The suggested method’s robustness and significance were validated statistically using ANOVA and paired t-tests. Significant improvements over baseline models were confirmed by statistical validation with paired t-tests and ANOVA (p < 0.05). Overall, the findings show that the suggested EELM offers a clinically applicable, statistically supported, and computationally effective method for classifying thyroid diseases.Keywords: Machine learning, ELM, Thyroid disease, Disease diagnosis, ClassifierSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Diseases, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

One of the body’s most vital organs is the thyroid gland. It generates thyroid hormone, which controls the metabolism of the body. The second most prevalent endocrine problem is thyroid disease, which is lethal if ignored. An endocrine gland in the form of a butterfly, the thyroid is located close to the base of the neck1. On the front of the windpipe, it could be located beneath Adam’s apple. Pituitary gland releases Thyrotropin, commonly known as thyroid-stimulating hormones, is used to stimulate the thyroid gland’s production of thyroid hormones1. The 2 thyroid hormones that are active in the thyroid gland are thyroxin (abbreviated as T4) and triiodothyronine (referred to as “T3”)1. Thyroid hormones maintain the body’s temperature, heartbeat, and metabolism. Thyroid Hormones also help to burn calories and digest proteins1. The thyroid gland uses iodine from our food to prepare T3 and T4. The iodine imbalance in intake produces problems and results in thyroid disorders2. Many adverse effects can arise in individuals who are suffering from thyroid disease. Women with thyroid disorders may experience weight (gain/loss) variations and fertility problems2.

Hypothyroidism and Hyperthyroidism are the two most-frequent thyroid conditions. Thyroid hormone production is reduced in hypothyroidism, whereas thyroid hormone production is increased in hyperthyroidism1,3,9. Anxiety, agitation, hyperactivity, sweating or feeling hot, tremor in the hands (shaking), hair loss, and lighter or absent menstrual cycles are all symptoms of hypothyroidism. Sleep issues, exhaustion, difficulty concentrating, dry skin and hair, depression, sensitivity to low temperatures, frequent and heavy periods, and joint and muscle stiffness are all symptoms of hyperthyroidism3,4. The level of hormones generated by the thyroid gland is significantly lower in euthyroid (thyroid glands are functioning normally) individuals with transient illness. Euthyroid refers to a thyroid gland that is working normally (negative)2.

Motivation

Thyroid illness is a common ailment in today’s society and can have severe consequences for one’s health and well-being. Thyroid disorder causes the body to go into auto-saving mode because hormones are produced that pulverize the thyroid organs. Because this condition permanently affects the body, it is critical to avoid it. Since it is challenging to cure this disease after it has progressed to its ultimate stages, prevention of this disease necessitates an understanding of its occurrence4. According to the research, congenital hypothyroidism affects one out of every 38000 people on the planet6. Thyroid diseases affect more than 300 million people globally, with female patients accounting for almost 610 times as many as male patients and females over 40 accounting for roughly 10% to 20% of the total. One in every 100 Americans, or 1.2 percent, had hyperthyroidism, according to another research. Approximately 4.6 percent of individuals over 12 have hypothyroidism, according to the same study7. By automating the classification of medical data, recent developments in artificial intelligence, including machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL), have improved disease detection10. However, when applied to tiny or noisy medical datasets, current ML models like Support Vector Machines (SVM), Decision Trees, and conventional Extreme Learning Machines (ELM) frequently experience over-fitting, limited generalization, and sensitivity to data imbalance. DL models are more accurate, but their use in many healthcare contexts is limited by their need for huge, labeled datasets and powerful computers.

Motivated by these limitations, this study proposes an Enhanced Extreme Learning Machine (EELM) integrated with a drop connect regularization mechanism to achieve a balance between computational efficiency and predictive accuracy. The Drop Connect approach randomly removes neuron connections during training, effectively reducing over-fitting and improving generalization performance. By leveraging this method, the proposed system aims to enhance early diagnosis of thyroid disorders, providing a reliable and cost-efficient solution for clinical use.

Manual diagnosis methods

There are many manual detection methods for diagnosing thyroid diseases. By looking at the thyroid gland, a patient-doctor can determine the location, size, form, surface condition, degree of mobility, and symmetry of the gland during swallowing6. After this, doctor recommend any manual detection method like Clinical or laboratory method (blood tests) or Instrumental method (ultrasound, radiological, pathomorphological) for checking Thyroid level6. These manual thyroid detection methods are very time-consuming and have variations in results that affect performance accuracy. Safety measures, storage, specimen collection, handling procedures such as preparation of reagents, criteria for specimen rejection, controls, calibration (standards), and all other materials such as equipment and instrumentation are all involved in manual detection8. These procedures are very time-consuming, risky, and complex. Also, the accuracy of manual detection methods is very low compared to Artificial Intelligence detection methods, so we can say Artificial Intelligence detection methods resolve that manual detection method limitations. Artificial intelligence currently solves a significant number of problems in the development of professional systems for the diagnosis of different types of defects with high precision54.

Diagnosis techniques

Many ML techniques2,18 such as SVMs1,4,6,13,15,51; ANN1,18; MLP,14,16; Logistic Regression5,6,13,15,52; Multi-Linear Regression20; Random Forest6,11,14,19,45; Decision Tree3,6,12,13,19; K-Nearest Neighbor (KNNs)12,49, Nave Bayes12,13,49 and Fuzzy Logic19 are used for thyroid disease detection, while Deep Learning techniques21–23 such as (CNNs)2,24,29,53, (LSTMs)22, Neural Networks27, GAN and KAN 54, Hybrid CNN-LSTM55, (RNNs)25, Restricted Boltzmann Machines( RBMs)26, Auto-encoders26,28, Generative Adversarial Networks30 and Radial Basis Function (RBF)33. Recent developments in deep learning architectures have shown impressive results in a number of medical imaging fields. To illustrate the potential of customized neural network designs in healthcare applications, Abdelhamid et al.57 used hybrid vision transformers to obtain notable gains in pulmonary embolism diagnosis. These advancements highlight the larger trend toward task-specific architectures that strike a balance between accuracy and computational efficiency a concept that drives our DropConnect-regularized EELM framework even though our work focuses on numerical laboratory data rather than imaging.

Contributions of the study

The proposed research contributes to the early and precise diagnosis of thyroid conditions in both a scientific and practical way. By offering a dependable and computationally effective diagnostic tool that can diagnose several thyroid diseases at once, it helps the medical community save time for both patients and professionals. The suggested framework is intended to classify several thyroid problems within a single model, in contrast to the majority of earlier research that concentrated on just three types of thyroid conditions (hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, and normal). This more extensive classification capability facilitates more thorough diagnostic coverage and improves clinical decision-making.

This work presents an EELM combined with the Drop-connect regularization method at the methodological level. Although Huang’s traditional ELM effectively uses a least squares approach to train single hidden layer feed-forward neural networks, it frequently suffers from over-fitting and restricted generalization when applied to medical data. By decreasing co-adaptation between neurons, increasing model robustness, and randomly eliminating neural connections during training, the integration of Drop-connect overcomes these difficulties.

Related work

Thyroid cancer has been detected using a variety of ML and DL approaches. Some of the most recent work in this dimension is discussed as follows.

Temurtas2 discusses and compares various Neural Network techniques for detecting thyroid diseases. He used Thyroid disease datasets from the UCI machine learning database consisting of 215 samples and three classes that are Calss1: normal, Class2: hyper, and Class3: hypo. Temurta discusses the k-fold (3-fold and 10-fold) cross-validation method for classification. He compares his study Evolutionary Discriminate Analysis, Wrapped Evolutionary Discriminate Analysis, Feature Space Mapping, Fuzzy Classifier, Hybrid Bayes Classifier, and High Order Fisher Discriminate Analysis. The highest accuracy of 94.81% with PNN gained. Sidiq et al.3 Use data mining and machine learning techniques like K-Nearest Neighbor, SVM, Naïve Bayes, and Decision Tree to identify various thyroid issues. They compare their proposed work with MLPNN with CSFNN, BP, RBF, AIRS, FBP, AIRS with Fuzzy, LM, PNN, ESTDD, LVQ, LM, FKNN, SVM, GDA-WSVM, and Decision Tree. The consistency of the categorization using the classification techniques is assessed using ten-fold cross-validation in this study. The Decision Tree had the most remarkable test accuracy of 98.89% of the four classifiers tested in this study.

Ma et al.4 Used a CNN with optimization to detect thyroid abnormalities from SPECT pictures. In the training phase, transfer learning, improved network architecture (Dense Net), data augmentation, and optimization-based training with Flower Pollination Algorithm stages are involved. Other CNN methods are implemented, including ResNet101, DenseNet121, VGG19, InceptionV3, Google Net, Modified VGG, and SDAE for comparison in this paper. The effectiveness of the suggested system is assessed using on Recall, Precision, Accuracy, F1-Score, and Specificity. In the proposed method, Grave’s disease accuracy is 99.08%, Hashimoto’s disease accuracy is 99.25%, for subacute thyroiditis accuracy is 99.67%.

Shukla and Vaishali7 presented how to classify hypothyroidism using the Optimized SVM approach. There are three main stages in this proposed system. In the first stage, pre-processing of data takes place. The four widely supervised machine learning classification techniques Artificial Neural Network, SVM, Logistic Regression, and K-nearest neighbor, are discussed. The two methods, the Train/Test Split method and the cross-validation method, are used for acquiring the best classifier. According to the comparison, Logistic Regression has good accuracy over both train/test split and cross-validation methods. The performance of SVM is optimized by using the Grid Search parameter Tuning method, which has two parameters: C and Radial Basis Function Kernel. The accuracy achieved is 99.08%.

Thyroid dysfunction identification using Nave Bayes and K-nearest neighbor classification approaches was compared by Chandel et al.12.Only one dataset of 3 classes having 7200 samples was being used for experiments. Many other classification techniques like Decision tree (DT), ANN, and SVM are discussed in this paper. The RapidMiner tool is used in this study for predictive analysis. Training, testing, and validation of both Naïve Bayes and K-nearest neighbor techniques were done with the help of RapidMiner. At last, by comparing both techniques, the result that the authors concluded is 93.44% accuracy of K-Nearest Neighbor and 22.56% accuracy of the Naïve Bayes algorithm obtained in this study.

Pan, Zhang, et al.13 discuss an improved ensemble classification method for thyroid disease detection based on the Random Forest technique. The classification method of the developed framework consists of three stages. Stage one is data pre-processing, in which missing thyroid datasets can be formed through multiple operations like merge, transformation, selection, integration, and so on. The second stage uses the PCA method for feature dimension reduction. In the third stage, categorization and prediction with the help of the Random Forest Algorithm. The methodology of the algorithm used in this proposed system is compared with different other techniques like multilayer perception, learning vector quantization, radial basis function, RF, and MNN. This proposed system uses two datasets with 215 samples and 5161 of three classes. In actual medical data, this technique attains 96.16% accuracy for dataset 1 and 95.63% for dataset 2.

Al-muwaffaq and Bozkus14 discussed the ML tool for Thyroid Disease Diagnosis (MLTDD), created with PyDev, an Eclipse-based Python IDE. There are four stages used in the implementation of this system model. The first stage of pre-processing, feature extraction, and feature reduction occur. The second stage is Model Building, in which classification is done by supervised ANN and decision tree technique with divide and conquer algorithm. The third stage is Model Evaluation, in which performance and accuracy measures are evaluated. The fourth stage is the Tool Designing stage. A Dataset of 7200 samples for three classes was used for experiments. In this proposed system, when 23% of testing datasets and 21 features are used, 99.82% accuracy is obtained, and when 32% of testing datasets and 11 features are used, 99.70% accuracy is obtained.

Raghuraman et al.15 studied machine learning strategies such as Multiple Linear Regression, SVM, and Decision Trees to compare thyroid problem prognosis. Problem identification

There still exist a number of issues with the current literature, despite the fact that several computational methods have been developed for the identification of thyroid disease. Most of the earlier research uses only a portion of the Thyroid Disease Dataset’s features, frequently focusing on repetitive or uninformative characteristics, which lowers feature diversity and diagnostic reliability. Additionally, most current systems only define three types of thyroid conditions: hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, and normal. Other clinically significant thyroid abnormalities are not included in the classification. As a result, unified multi-class frameworks that can concurrently diagnose all main thyroid disorders including hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, sick-euthyroid, and normal conditions are lacking. The lack of such thorough diagnostic models limits practical applicability and prevents endocrinological decision-making from being completely supported by automated systems.

Furthermore, due to variations in data pretreatment, susceptibility to parameter adjustment, and a lack of uniform evaluation methodologies, many current algorithms exhibit uneven performance. Despite their strength, deep learning-based techniques are limited in their applicability in real-world healthcare settings since they usually rely on medical imaging data and necessitate intricate network structures with significant processing resources. Furthermore, noisy and missing variables in thyroid datasets hinder robust generalization and further reduce classification performance. In order to address these issues, this work focuses on creating a feature-complete, scalable, and computationally efficient framework that uses an EELM integrated with Drop Connect regularization to provide a unified four-class classification capability, handle missing data, and maintain robustness across multiple thyroid categories. These drawbacks underscore the need for a more feature-complete, efficient, and generalizable diagnostic model that can handle missing data, enhance classification performance across various thyroid conditions, and achieve high diagnostic accuracy without the computational burden of deep architectures. Problem solution

We proposed a thyroid disease detection system using a simple form of Artificial Neural Network, the ELM technique. Instead of images, we use numerical data from Kaggle for processing. So, by using the simple algorithm of the ELM technique and numerical data as input, we will diagnose four types of thyroid diseases. Also, by using numerical data, we proposed a solution for the first problem: using an image dataset requires a complex algorithm for processing. The solution to our second problem is using ELM, through which we can classify four thyroid diseases. Although we can use many other techniques for this purpose, ELM e powerful and easy to diagnose thyroid diseases. Figure. 2 shows current proposed strategy for thyroid diseases.

Current proposed strategy.

Ensuring ELM’s generalization performance requires a high number of hidden nodes. But another problem faced during the implementation of the ELM model for classifying thyroid disease detection is that setting the number of hidden neurons more than the fixed value causes the model to be over-fit. In the hidden layer, the number of neurons that influences accuracy in the ELM model is determined through experimentation. To solve this problem, we use the Drop Connect approach, which is an extension of Dropout for regularizing large fully connected layers in neural networks7. Within each layer, a randomly selected portion of activations is set to zero during training using Dropout. Drop Connect, on the other hand, randomly resets the weights of a network segment. Consequently, each unit gets input from a random subset of the units from the previous layer7. Figure. 3 shows over-fitting in ELM.

Methodology

Several approaches for detecting thyroid disease have been developed. The current strategy for detecting thyroid sickness is based on the Extreme Learning Machine algorithm, an extended variant of the Feed-Forward Neural Network (FNN). The proposed methodology has many steps. The first stage is the Data Acquisition stage, in which data is gathered and merged from all relevant sources. The second stage is data pre-processing which is used to correct various problems, including missing values, redundant data, and noisy data. After this, categorical data is encoded. The dataset must be divided as the following step in data preparation. Every dataset for a model must be divided into two sets: training and testing. The data pre-processing comes to an end with the feature scaling stage. After data pre-processing, the next stage is the Model development stage, in which the ELM model is defined and created. Then the model is compiled for training and testing. In the last stage, the performance of the ELM, the model is evaluated based on F1-Score, Confusion matrix, Sensitivity, Accuracy, Recall, Precision, ROC, Specificity, and AUC parameters. Data preprocessing

The study’s dataset, which included both continuous and discrete characteristics relevant to the diagnosis of thyroid illness, was taken from publicly accessible sources on Kaggle.com. To guarantee data consistency, dependability, and appropriateness for the suggested EELM framework, a number of preparation procedures were used before model training.

[1]. Data Cleaning and Integration: Following data collection, the raw datasets were examined for formatting errors, redundant records, and inconsistencies. To make sure that only completely identical records were deleted, duplicate samples were found and eliminated using all feature columns and the associated class label. To avoid biased performance evaluation, this step was carried out after dataset integration but before data splitting. Reliable outcomes and a reduction in computational overhead depend on high-quality and well-prepared data43.

Section "Enhanced Extreme Learning Machine" has an updated overview of ELM. ELM has a strong generalization ability and a lightning-fast learning rate. One of the most critical unresolved difficulties in ELM applications, comparable to other feed-forward neural network approaches, is obtaining a suitable network architecture. On the one hand, a network with insufficient nodes may be unable to represent the data41 appropriately. As the number of hidden layer neuron nodes rises, the classification accuracy of the training samples improves fast, eventually nearing the objective value. If the neurons in the hidden layer continue to grow, the training data classification accuracy may reach 100%35. But a network with too many nodes may experience over-fitting41. The testing samples’ classification accuracy rises quickly as the number of hidden layer neurons rises, but it does not approach the objective value as soon as the training samples. Consider the following alternatives instead: When categorization accuracy grows significantly and reaches its peak, it gradually decreases. When the categorization accuracy peaked, it began to drop before stabilizing. When the classification accuracy grew fast and peaked, it first fell, then rose again, finally nearing a new stationary value35. The most common method for determining a suitable network size (i.e., the number of hidden units) is to estimate the prediction error for several frameworks and then choose the model with the lowest generalization error. However, this process can be complex and time-consuming when data has a high-dimensional or massive set of variables41. When the nodes of hidden layer neurons are not significantly different, there is a significant variation in ELM classification accuracy, surpassing 20%35. The stochastic model is responsible for the ELM input weights. However, the main reason is that every time ELM’s hidden layer node is modified, the input weight matrix is altered as well, causing ELM to lose its capacity to self-optimize and dramatically increasing the time it takes to find the ELM optimum structure35,48. As we encountered the dilemma while implementing the ELM model for detecting thyroid illnesses, increasing the number of hidden neurons over a predetermined amount leads the model to be over-fit. Experimentation is used to establish the number of neurons in the hidden layer that impact accuracy in the ELM model.

In the field of machine learning, regularization terms are often used as a means of avoiding model over-fitting41. Regularization is a frequent technique for overcoming over-fitting34. There are several regularization techniques available41. For regularizing large fully-connected layers in neural networks, use the Drop Connect approach, which extends Dropout. During training, a randomly selected percentage of activations in each layer is set to zero using Dropout. Drop Connect, on the other hand, randomly sets the weights of a network segment to zero. The output of the hidden nodes is set to zero with probability ρ during training; otherwise, it is preserved with probability (1-ρ)34. Figure 6 shows Drop Connect Network. Consequently, each unit gets input from a random subset of the units from the previous layer33. Each training data is forward propagated in Drop Connect, with some network weight elements kept at random42. The probability ρ is a key constant that must establish ahead of time34. Interpretation

Instead of altering the ELM training procedure, Drop-Connect can be conceptually seen as a feature-space perturbation mechanism. The stochastic masking of connections effectively projects each input sample onto a slightly different random subspace. This enhances generalization by encouraging the model to learn representations that are less reliant on particular input-hidden links.

From the standpoint of linear algebra, Drop-Connect randomly suppresses contributions from specific input-hidden connections, changing the column space of the hidden layer matrix H. As long as the final matrix drops Hdrop keeps the pseudo-inverse solution well-defined and numerically stable. For clinical decision-support systems, where quick and accurate model training is crucial, the resulting stochastic regularization minimizes co-adaptation between hidden neurons while maintaining the computational efficiency of ELM.

The suggested Enhanced Extreme Learning Machine (EELM) incorporates the Drop-Connect regularization algorithm into the conventional ELM structure to enhance generalization and reduce over-fitting. During the forward propagation phase, the input–hidden weight matrix (W) was specially subjected to the Drop-Connect mask. The Drop-Connect probability (ρ) was used to randomly deactivate a subset of neuron connections at each training iteration. By forcing neurons to learn independent representations, this stochastic connection dropping reduces co-adaptation and improves robustness. Training process and use of drop-connect

The suggested Enhanced Extreme Learning Machine employs the conventional ELM training paradigm, wherein a regularized least-squares solution is used to calculate the output weights analytically. Back-propagation and iterative optimization are not used. The input-hidden weight matrix is subjected to Drop-Connect regularization only during training, which is accomplished by multiplying it element-wise using a Bernoulli mask sampled with probability y 1−ρ. The hidden-layer output matrix is calculated using the obtained masked weights and then sent to the closed-form ELM solution for output weight estimation. To guarantee deterministic predictions, Drop-Connect is totally turned off during inference. The input-hidden weights are scaled by their predicted value using the conventional Drop-Connect procedure, W test = (1 − ρ) W, prior to calculating the output of the hidden layer. This maintains the regularization impact that was introduced during training while ensuring consistency between training and testing.

Discussion

In this work, an Enhanced Extreme Learning Machine (EELM) coupled with Drop-Connect regularization is introduced for the efficient detection of thyroid disorders using numerical laboratory data. The suggested method successfully bridges ideas from both the machine learning and deep learning paradigms by enhancing diagnostic accuracy while maintaining computing efficiency by combining connection-level stochastic regularization with the fast learning speed of Extreme Learning Machines. Its suitability for medical diagnostic applications is shown by comparative trials with numerous ML and DL baselines, which show that the proposed EELM consistently delivers improved classification performance with much lower computing cost. Hypercholesterolemia and cardiovascular problems are among the major long-term risks to health linked to thyroid dysfunction47. The suggested method offers actual clinical utility by offering a single unified framework that can diagnose numerous thyroid diseases. In contrast to numerous other research investigations that concentrated on specific diagnostic categories, usually hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, or normal states1,3,12,14–16,18, or32, the proposed EELM improves diagnostic coverage to encompass sick-euthyroid instances in a single multi-class formulation. This greater scope enables early intervention, reduces the diagnostic burden on medical personnel, and is more compatible with real clinical operations.

The Drop-Connect regularization technique is largely responsible for the suggested EELM’s better generalization performance when compared to traditional classifiers like K-Nearest Neighbors, SVM, and similar approaches4–6,12,14,19,21 and23. Strong inter-feature correlations, inevitable experimental noise, and a small number of highly predictive biochemical features (such as TSH, T3, and T4) are characteristics of thyroid datasets. Because they rely too heavily on a limited number of dominating features or fixed neuron co-adaptations, classic models, such as standard ELM, are prone to over-fitting in these situations. This problem reduces by Drop-Connect, which requires the model to acquire numerous and redundant feature representations by randomly deactivating each input-hidden connection during training. Decision boundaries become more stable and broadly applicable as a result to avoid over-specialization and decreasing sensitivity to noisy laboratory values.

With an accuracy of up to 99.89%, the suggested EELM demonstrated its best performance under 10-fold cross-validation in binary thyroid classification tasks (Hypothyroid vs. Normal, Hyperthyroid vs. Normal, and Sick-Euthyroid vs. Normal). This suggests that the model successfully takes use of the obvious class separability found in binary diagnostic contexts. The mean accuracy dropped to about 82% when the work was expanded to a unified four-class classification occupation, which more closely mimics clinical diagnosis in the real world (Tables 9 and 11). Rather than being a drawback of the suggested strategy, this decrease is anticipated as a result of the increased diagnostic complexity brought on by overlapping biochemical signatures, class imbalance, and greater inter-class ambiguity. All studies used rigorous 10-fold cross-validation with randomized shuffling and rigorous separation between training and test sets, together with preprocessing and normalization done before data splitting to prevent information loss and to ensure reliable interpretation of results. The model’s achievement is driven by true generalization rather than memorization, as evidenced by the continuously low standard deviation (±0.006) seen across folds and repeated runs. Additionally, consistent F1-score, precision, and recall values across a range of train-test ratios (about 0.79–0.83 in Table 9) show that the EELM remains robust regardless of data partitioning, performing somewhat better under higher training proportions. Low standard deviations acquired during 10-fold cross-validation Table 11 further strengthened the results’ robustness by showing that the stated performance is repeatable and not a result of data leakage or random initialization artefacts. The performance gains of the suggested EELM over baseline models (Random Forest, CNN, and GAN+KAN) are statistically significant at the 95% confidence level (p < 0.05), according to statistical validation using paired t-tests. Furthermore, McNemar’s tests and one-way ANOVA results (F = 17.64, p < 0.001) confirmed that the observed differences are not the result of chance. Overall, Table 11's minimal variability further supports the suggested model’s stability and repeatability. Despite its impressive outcomes, there are a few things to be aware of. The model may not accurately reflect the variety of clinical data found in the real world because it was trained on a single numerical dataset. Clinical comprehensiveness may be limited by the missing of multimodal data, such as biochemical characteristics and imaging. Furthermore, even though Drop Connect enhances regularization, the model is still a black-box system that has limited interpretability, which may have an impact on clinical acceptability. Future work will incorporate explainable. AI techniques like SHAP or LIME for feature-level attribution in order to improve model transparency and clinical trust. Additionally, the proposed EELM will be validated on multi-center clinical datasets to assess robustness across diverse patient populations.

The random initialization of hidden-layer weights and biases is a well-known drawback of classical ELM that may cause variation between runs. Drop-Connect regularization, which stabilizes the impact of individual weights and introduces controlled stochasticity at the connection level, is how the suggested EELM resolves this problem. To guarantee reproducibility, fixed random seeds and is typical over several independent runs were also used. These steps increase consistency and robustness while maintaining ELM’s quick training advantage. The single hidden-layer architecture of the suggested EELM preserves the efficiency benefits of the standard ELM from a computational standpoint, leading to a linear time complexity of O(N × H). The model can be used in real-time clinical decision-support systems because training and inference take only milliseconds per prediction. Accuracy, computational efficiency, and deployment practicality are all positively balanced in the suggested method as compared to more intricate deep learning architectures like CNNs or GAN-based models.

Even though some binary classification tests obtained near-perfect accuracy (99%+), results on medical datasets should be interpreted carefully. Some binary class boundaries can be quite easy to separate, especially in controlled benchmark circumstances, due to the modest number of highly discriminative features seen in thyroid laboratory datasets, especially TSH. This does not imply data loss, but it raises the possibility that the binary tasks are inherently easier than actual diagnosis. The unified four-class experiment’s more moderate performance (about 82% accuracy) more accurately captures the clinical complexity brought on by overlapping biochemical profiles and class imbalance, offering a more realistic evaluation of the usefulness of the suggested model in practice.

These results collectively show that the Drop-Connect-based EELM provides a statistically validated, computationally efficient, and robust framework for classifying thyroid diseases, with good performance in both binary and clinically realistic multi-class diagnostic contexts.

Conclusion and future work

Using numerical laboratory data, this work demonstrated an Enhanced Extreme Learning Machine (EELM) based on Drop Connect for the identification of thyroid diseases. The importance of the suggested EELM for thyroid problem identification is highlighted by experimental results that show it consistently outperforms the standard ELM and a number of well-known machine learning and deep learning models36,41,42, and43. Although nearly perfect performance (up to 99.89% accuracy) was attained in binary thyroid classification tasks, the unified four-class classification experiment yielded a mean accuracy of roughly 82% under 10-fold cross-validation, representing a more clinically realistic diagnostic scenario. This contrast highlights the fundamental complexity of multi-class thyroid diagnosis as well as the efficacy of the suggested EELM. The Drop Connect regularization technique, which maintains the computational efficiency of the ELM framework while reducing co-adaptation among hidden neurons, minimizing overfitting, and enhancing generalization, is responsible for the performance improvements. The suggested model is appropriate for real-world clinical decision-support systems due to its single hidden-layer architecture, which allows for quick training and real-time inference with linear computing complexity. But before using the suggested paradigm in actual healthcare settings, a number of significant constraints should be acknowledged despite its promising outcomes:

Data Heterogeneity: The model may not accurately represent the variation in thyroid profiles among hospitals, geographical areas, and demographic groups because it was trained on a single benchmark numerical dataset. When applied to a variety of clinical populations, this variation may affect the predictions’ generalizability and dependability. More specifically, to assess generalization across hospitals, laboratory methods, and demographic groups, validation will be carried out in the future on extensive multi-institutional clinical datasets like MIMIC-IV and eICU.

Future implementations should use explainable AI (XAI) approaches like SHAP or LIME to give clear reasoning for model decisions in order to improve applicability. To increase robustness, multi-center validation using a variety of clinical datasets is also advised. To handle missing values and guarantee reproducibility, data preprocessing methods should be optimized. Clinical relevance and usefulness will be further ensured by working together with medical professionals during model validation. To enhance generalization across heterogeneous datasets, future research should investigate hybrid architectures that combine EELM with deep feature extraction or transfer learning. More thorough diagnostic help might result from extending the framework to incorporate multimodal inputs, such as ultrasound pictures, test results, and patient history. Furthermore, it will be crucial to implement the suggested EELM as a real-time decision support system in hospital settings and assess its effectiveness under practical limitations. The model can be modified for various medical classification tasks that demand high accuracy and efficiency in addition to thyroid disorders51,52.

The model reached up to 99.89% accuracy in binary classification scenarios (e.g., hypothyroid vs. normal), which reflects the relative division of these particular disorders within the dataset rather than overall diagnostic perfection. The underlying complexity of multi-class thyroid condition identification was highlighted by the unified four-class classification test, which had a mean accuracy of roughly 82% and was more similar to real-world clinical diagnosis. Therefore, even if the results show great promise, strong external validation, better interpretability, and respect to privacy and ethical standards are necessary for real-world clinical use. According to the results, AI-assisted diagnosis can reduce diagnostic delays and help physicians in decision-making if trustworthiness and transparency are constantly assured.

In conclusion, the suggested Drop Connect-based EELM offers a solid, effective, and high-performing diagnostic framework that establishes the groundwork for trustworthy AI-based thyroid illness diagnosis. Its effective incorporation into future intelligent healthcare systems will be made possible by addressing its present limits by extensions that are both clinically verified and explainable.
