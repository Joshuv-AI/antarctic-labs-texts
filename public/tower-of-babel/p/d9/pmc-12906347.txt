Comparison of Sprint Deceleration Capacity in Front‐ and Side‐Facing End Stances in Multidirectional Team Sport Athletes
Authors: Millicent A. West, Heidi R. Compton, Ben J. Dascombe, Josh L. Secomb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDeceleration is a crucial task in multidirectional team sports. However, no research has explored how different end stances impact deceleration performance. This is important due to the multidirectional nature and variance of these directional changes in such sports. This study aimed to compare sprint deceleration capacity between front‐facing (FF) and side‐facing (SF) end stances using a deceleration threshold methodology to standardize the start point of deceleration. 20 team‐sport athletes completed three maximal sprint deceleration trials in three conditions: FF and SF with both preferred leg stance (SFPREF) and nonpreferred leg stance (SFNON‐PREF). Key metrics of time‐to‐stop (TTS), distance‐to‐stop (DTS), average deceleration (DECave), and maximum deceleration (DECmax) were analyzed using linear mixed models. Results indicated significantly different deceleration profiles between FF and SF conditions with FF decelerations exhibiting greater TTS and DTS but lower DECave and DECmax compared to SF tasks (p < 0.05). No significant differences were determined between SFPREF and SFNON‐PREF task conditions (p = 0.64–1.00). Correlation analysis demonstrated high similarity in DTS across tasks (r = 0.87–0.92) but greater divergence in TTS and DECave (r = 0.55–0.88). These findings suggest that deceleration mechanics are task dependent with SF tasks eliciting greater braking demands. Practitioners should consider sport‐specific deceleration requirements when designing training interventions.

Introduction

Deceleration is a high‐intensity locomotive movement that is crucial for performance in multidirectional team sports (i.e., Australian rules football, rugby league, rugby union, rugby sevens, field hockey, and football [soccer]) (D. J. Harper et al. 2019). These efforts typically either precede a change‐of‐direction (COD) maneuver (Dos'Santos et al. 2019; Dos’Santos et al. 2018) or are performed to reduce momentum following a sprint effort (D. J. Harper et al. 2019; D. J. Harper and Kiely 2018). Although research on these efforts is evolving, some evidence suggests that deceleration efforts are performed more frequently than acceleration efforts of an equivalent intensity during match play (Ade et al. 2016; McBurnie et al. 2022). This is likely attributed to the specific movement demands of offensive and defensive actions required in these sports, such as pressing, counter pressing, and transitional play (D. J. Harper et al. 2019; Moreno‐Azze et al. 2025). Further, most existing research has focused on forward‐facing (FF) deceleration assessments; however, in sports such as football, the majority of deceleration efforts involve a side‐facing (SF) stop that precedes a COD (Martínez‐Hernández et al. 2023). Therefore, research aimed at improving practitioners' ability to assess and monitor deceleration efforts across different orientations and end stances is warranted to better reflect the multi‐directional nature of game play.

A greater ability to decelerate rapidly, whereby the athlete can reduce their forward velocity and whole‐body momentum over shorter distances and/or durations is desirable in many sports (Marvin et al. 2024). D. J. Harper et al. (2022) further noted that these efforts “must be executed within specific constraints and requires a set task objective to be achieved, whilst skillfully attenuating and distributing the braking forces.” As such, those who can generate greater braking forces and/or in a shorter period of time possess superior deceleration performance potential as they may have the ability to approach the task at higher velocities or decelerate in a shorter distance or time when approaching the effort at the same velocity (Dos’ Santos et al. 2021; D. Harper et al. 2024; Jones et al. 2017). Therefore, time‐to‐stop (TTS), distance‐to‐stop (DTS), and the average of all instantaneous velocity values throughout a deceleration effort (DECave) are key metrics to analyze when assessing deceleration performance (D. J. Harper et al. 2021; D. J. Harper et al. 2023; West et al. 2025). Furthermore, as DECave combines the variables of time and distance, it may better represent overall deceleration performance. However, it has yet to be determined if the end stance position of a deceleration task affects the mechanical demands, and hence, these key metrics.

Recent developments in technology have enabled a new assessment methodology to quantify deceleration performance (West et al. 2025). Specifically, the deceleration threshold method proposed by West et al. (2025) utilizes a motorized sprint device and provides a standardized start point of the deceleration effort noted as the point when the athlete reaches an instantaneous deceleration (negative acceleration) of −1.5 m·s−2. Although this method was reported as reliable for the key deceleration metrics of TTS, DTS, and DECave during a front‐facing (FF) task (ICC = 0.93, CV% = 6.4; ICC = 0.97, CV% = 5.3; ICC = 0.92, CV% = 7.1, respectively), no known research has assessed deceleration performance using this methodology for pre‐planned deceleration tasks with different end stances (West et al. 2025). Specifically, it is necessary to assess and compare both FF and side‐facing (SF) deceleration efforts, as FF deceleration is associated with performance outcomes such as initiating or avoiding tackles and contact in invasion sports, whereas SF deceleration is more prevalent in sports such as netball, basketball, and tennis where athletes frequently adopt a lateral stance to plant their feet and respond to rapid directional change (Dos'Santos et al. 2017; Jones et al. 2009; Sheppard and Young 2006). Such data are crucial for practitioners to better understand whether performance in these tasks is related to one another, as this has important implications for both athlete assessment and training prescription. Therefore, the aim of this study was to compare sprint deceleration performance during FF and SF pre‐planned tasks using the deceleration threshold method. Specifically, the research sought to evaluate these tasks in an outdoor environment using specialized equipment with the purpose of enhancing practitioner understanding how different end stances influence deceleration performance.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Subjects initially attended a familiarization session 1 week prior to the commencement of the study where they performed at least five trials of the sprint deceleration assessment protocol and also underwent an anthropometric assessment (height and body mass). Subsequently, a testing day was completed 1 week later with a standardized warm‐up undertaken that consisted of submaximal aerobic exercise, dynamic stretches, and incremental intensity acceleration and deceleration efforts performed at the start of each session. All testing was performed on an undercover, concrete playing surface and subjects wore the same footwear for the familiarization and testing day. Together, this ensured consistency in the shoe‐surface interface across all trials. Subjects were also instructed to restrict themselves from any high‐intensity physical training in the 24 h prior to testing.

Subjects

Twenty subjects (female: n = 10, 23.1 ± 4.7 years, 164.3 ± 4.1 cm, 68.7 ± 10.1 kg; and male: n = 10, 23.3 ± 3.7 years, 180.6 ± 10.8 cm, and 77.5 ± 9.7 kg) from multiple running‐based field and court team sports, rugby league (n = 1), soccer (n = 11), netball (n = 1), Australian football (n = 3), touch football (n = 3), and American football (n = 1) volunteered to participate in this study. To be eligible for inclusion, all subjects were required to be aged between 18 and 35 years old currently competing at a minimum of regional level and be free of any injury or illness that could be worsened by their participation. An a priori estimation revealed that to achieve a minimum power of 0.80 with a large effect (0.45) for comparing sprint deceleration performance in various end‐stances, a sample size of 20 subjects was required. Prior to providing written informed consent, all subjects were informed of the research purpose and requirements and screened for medical contraindications. Ethical approval was obtained through the University of Newcastle Human Ethics Committee (H‐2023‐0013).

Procedures

Sprint Deceleration Assessment

To assess maximal sprint deceleration performance, subjects completed three maximal effort trials of three different deceleration tasks (nine in total) that were each separated by at least 3 minutes of passive rest. These trials were performed in a consistent sequence. For each trial, subjects were required to perform a 30 m maximal effort sprint to a set of markers where they were required to stop as quickly as possible. The verbal instructions provided were: “sprint all the way through the markers before stopping as quickly as possible”. Subjects were not given any specific technique instructions regarding the final stopping positions; however, they were provided with a demonstration from the lead researcher, which involved stopping with the feet parallel and approximately shoulder‐width apart. The three different tasks were determined as: (1) “FF” and “SF”, which required a 90° turn after the marked 30m to the (2) “preferred leg” (SFPREF) or the (3) “nonpreferred leg” (SFNON‐PREF) side (Figure 1) with each task describing which direction the subject reached at the completion of the sprint deceleration effort. For the deceleration task, the preferred leg was defined as the leg furthest from the start point once the subject had completed a 90° turn as the final position of the deceleration effort, and conversely nonpreferred leg was when that same leg was the leg closest to the start point when a 90° turn was executed. A subject's preferred leg was determined by which leg they caught themselves on when gently pushed in the back from a standing position with feet together. To verify the preferred leg identified by the gentle push method, subjects performed one sub‐maximal trial to each side prior to the maximal effort trials. All subjects confirmed that the leg initially identified as their preferred corresponded with the side that felt most natural when stopping in the SF position.

End stance demonstration for sprint deceleration assessment. (A) Front‐facing position, (B) side‐facing preferred leg, and (C) side‐facing non‐preferred leg.

Throughout the sprint deceleration effort, subjects were tethered to a motorized linear encoder device (1080 Sprint; 1080 Motion, Sweden) with a purpose‐built strap attached around their waist. The device was set to the “isotonic” mode with 1 kg of resistance applied to minimize any slack in the cable. This protocol has been validated to assess maximal sprint performance (Rakovic et al. 2022) and aligns with the deceleration threshold method reported West et al. (2025) as reliable for the FF deceleration task. Since the prior study focused only on the FF task, the within‐session reliability for all subjects in this study was calculated using the two best trials as determined by highest deceleration average from the current dataset to ensure methodological robustness. In this study, the key metrics of TTS, DTS and DECave were deemed reliable for both the SFPREF (TTS: ICC = 0.94, CV% = 5.68%; DTS: ICC = 0.98, CV% = 5.82; DECave: ICC = 0.94, CV% = 4.65; DECmax: ICC = 0.92, CV% = 4.85) and SFNON‐PREF (TTS: ICC = 0.94, CV% = 5.45%; DTS: ICC = 0.96, CV% = 6.34; DECave: ICC = 0.93, CV% = 4.85; DECmax: ICC = 0.92, CV% = 4.60). These analyses were performed as supplementary evaluations to support the integrity of this data though not the central focus of this study.

Subjects started each trial with their front foot on a line that was 30 m away from the markers and initiated the trial once the lead researcher (MW) indicated that they could commence. The recording for each trial commenced when the device detected a change in cable position of > 0.2 m·s−1 and finished automatically once the subject reached a stationary position. All trials were extracted as comma‐separated value files from the proprietary online server (https://webapp.1080motion.com/) for the raw position‐time data that was sampled at 333 Hz. To analyze the data, a customized code (RStudio, v2024.04.1 + 748, Boston, Massachusetts) was created for this study, whereby the raw position‐time data were initially filtered with a low order 200 Hz filter (fourth‐order, zero lag Butterworth Filter) as outlined by Sugisaki et al. (2024). From this filtered data, the instantaneous velocity (1) and acceleration (2) were calculated using the following equations: (1) Velocitym·s−1=df−ditf−ti (2) Accelerationm·s−2=vf−vitf−ti Where d is distance, v is velocity, t is time, f is the final velocity or time, and i is the initial velocity or time.

To analyze deceleration performance, the start point of the deceleration effort was determined when the participant reached the deceleration threshold (West et al. 2025). The end of the deceleration effort was recognized as the point where the subject reached a stationary position. For each trial, the peak sprint velocity was recorded, and the difference between the time and position values at the start and end points of the deceleration effort used to calculate the TTS and DTS. The best trials for each deceleration task were determined as the two with the highest DECave value as suggested by D. J. Harper et al. (2023), which was calculated by averaging all instantaneous negative acceleration values from the start to the end of the deceleration effort. The maximum instantaneous deceleration value was also recorded (DECmax). Additionally, velocity decrement was determined as the difference from peak velocity to the instantaneous velocity at the start point of the deceleration effort and calculated as a relative percentage for descriptive purposes.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation [SD], 95% confidence intervals [CI]) from the two best trials of each task were reported for the outcome measures (peak velocity, velocity at the start of the deceleration, percentage of velocity decrement from peak to deceleration start velocity, position at which the deceleration begins, TTS, DTS, DECave, and DECmax). To assess the effect of tasks type (FF, SFPREF and SFNON‐PREF) on each of the outcome measures, linear mixed models were used. Each outcome was analyzed independently (in separate models) and was conducted using customized software in RStudio (Version 2024.09.0 + 375) using packages lme4, lmerTest, and emmeans. In these models, using a random intercept design, the dependent variable was the deceleration performance outcome measure, task type as a fixed effect (predictor), and subject identification as a random intercept to account for inter‐individual variability. Following this, pairwise comparisons were conducted using estimated marginal means (least‐square means) for each task to investigate specific differences between the tasks (FF v SFPREF, FF v SFNON‐PREF, and SFPREF v SFNON‐PREF). Each comparison was determined for each variable using emmeans package, which adjusts for multiple comparisons through Tukey's HSD.

To assess the relationships between performance in the FF and SF deceleration tasks for each deceleration metric, Pearsons's correlation coefficients were conducted on the same pairings. To interpret the magnitude of the correlations the following thresholds were applied: 0.00 to 0.10 (trivial), 0.11 to 0.30 (small), 0.31 to 0.50 (moderate), 0.51 to 0.70 (large), 0.71 to 0.90 (very large), and 0.91 to 1.00 (nearly perfect) (Hopkins 1997). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Discussion

The aim of this study was to compare the maximal deceleration performance of team sport athletes across three different pre‐planned deceleration tasks: the FF, SFPREF, and SFNON‐PREF end stance tasks. Significant differences were observed between the FF and both SF tasks for the metrics of TTS, DTS, DECave, and DECmax, peak velocity, and deceleration start velocity. Specifically, in the FF task, the athletes commenced the deceleration effort with a significantly greater velocity, but the TTS, DTS, and DECave were significantly less than the SF tasks. However, no significant differences were identified between the two SF tasks for any of these metrics. Additionally, correlational analyses revealed high similarity between tasks for DTS but greater divergence between FF and SF tasks for TTS and DECave. Together, these data suggest that the task‐specific mechanics of each end stance influenced maximal deceleration performance, which has important implications for practitioners. When selecting a protocol to assess maximal deceleration performance, practitioners should choose the task that best reflects the typical deceleration patterns observed in the sport.

D. J. Harper et al. (2022) stated that deceleration actions must be executed “within the constraints and in accordance with the specific objective of the task.” The current data demonstrate that deceleration performance is different between FF and SF tasks with key metrics that are largely independent of each other due to task‐specific demands. Comparatively, there was a 1.0% greater peak instantaneous velocity recorded in FF and a 1.3%–1.6% faster velocity at the start point of the deceleration effort compared to the SF tasks. In addition to orientation‐specific mechanical demands, the significantly higher entry velocity observed in the front‐facing condition may reflect greater task familiarity or perceived controllability when performing a front‐facing stop resulting in a more aggressive approach strategy. However, as perceptual factors such as confidence were not directly assessed in the present study, this interpretation remains speculative and should be considered alongside the mechanical influence of higher entry velocity on deceleration‐related outcomes. For the FF task, this was associated with a 20% greater TTS, an 8%–10% longer DTS, and a 14% slower DECave. This emphasizes how seemingly small (1%–2%) changes in peak velocity can have much larger effects on other metrics of deceleration performance. However, the faster deceleration observed in the SF tasks is likely due to the athlete's ability to establish a wider base of support oriented in the direction of forward momentum. This positioning allows for more effective attenuation of the forward, horizontal motion, resulting in a more rapid stop compared to FF.

Although both SF tasks resulted in the same TTS, the SFNON‐PREF showed a nonsignificant trend toward a greater DTS, lower DECave, and slightly higher DECmax. These subtle task‐specific differences between sides suggest that asymmetries may exist in deceleration performance. However, this interpretation should be considered in the context that the preferred and nonpreferred side classifications in the present study were based on subjective movement selection rather than objective dominance in performance, which may limit the ability to detect clear between‐side differences. Therefore, practitioners should assess both sides during athlete profiling and prescribe targeted training to improve deceleration capacity on each side. Additionally, significantly higher DECmax values, approximately 17% higher, were recorded during both SF tasks compared to the FF task. Although SF deceleration tasks appear to be less commonly assessed within the current deceleration testing literature than front‐facing tasks, the higher peak deceleration demands observed in the present study may have important implications for performance monitoring and injury risk assessment. Specifically, athletes who are not regularly exposed to SF deceleration mechanics may be more vulnerable if required to perform such movements during high‐intensity phases of match‐play or training. It is also important to note that although DECmax provides valuable insight into peak braking demand, within‐session reliability analyses demonstrated slightly lower relative reliability for DECmax compared with other deceleration metrics (ICC = 0.92), despite low coefficients of variation (CV% = 4.60–4.85). As such, interpretations involving DECmax should consider both its reliability characteristics and the broader biomechanical context of task orientation and force‐application direction.

Comparing the current data to other studies where the key metrics of deceleration have been reported (D. J. Harper et al. 2021; D. J. Harper et al. 2023) is difficult given that despite the same metrics, different protocols have been employed. Specifically, other studies (D. J. Harper et al. 2021; D. J. Harper et al. 2023) have used 20 m sprint distances, whereas the current study used 30 m (Rumpf et al. 2016; West et al. 2025). The selection of this greater sprint distance was intentional to allow participants to reach near‐peak running velocity, thereby imposing high deceleration demands; however, true maximal velocity was not directly verified in this study. Interestingly, to the authors’ knowledge, this is the first study to report isolated SF deceleration tasks despite the similarity in COD body position and relevance for sports including netball, basketball, and tennis, which frequently require athletes to plant their feet in a lateral stance.

The data of this study highlight that FF and SF deceleration tasks are distinctly different. This indicates that deceleration abilities between the different end stance tasks are not interchangeable; for example, if an athlete has a good FF deceleration ability, it does not directly translate to a good SF performance. As such, deceleration assessments should incorporate the specific end stance that is the most relevant to the sport and the associated requirements. Further, using the “Bloomfield Movement Classification” (Bloomfield et al. 2004), it was reported that soccer athletes perform 726 ± 203 turns during a match with 609 ± 193 being between 0° and 90° (either left or right) (Bloomfield et al. 2007). Additionally, rugby league athletes have been reported to complete approximately 49 decelerations per game across all positions (Glassbrook et al. 2019). However, no research to date has investigated the frequency of FF and SF deceleration tasks in these sports, which may provide additional contextual information to support practitioners in deciding the relevance of each end stance task in athlete profiling.

In conclusion, this study compared deceleration performance in three different end stance tasks (FF, SFPREF, and SFNON‐PREF) in team‐sport athletes. Significant differences were observed in key metrics between deceleration performance between FF and SF tasks, highlighting the influence of end‐stance on braking characteristics. As such, it is necessary for practitioners to consider the requirements of the sport their athletes compete in to determine which specific end stance task may be most suitable to quantify deceleration performance. From this data, it is suggested that the association between a higher DECmax and the potential for injury risk especially if the athletes are not frequently performing SF deceleration tasks. Given that DECmax demonstrated relatively lower reliability than other deceleration indices, interpretations involving this variable should be made with caution. Future research may benefit from comparing better‐ and weaker‐performing sides based on objective deceleration outcomes particularly when the primary aim is to identify performance asymmetries. Such an approach would complement the present preference‐based classification, which reflects subjective movement selection rather than maximal braking capacity. Moreover, although DECmax contributed to the interpretation of task differences, these outcomes are better explained by biomechanical distinctions between front‐ and side‐facing stances that modify braking‐force orientation and body alignment. Consequently, DECmax should be interpreted with this broader mechanical context rather than as the sole determinant of observed differences. Additionally, identifying the strength and power qualities associated with deceleration performance may aid practitioners in improving this capacity in multidirectional team sport athletes.
