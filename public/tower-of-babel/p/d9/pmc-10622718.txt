Urinary-Tract-Infection Induced Noncirrhotic Hyperammonemic Encephalopathy Mimicking Nonconvulsive Status Epilepticus
Authors: Yujin Lee, Dong Won Kwack, Dong Wook Kim
Published: 2023

Dear Editor,

Ammonia is one of the most important sources of nitrogen for the body, but hyperammonemia can cause encephalopathy by increasing excitatory glutamate and consequently resulting in brain edema. Patients with hyperammonemic encephalopathy may present with lethargy, irritability, seizure, stupor, and coma. While hyperammonemia in adults is frequently associated with severe liver disease, there are also nonhepatic causes including infection, prolonged seizure, and medications such as valproic acid. Early recognition of the causes of noncirrhotic hyperammonemia is important for prompt diagnosis and appropriate treatment. We recently encountered a patient with noncirrhotic hyperammonemic encephalopathy due to a urinary tract infection (UTI), initially mimicking non-convulsive status epilepticus (NCSE).

A 72-year-old female presented to the emergency room with a 1-day history of sudden disorientation. An initial neurological examination revealed that she was confused and disoriented to time and place, while not having any focal neurological abnormality. The findings of laboratory tests including a liver and renal battery were unremarkable except for elevated blood ammonia level (320 µg/dL, normal range: 20–80 µg/dL) and microscopic hematuria. Treatment with lactulose enema resulted in blood ammonia normalizing to 57 µg/dL, and the patient was discharged in an alert mental status after 12 hours. However, she revisited the emergency room with progressive confusion on the following day. Laboratory tests showed only mild impairment of renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate: 57 mL/min/1.73 m2) and the findings of other laboratory tests including electrolytes, liver battery, and the blood ammonia level were normal. Brain MRI showed no abnormal findings but EEG showed slow background activity with intermittent rhythmic theta to delta rhythmic activities in both frontal areas (Fig. 1A). The tentative diagnosis of NCSE of frontal origin was made, and she was treated with lacosamide at 100 mg twice a day after a loading dosage of 400 mg.

EEG recordings showing initial frontal intermittent rhythmic activities in the theta-to-delta range (A), and frequent triphasic waves (arrows) during hyperammonemic encephalopathy (B). Abdominal CT (C) showing marked urinary bladder distension with irregular bladder-wall thickening (dashed arrows).

During the second day of hospitalization her mental status worsened. We added multiple antiseizure medications (ASMs) including valproic acid (300 mg twice a day) and lamotrigine (25 mg once a day). The blood ammonia level increased markedly on hospital day 3, from 54.7 to 191.1 µg/dL, and follow-up EEG showed large amounts of delta slow activities mixed with triphasic waves (Fig. 1B), indicating toxic or metabolic encephalopathy including hepatic encephalopathy. At that time, macroscopic hematuria and marked urinary bladder distension with irregular bladder-wall thickening were found in abdomen CT for the evaluation of liver disease (Fig. 1C). We stopped all ASMs and treated her with bladder catheterization and oral cefpodoxime at 100 mg twice a day. Her blood ammonia level had normalized to 47.2 µg/dL on hospital day 6, and she returned to her premorbid mental status. Normal EEG results were obtained on hospital day 7. Escherichia coli (E. coli) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) were found in a urine culture.

This patient was first diagnosed as NCSE because acute confusion and recurrent rhythmic delta activity in EEG can be clinical manifestations of NCSE in the elderly,1,2 although this EEG pattern can also be observed with diverse causes of metabolic encephalopathy. Elevation of blood ammonia level is also one of early markers of seizure in an emergency setting.3 Actually, it is quite difficult to distinguish NCSE and metabolic encephalopathy4 and recognizing the distinction between these two quite related conditions based on EEG is practically impossible in certain types of secondary encephalopathy such as lithium- and cefepime-induced encephalopathy.5,6 However, noncirrhotic hyperammonemic encephalopathy due to a UTI could be diagnosed in our patient due to the persistent elevation of ammonia after treating NCSE with multiple ASMs and the appearance of the typical pattern of triphasic waves in follow-up EEG. We had initially failed to accurately diagnose UTI-induced hyperammonemic encephalopathy, and the administration of valproic acid (another well-documented cause of hyperammonemia or even drug-induced NCSE) might have had a detrimental effect on the hyperammonemic encephalopathy in our patient.7

This case report indicates that a UTI that can be a cause of noncirrhotic hyperammonemic encephalopathy. Urease-producing bacteria, such as Proteus and Klebsiella species, colonize in the urinary tract and hydrolyze urea into ammonia in highly alkaline urine, which can be easily transferred to the vesical venous plexus. In obstructive UTIs, urine stagnation influences ammonia diffusion to the vesical venous plexus and perivesical circulation,8 which contributes to ammonia accumulation in the brain. The bacteria isolated in our patient were E. coli and K. pneumoniae. Although E. coli is a non-urease-producing bacterium, it is also documented as one of the frequent causes of UTI-associated noncirrhotic hyperammonemic encephalopathy.9

In summary, this report indicates that hyperammonemic encephalopathy can occur in patients without liver disease. Careful investigations including of the blood ammonia level should be considered when evaluating of unconscious patients, and clinicians should be aware of noncirrhotic causes of hyperammonemia such as a UTI.
