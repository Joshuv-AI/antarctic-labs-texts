High-efficiency electrochemical air capture enabled by thiadiazole redox carrier with tunable gas-selective channels
Authors: Jing Hou, Yingying Cheng, Tao Yan, Zhikun Liu, Peng Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical direct air CO2 capture (eDAC) using redox carriers enables sustainable decarbonization but suffers efficiency losses from O2-induced side reactions such as oxygen reduction and carrier oxidation. We introduce a composite electrode BPT−GPL by integrating 2,5-bis(4-pyridyl)−1,3,4-thiadiazole (BPT) with ether oxygen (–O–) enriched gas permeation layer (GPL), forming a tunable gas transport channel that facilitates CO2 diffusion while limiting O2 penetration. Under atmospheric conditions (~400 ppm CO2, 21% O2), the BPT − GPL system demonstrates high eDAC capacity of 3.3 ± 0.2 mmol g–1BPT across 48 cycles with negligible redox carrier degradation. The thiadiazole ring-based BPT molecules increase electron delocalization, exhibiting moderate CO2 binding strength and high O2 tolerance. Moreover, GPL allows CO2 diffusion and suppresses O2 permeation creating low-O2 microenvironment that inhibits side reactions and maintains cycling stability. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations suggest that ether oxygen’s stronger affinity for CO2 than O2 increases CO2 solubility within GPL, resulting in preferential CO2 permeation. The BPT − GPL system favors CO2 delivery and restricts O2 transport, enabling efficient eDAC and highlighting the potential for practical implementation.

Introduction

CO2 emissions from fuel consumption leads to undesirable climate change1,2. Direct air capture (DAC) offers a promising pathway to mitigate atmospheric CO2. Conventional temperature-swing DAC using alkaline sorbents enables significant atmospheric CO2 uptake through chemical interactions, but requires high sorbent regenerate heat (typically exceeding 100 °C) and suffer from sorbents degradation and extensive operational footprint3–7. Electrochemical direct air capture (eDAC) with redox carriers enables reversible CO2 capture under ambient conditions with lower energy costs, providing a promising alternative for sustainable atmospheric CO2 removal.

In redox carrier-based eDAC, neutral redox molecules (denoted as R) demonstrate weak CO2 affinity, whereas upon reduction, the generated nucleophilic centers bind CO2 to form R–CO2 adducts, and subsequent re-oxidation step releases CO2 then regenerates neutral molecules. This reversible redox process not only enables efficient eDAC but also continuously regenerates active molecules, thereby improving cyclic stability. In the eDAC system, rational molecular design can regulate CO2 binding affinity, achieving optimal balance among redox potential, CO2 capture capacity and energy efficiency8,10,11.

Bipyridines are attractive redox carriers due to strong nucleophilicity. However, conventional bipyridine systems (for example, 4,4’-bipyridine (BPy))8,9 require high oxidation potential to dissociate BPy–CO2 adduct, leading to excessive energy consumption10,11. Thus, it is critical to develop effective molecules that enhance electron delocalization, positively shift reduction potential, and weaken CO2 strength, enabling highly efficient and reversible CO2 capture and release. Thiadiazole has an extended π-conjugated framework that favors electron delocalization, which has been widely applied in organic semiconductors12. Due to high electronegativity of S (2.58) and N (3.04) centers, thiadiazole acts as an electron-withdrawing group in 2,5-bis(4-pyridyl)-1,3,4-thiadiazole (BPT), inducing anodic shift in reduction potential relative to BPy13,14, making it suitable as a redox carrier.

Oxygen sensitivity remains a major challenge for eDAC systems, requiring alternative strategies to improve redox carriers’ stability in O2-rich conditions. Oxygen reduction reaction (ORR, O2/O2•−) has a more positive reduction potential than most redox carriers, which competes with CO2 capture reactions thus reducing system efficiency8,15. Moreover, ORR not only causes oxidative deactivation of redox carriers but also generates destructive superoxides, diminishing CO2 capture capacity10,11,16. To address O2 sensitivity issue, introducing a gas permeation layer (GPL) with gas-selective channel serves as a physical barrier layer that restricts O2 permeation17–19. Rational material design is crucial to limit O2 crossover while ensuring CO2 delivery. Pebax composes poly(ethylene oxide) (PEO) chains enriched with ether oxygen (–O–) functionalities is widely used for separating polar or polarizable gases (e.g., CO2) from non-polar gases (e.g., N2, O2, CH4) owing to intrinsic polarity and flexible chain structure20. The CO2 affinity of ether oxygen facilitates CO2 solubility-diffusion, impeding O2 access to redox carriers and consequently suppressing side reactions.

Herein, we construct a composite system (BPT−GPL) by integrating GPL with BPT redox carrier, achieving efficient CO2 capture and O2-induced side reactions suppression. Under atmospheric conditions (~400 ppm CO2, 21% O2), BPT−GPL system maintains high O2 stability and obtains a high CO2 capture capacity of 3.3 ± 0.2 mmol g–1BPT over 48 cycles without significant degradation. BPT serves as an effective redox carrier for CO2 capture by enhancing electron delocalization, thus anodically shifting reduction potential and lowering O2 sensitivity. Meanwhile, GPL acts as an O2 diffusion barrier, protecting the redox carrier from oxidative deactivation during eDAC.

Results

Thiadiazole redox carrier for CO2 capture

The optimized redox carriers should meet several major criteria for high performance eDAC system. Since ORR competes with redox carriers’ reduction, redox carriers should exhibit a relatively positive reduction potential to reduce O2 reduction. Second, redox carriers should have a moderate CO2 affinity to reduce the energy consumption for releasing CO2. Designing a polycyclic aromatic system is an effective approach to modulate the electronic structure of redox carriers8,12,15. Thiadiazole has a planar structure, which enhances conjugation and electronic communication while functioning as an electron-withdrawing group, lowering reduction potential and optimizing CO2 capture affinity21.

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) analysis is used to assess the redox activity of BPT (Fig. 1a). Under N2, BPT displays two distinct reduction peaks at −1.78 V and −2.24 V vs. Fc⁺/Fc, reflecting a reversible, stepwise two-electron transfer to form anion radicals (BPT•–) and dianions (BPT2–). These reduced species elevate basicity of lone pairs, providing sufficient nucleophilicity to combine with CO2. Under CO2 atmosphere, the peaks merge into a single and quasi-reversible wave and the first peak shifts from −1.78 V to −1.64 V with a 2-fold current density enhancement, indicating CO2 binds to BPT•–, stabilizing the intermediate and driving a concerted two-electron transfer to form a BPT–CO2 adduct. The performance enhancement of BPT is primarily attributed to the decreased electron density on sp2 N, achieved through large-conjugation and the electron-withdrawing properties of thiadiazole ring. BPy possesses a less conjugated system, preventing sp2 N electron density from decreasing compared to BPT. BPy shows a more negative reduction potential under N2 (−2.38 and −2.64 V) and CO2 (−2.00 V vs Fc+/Fc), resulting in reduced O2 stability (Fig. 1b). Although 2,5-di(pyridin-4-yl)thiophene (DPT) and 1,4-di(4-pyridyl)benzene (DPyBz) has large-conjugated system, thiophene and benzene as electron-rich aromatic rings counteract conjugation benefits by increasing electron density on the pyridine units. Thus, DPT and DPyBz display more negative reduction potentials (−2.13 and −2.36 V vs. Fc⁺/Fc, respectively) than BPT, indicating less favorable redox characteristics (Fig. 1c, d). The peak centers shift to −1.82 V and −2.08 V vs. Fc⁺/Fc under CO2, and the peaks are irreversible, characterized by large peak separations (ΔEp > 200 mV) and obvious anodic peak suppression. This irreversibility implies unstable CO2 adducts or competing side reactions, may originate from oxidative degradation or excessive binding strength, thus inhibiting controlled CO2 capture. BPT’s structural advantages enable a more thermodynamically favorable and kinetically stable CO2 capture process.

Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) spectroscopy further validates the reaction behavior of BPT during electrochemical reduction. Before electroreduction, BPT shows an absorption peak at 280 nm. Upon reducing under CO2, the peak intensity decreases, attributed to the formation of new adducts, including BPT(CO2)22– (Fig. 1e). The structure change is reflected by the chemical shifts in the proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectrum after capture electrolysis (Fig. S1)22–24. Similar behavior is observed when reduced BPT is bubbled with CO2, presumably from the combination of reduced BPT (BPT2–/BPT•–) with CO2 (Fig. 1f). Peak intensity recovers upon oxidation, suggesting quasi-reversible adduct formation and dissociation, showing its potential for stable capture-release performance in long-cycle tests.

Effect of O2 and H2O on eDAC

In practical applications, O2 and H2O impurities may affect CO2 capture. ORR (−1.12 V vs Fc⁺/Fc) under >15% O2 or air exhibits a more positive potential than BPT reduction (Figs. 2e, S3), leading to reduced BPT deactivation. Encouragingly, reduced BPT displays a quinoidal-type skeleton31, facilitating charge delocalization within the three-ring conjugated system. Oxygen, as an electron acceptor, preferentially attacks regions of high electron density in molecules, achieving oxidation via single-electron transfer32,33. High electron density promotes oxygen’s electrophilic attack and accelerates the oxidation rate. In the BPT system, charge delocalization promotes a uniform distribution of electron density, reducing accessible oxygen attack sites, thereby suppressing oxidative degradation and stabilizing the reduced species. BPT’s electrochemical behavior suggests low O2 sensitivity, with a reduction potential shift of less than 10 mV even at 20% O2 (Figs. 2e, S3). O2 reduction operates as a distinct process, keeping BPT–CO2 adduct (e.g., BPT(CO2)•–) stable as evidenced by the unchanged reversibility and position of the CO2-related peak (Fig. S4). While O2 slightly reduces electron utilization efficiency, BPT’s preferential CO2 binding ensures consistent capture performance. These findings highlight BPT’s high tolerance to impurities with minimal influence to CO2 capture.

Reduced BPT (BPT•– or BPT2–) is susceptible to protonation in the presence of H2O, but this has a negligible impact on its CO2 binding affinity. CV scans reveal a minor anodic shift in reduction potential (~43 mV) of BPT with increasing H2O content (Figs. 2f, S5). The relatively symmetric anodic and cathodic peaks (ipa/ipc = 0.89) (Fig. S6) evidences BPT retains quasi-reversible even up to 200 kg m−3 H2O, far exceeding humidity air (<0.1 kg m−3, 30–90% relative humidity, RH). The exceptional H2O-tolerance is attributed to a robust conjugated structure that facilitates charge delocalization, lowering the Lewis basicity of reduced BPT species and mitigating protonation-induced degradation.

Investigation of tunable gas-selective channels

To mitigate O2-induced redox carrier deactivation and competitive reactions in practical scenarios (Fig. S7), GPL was integrated with BPT to form a CO2 selective-capture electrode (named as BPT−GPL) (Fig. 3a, b)17–19. GPL fulfills a gas selective-transport role via physical sieving based on kinetic diameter and different chemical interactions with polar CO2 and non-polar O2, selectively decreasing the O2 concentration of the redox carrier interface. The energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping images (Fig. S8) and high-resolution XPS spectra of N 1 s and S 2p reveal the successful preparation of BPT−GPL electrode (Fig. 3c, d)34. FT-IR spectra of GPLs with various Pebax loading (GPL-x; x = 0.1, 0.2, 0.5 mg cm–2) show characteristic peaks corresponding to HN–C=O, O–C=O and –NH– bands at 1635, 1730 and 3300 cm–1 (Fig. 3e). SEM reveals a rough, aggregation-free GPL surface (Fig. S9). GPL-0.1 exhibits some defects (gaps, pinholes), while higher loadings (e.g., GPL-0.2 and GPL-0.5) reduced defects and thickened the layer (~4 μm, Fig. S10), optimizing selectivity. GPL coating selectively inhibits O2 permeation to redox carrier compared to carbon paper (CP). Gas permeability measurements reveal CO2 permeability increased from 2.3 × 109 for CP to 6.2 × 109 cm3 m–2 d–1 Pa–1 for GPL, while O2 decreased (2.6 × 109 for CP vs. CP 1.1 × 109 for GPL), resulting in enhanced CO2/O2 selectivity (Table S1). To gain insights into gas-selective transport through the GPL, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations was performed, following energy minimization (Fig. S13 and Supplementary Data 1). The radial distribution function (RDF) analysis indicates the first peak value for CO2-Pebax is higher than that of O2-Pebax, due to dipole-quadrupole interactions between CO2 and Pebax’s ether oxygen groups, suggesting the stronger CO2-Pebax affinity (Fig. 3f)20,35. The higher interaction energies of CO2-Pebax (−208.2 kcal mol−1) promote CO2 to preferentially interact with and rapidly diffuse through GPL compared to O2 (−31.9 kcal mol−1) (Fig. S14). Notably, the smaller kinetic diameter of CO2 (3.33 Å) also makes it more readily pass through GPL contrasted with O2 (3.46 Å). Given the synergistic effect of chemical interaction and physical sieving of Pebax material, GPL with tunable gas-selective transport channels is obtained. As such, GPL ensures sufficient local CO2 availability while restricting O2 delivery and inhibiting O2-induced side reactions (such as O2 reduction and redox carrier deactivation). Moreover, GPL also promotes CO2 capture by creating a three-phase microenvironment (solid, electrolyte and gas), resulting in decreased CO2 diffusion resistance and elevated CO2 availability near the redox carrier, thereby maintaining carbon capture performance under air conditions.

eDAC performance evaluation

The intrinsic cyclic capture was examined in a gas-circulating cell at 1,000 ppm CO2 balanced with N2 (Fig. S15), employing BPT on CP as the work electrode (BPT−CP) with lithium bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide (LiTFSI) as supporting salt (Fig. S16). CO2 concentration oscillates periodically with charge-discharge cycles at 0.2−0.8 mA cm–2 (Fig. S17−20), evidencing BPT’s reversible CO2 capture. At 0.2 mA cm–2, charging reduced CO2 from ~1,000 ppm to 253 ppm (Fig. S17), with discharge oxidizing the BPT–CO2 adduct to recover CO2 levels, achieving 2.7 ± 0.3 mmol g–1BPT capacity (Fig. S20). Increasing current density to 0.4 mA cm–2 causes a dropped oscillations and capture capacity, which becomes more pronounced with 0.8 mA cm–2 (Figs. S18, S19), reflecting the rate of BPT reduction exceeded BPT–CO2 adduct formation. In subsequent tests, a rest period is introduced following a reduction to improve CO2 capture capacity and efficiency by facilitating the utilization of reduced BPT species.

To study eDAC using BPT−GPL-0.2 in practical conditions, long-term cycling was conducted using a two-chamber gas-phase flow cell under ambient air (~400 ppm, 21% O2) (Figs. 4a, S21). A stable variation of CO2 concentration is observed with near-100% of CO2 release/capture efficiency, validating BPT’s selective and reversible CO2 capture (Fig. 4b, c, S22). The GPL-based cell achieves an average CO2 capacity of 3.3 ± 0.2 mmol g–1BPT (~1.1 kg m–3electrode) and Faradaic efficiency (FE) of ~79.6 ± 3.8% with energy consumption of 106.9 kJ mol–1 CO2. In this case, GPL elevates O2 diffusion barrier owing to a weak O2 affinity with GPL, resulting in sluggish kinetics of O2-induced side reactions, favoring dilute CO2 capture (Fig. 4d). In contrast, the untreated BPT−CP permits co-diffusion of CO2 and O2 to redox carrier through intrinsic micropores, exhibiting a capacity decline from 3.1 ± 0.3 to 2.6 ± 0.4 mmol g–1BPT and FE drop from 78.0 ± 4.9% to 65.7 ± 2.9% (Fig. S23, S24). Notably, BPT exhibits competitive CO2 capacity with the recently reported redox carriers, attributed to the synergistic effects of GPL enhancing O2 tolerance via suppressed O2 diffusion and favorable CO2-GPL interactions (Table S2). Furthermore, BPT−GPL maintains highly structural stability, confirmed by the unchanged XPS spectrum of GPL and BPT after cycling tests (Fig. S25, S26). Air usually contains a certain amount of water, thus a more realistic assessment of the cyclability was implemented using BPT−GPL system under humid air (55% RH). Stable operation is achieved over 46 cycles while maintaining a FE of ~77.9 ± 4.1% (Fig. 4e, f). The FE shows a slight decrease after 46 cycles, attributed to competitive permeation of water molecules in GPL, decreasing the hydrophobicity of the electrode, thus damaging a three-phase interface and preventing gaseous CO2 from reaching the redox carrier.

The GPL proves crucial in enhancing O2 tolerance of BPT–CO2 adduct, as evaluated by quantifying oxidation under air (Fig. S27, S28). BPT−CP and BPT−GPL were reduced under CO2, rested in air, and re-reduced under CO2 to measure capacity loss. BPT−GPL shows less loss (14.7%) compared to BPT−CP (27.6%), attributed to ether oxygen groups restricting O2 transfer while selectively facilitating CO2 permeation, affirming GPL’s protective role.

Mechanistic studies

To understand the formation mechanism of BPT−CO2 adduct, in situ IR and DFT calculations were employed during the electrochemical process. Under CO2 and N2, C–Npyridine stretching (1563 cm–1) and C–Npyridine bending (1440 cm–1) displays reversible intensity changes during cathodic and anodic potential scan, affirming the reversibility of BPT (Figs. 5a, S29, S30)36–38. Compared with the spectroscopy under N2, a new peak under CO2 at 1580 cm–1 belonging to C=O stretching exhibits a gradual rise upon electro-reduction, and then decreases upon electro-oxidation, corresponding to the dissociation and formation of BPT−CO2 adduct (Fig. 5a, b)33,39. The characteristic C–Npyridine has a slight redshift (from 1558 to 1563 cm–1) and the appearance of Npyridine–CO2 peak is at 1309 cm–1 under CO2, indicating the sp2 N of pyridine units is more likely to bind CO2 (Fig. 5a)36. The complexation of BPT with CO2 proceeds via an ECE mechanism (where E represents electron transfer and C represents chemical reaction), as suggested by CV analysis. The current intensity under CO2 is nearly doubled compared to that of the first reduction peak under N2 (Fig. 1a). The current intensity increases with increasing scan rate as steady is approached under CO2 and N2, suggesting a scan-rate dependence is consistent with a typical ECE mechanism (Fig. S31)8,40,41. Overall, the reaction mechanism between CO2 and BPT is proposed (Fig. 5d). BPT redox carrier first accepts one electron to form BPT•–, simultaneously binding to CO2 (BPT(CO2)•–). Due to decreased electron density for BPT•– via the electron-withdrawal effect of CO2, BPT(CO2)•– is further reduced at the same potential for the following complexation with the second CO2 (BPT(CO2)22–).

The negative charge in reduced BPT species is delocalized through resonance effects, enhancing the stability of reduced BPT and promoting a positive shift in the reduction potential. This phenomenon is demonstrated by comparing BPT with 2,5-di(3-pyridyl)-1,3,4-thiadiazole (2,5-DPT), which lacks resonance effects (Fig. 5e, f). In the reduced state, 2,5-DPT cannot switch to a resonance structure, leading to a negative shift in its reduction potential. Additionally, 2,5-DPT exhibits a more positive oxidation potential, thus requiring more energy to release CO2 (Fig. 5g). The resonance effects of BPT prove advantageous for CO2 release, highlighting its favorable performance compared to 2,5-DPT.

Discussion

The success of eDAC relies on the design of redox molecules and mitigating O2 interference, both of which are effectively addressed by the BPT−GPL system. By optimizing the conjugation and incorporating electron-withdrawing groups, BPT exhibits an optimized electronic structure that preferentially binds CO2 over competing O2 species. GPL plays a pivotal role in suppressing O2-induced side reactions for eDAC and reducing BPT inactivation. The GPL leverages the polarity difference between CO2 and O2 to create gas-selective channels, promoting CO2 diffusion while restricting O2 access to the redox carrier. The dual-role strategy by combining BPT’s molecular stability with GPL’s gas-selective transport outperforms conventional approaches like amine-based sorbents, which degrade rapidly under an O2-rich environment, providing a robust and energy-efficient alternative method for eDAC.

Coupling the BPT−GPL system with electrochemical conversion can directly convert atmospheric CO2 into fuels and chemicals such as formic acid or ethylene, avoiding additional CO2-storge while achieving a carbon-neutral cycle. The rapid operation of eDAC can be integrated with intermittent renewable energy sources, achieving sustainable development of resources.

This work presents an efficient eDAC strategy by integrating the BPT redox carrier with gas-selective GPL channels. The electron-deficient thiadiazole ring and extended conjugation in BPT facilitate electron delocalization, inducing anodic shift in reduction potential and improving O2 tolerance. By exploiting the polarity difference between CO2 and O2, gas-selective transport channels are constructed to restrict O2 delivery and create a low-O2 microenvironment, ensuring the stability of reduced BPT species. The BPT−GPL electrode achieves a high CO2 capture capacity of 3.2 ± 0.2 mmol g–1BPT over 48 cycles without O2-induced degradation, effectively suppressing side reactions. MD simulations further confirm that ether oxygen groups in GPL exhibit weaker O2 affinity, limiting O2 diffusion to the redox carrier while ensuring CO2 transport. This engineered BPT−GPL architecture not only paves the way for efficient eDAC but also provides a next-generation platform for CO2 capture under O2-rich conditions, advancing the development of practical, low-energy routes for sustainable carbon management and global decarbonization.

Methods

Materials

All chemicals were purchased and not further purified. 2,5-bis(4-pyridyl)-1,3,4-thiadiazole (CAS No.: 15311-09-8, purity: >95%), 4,4’-bipyridine (CAS No.: 553-26-4, purity: >98%), 2,5-di(pyridin-4-yl)thiophene (CAS No.: 141511-42-4, purity: >95%), 1,4-di(4-pyridyl)benzene (CAS No.: 113682-56-7, purity: 97%) and 2,5-di(3-pyridyl)-1,3,4-thiadiazole (CAS No.: 15362-52-4, purity: 97%) were purchased from Pharmatech Ltd. DMSO (anhydrous, ≥99.9%) and 1-butyl-3-methylimidazolium bis(trifluoromethyl sulfonyl)imide (BmimTFSI) (purity: 98%) and LiTFSI (purity: 99%) were purchased from Aladdin Industrial Co. Ltd. Pt wire and Ag+/Ag electrodes were purchased from Ida Co. Ltd.

Material characterization

The morphology of all samples was characterized by a Hitachi Regulus 8100 scanning electron microscopy. XPS spectra were collected by a Thermo ESCALAB 250XI spectrometer. Single gas permeability was recorded with the Labthink BTY-B2P breathability tester. IR spectra were obtained on a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (iS50).

UV-vis spectroscopy

BPT (5 mmol) in DMSO (7 mL) with TBAPF6 as supporting electrolyte was electrochemically reduced at a constant current of 2 mA under N2, then exposed to a CO2 atmosphere for 30 min, and finally put under N2 to be oxidized. For comparison, BPT (5 mmol) in DMSO (7 mL) with TBAPF6 as supporting electrolyte was electrochemically reduced at a constant current of 2 mA under CO2, and put under N2 to be oxidized. The optical absorption changes of the BPT solution were examined by a N5000Plus ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer (wavelength from 190 to 1100 nm).

Preparation of the BPT−GPL electrode

The ink was prepared by dispersing BPT (10 mg) and Vulcan XC 72 R (2 mg) in a mixture of isopropanol (0.5 mL) and chloroform (0.5 mL) containing Nafion (5 wt%, 50 μL). Then, the mixture solution was ultrasonicated for 1 h to obtain a homogeneous ink. The ink was sprayed on CP at a set temperature of 40 °C. The loadings of the catalysts were controlled to be 1 mg cm–2. The exposed geometrical surface area was maintained at 2 × 2 cm2. The GPL layer was prepared by a solvent evaporation method17,20. To obtain a polymer solution (4 mg mL–1), Pebax 1657 was dissolved in a mixture of ethanol and H2O (v/v = 7/3) at 70 °C. Then, the polymer solution was sprayed on CP to form a GPL layer, with loading about 0.1, 0.2, and 0.5 mg cm–2. After spraying, the electrode was dried in air for 12 h and then dried in a vacuum oven for 24 h at 80 °C.

Electrochemical characterization

Electrochemical characterization was carried out using a CHI 660E electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, Chenhua) in a conventional single-compartment electrolytic cell (Ida, Tianjin) at 25 °C. A Pt wire and an Ag/Ag+ electrode (R0202, Ida) were used as counter electrode and quasi-reference electrode with ferrocene as the internal reference, respectively. A glassy carbon electrode (0.071 cm2) was applied as a work electrode. CV data were collected for the molecules (2 mmol) in DMSO (10 mL) with TBAPF6 (0.1 mol L–1) under N2 and CO2 atmospheres, respectively. The scan rate is 50 mV s−1, unless otherwise specified. To study the effects of cationic species on the redox behavior of molecules, LiTFSI, NaTFSI, and TBAPF6 were used as the supporting salt. The Ag+/Ag quasi-reference electrode was calibrated by ferrocene as the internal reference. Assemble of flow-based eDAC

Electrochemical CO2 capture and release tests were conducted in a two-chamber flow cell (details in Figs. 4a and S21). The system was assembled by using BPT−GPL and LiFePO4 as CO2 capture and counter electrodes, respectively, which were separated by a glass microfiber filter. The electrolyte solution was prepared by dissolving LiTFSI (1 mol L−1) in BmimTFSI ionic liquid before each test. 320 and 200 μL of electrolyte were placed on the counter and working electrode, respectively. Treatment of the glass microfiber filter

The glass microfiber filter (3×3 cm2, Whatman, GF/A) was dried in vacuo at 100 °C for 24 h before use.

DFT calculations

DFT calculations have been carried out by ORCA quantum chemistry software42 (Version 6.0.0). The B3LYP functional was adopted for all calculations. For geometry optimization and frequency calculations, the 6-311 + G(d,p) basis set43 was used, and the optimal geometry for each compound was determined. The single point energy calculations were performed with a larger basis set, the 6-311 + + G (2df, 2pd) basis set. The DFT-D3 dispersion correction with BJ-damping44 was applied to correct the weak interaction to improve the calculation accuracy. The SMD implicit solvation model was used to account for the solvation effect. Finally, the single point energy of each compound was added to the free energy correction terms calculated before to obtain the Gibbs free energy. Then, the Gibbs free energy change of the binding process was calculated.

MD simulations

The MD simulations were performed in the GROMACS 2021 software package45–47, and the simulations were performed for 20 ns. In NPT simulations, the temperature was maintained by the V-rescale thermostat at 298.15 K, and the pressure was maintained at 1 bar by the Berendsen barostat in an isotropic manner. The Particle-Mesh-Ewald (PME) with a fourth-order interpolation was used to evaluate the electrostatic interactions, and a cutoff of 1.4 nm was employed to calculate the short-range van der Waals interactions48.
