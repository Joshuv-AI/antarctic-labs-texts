Short-term exposure to ultrafine particles and asthma hospital admissions in children in Copenhagen, Denmark
Authors: Marie Bergmann, Zorana J Andersen, Andreas Massling, Steffen Loft, Thomas Cole-Hunter, Claus Nordstrøm, Stéphane Tuffier, Jiawei Zhang, Youn-Hee Lim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUltrafine particles (UFP; <0.1 µm in diameter) are not regulated or commonly monitored but may be harmful to human health, particularly for children. In this study, we aimed to examine the association between short-term exposure to UFP and asthma hospital admissions in children.MethodsDaily UFP concentrations (2002–2018) were monitored at an urban background station in Copenhagen, Denmark. Asthma hospital admissions, demographic and socioeconomic information of children (0–18 years) were obtained from registries. A case-crossover design was applied to estimate the association between hospital admissions and up to 6-day UFP exposure windows for all children, and stratified by age, sex, family income, mother’s education, prior asthma or prior respiratory infection.ResultsWe observed 15 903 asthma hospital admissions in total. An IQR increase in UFP was significantly associated with asthma hospital admissions, strongest at 2-day exposure windows (risk ratio (RR): 1.17 (95% CI: 1.09, 1.25)). These associations remained unchanged when adjusting for particulate matter <2.5 µm in diameter (PM2.5) or nitrogen dioxide (NO2), for which we also detected significant positive associations. Associations with UFP were stronger for school-aged children (5–14 years: RR: 1.26 (95% CI: 1.15, 1.38)) than for children younger than 5 years (1.01 (95% CI: 0.93, 1.10)).ConclusionsIn this large study in a low-exposure setting, we find that short-term exposure to UFP can trigger asthma hospital admissions in children, independently of associations with PM2.5 or NO2. This study adds evidence calling for the regulation and improvement of UFP exposure assessment to protect children’s health in urban areas.Keywords: Asthma, Paediatric asthma, Asthma Epidemiology

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Previous studies suggest potential associations of short-term exposure to ultrafine particles and asthma hospital admissions, which might be strongest in children. Few studies have examined this association in children, of which two found significant increases in asthma emergency room and outpatient visits in children in China, robust to adjustment for copollutants. Others found weak, non-significant associations with asthma hospital admissions. It remains unclear whether these associations are independent from those with larger particles or nitrogen dioxide, as associations diminished after adjustment in some of these studies, or two-pollutant models were not presented.

WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

We add to the limited and inconclusive evidence base on the association between short-term exposure to ultrafine particles and asthma hospital admissions in children by presenting results of an exceptionally long time series (17 years) of monitoring data of ultrafine particles and detailed, individual-level sociodemographic and health data from comprehensive Danish registers. We apply state-of-the-art case-crossover models and analyse different lag structures, effect modification by age and sex, and two-pollutant models. Additionally, we examine possible effect modification by socioeconomic status and previous health conditions, which has not been done in previous studies.

Introduction

Increasing evidence suggests that ultrafine particles (UFP; <0.1 µm in diameter) are harmful to human health.1,3 Their small size and large surface-area-to-mass ratio allow them to penetrate deep into the lung and translocate to other organs, while carrying relatively large amounts of potentially toxic organic compounds.1 Exposure to UFP can cause oxidative stress in the body, leading to inflammation and tissue damage, and subsequently to respiratory diseases.1 2 However, epidemiological research on the health effects of UFP is hampered by a lack of monitoring data. Unlike particle metrics measured as mass and including larger particles, such as particulate matter with a diameter less than 2.5 µm (PM2.5) or 10 µm (PM10), for which health effects are more established, particle number in general and especially UFP are not regulated and routinely monitored. The recently updated WHO Air Quality Guidelines include a ‘Good Practice Statement’ on UFP, emphasising a need for more monitoring and epidemiological studies of UFP with homogeneous study designs and exposure assessment.4

Studies on short-term exposure to UFP and hospital contacts due to respiratory diseases have suggested that these effects may be strongest among children.2 5 This is likely explained by children’s continuously developing respiratory system, immature defence mechanisms for detoxification and a different metabolism compared with adults, making children more susceptible to the adverse health effects of UFP.6 In addition, children’s exposure patterns are different from those of adults, as they spend more time outdoors, are more physically active, and inhale more air volume per body weight than adults, leading to relatively higher exposures within the same environment.6 Studies have found that short-term exposure to UFP can lead to systemic inflammation, decreased lung function, alterations in airway inflammatory biomarkers or symptoms such as wheezing in children, especially in those with existing respiratory conditions such as asthma.6 7 There are only a few studies that examined the association of short-term exposure to UFP and asthma hospital admissions in children, and they show somewhat mixed results. Two Danish studies found weak, non-significant associations between UFP and asthma hospital admissions in children, which diminished after adjustment for larger particles or nitrogen dioxide (NO2).8 9 A Finnish study detected associations between UFP and asthma emergency room visits in children, which were independent of PM2.5 but diminished after adjustment for NO2.10 A US study detected associations between UFP and asthma exacerbation in children with asthma in single pollutant models but did not evaluate whether these were robust to adjustment for NO2 or PM2.5.11 Lastly, two studies from China found significant increases in asthma emergency room and outpatient visits in children, which were robust to adjustment for NO2 and PM2.5.12 13

There are a few factors that possibly alter the susceptibility to short-term exposure to UFP in children. Besides age and sex as known effect modifiers, socioeconomic status (SES) is an important determinant of children’s health. A recent scoping review of SES as an effect modifier of the association between air pollution and children’s respiratory health found increased risks for children in lower SES groups.14 Moreover, childhood acute respiratory infections such as respiratory viruses like human rhinovirus (HRV), respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), bocavirus or influenza, and bacterial infections have been hypothesised to affect children’s risk of developing asthma later in life.15 While the differential susceptibility by sex and age groups has been examined in three studies on UFP and asthma hospital admissions in children,9 12 13 no study to date has considered SES, prior asthma or childhood respiratory infections.

In Copenhagen, UFPs have been monitored as part of the Danish Air Quality Monitoring Programme since 2001. In addition, well-established Danish registries make it possible to access complete, validated information on the population’s health status, as well as individual demographic and socioeconomic characteristics. In this study, we use 17 years of UFP routine measurements between 2002 and 2018 to examine the associations between short-term exposure to UFP and asthma hospital admissions in children and identify susceptible groups by age, sex, SES, prior asthma or prior acute respiratory infections.

Methods

Study population, demographic data and SES

We included all asthma hospital admissions in children (0–18 years at admission) living in Greater Copenhagen (online supplemental figure S1). Information on municipality of residence, age and sex was available from the Danish Civil Registration System, which was linked to health registries based on individual civil registration numbers. In addition, we used two indicators of SES: household disposable income, which is the total income in a household weighed by the number of adults and children in a family, and mothers’ highest attained education, extracted from income and education registers.16 17

Health outcome data

Admissions to eight hospitals located within the study area (online supplemental figure S1) were obtained from the Danish National Patient Register18 for the years 2002 to 2018. We included inpatients, outpatients and emergency room visits, with a primary diagnosis of asthma (International Classification of Disease 10th revision (ICD-10) codes J45–46). ICD-10 codes have been used in Denmark since 1994. Readmissions for asthma within less than 28 days after a previous admission, likely follow-up visits, were excluded. We included only patients with a registered address within the study area at the time of admission. Moreover, we recorded previous hospital contacts (inpatients, outpatients and emergency room visits) for respiratory infections (HRV, RSV and influenza, which typically dominate in Denmark19) if they occurred before the age of 5, to represent early childhood and before the first asthma admission (ICD-10 codes in online supplemental table S1).

Exposure assessment

Detailed information on the study’s exposure assessment can be found elsewhere.20 In short, air pollution data used in this study originate from a regulatory urban background monitoring station in central Copenhagen (online supplemental figure S1), which is located on top of a building at a height of ca. 20 m. Measurements of UFPs were performed using a custom-built differential mobility particle sizer, which counts particles with an atmospheric diameter of 6–700 nm at 2.5 min time resolution, averaged to 30 min intervals, as total particle number concentration (PNC), which is a common proxy for UFP. It was found at a later stage that measurements below 11 nm were associated with high uncertainties in the size selection and in the counting statistics. This issue is based on the hardware used in the instrument, and for that reason, data below 11 nm in size were removed. Thus, the final dataset included PNC of particles with diameters of 11–700 nm. Additionally, we obtained daily mean concentrations of PM2.5, NO2 and ozone (O3), as well as temperature and relative humidity, from the same monitoring station.

Statistical analysis

First, we calculated daily mean values for all exposure variables and merged them with numbers of hospital admissions on the same day. We summarised daily data as number of observations (n), median, arithmetic mean, SD, minimum and maximum values, percentiles and IQR. We calculated Spearman correlation coefficients to evaluate the correlation between air pollutants.

Next, we applied a time-stratified case-crossover design21 to estimate the association between air pollutants and asthma hospital admissions. Specifically, we compared the air pollution concentration on the day of a hospital admission to the concentration on the same weekdays of the same month and calendar year, using conditional logistic regression. Thus, each case served as its own control, which prevents confounding by personal characteristics that do not vary over time. Moreover, confounders that vary slowly over time, such as seasonality and long-term trends, are controlled for by choosing control days close to the admission day. To ensure that key assumptions for conditional logistic regression were met, we (1) assessed multicollinearity of predictor variables in the model through variance inflation factors (VIF), (2) tested the linearity of the exposure response function using the Akaike information criterion (AIC)22 and (3) assessed the independency of observations in our dataset. Assumption (1) was met with VIFs for included variables ranging from 1.00 to 1.04. Assumption (2) was met, as a comparison of AICs between a linear and non-linear exposure response function indicated a better model fit for a linear term. Assumption (3) was met through the nature of the case-crossover design, which matches weekdays of daily data of air pollution and numbers of hospital admissions within the same months. Both of these are independent observations within the strata. Effect estimates are reported as risk ratio (RR) and 95% CIs associated with IQR increases in daily mean concentrations of air pollutants. We adjusted for meteorological variables by including daily mean temperature and relative humidity in the model. Furthermore, we included a categorical variable for public holidays or the day after a public holiday. In order to account for delayed health effects, we investigated exposure windows of 2-day moving averages (ie, lag 0–1: mean of the concentrations on the day before plus the day of the asthma hospital admission) to 7-day (ie, lag 0–6: mean of the concentrations on the day of the hospital admission plus six preceding days’ concentrations) moving averages of air pollutants prior to an asthma hospital admission,23 which have been identified as the period relevant for health effects of short-term exposure to air pollution.24 These moving averages represent cumulative exposure to UFP prior to a hospital admission for up to a week. Exposure-response curves were plotted using generalised additive models with Poisson distributions, adjusting for temperature (using thin plate regression splines with four df, chosen based on the AIC), time trend (5 df per year) and categorical variables for the day of the week and public holidays. The attributable fraction of asthma cases was calculated by first determining the daily excess RRs relative to a counterfactual scenario of UFP concentration of zero. The daily fraction of asthma cases attributable to UFP was then computed using the formula (RR-1)/RR.

Possible effect modification was examined by stratifying analyses by sex (female/male), age (0–4/5–14/15–18 years old), categories of disposable household income (quartiles, calculated separately for each year, to account for inflation), mother’s education (primary/upper secondary/vocational/short cycle higher education/college+), prior asthma admission (first/second+admission) or prior acute respiratory infections (before age of 5 and prior to asthma admission). We then compared point estimates and CIs between groups to test whether differences were statistically significant.25 In two-pollutant models, we additionally adjusted for PM2.5 and NO2. A comparison of single-pollutant and two-pollutant models was made based on a complete dataset of daily information on all three pollutants (UFP, PM2.5 and NO2).

We performed several sensitivity analyses. First, to account for possible exposure misclassification owing to the use of a single monitoring station, we restricted cases to children living in the two municipalities closest to the monitoring station (ie, in an 8 km radius) and repeated the analysis using the same models as described above. Second, to account for missing exposure data, we restricted our analysis to those days with at least three available control days with available daily mean UFP on the same weekdays within the same month and year as the event day, excluding all other days and repeating the same analysis as described above.

Statistical analyses were performed in R statistical software (V.4.1.2; The R Foundation, 2021) using the season and mgcv functions.

Results

Descriptive statistics

On 6209 days, we counted 15 903 asthma hospital admissions in children up to 18 years, with three daily admissions on average (table 1). The highest absolute number of admissions was observed in children younger than 5 years (n=7537), followed by children aged 5–14 years (n=6881). Boys were more often admitted for asthma than girls (n=9984 and 5919, respectively). Most admissions were first-time admissions (n=10 370), while 5533 children were readmitted after a prior asthma admission. Moreover, 285 asthma hospital admissions occurred among children with a prior acute respiratory infection before the age of 5. Daily mean concentrations of air pollutants were 5028 (SD: 2592) pt/cm3 for UFP, 13 (8) µg/m3 for PM2.5, 10 (5) ppb for NO2 and 33 (8) for O3 (table 2). Mean concentrations of UFP and NO2 decreased during the study period, while PM2.5 showed no clear trend and ozone concentrations increased (online supplemental figure S2). Both UFP and asthma hospital admissions showed a decreasing trend during the study period (figure 1). Due to different monitoring periods and data removed during quality control, daily concentrations of UFP and PM2.5 were missing for about 44% and 43% of the study period, respectively (see online supplemental figure S2). Days with missing UFP did not differ in terms of relative humidity, day of the week, or PM2.5 or NO2 concentrations, but were slightly warmer than days with available UFP (online supplemental table S3). UFP was moderately correlated with NO2 (Spearman’s r correlation, rs=0.55, p<0.001) and PM2.5 (rs=0.40, p<0.001) (online supplemental figure S3) and weakly correlated with temperature and relative humidity (online supplemental table S2).

Associations between air pollutants and hospital admissions

One IQR increase in UFP was significantly associated with an increase in asthma hospital admissions in children up to 18 years on the same day (RR: 1.12 (95% CI: 1.06, 1.19)). We observed positive, significant associations for all exposure windows up to 7-day means (table 3), with the strongest associations at 2-day means of UFP (RR: 1.17 (95% CI: 1.09, 1.25)). The mean daily attributable fraction for the 2-day mean UFP exposure was 22%. Over the 17-year study period, this corresponded to an estimated total of 3526 asthma hospital admissions, with 207 admissions per year on average, attributable to UFP. Similarly, an IQR increase in PM2.5 was positively, significantly associated with asthma hospital admissions in children up to 18 years on the same day and up to 6-day exposure windows, with the highest associations at 2-day means (RR: 1.13 (95% CI: 1.09, 1.18)). An IQR increase in NO2 was associated with increases in asthma hospital admissions in children up to 18 years on the same day (RR: 1.04 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.07)) and at 2-day means (RR: 1.05 (95% CI: 1.01, 1.10)). Warm-season O3 was associated with increases in asthma hospital admissions in the same age group at 4-day means (RR: 1.18 (95% CI: 1.02, 1.37)).

Among the age subgroups, associations were strongest for children aged 5–14 years, where an IQR increase in UFP on the same day was associated with an RR of 1.26 (95% CI: 1.15, 1.38) (figure 2). Moreover, associations were positive and significant for adolescents aged 15–18 (RR: 1.24 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.54)) and children aged 0–14 (RR: 1.11 (95% CI: 1.05, 1.18)), while they were non-significant for children under the age of 5 (RR: 1.01 (95% CI: 0.93, 1.10)). Furthermore, associations were slightly, but not significantly, stronger for boys (RR: 1.15 (95% CI: 1.07, 1.24)) than for girls (RR: 1.07 (95% CI: 0.97, 1.19)). There were no significant differences in associations between the different categories of family income or mother’s education. For children with a prior asthma admission, the associations between UFP (same day) and asthma hospital admissions were considerably higher than for those who were admitted for the first time (RR: 1.24 (95% CI: 1.12, 1.37) and RR: 1.06 (95% CI: 0.98, 1.14), respectively). Moreover, we found strong, yet non-significant associations for children with an acute respiratory infection before the age of 5 (RR: 1.45 (95% CI: 0.84, 2.49)).

Exposure-response curves of the association between UFP and asthma hospital admissions in children up to 18 years showed a steep increase up to UFP concentrations of approximately 5000 pt/cm3, whereafter CIs widened (online supplemental figure S4). In a comparison of single-pollutant and two-pollutant models using a subset of data with complete information on UFP, PM2.5 and NO2 (n=2009 days), we did not observe significant changes after mutual adjustment of air pollutants (online supplemental figure S5).

In sensitivity analyses, we found that associations between UFP and asthma hospital admissions remained positive, but not significant, when restricting the analysis to children living close to the monitoring station (online supplemental table S4). In analyses restricted to those days that had at least three available control days (n=3033 days), we found a similar association to that in the main analysis (RR: 1.07 (95% CI: 0.99, 1.14), lag 0).

Discussion

We analysed the associations of short-term UFP exposure and other air pollutants with asthma hospital admissions in children based on 17 years of data from Copenhagen, Denmark. We found significant, positive associations between short-term UFP exposure and asthma hospital admissions on the same day, as well as for multiple-day exposure windows of up to 1 week. Those associations were strongest in children aged 5–14 years, and in boys. Moreover, we observed stronger associations for those with a prior asthma admission and those who had been admitted to the hospital for an acute respiratory infection (HRV, RSV or influenza) under the age of 5. Different indicators of SES, or adjustment for PM2.5 or NO2, did not change associations significantly.

The few previous studies on short-term exposure to UFP and asthma hospital admissions in children found mostly positive, but non-significant associations. Specifically, a study from Helsinki, Finland, found a 6.60% (95% CI: 2.34, 11.00) increase in asthma-related emergency room visits of children younger than 15 years per 5760 pt/cm3 increase in PNC lagged 4 days that were independent of PM2.5 but diminished after adjustment for NO2.10 In Copenhagen, two previous studies of shorter time series (4–8 years) found positive, but non-significant associations between UFP and asthma hospital admissions of children up to 18 years (OR: 1.06 (95% CI: 0.98, 1.14) per 3813 pt/cm3 increase in 5-day mean PNC),9 and children aged 5–18 years (RR: 1.07 (95% CI: 0.98, 1.17) per 3907 pt/cm3 increase in 6-day mean PNC), both of which diminished after copollutant adjustment.8 Two studies from Shanghai, China, found positive associations between 1800 pt/cm3 increases in 3-day mean PNC and asthma emergency room visits (RR: 1.35 (95% CI: 1.14, 1.59))12 and outpatient visits (RR: 1.21 (95% CI: 1.07, 1.38))13 in children. Studies that included all respiratory hospital admissions in children, beyond asthma, generally found positive, but non-significant associations with UFP concentrations at longer exposure windows.26 27

Generally, in research on air pollution, even a small risk estimate may correspond to a large number of cases attributed to air pollution due to the ubiquitous nature of the exposure. As such, we estimated that 22% of all asthma hospital admissions in children in our 17-year study period, corresponding to 207 admissions per year, were attributable to short-term increases in UFP concentrations.

For PM2.5, both previous studies from Copenhagen found significant, positive associations similar to our study, where we, however, observed stronger associations for longer exposure windows. Our findings are also in line with a meta-analysis of studies on short-term PM2.5 exposure and children’s asthma hospital admissions and emergency room visits, which found an RR of 1.05 (95% CI: 1.03, 1.07) per 10 µg/m3 increase in PM2.5.28 Similarly, our results for NO2 are comparable to the previous findings from Copenhagen and to a meta-analysis of short-term exposure to NO2 and hospital admissions or emergency room visits due to asthma.29 The same meta-analysis also found positive, significant associations for O3, in line with the positive associations in our current study.

Concentrations of air pollution are comparably low in Copenhagen, with mean UFP concentrations at ~5000 pt/cm3. Other studies using background monitor UFP data with comparable cut-offs for particle diameter found significantly higher mean concentrations, such as 6300–7700 pt/cm3 in three German cities,27 12 000 pt/cm3 (median) in London,30 8200 pt/cm3 in Helsinki10 or 11 400 pt/cm3 in Barcelona.31 This could be attributed to Copenhagen’s policies for improving air quality, to its geographical location with no major UFP emission areas nearby, but also to the relatively high placement of the background monitor at 20 m height.

We assessed several possible effect modifiers in our study. First, differential susceptibility to short-term UFP exposure and asthma hospital admissions by age has been assessed in three previous studies, of which one found no differences between age groups,9 while two found stronger associations among school-aged children (<13 years).12 13 In our study, we found a clear difference in susceptibility among school-aged children compared with children under 5, where we found no associations. This could, among other factors, be related to difficulties in diagnosing asthma in children under 5,32 leading to outcome misclassification and bias towards the null. Second, findings indicate that pre-existing respiratory conditions (prior asthma or childhood respiratory infections) play an important role in children’s susceptibility to adverse health effects of UFP. This is in line with a previous study from Rochester, New York, where a 2088 pt/cm3 increase in 4-day mean UFP was associated with an OR of 1.27 (95% CI: 0.90, 1.79) for asthma exacerbation in children.11 In addition to that, studies of respiratory biomarkers, such as lung function and airway inflammation, suggest that asthmatic children are particularly susceptible to adverse health effects of short-term UFP exposure.33 34 Lastly, we did not find differential effects by SES in this study. This relationship has not previously been explored for UFP. However, a scoping review found that most available studies report greater associations between air pollution and respiratory diseases for children with lower SES,14 possibly related to a higher burden of existing health conditions. Nonetheless, SES could also have an effect in the opposite direction, as higher individual-level SES has been associated with higher UFP exposure in Denmark.35

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this study include the use of health and exposure data covering a long study period of 17 years. Furthermore, we were able to link to detailed pre-existing health conditions and individual-level indicators on SES to identify susceptible groups, which is not usually done in time-series studies.

Some limitations have to be acknowledged. First, a commonly discussed limitation of comparable studies is the use of a single urban background station for exposure assessment. In order to assess the city-wide representativeness of background-level UFP measurements, we have previously compared them to measurements from a street-level monitoring station in central Copenhagen (2002–2016) and found moderate correlation (r=0.58) of daily means, indicating that background-level UFP might accurately represent temporal variability of UFP throughout the city.20 Additionally, a recent measurement campaign of UFP in Copenhagen found good agreement between daily mean concentrations at 7 fixed monitoring stations and 37 residential locations across Copenhagen.36 Moreover, the current study’s results did not change significantly when restricting the analysis to children living close to the monitoring station. Nonetheless, microenvironments and activity patterns play an important role in children’s UFP exposure. As children spend most time indoors, at home and in schools, where UFP concentrations vary substantially based on location, on activities such as cooking or smoking inside, mould/dampness or pet ownership, those exposures should ideally be considered in epidemiological studies.1 6 Furthermore, outdoor UFP concentrations vary greatly by distance to traffic, and peak exposures can be experienced while moving through the city.37 While it would be informative to examine children’s exposure patterns in Copenhagen, we do not expect exposure misclassification to have led to an overestimation of effect estimates. In fact, exposure misclassification generally leads to a bias towards the null, which is why our results might be even higher without exposure misclassification.38 A second limitation is the amount of missing air pollution data in our study, which was as high as 44% and 43% for UFP and PM2.5, respectively. However, missing air pollution data did not follow a systematic pattern, such as by month, day of the week or meteorology, and sensitivity analyses suggested that associations for cases with at least three available control days were similar to our main analysis. Lastly, we did not explore possible interactions between UFP and other relevant exposures such as temperature or pollen, which should be addressed in future studies.

Conclusions

Short-term exposure to UFP may be associated with asthma hospital admissions in children in Copenhagen. Children aged 5–14 years and those with prior asthma might be most susceptible to the adverse health effects of UFP. Those findings suggest that preventive measures to reduce UFP exposure could reduce asthma morbidity in children and should specifically target the susceptible subgroups identified in our study. Finally, the body of evidence on this association is still small, and more studies in more diverse settings with detailed information on the distribution of UFP and other air pollutants are needed.
