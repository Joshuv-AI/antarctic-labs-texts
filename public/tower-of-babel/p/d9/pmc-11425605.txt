Optimization Role of Potassium Carbonate in the Leaching of Guizhou’s Carlin-Type Gold Ore System with Potassium Chlorate
Authors: Xin Yuan, Daowen Tang, Tao Zou, Xiugao Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study addresses the environmental pollution and safety hazards associated with the cyanide leaching process in gold mining, proposing a more environmentally friendly and cost-effective potassium chlorate leaching method. The feasibility of this method was verified through thermodynamic analysis. Building upon single-factor experiments, the study utilized a response surface methodology to investigate the effects of potassium chlorate dosage, liquid-to-solid ratio, reaction temperature, and reaction pH on leaching efficiency. Results indicate that the order of influence on leaching efficiency is KClO3 dosage > liquid-to-solid ratio > temperature > pH, with significant interactions observed between KClO3 dosage and temperature. Optimal process parameters were determined as follows: initial potassium chlorate dosage of 21 g, liquid-to-solid ratio of 8.2/1, reaction temperature of 34 °C, and initial reaction pH of 12, achieving a gold leaching rate of 86.37%. To further optimize leaching efficiency, potassium carbonate was introduced to maintain system pH stability, promoting the formation of soluble iron carbonate complexes to reduce the re-encapsulation of minerals by Fe(OH)3 and prevent gold from existing as Au(OH)3, thus hindering gold leaching. Electrochemical studies revealed that increasing the potassium carbonate dosage enhances the dissolution of the passivation film. Under conditions of a potassium carbonate dosage of 0.75 mol/L and initial pH of 12, the gold leaching rate increased to 91.69%, with the system pH maintained above 11.68. Therefore, the addition of potassium carbonate effectively reduces the re-encapsulation of gold during leaching, further improving leaching efficiency.

Introduction

Gold, as a rare and precious metal, serves not only as a unique currency for reserves and investments but also as a significant material in industries such as jewelry, electronics, aerospace, and aviation.1 As global gold mines continue to be exploited, easily extractable gold ore resources are gradually depleting, and vast reserves of refractory gold ore are becoming an essential raw material for the gold production industry.2,3

Cyanide leaching is the primary method for industrial gold extraction. In cyanide solutions, gold can form stable gold cyanide complexes, making it dominant in the gold extraction industry due to its low cost and simple process.4−6 However, cyanide leaching has many drawbacks, including long leaching times, poor leaching efficiency for refractory gold ores containing Cu, As, C, or Sb, and severe environmental pollution and health hazards due to the toxicity of cyanide.7 Consequently, in recent years, as people increasingly focus on health and the environment, many countries have banned cyanide leaching, and noncyanide leaching methods are expected to become the mainstream in gold leaching in the future.8

With ongoing exploration of noncyanide gold leaching, methods such as thiourea leaching, thiosulfate leaching, polysulfide leaching, microbial leaching, and chloride leaching have emerged as more environmentally friendly alternatives.9 However, for various reasons, these methods have not been widely used in industry. Thiourea leaching is environmentally friendly, efficient, and selective, with easy recycling of waste solutions, but it suffers from drawbacks such as easy decomposition, carcinogenicity, and high cost.10,11 Thiosulfate leaching offers advantages such as high gold leaching rates, fast leaching rates, low production costs, and minimal pollution. However, it faces challenges such as instability in leaching systems, high consumption of leaching agents, difficulty in gold recovery from leach liquor, and complicated subsequent treatment of leach residues.12−15 Polysulfide leaching is suitable for gold ores with high sulfur content and containing arsenic and antimony sulfides due to its strong complexing ability. However, it is hindered by issues such as instability of polysulfides, generation of toxic and odorous gases (H2S and NH3), deterioration of the production environment, increased consumption of leaching agents, and higher industrial production costs.16,17 Microbial leaching has advantages, such as the ability to process complex polymetallic minerals, low production costs, environmental friendliness, and relatively simple equipment. However, it suffers from slow gold leaching rates and long leaching times, typically requiring 15 to 20 days, making it challenging to apply on an industrial scale.18,19

Chloride leaching for gold is increasingly valued for its minimal environmental pollution, low cost, fast leaching rates, high leaching efficiency, and wide applicability. This method, which originated in 1848, was initially the primary method for gold production but was later replaced by cyanide leaching due to cost and safety concerns.20,21 In recent years, with the demand for environmental protection and the development of refractory gold ore resources, coupled with the fact that chlorine gas can be replaced by hypochlorite and other alternatives as well as solutions to corrosion-resistant materials, research on chloride leaching for gold has surged. Potassium chlorate (KClO3) is a cheap, nontoxic leaching agent belonging to chloride leaching, possessing both complexing and strong oxidizing properties, enabling one-step gold leaching.22,23

Traditional process studies often use orthogonal experimental designs to optimize data and require a large number of experiments. Particularly, when there are many influencing factors and levels, the number of experiments can be enormous. In contrast, the response surface methodology (RSM) can quickly find quadratic relationships between response values and factors and optimize experimental parameters, accurately predicting experimental results and saving significant time and costs. Therefore, the RSM is widely used in metallurgy, chemistry, biology, food, and other fields.24,25

During the oxidation and dissociation process of gold-bearing sulfide minerals, Fe(OH)3 will form, which can re-encapsulate the gold concentrate, thereby reducing the efficiency of the oxidation reaction and gold leaching.26 Additionally, since the oxidation and dissociation of gold-bearing sulfide minerals and the leaching of gold require specific pH conditions, the pH must be strictly controlled to achieve optimal efficiency. Numerous studies have shown that in alkaline solutions, carbonate can form soluble iron carbonate complexes with Fe(OH)3. This helps to remove the passivating iron oxide layer from the surface of the minerals, exposing fresh surfaces and thereby enhancing the oxidation of the minerals.27,28

Therefore, this study uses efficient, low-toxicity, and environmentally friendly potassium chlorate as a leaching agent to leach Guizhou’s Carlin-type gold ore. By employing the RSM, the study establishes quadratic relationships between response values and various factors, determines the significant effects of factors on gold leaching rates, and optimizes experimental parameters to find the optimal conditions for potassium chlorate leaching of refractory gold ores in Guizhou. Under the optimal conditions, by adding potassium carbonate, iron oxide encapsulation of the gold ore is removed, maintaining an optimal pH in the system and enhancing the leaching rate of the Carlin-type gold ore.

Experimental Methods

Gold Leaching Method

After crushing, ball milling, screening, drying, and preblending, 30 g of ore powder is transferred into a 500 mL beaker. The prepared leaching agent is added to a constant-temperature water bath and stirred at 400 rpm. After leaching, the slurry is filtered using medium-speed qualitative filter paper, and the filter residue is dried. Then, 20 g of dried and finely ground leach residue is placed in a 650 °C oven for 2 h of roasting. After being cooled, it is thoroughly mixed with tartaric acid and sodium fluoride. Subsequently, the mixture is heated with aqua regia until dissolved. The solution is poured into foam plastic and subjected to 70 min of reciprocating shaking. After washing the foam plastic, it is transferred to a colorimetric tube and heated for 15 min. Next, 5 mL of filtrate is transferred to a 50 mL container, and sulfur thiourea solution and hydrochloric acid solution are added, followed by dilution with deionized water to prepare the test sample. The mineral gold content before and after leaching is assessed by using foam plastic enrichment flame atomic absorption spectrometry. Electrodes

In the electrochemical tests conducted in this study, a three-electrode system was uniformly employed. Among them, the reference electrode is a saturated calomel electrode, the auxiliary electrode is a platinum electrode, and the working electrode is a self-prepared mineral paste electrode.

Mineral Paste Electrode

The mineral paste electrode used in the experiment was made using pyrite as the raw material. In the preparation process, 1.5 g of powder was weighed, and before weighing, the raw material was sieved using a 200-mesh sieve, with a requirement that the particle size distribution of 74 μm should be >95%. Next, 3 g of high-purity graphite powder was weighed. The two were mixed evenly and ground in a mortar for no less than 10 min. After sufficient grinding, 1.2 mL of liquid paraffin was dropped into the mortar and gently stirred until a uniform paste-like substance was formed. Then, this paste mixture was injected into a glass tube with a length of 7 cm and a diameter of 0.5 cm and gently compressed with a glass rod to ensure tight filling. The presence of gaps in the tube was minimized as much as possible to affect the accuracy of the electrochemical test results. Finally, a pure copper wire was inserted to make the mineral paste electrode.29 See Figure 2 for an electrode schematic.

Platinum Electrode

A square platinum piece (side length 10 mm) was welded to a pure copper rod (diameter 2 mm). The area around the wire was sealed with epoxy resin tubing.

Iron Ion Dosage Testing Method

The dosage of iron (Fe) ions in the leachate is determined according to the national standard GB/T3049-2006, “General methods for the determination of iron content in industrial chemical products”, using the 1,10-phenanthroline spectrophotometric method. In this method, the sample to be tested is treated with ascorbic acid to reduce Fe3+ to Fe2+, thus obtaining the total iron content, and three parallel tests are conducted.

Results and Discussion

Theoretical Analysis of Potassium Chlorate Leaching for Gold

Thermodynamic Analysis of the Au–Cl–H2O System and Cl–H2O System

Chloride leaching for gold primarily leverages the complexing ability of chloride ions with gold and the strong oxidizing properties of chlorate ions to facilitate gold leaching. To maximize gold dissolution in chloride-containing aqueous solutions, it must exist in the form of soluble gold ions. Below, analysis of the Au–Cl–H2O system and the Cl–H2O system E-pH diagrams is provided, as shown in Figure 3.

(a) Au–Cl–H2O system potential–pH diagram (298 K); (b) Cl–H2O system potential–pH diagram (298 K).

From Figure 3a, it can be observed that there is a stable region for AuCl4– under high potential and acidic conditions, indicating the feasibility of chlorate leaching for gold. As the potential decreases and the pH increases in the solution, gold begins to precipitate in the form of Au(OH)3. When the pH continues to increase, Au(OH)3 exhibits amphoteric properties and dissolves to form H2AuO3– and HAuO32– under alkaline conditions.

As shown in Figure 3b, ClO3– has a potential higher than O2, indicating that the oxidizing ability of chlorate ions is greater than that of oxygen. Therefore, ClO3– can oxidize many substances that O2 cannot oxidize.

Single-Factor Experiments

Influence of Initial KClO3 Dosage

Figure 4a illustrates the impact of the KClO3 initial dosage on the percentage of gold leached under conditions of pH 12, a liquid-to-solid ratio of 9:1, and a temperature of 40 °C.

(a) Effect of initial KClO3 dosage on gold leaching rate, (b) effect of liquid-to-solid ratio on gold leaching rate, (c) effect of temperature on gold leaching rate, and (d) effect of pH on gold leaching rate.

As the dosage of KClO3 increases, the leaching rate of gold initially increases and then decreases. When the KClO3 dosage reaches 21 g, the leaching rate of gold reaches its maximum value at 82.03%. At low dosages, the leaching rate of gold is lower due to the consumption of ClO3– by other components. However, as the dosage of ClO3– increases, the solution’s oxidative properties enhance, leading to more dissociation of gold-bearing sulfide minerals and thus improving the leaching rate of gold. Nevertheless, continuing to increase the KClO3 dosage eventually leads to a decrease in the leaching rate of gold. This is because the enhanced oxidative properties of the solution result in the oxidation of gold-bearing sulfide minerals and the formation of Fe(OH)3 colloids, which re-encapsulate the gold concentrate, hindering its leaching. Therefore, the optimal dosage for the KClO3 leaching system is considered to be 21 g.

Effect of Liquid-to-Solid Ratio on Gold Leaching Rate

Figure 4b depicts the influence of the liquid-to-solid ratio on the gold leaching rate under conditions of pH 12, a temperature of 40 °C, and an initial KClO3 dosage of 21 g.

When the liquid–solid ratio increases from 6:1 to 8:1, the leaching rate of gold will also increase. When the liquid/solid ratio is 8:1, the leaching rate of gold reaches the maximum value of 82.54%. From the perspective of kinetics, when the liquid/solid ratio is 6:1, the slurry is too viscous, resulting in an increase in internal viscosity resistance between the gold concentrate and the leaching agent, which limits the leaching of gold. However, when the liquid–solid ratio is greater than 8:1, increasing the liquid–solid ratio will reduce the internal viscosity resistance so that there is a more thorough contact between the gold concentrate and the leaching agent, thereby improving the leaching rate of gold. However, once the liquid/solid ratio exceeds 8:1, the gold leaching rate increases slowly. Therefore, we believe that the optimum liquid/solid ratio of the potassium chlorate leaching system is 8:1.

Influence of Temperature on Gold Leaching Rate

Figure 4c presents the effect of leaching temperature on the gold leaching rate under conditions of pH 12, a liquid-to-solid ratio of 8:1, and an initial KClO3 dosage of 21 g.

From Figure 4c, it is evident that the temperature has a significant impact on KClO3 leaching of gold. KClO3 leaching of gold requires a certain reaction temperature, but excessively high temperatures may cause thermal decomposition of KClO3, leading to excessive consumption of the leaching agent. When the leaching temperature increases from 20 to 30 °C, the highest gold leaching rate increases from 78.32 to 84.48%, indicating a significant improvement in gold leaching rate. However, when the temperature exceeds 35 °C, partial decomposition of KClO3 occurs, resulting in a slight decrease in the gold leaching rate. Therefore, the optimal leaching temperature for KClO3 leaching of gold is considered to be 35 °C.

Influence of pH on Gold Leaching Rate

Figure 4d illustrates the effect of initial pH on the gold leaching rate under conditions of a liquid-to-solid ratio of 8:1, an initial KClO3 dosage of 21 g, and a temperature of 35 °C.

From Figure 4d, it can be observed that as the initial leaching pH increases, the gold leaching rate initially increases, reaching a maximum value of 85.23% at an initial leaching pH of 12, and then gradually decreases. Thermodynamic analysis from previous sections suggests that as the initial pH value exceeds 11, Au(OH)3 exhibits amphoteric behavior and dissolves, forming H2AuO3– and HAuO32– ions in the solution. The dissolution of Au(OH)3 requires more OH–, explaining why the leaching rate is highest at a leaching pH of 12. However, when the pH exceeds 12, an adequate dosage of OH– reacts with Fe3+ to form colloidal iron hydroxide, leading to re-encapsulation of the gold concentrate and thus reducing the gold leaching rate. Therefore, the pH value for the KClO3 leaching system is considered to be 12.

Effect of Potassium Carbonate Addition on the Leaching Process of Gold Concentrate

Mechanism of Action of Potassium Carbonate

During the leaching process, the formation of iron oxides can lead to the formation of a passivation layer that encapsulates the gold concentrate, thereby affecting the leaching rate of gold. Therefore, the addition of potassium carbonate allows the formation of soluble iron carbonate complexes [Fe(CO3)2–, Fe(CO3)22–, FeCO3, and FeHCO3+] to prevent the formation of the passivation layer. Analysis of the E-pH diagram for the Fe–CO3–H2O system at 35 °C is shown in Figure 6.

Fe–CO3–H2O system potential–pH diagram (308 K).

From Figure 6, it can be seen that at low potentials and pH values ranging from 6 to 13, the main soluble iron carbonate complexes are FeHCO3+, FeCO3, and Fe(CO3)22–. However, at higher potentials and pH values ranging from 4 to 12, the Fe3+ carbonate Fe(CO3)2– is the predominant dissolved ion. Since potassium chlorate has strong oxidizing properties and most of the iron in the gold concentrate exists in the form of Fe3+, as indicated by the corresponding surface fittings from earlier stages, in the potassium chlorate leaching system of Guizhou kaolin-type gold ores, the highest leaching rate is observed at an initial pH of 12. Additionally, with the consumption of hydroxyl groups in the initial sodium hydroxide solution over time, the pH decreases. Therefore, in this system, the passivation layer of iron oxides in the gold concentrate will partly enter the leaching solution as Fe(CO3)2–.

As the dosage of K2CO3 continues to increase, both Rs and Rct gradually decrease. Additionally, at a K2CO3 dosage of 1 mol/L, Rct reaches its minimum value of 0.852 × 105 Ω·cm2. This is because, in the alkaline gold leaching system with KClO3, Fe3+ from gold-bearing sulfide minerals gradually forms Fe(OH)3, creating a passive film that wraps around the electrode surface, hindering the oxidation reaction between the electrode and the solution. However, with the continuous addition of K2CO3, the passive film continuously dissolves, forming soluble Fe(CO3)2– ions. The decreasing trend weakens after the K2CO3 dosage exceeds 0.5 mol/L. Considering the experimental data and economic costs, it is believed that the optimal K2CO3 dosage is 0.75 mol/L.

Therefore, the pH of the system will decrease. Analysis from the E-pH diagram of the Au–Cl–H2O system in the previous context reveals that when the system pH is below 11, Au(OH)3 forms, which is unfavorable for gold leaching. In the leaching system with the addition of 0.75 mol/L potassium carbonate, although the pH also decreases, the decline is slow. At 8 h, it drops to a minimum of 11.68, effectively maintaining the system pH and reducing the formation of Au(OH)3.

Conclusions

(1)The feasibility of gold leaching with potassium chlorate was proved by thermodynamics. Based on the single-factor experiment, the RSM was used to optimize the experiment. The order of factors affecting the leaching rate was as follows: KClO3 dosage > liquid–solid ratio > temperature > pH, and the interaction between potassium chlorate dosage and temperature had a significant effect on the response value. The optimal conditions for the optimization of the model were as follows: the dosage of KClO3 was 21 g, the temperature was 34 °C, the liquid–solid ratio was 8.2, and the initial pH was 12. It was predicted that the gold leaching rate could reach 86.32% under this condition. Under the optimal process conditions, the actual gold leaching rate can reach 86.37%. The response surface model has high significance and good fitting degree.(2)Analysis of the Fe–CO3–H2O system E-pH diagram reveals that at low potentials and pH levels ranging from 6 to 13, the main soluble iron(II) carbonate complexes are FeHCO3+, FeCO3, and Fe(CO3)22–. However, in the high potential range and at pH levels ranging from 4 to 12, Fe3+ carbonate species Fe(CO3)2– are the primary dissolved ions.(3)The electrochemical results of potassium carbonate showed that the reaction rate of potassium chlorate to remove the passivation film was mainly controlled by charge transfer. When the dosage of potassium carbonate is increased from 0 to 1 mol/L, in Tafel polarization curve tests, the self-corrosion current density increases from 6.831 × 10–6 to 13.909 × 10–6 (A/cm2), and the linear polarization resistance decreases from 8545.1 to 2823.8 (Ω). In EIS tests, the charge transfer resistance decreases from 1.882 × 105 to 0.852 × 105 (Ω·cm2). Therefore, it can be concluded that with the continuous increase in potassium carbonate dosage, the dissolution degree of the Fe(OH)3 passivation film also increases.(4)Under the optimal leaching conditions, when the potassium carbonate dosage is 0.75 mol/L, the gold leaching rate increases to 91.69%. With an initial system pH of 12, the system pH is effectively maintained above 11.68. When the potassium carbonate dosage reaches 1 mol/L, the iron content in the leachate reaches its highest value of 9.68 mg/L. Therefore, the addition of potassium carbonate can effectively reduce the re-encapsulation of minerals by Fe(OH)3 and prevent gold from existing in the form of Au(OH)3, making it difficult to extract.
