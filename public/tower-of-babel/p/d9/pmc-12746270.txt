Erectile dysfunction and cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome: Insights from the all of us research program
Authors: Cameron M Blazoski, Zhiqi Yao, Tobias S Kohler, Martin M Miner, John Erhabor, Michael J Blaha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionErectile dysfunction (ED) is associated with cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors and is a potential indicator for future CVD events, but ED’s association with cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome has not been systematically studied.MethodsThis study used data from the All of Us Research Program covering 2017 to 2023. The primary exposure was prevalence of electronic health record-diagnosed ED with cross-sectional analyses measuring the association between prevalent ED and prevalent CKM conditions. In participants without CKM conditions at baseline, we performed survival analyses to evaluate the association between prevalent ED and the development of future CKM conditions with a follow up period ranging from a median of 2.1–4.0 years.ResultsOf the 97,475 male participants in this study, 5,575 (5.7 %) had a documented baseline ED diagnosis. The highest prevalence by race was white individuals (7.2 %) and by age range was 75–80 (12.7 %). Participants with ED versus those without ED had a higher rate of CKM conditions including diabetes mellitus (T2DM) (19.3 % vs 7.3 %), hypertension (HTN) (47.6 % vs 18.8 %), chronic kidney disease (CKD) (10.3 % vs 2.8 %), heart failure (HF) (5.7 % vs 2.0 %), atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) (3.0 % vs 1.3 %), and atrial fibrillation (AF) (7.3 % vs 2.5 %). Baseline prevalent ED was associated with higher risks of developing CKM conditions of CKD, HF, AF, ASCVD, and HTN but not the development of T2DM.ConclusionA diagnosis of ED was significantly associated with both the prevalence and future development of cardiovascular and metabolic conditions, suggesting that ED assessment should be incorporated into routine cardiometabolic risk evaluation.Keywords: Erectile dysfunction, CKM syndrome, Cardiovascular disease

Introduction

Erectile dysfunction (ED) and cardiovascular disease (CVD) are increasingly recognized as interconnected conditions, sharing common risk factors and pathophysiological mechanisms. Once regarded primarily as a psychological disorder, ED is now understood to be largely vasculogenic in nature, resulting from systemic vascular disease that also contributes to CVD [1,2]. As a result, ED has emerged as a potential early indicator of cardiovascular risk, preceding clinical CVD by several years and offering a critical window for early intervention [3].

ED affects approximately 18 % of American men at least 20 years of age [4]. The condition appears to be particularly common in those with metabolic and cardiovascular risk factors such as insulin resistance, obesity, dyslipidemia, and smoking [5,6]. A significant overlap exists between ED and CVD, with studies showing that up to 70 % of men with overt CVD also have ED [4]. ED symptoms frequently precede clinical manifestations of CVD[1,7], and there is growing research that ED is potentially a predictor of future cardiovascular events [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]].

Recent research is investigating ED’s relationship with the newly defined cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome. CKM syndrome encompasses the interrelated pathophysiological processes of metabolic dysfunction, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular pathology, all of which contribute to multiorgan dysfunction and an elevated risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes [15,16]. Metabolic syndrome and insulin resistance are well-documented contributors to endothelial dysfunction and vascular impairment, further exacerbating ED [17]. Given that ED often precedes overt CVD, it can serve as an early marker of heightened cardiometabolic risk, necessitating proactive screening and intervention [18,19].

This study aimed to build upon prior research by evaluating the association between ED and CKM conditions in a large, diverse population, emphasizing longitudinal follow-up for incident conditions, with implications for ED as a marker of future cardiometabolic disease.

Methods

Study resource

This study was conducted using the National Institutes of Health-funded All of Us (AoU) Research Program, a longitudinal cohort designed to build a large diverse healthcare database from over 1 million participants to advance individualized healthcare. The cohort includes data from adult persons in the United States with a specific focus on including racially and ethnically diverse patient populations. The recruitment methods, sites, and rationale for AoU have been described previously [20]. The data repository is formed from health questionnaires, electronic health records (EHRs), physical measurements, digital health technology, and biospecimens. This secondary data analysis was within the scope of past studies, for which our organization has received approval from an institutional review board for the use of deidentified archival data. This study used the v8 controlled tier AoU data, covering information from calendar years 2017 to 2023.

Study population

The study included male participants in the AoU dataset who have linked EHRs (N = 149,867). It excluded 52,392 individuals who had used any statin medications 3 years prior to study onset. The final study population was 97,475 individuals.

We specifically excluded recent statin users to reduce potential bias in CVD outcome ascertainment. Statin use often indicates more proactive healthcare engagement and frequent monitoring, which may increase the likelihood of receiving new diagnoses during follow-up. The 3-year window was selected to strike a balance between excluding those with recent cardiovascular risk management and retaining a sufficiently large cohort for analysis. This approach aligns with the focus of our study, which is to examine the association between prevalent ED and incident CVD in a population largely naïve to cardiovascular risk assessment and pharmacologic prevention. Given that statins are the most commonly prescribed agents for primary and secondary CVD prevention, their use reflects a clinical decision point that could confound this relationship. Excluding recent users allows us to better isolate ED as an early signal of cardiovascular risk. We did not apply similar exclusions for other preventive medications like antihypertensives or antidiabetic agents, as these are often initiated for non-CVD indications and may reflect different risk pathways. Statins are more directly linked to cardiovascular prevention, making them a more specific marker for prior CVD risk assessment and intervention. This design choice helps minimize reverse causation and ascertainment bias, though we acknowledge that it may slightly underestimate the overall burden of risk in populations under active cardiovascular management

Exposure

Erectile dysfunction was identified using EHR data, based on Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine–Clinical Terms (SNOMED-CT) codes. Prevalent cases were defined as those with a documented EHR diagnosis prior to or at the time of each participant’s baseline, which was established as the date of completion of the Basics Survey under the rolling enrollment design. Self-reported diagnoses or other symptoms were not captured or considered in the exposure definition.

CKM scope outcomes

We included the following CKM-linked conditions as outcomes: type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), hypertension (HTN), atrial fibrillation (AF), heart failure (HF), atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Diagnoses were ascertained using EHR data mapped to SNOMED-CT codes. Relevant SNOMED-CT terms included "type 2 diabetes mellitus," "essential hypertension," "atrial fibrillation," "heart failure", and "chronic kidney disease.” ASCVD was defined as a composite of "cerebrovascular accident" and "myocardial infarction." Prevalent cases were identified based on diagnoses documented prior to or at the participant’s baseline (the date of Basics Survey completion), and incident cases in individuals without these conditions at baseline were captured during follow-up through ongoing EHR updates.

Covariates

Sex, age, race/ethnicity, education level, and income level were self-reported by participants at baseline through the Basics Survey. Body mass index (BMI) and systolic blood pressure (SBP) were measured during the AoU physical examination visit. Prevalent major depression was ascertained through EHR data, using SNOMED-CT codes corresponding to a clinical diagnosis of “major depressive disorder”.

Statistical methods

We described baseline characteristics of participants by ED status, presenting categorical variables as counts with percentages and continuous variables as means with standard deviations.

We estimated the prevalence of clinically diagnosed ED overall and stratified by race, age groups, and their jointly defined strata.

In the cross-sectional analysis, we evaluated the associations between prevalent ED and prevalent conditions from CKM syndrome by calculating the odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95 % confidence intervals (CIs) using multivariable logistic regression models. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis of individuals aged 40–65 to emphasize middle-aged adults and exclude extremes of age.

We then conducted a survival analysis restricted to a primary prevention population, excluding individuals with prevalent ASCVD, HF, and CKD at baseline. For each incident condition, participants with that condition already present at baseline were also excluded from the corresponding analysis. This analysis assessed the association between prevalent ED and time-to-subsequent development of CKM conditions during follow-up. Cox proportional hazards models were used and hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95 % Cis were calculated.

Both cross-sectional and survival analyses were adjusted sequentially in three models. The first model included age, race, education level, and income. The second additionally adjusted for BMI categories (normal weight, overweight, class I obesity, class II obesity, class III obesity, and underweight) and SBP. The third model further adjusted for a baseline diagnosis of major depression.

A two-sided p-value<0.05 was considered as statistically significant. All analyses were performed within the AoU Research Workbench using Jupyter Cloud Environment and R programming language.

Discussion

The results of this study are among the first to comprehensively describe the relationship between ED and cardiometabolic risk, particularly within the broader context of CKM syndrome. Our findings demonstrate that ED is common and is significantly associated with both the prevalence and incidence of multiple cardiovascular, metabolic, and kidney conditions, including hypertension (HTN), atrial fibrillation (AF), heart failure (HF), atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), and chronic kidney disease (CKD). These associations persisted across multiple models adjusting for potential confounders.

Our cross-sectional analysis revealed particularly strong associations between ED, prevalent hypertension, and prevalent CKD, aligning with previous research highlighting the shared vascular pathology underlying these conditions. The interplay of insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, and renal dysfunction contributes to endothelial impairment, systemic inflammation, and neurohormonal dysregulation, all of which promote ED and cardiovascular pathology [1,11,15,16,21]. Studies suggest that patients with diabetes - a major driver of CKM syndrome - experience ED at significantly higher rates than the general population, with severity correlating with glycemic control and disease duration [22,23]. Additionally, CKD is associated with vascular calcification, autonomic dysfunction, and uremia-induced endothelial damage, further exacerbating ED and cardiovascular risk [24,25]. Given these connections, ED may serve as an early warning sign of CKM syndrome, suggesting its inclusion in comprehensive risk assessment models [26,27].

The longitudinal analysis is novel in underscoring the role of ED as a harbinger of future cardiovascular and renal disease. ED was independently associated with increased HRs for developing CKD (HR: 1.42, 95 % CI: 1.20–1.70 in Model 3), HF (HR: 1.32, 95 % CI: 1.06–1.64 in Model 3), and HTN (HR: 1.36, 95 % CI: 1.20–1.54 in Model 3). These findings align with prior studies suggesting that ED may precede overt cardiovascular disease by several years, offering a valuable window for early intervention. A 2016 study by Feldman et al. found that men with ED had significantly higher coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores, greater carotid plaque burden, and poorer vascular function [8]. Similarly, a 2017 study by Osondu et al. linked ED with impaired flow-mediated dilation and increased carotid intima-media thickness, both of which are established markers of subclinical atherosclerosis [9]. These vascular changes help explain why men with ED face a heightened risk of future cardiovascular events, as shown in a 2013 meta-analysis by Vlachopoulos et al., which found that ED is associated with increased risks of CVD and all-cause mortality, with an even greater relative risk in younger men [11]. The particularly strong relationship between ED and incident CKD also supports the growing recognition of CKM syndrome as an interconnected disease spectrum where metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular dysfunction contribute to progressive organ damage. It also emphasizes that men presenting with ED may warrant additional screening for these high-risk conditions, particularly in those with other metabolic risk factors.

ED should be considered not just a quality-of-life issue but a potential sentinel marker of systemic vascular disease. A particular result to highlight from our study is that - across all models - ED was associated with future ASCVD over just ∼4 years of follow-up. Our results should be compared to those of Uddin et al. from the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis 2018. However, in the paper by Uddin et al., the initial significant finding between ED and “hard CHD events” did not remain significant when adjusting across all models, while our study’s results remained significant, likely due the superior statistical power of the present study.

These findings highlight the need for a paradigm shift in cardiovascular risk assessment. While the British QRISK-3 calculator has incorporated ED into cardiovascular risk stratification[14], most widely used U.S. and European risk models have yet to follow suit. Given the strong predictive associations observed in this study, there is a compelling need to revise clinical practice guidelines to incorporate erectile dysfunction into routine cardiovascular risk assessment—particularly among younger men. This is especially important considering that ED in men under 50 has been shown to be a stronger predictor of future cardiovascular disease compared to older men, underscoring its value as an early warning sign [12]. Compounding this issue is the fact that men, in general, are less likely than women to engage in preventive healthcare measures, including screenings for hypertension, cholesterol, and smoking cessation [28]. It is also important to consider the severity of ED within risk models, as studies have found associations between severity of ED and the burden of CAD [29]. If not directly incorporated into risk scores, ED should be considered as a risk-enhancing factor for future CVD, consistent with that recommended by Princeton IV. Urologists and primary care physicians who diagnose ED should consider following the Princeton IV algorithms and initiating formal risk assessment, and referring patients with high risk or high CAC scores to a cardiologist.

This study has several notable strengths, including its large, diverse sample size of nearly 100,000 participants and its use of both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses to evaluate the relationship between ED and multiple CKM-related outcomes. The use of multivariable models adjusting for relevant confounders such as major depression disorder strengthens the validity of the observed associations, and the inclusion of a broad spectrum of conditions provides a comprehensive assessment of ED’s predictive value in CKM syndrome. Our study is the first in our knowledge to specifically look at ED and components of CKM, rather than focusing only on a single outcome (like CVD).

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the observational nature of the study precludes definitive conclusions about causality. Additionally, ED was assessed based on clinically diagnosed ED, which may underestimate its true prevalence due to underreporting or lack of clinical documentation, particularly among younger men or those with less access to healthcare. Data would be more robust if ED was stratified by degree of severity with the use of the International Index of Erectile Function or the Sexual Health Inventory for Men. In addition, we also do not have data on how many men with prevalent ED were using phosphodiesterase 5 (PDE5) inhibitors, an ED medication that would also likely reduce risk of CVD and CKM conditions. There is also the potential for residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as medication use, lifestyle behaviors, or psychosocial variables. Our paper also is limited by a relatively short length of follow up, which would under-measure any long-term associations between ED and cardiometabolic outcomes. Despite these limitations, the study provides robust evidence supporting the role of ED as a clinically meaningful indicator of cardiovascular and metabolic risk.

Conclusion

This study adds to the growing body of evidence positioning ED as an early, clinically relevant marker of systemic vascular disease and CKM syndrome. The strong associations observed between ED and both prevalent and incident hypertension, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, ASCVD, and CKD highlight its value in predicting future cardiovascular risk. Incorporating ED assessment into routine risk stratification may offer a timely opportunity for early intervention and improved long-term outcomes.
