Linking the production of reactive oxygen species to the expression of antimicrobial peptides through aquaporin in an arthropod
Authors: Zhe Wei, Ping-Ping Liu, Wei-Guang Wang, Xian-Wei Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe dual oxidase 2 (Duox2)/reactive oxygen species (ROS) system and the Relish/antimicrobial peptides (AMP) pathway have traditionally been regarded as distinct branches of arthropod antibacterial immunity. However, this study reveals that these two branches are interconnected through an aquaporin 4 (Aqp4) in crayfish. Aqp4 facilitates the transport of extracellular ROS into the cytosol, where ROS act as signaling molecules to enhance AMP expression. This mechanism not only strengthens the effectiveness, but also extends the duration of host antibacterial response. The finding of this aquaporin-mediated link underscores the synergy and crosstalk between the two principal antibacterial strategies, offering insights into the complexity and systematicity of arthropod immunity.

In arthropods, two principal antibacterial strategies have been identified and extensively characterized: production of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) through immune deficiency (Imd)/Relish pathway and generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) by dual oxidase 2 (Duox2) (1, 2). Bacterial peptidoglycans are recognized by the peptidoglycan recognition proteins (PGRP) on the cell membrane (3). The immune signals are then transmitted to the intracellular adaptor protein Imd and ultimately to NF-κB transcription factor Relish. The active form of Relish migrates into the nucleus to increase AMP transcription (4). Besides peptidoglycans, certain bacterial metabolites, such as uracil, can also be sensed as invading signals (5). When uracil is recognized by a currently undetermined G protein-coupled receptor, it activates phospholipase C-β and protein kinase C-dependent calcium mobilization, which in turn stimulates Duox2 activity and production of extracellular ROS (6, 7).

Both AMPs and ROS are effective bactericidal agents. AMPs are typically amphipathic peptides capable of interacting with microbial cell membranes. This interaction disrupts the integrity of the lipid bilayer structure, leading to the leakage of intracellular materials (8). In contrast, ROS induces oxidative damage to microbes. This oxidative damage manifests as the impairment of structural components of the microbial cell membrane, as well as the oxidation of proteins and DNA, ultimately resulting in microbial death (9–11). Therefore, the Relish/AMPs and Duox2/ROS systems play crucial roles in host–bacterial interactions. Flies lacking key AMPs, as well as those with a deletion of Relish, exhibit increased loads of Acetobacter species in gut and are more susceptible to Acetobacter infection (12). Additionally, Duox2-knockdown flies, which are unable to produce extracellular ROS, show heightened susceptibility to the lethal effects caused by the pathogen Erwinia carotovora (13–15). Together, the production of AMPs and ROS significantly enhances arthropod antibacterial immunity. However, whether there is any connection between these two strategies, which have long been regarded as distinct branches, remains largely unknown.

Aquaporins (AQPs) are a class of transmembrane proteins that exist as homotetramers (16). Based on their transporting activities, AQPs are divided into three classes: classic or orthodox AQPs, which are permeable to water; aquaglyceroporins, which transport water and glycerol; and superaquaporins, which are localized in the endoplasmic reticulum and sense oxidative stress and osmotic change (17). By facilitating the transport of water, glycerol, CO2, and other small molecules, AQPs play essential roles in various physiological processes including barrier function, energy metabolism, angiogenesis, cell migration, and immunity (18, 19). Interestingly, due to the similarities in diameter, surface charge distribution, and dipole moment between water and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), aquaglyceroporins can also function as H2O2 channels. Since H2O2 is uncharged and possesses inherent redox capacity, H2O2 transported across the cell membrane may act as a second messenger to regulate intracellular signaling pathways (20). For instance, the transport of extracellular H2O2 by AQP1 facilitates cardiomyocyte hypertrophy in both human and mice (17), while the uptake of H2O2 via human AQP3 promotes the activation of small GTPases and chemokine-dependent T cell migration (21). In particular, AQP-facilitated transmembrane diffusion of H2O2 represents an important signaling mechanism for immune regulation. For example, in humans, AQP3 mediates the import of extracellular H2O2 to activate NF-κB signaling and expression of interleukin 23 (22); in rice, extracellular H2O2 internalized via AQPs triggers the mitogen-activated protein kinase cascade, leading to the transcription of defense-related genes and enhanced plant resistance (23). Thus, AQPs-mediated redox signaling is an important mechanism for immune regulation.

During a screen for key factors in crayfish antibacterial response, we found that the expression of an AQP, Aqp4, was significantly upregulated following bacterial infection. We found that Aqp4 was essential for the effective induction of AMPs, but not ROS, after bacterial infection. Interestingly, the role of Aqp4 in AMP induction was found to depend on ROS production system. This finding suggested a potential link between AMPs system and ROS system. We therefore sought to determine whether the increase in extracellular ROS resulting from pathogenic infection could serve as a trigger for an intracellular signaling cascade, ultimately leading to enhanced AMP production. Using bacterial uracil as a specific stimulator for ROS production, we demonstrated that Aqp4 transports extracellular ROS into the intracellular compartment, where the elevated ROS levels lead to c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) phosphorylation and subsequent nuclear localization of FoxO. This, in turn, promotes the expression of Relish and Relish-targeting AMPs. Therefore, by demonstrating the significance of Aqp4 in enhancing the effectiveness and persistence of the immune response through linking Duox2/ROS system to Relish/AMPs pathway, this study provides insights into the regulatory mechanism of arthropod antibacterial immunity.

Results

Aqp4 Is a Key Factor in Crayfish Antibacterial Immunity.

To identify key genes in crayfish antibacterial immunity, we stimulated the animals with Aeromonas hydrophila, a major bacterial pathogen for crayfish, and used RNA Sequencing (RNA-seq) to screen for upregulated genes in hemocytes and intestines. As shown in Fig. 1A, the expression of a member of the AQP family, Aqp4, was significantly induced by bacterial infection. The RNA-seq results were validated by qPCR (Fig. 1B), confirming that Aqp4 expression indeed responds to bacterial stimulation. Next, the role of Aqp4 in crayfish–bacteria interactions was investigated using RNA interference (RNAi). Aqp4 knockdown did not affect crayfish survival in the absence of exogenous bacterial infection. However, the survival rate of the Aqp4 knockdown group was significantly lower than that in the control group following A. hydrophila infection, indicating that Aqp4 knockdown accelerated mortality from bacterial infection (Fig. 1C). Additionally, A. hydrophila load in crayfish tissues was higher in the Aqp4 knockdown group compared to the control group (Fig. 1D), suggesting a protective role of Aqp4 in crayfish antibacterial response. To further prove this role, intestinal tissues were sectioned and stained. As shown in Fig. 1E, the microvilli in the Aqp4 knockdown group exhibited more severe structural damage than those in the control group. Together, these findings demonstrate that Aqp4 plays a critical role in defending against bacterial infection.

Aqp4 Regulates AMPs Production by Enhancing FoxO-Mediated Relish Transcription after Bacterial Infection.

To determine how Aqp4 contributes to antibacterial immunity, we analyzed whether Aqp4 knockdown would influence ROS production or AMPs expression, as ROS and AMPs are the two major antibacterial factors in arthropods. As shown in Fig. 2A, A. hydrophila infection significantly increased extracellular ROS levels in crayfish hemolymph and intestines, whereas Aqp4 knockdown did not affect ROS production (Fig. 2A). However, Aqp4 knockdown significantly, though partially, reduced the induction of Alf3 and Eiger (Fig. 2B), two antibacterial effectors that have been demonstrated as transcriptional targets of Relish. Moreover, Aqp4 knockdown also partially reduced Relish induction (Fig. 2B), suggesting that Aqp4 regulates AMPs expression by modulating Relish transcription.

Since FoxO serves as a direct transcriptional regulator of Relish expression in arthropods, we investigated whether Aqp4 knockdown caused any influence on FoxO. We first performed immunocytochemical analysis to assess FoxO nuclear localization after A. hydrophila infection. Bacterial stimulation enhanced FoxO nuclear localization, but this enhancement was partially suppressed following Aqp4 knockdown (Fig. 2C). This result was confirmed by immunoblotting assay (Fig. 2D). To determine whether the effect of Aqp4 knockdown on Relish expression was mediated through FoxO, a ChIP assay was performed to analyze the binding between FoxO and the FoxO-responsive element present in Relish promoter (Fig. 2E). As shown in Fig. 2F, A. hydrophila infection increased the binding between FoxO and Relish promoter fragment, while Aqp4 knockdown partially suppressed this increase, indicating that A. hydrophila-induced Relish transcription was inhibited by Aqp4 knockdown. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Aqp4 contributes to crayfish antibacterial immunity by enhancing FoxO-nuclear localization, ultimately promoting the expression of Relish and Relish-targeting AMPs.

Aqp4 Mediates FoxO-Activation Caused by Infection/Uracil-Induced Extracellular ROS.

Next, we explored the underlying mechanism by which Aqp4 is involved in this regulation. Since peptidoglycan is a typical bacterial component that potentiates FoxO activity and AMPs expression in crustaceans (24, 25), we first examined whether Aqp4 knockdown could affect peptidoglycan (MDP, the minimal bioactive motif)-induced FoxO activation. Although MDP challenge significantly induced FoxO-nuclear localization, Aqp4 knockdown did not affect this induction (Fig. 3 A and B). This result is intriguing because it aligns with the data in Fig. 2 B–D, showing that Aqp4 knockdown partially, but not completely, suppressed FoxO activation and Relish/AMP induction caused by A. hydrophila infection. This suggests that A. hydrophila infection may activate FoxO through an additional, peptidoglycan-independent mechanism, in which Aqp4 plays a role.

Bacterial infection also leads to extracellular ROS production. Notably, FoxO responds to cellular stress and redox signaling (26). Moreover, Aqp4 possesses the NPA motif and a conserved aromatic arginine region (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), hallmarks of aquaglyceroporins that are capable of transporting H2O2. These clues led us to hypothesize that excessive extracellular ROS are transported by Aqp4 into cytosol, where it promotes FoxO activation and Relish/AMP expression. To validate this hypothesis, we first needed to establish a model in which ROS production is the sole consequence of immune challenge, avoiding interference from other bacterial components. Uracil has been shown to effectively activate the Duox2/ROS system in Drosophila (5), we confirmed that, in crayfish, uracil challenge indeed induced significant ROS production. Furthermore, this induction was abolished by Duox2 knockdown (SI Appendix, Fig. S2). Therefore, uracil was used as the stimulant for further validation.

As expected, uracil challenge increased FoxO nuclear level, as revealed by the immunocytochemical and immunoblotting analyses (Fig. 3 C and D), and enhanced the binding between FoxO and Relish promoter fragment, as shown by ChIP results (Fig. 3E). Importantly, these increases in FoxO nuclear localization and FoxO–promoter binding were completely deprived by Aqp4 knockdown (Fig. 3 F–H). Collectively, these data support our hypothesis that Aqp4 may transport extracellular ROS into the cytosol to activate FoxO, thereby inducing Relish/AMP expression.

Uracil-Induced Extracellular ROS Enters Cytosol through Aqp4 to Activate FoxO.

To further validate the hypothesis, we examined whether Aqp4 could indeed mediate the entry of extracellular ROS. We first demonstrated that the intracellular ROS signal in crayfish hemocytes increased after uracil treatment, as revealed by fluorescent probe labeling (Fig. 4A), and flow cytometry assays (Fig. 4B). Given that Duox2 had been demonstrated to exclusively produce H2O2 other than other ROS (27), we employed a specific probe and confirmed that uracil treatment indeed increased intracellular H2O2 levels (Fig. 4C). To clarify that this increase was caused by the entry of extracellular ROS produced by Duox2, rather than intracellular ROS produced by NADPH oxidases (Noxs), we silenced Duox2 and Noxs expression to determine the source of the increased ROS signal after uracil challenge. We found that Duox2 knockdown significantly reduced the intracellular ROS levels, whereas Nox4 and Nox5 knockdown had no effect on the ROS signal (Fig. 4 D–F). These results indicated that the increase in intracellular ROS signal following uracil challenge originated from the Duox2/ROS system. Furthermore, Aqp4 knockdown blocked this increase (Fig. 4 G–I), confirming the critical role of Aqp4 in mediating the entry of extracellular ROS into the intracellular compartment.

Next, we investigated whether the extracellular ROS entry was responsible for FoxO activation. We found that the enhancement of FoxO nuclear localization triggered by uracil was abolished by Duox2 knockdown (Fig. 5 A and B), highlighting the necessity of extracellular ROS. To validate these findings, ascorbic acid, a strong reducing agent that neutralizes extracellular ROS (28), was used to clear extracellular ROS after uracil challenge. As shown in Fig. 5 C and D, ascorbic acid treatment also blocked the uracil-induced FoxO-nuclear localization. Next, we applied 2-(nicotinamide)-1,3,4-thiadiazole (TGN020), a selective inhibitor for human AQP family that was predicted to interact with essential sites conserved in crayfish Aqp4 with high affinity (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). The data showed that TGN020 application effectively suppressed uracil-induced FoxO activation (Fig. 5 E and F), further confirming the involvement of Aqp4 in this regulatory process.

We next demonstrated that uracil-induced extracellular ROS activates FoxO via JNK (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), a critical upstream kinase of FoxO that serves as an oxidase sensor responsive to ROS signals (29, 30). Collectively, these data support the presence of sequential ROS/Aqp4/JNK/FoxO/Relish regulatory axis.

The Regulation of AMPs Production by ROS System Enhances Antibacterial Immunity.

We have demonstrated that extracellular ROS can be transported into the cytosol through crayfish Aqp4, leading to FoxO activation, Relish transcription, and ultimately, AMPs expression. The results shown in Fig. 2B suggest this mechanism contributes to the induction of AMPs expression. This prompted us to reexamine the previously established model in which peptidoglycans are the only known stimulant for Relish/AMPs system following bacterial infection. Therefore, we analyzed the expression of a series of Relish-targeting genes and found that uracil challenge effectively induced their expression (Fig. 6A). Furthermore, Duox2 knockdown (which blocks extracellular ROS production, Fig. 6B), ascorbic acid treatment (which scavenges ROS, Fig. 6C), Aqp4 inhibition (which prevents ROS entry, Fig. 6D), FoxO knockdown (which suppresses Relish transcription, Fig. 6E), and Relish knockdown (Fig. 6F) all inhibited the uracil-induced AMPs expression. These findings confirmed the sequential regulatory mechanism in which extracellular ROS is transported via Aqp4 into cytosol, where it activates FoxO to promote Relish transcription and ultimately AMPs expression, and clearly demonstrated the link between ROS production and AMPs expression, the two principal antibacterial strategies in arthropods.

We next investigated the biological significance of this link. As shown in Fig. 6G, treatment with uracil, which directly induces ROS production and indirectly induces AMPs expression, significantly enhanced antibacterial immunity, as evidenced by the decreased bacterial load in crayfish intestines. Interestingly, Duox2 knockdown completely abolished, while FoxO knockdown or Relish knockdown partially reduced the immune-enhancing effect of uracil. Moreover, Aqp4 knockdown also partially diminished this effect (Fig. 6H). Together, these results suggest that the indirect induction of AMPs contributes to uracil-induced antibacterial immunity. Thus, the regulation of the AMPs system by the ROS system provides host with a more robust immune response.

More intriguingly, we found that this regulatory mechanism also extends the persistence of the antibacterial immune responses. As shown in Fig. 6I, uracil-induced extracellular ROS production is a relatively short-term response, with ROS levels returning to baseline by 24 h after uracil treatment. However, the induction of AMPs by ROS is a more persistent response. The protein level of Relish-targeting antibacterial effector remained significantly higher in the uracil-treated sample compared to the control sample at 24 h after uracil treatment (Fig. 6J). Therefore, by transforming the immune effect from short-lived ROS to long-lived AMP proteins, this mechanism enhances the persistence of antibacterial immunity.

We next confirmed that this mechanism contributes to enhanced pathogen resistance. As shown in Fig. 6K, uracil pretreatment significantly increased host survival rates following exogenous A. hydrophila infection; however, this protective effect was abrogated by Aqp4 inhibition. These data further underscore the significance of this mechanism in augmenting crayfish antibacterial immunity. Given that conserved H2O2-transport motifs are present in Aqp4 sequences across arthropods, this mechanism may be not limited to crayfish (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). We therefore examined kuruma shrimp, another decapod crustacean. Inhibition of shrimp Aqp4 suppressed uracil-induced FoxO nuclear localization, Relish transcription, and antibacterial immunity enhancement (SI Appendix, Fig. S5), confirming the functional conservation of the ROS/Aqp4/FoxO/Relish signaling axis. Collectively, these findings suggest that this regulatory mechanism may be evolutionarily conserved among arthropods.

Discussion

This study reveals an intriguing link between the Duox2/ROS system and the Relish/AMPs system, two major strategies in arthropod immunity. These two systems are generally regarded as distinct branches because they are activated by different bacterial components and lead to distinct outcomes. Moreover, they appear to have distinct roles in antibacterial immunity. The Duox2/ROS system is activated by pathogenic bacteria-derived uracil, and robust production of extracellular ROS effectively kills invading bacteria (5). In contrast, the Relish/AMPs pathway is primarily elicited by meso-diaminopimelic acid-type peptidoglycan, which is released during bacterial growth, and thus plays a role in tolerating commensal microbiota (31, 32). We had previously uncovered a cross-talk between these two systems. By regulating the expression of Eiger, a cytokine that stimulates Duox2 expression, the Relish/AMPs system contributes to maintaining basal ROS levels in the context of commensal microbiota homeostasis (33). Here, in the context of pathogenic bacterial infection, we establish a connection between these two branches. The aquaporin Aqp4, as a key intermediate, links the ROS system to the AMP system by transporting extracellular ROS into the intracellular compartment, where the increased ROS levels potentiate FoxO activation and Relish expression. These linking mechanisms demonstrate that the two antibacterial responses are not entirely separate and that the outcomes of each branch are achieved through complex regulation. This cross-talk makes a significant, albeit previously unnoticed, contribution to the antibacterial response.

The current regulatory mechanism has been proven to effectively enhance the strength and persistence of antibacterial immunity. Due to the intrinsic oxidizing ability of ROS, the burst of extracellular ROS can directly cause indiscriminate damage to pathogens (5, 34, 35). Meanwhile, by inducing the expression of AMPs, which selectively target bacterial cell membranes and lead to the destruction of bacterial cells without harming host cells, extracellular ROS indirectly enhance the effectiveness of host immunity against pathogens (36, 37). The combination of ROS production and AMP expression expands the antimicrobial spectrum and confers the host a robust immune response to resist pathogenic infection (31). Beyond increasing the strength of the immune response, this link also extends the duration of antibacterial immunity. While ROS have a relatively short half-life and their inhibitory effects are transient, the synthesis of AMPs maintains immunity over a longer period. By extending the immune response from ROS production to AMP expression, this link significantly contributes to the persistence of antibacterial immunity. Therefore, this mechanism is more involved in defending against pathogenic infections than in regulating microbiota homeostasis. Under normal conditions, extracellular ROS levels are low and insufficient to activate the intracellular Relish/AMP pathway, allowing AMP production to remain at a basal homeostatic level. In contrast, under pathogenic conditions, the burst of extracellular ROS acts as a signal to potentiate AMP expression, which, together with ROS, combats pathogenic infections.

The finding that crayfish Aqp4 mediates the entry of ROS into the intracellular compartment to play an immunomodulatory role highlights the significance of aquaporins in animal immune regulation. A typical role of aquaporins is transmembrane water transport. By facilitating water movement across biological membranes, aquaporins play critical roles in water homeostasis (38). For example, AQP1-deficient mice exhibit defects in urine concentration due to reduced water permeability and fluid absorption in the thin limbs of the loop of Henle, vasa recta, and proximal tubule (39). Beyond water, H2O2, due to its lack of charge and ability to freely pass through cell membranes, is a good candidate for transport by aquaporins (40). All permeable aquaporins are H2O2 channels with varying degrees of permeability (41). The transport of H2O2 by aquaporins has been shown to be involved in multiple physiological processes, including immune regulation (22, 42). However, compared to the extensive knowledge in mammals, little is known whether nonmammalian aquaporins are also involved in immune regulation. This study provides evidence for this issue, using crayfish as a model. Moreover, the identified Aqp4-mediated signaling axis is not confined to crayfish, but is conserved in other crustaceans and likely functions broadly across arthropods. Therefore, the involvement of aquaporins in animal immunity through transporting ROS is a ubiquitous mechanism.

Interestingly, there is a striking similarity between the current Duox2/ROS/Aqp4 axis and the corresponding mechanism in plants. The ROS burst generated by plasma membrane (PM)-localized RESPIRATORY BURST OXIDASE HOMOLOG (RBOH) NADPH oxidase, a functional homolog of arthropod Duox2, is a hallmark of plant–pathogen interactions (43). Multiple studies have shown that these apoplastic ROS can be transported by PM intrinsic proteins (PIPs), the plant aquaporins, across the PM into the cytoplasm to regulate plant immunity (44, 45). For example, in Arabidopsis thaliana, after bacterial infection, extracellular H2O2 is transported via AtPIP1;4 into the cytoplasm to regulate systemic acquired resistance by enhancing pathogen-associated molecular pattern-triggered immunity, which is similar to peptidoglycan-activated signaling pathways in arthropods (46). Thus, by linking apoplastic H2O2 induction to innate immune pathways, the aquaporin AtPIP1;4 confers plants with more robust disease resistance. It is fascinating that the regulatory mechanisms in arthropods and plants are remarkably similar at each step, suggesting that the aquaporin-mediated link between ROS production and immune gene expression has been employed by both animals and plants as a powerful tool to defend against pathogenic infections. Notably, we found that the expression of crayfish Aqp4 is also regulated by Relish and that uracil significantly induces its expression through the sequential mechanism identified in this study (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), suggesting the amplification of this mechanism during infection. This amplification may further enhance the aquaporin-mediated link and underscore its significance in antibacterial immunity.

In summary, we have demonstrated that in crayfish, the Duox2/ROS system is linked to the Relish/AMPs pathway through the aquaporin Aqp4. In this regulatory mechanism, Aqp4 mediates the entry of extracellular ROS into the cytosol, where ROS act as signaling molecules to enhance AMP expression (Fig. 7A). By expanding the immune consequence from short-lived ROS to long-lived AMP protein synthesis, this signaling mechanism strengthens the effectiveness and duration of host antibacterial response (Fig. 7B). This aquaporin-mediated link, ROS/Aqp4/FoxO/Relish regulatory axis, highlights the synergy and crosstalk between two principal antibacterial responses and provides insights into the complexity and systematic nature of arthropod immunity.

Working model of current study. Extracellular ROS generated by Duox2 enter the cell via Aqp4, activate the JNK/FoxO axis for Relish transcription, and ultimately the induction of Relish-targeting AMPs (A). This mechanism strengthens the effectiveness and duration of the host antibacterial response (B).

Materials and Methods

Animals and Immune Challenge.

Red swamp crayfish (P. clarkii; approximately 10 g) were obtained from an aquaculture farm in Huaian, China. Kuruma shrimp (Marsupenaeus japonicus; approximately 10 g) were obtained from a farm in Jimo, China, and cultured in air-pumped seawater. After 1 wk of acclimatization in the laboratory, healthy animals were randomly selected. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the School of Life Sciences, Shandong University (SYDWLL-2021-98).

A. hydrophila was cultured in Luria broth medium at 30 °C for 12 h. The bacterial pellet was collected via centrifugation and resuspended in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 137 mM NaCl, 10 mM Na2HPO4, 2.7 mM KCl, and 2 mM KH2PO4; pH 7.4). The bacterial concentration was determined by spreading the resuspension on agar plates. For oral infection, crayfish were fed 100 μL of bacterial suspension (2 × 107 CFU/mL). Uracil (A610564-0500; Sangon Biotech) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) as a stock solution and diluted to 100 nM (2% DMSO) before use. MDP (A9519, Sigma) was dissolved in water. The dilutions were orally instilled into crayfish intestine, using corresponding dissolvent as the control. The tissues were sampled at selected time after immune stimulation.

Expression Profiles Analysis.

Total RNAs were extracted from hemocytes and intestines using TRIzol (15596-026, Invitrogen). cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (R323-01, Vazyme). The expression of selected genes was detected using quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR). Primers used are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. Samples were analyzed using the CFX96 Real-time System (Bio-Rad) with following cycling protocol: 94 °C for 3 min; 40 cycles of 94 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 1 min; and a melting curve from 65 to 95 °C. β-actin was used as internal reference, and data were processed using the 2−ΔΔCT method. Four independent experiments were performed. Expression levels are presented relative to those of the control sample.

RNA-seq.

RNA-seq was performed to identify genes regulated by A. hydrophila. Total RNAs were extracted from crayfish hemocytes and intestines after bacterial infection using TRIzol. Each sample was pooled from at least 30 animals. RNA was divided into two halves: one half was retained for reverse transcription and qPCR validation, while the other half was sent for commercial RNA-seq. For RNA sequencing, RNA integrity was evaluated using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA), with all samples demonstrating RNA integrity numbers (RIN) > 8. Polyadenylated mRNA was enriched from 0.75 μg of total RNA, fragmented, and utilized as template for first-strand cDNA synthesis. Double-stranded cDNA was amplified by PCR, denatured, and sequenced using the BGISEQ 500 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China) with a bipartite sequencing length of 150 bp. High-quality reads were obtained by filtering raw reads and mapping them to crayfish genome (GenBank GCF_020424385.1). The fragments per kilobase per transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) value was used to determine the transcript's abundance.

RNAi.

Suitable siRNA target sequences were predicted using the siRNA online design tool (invivogen.com/sirnawizard/design_advanced.php) and subjected to BLAST analysis against the P. clarkii genome (GenBank GCF_020424385.1) to prevent off-target effects. The target sequences were scrambled to generate the corresponding control siRNA, which shares the same nucleotide composition as the testing siRNA but lacks significant sequence similarity to any known mRNA. Two pairs of reverse complementary oligonucleotide strands containing the siRNA sequence and T7 promoter were commercially synthesized (SI Appendix, Table S1). siRNAs were transcribed using the T7 RNAi Transcription Kit (TR102; Vazyme) and purified by phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (25:24:1). After verification and quantification, siRNA (5 µg/g crayfish) was injected into the crayfish hemocoel. The control group was injected with an equal amount of scrambled siRNA. Samples were collected 24 h post injection to determine RNAi efficiency using qPCR. After having validated the RNAi efficiency, subsequent experiments were performed 24 h after siRNA application.

Measurement of the Total Intestinal Bacterial Abundance.

The surface of animals was sterilized with 75% ethanol. Total DNA from the intestine was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (51104; Qiagen). qPCR was performed using universal primers for bacterial 16S rDNA (SI Appendix, Table S1). Bacterial abundance was characterized as the ratio of 16S rDNA to intestinal β-actin.

Tissue Morphology Detection.

A. hydrophila (107 CFU) was orally introduced to crayfish intestines. At 5 d after infection, the intestines were collected, rinsed in precooled PBS, blotted with clean filter paper, and immersed in Davidson’s AFA fixative (11.5% acetic acid, 22% formalin, and 30% ethanol) for 48 h. Tissues were dehydrated in ethanol solutions (70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100%) for 40 min each, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 µm) were cut using a Leica CM1860 microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany), deparaffinized, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Images were captured using a DP71 fluorescent microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

ROS and Hydrogen Peroxide Detection.

ROS levels in crayfish intestines and hemocytes were detected using the ROS probe dichloro-dihydro-fluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) (S0033S; Beyotime) 3 h after A. hydrophila or uracil treatment. Intestines were collected, rinsed three times with precooled PBS, and incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA for 30 min at 37 °C under light-avoidance conditions. The samples were rinsed three times with PBS (3 min each), placed on slides, covered with glycerol, then observed and imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The hemolymph was collected into equal volume of anticoagulant (0.1 M glucose, 0.14 M NaCl, 26 mM citric acid, 30 mM trisodium citrate, 10 mM EDTA, pH 4.6), and centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to collect hemocytes. Hemocytes were incubated in Petri dishes for 1 h to allow adequate attachment. DCFH-DA was added to incubate at 37 °C for 30 min. The hemocytes were then fluorescently imaged using a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope and analyzed using the ZEN software (Zeiss). The fluorescence intensities were quantified using the ImageJ software.

To detect hydrogen peroxide levels in the intestinal lumen and circulating hemolymph, the intestinal lumen was rinsed with precooled PBS, and the resulting rinsate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The hemolymph was collected in an anticoagulant and centrifuged at 1,200 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to obtain the supernatant. The hydrogen peroxide levels were determined using a Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit (S0038; Beyotime). Generally, 50 μL of the supernatant was added to a 96-well plate, followed by the addition of 100 μL of the assay kit to the sample wells. After gentle shaking, the mixture was incubated with a hydrogen detection solution at 25 °C for 30 min. Absorbance was measured at 560 nm using a Multiskan FC enzyme labeled (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Detection of Hemocytes ROS by Flow Cytometry.

Hemolymph was collected in an equal volume of anticoagulant and centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min at 4 °C to collect hemocytes. Hemocytes were rinsed three times with PBS, and 10 μM DCFH-DA (S0033S; Beyotime) was added. Next, the hemocytes were incubated at 30 °C for 30 min with rotation. The hemocytes were then centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The precipitate was resuspended in PBS. The hemocyte suspension was filtered using a 300-mesh filter. The fluorescence intensity of the ROS probe was analyzed using an ImageStreamX Mark II flow cytometer (Merck) and 10,000 cells were analyzed. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using the IDEAS software (Merck).

Separation of Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Proteins.

To determine the amount of FoxO in the nucleus, nuclear proteins were extracted using a Nuclear Protein Extraction Kit (R0050; Solarbio). Crayfish intestines were homogenized in cytoplasmic protein extraction buffer containing a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (M7528; AbMole Bioscience) and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) 3 h after A. hydrophila, MDP, or uracil stimulation. The homogenate was left on ice for 5 min and mixed using a vortex shaker for 20 s in four replicates. The homogenate was then centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The precipitate obtained by centrifugation was washed three times with PBS and resuspended in a nuclear protein extraction reagent containing a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail and 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF). The resuspension was again subjected to four rounds of ice bath and shaking, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C for 15 min at 12,000 × g. The resultant supernatant was used as the nuclear protein sample. The protein concentration was determined using a Bradford Protein Assay Kit (C503031; Sangon Biotech). The nuclear protein sample was processed using SDS-PAGE sample loading buffer (P0015; Beyotime) and boiled for 10 min for subsequent blotting assay.

Antibodies and Inhibitors.

Polyclonal antibodies against crayfish FoxO, JNK, Eiger, and β-actin were generated by immunizing rabbits with recombinant proteins as described in our previous studies and used at a dilution of 1:500 for the blotting assays (33, 47). Commercial antibodies included Phospho-JNK1/2-T183/Y185 Rabbit pAbs (AP0473; Abclonal; 1:1,000 dilution), Histone H3 Rabbit pAbs (17168-AP-1; ProteinTech; 1:3,000 dilution) and HRP-conjugated Goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (ZB-2301; Bio-Tech; 1:3,000 dilution).

The JNK inhibitor SP600125 (S1460; Selleck Chemicals) and Aqp4 inhibitor TGN-020 (HY-W008574; MCE) were dissolved in water containing 5% DMSO and 40% PEG300. Inhibitors were injected at final concentrations of 10 mM (SP600125) and 8 mM (TGN-020) in 20 µL per crayfish.

Western Blot.

The tissues were homogenized in RIPA buffer (P0013D; Beyotime) containing 1 mM PMSF. The homogenate was centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected, processed using loading buffer and boiled for 10 min. The protein samples were separated by 12.5% SDS-PAGE, with 100 μg protein per lane. The samples were then transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes using a Jim-X semidry transfer apparatus. After blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C for 12 h. After washing with TBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 (TBST), the membrane was incubated with the secondary antibody for 3 h at room temperature and washed three times with TBST. Finally, the signal was visualized using a high-signal enhanced chemiluminescence western blotting substrate (180-5001; Tanon) and a 5200 Chemiluminescence Imaging System (Tanon).

Immunocytochemical Analysis.

The subcellular localization of FoxO was analyzed using immunocytochemistry. At 3 h after A. hydrophila, MDP, or uracil stimulation, crayfish hemolymph was collected in a precooled anticoagulant containing 4% paraformaldehyde and fixed for 15 min. The sample was then centrifuged at 800 × g for 8 min at 4 °C to collect hemocytes. After washing thrice with PBS, the hemocytes were resuspended and spread onto poly L-lysine-coated glass slides. After attachment, hemocytes were washed thrice with PBS and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 10 min. After washing three times with PBS, the hemocytes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA, dissolved in PBS) for 1 h at 37 °C. and then incubated with anti-FoxO antibodies (1:100, diluted in 3% BSA) overnight at 4 °C. The slides were washed thoroughly with PBS and blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min at room temperature. Next, goat anti-rabbit antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 (A23220, Abbkine; 1:1,000 dilution in 3% BSA) was added to the slides and incubated in dark for 2 h. After washing three times with PBS, DAPI (AS-83210, AnaSpec) was added to the slides and incubated for 10 min. After washing five times with PBS, the slides were sealed, and observed under an LSM 900 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). The images were processed using the ZEN software (Zeiss).

Analysis of the Promoter Region.

The SMARTer RACE 5′ Kit (634868; Clontech) was first used to clone the 5′-end of Relish cDNA. The transcription start sites of Relish were identified by integrally comparing and analyzing cDNA sequences and transcriptome sequencing datasets. Sequences upstream of the transcription start sites were obtained from the P. clarkii genome (GenBank accession number GCF_020424385.1). Validation was performed by PCR and sequencing. Online PROMO 3.0 (alggen.lsi.upc.es/cgi-bin/promo_v3) and JASPAR (jaspar.genereg.net) tools were used to predict potential FoxO-binding sites in Relish promoter.

Chromatin Immunoprecipitation (ChIP) Assay.

ChIP assay was performed to detect the transcriptional regulation of Relish by FOXO. Hemocytes were collected 3 h after A. hydrophila or uracil stimulation and were processed as pools for ChIP. The ChIP assay was performed using a kit (P2078; Beyotime). Mostly, 106 hemocytes were washed three times with precooled PBS and resuspended in PBS containing 1% formaldehyde. The suspension was incubated at 37 °C for 15 min to allow sufficient genomic DNA–protein cross-linking, which was terminated by the addition of 1/10 volume of glycine solution (10×) and incubation at 25 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, the hemocytes were collected and lysed on ice in 0.5 mL SDS lysis buffer for 15 min. Genomic DNA was fragmented into 200 to 1,500 bp fragments by ultrasonic treatment (60 W power, 5 s impact, 5 s gap, for 20 passes). The suspension was centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to collect the supernatant. The ChIP dilution buffer was then added to the supernatant at a total volume of 1.5 mL. The suspension (15 μL) was retained as an input sample, and Protein A+G Agarose/Salmon Sperm DNA (60 μL) was added to the remaining suspension and prepurified by rotary incubation at 4 °C for 40 min. The suspension was then centrifuged to remove the agarose. The supernatant then separated into three portions. FoxO antibody (10 μg) and another unrelated antibody not cross-binding to any crayfish proteins were added separately and incubated for 12 h at 4 °C with rotation. The suspensions were then spiked with 40 μL of Protein A+G Agarose/Salmon Sperm DNA, and rotationally incubated at 4 °C for 40 min to capture antibody–protein–DNA complexes. Next, the suspension was centrifuged at 1,000 × g for 1 min at 4 °C to collect the agarose precipitate. The precipitates were then washed sequentially with low-salt, high-salt, and LiCl immunocomplex wash buffers, and finally washed twice with TE buffer. The bound precipitates were eluted by vortex centrifugation with elution buffer (0.1 M NaHCO3, 1% SDS). The supernatant was incubated for 5 h at 65 °C for the de-cross-linking reaction after addition of 1/20 volume of 5 M NaCl. Immunoprecipitates were detected using PCR to determine the presence and amount of specific DNA fragments.

Statistical Analysis.

All images shown are representative of three independent experiments, and all bar graphs show the mean ± SD from four independent replicates. At least six animals were used for each sample. Significant differences (*0.01 < P < 0.05; **0.001 < P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001) were analyzed for the obtained data using the Student’s t test by GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Inc., La Jolla, CA). Survival data were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test.
