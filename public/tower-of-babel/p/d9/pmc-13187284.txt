Tampa scale for kinesiophobia in individuals with knee osteoarthritis: a cross-sectional psychometric evaluation using item-level and exploratory factor analyses
Authors: Prasert Sakulsriprasert, Tipwadee Bunprajun, Ninwisan Hengsomboon, Pichaya Hengsomboon, Pavinee Harutaichun, Chompunoot Suwanasri, Pinyada Warathanagasame, Chutiporn Thammajaree, Akenarin Chocknakawaro, Nattapa Ariyakitsakul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated floor and ceiling (F/C) effects within the Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK) and examined item-level correlations with total TSK scores and pain intensity in individuals with knee osteoarthritis (KOA). A cross-sectional study was conducted involving 134 participants diagnosed with KOA. Each TSK item was analyzed to identify F/C effects, with a threshold of 15% set as the criterion for significance. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was employed to assess the relationships between each TSK item and the total TSK score, as well as between each item and pain intensity. Also, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA), a varimax rotation, was performed on the 17 items. Notably, significant floor effects were observed in items 4 and 12, while a ceiling effect was noted in item 13. Statistically significant correlations between individual items and the total TSK score were identified for all items except for items 8, 12, and 16. Furthermore, a significant correlation was found between item 3 and pain intensity. For the EFA analysis, the data were appropriate, the KMO and Bartlett sphericity tests were 0.748 and p < 0.001. the EFA suggests 4 components which explained 53% of the total variance. Items 4, 8, 12, 13, and 16 of the TSK demonstrated either F/C effects. Correlations with the total TSK score were generally non-significant, with values ranging from r = -0.053 to 0.308. For grouping of the items, 4 components are suggested. Therefore, it is recommended that these items may require further evaluation in future adaptations or psychometric evaluations of the TSK for this population.Keywords: Fear of movement, Knee osteoarthritis, Psychometric, Validity, AnalysisSubject terms: Diseases, Health care, Medical research, Rheumatology, Signs and symptoms

Introduction

The Tampa Scale of Kinesiophobia (TSK) was first created as an assessment tool to assess fear of movement or (re)injury in individuals experiencing pain. Notably, the term kinesiophobia had been introduced to describe a clinical phenomenon characterized by an excessive, irrational, and debilitating fear of movement and/or activity resulting from a feeling of vulnerability to painful injury or reinjury1,2. The conceptualization of kinesiophobia represented a significant advancement in the understanding of pain-related fear, especially in individuals with musculoskeletal conditions such as low back pain3–5, neck pain1,6–9, hip conditions10,11, and knee conditions12–15. The TSK has become a widely used assessment tool in clinical and research settings, facilitating the identification of patients whose avoidance behaviors and fear of movement may influence recovery and rehabilitation outcomes.

The original version of the TSK consists of 17 items, each evaluated using a 4-point Likert scale, with response options: strongly disagree, disagree, agree, and strongly agree. This version was initially developed in the English language2. Over the years, the TSK has undergone extensive cross-cultural adaptation and translation into various languages to ensure its applicability across diverse populations such as knee osteoarthritis, knee arthroplasty, and other chronic musculoskeletal conditions. For instance, validated versions of the TSK have been developed in languages such as Chinese16,17, Danish18, Dutch19, Finnish20, Italian21, Japanese22, Norwegian23, Persian8, Spanish24, Swedish25,26, Thai15, and Turkish27. Additionally, psychometric properties such as test–retest reliability20, predictive validity5,28, concurrent validity3,26,29, construct validity7,12,30–32, and responsiveness33,34 have been investigated. Previous studies have investigated the construct validity of the TSK across various musculoskeletal conditions in either full or short-form version, including neck9, shoulder35, low back36, and foot pain37, as well as femoroacetabular impingement syndrome10.

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) is a highly prevalent degenerative condition affecting the musculoskeletal system38–40. Previous studies have highlighted the association between knee pain and fear of movement or (re)injury in individuals with KOA, as assessed by the TSK41–43. The TSK has demonstrated robust psychometric properties when utilized as a measurement tool for assessing kinesiophobia in individuals with KOA. Specifically, it exhibits excellent internal consistency, as indicated by a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.90. Additionally, the scale shows excellent test–retest reliability over a 24-h interval, with an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC2,1) of 0.934. The TSK also demonstrates good convergent validity, as evidenced by significant correlations with related constructs such as pain intensity, anxiety levels, and functional disability15. However, the construct validity of the TSK in individuals with KOA awaits formal investigation. For KOA specifically, considering item-level will provide more details and precise clinical picture of the individual’s status.

Another critical aspect of the psychometric properties is the identification of floor and ceiling (F/C) effects which indicate the instrument’s ability to distinguish among respondents who score at the extreme lower (floor) or upper (ceiling) ends of the measurement scale44,45. The presence of significant F/C effects suggests potential limitations in the sensitivity that can be found in assessing kiensiophobia in individuals with KOA. In addition, it is essential to examine the relationship between each item and the total score, as this may reflect the relative importance or contribution of each item to the overall construct being measured. In addition, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) is useful in refining scales and construct validity. Therefore, this study aimed to explore the frequency of choice selection for determining the F/C effects, the relationship between each item of the TSK and the total score and pain intensity for determining the significance of each item, and the EFA evaluation for refining scale and suggesting next step for construct validity in representing the fear of movement or (re)injury in individuals with KOA.

Methods

Participants

A convenience sample of 134 individuals was recruited from several communities in Bangkok and Nakhon Pathom, Thailand. The participants provided written informed consent before participation. The inclusion criteria were men or women aged at least 50 years with unilateral knee osteoarthritis (KOA) diagnosed by a physician or met the clinical classification criteria for KOA by the American College of Rheumatology46,47 consisting of knee pain and had at least three of the following additional clinical criteria: (1) morning stiffness lasting less than 30 min, (2) crepitus, (3) bony tenderness, (4) bony enlargement, and (5) absence of palpable warmth. Exclusion criteria were a history of knee injury, knee replacement surgery, lower limb fracture, or steroid injections in the knee joints within three months prior to the study, the diagnosis of neurological conditions such as stroke or Parkinson’s disease, radicular low back pain that radiates to the lower limbs, and independent ambulation limitations. For sample size, we recruited 134 individuals with KOA as a sufficient amount according to the recommendations of the COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement Instruments (COSMIN), according to the 7–10 participants per item for the 17-item TSK48.

Study design and procedure

The research design was a cross-sectional study, which the Mahidol University Central Institutional Review Board approved with COA No. MU-CIRB 2024/004.0501 in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participants were interviewed to collect demographic information, including sex, age, weight, body mass index (BMI), and education (years). In addition, clinical data were obtained, such as pain intensity during symptom aggravation, assessed using a Visual Analog Scale (VAS), and the duration of symptoms. Following the interview, participants were asked to complete the original Thai version of the TSK, comprising 17 self-administered items15, with a total score ranging from 17 to 68. During data collection, assistance such as verbal explanation could be provided if needed.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 23 (IBM SPSS Inc., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05. Data normality was evaluated with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov Goodness–of–Fit test; the data were not normally distributed. Descriptive statistics were used to present demographic and clinical data, presented as mean ± standard deviation, frequency, and percentage of participants. The response of each item of the TSK was reported as percentages to determine the F/C effects; significant F/C effects were set at 15%44. The Spearman’s Correlation was used for correlating between each item of the TSK and the total score of the TSK and pain intensity; a coefficient below 0.4 was considered weak, 0.4–0.7 moderate, and greater than 0.7 a strong correlation. Also, the effect size (ES) was reported accordingly. Subsequently, to verify the suitability of the data for factor analysis, we performed both the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Following confirmation of appropriateness, an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was carried out on the 17 items to explore the possible model rather than to confirm the structure. For reverse-scored items (items 4, 8, 12, and 16), the reverse scoring was done before analysis. The multicollinearity has been investigated. To identify the underlying factor structure as a simple model, an orthogonal rotation, the varimax method, was applied. The number of factors to retain was determined through examination of the scree plot. An item was considered to load significantly on a factor if its factor loading was equal to or greater than 0.4049 for determining the strength of items to be kept in a model.

Results

Of all participants, 84% were female, reflecting a predominantly female sample. The overall mean age of participants was approximately 60 years, indicating a middle to older adult population50. The average BMI was greater than 25 kg/m2, which is classified as overweight51. Knee pain during symptom aggravation, as measured by the VAS, exceeded a score of 6. The duration of symptoms was more than 34 months. The average total score on the TSK was 43, which indicates the presence of kinesiophobia, given that the established cut-off point is 3752, as shown in Table 1.

Discussion

This study aimed to analyze the items of the TSK regarding the F/C effects for the distribution of item responses and the relationships between each item and the total score of the TSK and pain intensity in individuals with KOA. The participants in this study were predominantly female and over 60 years of age, which is consistent with the demographic profile of the majority of individuals affected by KOA53.

Even though in individuals with KOA, the TSK has excellent internal consistency, excellent test–retest reliability, and good convergent validity15. The F/C effects and the relationships between each item and the total score of the TSK and pain intensity, to reflect the ability of each item to classify individuals with or without kinesiophobia, have yet to be formally investigated. However, this study did not assess the discriminative ability of the TSK. Regarding the floor effect, items 4 “My pain would probably be relieved if I were to exercise” and 12 “Although my condition is painful, I would be better off if I were physically active” demonstrated evidence of this phenomenon, as the proportion of respondents selecting the lowest response option (choice 1) was 15.7% and 17.9%, respectively. Conversely, a ceiling effect was observed in item 13, where 15.7% of respondents selected the highest response option (choice 4). The presence of both floor and ceiling effects suggests a limitation in the sensitivity of these items. Specifically, the concentration of responses at the extreme ends of the scale indicates that these items may not be adequately capturing the full range of respondent experiences or perceptions, potentially reducing their discriminatory power within the measurement instrument44,45.

The correlations observed for all items ranged from weak to moderate. Except items 8 “Just because something aggravates my pain does not mean it is dangerous”, 12, and 16 “Even though something is causing me a lot of pain, I don’t think it’s actually dangerous” which no significant correlation was found. Notably, these three items are reversely worded and require reverse scoring. This observation suggests that the negative phrasing and reverse scoring format may influence how respondents interpret and respond to these items, potentially affecting their correlation with the total score. However, item 4 is another item that is negatively phrased, as well as items 8, 12 and 16, but item 4 was significantly correlated with the TSK total score. The previous study, conducted in individuals with chronic low back pain, manifested that the four negatively-phrased items (items 4, 8, 12 and 16) had low correlations and considered to be removed to create a short-form TSK30.

The relationship between individual items and pain intensity revealed that Item 3 demonstrated a moderate correlation with knee pain severity. This finding aimed to evaluate the concurrent validity of each item in relation to knee pain. Specifically, Item 3 “My body is telling me I have something dangerously wrong” showed a meaningful association, indicating its potential utility in reflecting the subjective experience of pain intensity or somatic misinterpretation. If a patient has a high score on Item 3, their pain intensity is no longer just a biological signal; it is a threat signal, which could be clinically meaningful for understanding patients and designing appropriate pain education. According to the two-factor classification model, this item belongs to the Somatic Focus domain, which encompasses beliefs and perceptions that emphasize physical symptoms and perceived bodily threats. The correlation observed suggests that individuals who strongly endorse somatic focus statements may also report higher levels of pain intensity, thereby supporting the relevance of this psychological dimension in understanding pain experiences54.

Regarding the EFA, the factor loading results suggested the presence of four distinct components. Component 1 comprised items 7, 5, 9, 3 and 2. Component 2 included items 10, 15, 1, 6 and 11. Component 3 consisted of items 4, 17, 12 and 14. Finally, Component 4 encompassed items 8, 16 and 13. The 4 components labeled as Somatic focus, Symptom concern, Activity concern, and Bargaining that might be related to fear-avoidance55, hypervigilance56, self-efficacy and avoidance learning57, and coping and adaptation58, respectively. Originally, the TSK suggested 2 components, Activity Avoidance and Somatic Focus5,54. Therefore, our findings suggest that two additional components may serve as supplementary elements for assessing individuals with KOA.

However, to further study regarding version revision in clinical and research settings, further validation is warranted. Specifically, the next phase of validation involves Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) to test the fit of the EFA-derived model. By evaluating the TSK after item deletion, we can confirm that the parsimonious four-factor structure, comprising Somatic focus, Symptom concern, Activity concern, and Bargaining, offers superior construct validity. This statistical approach would help determine whether the revised scale accurately reflects the underlying theoretical structure of kinesiophobia in individuals with KOA. Establishing such evidence is critical for ensuring that the scale provides reliable, valid, and interpretable scores that can inform patient assessment and intervention planning. The future study should consider the application of Rasch modeling or Item Response Theory (IRT) to further assess item fit and measurement precision, especially if scale refinement is a long-term goal. While this study uses a cross-sectional design, future longitudinal research is needed to monitor short- and long-term clinical changes in kinesiophobia among individuals with KOA.

The limitations of this study included the lack of radiographic evidence to support the presence of KOA. Additionally, further investigation into other psychometric properties of the instrument or assessment method is recommended. Specifically, future studies should examine aspects such as reliability, construct validity by using CFA, and responsiveness to ensure a more robust evaluation of its measurement properties. Furthermore, as the participants were predominantly female and recruited from community settings, these findings may not be fully generalizable to male populations or patients seen in acute clinical environments.

Conclusion

This study provides a detailed psychometric evaluation of the TSK in individuals with KOA, revealing specific areas that impact its clinical utility. Our findings identified significant floor effects in items 4 and 12, along with a ceiling effect in item 13. Clinically, this suggests that these specific items may lack the sensitivity required to detect meaningful changes in patients with extreme levels of fear-avoidance. Specifically, the scale may struggle to capture improvements in patients with very high baseline kinesiophobia or, conversely, may fail to differentiate between patients with very low levels of movement-related fear.

Furthermore, the EFA suggested a four-component structure for kinesiophobia in this population. This multidimensionality implies that kinesiophobia in KOA is not a monolithic construct; rather, it may be driven by distinct psychological sub-domains. For the clinician, this highlights the importance of looking beyond the total TSK score. Understanding which specific factor a patient scores highly in—be it somatic focus or activity avoidance, could allow for more tailored rehabilitation strategies, such as targeted cognitive-behavioral interventions or graded exposure therapy. While these results offer a more nuanced understanding of the TSK’s performance, subsequent psychometric evaluations, particularly using CFA, are essential. Such research is necessary to confirm the construct validity of this four-factor model and ensure the TSK remains a robust and reliable tool for guiding clinical decision-making in KOA rehabilitation.
