Developing a protocol to counteract spontaneous in vitro activation of intestinal fibroblasts using design of experiments
Authors: Zahra Mohammadalizadeh, Samanvitha Sudi, Daniel A. Basto Moreno, Katherine Bauer Estrada, Morgenne Almonte, Kaitlin C. Fogg, Ana Maria Porras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In vitro studies of intestinal fibrosis are confounded by spontaneous fibroblast activation on tissue culture polystyrene, hindering the investigation of early events that initiate fibrosis. Here, we present a statistically optimized culture protocol that suppresses fibroblast activation while preserving cell viability under standard culture conditions. Using a design of experiments (DOE) framework, we systematically evaluated combinations of extracellular matrix proteins and soluble factors to identify conditions that reduce myofibroblastic marker expression and extracellular matrix production. Fibroblasts cultured under optimized conditions remained spindle-shaped, exhibited low myofibroblastic marker expression, and showed reduced collagen and fibronectin secretion without evidence of cytotoxicity. The protocol was further validated in primary human colonic fibroblasts from both male and female donors, yielding consistent suppression of activation markers with high viability. This accessible and scalable approach provides a reproducible baseline for studying fibroblast activation and supports the use of DOE as a powerful strategy for defining microenvironmental conditions that regulate fibroblast behavior in vitro.

Introduction

Intestinal fibroblasts are the primary mediators of gastrointestinal fibrosis, orchestrating extracellular matrix (ECM) synthesis and remodeling during tissue repair1–3. Under homeostatic conditions, fibroblasts exhibit low expression of myofibroblastic markers, limited stress fiber organization, and are commonly described as quiescent4,5. Upon intestinal damage, fibroblasts transition toward an activated myofibroblast phenotype characterized by elevated α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) expression, prominent actin stress fibers, increased contractility, and enhanced ECM synthesis6–9. This process is normally resolved through reversion to a non-activated state or apoptosis10. However, in chronic inflammatory conditions such as Crohn’s disease, persistent pro-inflammatory cues disrupt this balance. The result is sustained fibroblast activation that drives cycles of matrix degradation and deposition that ultimately result in fibrosis, pathological ECM accumulation11–13. Uncontrolled fibrosis can lead to painful strictures and bowel obstructions requiring surgical intervention14,15. Furthermore, existing therapies for Crohn’s disease primarily target inflammation but do not prevent or reverse fibrosis progression16. This therapeutic gap is driven, in part, by an incomplete understanding of the early microenvironmental cues that regulate fibroblast activation and ECM dysregulation.

Our ability to investigate early fibroblast activation is hindered by the failure of many in vitro models to support quiescent and non-activated fibroblast phenotypes. Typically, colonic fibroblasts are cultured on tissue culture polystyrene (TCPS), a rigid substrate that induces spontaneous myofibroblastic activation17–21. As a result, fibroblasts cultured under these conditions often acquire a proto-myofibroblast phenotype, characterized by stress fiber formation and increased contractility, even before exposure to additional profibrotic stimuli22,23. These proto-myofibroblasts further differentiate into mature myofibroblasts upon sustained mechanical tension or treatment with soluble profibrotic factors, such as transforming growth factor beta-1 (TGF-β1)12,24–27. The spontaneous activation observed in conventional culture limits the ability to study the transition from non-activated fibroblasts to proto- and myofibroblasts.

One strategy to address this challenge has been the use of biomaterials or engineered substrates that approximate tissue-scale mechanics and reduce fibroblast activation driven by substrate stiffness28,29. While these systems have provided important insights into the mechanoregulation of fibroblast behavior, they often rely on specialized materials, relatively complex fabrication workflows, or custom platforms that limit scalability, reproducibility, and routine cell maintenance or expansion. As a result, there remains a need for accessible culture strategies that can attenuate fibroblast activation while remaining compatible with standard two-dimensional workflows used for fibroblast expansion and downstream experimentation.

Guided by this need, we developed a reproducible two-dimensional culture protocol designed to counteract spontaneous fibroblast activation in colonic fibroblasts. Because fibroblast activation is sensitive to both matrix-associated cues and soluble regulators1,28,30–32, we focused on systematically evaluating a defined set of ECM proteins and soluble factors with well-documented effects on fibroblast behavior in the intestine. Collagen types I and III, and laminin represent major structural components of intestinal ECM, and play key roles in shaping fibroblast adhesion, proliferation, and myofibroblastic marker expression11,33,34. Similarly, vitamin D and basic fibroblast growth factor (FGF) have been documented to modulate fibroblast activation, survival, and matrix remodeling in the colon in the context of fibrosis and wound healing35–41.

Given this defined set of ECM coatings and soluble factors, testing all potential combinations using traditional one- or two-variable-at-a-time experiments would be both time- and resource-intensive; thus, we employed a design of experiments (DOE) framework. DOE is a statistical optimization method that enables the simultaneous evaluation of multiple variables and their interactions, reducing experimental burden while enhancing experimental efficiency, scalability, and reproducibility42–46. Here, we used DOE to systematically evaluate combinations of ECM coatings and soluble factors, and identify culture conditions that counteract spontaneous myofibroblastic activation in vitro. Based on these findings, we developed and validated a reproducible two-dimensional protocol for maintaining CCD-18Co and primary colonic fibroblasts in a controlled, non-activated state compatible with standard culture workflows.

Results

Spontaneous activation of colonic fibroblasts on TCPS after 24 hours

We first confirmed the spontaneous activation of colonic fibroblasts upon conventional culture on TCPS. Both CCD-18Co fibroblasts and primary colonic fibroblasts (male and female donors) were seeded on TCPS immediately upon thawing of the frozen cell vial received from their respective vendors. Fibroblast activation was evaluated after 24, 48, and 72 h in culture on TCPS by immunostaining for α-SMA, a canonical marker of myofibroblastic differentiation12,47, and fibroblast activation protein (FAP), a marker associated with myofibroblasts involved in intestinal stricture formation6,10.

As early as 24 h after the initiation of in vitro culture, all colonic fibroblasts (CCD‑18Co, primary female, and primary male) exhibited a transition toward an activated phenotype, indicated by positive α‑SMA and FAP staining across all cell populations (Fig. 1). This activation was sustained beyond the 24-h time point (Supplementary Fig. 1). The high and widespread expression of both markers indicates that conventional culture on TCPS is sufficient to rapidly drive activation in both cell line and primary colonic fibroblasts, even in the absence of exogenous profibrotic stimuli. Individual exposure to ECM coatings or soluble factors is insufficient to suppress activation

To address the challenge of spontaneous activation by culture on TCPS, we first assessed the impact of individual ECM coatings on the expression of α-SMA by CCD-18Co colonic fibroblasts (Fig. 2A). Specifically, we evaluated collagen I, collagen III, and laminin as intestinal ECM components known to modulate fibroblast adhesion, proliferation, and myofibroblastic marker expression. Collagen I and III are the principal fibrillar collagens in the colonic submucosa and muscle layer, where they provide mechanical support and structural integrity that can modulate fibroblast behavior52,53. Previous studies have demonstrated that cardiovascular fibroblasts cultured on collagen matrices and coatings can lower the expression of myofibroblastic markers9,30,54. CCD-18Co fibroblasts were cultured for 5 days in medium supplemented with 2% serum on TCPS plates coated with collagen I, collagen III, or laminin at concentrations of 2, 15, or 30 μg/cm². An uncoated TCPS control group was included for baseline comparison. Fibroblasts grown under these control conditions exhibited high levels of α-SMA expression, consistent with an activated phenotype (Fig. 2A). Culture on the collagen I coatings had a minimal effect on α-SMA expression, with only 15 μg/cm² leading to a 12% reduction compared to the control (p = 0.06, Fig. 2A, B). Similarly, collagen III had no significant effect on the production of α-SMA at any concentration (Fig. 2A, C). Finally, α-SMA intensity was 15% lower at higher laminin densities (15 and 30 μg/cm²) compared to the control; however, these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.3, Fig. 2D). Collectively, these findings indicate that individual ECM coatings alone may only partially influence α-SMA expression patterns.

Next, we evaluated whether supplementing the culture medium with vitamin D or human FGF could more strongly influence cell phenotype. Vitamin D has been reported to suppress intestinal myofibroblast activation35–37, whereas FGF, a central regulator of cellular migration and survival, exerts context-dependent effects on ECM dynamics and myofibroblast activation39,40,57. For these experiments, CCD-18Co fibroblasts were cultured on TCPS in medium containing 2% serum and treated for 5 days with either vitamin D (1, 5, or 10 μM) or FGF (1, 10, or 20 ng/mL).

FGF treatment at 1 and 10 ng/mL led to a ~35% reduction in α-SMA staining intensity; however, these effects were not statistically significant (p = 0.17 and p = 0.14, Fig. 2E, F). In contrast, vitamin D decreased α-SMA expression in a dose-dependent manner, with both the 5 and 10 μM concentration showing a statistically significant 3-fold (p = 0.02) and 5-fold (p = 0.0007) decrease, respectively, compared to the control (Fig. 2E, G). However, further analysis revealed a substantial drop in cell viability at 10 μM vitamin D concentrations (p = 0.03, Fig. 2H), with more than 40% of cells classified as non-viable. These observations were confirmed through an analysis of cell apoptosis at the same vitamin D concentrations, where we observed statistically significant increases in caspase 3/7 activity at both the 5 μM (p = 0.02) and 10 μM (p < 0.0001) concentrations compared to the control, with the 10 μM concentration reaching levels comparable to those of the ethanol positive control (Fig. 2I). In contrast, cell viability was unaffected by FGF at all concentrations (Fig. 2J), and caspase activity remained comparable to that of the untreated control (Fig. 2K). These results suggest that while vitamin D can suppress fibroblast activation, its utility is limited by cytotoxicity at effective doses.

These single-factor experiments demonstrate that individual ECM coatings or soluble cues are insufficient to counteract TCPS-induced activation, motivating the use of a multivariable optimization approach.

Identification of culture conditions that counteract fibroblast activation using DOE

We applied a DOE strategy to investigate whether combinations of ECM coatings and soluble factors more effectively mitigate fibroblast activation than single cues. Specifically, we evaluated the effects of five continuous input variables—collagen I (2–30 µg/cm²), collagen III (2–30 µg/cm²), laminin (2–30 µg/cm²), vitamin D (1–10 µM), and FGF (1–20 ng/mL)—on four key cellular outcomes: α-SMA intensity, fibronectin secretion, cell viability and proliferation. The concentration ranges for each input variable were informed by published in vitro studies using these ECM components and soluble factors to influence fibroblast and mesenchymal cell behavior30,33,37,54,58–60. The ranges were also selected to capture a wide range of outcomes, including both non-cytotoxic and cytotoxic responses, as demonstrated by the single-factor data (Fig. 2). The phenotypic outcomes were selected based on their relevance to established phenotypic features of fibroblast activation and quiescence. In the quiescent state, fibroblasts generally display low levels of activation markers, such as α-SMA4,9,61, produce less fibronectin and other ECM proteins56,62, and exhibit reduced proliferative activity63,64. Cell viability was also selected as a marker because of the cytotoxic effects of vitamin D observed in the preliminary experiments (Fig. 2H, I).

We specifically employed a Box–Behnken design resulting in 42 unique experimental conditions (Supplementary Table 1). The control condition consisted of cells seeded on TCPS without coatings and fed medium without soluble factor supplementation. For each condition, α-SMA intensity, fibronectin secretion, viability, and proliferation were measured after 5 days in culture. All output variables were significantly affected by at least one input variable, with statistically significant linear and second-order interaction effects identified for most responses (Table 1). The ANOVA results also demonstrate that none of the responses exhibited a significant lack of fit (all p values > 0.40), indicating that the pure error is small relative to the variation explained by the model (Table 1). All output variables had an R² value greater than 0.5, demonstrating that the fitted models explain a substantial proportion of the observed variance. For fibronectin secretion, vitamin D emerged as the only significant input factor, with a positive quadratic effect (p = 0.0094), leading to decreased fibronectin secretion at low to mid (3–4 μM) vitamin D concentrations (Fig. 3B). Neither FGF nor any ECM proteins showed significant main effects or interactions that impacted this response (Table 1). Furthermore, the absence of significant interaction terms between vitamin D and other input factors (Table 1) indicates that this soluble factor influences fibronectin secretion independently of the ECM substrates and FGF.

Cell proliferation was significantly affected by vitamin D and FGF (Table 1), with proliferation ranging from 1 to 25% of the total cell population (Fig. 3C). FGF concentration demonstrated a positive linear relationship with proliferation (p < 0.0001), while vitamin D exhibited a negative quadratic effect (p = 0.0024), with maximal proliferation at a dosage between low and mid levels (1.8–3.8 μM). FGF exhibited a main effect on proliferation but did not have significant interaction terms with any of the ECM coatings or vitamin D (Table 1). On the other hand, vitamin D had a significant interaction with collagen III (p = 0.02), with wider proliferation ranges observed in the response to vitamin D at high (22.49%) versus low (10.71%) collagen III coating densities (Fig. 4D).

Finally, cell viability displayed a range from 78.4 to 99.3% and was significantly influenced by both collagen I and vitamin D (Table 1). The factor effect plot revealed a slight negative quadratic relationship for collagen I (with maximal viability at 20–24 μg/cm2, p = 0.0277) and a more pronounced quadratic relationship for vitamin D (p < 0.0001), with a peak between 2 and 4 μM, closer to the lower end of the concentration range (Fig. 3D). Collagen I did not have significant interaction terms with any of the other input factors (Table 1). In contrast, vitamin D had a significant interaction with FGF (p = 0.05), with high doses of FGF mitigating the cytotoxic effects of high vitamin D concentrations (Fig. 4E).

In contrast to the single-factor preliminary experiments, which produced only modest or variable effects, the DOE model revealed synergistic interactions between matrix-associate and soluble cues that more effectively counteracted spontaneous fibroblast activation.

Validation of DOE model predictions

Following the primary goal of this study, we first evaluated a multi-objective optimization designed to suppress myofibroblastic activation by simultaneously minimizing α-SMA expression, fibronectin secretion, and proliferation while maximizing cell viability (Opt.all, Table 2). These target outcomes were selected based on well-established phenotypic features commonly associated with quiescent fibroblasts across multiple tissue types: reduced expression of myofibroblastic markers, low ECM production, and low proliferation rates4,9,62,63. This framework provided an initial benchmark for assessing whether the DOE model could identify culture conditions that broadly shift colonic fibroblasts away from an activated, myofibroblastic state.

Although reduced proliferation is often considered a hallmark of quiescent fibroblasts, prior studies in colonic fibroblasts report variable and conflicting reports on the relationship between proliferative activity and myofibroblastic activation12,27,65. Consistent with this uncertainty, inspection of the experimental data used to fit the DOE model revealed substantial variability in the relationship between α-SMA expression and proliferation across conditions, with no stable pattern linking these two outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 3). To account for this variability, we evaluated two multi-objective optimization strategies: one that included proliferation as a target outcome (Opt.all), and a second that excluded proliferation while optimizing all other responses (Opt.all (-prolif.); Table 2). Finally, we identified the culture conditions predicted to lead to the “Optimal” (most desirable) and “Pessimal” (least desirable) outcomes for each response variable (Table 2).

Experimental validation after 5 days of treatment demonstrated that the DOE model indeed predicted culture conditions that produced statistically significant reductions in α-SMA intensity relative to the TCPS control (Fig. 5A, B). Across all optimization objectives (including both optimal and pessimal predictions), α-SMA intensity was significantly reduced compared to the control, with decreases ranging from approximately 2- to 10-fold (Fig. 5B). Among all tested predicted conditions, the multi-objective optimization excluding proliferation (Opt.all (-prolif)) yielded the lowest α-SMA expression levels, reaching 10 ± 1% of the control levels (p < 0.0001). Similar to the α-SMA results, fibronectin secretion differed significantly between optimal and pessimal predictions across all optimization objectives (Fig. 5C). In all cases, the optimal and pessimal predictions followed the expected optimal–pessimal pattern for fibronectin secretion, with optimal conditions producing significantly lower fibronectin levels than their corresponding pessimal counterparts (Opt.FN: p < 0.0001; Opt.all: p = 0.015; Opt.all (-prolif): p < 0.0001). Notably, exclusion of proliferation from the multi-objective optimization resulted in significantly lower fibronectin secretion compared to Opt.all (p = 0.0074), with Opt.all (-prolif) producing a 51 ± 0.5% reduction in fibronectin production relative to the control (p < 0.0001), which was equivalent to the reduction observed when fibronectin was optimized alone. These results indicate that excluding proliferation from the optimization more effectively suppresses ECM deposition in colonic fibroblasts.

Cell viability remained high across the multi-objective optimal conditions, with both Opt.all and Opt.all (-prolif) maintaining above 98% viability, comparable to the TCPS control and the single-objective viability optimization (Opt.viab; Fig. 5D). To evaluate whether this preservation of viability extended to single-objective optimizations, we compared cell viability across all optimal predicted conditions. All single-objective optimal conditions other than Opt.viab significantly reduced viability relative to the control, ranging from 73 to 87% (Supplementary Fig. ). As a result, cell viability emerged as a key constraint when comparing optimized conditions, favoring multi-objective solutions that reduced myofibroblastic marker expression and ECM production while maintaining cell survival.

Proliferation outcomes did not follow a consistent optimal–pessimal pattern across optimization objectives (Fig. 5E). When proliferation was included as a target response (Opt.all), no statistically significant differences were observed between optimal and pessimal predictions (p > 0.9999). When proliferation was excluded from the optimization (Opt.all (-prolif)), the optimal condition exhibited higher proliferative activity (~20%) than its pessimal counterpart (~4%; p < 0.0001), despite showing the lowest α-SMA expression and reduced fibronectin secretion. These results indicate that, in CCD-18Co fibroblasts, reduced myofibroblastic activation can occur independently of suppressed proliferation. In agreement with prior reports describing variable relationships between proliferation and activation in colonic fibroblasts12,27,65, proliferation did not serve as a reliable discriminator of fibroblast inactivation in this system.

Collectively, these results support the selection of the Opt.all (-prolif) condition as the final optimized culture condition. It consistently yielded the lowest α-SMA expression, strong suppression of fibronectin secretion, and high cell viability, without the inconsistent outcomes observed when proliferation was included as an optimization target. Although several single-objective optimizations effectively suppressed individual responses, these conditions were often associated with reduced viability. In contrast, Opt.all (-prolif) provided the most robust and reproducible combination of reduced myofibroblastic activation and preserved cell survival (Supplementary Fig. 4) and was therefore selected for further validation and protocol development.

The optimized protocol suppresses fibroblast activation in CCD-18Co fibroblasts

Based on the DOE validation results (Fig. 5), we selected the multi-objective Opt.all (-prolif) optimization as the final culture protocol for downstream characterization. The final optimized conditions consisted of culture on ECM coatings composed of 27.3 μg/cm² collagen I, 2 μg/cm² collagen III, and 30 μg/cm² laminin, and in medium supplemented with 5.3 μM vitamin D and 20 ng/mL FGF. CCD-18Co fibroblasts were maintained under these conditions for 5 days, after which we performed a comprehensive analysis of key viability and activation-associated readouts. The control for these experiments consisted of conventional culture on TCPS without ECM coatings or soluble factor supplementation. Cells treated with TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL) were also included as a positive control.

To confirm that the optimized protocol did not induce cellular stress or cytotoxicity, we first assessed apoptosis and oxidative stress. Caspase-3/7 activity under the optimized protocol was comparable to that of the untreated control (p < 0.0001; Supplementary Fig. 5A). Similarly, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels were significantly reduced under the optimized protocol compared to the untreated control (p = 0.0008; Supplementary Fig. 5B). These results indicate that the optimized culture conditions maintain cell viability without inducing apoptosis or oxidative stress.

Finally, we evaluated the expression of SHOX2, a marker that has been reported to be downregulated in myofibroblasts and upregulated in less-activated fibroblast states61,69. SHOX2 expression was upregulated in the cells cultured with the optimized protocol compared to the control and TGF-β1-treated cells; however, this increase was not statistically significant (Supplementary Fig. 5C; p = 0.09).

These data confirm that the optimized protocol successfully downregulates the expression of myofibroblastic markers at both the gene and protein levels.

Because activated fibroblasts also produce more ECM4,5, we also confirmed that the optimized protocol decreased the production of ECM proteins beyond fibronectin. The cells treated with the optimized cultured conditions displayed reduced transcription of the ECM genes COL3A1 (p = 0.0001; Fig. 6J) and COL1A1 (p = 0.04; Fig. 6K) compared to cells in conventional culture. In contrast, treatment with TGF-β1 led to the significant upregulation of COL1A1 compared to the untreated control (p = 0.03). To confirm these changes at the protein level, we quantified secreted collagen type I in the supernatant (Fig. 6L). TGF-β1-treated cells secreted relatively high amounts of collagen (p < 0.001). In contrast, treatment with the optimized condition led to a statistically significant 4-fold reduction in collagen I secretion compared to this positive control (p < 0.0001).

Altogether, these results suggest that the protocol successfully induced a quiescent state in CCD-18Co colonic fibroblasts, as indicated by changes in cell morphology consistent with quiescence, and the downregulation of both myofibroblastic marker expression and ECM production.

Discussion

The spontaneous activation of colonic fibroblasts during conventional in vitro culture on TCPS represents a significant limitation for experimental studies of intestinal fibrosis, as it masks early transitions in fibroblast state. In this study, we sought to identify defined microenvironmental cues, including ECM substrates and soluble factors, that modulate colonic fibroblast activation. Using a DOE framework, we systematically optimized combinations of these cues and identified culture conditions that counteract spontaneous myofibroblastic activation in both the CCD-18Co cell line and primary human colonic fibroblasts. Fibroblasts cultured using the optimized protocol exhibited robust suppression of myofibroblastic activation markers and reduced ECM production, while maintaining high cell viability. These findings highlight the significant roles that ECM substrates and soluble agents play in shaping fibroblast phenotype, while also illustrating the utility of DOE for uncovering complex, non-linear relationships between these cues that are difficult to resolve using traditional experimental approaches.

Vitamin D emerged as the most important input factor in the DOE model, with significant influence on all response variables through both direct effects and interactions with other factors. This finding aligns with clinical and experimental reports on the role of vitamin D in intestinal fibrosis35,37,71,72. Clinically, vitamin D deficiency is more prevalent in patients with Crohn’s disease than in the general population35,72. Furthermore, vitamin D receptor (VDR) expression is decreased in stenotic colonic tissues, and knocking down VDR has been shown to induce myofibroblastic activation and increased fibronectin deposition in colonic fibroblasts in vitro37,71.

These observations suggest that vitamin D may play a critical role in attenuating fibroblast activation. In fact, vitamin D supplementation inhibits myofibroblast activation in vitamin D-deficient mice with chronic colitis36, and reduces disease activity and relapse risk in Crohn’s patients73–76. Interestingly, we found that the effect of vitamin D on fibroblast phenotype is highly context dependent. For example, vitamin D alone did not significantly affect α‑SMA expression; instead, its impact on this marker emerged through synergistic interactions with collagen III. Similarly, the influence of vitamin D on ECM secretion was complex: low doses suppressed fibronectin secretion, whereas high doses stimulated its production and induced cytotoxicity. Reflecting these conflicting findings, our final protocol includes vitamin D at the mid concentration of 5.3 μM. Overall, our results highlight vitamin D as a critical but context-sensitive modulator of colonic fibroblast activation, whose antifibrotic effects depend on both dosing and microenvironmental interactions.

Our findings also highlight FGF as a potent regulator of fibroblast behavior, exerting significant effects on α-SMA intensity, cell proliferation, and viability. Not much is known about the specific contributions of FGF to intestinal fibroblast activation. Available evidence suggests that FGF supports intestinal wound healing39,77,78, with one study reporting increased fibroblast activation and ECM deposition after FGF treatment in a mouse model of colonic anastomoses78. However, FGF has been shown to suppress myofibroblast activation and TGF-β1 signaling in other fibroblast types79–81. Additional studies across multiple tissues indicate that FGF promotes regeneration by increasing fibroblast proliferation, survival, and migration while impeding the full transition to a myofibroblastic phenotype82–84. Consistent with these reports, high concentrations of FGF were associated with low α‑SMA expression and high cell proliferation in our model, and partially rescued the negative effects of vitamin D on cell viability. These data indicate FGF might also induce a proliferative but non-contractile phenotype in intestinal fibroblasts.

ECM composition also emerged as a critical modulator of fibroblast behavior, with both collagen I and III significantly influencing α‑SMA expression. Collagens are the most abundant proteins in submucosal ECM, providing important structural support and maintaining stromal homeostasis in intestinal tissue; yet, their abnormal accumulation is a hallmark of fibrosis in Crohn’s disease85–87. The DOE model revealed a positive quadratic effect of collagen I on α-SMA intensity, with both low and high concentrations associated with increased expression of this activation marker. In contrast, collagen III alone did not significantly affect α-SMA expression but displayed strong interaction effects with other input factors that modulated this activation marker. The optimized coating composition that minimized myofibroblastic activation consisted of high collagen I (27.3 μg/cm2) and low collagen III (2 μg/cm2) densities. Comparable collagen I coating densities have been previously demonstrated to decrease the expression of myofibroblastic markers in valvular interstitial cells30,54, whereas fibroblasts isolated from strictures of Crohn’s disease patients exhibit more collagen III deposition relative to healthy controls11. Nonetheless, it is important to note that collagen coatings lack a fibrillar structure, which may impact the cellular response to these substrates due to differences in integrin signaling or the ability to generate tension compared to fibrillar configurations88,89. For example, hybrid nanofibril matrices composed of both collagen I and III at a 2:1 ratio lead to vaginal fibroblast activation more effectively than matrices composed of either protein alone90, underscoring the importance of collagen composition and architecture in regulating fibroblast activation states.

While the precise contribution of laminin to intestinal fibrosis remains poorly defined, evidence from other organs indicates that it functions not only as a structural epithelial scaffold but also as a dynamic regulator of fibrotic processes55,91, with effects that vary by tissue type and disease state. Reduced laminin expression in skeletal muscle and lung epithelium has been associated with increased cell death, fibrosis, and impaired tissue repair92–94, whereas excessive laminin accumulation is a feature of fibrotic liver tissue91. In our study, laminin exhibited a negative quadratic effect on α-SMA expression, with both high and low concentrations resulting suppressing this activation marker. Moreover, laminin displayed synergistic interactions with collagen III, further highlighting its context-dependent influence on fibroblast behavior.

One of the central findings of this study was that myofibroblastic marker expression and proliferation were dissociated in CCD-18Co fibroblasts. Consistent with prevailing models of fibroblast activation in the intestines and other tissues1,2,12,30,95,96, we initially hypothesized that suppression of myofibroblastic markers, ECM production, and proliferation would co-occur. However, experimental validation of the DOE predictions revealed a different pattern in colonic fibroblasts: conditions associated with elevated α-SMA expression exhibited low proliferation, and conditions that decreased α-SMA expression were often accompanied by increased proliferation. Examination of the single-factor effects in the DOE model reinforced this dissociation, as high FGF and low vitamin D concentrations both reduced α-SMA while enhancing proliferation. In support of these observations, Simmons et al. previously demonstrated that treatment with TGF-β1 inhibits proliferation in CCD-18Co fibroblasts while increasing α-SMA expression and collagen production97. Similar uncoupling of proliferation and myofibroblastic differentiation has been described in other fibroblast populations, including corneal fibroblasts83 and cardiac fibroblasts9. Collectively, these results support an emerging view of fibroblast activation as a continuum of phenotypic states7,98,99, in which myofibroblastic differentiation and proliferation can be independently tuned in a tissue- and context-dependent manner.

Our preliminary experiments demonstrated that no single cue was sufficient to counteract fibroblast activation, underscoring the need for a combinatorial approach. By applying DOE, we identified specific combinations of ECM substrates and soluble factors that produced consistently attenuated myofibroblastic activation while preserving cell viability, with substantially greater efficiency than traditional trial-and-error methods46. This strategy not only revealed synergistic relationships between matrix-associated and soluble cues that would have been difficult to predict a priori but also highlighted the multifactorial nature of fibroblast state regulation within the intestinal microenvironment.

The culture conditions identified here establish a controlled, non-activated baseline in vitro culture state that is well-suited for probing pro-fibrotic signaling pathways. While this study focused on TGF-β1 as a canonical driver of myofibroblast differentiation, intestinal fibrosis is shaped by additional inflammatory and developmental pathways1,2, including IL-6–STAT3 and Wnt/β-catenin signaling, which were not examined in this work. The ability to maintain fibroblasts in a controlled, non-activated state provides a necessary foundation for future studies aimed at systematically probing how these and other pathways initiate or modulate activation trajectories. Consistent with this framework, we have previously shown that quiescent valve fibroblasts are more sensitive to profibrotic stimuli such as TGF-β1 and gut-derived metabolites than activated cells30,51. Thus, the protocol described has the potential to enable more precise investigation of early cellular and molecular cues that drive intestinal fibroblast activation. Looking ahead, this platform can be integrated into more complex in vitro systems incorporating inflammatory cytokines, microbial metabolites, three-dimensional culture matrices, or mechanical cues to dissect how diverse microenvironmental signals converge to drive intestinal fibrosis.

There are several limitations to this study that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, although the DOE approach enabled systematic exploration of selected ECM substrates and soluble factors, we did not incorporate biomechanical cues such as substrate stiffness, cyclic strain, or fluid shear stress, all of which are known to influence fibroblast activation and mechanotransduction. As a result, the culture conditions described here do not capture the full mechanical complexity of the intestinal microenvironment, and the observed phenotypes should be interpreted within the context of static two-dimensional culture. Even so, we were able to suppress spontaneous myofibroblastic activation using a limited set of biochemical and matrix-associated inputs, demonstrating that relatively simple culture modifications can exert strong control over fibroblast state. This simplicity also enhances the reproducibility and scalability of the protocol for use across diverse laboratories.

Second, the culture duration in this study was relatively short—5 days. Our prior work with valvular interstitial cells showed that longer culture periods can further stabilize the quiescent phenotype30, raising the possibility that extended time courses could enhance the effects observed here. Third, fibroblasts are increasingly recognized as a heterogeneous population comprising multiple transcriptional and functional subtypes that vary across anatomical location and disease state100,101. While we validated the optimized protocol in both CCD-18Co cells and primary human colonic fibroblasts, this study did not resolve subtype-specific responses or assess whether distinct fibroblast subsets are differentially regulated by the identified culture conditions.

In conclusion, we present a robust protocol for mitigating spontaneous activation in both CCD-18Co and primary colonic fibroblasts by leveraging statistical optimization through DOE. This approach consistently lowered myofibroblastic marker expression, reduced ECM production, and preserved cell viability under standard two-dimensional culture conditions, resulting in an inactivated spindle-shaped fibroblast phenotype. By enabling the simultaneous optimization of multiple cellular behaviors, DOE provides a powerful and generalizable strategy for defining culture conditions to manipulate fibroblast states in vitro. Because the final protocol relies on commonly used ECM coatings, soluble factors, and standard culture reagents, it can be readily scaled or adapted by other laboratories studying intestinal fibrosis. Together, these findings underscore the central role of microenvironmental cues in regulating fibroblast behavior and provide a reproducible starting point for mechanistic studies of intestinal fibroblast activation and fibrosis-associated processes in the colon.

Assessment of primary fibroblast activation after culture on TCPS

Human Colonic Fibroblasts (HCnF, female primary cells derived from healthy human colon) were purchased from Cell Application Inc. (product # 710Cn-05a), and Human Colonic Fibroblasts (HCoF, male primary cells isolated from healthy human colon), were purchased from ScienCell Research Laboratories (product # 2880). Primary cells were thawed directly from the vial obtained from their corresponding cell bank (received at passage 1) and seeded on TCPS 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells/cm². The primary fibroblasts were fed with Fibroblast Medium (ScienCell Research Laboratories, product # 2301) supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS; ScienCell, product # 0010), 1% fibroblast growth supplement (ScienCell, product # 2352), hereafter referred to as primary fibroblast complete medium. After 24 , 48 , and 72 h, the cells were fixed using 3.7% paraformaldehyde fixative solution (PFA; Thermofisher Scientific, product # J61984.AP) for 15 min at room temperature for evaluation of myofibroblastic activation markers. The primary cells were also expanded into TCPS flasks using primary fibroblast complete medium in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for further experiments.

ECM protein coatings

Tissue culture-treated well plates were coated with collagen type I (type I bovine collagen solution; Advanced Biomatrix, product # 5005), collagen type III (isolated from human placenta; Advanced Biomatrix, product # NC1201241) or laminin (mouse protein; Gibco, product # 23-017-015) in bicarbonate buffer (pH 9.6) at one of three densities (2, 15, or 30 μg/cm²). The plates were incubated overnight at 4 °C, then washed with sterile PBS prior to cell culture to remove residual coating solution.

CCD-18Co in vitro culture

CCD-18Co colonic fibroblasts (a cell line isolated from a healthy female donor) were obtained from ATCC (CRL-1459). For the spontaneous activation experiment, a subset of the cells was seeded on TCPS immediately after thawing of the cell vial obtained from ATCC (received at passage 10). The fibroblasts were seeded in a 96 well-plate at a density of 5000 cells/cm² in Corning Eagle’s Minimum Essential Medium (EMEM; Fisher Scientific, product # MT10009CV) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma Aldrich, product # F0926). The expression of fibroblast activation markers was assessed after 24 , 48 , and 72 h in culture.

The remaining CCD-18Co cells were expanded in TCPS flasks using EMEM supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma Aldrich, product # F0926) in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Upon reaching 80% confluency, the cells were trypsinized using 5× Trypsin-EDTA solution (Sigma Aldrich, product # T4174), centrifuged at 300×g for 5 min, and seeded at a density of 5000 cells/cm² in EMEM containing 2% FBS. The CCD-18Co colonic fibroblasts were used between passages 11 and 13 for all experiments, including the preliminary experiments, the DOE model, and the validation experiments.

For the initial exploratory experiments, only one factor was varied at a time to assess its individual effect on fibroblast phenotype. To evaluate the impact of individual ECM protein coatings, cells were seeded onto plates coated with either collagen I, collagen III, or laminin at three densities (2, 15, or 30 μg/cm²). In the soluble factor experiments, cells were seeded on non-coated plates; following a 24h incubation period, the media were replaced with EMEM supplemented with 2% FBS and either human basic FGF (1, 10, and 20 ng/mL; ThermoFisher Scientific, product # 100-18C), or vitamin D (1, 5, and 10 μM; Fisher Scientific; product # AAB2252406). For all experiments, the culture media were refreshed every other day. Control wells consisted of cells seeded on non-coated TCPS and maintained in 2% FBS without additional supplements. On day 5, cell viability and apoptosis were assessed for vitamin D and FGF across different concentrations, and the cells were fixed for downstream analyses, including measurement of α-SMA intensity and proliferation.

Immunocytochemistry for myofibroblastic markers

α-SMA expression was evaluated using standard immunocytochemistry techniques. Briefly, cells were fixed with 3.7% PFA for 15 min, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 20 min, and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Fisher Scientific, product # BP9703100) in PBS for 30 min. Cells were incubated for 2 h with the following primary antibodies diluted in 1% BSA: mouse monoclonal anti–α-SMA (1:500, clone 1A4; Sigma Aldrich, product # A5228), recombinant rabbit monoclonal anti–FAP (1:100, clone 5H0Y9; Invitrogen, product # MA5-51530), and recombinant rabbit monoclonal anti-Vinculin (1:100, clone 42H89L44; Invitrogen, product # 700062). After three washes with PBS to remove unbound antibodies, the cells were incubated with a goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 594 (1:1000 dilution in 1% BSA) (Invitrogen, product # A-11005) and goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (1:1000 dilution in 1% BSA) (Invitrogen, product # A-11001) for 1 h, followed by another wash step with PBS. Cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000 in PBS) for 5 min and washed with PBS to prepare for visualization. All staining steps were conducted at room temperature.

Immunofluorescence images were captured using a Keyence fluorescence microscope (BZ-X800 series), acquiring images in separate channels for DAPI (blue, nuclear stain) and the desired marker (red or green) for each field of view. For every well, six representative fields were selected and imaged in batch mode, recording the blue, red, or green, and corresponding overlay images for each location. Image sets from each condition (≥24 images per condition) were then analyzed using CellProfiler Image Analysis (version 4.2.5) software102.

Nuclei were first identified in the DAPI channel to provide an anchor point for each cell using adaptive thresholding with the Otsu method. Using nuclei as seeds, total cell boundaries were defined in the α-SMA or other marker channel, and cytoplasmic regions were segmented as secondary objects using the propagation method with adaptive thresholding (Otsu method). A threshold correction factor of 0.5–1 and an adaptive window size of 100 were applied. This approach segmented individual cells and quantified fluorescence intensity from the α-SMA (or other myofibroblastic marker) channel within those segmented boundaries. For each cell, mean cytoplasmic marker intensity was calculated by dividing the summed pixel intensity within the secondary object by the total number of pixels defining the cytoplasmic region. Identical image processing parameters were applied across experimental conditions (n = 4–6 wells/condition). The average fluorescence intensity per cell for each condition was averaged per well and then normalized to the average fluorescence intensity per cell in the control on the same well plate.

Assessment of cell viability

Cell viability was evaluated on day 5 using a Live/Dead cell imaging kit (488/570) (Invitrogen, product # R37601) following the manufacturer’s protocol. After removing all existing culture media, cells were incubated with a 1:2 dilution of Live/Dead solution in fresh culture medium (prepared by mixing equal volumes of the assay reagents and fresh culture medium) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. Post-incubation, the cells were imaged using a Keyence BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope with live cells fluorescing green and dead cells fluorescing red. A constant number of fields of view were imaged for each well, and the number of live/dead cells per well was quantified using the Keyence BZ-X800 image analyzer software. The viability percentage was then calculated as the ratio of live cells to the total cell number, multiplied by 100%.

Assessment of apoptosis

Cell apoptosis was quantified under different experimental conditions using the SensoLyte™ Homogenous AFC Caspase-3/7 Assay Kit (AnaSpec, product # AS-71114) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The cells were seeded on black, clear-bottom tissue culture microplates and cultured for 5 days. On day 5, 50 μL of the caspase-3/7 substrate solution was added to each well to achieve a final volume of 150 μL. Treatment with 70% ethanol for 30 min prior to substrate addition served as a positive control. The plate was then incubated on a shaker at 100–200 rpm at room temperature for 45 min, protected from light. The fluorescence intensity was measured at Ex/Em = 380 nm/500 nm using a microplate reader (Molecular devices SpectraMax® iD3) and was normalized to the number of cells in each condition.

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) quantification

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFDA / H2DCFDA - Cellular ROS Assay Kit (Abcam; product # ab113851) was used to measure under different treatments. The cells were seeded on black, clear-bottom tissue culture microplates and cultured for 5 days. On day 5, cells designated as positive control were treated with 200 μM tert-butyl hydrogen peroxide (TBHP) for 2 h at 37 °C. After treatment, the cells were washed twice with 1× assay buffer (provided with the kit) and then incubated with 10 μM 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DFCDA) for 45 min at 37 °C, protected from light. The resulting DCF fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader (Molecular Devices SpectraMax® iD3) with excitation/emission at 485 nm/535 nm and normalized to the number of cells per condition.

Quantification of ECM production

Fibronectin and collagen secretion were quantified using commercially available Human Fibronectin (FN) ELISA Kit (RayBiotech, product # ELH-FN1-1) and Human Pro-Collagen I alpha 1 (COL1A1) DuoSet ELISA (R&D Systems, product # DY6220-05) following the manufacturer’s instructions. At the end of the 5-day culture period, cell culture media supernatant was collected from the cells for analysis. For the collagen ELISA, an additional control was included, consisting of medium collected from ECM-coated wells in the absence of cells to account for collagen present in the coatings that might interfere with the readings. The samples were diluted 1:10 and 1:20 in assay diluent for COL1A1 and FN, respectively, and added to plates pre-coated with capture antibody, with each condition tested in six replicates. The samples and standards were incubated for 2.5 h. Following a washing step, biotin-labeled detection antibody was added for 1 h of incubation. After washing to remove unbound biotinylated antibodies, HRP-conjugated streptavidin was incubated for 30 min. Finally, the chromogenic substrate, 1-Step Ultra TMB-ELISA Substrate Solution (Fisher Scientific, product # PI34028), was added and incubated for 30 min. The reaction was stopped with sulfuric acid (Fisher Scientific, product # A300-500), and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an absorbance plate reader. All steps of this ELISA protocol were performed at room temperature. The fibronectin concentration in the culture media was calculated using the standard curve and normalized to the cell count for the corresponding well.

Design of experiments (DOE) and predictive modeling

DOE statistical optimization was employed to assess the effects and interactions of key ECM components and soluble factors on cellular phenotypes. A Box-Behnken design with five continuous input variables was generated using Stat-Ease 360 software (Stat-Ease Inc., version 23.1.6). The input variables included three ECM coatings (collagen I, collagen III, and laminin), all at densities ranging from 2–30 µg/cm², and two soluble factors: vitamin D (1–10 µM), and FGF (1–20 ng/mL). Each factor was examined at three equidistant levels (low point, midpoint, high point), resulting in a total of 42 unique experimental conditions (Supplementary Table 1), including two center point replicates to estimate experimental variance.

For each experimental condition, well plates were coated with the appropriate ECM protein concentrations as previously described. CCD-18Co colonic fibroblasts were seeded at a density of 5000 cells/cm² in EMEM containing 2% FBS. After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with 2% serum EMEM supplemented with FGF and vitamin D according to the assigned condition. The culture media were refreshed every other day throughout the experiment for a total of 5 days of treatment. All extraneous variables, such as cell line batch, passage number, and reagents, were meticulously controlled to ensure that the observed effects could be attributed to the factors under investigation. Each well plate included internal control wells for baseline comparison and normalization. The primary response variables measured were α-SMA intensity, fibronectin secretion, and cell viability.

Experimental validation of the DOE model predictions

To validate the DOE model predictions, CCD-18Co fibroblasts were cultured for 5 days under the ECM coatings and soluble factor conditions described in Table 2 for each objective. For the validation experiments of the final optimized conditions, culture wells were coated with 27.3 μg/cm² collagen I, 2 μg/cm² collagen III, and 30 μg/cm² laminin according to the optimized protocol excluding proliferation (Opt.all (-prolif), Table 2). CCD‑18Co colonic fibroblasts, as well as primary female and male fibroblasts (passage 2-4), were seeded on these wells at 5000 cells/cm² in EMEM supplemented with 2% FBS. After plating, the cells were allowed to attach for 24 h, and the medium was replaced with EMEM + 2% FBS containing 5.3 μM vitamin D and 20 ng/mL FGF per the optimized protocol. The culture media were refreshed every other day, and the treatments were maintained for 5 days. The negative control group was cultured in EMEM + 2% FBS without ECM coatings or vitamin D/FGF supplementation. Treatment with TGF‑β1 (10 ng/mL) was used as a positive control.

Analysis of gene expression via qRT-PCR

Gene expression levels were assessed via qRT-PCR after growing the cells for 5 days with the optimized quiescence protocol. Total RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, product # 74106), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The isolated RNA was then reverse transcribed into cDNA with the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, product # 4374967). qRT-PCR was subsequently conducted using TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Applied Biosystems, product # 4369016) targeting fibroblast activation markers—including α-SMA (ACTA2), Myosin Heavy Chain 11 (MYH11), Myosin Light Chain Kinase (MYLK), Amine Oxidase Copper Containing 3 (AOC3), Short Stature Homeobox 2 (SHOX2), and NK2 homeobox 3 (NKX2-3) as well as the ECM proteins collagen Type I (COL1A1) and collagen Type III (COL3A1) and fibronectin (FN1). Gene expression was quantified using the comparative Ct (ΔΔCt) method, normalized to the endogenous control (GAPDH), and expressed relative to the untreated control condition.

Evaluation of cell morphology

To quantify cell morphology, fixed and permeabilized cells were stained with HCS CellMaskTM green (Invitrogen, product # H32714) and Texas Red® Phalloidin (Biotium, product # 50-196-4061) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A 10 mg/mL stock solution was prepared by dissolving 250 µg of stain in 25 µL of Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO; supplemented in the HCS Cellmask stain). The 1× working CellMask solution was prepared, supplemented with phalloidin (1:1000 dilution), and applied to the cells for 30 min at room temperature, protected from light. The wells were then washed three times with PBS, and cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000 in PBS) for 5 min, followed by additional PBS washes. Cytoplasmic and nuclear fluorescence was subsequently imaged using a Keyence BZ-X800 fluorescence microscope. Cell area and aspect ratio were automatically quantified in Fiji (ImageJ 1.54p). Images were converted to 8-bit and segmented using Otsu’s threshold and binarization. Measurement settings for Area and Fit Ellipse were enabled, and cells were analyzed via “Analyze Particles.” Batch macro processing and export facilitated quantification of 50 cells per condition across 4 replicates. The aspect ratio was calculated as the major axis divided by the minor axis for each cell.

Statistical analysis

For all experiments outside of the DOE model, statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. For each condition, experiments included 3–6 technical replicates. Differences between groups were assessed using one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s or Bonferroni’s post hoc tests for multiple comparisons, and p values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
