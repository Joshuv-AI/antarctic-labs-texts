Optimizing Markers for Chronic Lymphocytic Leukemia Diagnosis by Flow Cytometry: Results From the Nationwide Thai Lymphoma Study Group
Authors: Ornnicha Sathitakorn, Sirorat Kobbuaklee, Chantiya Chanswangphuwana, Thanawat Rattanathammethee, Nisa Makruasi, Anothai Chintabanyat, Suporn Chuncharunee, Pisa Phiphitaporn, Archrob Khuhapinant, Peerapon Wong, Jakrawadee Julamanee, Chayapa Thookhamme, Lalita Norasetthada, Udomsak Bunworasate, Kitsada Wudhikarn, Tanin Intragumtornchai, Chantana Polprasert
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAccurate diagnosis of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is crucial for effective management. Although recent advancements in flow cytometry have significantly improved diagnostic accuracy, distinguishing atypical cases of CLL remains challenging. This study aimed to identify specific markers to enhance CLL diagnosis and prognostic assessment.MethodsBlood or bone marrow from patients presenting with persistent lymphocytosis (> 5 × 103/μL) was collected. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a customized CLL panel to evaluate the expression of CD markers for the diagnosis of CLL. Clinical and laboratory data were reviewed to support the CLL diagnosis.ResultsA total of 228 patients were included in the study, including 206 patients (90.4%) in the CLL group and 22 patients (9.6%) in the non‐CLL group. The use of CD5, CD19, and CD200 in combination for diagnosing CLL demonstrated a sensitivity of 82.5% (95% CI, 76.6–87.4) and a specificity of 90.9% (95% CI, 70.8–98.9). In the final predictive model, which incorporated five markers (CD5, CD19, CD200, CD31, and CD11c), both specificity and positive predictive value for CLL diagnosis were close to 100% (95% CI, 84.6–100 and 95.5–100, respectively). CD38 was identified as an independent predictor of increased relapse risk, while LAIR expression was associated with a reduced relapse rate.ConclusionExpression of CD5, CD19, and CD200 by flow cytometry demonstrated a highly sensitive and accurate diagnostic utility for CLL. Adding both CD31 and CD11c to the predictive model further improved diagnostic specificity, reaching as high as 100%. The identification of relapse‐associated markers provides valuable insights for personalized treatment strategies.Keywords: atypical CLL, CD markers, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, flow cytometry, lymphoproliferative disorders

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia/small lymphocytic lymphoma (CLL/SLL) is the most common lymphoid malignancy in North America and Europe, while the incidence is much lower among individuals of African or Asian descent [1]. Accurate diagnosis and differentiation of CLL from other B‐cell lymphoid malignancies are essential for informing effective treatment strategies [2, 3]. Recent advancements in flow cytometry technology have improved the precision of CLL diagnosis [4]. Essential diagnostic criteria for CLL according to the World Health Organization Classification of Hematolymphoid Tumors (WHO) 2022 rely on the morphology, histopathology/immunohistochemistry, and flow cytometry (on peripheral blood and/or bone marrow aspirate samples) showing an expression of CD19, CD5, CD20, CD23 (variable), and a weakly expressed monotypic light chain [5]. Additional criteria include flow cytometry positivity for CD200, ROR1, CD43 with an absence of FMC7, CD79b (can be weakly positive), CD10, and CD81 expression [5]. Atypical immunophenotypes (e.g., CD5‐, CD23‐, FMC7+, CD79b+ or strongly expressed surface immunoglobulin) are also recognized [6, 7, 8]. The WHO 2022 classification outlines diagnostic criteria for CLL; however, cases with ambiguous marker expression can pose challenges in distinguishing CLL from atypical CLL and other lymphoproliferative disorders [2].

A scoring system for CLL diagnosis was initially proposed by Matutes et al. [9] based on the flow cytometric evaluation of five markers: CD5+, CD23+, FMC7‐, weak expression of surface immunoglobulin (SmIg), and CD22, in which scores of 4–5 supported the diagnosis of typical CLL. This scoring system was later improved by Moreau et al. [10] replacing CD22 with CD79b, enhancing diagnostic accuracy from 91.8% to 96.8%. Despite these scores, challenges persist in differentiating atypical CLL from select cases of mantle cell lymphoma (MCL). Recent studies suggest that CD200 may be a valuable marker in distinguishing CLL from MCL and other B‐cell neoplasms [11, 12]. D'Arena et al. [13] proposed a modified Matutes CLL scoring system, substituting CD79b and FMC7 with CD200, requiring only four markers (weakly positive SmIg, CD200+, CD23+, and CD5+). However, misdiagnoses can still occur in real‐world clinical practice [3]. Currently, there is no consensus on how to integrate additional markers into diagnostic algorithms [14, 15]. In addition to its well‐established role in the diagnosis of CLL, flow cytometry also provides valuable prognostic information. Several flow cytometric parameters, including leukocyte‐associated immunoglobulin‐like receptor‐1 (LAIR‐1), CD200, CD11c, and CD38, have been identified as potential diagnostic and prognostic markers in CLL [4, 16]. Evaluation of these markers may aid in risk stratification and could complement existing clinicopathologic and molecular prognostic tools, such as the CLL‐International Prognostic Index (CLL‐IPI), to refine patient classification and management strategies.

In this study, we aim to identify flow cytometry markers that enhance diagnostic accuracy in CLL, particularly in cases presenting with atypical immunophenotypes. Furthermore, we investigate the association between antigen expression and clinical outcomes, with the goal of improving risk stratification and enabling a more precise assessment of disease progression.

Methods

Study Design

This prospective study enrolled adult patients presenting with persistent lymphocytosis (> 5 × 103/μL) for at least 3 months without other responsible causes. Recruitment was conducted across 12 centers under the Thai Lymphoma Study Group (TLSG) in Thailand between May 2018 and October 2023. The study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University and King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital, Thailand (IRB number 112/65), which was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patient Selection and Sample Size

The enrollment criteria included individuals aged ≥ 18 years suspected for CLL by the presence of an absolute lymphocytosis (> 5 × 103/μL), clinical characteristics, and morphology. The study sample comprised all eligible cases enrolled within the framework of the Thai Lymphoma Study Group (TLSG). Morphological, immunophenotypic, cytogenetic, and molecular analyses, along with clinical data, were collected to establish integrated diagnoses according to the WHO classification criteria [17]. The Matutes score [9] was calculated with a score of 3 or higher classified as CLL. Cases that initially met the diagnostic criteria but could not be definitively classified as CLL were categorized as non‐CLL lymphoproliferative disorders (LPD).

Sample Processing

Peripheral blood (PB) or bone marrow samples from CLL patients were collected in EDTA tubes from 12 participating centers and transported under controlled temperature conditions (4°C) to the central flow cytometry laboratory at King Chulalongkorn Memorial Hospital (KCMH), where they were processed and analyzed within 12 h of collection. A 100‐μL aliquot of PB or bone marrow was incubated in the dark with 5 μL of monoclonal antibodies for 10 min. Following incubation, samples were treated with BD FACS Lysing Solution (BD BioSciences, Heidelberg, Germany) to lyse RBC. The samples were then centrifuged, decanted, and washed twice with FACSFlow (Becton Dickinson) before analysis. All analyses were conducted at KCMH using a BD LSRFortessa flow cytometer (BD BioSciences, Heidelberg, Germany) [18]. Total 8‐color, 11‐parameter flow cytometric assay included fluorescently labeled antibodies as listed in Table S1.

Flow cytometry analysis was performed using a standardized gating strategy. First, lymphocytes were gated based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) characteristics. B cells were identified primarily by CD19 positivity, but in rare CD19‐negative cases, B‐cell lineage was confirmed using CD20 and light chain restriction. Marker expression (CD5, CD200, CD11c, CD31, CD38, LAIR‐1) was assessed within monotypic B‐cell populations.

Analysis was conducted with Kaluza Analysis Software (Beckman Coulter). The lymphocyte gate (forward vs. side light scatter) was employed, and normal T lymphocytes were used as internal controls. A dichotomous assessment of marker expression was required (i.e., positive vs. negative) using an expression cutoff of 20% of the monotypic B cell population as recommended [15, 19]. Flow cytometry data were independently reviewed by two hematopathologists to ensure consistency in gating strategies and interpretation. In cases of discrepancy, the differing assessments were jointly reviewed with a third hematopathologist to reach a final consensus.

Objectives and Outcomes

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate an appropriate marker panel for the diagnosis of CLL by flow cytometry. The secondary objective was to evaluate the association between antigen expression and prognosis in CLL. Collected data included patient demographics, laboratory results (e.g., complete blood count, lactate dehydrogenase, beta‐2 microglobulin, uric acid), Rai staging, treatment details, relapse rates, time to relapse, mortality rates, and time to mortality.

Statistical Analysis

Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR).

To assess the diagnostic performance of predictive markers, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) were calculated. The odds ratio (OR) was calculated by logistic regression. AUC analysis was used to evaluate the discriminatory ability of predictive models, and its predictive accuracy was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The Kaplan–Meier with log‐rank comparison was calculated for progression‐free survival and survival rate. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression were utilized to identify predictive antigen expressions for prognosis in CLL patients. Multivariable included covariates with p < 0.1 from univariable, using stepwise to select the final model. All p values reported are two‐sided. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Stata version 18 (Stata Corp., College Station, TX) was used for analysis.

Results

Baseline Clinical Characteristics and Immunophenotypic Data

A total of 228 patients were included in the study, with 206 patients (90.4%) in the CLL group and 22 patients (9.6%) in the non‐CLL LPD group. The non‐CLL LPD group exhibited a diverse spectrum of subtypes, including Follicular Lymphoma (FL) in 36.4%, Marginal Zone Lymphoma (MZL) in 27.3%, Mantle Cell Lymphoma (MCL) in 27.3%, Diffuse Large B‐cell Lymphoma (DLBCL) in 4.5%, and Lymphoplasmacytic Lymphoma (LPL) in 4.5% of cases. In the CLL group, the median age of participants was 65 years (interquartile range [IQR], 58–73 years), and 65.5% were male. The median white blood cell (WBC) count was 28.9 × 103/μL (IQR, 17.1–53.4 × 103/μL), and the median absolute lymphocyte count was 20.9 × 103/μL (IQR, 11.8–44.1 × 103/μL). Most patients were classified as Rai stage 0 (25%) or stage I (27.5%). A total of 83 patients (40.3%) required therapy at diagnosis. During the study period, 89 patients (43.2%) remained untreated, 68 (33.0%) received one line of therapy, 27 (13.1%) received two lines, and 22 (10.7%) received three or more lines. The proportion of relapse and mortality rates in this cohort were 26.2% and 13.1%, respectively. The 5‐year progression‐free survival (PFS) in this cohort was 74.8% (95% CI, 66.6–81.3), with the corresponding Kaplan–Meier curve illustrating PFS by Rai staging shown in Figure S1A. The 5‐year overall survival (OS) was 88.4% (95% CI, 82.2–92.5), with the graph depicting OS by Rai staging presented in Figure S1B. The median follow‐up duration was 3.9 years (IQR, 2.2–4.9 years). Model Development of Flow Cytometric Panel for CLL Diagnosis

We combined markers with strong diagnostic performance, including CD5 and CD200 from the modified Matutes CLL scoring system [13], both of which showed high PPVs in our study, along with CD19, which had a PPV of 93.6%, to assess diagnostic accuracy for CLL in statistical models. For Model 1 (CD5, CD19, and CD200), the sensitivity and specificity for CLL diagnosis were 82.5% (95% CI, 76.6–87.4) and 90.9% (95% CI, 70.8–98.9), respectively. This model yielded a PPV of 98.8% (95% CI, 95.9–99.9) and a NPV of 35.7% (95% CI, 23.4–49.6). The AUC was 0.87 (95% CI, 0.80–0.93), with an odds ratio of 47.22 (95% CI, 10.56–211), p < 0.01. Given the high PPVs of CD31 and CD11c (92.1% each), we incorporated these markers into the models. When CD31 expression was added to Model 1, forming Model 2 (CD5, CD19, CD200, and CD31), the specificity increased to 95.5% (95% CI, 77.2–99.9), with a PPV of 99.2% (95% CI, 95.4–100) and an odds ratio of 27 (95% CI, 3.64–209), p < 0.01. Similarly, in Model 3, which added CD11c to the original three Model 1 predictors (CD5, CD19, CD200), results also showed improved specificity to 95.5% (95% CI, 77.2–99.9) with a PPV of 98.9% (95% CI, 94.3–100), and an odds ratio of 17.6 (95% CI, 2.33–133). In the final predictive model, Model 4, where all five markers (CD5, CD19, CD200, CD31, and CD11c) were combined, the specificity and PPV approached 100% (95% CI, 84.6–100) and 100% (95% CI, 95.5–100), respectively. Association Between Flow Cytometric Parameters and Clinical Outcomes of Patients With CLL

In the univariate analysis, factors associated with relapse rate were hemoglobin (Hb) < 10 g/dL, WBC ≥ 65 × 103/μL, LDH ≥ 300 IU/L, and certain CD markers (Table 4). In the multivariate analysis, CD38 was associated with an increased relapse rate of CLL (aHR 2.73, 95% CI 1.40–5.32, p = 0.003). Conversely, leukocyte‐associated immunoglobulin‐like receptor (LAIR) was found to be a protective factor against relapse (aHR 0.34, 95% CI 0.17–0.67, p = 0.002). Regarding mortality rate, the univariate analysis identified Hb < 10 g/dL, WBC ≥ 65 × 103/μL, and LDH ≥ 300 IU/L as significant adverse factors. No CD marker was found to be associated with mortality (Table 5). In the multivariate analysis, Hb < 10 g/dL (aHR 6.02, 95% CI 1.42–25.53, p = 0.02) and LDH ≥ 300 IU/L (aHR 3.0, 95% CI 1.29–6.96, p = 0.01) were significantly associated with increased mortality.

Discussion

Our study explored the performance of flow cytometry markers in developing a diagnostic panel for CLL. The combined expression of the key markers CD5, CD19, and CD200 (Model 1) demonstrated high sensitivity with good accuracy and easy reproducibility. These expressions can be considered as backbone markers for any CLL diagnostic panel. Adding either CD31 or CD11c or both further enhanced diagnostic performance (Models 2, 3, 4), which may be useful in CD5‐negative CLL. In our cohort, there were 9 cases of CD5‐negative CLL in which CD31 and/or CD11c helped guide the diagnosis. CD19, although primarily used as a gating marker, was included in our model as a backbone B‐cell marker to ensure consistency, particularly given the presence of a rare CD19‐negative case in our cohort. Since the vast majority of the cases (90.3%) were positive for surface immunoglobulin and this criterion was well established in several studies [5, 9, 10, 13], we did not include this criterion in the model.

Currently, there is no single CD marker that is entirely specific to CLL [20, 21, 22]. Recent data show that CD5 positivity is not limited to CLL and MCL; it is also observed in other small B‐cell lymphomas such as FL, LPL, and splenic marginal zone lymphoma (SMZL) [20]. Additionally, CD200 expression has been reported in cases with CLL, atypical CLL, hairy cell leukemia (HCL), FL, SMZL, and other low‐grade non‐Hodgkin lymphomas (NHL) [21]. Several studies have evaluated the role of CD11c, CD38, CD31, and CD43 expressions in distinguishing between CLL and small B cell NHL [14, 22, 23, 24]. Expression of the cellular adhesion molecule CD11c has been reported in 33.5% to 90% of CLL cases, but has rarely been reported in MCL [8, 25, 26]. Kraus et al. noted that CD11c expression is uncommon in MCL and may assist in differentiating CD5+ B‐cell lymphomas [8]. There is currently no consensus on the optimal CD marker or combination of markers for the diagnosis of CLL. These findings underscore the need for a new combination of markers to enhance the diagnostic specificity of CLL.

CD23‐negative CLL cases are not uncommon [27]. Multiple studies have shown that CD23 negativity can occur in CLL [28, 29]. Evidence suggests that the presence or absence of CD23 in a CD19/CD5 cell population does not reliably distinguish MCL from CLL, as CD23 can also be expressed in MCL and absent in CLL, leading to potential misdiagnosis [21, 30, 31]. Therefore, additional markers beyond CD23 are needed. Our findings highlight the utility of CD5, CD19, and CD200 as valuable markers for CLL diagnosis, even in the absence of CD23.

Antigen expression profiles carry important prognostic implications [3, 32, 33, 34]. In our study, CD38 expression was associated with an increased risk of relapse, whereas LAIR‐1 expression appeared to confer a protective effect. These findings are consistent with previously reported literature, which associates CD38 expression with more aggressive disease and poorer outcomes in CLL [32, 33, 34], and are in line with recent studies that have incorporated CD38 and other flow cytometry markers into prognostic risk models [16]. LAIR, an inhibitor of B‐cell receptor mediated signaling that regulates kinase pathways involved in cell proliferation [35], was shown to be protective in our analysis, corroborating other studies that found LAIR positive status significantly associated with a longer time to first treatment [36]. LAIR negative patients, however, were more likely to present with a worse clinical stage at diagnosis [37]. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating prognostic markers such as CD38 and LAIR into clinical practice, not only to enhance diagnostic precision, but also to guide therapeutic decisions and improve the prediction of patient outcomes. Beyond antigen expression, our study also confirmed the established prognostic significance of elevated LDH and low Hb levels in CLL, both of which were significantly associated with increased mortality. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the independent association of elevated LDH with increased mortality, regardless of clinical stage at diagnosis [38, 39]. Together, these results highlight the importance of integrating prognostic markers such as CD38, LAIR, LDH, and Hb into clinical practice to guide therapeutic decisions, predict relapse, and enhance patient management and outcomes.

The strength of this study lies in its evaluation of flow cytometry markers for the diagnosis and prognosis of CLL within a multicenter cohort. Our proposed model is practical, easily reproducible, and enhanced by the incorporation of supplementary markers such as CD200, CD31, and CD11c, which improve the classification of atypical cases. However, the study has certain limitations, including the small sample size of the non‐CLL LPD subgroup, which may have influenced the accuracy of sensitivity and specificity calculations. The high prevalence of CD23 negativity observed in this study may be attributable to factors such as specimen preparation methods, selection of flow cytometry reagents, or technical variability. Additionally, the absence of correlation between diagnostic immunophenotypes and PFS or OS limits the evaluation of their prognostic significance. Lastly, TP53 mutation and IGHV mutation status were not available in our dataset; therefore, full integration with established prognostic models such as CLL‐IPI and International prognostic score for early‐stage CLL (IPS‐E) could not be performed. As our study identified specific flow cytometry markers associated with prognosis, further research integrating these markers with molecular data may provide additional prognostic value in future studies.

Our study proposed a practical set of customized flow cytometry markers that enhance the diagnosis of CLL by improving both sensitivity and accuracy. Moreover, these markers hold significant prognostic value. The identification of relapse‐associated markers provides critical insights to inform personalized treatment and monitoring strategies.
