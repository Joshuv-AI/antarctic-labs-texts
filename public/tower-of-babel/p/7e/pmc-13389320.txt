Beyond the Valve Itself: Extra-valvular Phenotyping in Mitral Stenosis: A Novel Pathophysiology-driven Echocardiographic Staging System
Authors: Gabriele Cordoni, Dania Mohty, Anastasia Shchendrygina, Domenico Galzerano, Hani Al Sergani, Giovanni Di Salvo, Valeria Pergola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current guideline-based classifications of mitral stenosis rely primarily on parameters ofvalvular narrowing and its associated clinical symptoms. In recent years, novel phenotyping approaches based on the concept of sequential cardiac chamber involvement have been developed for other cardiovascular conditions, including mitral regurgitation, aortic stenosis, and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Similarly, the development of an extra-valvular echocardiographic staging system for mitral stenosis, grounded in the extent of cardiac chamber involvement, appears warranted to improve risk stratification and tailor clinical management.

Mitral stenosis (MS) is characterised by narrowing of the mitral valve orifice, leading to impaired left ventricular (LV) filling, most commonly due to rheumatic heart disease or age-related degenerative calcific involvement of the mitral annulus.1 Rheumatic MS typically results from autoimmune scarring after rheumatic fever, leading to commissural fusion and leaflet thickening, often in younger patients and endemic in developing countries. In contrast, degenerative MS arises in older populations with mitral annular calcification (MAC) and comorbidities (e.g. hypertension, chronic kidney disease), with leaflets immobilised by calcium deposition rather than fusion. Despite different aetiologies, both forms of MS share a pathophysiology of chronically elevated left atrial (LA) pressure and subsequent multi-chamber cardiac involvement.1

Pathophysiology

As MS progresses, the increase in LA pressure leads to atrial dilation and functional impairment, frequently precipitating AF and increasing thromboembolic risk. Elevated LA pressure is transmitted retrograde to the pulmonary circulation, resulting in pulmonary venous hypertension and reactive pulmonary vasoconstriction. Over time, this process may evolve into pulmonary hypertension (PH) with associated pulmonary vascular remodelling. Chronic PH imposes a sustained pressure overload on the right ventricle (RV), often leading to RV hypertrophy, dilation and systolic dysfunction, together with functional tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and right atrial (RA) enlargement in advanced stages. This sequential ‘domino’ involvement of cardiac chambers contributes to progressive heart failure symptoms and adverse clinical outcomes if left unaddressed.2,3

PH already represents a key determinant in the management of patients with mitral regurgitation according to current guideline recommendations, and accumulating evidence supports the adoption of a similar paradigm in the setting of MS.4–6

In patients with severe rheumatic MS, Choi et al. demonstrated that echocardiographic estimation of pulmonary artery systolic pressure is a strong predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes, particularly at values exceeding 45 mmHg.2 Meneveau et al. further showed that residual mean pulmonary artery pressure after percutaneous mitral commissurotomy, as well as post-procedural mean mitral valve gradient, independently predict long-term adverse outcomes.7

Likewise, the development of RV dysfunction reflects advanced haemodynamic compromise and is consistently associated with increased mortality in left-sided valvular heart disease.3,8,9

Although transthoracic echocardiography represents the cornerstone for the non-invasive evaluation of pulmonary pressures, it may provide limited accuracy in a non-negligible proportion of patients. Invasive haemodynamic assessment by right heart catheterisation becomes particularly relevant when Doppler estimates are inconclusive or discordant with the clinical presentation, and it is essential to differentiate isolated post-capillary PH from combined pre- and post-capillary disease, a distinction that may reflect the chronicity and severity of mitral valve obstruction.

Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) has emerged as a key parameter in this context. A PVR threshold >2 Wood units is currently considered indicative of a pre-capillary component, and large contemporary studies in patients with left-sided heart disease have consistently shown that elevated PVR independently predicts mortality and heart failure hospitalisation, likely mirroring irreversible structural remodelling of the pulmonary vasculature.10,11

By contrast, data on the prevalence and prognostic significance of LV involvement in MS remain limited. LV involvement has been described in MS both in terms of diastolic and systolic dysfunction.12 Some studies have demonstrated that patients with mild to moderate MS exhibit reduced global longitudinal, circumferential, and radial strain compared with healthy controls, despite preserved left ventricular ejection fraction.13,14

In patients with LV diastolic dysfunction, symptoms of dyspnoea may be disproportionate to the anatomical severity of MS, and these patients often experience persistent symptoms and worse clinical outcomes after mitral valvotomy or valve replacement.15,16

Regarding systolic dysfunction, available evidence suggests that it is not solely attributable to impaired diastolic filling. Instead, LV systolic dysfunction appears to be influenced by factors beyond valvular narrowing, including abnormalities affecting LV filling, increased afterload, or superimposed cardiovascular conditions that reduce myocardial contractility.17,18 Notably, reduced LV systolic function does not appear to adversely affect peri-operative outcomes or early symptomatic improvement in patients undergoing intervention for MS.17

Current Classification

Current clinical classification of MS severity is primarily based on valvular anatomy – namely mitral valve area (MVA), given that transvalvular gradients are highly dependent on flow conditions and heart rate – and on symptom status, as reflected in the 2020 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Guidelines, which categorise MS into stage A (at risk), stage B (progressive), stage C (asymptomatic severe) and stage D (symptomatic severe).5 This staging system, similarly to that proposed in the European Society of Cardiology Guidelines, is fundamental for guiding the timing of intervention; although this approach takes into account the haemodynamic consequences of MS, it does not classify the degree of secondary cardiac involvement.6 Consequently, two patients with comparable MVA may have markedly different prognoses depending on whether MS has resulted in PH or RV failure. We hypothesise that the extent of cardiac involvement, as assessed by comprehensive echocardiography, provides incremental prognostic information beyond conventional measures of MS severity. Accordingly, there is a compelling need for a more nuanced staging system that incorporates the downstream cardiac consequences of MS.

A Shift of Paradigm

In recent years, a new paradigm for staging valvular heart disease based on the concept of extra-valvular sequential cardiac damage has emerged. In 2017, Généreux et al. first proposed a staging system for aortic stenosis (AS) grounded in the stepwise involvement of cardiac chambers; stage 0 denoted no extra-valvular damage, stage 1 LV damage, stage 2 LA or mitral valve damage, stage 3 PH or TR, and stage 4 RV failure.9 This AS staging system demonstrated progressively worse survival with increasing stages of cardiac damage. These findings were further supported in 2025, when Myagmardorj et al. reported, in a cohort of 734 patients with severe AS, that both the stage of cardiac damage assessed 6 months after transcatheter aortic valve implantation and subsequent worsening in staging were independently associated with 2-year all-cause mortality.19

The concept has since been extended to mitral valve disease. In 2022, van Wijngaarden et al. demonstrated that classifying patients with primary mitral regurgitation (MR) according to the extent of cardiac chamber involvement effectively stratified long-term mortality risk, with each incremental stage conferring an approximately 17% higher risk of all-cause mortality.8 Notably, a subsequent study conducted in patients with severe MAC and mitral valve dysfunction – defined as at least moderate MR and/or MS with MVA <2.0 cm² – showed that a similar echocardiography-based extra-valvular staging system provided meaningful prognostic stratification, as higher stages of extra-mitral involvement were associated with significantly increased mortality and heart failure hospitalisation rates.8 Importantly, subgroup analyses revealed that the prognostic implications did not differ significantly between MS-predominant and MR-predominant phenotypes.

Additionally, a recent work evaluated 92 patients with rheumatic MS (MVA <2.0 cm²) and stratified them into four groups based on the extent of extra-valvular cardiac damage. The authors observed that MVA was smaller in patients within the most advanced stage compared with those in lower stages and that, although not reaching statistical significance, a higher incidence of adverse events occurred in patients with a greater burden of extra-valvular involvement. These findings suggest that progression of MS severity is accompanied by increasing extra-valvular repercussions and a rising risk of adverse clinical events, despite the inherent limitations related to the small sample size.20

Collectively, these studies underscore that incorporating structural and functional cardiac changes beyond the diseased valve can refine risk stratification and support more informed clinical decision-making. However, downstream extra-valvular damage in degenerative MS deserves specific consideration. MAC typically occurs in elderly patients and frequently coexists with a high burden of cardiovascular comorbidities, including systemic hypertension, AS, coronary artery disease, heart failure, chronic kidney disease and AF.21 These conditions may independently affect cardiac structure and function at multiple levels, thereby making it particularly challenging to disentangle alterations directly attributable to mitral valve obstruction from those related to parallel disease processes.22

Consistently, in a large cohort of patients with MAC – among whom hypertension was present in 74%, coronary artery disease in 58%, and heart failure in 52% – both short- and long-term mortality were more strongly driven by age and associated comorbidities than by the severity of the valvular lesion itself, which remained a weaker, albeit significant, determinant of outcome.23

In parallel, heart failure with preserved ejection fraction – a condition that may coexist with degenerative MS and shares overlapping pathophysiological features – has recently been approached using a similar conceptual framework.24 In 2024, Shchendrygina et al. proposed a staging scheme for heart failure with preserved ejection fraction based on the sequential involvement of cardiac chambers assessed by transthoracic echocardiography. This approach demonstrated a strong prognostic correlation, with each additional chamber affected associated with a 50–89% increase in the risk of adverse outcomes.25 Analogously, the pathophysiology of MS reflects a stepwise multisystem process, beginning with LA remodelling, followed by pulmonary vascular involvement, and ultimately progressing to right heart failure.

As discussed above, the aetiological heterogeneity underlying degenerative MS modifies the interpretation of any staging framework intended to capture the prognostic impact of the valvular lesion through its downstream consequences. In this setting, extra-valvular abnormalities may not solely represent the haemodynamic footprint of mitral obstruction but rather the integrated effects of multimorbidity and age-related cardiovascular remodelling.

Unlike previously proposed classifications, we incorporated LA strain as a criterion of LA myopathy. Growing evidence supports the use of 2D speckle-tracking echocardiography for the early detection of LA dysfunction in MS, thereby enhancing risk assessment. Indeed, reductions in LA strain have been associated with MS severity, worsening functional status, impaired atrial compliance, development of AF, and heart failure.26

In 2024, Figueiredo et al. demonstrated, in a cohort of 493 patients with rheumatic MS evaluated by 2D speckle-tracking echocardiography, that LA reservoir strain (LASr) was the strongest predictor of new-onset AF, with an optimal threshold of 17.9%, and also a key predictor of adverse clinical outcomes at a threshold of 12.8%.27 Concurrently, Chan et al. showed that an LASr cut-off <15.8% was independently associated with a composite endpoint of all-cause mortality, heart failure hospitalisation, and valve intervention in a cohort of 237 patients with moderate or severe MS, based on multivariable Cox regression analysis.28 Other LA parameters, including LA volume index and conduit and pump strain, were also assessed but did not show a significant association with the study endpoint.

The LASr values identified in the context of MS appear consistent with reference thresholds currently proposed for the assessment of LV diastolic dysfunction.29

In contrast, an additional stage comprising patients with MS and isolated LV remodelling was not included in the proposed staging system. In the previously mentioned study of 953 patients with MAC, no significant prognostic differences were observed between the first two stages of extra-valvular disease – namely, no cardiac damage and isolated LV remodelling – with both groups demonstrating the most favourable survival, irrespective of whether the predominant phenotype was stenotic or regurgitant.3

Moreover, despite the scarcity of available data, evidence suggests that the assessment of LV systolic and diastolic function may warrant separate consideration, as it appears to reflect heterogeneous mechanisms often unrelated to the severity of valvular obstruction itself. Patients with elevated LV filling pressures may develop a mismatch between symptom burden and MVA, frequently in the presence of concomitant cardiac conditions, and therefore, may not derive substantial clinical benefit from valve-correcting interventions.

Some studies have shown that RV global and free-wall longitudinal strain values tend to be impaired in patients with MS; additionally, these parameters seem to improve after balloon mitral commissurotomy. However, small sample sizes, lack of standardised acquisition protocols, absence of validated cut-off values and limited prognostic data currently restrict the applicability of RV strain–derived parameters and preclude their incorporation into the proposed classification.30–32

Conclusion

The proposed classification has the potential to enhance the prognostic relevance of current MS staging methods, thereby supporting a more individualised approach to clinical management. This review is intended to provide a conceptual framework for extra-valvular phenotyping in MS; however, formal prognostic validation is required. Specifically, it is essential to determine whether, and to what extent, echocardiographic extra-valvular cardiac involvement confers incremental prognostic value beyond valvular severity alone in patients with isolated MS. Further, it remains to be established whether patients with more advanced chamber involvement experience worse clinical outcomes and whether, at advanced stages, interventions such as percutaneous mitral commissurotomy or valve replacement may become ineffective or even detrimental. Finally, clarification is needed regarding potential differences in chamber remodelling patterns between degenerative and rheumatic MS, and whether the proposed staging system carries comparable prognostic implications across these populations.
