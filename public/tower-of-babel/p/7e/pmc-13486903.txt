A call for action: Closing the evidence gap in management of stimulant‐induced psychosis
Authors: Anne Bouthillier, Heidar Sharafi, Adam Bisaga, Didier Jutras‐Aswad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStimulant‐induced psychosis (StIP) is emerging as one of most pressing health challenges. Over the past two decades, stimulant‐related harms and psychiatric care demands have risen sharply. Individuals with StIP often present with severe agitation and high suicide risk, and up to half progress to chronic psychotic illness within two years. Despite this burden, clinical recommendations remain sparse and largely based on consensus rather than evidence. This article aims to critically appraise current conceptual and therapeutic approaches to StIP, highlight evidence gaps, and outline priorities for research and clinical practice.ArgumentWe propose a re‐evaluation of StIP nosology, shifting away from DSM‐5 distinctions that require symptom presence beyond abstinence and often fail in real‐world settings. Management might require stage‐specific approaches— acute (safety and short‐term antipsychotics), subacute (stabilization and relapse prevention), and long‐term (management of recurrent or persistent psychosis)— yet no validated staging models exist. Antipsychotic prescribing remains guided by expert opinion, with unresolved questions around choice of agent, treatment duration, and relapse prevention strategies. Evidence for psychosocial and behavioral interventions is very limited. Individualized care, supported by predictive tools, biomarkers, and longitudinal risk stratification, is essential to identify individuals at highest risk of progression and chronicity while minimizing unnecessary long‐term pharmacotherapy.ConclusionsStimulant‐induced psychosis (StIP) exemplifies a widening evidence‐practice gap. Addressing this requires a coordinated research agenda to address nosology and phenomenology, validate diagnostic algorithms, develop stage‐specific management models, and test integrated pharmacological and behavioral strategies. Without such efforts, outcomes will remain poor, and StIP will continue to represent one of psychiatry's most consequential yet neglected syndromes.

INTRODUCTION

Over the past two decades, stimulant‐related harms have been escalating at an unprecedented pace, straining healthcare systems and reshaping psychiatric care demands. For instance, in the USA, national survey data demonstrated a 43% increase in past‐year methamphetamine use from 2015 to 2019 (from 1.4 to 2.0 million people) [1]. Between 2013 and 2016, the annual number of methamphetamine‐related emergency department visits was 245% higher than in 2003, and between 2014 and 2019, psychiatric hospitalization rates involving methamphetamine increased by 68% [2, 3]. These trends reflect not only the increasing prevalence of stimulant use, but also a growing burden of acute, substance‐related psychiatric presentations in emergency and in‐patient settings.

Among stimulant‐related harms, stimulant‐induced psychosis (StIP) stands out as a distinct and consequential clinical entity. It is characterized by acute psychotic symptoms that are temporally related to stimulant use [4]. The timely recognition and management of StIP are critical for two reasons. First, the acute presentation often involves severe behavioral disturbance and heightened risk of harm to self or others, necessitating urgent intervention in emergency or in‐patient settings [5]. Second, StIP is not always transient; longitudinal studies indicate that 25%–50% of cases progress to a chronic psychotic disorder, particularly within the first 2 years after the initial episode, underscoring the need for early identification and sustained follow‐up [6, 7, 8, 9]. Moreover, methamphetamine‐associated psychosis has been linked to the highest rates of suicide attempts and hospital readmissions among psychotic disorders, a poor prognosis largely driven by frequent relapses and comorbid substance use [10, 11].

The severity and prevalence of StIP stand in contrast to the scarcity of tailored clinical recommendations for this condition. The American Society of Addiction Medicine/American Academy of Addiction Psychiatry (ASAM/AAAP) Clinical Practice Guideline on the Management of Stimulant Use Disorder [12] represents a landmark and much‐needed resource in stimulant use disorder (StUD) care. However, its recommendations for managing StIP remain limited owing to a lack of high‐quality evidence, similar to other guidelines that rely primarily on expert consensus rather than research [13, 14]. This article aims to critically appraise current conceptual and therapeutic approaches to StIP, highlight evidence gaps, and outline priorities for research and practice.

DIAGNOSTIC CONSIDERATIONS

Differentiating transient psychotic‐like symptoms that can occur during acute stimulant intoxication, StIP that extends beyond the period of intoxication or withdrawal, and a chronic primary psychotic disorder requires careful consideration of timing, context and clinical evolution. The acute effects of stimulants can precipitate or mimic psychotic symptoms, making it difficult to determine whether these symptoms reflect acute intoxication, persist beyond intoxication but remain substance related, or signify an underlying primary psychotic disorder [4].

According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5), a primary psychotic disorder is considered more likely when symptoms precede substance use, persist for more than 1 month after cessation or when other evidence supports a non‐substance‐induced etiology [4]. However, temporal criteria that rely on a sustained period of abstinence may be difficult to achieve in real‐world clinical settings, particularly among individuals with co‐occurring StUD, where periods of abstinence are often interrupted by recurrent relapse [15, 16, 17].

In practice, distinguishing StIP from the unmasking of a primary psychotic disorder remains one of the greatest diagnostic challenges in psychiatry. StIP trajectories frequently overlap with those of schizophrenia spectrum disorders [6, 7, 8, 9]. Longitudinal data indicate that approximately 30% of individuals with methamphetamine‐induced psychosis later develop a primary psychotic disorder [18, 19], underscoring that stimulant exposure may act as both a direct precipitant and an environmental trigger in those with latent vulnerability. This trajectory is reminiscent of patterns observed with cannabis, where heavy or prolonged use has been shown to precipitate persistent psychotic disorders in predisposed individuals [20, 21]. Some experts have recently proposed a distinct entity, chronic methamphetamine‐induced psychosis, characterized by psychotic symptoms persisting for more than 1 month after the discontinuation of methamphetamine use [22, 23, 24]. Although hypothesized to reflect methamphetamine‐related neurotoxic or neuroinflammatory changes, this clinical phenotype remains indistinguishable from primary psychotic disorders based on symptom profile alone [25]. However, this notion remains controversial: other authors argue that such cases represent primary psychotic disorders that were precipitated or unmasked by stimulant exposure in individuals with underlying vulnerability [22, 26]. Labeling these presentations as purely ‘drug‐induced’ may contribute to diagnostic overshadowing within medical settings and potentially delay appropriate follow‐up and early intervention, a concern consistent with literature documenting disparities in the quality of care experienced by individuals with substance use disorders (SUDs) [27, 28].

The onset and temporal relationship of psychotic symptoms with stimulant use are therefore critical to diagnostic formulation. When prodromal symptoms (such as social withdrawal, cognitive decline or subtle behavioral changes) precede substance use, an underlying primary psychotic disorder is strongly suspected [29]. However, these same prodromal symptoms can also drive the initiation of stimulant use as a form of self‐medication. Notably, recent studies indicate that up to 14% of individuals presenting with a first‐episode psychosis report concurrent stimulant use [30], further blurring the diagnostic boundary at initial presentation.

Major clinical guidelines converge on the importance of systematically screening all psychosis presentations for SUDs [31, 32, 33, 34]. In the UK, the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) emphasizes the need for a detailed substance use history while discouraging routine biological testing because of its potential to work against a collaborative approach. Conversely, the ASAM/AAAP guideline recommends toxicology testing when it is likely to influence clinical management [12, 33]. Despite consensus on systematic screening, no standardized algorithms exist to help clinicians interpret and integrate results into diagnostic decision‐making, for instance, in distinguishing substance‐induced from primary psychotic disorders.

Several comparative studies have attempted to delineate clinical differences between primary psychotic disorders and StIP. Individuals who ultimately develop a primary psychotic disorder tend to exhibit more pronounced thought disorder, negative symptoms and cognitive deficits, compared with those with methamphetamine‐induced psychosis [18, 35]. Yet, these differences are subtle and often unreliable diagnostically at first contact, which typically occurs in the context of a crisis—an environment not conducive to detailed evaluation—thereby reinforcing the need for longitudinal assessment and integrated follow‐up. Without diagnostic clarity, developing evidence‐based, stimulant‐specific management guidelines is inherently challenging, as treatment decisions depend on an accurate diagnosis.

These challenges call for the development of structured diagnostic algorithms to assist clinicians in diagnostic clarification and the stage‐specific management of StIP.

CLINICAL PRESENTATION

Early observations demonstrated a clear causal link between amphetamine use and the onset of acute psychosis. Experimental studies showed that escalating doses of amphetamine could induce psychotic symptoms even in healthy individuals, which generally resolved within several days of discontinuation [36].

The presentation of StIP mirrors that of acute schizophrenia spectrum psychosis, including persecutory delusions, disorganized thoughts, increased psychomotor activity, decreased concentration, anxiety, suspicion, poor insight and auditory hallucinations [37, 38, 39]. A characteristic feature of StIP is formication—a tactile hallucination of insects or vermin crawling on or under the skin—which can lead to extensive excoriations and secondary skin lesions resulting from scratching [40]. Some studies have also reported that StIP is more frequently associated with grandiose delusions and visual hallucinations compared with primary psychotic disorders [41].

CLINICAL COURSE AND STAGE‐SPECIFIC MANAGEMENT

The clinical course of StIP is highly variable, ranging from brief, self‐limited episodes to chronic and relapsing forms that are clinically indistinguishable from primary psychotic disorders. Effective StIP care requires a staged approach that enables individualized support aligned with the phases of clinical syndromes and evolving patient needs. Most existing guidelines on psychosis with co‐occurring SUD (e.g. Australian and Canadian guidelines; NICE; Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network, SIGN; Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, SAMHSA) provide only general recommendations that are not stimulant specific [12, 32, 34, 42, 43]. The ASAM/AAAP Clinical Practice Guideline on the Management of Stimulant Use Disorder and the SAMHSA Treatment for Stimulant Use Disorders offer slightly more specific direction for StIP and will be referenced here [12, 34]. With a lack of controlled trial data, even these documents remain broad and lack detailed, evidence‐based guidance on how to adapt and personalize treatment intensity or duration across clinical stages.

We propose a pragmatic, conceptual framework dividing the course of StIP into three phases: an acute phase, characterized by prominent agitation, disorganization and psychosis temporally linked to stimulant use; a subacute phase, marked by a partial remission of psychotic symptoms but persisting vulnerability and relapse risk; and a long‐term phase, centered on relapse prevention and the management of chronic or recurrent psychosis that may become clinically indistinguishable from primary psychotic disorders. In the acute phase, the priority is to ensure safety and reduce agitation through environmental containment, supportive care and short‐term pharmacologic management [12]. The ASAM/AAAP guideline emphasizes that physical restraints should be avoided unless absolutely necessary for safety. Hospitalization is often required at this stage for stabilization, diagnostic clarification and antipsychotic initiation if psychotic symptoms persist. The subacute phase is notably under‐represented in existing guidelines. The guidance is limited to the suggestion that antipsychotic medication may be tapered once symptoms resolve, as discussed in the next section. This phase, however, represents an opportunity for relapse‐prevention interventions and re‐engagement in StUD care. The long‐term phase addresses recurrent or persistent psychosis and should include integrated care for StUD and co‐occurring psychiatric conditions. Treating comorbid conditions such as depression, anxiety and eating disorders is essential, in coordination with StUD care, as they are frequent triggers for relapse and psychiatric destabilization.

Despite the practicality of this phased model, its implementation remains largely theoretical. Current guidelines provide no recommendations on the optimal duration of follow‐up after the episode, frequency of clinical reassessment, or criteria for tapering pharmacologic or psychosocial interventions. Similarly, there are no empirically derived thresholds for transitioning patients from specialized in‐patient or crisis care to community‐based or addiction‐focused services, or guidance for re‐integrating care when psychotic or substance use symptoms recur. The timing and intensity of multidisciplinary involvement (e.g. addiction medicine, psychiatry, psychology and social services) are left entirely to clinical discretion.

These gaps leave clinicians navigating complex trajectories without structured algorithms for treatment sequencing or continuity of care. This underscores the urgent need for systematic longitudinal studies to define evidence‐based staging parameters, follow‐up intervals and coordinated pathways of care across the phases of StIP.

ANTIPSYCHOTIC DILEMMAS

Despite their central role in the acute management of StIP, antipsychotic prescribing remains guided more by clinical convention than by empirical evidence. ASAM/AAAP recommends initiating antipsychotics during acute episodes, with tapering after the remission of psychotic symptoms, but offers no data‐driven guidance on treatment duration or management of patients with ongoing stimulant use, with the possible aim of preventing further episodes [12]. This recommendation is based on expert opinion driven by concerns about adverse effects, rather than on empirical evidence assessing the balance of effectiveness and relapse prevention versus over‐treatment. To date, no studies have specifically examined the long‐term risk–benefit profile of antipsychotic maintenance in individuals with a single stimulant‐induced psychotic episode versus those with recurrent or persistent StIP.

In clinical practice, long‐acting injectable (LAI) antipsychotics are occasionally used pragmatically in individuals with recurrent StIP, poor adherence or co‐occurring StUD, for whom treatment continuity is often challenging. However, this approach is largely extrapolated from schizophrenia care rather than supported by stimulant‐specific evidence. The potential advantages, such as improved adherence, relapse prevention and reduction of emergency presentations, must be weighed against risks of over‐treatment in those who might otherwise achieve full and sustained remission after stimulant cessation. Controlled studies are needed to determine whether LAI antipsychotics confer distinct benefits or harms in this population, and to define which patient subgroups might derive net benefit from long‐term maintenance therapy.

Regarding choice of antipsychotic agent, the ASAM/AAAP guideline defers to clinical judgment, while advising against chlorpromazine and clozapine because of the risk of seizure [12]. The SAMHSA guidance lists several agents as potentially useful— aripiprazole, haloperidol, olanzapine, quetiapine and risperidone—but acknowledges the lack of head‐to‐head stimulant‐specific evidence [34]. The limited research available underscores this gap. A meta‐analysis performed by Srisurapanont et al., including six trials, suggests that olanzapine or quetiapine may be preferred for reducing psychotic symptoms in methamphetamine‐induced psychosis, while aripiprazole appeared inferior to other antipsychotics [44]. No difference in dropout rates was observed between antipsychotics. Another systematic review conducted by Sabioni et al. found that atypical antipsychotics, especially aripiprazole, reduced cocaine use and craving in some studies, although contradictory findings were also reported [45].

Beyond potential differences in efficacy, the safety profile of antipsychotics warrants careful consideration as part of the shared decision‐making with StIP patients. Of note, a theoretical concern discussed in the literature is that antipsychotics may exacerbate anhedonia and blunt reward sensitivity, thereby heightening vulnerability to stimulant craving or relapse. Preliminary studies [46, 47, 48] lend support to this hypothesis, underscoring the need for individualized risk–benefit assessment when considering long‐term antipsychotic therapy in individuals with StIP and StUD. Beyond this, well‐documented safety reports from regulatory agencies have highlighted that partial dopamine agonists such as aripiprazole may lead to impulse‐control disorders, including pathological gambling, compulsive shopping and hypersexuality [49, 50]. Two recent case reports described the emergence of compulsive substance use under aripiprazole treatment in individuals with a history of addiction, resolving after discontinuation [51]. However, a systematic review including 283 patients (160 from controlled trials) found that both oral and LAI aripiprazole were generally well tolerated and associated with clinical improvement and reduced craving in individuals with severe mental illness and co‐occurring SUD [52]. Conversely, a trial in individuals with amphetamine dependence reported significantly higher rates of amphetamine‐positive urine samples with aripiprazole compared with placebo, raising concerns about its use in StUD [53]. Taken together, these findings illustrate the dual potential of aripiprazole and other dopaminergic agents to either mitigate or exacerbate reward dysregulation, underscoring the importance of close monitoring and individualized treatment planning in individuals with StIP.

For StIP‐associated acute agitation, pharmacological guidance on efficacy and safety remains limited. The ASAM/AAAP guideline recommends benzodiazepines as first‐line treatment for agitation related to stimulant intoxication, and antipsychotics for stimulant‐induced psychosis, yet offers no specific recommendations for managing agitation occurring within the context of StIP itself, where both mechanisms may coexist.

Collectively, these gaps underscore the need for rigorous, stimulant‐specific pharmacologic studies to inform safe and effective treatment algorithms for both the acute and maintenance phases of StIP management.

BEHAVIORAL INTERVENTIONS

Most existing guidelines on psychosis with co‐occurring SUD (e.g. Australian, Canadian, NICE, SAMHSA and SIGN guidelines) offer general recommendations that are not stimulant‐specific [12, 32, 34, 42, 43]. They consistently emphasize integrated care, encouraging clinicians to address StUD and co‐occurring psychosis simultaneously through coordinated treatment and ongoing monitoring, as continued stimulant use is a major risk factor for relapse and poor outcomes.

In the acute phase, non‐pharmacological and environmental interventions remain a critical yet understudied component of care. To date, no controlled studies have evaluated the effectiveness of specific environmental or supportive strategies for managing stimulant intoxication or withdrawal, leaving existing recommendations dependent on expert consensus. The ASAM/AAAP guideline advises treatment in a quiet, low‐stimulation environment that promotes rest and safety while avoiding further stimulant exposure or distress [54]. Behavioral management strategies, including verbal and non‐verbal de‐escalation techniques, are recommended as first‐line approaches for agitation and psychosis related to stimulant intoxication. Physical restraints should be avoided unless necessary to ensure safety; while they can temporarily prevent violent behavior, their use increases the risk of injury to patients and staff and may be psychologically traumatic. When restraints are unavoidable, medications should be administered promptly to reduce agitation with close monitoring for hyperthermia and dehydration. The SAMHSA guideline emphasizes structured assessment and de‐escalation frameworks, such as the AGRO+ model, to reduce agitation, foster engagement and restore a sense of safety [34]. Despite these consensus‐based recommendations, there remains no evidence‐based framework for implementing or scaling behavioral strategies across acute, subacute and community settings. Beyond the acute setting, no studies assessed and compared the effectiveness of specific integrated behavioral treatments for StIP, and the most effective type of behavioral intervention remains unknown. Existing studies on integrated care for SUDs and severe mental illness focus on broader populations and are not specific to stimulants or psychotic disorders [55, 56]. It can be reasonably assumed that approaches shown to be effective for StUD (such as contingency management, the community reinforcement approach, cognitive behavioral therapy, CBT, and the Matrix Model) could also benefit individuals with StIP, given the central role of decreasing stimulant use in preventing psychosis relapse [12, 57]. However, this hypothesis has not yet been empirically tested.

A narrative review by Chiang et al. (2019) suggested that certain interventions, such as CBT, mindfulness‐based relapse prevention and exercise‐based therapies, might be effective for methamphetamine use disorder and for psychosis separately, but these approaches have not been studied specifically for StIP [58].

Emerging harm‐reduction strategies are beginning to appear but are not yet addressed in guidelines. Providing take‐home olanzapine to patients who were seen in a psychiatric emergency service for methamphetamine‐induced psychosis was associated with fewer psychiatric emergency visits over a period of 6 months after receipt [59]. Most widely implemented and evidence‐based harm reduction interventions—such as naloxone distribution, sterile equipment programs, opioid overdose education and supervised consumption sites—were originally developed in response to opioid‐related harms and the prevention of blood‐borne infections [60, 61, 62, 63]. Far less attention has been devoted to the specific needs of people who use stimulants, the risks of which include agitation, cardiac complications, psychosis and seizures, and differ substantially from the respiratory depression characteristics of opioid use [5, 64, 65]. Existing interventions, often adapted from opioid‐focused models, fail to address stimulant‐specific risks.

Current evidence leaves major gaps regarding the optimal behavioral and psychosocial approaches for StIP. Moving forward, the field should prioritize the development and testing of phase‐specific psychosocial and behavioral strategies, including scalable, low‐resource adaptations suitable for global health settings. Future work should prioritize developing and evaluating models of care that can be applied not only in emergency and hospital contexts but also in harm‐reduction and community‐based programs, where many individuals with StIP first present. Such models should promote safety, minimize coercive interventions, and facilitate the continuity of psychiatric and substance use care.

TOWARD INDIVIDUALIZED CARE

Despite the growing awareness of the complexity of StIP, clinical approaches remain largely uniform, with limited consideration of individual variability in vulnerability, course and treatment response. A more nuanced understanding of risk profiles is needed to move from reactive, one‐size‐fits‐all care toward personalized, preventive and risk‐adapted strategies.

Several factors have been proposed to explain why only a subset of individuals who use stimulants develop persistent psychosis. A recent systematic review identified frequency of methamphetamine use, severity of dependence and concurrent use of other substances as the most consistent predictors of psychosis onset, whereas socio‐demographic characteristics showed no clear association [66]. Evidence for other risk factors, such as family history of psychotic disorders, psychiatric comorbidity and trauma history, remains inconclusive.

Not all individuals with StIP progress to chronic illness, underscoring the need for stratified risk‐adapted approaches. To date, very few predictors of chronicity after StIP have been identified. StIP appears to convert to schizophrenia spectrum disorders more frequently in males [67]. Furthermore, an early onset of stimulant use, particularly before the age of 20 years, has been linked to a higher likelihood of chronic psychosis, as has a history of brain disease and of learning difficulties, although the latter association may be less robust [68]. Biological studies have identified several candidate genes (n = 7) potentially associated with vulnerability to StIP and with poorer clinical trajectories within this group [69]. Other small‐scale studies have suggested that homelessness and fewer hallucinatory symptoms at baseline may predict transition to schizophrenia, though findings are limited by sample size and methodological variability [70].

Beyond static predictors, dynamic factors such as substance use patterns and environmental stressors play a critical role. Relapse into psychosis is frequently precipitated by stimulant reuse, even in small amounts after prolonged abstinence [71, 72]. This phenomenon, described as sensitization or reverse tolerance, has been suggested as a long‐lasting neurobiological susceptibility, wherein even minimal stimulant exposure can trigger psychotic relapse years after the initial episode. Other factors include other substance use (notably alcohol), sleep deprivation and psychosocial stressors [73, 74, 75].

These findings highlight the need for multi‐dimensional models integrating biological, psychological and social factors to guide the personalized prevention and treatment of StIP, with predictive tools to identify those at risk for chronicity and minimize unnecessary long‐term antipsychotic use.

CONCLUSION

The management of StIP continues to be undermined by critical evidence and practice gaps. These include the lack of validated diagnostic tools to distinguish StIP from primary psychosis in acute settings or non‐abstinent presentations, the absence of StIP‐specific pharmacological guidelines and evidence on antipsychotic selection, insufficient data‐driven recommendations on treatment duration or discontinuation, the lack of integrated behavioral treatment models tested in StIP populations, and the absence of tailored guidelines for the pharmacological management of StIP‐related agitation.

Given the severity, rising prevalence and clinical complexity of StIP, continuing to rely on extrapolated, non‐specific recommendations is inadequate and risks compromising patient outcomes. We call for targeted multidisciplinary research to develop validated StIP screening tools, stimulant‐specific diagnostic algorithms, and evidence‐based pharmacological and behavioral treatment frameworks spanning the acute, maintenance and relapse‐prevention phases of care. Without such efforts, StIP care will remain reactive, fragmented and suboptimal—leaving one of psychiatry's most pressing syndromes without the tailored responses it urgently requires.
