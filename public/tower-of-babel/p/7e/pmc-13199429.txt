Reconstitution of spermatogenesis and continuous generation of functional haploid germ cells in mouse testicular organoids
Authors: Cong Wan, Qi Li, Zhaokai Yao, Zhibin Ye, Jiexiang Zhao, Shaofang Ren, Xingguo Xue, Kang Tang, Qi Rao, Yetian Weng, Hongxia Xu, Wen Wang, Chuxin Zhong, Xiaotian Li, Xinyan Yang, Minjia Pan, Manman Cui, Linzi Ma, Dingyao Chen, Chaohui Li, Ziye Zheng, Yi Zheng, Zhaoting Liu, Gang Chang, Kai Miao, Fang Luo, Xiao-Yang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Testicular organoids that support spermatogenesis and generate functional haploid germ cells are still lacking. Here, we developed a formation-differentiation culture approach to generate optimized testicular organoids (O-Torgs) derived from neonatal mouse primary testicular cells. These O-Torgs could essentially recapitulate the seminiferous tubule morphogenesis, Sertoli cell maturation, and testosterone secretion. Importantly, O-Torgs not only support spermatogenesis but also sustain proliferative undifferentiated spermatogonia and continuous generation of functional haploid cells for up to three months, with offspring derived from these haploid cells displaying normal growth and reproductive capability to the F2 generation. Mechanistically, we found that the formation stage enhanced the reconstruction of tubule-like structures and subsequent spermatogenesis by providing a more conducive extracellular matrix niche. Finally, O-Torgs were proven to be effective in modeling male infertility and drug screening, with BTT-3033 acting as a potential drug in protecting busulfan-mediated germ cell loss. Overall, our work establishes a strategy to obtain functional testicular organoids, offering promising avenues for male infertility modeling and drug discovery.

Introduction

Up to 1% of the entire male population suffers from azoospermia1. Microscopic testicular sperm extraction has been used for the treatment of some azoospermia cases. However, if there are no mature spermatozoa or spermatids in the testis, the patients are unable to have their own offspring. In mammals, spermatogenesis is a highly complex process that involves spermatogonial proliferation and differentiation, meiosis of spermatocytes, and spermiogenesis2. This process requires the delicate testicular architecture, which facilitates the essential interactions between germ cells and testicular somatic cells2–4, otherwise it can cause male infertility. Therefore, the establishment of in vitro spermatogenesis systems is not only an alternative strategy to obtain functional haploid male germ cells, but also of great importance for modeling male infertility and drug screening.

Great efforts have been made to accomplish spermatogenesis in vitro. To date, the generation of functional spermatids has mainly relied on the organ culture or two-dimensional (2D) culture systems5,6. Although tissue fragment culture can effectively replicate the testis-specific signaling and morphological elements essential for spermatogenesis, it is challenging for targeted cell manipulation and unsuitable for drug screening5. In addition, the 2D culture system is inconsistent with the spermatogenesis process in vivo due to the absence of spermatogonia and cannot truly recapitulate the intercellular communication within the testis6, which largely limits in vitro gametogenesis and male-infertility modeling studies.

Organoids derived from primary tissues or pluripotent stem cells have been regarded as an ideal model to advance both biological research and therapeutic development7,8. The ideal testicular organoid should closely resemble in vivo testicular tissue in terms of both structure and function9. Previous studies have made progress in mimicking in vivo testicular features. For instance, organoids derived from immature rodent testicular cells could form the blood-testis barrier (BTB), sustain long-term hormone secretion, and generate round spermatids or even spermatozoa10–17. Similarly, organoids derived from human or large animal testicular cells have been shown to maintain undifferentiated spermatogonia or initiate meiosis18–20. However, several limitations still exist, such as deficient reconstruction of testicular tubule structures or the absence of key testicular cell types, especially functional haploid germ cells9,15,17,21. More importantly, all testicular organoids established so far were unable to recapitulate the spermatogenesis process and the continuous generation of haploid germ cells.

In this study, we developed a formation-differentiation stage culture method to establish testicular organoids that were generated from neonatal mouse primary testicular cells, which could recapitulate key in vivo testicular developmental processes. Furthermore, testicular organoids could effectively support the process of spermatogenesis and continuous generation of functional haploid germ cells for up to 90 days, with the resulting round spermatids being fertilization-competent and capable of generating healthy and fertile offspring. Thus, our work establishes a reliable model for mechanistic investigations of spermatogenesis and male infertility modeling.

Results

A formation-differentiation culture approach for establishing mouse testicular organoids that promotes the generation of haploid germ cells

Increasing evidence indicates that the improvements of the internal architecture of organoids are closely related to physiological functions22,23 and controlling the initial conditions to improve organoid development and reproducibility is of great importance24. Thus, we screened several culture media for their effectiveness in testicular organoids establishment, using the proper formation of seminiferous tubule-like structures at the initial stage as the key standard. Among these conditions, spermatogonial stem cell culture medium (abbreviated as SSCM)25 yielded testicular organoids with significantly larger diameters and the highest number of tubule-like structures compared with other media (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). Nevertheless, long-term culture of testicular organoids using SSCM alone failed to produce spermatids (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

αMKM, which consists of αMEM supplemented with 10% knockout serum replacement (KSR), has been reported to be effective for culturing testicular tissue fragments to generate functional male gametes5. Combined with our finding that SSCM could facilitate the formation of seminiferous tubule-like structures at the early culture stage, we therefore adopted a stage-by-stage culture approach to fulfill the requirements for both tubule-like structure formation and germ cell differentiation. In detail, several combinations were designed, in which SSCM was initially used for the formation of tubule-like structures and followed by αMKM for the differentiation (Fig. 1a). We first quantified the localization of germ cells and found that more than 96% of SYCP3+ cells were localized inside the tubules, which could be consistently observed in all groups (Supplementary Fig. 1d). Among these combinations, testicular organoids were first cultured in SSCM for six days (Group 4) and then switched to αMKM generated the highest ratio of pachytene/diplotene spermatocytes at day 14 of culture (Fig. 1b, c). Notably, testicular organoids cultured in Group 4 also exhibited the highest efficiency in generating CREM+ round spermatids when the cultivation period was extended to day 25 (Fig. 1c, d). To clearly track the spermatid production, testicular organoids were established using testicular cells expressing DsRed under the control of protamine 1 (Prm1) promoter. DsRed signal was first detected in round spermatids at postnatal day 25 (PND25) testes and increased at PND26 (Supplementary Fig. 1g, h). We found that the proportion of organoids exhibiting DsRed signal peaked at 37.5% in Group 4 at day 40 (Fig. 1c, e). Consequently, the stage-by-stage culture method utilized for Group 4 was referred to as the formation-differentiation culture method, which involved the initial six days of cultivation in SSCM as the formation stage, followed by subsequent cultivation in αMKM as the differentiation stage. Furthermore, the organoids cultured in this method were named as the optimized testicular organoids (abbreviated as O-Torgs), while the organoids cultured with constant αMKM were designated as traditional testicular organoids (abbreviated as T-Torgs).

To further verify that the formation stage culture was important for the subsequent spermatogenesis, testicular organoids were cultured with either SSCM or αMKM for six days before being transplanted under the skin of nude mice (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Intriguingly, more testicular organoids with prominent Prm1-DsRed signal were observed at day 45 post-transplantation in the group cultured with SSCM relative to that cultured with αMKM (replicate 1: 18.7% vs. 0% and replicate 2: 21.0% vs. 7.7%) (Supplementary Fig. 2b). Consistently, higher number of spermatids were also detected in SSCM-cultured organoids (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

Furthermore, the formation-differentiation culture method also supported the establishment of testicular organoids from cryopreserved testicular cells and enabled the generation of Prm1-DsRed spermatids (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). These results indicate that the formation-differentiation culture method could significantly boost the generation of haploid germ cells in O-Torgs.

O-Torgs essentially model specific structural and functional features of the testis

Three-dimensional reconstruction through whole-mount immunofluorescence staining further confirmed the alterations of tubule-like structures in O-Torgs from day 6 to day 40, with their length increasing notably over this period (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Movies 1–3). Next, we identified an upregulation of androgen receptor (AR) expression, coupled with a downregulation of anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) and MKI67 levels before day 25 (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). This pattern suggests that Sertoli cells may be undergoing developmental maturation within the O-Torgs. In addition, we assessed testosterone secretion in O-Torgs and compared it with in vivo developmental trends. We found that the serum testosterone levels at PND4, PND28, and PND42 exhibited a progressive increase from prepubertal to adult stages. This pattern could be essentially recapitulated in O-Torgs, with a steady increase from day 6 to day 40. And the addition of luteinizing hormone (LH) could effectively increase the concentration of testosterone in O-Torgs (Fig. 2d). Collectively, these findings suggest that the developing O-Torgs could essentially recapitulate several key in vivo testis processes, including seminiferous tubule morphogenesis and enlargement, as well as Sertoli cell maturation and testosterone secretion.

O-Torgs are with an in vivo testis-like cellular organization

To assess whether O-Torgs could recapitulate key testicular features, we conducted an extensive characterization at day 40. The seminiferous tubule-like structure was surrounded by a defined basement membrane, visualized by ACTA2 and Laminin staining27 (Supplementary Fig. 5a). A closer examination showed that seminiferous tubule-like structures were primarily assembled by ACTA2+ peritubular myoid (PTM) cells and SOX9+ Sertoli cells, with F4/80+ macrophages and HSD3B1+ Leydig cells present within the testicular interstitium (Supplementary Fig. 5b). Histological analyses of O-Torgs revealed seminiferous epithelium-like structures with Sertoli cells and PTM at the basement membrane, along with a continuum of developing germ cells in the tubule-like structures (Supplementary Fig. 5c). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results showed that BTB-like structures with tight junctions and desmosomes were formed in O-Torgs (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. 5d).

To assess the development of germ cells in O-Torgs, we used TEM to confirm the presence of spermatogonia, spermatocytes, and spermatids with acrosomes, which exhibited characteristics similar to their in vivo testis counterparts (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 5e, f). A detailed analysis revealed that ID4+LIN28A+ and GFRA1+LIN28A+ spermatogonial stem cells could be detected (Fig. 2g). Meiotic chromosome spread showed the robust expression of DMC1, RAD51, MLH1, SYCP3, and SYCP1 in primary spermatocytes (Fig. 2h), indicating the initiation of meiotic recombination. The accumulation of γH2AX on unsynapsed sex chromosomes and its disappearance from autosomes indicated the completion of synapsis (Fig. 2h). The average numbers of DMC1 and MLH1 foci per cell in O-Torgs were about 106.8 ± 12.8 and 21.6 ± 5.6, respectively, both slightly lower than the in vivo testes values of 118.4 ± 23.8 and 23.2 ± 3.9 (Fig. 2i). Subsequently, haploid germ cells were confirmed by the expression of spermatid-specific markers, including CREM, TNP1, Prm1-DsRed, and PNA (Fig. 2j). The mitotic and meiotic indices28,29 in O-Torgs were about 1.58 ± 0.6 and 0.48 ± 0.2, respectively. These values were also lower than those in testes (10.77 ± 3.3 and 2.69 ± 0.5; Fig. 2k).

O-Torgs can effectively support long-term spermatogenesis

To evaluate whether O-Torgs could support the long-term generation of spermatids, we then analyzed the cell composition in days 25, 40, and 90 O-Torgs. Undifferentiated spermatogonia with proliferative capacity were consistently observed, which were comparable among days 25, 40 and 90 O-Torgs (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Notably, the Prm1-DsRed signal remained visible in day 90 O-Torgs (Fig. 3a). Further analyses showed that the number of spermatids was highest at day 40, but still maintained a high level at day 90 (Supplementary Fig. 6b). Given that germ cells, ranging from spermatogonia to spermatids, could still be detected in day 90 O-Torgs (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Fig. 6d), we next asked whether O-Torgs could support continuous spermatid production, a key feature of in vivo testis that has not been achieved in previous studies21. To address this, O-Torgs were labeled with 5-ethynyl-2’-deoxyuridine (EdU) at day 28, followed by 5-Bromo-2’-deoxyuridine (BrdU) labeling at day 35, each for 2 days (Supplementary Fig. 6e). At day 30, only undifferentiated spermatogonia and preleptotene spermatocytes were labeled with EdU (Supplementary Fig. 6f). By day 55, however, EdU or BrdU labeling was present not only in undifferentiated spermatogonia but also in spermatocytes and spermatids (Supplementary Fig. 6g). Crucially, spermatids positive for both EdU and BrdU were identified in day 55 O-Torgs (Fig. 3d). The statistical analysis of EdU+ or BrdU+ spermatids further supports the ongoing process of spermatid generation (Fig. 3d). These results suggest that O-Torgs could support the continuous differentiation of germ cells, thereby promoting sustained spermatid production.

Round spermatids derived from O-Torgs can support the generation of healthy, fertile offspring

Given that the O-Torgs system could sustain long-term spermatogenesis, we next want to know whether the haploid germ cells in O-Torgs could support the generation of healthy offspring. To this end, we sorted spermatids from O-Torgs at days 25, 40, and 90 for intracytoplasmic round spermatid injection (ROSI), using spermatids from day 40 T-Torgs and from in vivo PND30 and PND35 testes as controls. Flow sorting results showed that 3.4%, 4.8%, and 2.5% of 1 C DNA cells could be detected in the O-Torgs at these three time points, respectively (Fig. 3e). In contrast, only 1.6% of cells in T-Torgs were classified as having 1 C DNA content, but these signals were primarily caused by false noise rather than true haploid cells (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 7a). Further bisulfite sequencing results showed that the imprinting patterns of both Snrpn and H19 in haploid germ cells sorted from day 40 O-Torgs (Fig. 3f) were similar to those of their in vivo counterparts (Fig. 3g).

ROSI was then performed (Table 1 and Supplementary Data 1). Of the 68 oocytes injected with spermatids from day 25 O-Torgs with the CAG-EGFP strain, 54 could develop to the 2-cell stage, resulting in 2 healthy pups with green fluorescence after embryo transfer (Supplementary Fig. 7b and Table 1). In total, among the 162 oocytes injected with spermatids from day 40 O-Torgs with either the CAG-EGFP or Prm1-DsRed strains, 154 could develop to the 2-cell stage (Fig. 3h and Table 1), and gave birth to 24 live pups (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 7c). Half of these pups were positive for the Prm1-DsRed transgene because heterozygous transgenic testicular cells were used here (Fig. 3i). Bisulfite sequencing revealed a pattern correlated with maternal and paternal genetic contributions (Fig. 3j). Importantly, the live-birth rate from O-Torgs at day 40 (15.6%) was much higher than at day 25 (3.7%), and comparable to day 90 (12.5%) and in vivo testes (12.8%) (Table 1). All these pups had normal placental histology and grew into healthy and fertile adults (Fig. 3k and Supplementary Fig. 7e-g), without obvious abnormalities in the F1 offspring (Table 1). F2 progeny also exhibited normal physiological metrics, including body and reproductive organ development, DNA methylation patterns, mating competence (Supplementary Fig. 7h-k), and fertility (Fig. 3k). In contrast, only two embryos derived from day 40 T-Torgs developed to the 2-cell stage, and no viable offspring were obtained (Table 1).

The developmental trajectory and transcriptional signatures in testicular organoids

Then the transcriptional signatures of germ cells were compared between O-Torgs and T-Torgs. We found that the number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) increased significantly upon entry into meiosis, with the pachytene and diplotene exhibiting the highest numbers of DEGs (Fig. 4e). The upregulated genes in spermatocytes of O-Torgs were highly associated with meiosis, such as “XY body” and “DNA repair” (Fig. 4f), while the downregulated genes were mainly enriched in “Positive regulation of programmed cell death”, “Regulation of inflammatory response”, and “Regulation of apoptotic signaling pathway” (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Further analysis showed that the expression levels of genes associated with meiosis were significantly higher in spermatocytes of O-Torgs compared with T-Torgs, such as H2afx, Mov10l1, Piwil2 and Sycp1 (Fig. 4g). Chromosome spreading showed that γH2AX signal was normally distributed in XY body of O-Torgs spermatocytes; however, the γH2AX signal was either aberrantly remained on autosomes or absent in pachytene-like spermatocytes of T-Torgs (Fig. 4h). The abnormal ratio of XY body was much lower in O-Torgs (16.6%) compared to T-Torgs (38.7%) (Fig. 4i). Correlation results further revealed that the germ cells in O-Torgs were more similar to those in testes (Fig. 4j and Supplementary Fig. 9b). Together, these results demonstrate that the developmental trajectory and transcriptional signatures of spermatogenic cells in O-Torgs closely resembled those observed in the testis.

Testicular niche is properly maintained in O-Torgs

The testicular niche is important for male germ development, and its disruption could result in male infertility31–33. To determine whether O-Torgs possessed a relatively normal testicular niche, we further analyzed testicular somatic cells using scRNA-seq data. Endothelial cells were excluded from the analysis due to their rarity or absence in the samples (Supplementary Fig. 10a). Among these cell types, we found that Sertoli cells exhibited the most prominent differences between O-Torgs and T-Torgs (Fig. 5a and Supplementary Fig. 10b). The DEGs in Sertoli cells accounted for 50.9% of all DEGs detected in somatic cells (Fig. 5b). A primary function of Sertoli cells is to form the BTB, which plays essential roles in spermatogenesis34. Therefore, we examined the expression of BTB-associated genes and found their expression levels were significantly higher in Sertoli cells of O-Torgs compared to those of T-Torgs (Fig. 5d). Immunostaining results further showed that TJP1 was significantly upregulated in O-Torgs compared to T-Torgs (Fig. 5e). A biotin tracer assay was performed to assess the integrity of BTB, and a much lower proportion of biotin leaky seminiferous-like tubules was detected in O-Torgs compared with T-Torgs (Fig. 5f), indicating that the O-Torgs had better BTB integrity that might benefit the testicular niche.

To explore the differential regulation of target cells by Sertoli cells, the intercellular communication was analyzed using CellChat35. The results showed that the interactions between Sertoli cells as well as the interactions between Sertoli cells and germ cells were notably higher in O-Torgs compared to T-Torgs (Fig. 5g). Notably, the interaction strength of certain ligand-receptor pairs within Sertoli cells was related to the formation of the BTB, such as Jam2-F11r and F11r-F11r (Supplementary Fig. 10c). In O-Torgs, the upregulated ligands between Sertoli cells and germ cells were likely to regulate downstream genes that were essential for spermatogenesis, particularly meiotic genes such as Hormad1, Piwil2, and Sycp3 (Fig. 5h and Supplementary Fig. 10d). These findings suggest that Sertoli cells in O-Torgs might promote the regulation of meiosis-related genes to ensure the normal progression of meiosis.

In addition, the upregulated genes in Leydig cells of O-Torgs were highly associated with endocrine functions such as “Regulation of hormone levels” and “Cellular homeostasis” (Supplementary Fig. 10e). Consistent with this finding, testosterone levels in O-Torgs were significantly higher than those in T-Torgs cultured under normal conditions or with LH added (Supplementary Fig. 10f). In summary, these results suggest that the testicular niche was properly maintained in O-Torgs, which might be essential for in vitro spermatogenesis.

Extracellular matrix is crucial for the formation of tubule-like structures at the formation stage

A prominent phenotype was that SSCM could promote the organization of tubule-like structures more than αMKM at the formation stage, as further evidenced by increases in both the number and regularity of these structures (Fig. 6a, b). To dissect the underlying mechanisms, proteomic analyses were performed on both O-Torgs and T-Torgs at the formation stage (Fig. 6c). We found that the differentially expressed proteins (Fold change > 1.5) in O-Torgs compared with T-Torgs were enriched in “Mitotic cell cycle process” and “Tube morphogenesis” pathways (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 11). In addition, extracellular matrix (ECM)-related pathways were predominantly enriched, including “Collagen-activated signaling pathway”, “Collagen formation”, “Extracellular matrix organization”, and “Glycosaminoglycan metabolic process” (Fig. 6d). ECMs are ordered three-dimensional network structures composed of multiple components, playing pivotal structural and functional roles in tissue organization, remodeling, and the regulation of cellular processes36,37. In the testis, ECM proteins are crucial for the establishment of the sex-specific structure of developing gonads38. We hypothesized that ECM might facilitate the formation of tubule-like structures in O-Torgs, which might be important for spermatogenesis. To test this, we investigated whether the disruption of the ECM niche would affect the growth of O-Torgs. The addition of Collagenase type IV (Collagenase) or 4-methylumbelliferone (4-ME), which inhibits hyaluronan synthesis39,40, could remarkably reduce the tubule-like areas at day 6 in O-Torgs (Fig. 6f). Closer examination showed that the number and regularity of tubule-like structures were significantly decreased in treated O-Torgs (Fig. 6g, h), suggesting that disruption of the ECM niche adversely affects the formation of these structures. Consistently, treatment with Collagenase or 4-ME could indeed impact the generation of spermatids in day 40 O-Torgs (Fig. 6i). These findings collectively suggest that the formation stage using SSCM might provide a more conducive ECM niche to promote the formation of tubule-like structures, which could benefit spermatogenesis.

To probe the potential regulatory roles of the ECM, single-cell RNA-seq was also performed (Fig. 6c), and 7 distinct populations were identified based on well-defined markers (Supplementary Fig. 12a–c). We first focused on the expression of ECM-related genes, which were highly expressed in Mesenchymal.P, PTM1, and PTM2 (Supplementary Fig. 12d), suggesting that these cells might be the primary ECM producers. Then we employed CellChat to explore ECM regulatory mechanisms through putative interactions. The results showed that a twofold increase in interaction strength of ligands and receptors was detected in O-Torgs compared to T-Torgs (Supplementary Fig. 12e). Among the source cells, Mesenchymal.P, PTM1, and PTM2 exhibited more extensive putative interactions than other cell types in O-Torgs. Among the target cells, PTM1, Leydig cells and Sertoli cells displayed more putative interactions (Supplementary Fig. 12f). Overall, significantly enriched interaction pathways were closely associated with ECM, such as COLLAGEN and LAMININ (Supplementary Fig. 12g). Specifically, the ligand-receptor pairs [e.g., Col4a1-Sdc1, Col4a2-(Itga9 + Itgb1), Col1a1-Sdc4, Lama2-(Itga6 + Itgb1)] were highly expressed in PTM1, Leydig cells and Sertoli cells (Supplementary Fig. 12h), implying that the ECM in O-Torgs might exert potential regulatory effects on these cells.

Testicular organoid self-assembly is age-dependent, being reduced and delayed in pubertal cells and absent in adult cells13. Therefore, we tested SSCM that was employed during the formation stage for pubertal testicular cells, addressing the challenge of developing testicular organoids derived from non-neonatal sources. We found that the self-assembly rate of testicular organoids decreased with age, corresponding with the ECM score (Supplementary Fig. 13a-c), which dropped sharply after PND10. Surprisingly, cells cultured in SSCM exhibited a notably higher assembly efficiency for samples from PND8 to PND21 (Supplementary Fig. 13b, c). Remarkably, O-Torgs derived from PND14 testicular cells cultured for 28 days could support the formation of tubule-like structures, which were not observed in T-Torgs (Fig. 6j). Immunostaining of ACTA2 and SOX9 further confirmed the presence of typical seminiferous tubule-like boundaries, whereas T-Torgs exhibited a disorganized pattern (Fig. 6k). Together, these findings underscore the function of SSCM in promoting tubule-like structures formation from pubertal sources.

O-Torgs can be applied to screen drugs with potential use for male-infertility treatment

The efficient generation of functional haploid germ cells from O-Torgs makes them promising for disease modeling and drug screening. Busulfan (BU) is widely used to treat lymphoma and leukemia but can lead to male infertility, including prepubescent male patients41,42. Therefore, we first identified 2.5 μM BU (referred to as BU-2.5) as the optimal concentration for subsequent studies (Supplementary Fig. 14a, b). Compared to dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO)-treated controls, BU-2.5 treatment caused a significant reduction in organoid area from day 10 to day 40 (Fig. 7a, b). We next investigated whether O-Torgs could mimic the drug response observed in vivo and explored the potential for drug screening (Fig. 7e). First, we selected four known drugs (Sitagliptin, Puerarin, Melatonin, 5-Methylcytidine), which have been reported to be useful in protecting against busulfan-induced damage43–46. Among these four drugs, Melatonin significantly increased the number of undifferentiated spermatogonia, consistent with a previous study in vivo45 (Supplementary Fig. 14c, d). Three known drugs (Sitagliptin, Melatonin, 5-Methylcytidine) demonstrated marked efficacy in increasing spermatids (Fig. 7f, g and Supplementary Fig. 14e). These findings emphasize that the responses of O-Torgs to drugs were similar to those observed in vivo. Then, another four additional drugs (Naringin, Icariin, Tribulus Terrestris P.E., BTT-3033) were also tested to ascertain whether they could be used to rescue the phenotypes caused by busulfan. Interestingly, BTT-3033 was identified to be effective in promoting the numbers of both undifferentiated spermatogonia and spermatids (Fig. 7f, g and Supplementary Fig. 14e), and 5 μM was determined to be the optimal concentration (Supplementary Fig. 14f).

To verify whether BTT-3033 could indeed enhance spermatogenesis under busulfan-treated conditions, in vivo animal experiments were performed. After being injected with 4 mg/kg of busulfan, PND10 male mice were orally administered BTT-3033 for 1 month (Fig. 7h). We found that BTT-3033 administration significantly restored the sizes of both testes and epididymis (Fig. 7i, j). H&E staining further showed that germ cells in the seminiferous tubules and sperm accumulation in the cauda epididymis could be observed in BTT-3033-treated mice (Fig. 7k, l). Further statistical analyses revealed that BTT-3033 administration could significantly increase the numbers of undifferentiated spermatogonia, spermatocytes, and spermatids (Fig. 7m and Supplementary Fig. 15a). Collectively, these findings suggest that O-Torgs could be used to screen potential drugs for treating male infertility.

To dissect the potential mechanisms of BTT-3033, we performed transcriptomic analysis of testicular samples from Vehicle, BU + PBS, and BU + BTT-3033-treated mice. We found that BTT-3033 could rescue the dysregulation caused by busulfan to a certain extent (Supplementary Fig. 15b, c). Specifically, GO enrichment highlighted significant upregulation of pathways including spermatogenesis, sperm motility, and meiotic cell cycle in BTT-3033-treated mice, alongside downregulation of cytokine production, regulation of apoptotic signaling pathway, and inflammatory response (Supplementary Fig. 15c). These findings suggest that BTT-3033 may facilitate spermatogenic recovery by alleviating testicular inflammation and apoptosis.

Discussion

The establishment of testicular organoids that could essentially recapitulate the process of spermatogenesis and generate functional haploid spermatids remains a long-lasting question in the field of reproduction9,21,47. However, several key limitations still existed in this field: (1) A previous study achieved in vitro reconstitution of the whole male germ-cell development, but spermatogenesis still relied on organ culture48; (2) Although the in vitro generation of functional spermatids could be obtained in a 2D culture system, the absence of spermatogonia limited the generation of spermatid to only one time6; (3) To the best of our knowledge, all the reported testicular organoids were unable to support the continuous generation of functional spermatids.

In this study, we developed a stage-by-stage culture method to establish O-Torgs derived from neonatal mouse primary testicular cells. These O-Torgs could essentially recapitulate several in vivo testicular developmental processes, including seminiferous tubule morphogenesis, Sertoli cell maturation, and testosterone secretion. Remarkably, O-Torgs not only supported spermatogenesis in vitro but also generated functional spermatids. Furthermore, O-Torgs derived from different transgenic mice testicular cells were all able to support the production of healthy and fertile offspring, demonstrating the robustness and reproducibility of our approach. And these F2 offspring derived from O-Torgs showed no detectable abnormalities in the aspects of body weight, reproductive organ development, DNA methylation levels, and fertility. More strikingly, continuous generation of functional spermatids was observed in O-Torgs for up to 90 days. It will be interesting if the spermatogenic wave could be accurately controlled in O-Torgs.

Our study developed a formation-differentiation strategy to establish testicular organoids through the sequential use of SSCM followed by αMKM. In this strategy, the initial formation stage enabled neonatal testicular cells to reorganize into more typical tubule-like structures, which subsequently facilitated the generation of functional spermatids. Indeed, self-organization is a process that occurs iteratively and cumulatively, highlighting the importance of controlling the early stages of culture24. In addition, the precise use of culture medium during early culture phases is indeed crucial, since extended exposure leads to the formation of diploid spermatid-like cells49. Therefore, our work provides key insights that accurate self-organization into seminiferous tubules in the early culture stage is crucial for establishing functional testicular organoids.

Mechanistically, the initial use of SSCM was conducive to tubule-like structure formation and spermatogenesis by facilitating a more favorable ECM niche, as evidenced by the adverse effects on these processes when the ECM niche was disrupted. Indeed, the ECM plays key roles in reproduction-related processes such as BTB remodeling, elongated spermatids release, and gonad structure shaping38,50. Interestingly, we also observed a gradual decrease of ECM from neonatal to adult testes, which might explain why self-assembly could only be established from the neonatal testicular cells but not from those of adults. Given that the ECM is a multifaceted macromolecular network composed of collagens, proteoglycans, glycosaminoglycans, elastin, elastic fibers, laminins, fibronectin, and matricellular proteins36, further studies are warranted to determine which specific components of SSCM contribute to ECM production and which ECM component exerts a prominent role in the aggregation of testicular cells.

Unlike the spermatogenesis reconstitution from pluripotent stem cells, which depends on the transplantation into the seminiferous tubules48, our O-Torgs provide a more direct and accessible in vitro platform. We demonstrated that O-Torgs could simulate the impaired spermatogenesis caused by busulfan, similar to that observed in vivo. Using this model, several drugs were screened for their roles in protecting against germ cell loss phenotypes resulting from busulfan treatment. Among these candidate drugs, BTT-3033 was further validated to be effective in enhancing spermatogenesis through in vivo animal experiments, potentially by alleviating testicular inflammation and apoptosis.

Although O-Torgs show the capability to produce functional spermatids, several challenges are still unresolved. These are primarily characterized by a relatively low efficiency of spermatogenesis and the failure to produce elongating spermatids. This low efficiency might result from meiotic abnormalities, such as double-strand break (DSB) repair and synapsis defects, also seen in other in vitro spermatogenesis systems51–53. Additionally, the absence of vascular networks and suboptimal spatial organization in O-Torgs might also affect spermatid generation and maturation5,54,55. It warrants further optimization of testicular organoid culture by incorporating vascular components and using advanced biomaterials.

Methods

Animals

All animal studies conformed to the ethical guidelines of Southern Medical University Ethics Committee (L2016149) and the national standard Guidelines for Welfare and Ethical Review of Laboratory Animals (GB/T 35892-2018). ICR, C57BL/6, DBA/2 and BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Southern Medical University Laboratory Animal Center. CAG-EGFP transgenic mice were purchased from Nanjing Biomedical Research Institute of Nanjing University. Prm1-DsRed transgenic mice were a gift from Wolfgang Engel (University of Göttingen). Testicular cells were derived from the testis of postnatal day 3 (PND3) mice. All mice were housed in air-conditioned rooms with appropriate temperature and humidity under a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle, and were allowed free access to water and food in individually ventilated, specific pathogen-free, and autoclaved cages.

Generation and cultivation of optimized testicular organoids (O-Torgs) derived from neonatal mouse primary testicular cells

Testicular organoids were generated following the modified protocols. First, dissociate primary testicular cells and reaggregate them for organoid formation. Primary testes from PND3 mice were cut into small pieces with forceps, washed twice with PBS, followed by a two-step digestion using 0.1 mg/mL Collagenase type IV (Gibco, 17104019) and 0.05% Trypsin-EDTA (Gibco, 25300062) separately at 37 °C for 5 min. Cell dissociation was quenched with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, 12800-058) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, VISTECH, SE200-ES). The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a membrane with a 40 μm pore size and then resuspended in α-Minimum Essential Medium (αMEM) supplemented with 10% knockout serum replacement (KSR) and 20 ng/mL recombinant rat GDNF. Subsequently, approximately 5×104 cells were seeded into each well of a low-cell-binding U-bottom 96-well plate (Corning, 7007) and incubated at 37 °C for 2 days. Second, prepare agarose gel stands as the culture support. Agarose (Sigma-Aldrich, A9045) was dissolved in PBS by heating to achieve a final concentration of 1.5%. The solution (35 mL per dish) was poured into 10-cm petri dishes. After cooling and solidification, the gels were cut into approximately 10 × 10 mm cuboids and transferred into 24-well plates. Third, carefully transfer the cell aggregates onto the agarose gel stands. The aggregates were transferred to the flat surface of a cube of agarose gel by using a 20 μL pipette. Each gel cuboid was loaded with 3 to 5 organoids. Fourth, add pre-warmed specialized culture medium to sustain organoid development. The organoids were initially cultured in SSCM25 (Supplementary Data 2) for 6 days. Subsequently, the medium was changed to αMKM (Supplementary Data 2) and maintained under a gas-liquid interphase culture condition. The culture medium volume was adjusted to approximately two-thirds the height of the agarose gel and changed every 4 days. Sterile water was added to the surrounding wells to maintain humidity. Incubations were supplied with air containing 5% CO₂ and maintained at 34 °C.

Immunofluorescence staining

Organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 12 h at 4 °C, embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 7-8 μm slices. After deparaffinization and rehydration in xylene and graded ethanol, sections underwent antigen retrieval at 95 °C for 10 min. They were blocked with 2% BSA for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated overnight with primary antibodies (Supplementary Data 2) at 4 °C. After 3 PBS washes, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies (Supplementary Data 2) and Hoechst at room temperature for 1 h. Images were captured using a ZEISS LSM880 confocal microscope.

Recipient animals and procedures for transplantation

6-week-old male nude mice with a deficient T lymphocyte system were used. The mice were first castrated, and during the same surgery, organoids cultured with SSCM or αMKM were grafted under the back skin of castrated immunodeficient mice. 2 nude mice were transplanted with organoids for each group. After 45 days, the nude mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation, and the grafts were collected for further analysis.

Whole-mount immunofluorescence staining

Organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight, cleared with a hydrophilic clearing reagent kit (NUOHAI, NH-CR-210701), and incubated with primary antibodies at 37 °C for 4 days and then at 4 °C for 2 days. After three 2 h PBS washes, samples were incubated with secondary antibodies at 4 °C for 2 days. Images were acquired via Z-stack scanning on a Zeiss confocal microscope, and 3D reconstructions were generated using Imaris software. The morphological analysis was performed using FIJI ImageJ software. At least 50 tubule-like lengths were measured per organoid, and three organoids were counted for each group.

Testosterone analysis

The culture medium and mouse serum were collected to examine the testosterone level. LH was added at a concentration of 7.5 IU/mL in cultured organoids for 2 days. Testosterone concentrations were calculated using a commercial ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Solarbio, SEKSM-0003).

Electron microscopy

Organoids and C57BL/6 male mice testes (4-5 weeks) were fixed in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde, 5% PFA, and 5% sucrose, and were post-fixed in osmium tetroxide in veronal-acetate buffer (1%). Samples were incubated overnight in 0.5% veronal-acetate buffer, and then dehydrated and embedded in Embed-812 resin. Fifty-nanometer sections were prepared using a Leica Ultracut UCT microtome equipped with a Diatome diamond knife and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. The sections were examined using a FEI Tecnai Spirit at 80 keV.

Histological examination

Organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 12 h at 4 °C, then embedded in paraffin and cut into 5 μm sections. After deparaffinization with Xylene and a graded series of ethanol, the sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin for 5 min and 1 min, respectively. Images were captured using a Carl Zeiss microscope.

Chromosomal spreads

Single-cell suspensions of organoids and C57BL/6 male mice testes (4–5 weeks) were prepared. Then the cell suspensions were centrifuged and lysed in a hypotonic solution. The hypotonicity was terminated by adding an equal volume of sucrose solution, and the cells were then fixed on glass slides with 4% PFA. Immunostaining was performed with antibodies, and images were captured using a ZEISS LSM880 confocal microscope.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)

Organoids and testes (4-5 weeks) were washed with PBS, dissociated into single cells using 0.5 mg/mL Collagenase type IV for 20 min at 37 °C, and digestion was stopped with DMEM containing 10% FBS. Cells were filtered through a 40 μm mesh, centrifuged, and resuspended in DMEM with 10% FBS and 4 µg/mL Hoechst for 20 min at 37 °C. FACS assay was performed with a MoFlo XDP (Beckman Coulter) cell sorter.

Intracytoplasmic round spermatid injection

Superovulation in C57BL/6 × DBA/2 F1 (BDF1) female mice (6–7 weeks) was achieved by administering 5 IU of equine chorionic gonadotropin and, 48 h later, 5 IU of human chorionic gonadotropin. After 12 h, cumulus-oocyte complexes were extracted and treated with 0.1% hyaluronidase to remove cumulus cells. Oocytes were activated using a cytochalasin B solution for 10 min, and round spermatids were injected into them. The oocytes were incubated at 37 °C in the activation solution, then switched to M16 medium after 6 h. 2-cell embryos were subsequently transferred to pseudopregnant ICR females (7–8 weeks).

Bisulfite sequencing

Genomic DNA samples were obtained either from more than 10,000 spermatids or from tail tips of neonatal mice for downstream experimental analyses. Briefly, bisulfite sequencing of genomic DNA was performed using the EZ DNA Methylation-direct kit (Zymo Research, EM101). PCR amplification was performed using TAKARA HS DNA polymerase (TAKARA Ltd., Dalian, China, RRO06Q) with specific primers for the H19 and Snrpn DMR regions (Supplementary Data 2). To determine the methylation status of individual CpG sites, PCR products were extracted from the gel, subcloned into the pMD18T vector, and then sequenced. Bisulfite sequencing data were analyzed for visualization and quantification using QUMA.

DNA methylation pattern

Genomic DNA was isolated from mouse tissue using the FastPure Cell/Tissue DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, DC102). 5 ng of the isolated genomic DNA was mixed with 60 pg of unmethylated lambda DNA and underwent bisulfite conversion. This converted DNA was subsequently purified using the Zymo EZ DNA Methylation-Gold Kit (Zymo, D4014). Library construction involved random priming, amplification, and sequencing. Sequencing reads were processed with Trim_Galore (v0.6.10) to remove adapters and low-quality reads, using ‘--clip_R1’ 9 and ‘--clip_R2’ 9 for paired-end reads. Cleaned reads were aligned to mm10 (UCSC) with Bismark (v2.5.4) in paired-end mode and ‘--non_directional’ option. Picard (v2.18.29) ‘MarkDuplicates’ removed PCR duplicates. MethylKit (v1.20.0) extracted CpG methylation sites, requiring at least 3 reads per site. ‘percMethylation’ calculated the average methylation level per sample. Refer to previous work56 for annotating imprinting control regions (ICRs), and ggplot2 (v4.0.1) visualized the ICRs' methylation levels.

Single-cell RNA-seq library construction and sequencing

The single-cell RNA-Seq library was prepared using the SeekOne® Digital Droplet Single Cell 3’ library preparation kit (SeekGene catalog number: K00202). Briefly, an appropriate number of cells were mixed with reverse transcription reagents and then added to the sample wells of the SeekOne® Chip S3. Next, Barcoded Hydrogel Beads (BHBs) and partitioning oil were dispensed into the corresponding wells of the S3 chip, respectively. After droplet generation, reverse transcription was carried out at 42 °C for 90 min and inactivation was performed at 85 °C for 5 min. Subsequently, cDNAs were purified and used for PCR amplification. Then, the amplified cDNA products were cleaned, fragmented, subjected to end repair, and had their tails ligated to the sequencing adapters. Finally, the DNA of the 3’ poly (A) portion of the genes was amplified by index PCR, which contains Cell Barcode and Unique Molecular Index. The sequencing library was cleaned up using VAHTS DNA Clean Beads (Vazyme, N411-03) and analyzed using Qubit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Q33226) and Bio-Fragment Analyzer (Bioptic, Qsep400). The library was sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with a read length of PE150.

Single-cell RNA-seq data processing

The raw sequencing data were processed by Fastp57 to trim primer sequences and low-quality bases. Subsequently, Seeksoul tools (Seekgene, v1.0.0) were used to process the resulting data, demultiplex cellular barcodes, and map reads to the mouse mm10 reference genome (refdata-gex-mm10-2020-A downloaded from 10X Genomics) to generate a feature-barcode matrix for downstream analyses.

Clustering and identification of cells

We created Seurat (v4.4.0) objects to store the data. Only cells expressing more than 500 genes and containing less than 25% mitochondrial reads were included in our analysis. Afterwards, we applied global-scaling normalization method ‘LogNormalize’ for data normalization. ‘FindIntegrationAnchors’ and ‘IntegrateData’ functions were used to integrate different batches of scRNA-seq data (batch information was provided in Supplementary Data 3). Subsequently, PC1 to PC30 were selected to perform the ‘RunPCA’ function. For day 40 data, PC1 to PC30 were selected, and the ‘RunUMAP’ function was executed to generate a UMAP plot. For day 6 data, PC1 to PC21 were selected, and the ‘RunTSNE’ function was run to generate a t-SNE plot. Cells were clustered by the shared nearest neighbor (SNN) analysis using the ‘FindNeighbors’ and ‘FindClusters’ functions in the Seurat package, and cell types were assigned to each cluster using known marker genes.

Identification of differentially expressed genes

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the Seurat ‘FindMarkers’ function with the default Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Only the genes expressed in more than 10% of the cell cluster, with a |log2 fold-change| greater than 0.5 and p value smaller than 0.05, were retained (Supplementary Data 4 and 5). p-values were calculated by the FindMarkers function in Seurat using the default Wilcoxon Rank Sum test. Enrichment analysis, such as GO analysis, was performed with Metascape (www.metascape.org).

Correlation analysis

Transcriptional similarities between organoids and in vivo tissues were evaluated through a correlation analysis. For germ cells, Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated using a set of 704 genes associated with spermatogenesis (Supplementary Data 6), which were downloaded from www.informatics.jax.org. For somatic cells, 3,000 highly variable genes were selected for the analysis.

BTB integrity assay

Organoids were incubated with EZ-Link Sulfo-NHS-LC-Biotin (10 mg/mL in PBS containing 1 mM CaCl2, Thermo Fisher, 21335) for 30 min at 37 °C. Then fixed overnight in 4% PFA, embedded in paraffin blocks, and sectioned at 6-µm-thick for immunofluorescence staining.

Intercellular communication analysis

Intercellular communication analysis was performed using the CellChat package35. In the “filterCommunication” step, the minimum number of cells required for intercellular communication in each cell population was set to 5 cells. For other processes, standard parameters were employed. We summarized the likelihood of ligand-receptor interactions under each pathway as the contribution of that pathway to intercellular interactions. Ligand and target genes were predicted using the NicheNet package58.

Proteome analysis by LC-MS/MS

Protein extraction and tryptic digestion of day 6 testicular organoids were performed with the iST kit. Each replicate consists of approximately 200 organoids, and each group has three independent biological replicates. After heating and cooling, samples were incubated, then cleaned and desalted. Peptides were separated on a Vanquish Neo UHPLC system with a C18 column, and DIA data were collected in diaPASEF mode using a Bruker timsTOF HT mass spectrometer. The raw DIA data were processed with Spectronaut 19 against the 2024 mouse UniProt database, accounting for variable and fixed modifications. Dynamic iRT was used for retention time prediction, and Q value cutoffs were set at 1% for precursors, peptides, and proteins. Decoy generation was mutated, and local normalization was applied. The MaxLFQ method was used to determine major group quantities from peptides exceeding the 1% Q value threshold. Differential proteins were identified by comparing the fold change of the biological repeat mean between the two groups. Proteins with a fold change > 1.5 and p < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed (Supplementary Data 7), p-values were calculated by unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Enrichment analysis was performed on differential proteins using Metascape (www.metascape.org) using default parameters.

ECM disruptions in O-Torgs

O-Torgs were cultured on top of agarose gel blocks and exposed to different ECM-interfering molecules in SSCM: 1.5 μg/mL Collagenase Type IV and 400 μM 4-methylumbelliferone (TargetMol, USA, 90-33-5) for 6 days with regular medium changes. Brightfield images were acquired after treatment to assess organoid sizes. Organoids were collected and processed for immunofluorescence staining as described above.

Analysis of gene expression scores

The AUCell (v1.20.0) package was used to score cells based on the expression of specific gene sets. These specific gene sets were derived from the intersection of gene sets of MatrisomeDB 2.059 and in vivo data (Supplementary Data 8).

Establishment of testicular organoid infertility model and drug screening

To establish a busulfan chemotherapy model, PND3 testes were digested and reaggregated in a U96 plate for 2 days before being transferred to agar blocks. On day 0, the organoids were treated with varying concentrations of busulfan (0 μM, 1 μM, 2.5 μM, and 5 μM) (Sigma, B2635). After 2 days of treatment, the organoids were transferred to SSCM and cultured for an additional 4 days. Finally, the organoids were cultured in αMKM medium for further culture. To screen for small-molecule drugs, 8 small molecule drugs were added individually to the culture medium during the entire air-liquid culture process. The drugs included 100 μM Sitagliptin (TargetMol, USA, 486460-32-6), 100 μM Puerarin (TargetMol, USA, 3681-99-0), 400 μM Melatonin (TargetMol, USA, 73-31-4), 10 μM 5-Methylcytidine (TargetMol, USA, 2140-61-6), 10 μM Naringin (TargetMol, USA, 10236-47-2), 10 μM Icariin (TargetMol, USA, 489-32-7), 1 μM Tribulus Terrestris P.E. (TargetMol, USA, 22153-44-2), 10 μM BTT-3033 (TargetMol, USA, 1259028-99-3). The medium was changed every 4 days.

Animal models and drug administration

We used PND10 male ICR mice for this study. 16 mice were randomly allocated to the control group and the experimental group. 5 control mice received an intraperitoneal injection of an equal volume of solvent and were gavaged with PBS. 11 mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of 4 mg/kg busulfan, 5 mice were gavaged with PBS, and 6 mice were gavaged with 10 mg/kg BTT-303360. Starting from the second day after receiving busulfan injection, gavage was administered once daily for 30 days.

Bulk RNA-sequencing

For mRNA library preparation, 1 µg of total RNA per sample was used. Then the mRNA was fragmented, and cDNA was synthesized and purified. The purified cDNA underwent end repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation. cDNA fragments of 200-300 bp were selected, PCR-amplified, and purified to create a library. The library quality was checked with the Agilent 4200 TapeStation, and data quality was assessed using FastQC (v0.12.1). BBmap (v39.01) and Trimmomatic (v0.39) were utilized to eliminate adapter sequences and low-quality bases. Then, HISAT2 (v2.2.1) was used to align the data back to the reference genome GRCm38 (Ensembl). StringTie (v2.2.1) constructed the expression matrix, and DESeq2 (v1.34.0) was used for downstream analysis. Upon normalization, the top 7500 highly variable genes were retained for Principal Component Analysis (PCA). The differential expression was performed using DESeq2 within Wald tests, applying against an absolute log2 fold change of 1 and a significance level p of 0.05 (Supplementary Data 9). The gene intersections were performed using VennDiagram (v1.7.3).

Statistics and reproducibility

The number of independent biological (not technical) replicates for each experiment is indicated in the figure legends and highlighted in different colors. Each independent biological replicate comprised three or more organoids. Sample sizes were not predetermined, and sample allocation was randomized. Statistical analyses were performed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test for comparisons between two groups or one-way ANOVA for multiple group comparisons (GraphPad Prism 6), with exact p values shown in figures. The experiments in Figs. 1d, 1e, 2b, 2e, 2f, 2g, 2h, 3a, 3b, 3f, 3h, 4h, 6j, 6k and Supplementary Figs. 1c, 3a, 3b, 5a, 5b, 5e, 6d, 6f were conducted at least two times independently with similar results. Regularity in Figs. 6b, 6h was assessed by comparing the theoretical and actual circumferences of tubule-like structures, with values near 1 indicating more regular shapes, calculated using the ellipse circumference formula.
