Synthesis, characterization, and evaluation of antimicrobial double-layer mat incorporating nisin and thyme essential oil to enhance food safety
Authors: Niloofar Shirdam, Mohammad Mir-Derikvand, Ali Hossein Rezayan, Mahsa Kalantar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antimicrobial active packaging is a promising strategy to control foodborne contamination, yet the instability and rapid inactivation of natural preservatives in complex food matrices limit their practical use. Here, we fabricate and evaluate an electrospun double-layer nanobiocomposite mat engineered to protect bioactives and enable their slow release. The inner layer comprises chitosan/poly (vinyl alcohol) loaded with nisin and Shirazi thyme essential oil (CS/PVA/N/EO), while the outer layer is cellulose acetate containing thyme essential oil (CA/EO). Solutions were electrospun and characterized by SEM, FTIR, TGA, contact angle, and tensile testing; antibacterial performance was assessed by agar diffusion and a dynamic shake-flask assay. SEM revealed bead-free fibers in the inner layer with an average diameter of 144.8 ± 28.33 nm. FTIR and TGA confirmed successful incorporation of actives and thermal stability within typical processing ranges. Surface wettability was asymmetric by design: the inner layer was hydrophilic (contact angle 46.53°–72.43°) to promote release, whereas the outer CA/EO surface was hydrophobic (95.5°) to resist moisture. The double-layer exhibited adequate mechanical integrity for handling and conversion with an elongation at break of 21.66 ± 0.62%. Antimicrobial tests confirmed inhibitory activity against both Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes) and Escherichia coli (E. coli), with inhibition zones reaching 27.26 ± 1.60 mm for L. monocytogenes and 25.13 ± 1.40 mm for E. coli. In the dynamic assay, a 5 × 5 cm, 0.36 mm pad reduced L. monocytogenes by 2 logs and maintained counts below the detection limit for 48 h, whereas the control increased to 9.58 ± 0.31 log CFU/mL. These results demonstrate an effective active mat with potential application as an antimicrobial liner.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-34848-w.

Introduction

Food-borne pathogens represent one of the major global challenges to public health and food safety. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), contaminated food accounts for approximately 600 million cases of foodborne illness and 420,000 deaths each year worldwide. Notably, nearly 30% of these deaths occur in children under five years of age1. Furthermore, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), approximately 1,600 cases of listeriosis are reported annually in the United States, leading to nearly 260 deaths2.

These microorganisms not only pose a serious threat to human health but also impose significant economic burdens on the food industry, particularly through product recalls. For example, in 2023 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recalled more than 400 products due to potential Listeria contamination3. Listeria monocytogenes (L. monocytogenes) is a pathogenic bacterium that can cause severe illness, particularly in pregnant women, newborns, and immunocompromised individuals. Infection typically occurs through the consumption of contaminated foods. This bacterium is able to grow over a wide range of conditions, including temperatures from 1 to 45 °C, pH values between 4.6 and 9.6, and high salt concentrations, making it extremely difficult to eliminate from foods and food processing environments4. Therefore, researchers are actively seeking strategies to control this pathogen, with electrospinning technology for antimicrobial active packaging emerging as a promising approach. Recent reports by Zhang et al. (2024) and Wang et al. (2024) indicate that publications on this subject have increased substantially since 2017, reflecting the growing scientific and industrial interest in applying electrospinning technology within the food sector5,6.

In addition to microbiological risks, plastic waste from conventional food packaging is a major environmental concern. Packaging accounts for a large share of plastics use (~40%), global plastic production reached ~359 million tonnes in 2018, and only ~9% is recycled. Estimates of plastic entering the oceans vary, but reported values range from ~4.8-12.7 million tonnes (2010) to ~8-10 million tonnes per year, threatening marine ecosystems and food chains. These impacts highlight the need for sustainable, biodegradable, and antimicrobial packaging to reduce pollution and improve food safety 7,8.

Recent investigations by Dai et al. (2022), Yu et al. (2023), Guan et al. (2024), and Li et al. (2024) have explored the potential of electrospinning technology for developing active antimicrobial food packaging materials. These studies reported encouraging outcomes, including effective inhibition of bacterial growth and extension of food shelf life. Nevertheless, additional research is required to optimize the design and functionality of such systems before they can be widely adopted in the food industry9–12. Antimicrobial-active food packaging incorporates bioactive ingredients and polymers capable of inhibiting or killing bacteria, including L. monocytogenes. Micro and nano-encapsulation of these bioactive compounds can protect them from inactivation by factors such as enzymes. Consequently, the use of biological preservatives reduces the reliance on chemical preservatives13,14.

Electrospinning is a scalable and reproducible technique that employs an electric field to generate continuous nanofibers from a polymer solution. This method enables the fabrication of food packaging materials capable of providing a sustained release of antimicrobial agents over time15. Unlike conventional film-forming approaches (such as casting, dip-coating, or extrusion) and fiber-forming methods (such as spray drying and melt spinning), which typically require high processing temperatures, electrospinning allows the incorporation of thermo-sensitive bioactive compounds into the polymer matrix without degradation16. Additional advantages of this technique include the ability to control porosity and nanofiber diameters, as well as the production of structures with a high specific surface area6,17.

On the other hand, nisin is a polypeptide bacteriocin produced by Lactococcus lactis, and it has been employed as a natural preservative in the food industry since the 1950s18. However, its antimicrobial activity can be reduced through interactions with food components such as proteolytic enzymes and fat compounds19. To achieve the desired antimicrobial effect, large amounts of nisin are often required, which is not only costly but may also encourage the development of resistant bacterial subpopulations20. Several studies have suggested that incorporating nisin into microcapsules, liposomes, or nanofibers, and co-encapsulating it with other antimicrobial agents such as essential oils, can enhance its bioactivity and protect it from inactivation21. Thyme essential oil is rich in phenolic compounds like thymol and carvacrol, is widely recognized for its natural antimicrobial and antioxidant properties, making it an effective natural preservative for food products22. Studies have shown that thyme essential oil exhibits strong antimicrobial activity against various foodborne pathogens, including Listeria monocytogenes23. The combination of nisin and thymol enhanced antibacterial activity against Listeria monocytogenes and Bacillus subtilis, allowing effective control with lower nisin levels and reducing the risk of resistant sub-populations24. Moreover, research demonstrates a synergistic antimicrobial effect when combining nisin (a bacteriocin) with thyme essential oil against L. monocytogenes, enhancing the preservation effect25. Furthermore, the combined effect of carvacrol, thymol, and nisin against Staphylococcus aureus and Salmonella Enteritidis showed significant inhibition of these pathogens. This supports the antimicrobial potential of combining thymol with nisin26.

Therefore, in this study, we incorporated both nisin and thyme essential oil into electrospun nanofibers. Building on our previous works in nanomaterial synthesis and their applications in biomedical fields27–31, the present study aimed to fabricate electrospun double-layer mats with broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against both L. monocytogenes and E. coli. The inner layer was designed as a hydrophilic CS/PVA nanofiber matrix loaded with nisin and Shirazi thyme essential oil, while the outer layer consisted of a hydrophobic cellulose acetate/thyme essential oil film. This novel double-layer design with asymmetric wettability was intended to promote directional and sustained release of bioactive compounds. Furthermore, the co-encapsulation of nisin and thyme essential oil was designed to exploit their synergy, extending antimicrobial activity from L. monocytogenes to E. coli. The mats were characterized for morphology, physicochemical, and mechanical properties, and their antibacterial efficacy was evaluated.

Materials and methods

Materials

Thyme essential oil (Shirazi thyme; Tabibdaru Co., Tehran, Iran) was used as received. Nisin (from Lactococcus lactis, CAS 1414-45-5, food-grade) and chitosan (medium molecular weight, CAS 9012-76-4; Sigma-Aldrich, 448877) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Poly (vinyl alcohol) (PVA; CAS 9002-89-5, Mw 89,000–98,000 g.mol⁻¹, ≥ 99% hydrolyzed; Sigma-Aldrich, 341584) was also obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Cellulose acetate (CA; CAS 9004-35-7, code 6180955) was supplied by Titrachem Co. (Iran). Acetic acid (glacial, CAS 64-19-7), acetone (≥ 99.5%, CAS 67-64-1), and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥ 99.9%, CAS 67–68-5) were purchased from Merck (Germany) and used without further purification. Deionized water was used to prepare all aqueous solutions.

Preparation of electrospinning solutions

Preparation of the first layer solution (CA/EO Sample)

To prepare the first-layer solution, cellulose acetate (16% w/v) was dissolved in a solvent mixture of acetone and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) at a 1:2 ratio. Subsequently, thyme essential oil (3% w/v) was added to the solution and stirred for 1 h at room temperature32.

Preparation of the second layer solution (CS/PVA/N/EO)

To prepare the second-layer solution, poly (vinyl alcohol) (11% w/v) was dissolved in deionized water under magnetic stirring at 90 °C for 1 h. Separately, chitosan (2% w/v) was dissolved in acetic acid and stirred for 30 min, after which nisin was added at a concentration of 15 mg/mL and mixed for an additional 30 min. After both polymers were dissolved, the poly (vinyl alcohol) solution was combined with the chitosan/nisin solution at a 30:70 ratio and stirred for 1 h. Subsequently, thyme essential oil (3% w/v) was added to the mixture and stirred for 30 min. Finally, the resulting solution was sonicated for 1 min to remove air bubbles (Table 1)33.

Synthesis of electrospun nanofiber (Electrospinning Process)

An electrospinning device (Nanomeghyas, Iran) was employed, with solutions separately loaded into 3 mL syringes (16-gauge needles) and mounted on a syringe pump. For fabrication of the double-layer sample, the CA/EO layer was electrospun first, followed immediately by the second nanofibrous layer. The optimized electrospinning parameters for the first layer were an applied voltage of 15 kV, a flow rate of 1 mL/h, and a needle-to-collector distance of 15 cm. For the second layer, the optimal conditions were a voltage of 28 kV, a flow rate of 0.5 mL/h, and a needle-to-collector distance of 15 cm.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The morphology of the films (1 × 1 cm) was examined using scanning electron microscopy (FEI ESEM QUANTA 200) after coating the samples with a thin layer of gold via PVD sputtering (COXEM, South Korea). Average fiber diameters were measured using ImageJ software, and the diameter distribution was analyzed by fitting a Gaussian curve with OriginLab software.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR and ATR-FTIR)

FT-IR spectra of chitosan powder, nisin, and thyme essential oil, as well as ATR-FTIR spectra of PVA, cellulose acetate, and nanofiber samples, were recorded in the wavenumber range of 500–4000 cm⁻¹ using a PerkinElmer FTIR spectrometer. The analysis was performed to identify functional groups and assess potential structural interactions among the polymers and active substances.

Thermogravimetric analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of electrospun CS/PVA, CS/PVA/N, and CS/PVA/N/EO samples was carried out using a TA Instruments SDT-Q600 under an argon atmosphere, in the temperature range of 25–600 °C, with a heating rate of 10 °C/min.

Water contact angle

Water contact angle measurements of CS/PVA, CS/PVA/N, CS/PVA/N/EO, and double-layer samples were performed using a CA-500 A contact angle analyzer (Sharif Solar, Iran) following the static sessile drop method at room temperature. A 12 µL droplet of distilled water was carefully placed on the surface of each sample, and images were captured with a high-resolution camera. The contact angles were then analyzed using ImageJ software.

Mechanical testing method and thickness measurement of the samples

The mechanical properties of the electrospun mats were evaluated by tensile testing using a universal testing machine (Santam Co., Iran). Rectangular specimens with dimensions of 5.5 × 2.5 cm were prepared and clamped in the grips of the tester. The tests were conducted at a crosshead speed of 5 mm/min under ambient conditions. Tensile strength (TS), Young’s modulus (YM), and elongation at break (EB) were calculated from the stress–strain curves. The thickness of the samples was measured using a digital micrometer (Mitutoyo Co., Japan) with an accuracy of 0.001 mm. For each specimen, measurements were taken at three different points, and the average value was reported34.

Antibacterial activity assay

The antimicrobial activity of the nanobiocomposite mats against Listeria monocytogenes (ATCC 19111) and Escherichia coli O157:H7 was evaluated using the agar diffusion and dynamic shake-flask methods.

Agar diffusion (Qualitative Measurement)

Agar diffusion tests were employed to qualitatively assess the presence and activity of antimicrobial compounds. In this study, the antimicrobial properties of the mats against L. monocytogenes and E. coli were evaluated following the Kirby-Bauer protocol35. Bacterial suspensions were prepared by inoculating target strains in Mueller-Hinton Broth under sterile conditions and incubating at 37 °C for 24 h. The suspensions were then adjusted to a final concentration of 1.5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL. Subsequently, the microbial suspension was uniformly spread on Mueller-Hinton Agar plates (for E. coli) and Mueller-Hinton Agar supplemented with 5% blood (for L. monocytogenes). Square mats (1 × 1 cm) were carefully placed on the inoculated surfaces and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, after which the diameters of inhibition zones (mm) were measured.

Dynamic shake flask test (Quantitative Measurement)

The dynamic shake flask (DSF) assay was conducted according to ASTM E2149, with minor modifications36. Three types of mats double-layered nanobiocomposite, CS/PVA/N/EO, and CA/EO were placed individually into Erlenmeyer flasks containing 50 mL of Mueller-Hinton Broth inoculated with 3 × 10³ CFU/mL of bacteria. The flasks were then incubated at 37 °C with continuous shaking at 80 rpm (Fig. 1). Bacterial counts were determined at 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h using serial dilution and the plate count method. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and comparative analysis between the groups were conducted with one-way analysis of variance ANOVA (p ≤ 0.05 significance level) and Duncan post hoc test by SPSS statistical software, (IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 26). Also, results were reported as the average ± standard deviation (SD).

Results and discussion

Morphology of the electrospun nanofibers

Figures 2 and 3 present the scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the electrospun mats. Figure 2 shows SEM images of three nanofiber samples: chitosan/poly (vinyl alcohol) (CS/PVA), CS/PVA with nisin (CS/PVA/N), and CS/PVA with nisin and essential oil (CS/PVA/N/EO). All samples exhibited uniform fiber distribution with no bead formation. The mean diameter of CS/PVA nanofibers was 125.7 ± 18.97 nm, which is consistent with previous reports. For instance, Hedayatyanfard. et al. (2019) reported a diameter of 104 ± 24.99 nm for nanofibers containing 3% chitosan and 5% PVA (80:20 ratio)37. Mei et al. (2019) observed a mean diameter of 118.49 ± 10 nm for nanofibers of 3% chitosan and 8% PVA (50:50 ratio)38. Similarly, Sosiati et al. (2020) reported 198 nm for nanofibers of 3% chitosan and 10% PVA (80:20 ratio)39, while Agrawal and Pramanik (2016) found 264 nm for nanofibers prepared from 2% chitosan and 10% PVA (30:70 ratio)33.

Accordingly, the diameter of chitosan–PVA nanofibers has generally been reported in the range of 100–270 nm, with variations attributed to factors such as polymer concentration, viscosity, and molecular weight. Upon the addition of 3% nisin (CS/PVA/N), the fiber diameter increased significantly to 142.60 ± 28.14 nm (p < 0.05). However, incorporating essential oil did not cause a significant further increase, and the mean diameter of CS/PVA/N/EO nanofibers was calculated as 144.8 ± 28.33 nm. The electrospun cellulose acetate (CA) nanofibers exhibited a mean diameter of 270 ± 52.47 nm, which shifted upon addition of 3% essential oil, resulting in the formation of a bioplastic layer (Fig. 3)37–39. The diameters of the electrospun nanofibers were measured using ImageJ software based on SEM images, as presented in the Supplementary Information.

FTIR analysis

Figure 4A shows the FTIR spectrum of nisin. The broad band at 3268 cm⁻¹ arises from O–H/N–H stretching. The band near 2900 cm⁻¹ corresponds to symmetric and asymmetric C–H stretching vibrations. The peak at 1677 cm⁻¹ is assigned to Amide I (C = O stretching) of peptide bonds, while the band at ~ 1540–1560 cm⁻¹ (weak in this spectrum) represents Amide II (N–H bending + C–N stretching). The signal at 1033 cm⁻¹ is attributed to C–O/C–N stretching.

Figure 4B illustrates the infrared spectrum of Shirazi thyme essential oil. Thymol is the main component of this essential oil, but it also contains other phenolic compounds. The peak at wavenumber 3537 cm− 1 represents the stretching vibration of the hydroxyl group (O–H). The strong peak at wavenumber 2962 cm− 1 is attributed to C–H vibrations in aromatic compounds, while peaks at wavenumbers 2927 cm− 1 and 2870 cm− 1 represent symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of CH3 groups. The presence of peaks in the range of 1516 cm− 1 to 1619 cm− 1 indicating C = C stretching vibration, confirms the presence of aromatic rings. The bending vibrations within and out of the plane of the aromatic rings result in peaks in the range of 1459 cm− 1 to 1257 cm− 1. The FTIR results for Shirazi thyme essential oil are consistent with those reported by Catauro et al.40.

ATR-FTIR spectra of CS, PVA, CS/PVA, CS/PVA/N, and CS/PVA/N/EO are shown in Figs. 4C–G. All samples display a broad O–H band, which becomes broader in the nanofibrous mats due to hydrogen bonding and overlap with N–H vibrations of chitosan, as described by Choo et al.41. The symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of C–H (CH2) can be observed in the chitosan, poly (vinyl alcohol), and nanofibrous samples at wavenumbers of around 2900 cm− 1. A carbonyl band at ~ 1731–1715 cm⁻¹ is observed in PVA-containing samples, originating from residual acetate groups of PVA; its slight shift and intensity change in the blends indicates interactions (hydrogen bonding) with chitosan and additives. The band at ~ 1560 cm⁻¹ in Figs. 4E–G corresponds to Amide II (N–H bending + C–N stretching) of chitosan, which is also consistent with the findings of Choo et al.41. Infrared spectroscopy results for cellulose acetate (CA) and CA/EO samples are shown in Fig. 5. Both spectra exhibit a broad O–H stretching band at 3378–3380 cm⁻¹ and symmetric/asymmetric C–H stretching vibrations near 2930–2932 cm⁻¹. In the CA spectrum, a strong absorption band at 1742 cm⁻¹ was observed, corresponding to the carbonyl (C = O) stretching vibration of the ester groups, which is a typical feature of cellulose acetate. The bands at 1028 cm⁻¹ were assigned to C–O stretching vibrations of ester groups. In the CA/EO spectrum, however, the intensity of the ester carbonyl band decreased significantly and a weaker, broadened absorption appeared at 1656 cm⁻¹, which can be considered a shifted or perturbed C = O stretching band due to strong intermolecular interactions. Furthermore, the C–O stretching band was slightly shifted to 1182 cm⁻¹ in the CA/EO sample. These spectral changes confirm the incorporation of EO into the CA matrix and suggest molecular interactions, likely hydrogen bonding, between the functional groups of cellulose acetate and the bioactive components of the essential oil.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The thermal stability of CS/PVA, CS/PVA/N, and CS/PVA/N/EO nanofiber mats was investigated by TGA in the range of 25–800 °C under an argon atmosphere at a heating rate of 10 °C/min (Fig. 6). The curves are reported as percentage of the initial mass. The CS/PVA sample exhibits three distinct weight-loss stages. The first stage, below ~ 170 °C, corresponds to the evaporation of physically adsorbed and bound water (~ 5% loss). The second stage, between ~ 170 and 380 °C, is attributed to the thermal decomposition of the polymeric component’s poly (vinyl alcohol) dehydration (~ 200 °C) and chitosan deacetylation (~ 290 °C) leading to ~ 60–65% mass loss. The third stage, from ~ 380 to 600 °C, is assigned to main-chain degradation and char formation, resulting in an additional ~ 25–30% loss. These features agree well with previous reports by Olvera-Bernal et al.46.

For the CS/PVA/N and CS/PVA/N/EO mats, an additional early mass-loss event is observed below ~ 220 °C, associated with nisin degradation and volatilization of thyme essential-oil constituents. At higher temperatures, the decomposition of PVA and chitosan overlaps, producing a broad degradation region between ~ 200 and 600 °C. The EO-loaded sample shows a larger early loss and a slightly lower onset temperature, indicating a modest reduction in thermal stability compared with CS/PVA and CS/PVA/N.

Contact angle test (Surface Wettability)

The degree of surface hydrophilicity or hydrophobicity is commonly evaluated by the contact angle test. A contact angle greater than 90° indicates a hydrophobic surface, whereas a lower value reflects a hydrophilic surface. As shown in Fig. 7, the contact angle of CS/PVA nanofibers, which are inherently hydrophilic polymers, was measured at 46.53°. Upon incorporation of nisin protein, the contact angle increased significantly to 64.5° (p < 0.05). Further addition of 3% thyme essential oil raised the contact angle to 72.43°, attributable to the hydrophobic and oily characteristics of thyme essential oil. A similar trend was observed with cellulose acetate, where the contact angle increased from 63.8° (CA sample) to 95.5° (CA/EO sample) after the addition of essential oil. For food packaging applications, the inner surface is required to be hydrophilic to enable the release of nisin and essential oils, whereas the outer layer should be waterproof and hydrophobic to prevent moisture transmission. Consequently, a higher degree of hydrophobicity in the outer layer is preferable.

Analysis of the mechanical properties and thickness

The mechanical properties and thickness of the samples are summarized in Table 3. We evaluated tensile strength (TS), Young’s modulus (YM), and elongation at break (EB), which characterize film strength, stiffness, and ductility, respectively. Tensile strength (TS) is the maximum stress sustained before failure; Young’s modulus (YM) is the slope of the initial linear region of the stress–strain curve, reflecting stiffness; and elongation at break (EB) is the strain at fracture, typically reported as a percentage.

Adding 15 mg/mL nisin to the CS/PVA blend did not significantly affect tensile strength, whereas Young’s modulus increased from 17.40 to 18.66 MPa and elongation at break increased from 25.76% to 28.79% (p ≤ 0.05). Incorporation of Thyme essential oil into CS/PVA/N markedly increased stiffness, with Young’s modulus rising from 18.66 to 47.61 MPa, and reduced ductility, as elongation at break decreased from 28.79% to 11.36%, while tensile strength remained statistically unchanged. In the cellulose acetate system, EO loading increased both tensile strength from 1.89 to 4.50 MPa and Young’s modulus from 42.53 to 171.65 MPa, while elongation at break was reduced to 0.67%. The double-layer film exhibited intermediate behavior: tensile strength and Young’s modulus were lower than those of the EO-reinforced single layers but comparable to or higher than the neat CS/PVA-based films, while elongation at break (21.66%) exceeded that of CA, CA/EO, and CS/PVA/N/EO yet remained below CS/PVA and CS/PVA/N (Fig. 8). Thicknesses ranged between 0.10 and 0.36 mm, with the double-layer being the thickest and CA/EO the thinnest49.

Antimicrobial activity

Disk diffusion test

The antimicrobial activity of the fabricated biocomposite mats was evaluated using the disk diffusion method. Initially, the antimicrobial properties of nisin powder were assessed by the same test. As shown in Table 4, pure nisin exhibited no inhibition against Gram-negative E. coli but was effective against Gram-positive L. monocytogenes. Similarly, the CS/PVA sample (negative control) showed no antimicrobial activity. Therefore, the inhibition zone observed in the CS/PVA/N mat can be attributed to the incorporation of nisin.

Upon the addition of 3% thyme essential oil (CS/PVA/N/EO sample), the anti-Listeria effect increased significantly, as the inhibition zone expanded from 11.68 ± 1.34 mm to 23 ± 1.95 mm. Moreover, owing to the synergistic interaction of thyme essential oil with nisin, this sample also exhibited inhibitory activity against E. coli. In contrast, CA and CA/EO mats did not show antibacterial activity. This result can be explained by the plasticizing effect of thyme essential oil, which increases hydrophobicity and hydrogen bond formation, thus hindering the diffusion of active compounds into the medium. Consequently, the double-layer mat exhibited a larger inhibition zone compared with its single-layer counterpart (Fig. 9).

In line with the initial aim of this study to fabricate electrospun double-layer mats with antimicrobial activity against both L. monocytogenes and E. coli the findings clearly demonstrate that the developed mats successfully inhibited both pathogens, confirming the effectiveness of combining nisin with Shirazi thyme essential oil in a double-layer structure.

Dynamic shake flask

Table 5; Fig. 10 present the time-kill curves of L. monocytogenes at 0, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h, with an initial inoculation level of 3.47 log CFU/mL. The results indicate that the L. monocytogenes population in the control sample (without composite nanofiber mats) increased from 3.47 to 9.58 ± 0.31 log CFU/mL after 48 h. In contrast, in the Double-layer (Two layer) nanobiocomposite sample, the bacterial population decreased significantly, and growth was completely inhibited for 48 h. It should be noted that in colony counting, when 100 µL of the diluted suspension is plated, the detection limit is 2 log CFU/mL. Therefore, the absence of colonies on the plate indicates that the bacterial count is below the detection limit, rather than zero. Moreover, the results show that the antimicrobial activity of the single-layer samples (CS/PVA/N/EO and CA/EO) was lower than that of the Double-layer samples but higher than the control. The double-layer structure, with its asymmetric wettability, enables directional release of active agents, improved diffusion compared with CA/EO alone, and benefits from the well-documented synergy between nisin and thyme essential oil. These mechanisms are consistent with our results, where the double-layer mats exhibited larger inhibition zones.

Potential application in commercial food packaging

The developed double-layer mat is designed as an active component that can be integrated into existing commercial packs rather than replacing the entire rigid or barrier structure. It can be used as a tray liner or pad inside conventional lidded trays, applied as a thin electrospun coating laminated onto food-contact films (e.g., PET/PE, PP, or paperboard), or employed as an inner wrap for ready-to-eat products. The double-layer mat exhibited strong antimicrobial activity in both agar diffusion and dynamic tests, confirming its effectiveness against foodborne pathogens. Its layered design provided controlled release on the food-contact side and moisture resistance on the outer surface. Mechanical evaluation also indicated sufficient flexibility and strength for handling and application. Moreover, the use of food-grade polymers and natural bioactives, verified by structural and thermal analyses, demonstrated that the material is both safe and stable under conditions relevant to food packaging. In practice, the mats can be electrospun directly onto carrier films or supplied as precut pads, enabling direct substitution of the absorbent pad or liner commonly used in meat, poultry, or ready-to-eat cheese and delicatessen products with antimicrobial versions that provide in-pack protection, particularly against L. monocytogenes in short shelf-life refrigerated foods. Although the present findings demonstrate promising antimicrobial potential, full commercial implementation will require additional validation, including migration testing, sensory evaluation, and barrier performance benchmarking, which are standard steps in qualifying new active packaging materials5,50,51. From an industrial perspective, the double-layer mat aligns with scalable electrospinning routes (multi-nozzle and free-surface systems demonstrated at pilot scale) and can be integrated as drop-in liners or coated inserts on existing PET/PE or paperboard lines, which is typically more cost-effective than full structure replacement. Commercialization will require migration and toxicological assessments of actives, compliance with EU and FDA food-contact frameworks, and barrier benchmarking alongside sensory and consumer-acceptance studies for essential-oil–based systems. These industrial aspects will be addressed in future work, including preliminary techno-economic considerations6,15,52,53. Figure 11 shows the process of the electrospun antimicrobial mat.

Conclusion

This study established an electrospun double-layer antimicrobial mat in which a hydrophilic CS/PVA inner layer loaded with nisin and Shirazi thyme essential oil is combined with a hydrophobic CA/EO outer layer. A coherent set of morphological, spectroscopic, thermal, wettability, and mechanical analyses confirmed successful incorporation of the bioactives, process-relevant stability, and asymmetric surface functionality. The CS/PVA-based inner layer formed bead-free nanofibers with an average diameter of ~145 nm, and the CA/EO outer layer exhibited a bioplastic film. Contact angles of ~ 95°, and contributed to overall film integrity with an elongation at break of 21.66 ± 0.62%, indicating adequate flexibility for handling and conversion. Across complementary antimicrobial assays, the double-layer mat consistently inhibited both Listeria monocytogenes and Escherichia coli. In agar diffusion tests, inhibition zones reached 27.26 ± 1.60 mm for L. monocytogenes and 25.13 ± 1.40 mm for E. coli, clearly outperforming single-layer controls. In the dynamic shake-flask assay, a 5 × 5 cm pad reduced L. monocytogenes by approximately 2 logs and maintained bacterial counts below the detection limit for 48 h. The study confirms the synergy between nisin and thyme essential oil and highlights the double-layer design’s role in directional release.

The developed mat can be easily integrated into existing chilled food packaging systems to control L. monocytogenes contamination. Such double-layer electrospun systems are consistent with contemporary trends in active and sustainable food packaging, where antimicrobial nanofiber mats are being explored not only to improve microbiological safety but also to reduce food spoilage, product recalls, and associated food waste along the supply chain. In this context, electrospun active packaging is increasingly discussed as a tool to support circular and greener food systems and to align with broader sustainability and food-security goals. Recent work on advanced electrospun structures for active and smart packaging, as well as analyses of their contribution to more resilient and sustainable value chains, reinforces the industrial relevance of the approach demonstrated in this study.

This work nevertheless has several limitations. Antimicrobial performance was evaluated in broth-based model systems using two reference strains, without testing in real food matrices or under variable storage conditions. In addition, migration behavior and release kinetics of nisin and essential oil, sensory impacts, barrier properties, and techno-economic aspects were not assessed. Future studies should therefore (i) validate the mats in representative foods such as ready-to-eat meats, cheeses, and minimally processed produce; (ii) quantify release and migration in relation to regulatory limits and evaluate sensory acceptability; (iii) integrate the double-layer concept with fully biodegradable supports and scalable needleless or multi-nozzle electrospinning; and (iv) explore incorporation of additional functionalities, such as antioxidant or colorimetric indicator components, coupled with life-cycle and techno-economic assessments, to move towards smart, sustainable active packaging solutions suitable for industrial implementation.
