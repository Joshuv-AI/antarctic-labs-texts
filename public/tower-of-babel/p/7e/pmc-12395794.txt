Association of early hyperglycemia with morbidity and mortality in very low birth weight infants
Authors: Jingwen Zhu, Xiyu He, Ming Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the association between hyperglycemia and morbidity/mortality in very-low-birth-weight infants.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed 255 very-low-birth-weight infants admitted to a Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (2020–2024). Infants were stratified by glucose levels: hyperglycemia (> 150 mg/dL) vs. normoglycemia (>47 mg/dl, ≤ 150 mg/dL). Hyperglycemic infants were further categorized by duration (≤ 72 vs. >72 h) and severity (150–220 vs. ≥ 220 mg/dL). Outcomes included retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), and mortality. Logistic regression was used to assess independent associations.ResultsHyperglycemia occurred in 44.7% (114 of 255) of infants. Compared to normoglycemic infants, hyperglycemic infants had higher incidences of ROP (p < 0.001), IVH (p < 0.001), and mortality ( p = 0.046). Logistic regression confirmed hyperglycemia as an significantly associated factor of ROP and IVH (p < 0.05) but not mortality (p = 0.777). Prolonged hyperglycemia (> 72 h) increased ROP risk (p < 0.05), while severe hyperglycemia (≥ 220 mg/dL) elevated IVH risk ( p < 0.05).ConclusionsHyperglycemia in very-low-birth-weight infants is associated with higher incidences of ROP and IVH, with both duration and severity influencing the risk. Strict glucose monitoring and timely intervention are crucial to reduce these complications.

Introduction

Glucose is essential for the growth and development of fetuses and neonates. Failure to maintain sufficient glucose availability or proper glucose metabolism in newborns can result in either hypoglycemia or hyperglycemia. These conditions are associated with significant adverse outcomes, one of which is an increased risk of brain damage [1]. The diagnostic criteria for neonatal hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia remain controversial [2–5], primarily due to the unclear association between blood glucose levels and the manifestation of clinical symptoms or long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Hyperglycemia is commonly defined as a blood glucose level exceeding 150 mg/dL [6]. Preterm birth is a major contributing factor to hyperglycemia development. The condition demonstrates high prevalence among preterm infants, with reported incidence rates ranging from 40 to 80% [7]. Multiple studies have established significant associations between hyperglycemia and adverse outcomes in preterm infants, including intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), sepsis, retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), and necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) [8, 9]. Existing literature consistently documents that hyperglycemia in preterm infants primarily manifests during the first postnatal week [10–14] and represents an independent risk factor for increased mortality and morbidity [15]. This study aimed to: (1) identify risk factors associated with prenatal and postnatal hyperglycemia in extremely preterm infants, and (2) evaluate the relationship between early-onset hyperglycemia (occurring within 7 days postbirth) and morbidity and mortality rates. To minimize confounding factors, we excluded preterm infants who developed grade III/IV IVH or severe sepsis during the early postnatal period, as these conditions may independently induce hyperglycemia. Our findings may provide clinically relevant insights for improving outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study included very-low-birth-weight infants admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) at the Fifth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital between January 2020 and December 2024. Clinical data were systematically collected from electronic medical records.

Inclusion criteria:1. Preterm infants admitted within 6 h after birth; 2. Gestational age < 32 weeks; 3.Birth weight < 1500 g; 4. Hyperglycemia occurring within 7 days after birth. Exclusion criteria: (1) Severe asphyxia or major congenital malformations; (2) Critical conditions such as IVH (grade III or IV), shock, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), or other life-threatening illnesses that occurred within the first 7 postnatal days; (3) Incomplete neonatal medical records or transfer; (4) Death that occurred within the first week of life.

Based on blood glucose levels, the infants were divided into two groups: the hyperglycemia group (blood glucose > 150 mg/dL) and the normoglycemia group (47 mg/dl< blood glucose ≤ 150 mg/dL) [16, 17]. The study aimed to compare the clinical characteristics, disease incidence, and mortality rates between these two groups.

Within the hyperglycemia group, infants were further stratified by the duration of hyperglycemia into a short-term group (≤ 72 h) and a long-term group (> 72 h). They were also classified by the severity of hyperglycemia into mild (blood glucose > 150 mg/dL but < 220 mg/dL) and severe (blood glucose ≥ 220 mg/dL) subgroups [16]. The study further examined the associations between the duration and severity of hyperglycemia and the incidence of diseases and mortality rates in preterm infants within the hyperglycemia group.

Data collection

Demographic and clinical data

Gestational age, birth weight, maternal complications (including gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP), premature rupture of membranes (PROM), mode of delivery, placental abruption, oligohydramnios, amniotic fluid contamination, 1-minute and 5-minute Apgar scores, prenatal steroid exposure, assisted reproductive technology (ART), respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), early-onset sepsis(EOS), hemodynamically significant patent ductus arteriosus (hsPDA), necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), retinopathy of prematurity (ROP), and mortality.

Ethics

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Fifth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital (approval number: KY-2025-3-42-1), with a waiver of informed consent. The retrospective analysis was conducted in strict accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 version).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp.). Categorical variables were expressed as number (percentage), and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, whichever was appropriate. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR). Group comparisons for non-normally distributed variables were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to assess the associations between variables.

Results

A cohort of 268 very low birth weight (VLBW) infants was initially enrolled in the study. After applying exclusion criteria, 13 neonates were excluded: 2 for admission exceeding 12 h post-delivery, 5 due to parental treatment withdrawal resulting in automatic discharge, and 6 requiring transfer for surgical interventions. The final analysis included 255 eligible VLBW infants.

The study population was stratified into two groups based on glycemic status: 141 infants (55.3%) comprised the normoglycemic group and 114 (44.7%) the hyperglycemic group (Fig. 1). The cohort had a mean gestational age of 29.7 ± 1.5 weeks (mean ± SD) with an average birth weight of 1,354 ± 145 g. Correlation analysis

The study revealed statistically significant differences in the incidence of ROP, IVH, and mortality between the two groups. To further investigate the potential association between hyperglycemia and these outcomes, we performed multivariable logistic regression analyses to assess the relationship between ROP, IVH, mortality, and their potential risk factors.

Logistic regression analysis of risk factors associated with ROP

Using the ROP as the dependent variable, covariates were selected based on the Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) for ROP, including variables with a direct causal influence on ROP : Gestational Age, Birth Weight, Asphyxia, Duration of oxygen therapy, EOS, Maximum blood glucose, and Transfusion.

Logistic regression analysis identified hyperglycemia as an significantly associated factor for ROP development (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.217, 95% CI: 1.059–1.400, p = 0.006), as shown in Fig. 2. Discussion

Hyperglycemia represents a prevalent metabolic disorder in preterm neonates, with substantial clinical evidence demonstrating its significant association with both morbidity and mortality. Kermorvant-Duchemin et al. (2020) demonstrated a significant correlation between elevated blood glucose levels and the onset of ROP (p < 0.001) [19]. A retrospective study of 72 preterm infants (gestational age < 32 weeks) demonstrated that the hyperglycemia group had significantly higher rates of intraventricular hemorrhage (p = 0.007), retinopathy of prematurity (p = 0.047), and mortality (p = 0.001) [20]. Another study involving 798 extremely preterm infants demonstrated that hyperglycemic infants had significantly higher mortality (p = 0.001) [21]. Hays et al. demonstrated that blood glucose concentration significantly influenced the occurrence of early mortality and severe IVH [9].

The precise pathogenesis of hyperglycemia in preterm infants remains to be fully elucidated. Current evidence indicates that elevated levels of inflammatory mediators (including tumor necrosis factor-α [TNF-α], interleukin-1 [IL-1], and interleukin-6 [IL-6]) and catecholamines may induce insulin resistance through modulation of insulin receptor signaling pathways, leading to impaired insulin sensitivity in the liver, skeletal muscle, and adipose tissue [22, 23]. Preterm infants exhibit impaired glucose-stimulated insulin secretion, primarily attributable to reduced β-cell mass and functional immaturity, which is associated with low expression of glucose transporter 2 (GLUT2) [24]. While preterm infants typically require intravenous glucose administration, unlike adults, their endogenous glucose production remains unsuppressed. This phenomenon may result from immature GLUT2 expression in the liver, leading to persistent hepatic glucose production, coupled with reduced glucose uptake in insulin-sensitive tissues (e.g., adipose tissue and skeletal muscle) due to their low GLUT4 expression [24].

Among 255 VLBW infants, 114 (44.7%) developed hyperglycemia in the first week. The hyperglycemic group had significantly lower gestational age than normoglycemic infants (p < 0.01) (Table 1). The rationale is that reduced pancreatic β-cell mass at lower gestational ages leads to impaired insulin secretion and decreased insulin sensitivity in peripheral tissues, especially in extremely preterm infants (< 28 weeks’ gestation) [25]. A significantly higher risk of hyperglycemia has been reported in extremely preterm neonates (< 28 weeks) compared with their late preterm counterparts (32–34 weeks), with an odds ratio (OR) of 2.5 [26]. Additionally, preterm infants with lower gestational ages have reduced hepatic glycogen stores but exhibit increased activity of key gluconeogenic enzymes. Under stress conditions, this pathway may become hyperactivated and remains unresponsive to blood glucose suppression, resulting in elevated endogenous glucose production [27]. Our study demonstrated that preterm infants in the hyperglycemic group had significantly lower birth weights compared to the normoglycemic group (p < 0.01) (Table 1), indicating an inverse association between birth weight and hyperglycemia. Preterm infants, particularly those with extremely low birth weight (ELBW), exhibit reduced non-fat mass (muscle and organs), limited glycogen stores, and impaired glucose oxidative capacity, rendering them susceptible to hyperglycemia when exogenous glucose administration exceeds their metabolic tolerance [1, 28].

We observed that the duration of oxygen therapy was significantly longer in hyperglycemic infants (p = 0.0017) (Table 1). Hyperglycemia can induce osmotic diuresis and electrolyte disturbances, which may lead to metabolic acidosis. This condition impairs lung function recovery and promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), thereby exacerbating pulmonary inflammation and prolonging the need for oxygen therapy [9, 29].

Our findings demonstrated a significantly prolonged membrane rupture-to-delivery interval in hyperglycemic versus normoglycemic infants (p = 0.031) (Table 1). This observation aligns with van der Lugt et al.‘s (2010) prior report [21], suggesting sepsis-mediated pathophysiology as a plausible mechanism. Specifically, maternal-fetal infections may potentiate the stress response while concurrently suppressing pancreatic β-cell insulin secretion and impairing peripheral glucose utilization [30].

In our univariate analysis, mortality rates differed significantly between hyperglycemic and normoglycemic cases (p = 0.046) (Table 3), a finding consistent with the results reported by Sabzehei et al. [31]. Elevated blood glucose levels may suppress immune system activity, thereby increasing susceptibility to bacterial and fungal infections. As preterm infants already have immature immune systems, hyperglycemia can further impair neutrophil and macrophage function, elevating the risk of severe infections [18]. Additionally, hyperglycemia may exacerbate insulin resistance, promoting accelerated lipolysis and excessive ketone body production. This metabolic cascade can precipitate acidosis, imposing further physiological stress on multiple organ systems [29].

The hyperglycemic group showed significantly increased incidence of both IVH and ROP (p < 0.01 for both) (Table 3). While prematurity itself (characterized by lower gestational age and birth weight) confers inherent vulnerability to ROP and IVH through immature vascular development [32–35], we employed multivariable logistic regression to evaluate hyperglycemia’s independent effect. The models specified ROP/IVH as dependent variables, adjusting for gestational age, birth weight, and other established perinatal risk factors.

Figure 2 shows that hyperglycemia was significantly associated with ROP development in preterm infants (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.217, 95% CI: 1.059–1.400, p = 0.006). Each 1 mmol/L increase in blood glucose was associated with a 21.7% higher risk of ROP. The underlying pathophysiology involves hyperglycemia-mediated activation of HIF-1α signaling and oxidative stress pathways, which synergistically upregulate VEGF expression to drive aberrant retinal angiogenesis [36, 37]. Hyperglycemia may suppress insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) synthesis and activity, a critical regulator of retinal vascular development. IGF-1 deficiency results in arrested vascular growth at immature stages (phase 1 ROP), followed by pathological neovascularization (phase 2 ROP) [38]. Hyperglycemia additionally generates excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) through both the polyol pathway and mitochondrial electron transport chain. This oxidative stress promotes pro-inflammatory cytokine release (including IL-6 and TNF-α), causing retinal vascular endothelial damage, blood-retinal barrier disruption, and ultimately leading to vascular homeostasis impairment and pathological fibrovascular proliferation [39]. Hyperglycemia causes direct retinal vascular endothelial damage through two key mechanisms: (1) accumulation of advanced glycosylation end-products (AGEs) and (2) protein kinase C (PKC) activation. These pathological changes lead to increased vascular permeability and subsequent pathological neovascularization [40, 41].

Figure 3 shows that hyperglycemia was significantly associated with IVH development in preterm infants (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 1.180, 95% CI: 1.027–1.375, p = 0.020). Each 1 mmol/L increase in blood glucose was associated with a 18.0% higher risk of IVH. These findings align with previous reports by Blanco et al. [29] and De Carolis et al. [42]. The hyperglycemia-osmotic diuresis cascade precipitates clinically significant volume depletion and hemodynamic compromise through three interrelated mechanisms: (1) direct osmotic water loss, (2) compensatory fluid shifts, and (3) impaired vascular autoregulation. These hemodynamic perturbations are particularly detrimental to the fragile germinal matrix vasculature in preterm infants, where structural immaturity (deficient pericytes and basal lamina) predisposes to rupture [10]. Hyperglycemia induces oxidative stress through both excessive free radical production and impaired antioxidant defense mechanisms, resulting in vascular endothelial injury and blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption. The concomitant inflammatory response further exacerbates vascular permeability, consequently increasing the risk of hemorrhage [26]. Hyperglycemia further disrupts platelet function and coagulation factor activity, impairing hemostatic capacity. Beardsall et al. (2008) demonstrated that preterm infants with uncontrolled hyperglycemia exhibit higher rates of coagulation abnormalities, while early insulin therapy reduces the incidence of intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH) [29].

Figure 4 demonstrates no significant association between mortality and blood glucose levels (P = 0.777). Due to the limited number of mortality events in this study, the results may be subject to bias, warranting further validation in future multicenter, large-scale studies.

A prespecified subgroup analysis was conducted focusing on hyperglycemic preterm infants. Notably, the severity of hyperglycemia showed a dose-dependent association with IVH incidence, with the most severely hyperglycemic subgroup (glucose > 220 mg/dL) demonstrating significantly elevated IVH rates compared to Mild hyperglycemic infants (150–220 mg/dL) (χ²=19.811, p < 0.001).We hypothesize that acute fluctuations in blood glucose levels may induce rapid plasma osmotic shifts, potentially altering cerebral blood flow dynamics and consequently increasing susceptibility to intracranial hemorrhage [43, 44].

Our subgroup analysis further demonstrated that prolonged duration of hyperglycemia was significantly associated with increased incidence of retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) (p = 0.007). Chronic hyperglycemia induces systemic dysregulation through two interrelated mechanisms: (1) sustained elevation of cytokines (particularly VEGF, IL-6, and TNF-α), and (2) disruption of metabolic homeostasis. In the developing retina, these alterations lead to the characteristic biphasic pathology of ROP - initial vaso-obliteration followed by aberrant neovascularization - representing a progressive, time-dependent disease process. Studies have demonstrated that bovine retinal pigment epithelial cells under chronic hyperglycemic conditions produce significantly higher levels of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) compared with controls, and this elevated VEGF expression can drive pathological retinal vascular changes [45]. Velamizarr et al. reported consistent findings, demonstrating that persistent hyperglycemia (≥ 5 days) exerted significant adverse physiological effects (including ocular diseases and infections), whereas shorter durations (< 5 days) showed no clinically relevant impact [46].

Hyperglycemia represents a complex entity, potentially acting as both a cause and a consequence of adverse outcomes. As a causative factor, it may contribute to complications such as altered blood osmolarity, hemodynamic instability (potentially linked to intraventricular hemorrhage, IVH), and vascular inflammation (an early driver in retinopathy of prematurity, ROP development). Conversely, hyperglycemia can also arise secondary to severe conditions like sepsis or IVH itself. Consequently, distinguishing its temporal and causal role is critical for interpreting our findings.

Within our study design, hyperglycemia was assessed during the first postnatal week. ROP diagnosis followed standardized screening initiated at week 4, establishing hyperglycemia exposure prior to ROP identification. IVH was diagnosed via cranial ultrasound on postnatal days 3–7. To prioritize temporal precedence of hyperglycemia over severe IVH, infants developing severe (grade III-IV) IVH within the first week were excluded. While this enhanced internal validity regarding temporal sequence, it potentially limits generalizability. Notably, no enrolled infant experienced grade III-IV IVH during this initial week. Consequently, sensitivity analysis yielded results identical to the primary analysis, bolstering the robustness of our main findings and substantially mitigating concerns of reverse causality as the primary explanation for the observed associations.Precisely delineating exposure-outcome temporality remains challenging in critically ill infants experiencing rapid clinical changes, despite our efforts to define clear time windows.

We recognize the profound association between ROP/IVH development and the underlying severity of prematurity-related conditions, which are themselves key precipitants of hyperglycemia. To address this critical confounding, our multivariable regression models rigorously adjusted for key markers of disease severity and prematurity vulnerability, including gestational age, birth weight, duration of oxygen exposure, early-onset sepsis, and vasopressor use. Importantly, the association between hyperglycemia and ROP/IVH remained statistically significant after stepwise adjustment for these potential confounders. Limitations inherent to our observational design must be acknowledged. Residual confounding due to unmeasured or imperfectly measured factors cannot be entirely excluded.

The strengths of our study include: (1) the study’s purpose is concisely stated, focusing on a clinically relevant question (hyperglycemia’s impact on VLBW infants); (2) the stratification by duration and severity of hyperglycemia enhances analytical depth.

Several limitations should be acknowledged: First, the single-center design, relatively small sample size (n = 255) and the exclusion of infants with severe IVH or severe sepsis may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, as a retrospective study, our analysis may not have fully accounted for all potential confounding factors.Third, certain parameters (e.g., glucose measurement frequency, timing of IVH) were extracted from medical records, potentially introducing measurement variability or incomplete data. Fourth, intermittent capillary blood glucose monitoring was employed rather than continuous measurement, which may reduce detection of glycemic variability. Fifth, the study was limited to short-term outcomes and lacked longitudinal assessment of long-term neurodevelopmental effects, particularly cognitive function evaluation beyond 2 years of age. We recommend future multicenter prospective studies with larger cohorts to validate these findings and evaluate long-term neurocognitive outcomes.

Conclusions

Our analysis of glycemic levels in preterm infants revealed significant associations between hyperglycemia and the incidence of both ROP and IVH.In our cohort of hyperglycemic preterm infants, prolonged duration of hyperglycemia was associated with higher incidence of ROP, while elevated peak glucose levels correlated with increased occurrence of IVH. Future multicenter randomized controlled trials are warranted to further validate the clinical value of precise glycemic management in reducing neurodevelopmental impairment and long-term disability rates in preterm infants.
