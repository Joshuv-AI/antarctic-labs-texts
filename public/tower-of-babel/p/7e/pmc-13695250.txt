Updating deficits in subjective cognitive decline: A differential pattern of executive dysfunction across clinical stages
Authors: Yuetan Wang, Wenni Zhan, Zhuoyan Wu, Yan Zhang, Zhuo Ma, Xiaobin Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Identifying objective cognitive markers in the preclinical stage of Alzheimer's disease (AD), particularly in Subjective Cognitive Decline (SCD), is critical for early intervention. While executive function comprises three core components (inhibition, updating/switching), their impairment trajectory during the SCD-to-Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) progression remains unclear. This study aims to systematically compare these components among Cognitively Normal (NC), SCD, and MCI individuals to determine the earliest sensitive cognitive indicators of AD pathology.Community-dwelling adults aged ≥50 years were classified into NC, SCD, and MCI groups based on comprehensive screening (MoCA, SCD-Q, CDR, ADL). All participants completed standardized computerized tasks assessing inhibition (Oddball, Stop Signal Task), updating (spatial 1-back, VWM task), and switching (task-cue paradigm). One-way ANOVA with post-hoc tests compared group differences in accuracy and reaction time.SCD showed no difference from NC in inhibition but intermediate switch costs; crucially, SCD’s spatial 1-back performance was significantly worse than NC and comparable to MCI, indicating early MCI-level updating deficits. Visuospatial storage capacity showed gradual impairment. Updating ability (visuospatial working memory) is the earliest and stably impaired indicator in SCD, serving as a sensitive marker for ultra-early AD screening.These findings suggest that updating ability, particularly visuospatial working memory, is the earliest and most stable impaired executive component, making it a sensitive behavioral marker for identifying high-risk SCD and enabling ultra-early AD screening. In contrast, significant impairments in inhibition and switching are delayed or progressive. This provides new scientific evidence for early assessment and targeted cognitive intervention.

Introduction

The neuropathological changes of Alzheimer's disease (AD) initiate decades prior to the onset of clinical symptoms (Jack et al., 2018, 2024). This prolonged preclinical phase affords a critical window for early interventions, with the core challenge lying in the accurate identification of high-risk individuals from the large population of cognitively normal older adults.

Clinical classification systems have identified several at-risk states along the cognitive decline continuum. Subjective cognitive decline (SCD) is defined as the persistent self-reported experience of cognitive deterioration in the absence of objective abnormalities on standardized neuropsychological assessments, and has been established as a high-risk clinical state for future cognitive decline and dementia, particularly AD (Jessen et al., 2014; Rabin et al., 2017). Individuals with SCD and MCI have a two- to fourfold higher risk of progression to dementia compared to cognitively normal older adults (Jia et al., 2020; Jessen et al., 2014; Molinuevo et al., 2017). However, SCD is a clinically defined syndrome rather than a biological stage of AD itself, and the SCD population exhibits substantial internal heterogeneity (Jessen et al., 2014; Slot et al., 2019). Thus, identifying objective and sensitive cognitive-behavioral markers that can differentiate high-risk individuals from the broader group of cognitively concerned older adults is of paramount importance for early intervention efforts.

Cognitive decline in aging adversely affects a wide range of cognitive domains, including perceptual function (Javitt et al., 2023), attention (Zanto & Gazzaley, 2014), and executive function. Mounting evidence suggests that the executive function network—predominantly involving the prefrontal cortex and temporoparietal junction—may be subject to early pathological insults in AD, particularly by tau pathology, even before the onset of amnestic mild cognitive impairment (Buckner, 2004; Gallego-Rudolf et al., 2024; Seeley et al., 2007).

Executive function is not a unitary cognitive construct. The classic model proposed by Miyake and colleagues (2000) decomposes it into three core, partially independent yet interrelated subcomponents: inhibition (the suppression of prepotent responses or interfering information), updating (the continuous revision and manipulation of working memory content), and shifting (the flexible switching between different tasks or mental sets). Existing studies have demonstrated that inhibition (Belleville et al., 2007; Brandt et al., 2009), updating/working memory (Belleville et al., 2007; Kirova et al., 2015), and shifting (Seo et al., 2016) are all impaired to varying degrees in the MCI stage. This componential decomposition provides a valuable framework for delineating the fine-grained profile of cognitive decline across clinically defined groups. Owing to their reliance on distinct neural circuits, these subcomponents may exhibit differential susceptibility to early pathological changes, thereby following distinct patterns of impairment across clinical groups—a phenomenon termed "dissociated impairment."

Although extensive research has confirmed the presence of widespread executive dysfunction in the MCI stage (Kirova et al., 2015), evidence regarding objective executive impairment in the SCD stage remains inconsistent and unsystematic. First, most studies assess executive function as a global construct or focus on a single subcomponent, lacking systematic and parallel comparisons of multiple key subcomponents within the same sample. This limitation hinders our understanding of the differential patterns of impairment across distinct cognitive subcomponents in the SCD and MCI groups. Second, the profile of objective impairment in executive function subcomponents in SCD—a clinically defined at-risk group—remains poorly characterized. While some evidence suggests that individuals with SCD may exhibit subtle abnormalities on specific cognitive tasks (Munro et al., 2023; Saykin et al., 2006), findings regarding the presence and magnitude of impairment in inhibition, updating, and shifting are conflicting, with a paucity of direct comparative investigations (e.g., Cedres et al., 2019 vs. Okonkwo et al., 2008).

To address these gaps, the present study employed a battery of computerized experimental paradigms—including the two-choice Oddball task and stop-signal task for proactive/reactive inhibition(Logan et al., 1984; Verbruggen & Logan, 2008), the spatial N-back task and visuospatial working memory task for updating(Kirchner, 1958; Owen et al., 2005), and the cued task-switching paradigm for shifting(Miyake et al., 2000)—to systematically compare the cross-sectional characteristics of multidimensional executive function performance across three carefully screened groups of older adults: NC, SCD, and MCI. Specifically, this study aimed to: (1) characterize the profiles of inhibition, updating, and shifting performance across clinically defined NC, SCD, and MCI groups; and (2) examine the differential sensitivity of each executive subcomponent in detecting subtle cognitive anomalies associated with SCD, relative to the other subcomponents.

Based on the theoretical framework of dissociable executive subcomponents and the vulnerability of the updating-related neural circuitry to early pathological changes, we propose the following cross-sectional hypotheses. The three clinically defined groups (NC, SCD, and MCI) will exhibit distinct patterns of executive function performance across the three subcomponents, with updating showing the most pronounced group differences at the SCD stage. Compared to inhibition and shifting, updating function will demonstrate greater sensitivity to the cognitive changes associated with SCD, as reflected by a larger effect size in differentiating SCD from NC. Inhibition will show significant impairment predominantly in the MCI group, while shifting will exhibit a more moderate pattern of decline across groups.

Methods

Participants

All procedures involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Psychology, Northwest Normal University (Approval Number: 20230407) and the Medical Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University (Approval Number: 2022A-460). The study was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration Number: ChiCTR2200061594). A total of 1000 questionnaires were distributed through public lectures and poster promotions in multiple communities in Lanzhou, China, to recruit 300 participants for cognitive screening. All participants provided voluntary written informed consent and received monetary compensation commensurate with their completion of the study protocols.

Neuropsychological assessment

Participants were included if they met all the following criteria: (1) Aged 50 years or older; (2) Essentially normal activities of daily living (ADL); (3) Educational attainment of primary school graduation or above, with the ability to cooperate in completing cognitive assessments; (4) Voluntarily participated in the study and completed the informed consent form; (5) No obvious behavioral or language impairments; (6) Willing to undergo all required examinations, assessments and research-related procedures; (7) Right-handed, with a consistent preference for the right hand in performing daily motor tasks including writing, using chopsticks, throwing objects, brushing teeth, using scissors and striking a match.

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) A current or past diagnosis of Alzheimer’s disease, epilepsy, brain tumor, encephalitis, cerebral infarction, intracerebral hemorrhage, intracranial infection, Parkinson’s disease, other cerebrovascular diseases, or a history of severe traumatic brain injury; (2) Comorbid physical illnesses or disorders that may interfere with clinical research, including disorders of consciousness, severe visual or hearing impairment, aphasia, severe heart disease, severe respiratory disease, uncontrolled diabetes mellitus, uncontrolled hypertension, malignant tumors, and hepatic or renal diseases; (3) A current or past diagnosis of anxiety disorder, major depressive disorder, schizophrenia, or other psychiatric disorders; (4) Use of neuroleptic, antidepressant, sedative, or antipsychotic medications within the two weeks prior to enrollment.

Cognitive and neuropsychological assessments

All participants completed a standardized battery of neuropsychological tests for group classification and the assessment of baseline cognitive status.

Global cognitive function: The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) was administered. This scale assesses multiple cognitive domains including attention, executive function, memory, language, visuospatial ability and abstract thinking, and is a sensitive tool for MCI screening (Nasreddine et al., 2005).

Subjective cognitive complaint: The self-report section of the Subjective Cognitive Decline Questionnaire (SCD-Q My Cog) was used. This 24-item scale quantifies self-perceived cognitive decline over the past two years across memory, language and executive function domains, and its reliability and validity have been verified in community-dwelling older adults (Rami et al., 2014, Meng et al., 2017). Additionally, the 9-item Subjective Cognitive Decline Questionnaire (SCD-9), a brief screening tool developed by Gifford KA and colleagues via psychometric modeling to extract valid items from an extensive pool of SCD-related questions (Jessen et al., 2014), was administered. Each item of the SCD-9 was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, with 1 indicating "never" and 5 indicating "frequently" (Hao et al., 2020).

Activities of daily living: The Basic Activities of Daily Living (BADL) scale, a 10-item measure, and the Modified Barthel Index (MBI) were used to assess basic ADL and exclude participants with significant functional impairment.

Clinical severity rating: The Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) scale was applied to ensure that all MCI participants were in the early stage of the disease (CDR = 0.5) (Morris, 1993).

Emotional status: The Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) and the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA), developed by Max Hamilton in 1960 and 1959 respectively, were used to evaluate the severity of depressive and anxious symptoms in clinical settings (Hamilton, 1959, 1960). All participants in the present study scored < 7 on both the HAMD and HAMA.

Group classification criteria

(1) Normal Cognitive Decline (NCD) group

Participants in the NCD group met the following criteria: normal ADL; no self-reported subjective cognitive decline; normal performance on cognitive assessments; no obvious behavioral or language impairments (Yang et al., 2021); SCD-Q score < 10; SCD-9 score < 5; MoCA score ≥ 26; CDR score = 0; and BADL score = 100.

(2) Subjective Cognitive Decline (SCD) group

Inclusion criteria for the SCD group were consistent with the published research criteria for SCD proposed by the Subjective Cognitive Decline Initiative (SCD-I) (Jessen et al., 2014), including: aged 50 to 80 years; self-reported subjective cognitive decline relative to one’s normal state, which was not associated with any acute event; normal performance on standard cognitive tests or scores not meeting the diagnostic criteria for MCI, with a MoCA score ≥ 25 (or ≥ 20 for participants with ≤ 6 years of education) after adjustment for gender, age and years of education (Cedres et al., 2019; Hao et al., 2017; Yan et al., 2018); SCD-Q score ≥ 10; SCD-9 score > 5; CDR score = 0; and BADL score = 100.

(3) Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) group

Inclusion criteria for the MCI group were based on the diagnostic criteria for MCI outlined in the 2018 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Dementia and Cognitive Disorders, including: aged 50 to 80 years; self-reported prominent memory decline or memory impairment; objective evidence of cognitive impairment on cognitive assessments, indicated by a MoCA score ≤ 24 (or ≤ 19 for participants with ≤ 6 years of education); SCD-Q score ≥ 10; SCD-9 score > 5; CDR score ≤ 0.5; normal or mildly impaired ADL with the ability to maintain independent daily living (BADLscore >71); and no meeting of the diagnostic criteria for dementia.

Behavioral paradigms for assessing executive function subcomponents

All participants completed four computerized cognitive tasks designed to assess the three core subcomponents of executive function: inhibition (proactive and reactive inhibition), updating and working memory, and cognitive shifting.

Inhibitory function tasks

Proactive inhibition

The two-choice Oddball paradigm was adopted (Yuan et al., 2017, Braver, 2012). The stimuli consisted of a frequently presented standard letter W (80%) and an infrequently presented deviant letter M (20%). Participants were required to make distinct keypress responses to the two types of stimuli (e.g., pressing the F key for W and the J key for M). This task demanded that participants suppress the prepotent response tendency to the high-frequency standard stimuli to make accurate responses to the low-frequency deviant stimuli. Accuracy and reaction time (RT) for deviant stimuli were calculated as indices of proactive inhibitory ability. The formal experiment comprised 2 blocks with a total of 300 trials. The task flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Flowchart of E-prime program for the two-choice oddball task.

Reactive inhibition

The stop-signal task was employed (Logan & Cowan, 1984). In the majority of trials, participants were instructed to press a key rapidly according to the direction of an arrow (← or →) (Go trials). In a subset of trials (approximately 25%), an auditory stop signal was presented immediately after the arrow, requiring participants to inhibit their keypress response instantaneously (Stop trials). The key index was stop signal reaction time (SSRT), which reflects the time required to successfully abort an initiated response; longer SSRT indicates poorer reactive inhibitory ability. The formal experiment included 2 blocks with 112 trials per block. The task flowchart is presented in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Flowchart of E-prime program for stop signal task.

Flowchart of E-prime program for stop signal task.

Updating and working memory tasks

Updating function

The spatial 1-back task was used (Jaeggi et al., 2010). A black square was presented randomly at one of eight positions on the screen. Participants were asked to judge whether the position of the current square matched that of the square in the preceding trial. This task required participants to continuously update the spatial location information in working memory online. Overall accuracy and RT were used as indices of updating ability. The task consisted of 2 blocks with a total of 120 trials. The task flowchart is presented in Fig. 3.Fig. 3Flowchart of E-prime program for spatial 1-back task.

Flowchart of E-prime program for spatial 1-back task.

Visuospatial working memory (Storage)

A delayed match-to-sample paradigm (i.e., the visuospatial working memory [VWM] task) was adopted (Luck and Vogel, 1997, Liu et al., 2021). First, a set of colored squares was presented simultaneously on the left and right sides of the screen (encoding phase, 1000 ms). Participants were required to memorize the color information of the squares on the cued side (left or right) only, as indicated by an arrow cue. Following a 900 ms blank screen delay, a probe phase was presented (2000 ms), in which participants were asked to judge whether the colored squares on the cued side had changed (press the F key for a color change and the J key for no change). This task primarily assesses the capacity for the temporary storage and maintenance of nonverbal visual information (van Ede & Nobre, 2023). The formal experiment included 3 blocks with a total of 240 trials. The task flowchart is presented in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Flowchart of E-prime program for VWM task.

Flowchart of E-prime program for VWM task.

Cognitive shifting task

The cued task-switching paradigm was utilized (Monsell, 2003). Prior to each trial, a cue (▲/◎ or >/<) indicated the type of task to be performed next: judging the parity of a digit or judging the magnitude of a digit relative to 5. The cue and target digit were then presented randomly on the stimulus screen, and participants were required to perform the corresponding task and press the key according to the cue prompt. Switch cost was calculated by comparing RT and accuracy between task-switch trials and task-repeat trials, and it was used as the index of cognitive flexibility and shifting ability. The task comprised 2 blocks with a total of 144 trials. The task flowchart is presented in Fig. 5.Fig. 5Flowchart of E-prime program for the task cue paradigm.

Flowchart of E-prime program for the task cue paradigm.

Experimental procedures

All assessments and experimental tasks were administered one-on-one in a quiet laboratory environment. First, experimenters trained in clinical medicine conducted neuropsychological scale evaluations for the participants (lasting approximately 40–60 min), after which the participants completed the three aforementioned executive function tasks on a computer (with a total duration of around 30–40 min). Appropriate rest intervals were scheduled between tasks to mitigate fatigue effects. The order of the experimental tasks was counterbalanced across all participants.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Behavioral data were collected using E-Prime 3.0 software, and statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0 software. First, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted on continuous demographic variables (age, years of education) and baseline neuropsychological scale scores (MoCA, SCD-Q), while chi-square test was used for sex, to verify group matching across the three groups. Second, one-way ANOVA was applied to the key dependent variables of each executive function task (accuracy rate, reaction time, SSRT, switch cost). Partial eta-squared (ηp2) was reported as the effect size measure. If the ANOVA results were statistically significant, Least Significant Difference (LSD) post-hoc tests were further conducted for pairwise comparisons between groups. The significance level for all statistical tests was set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed).

Discussion

This study systematically compared the three core subcomponents of executive function—inhibition, updating, and shifting—across three clinically defined groups of older adults: cognitively normal (NC), subjective cognitive decline (SCD), and mild cognitive impairment (MCI). The results revealed a pattern of dissociated cross-sectional heterogeneity in executive function subcomponents across these clinical cognitive states. Specifically, inhibitory function was impaired only in the MCI group, whereas updating function (as measured by spatial 1-back accuracy) was already significantly compromised in the SCD group, with a level of impairment comparable to that of the MCI group. Shifting function showed a graded, continuous decline across the three groups. These findings suggest that updating ability, particularly visuospatial working memory updating, may be a sensitive behavioral marker for detecting subtle cognitive decline in SCD individuals, who are clinically defined as having subjective cognitive complaints without objective deficits on standardized neuropsychological assessments.

Cross-sectional heterogeneity of executive function subcomponents across clinically defined cognitive states

Inhibitory Function. Our results showed that the SCD group and the NC group exhibited comparable performance on both the two-choice oddball task (proactive inhibition) and the stop-signal task (reactive inhibition), whereas the MCI group demonstrated significantly poorer performance than both groups. This pattern is consistent with prior research indicating that inhibitory control remains relatively preserved in individuals with subjective complaints alone, and that objective impairment in inhibition becomes detectable only when cognitive decline reaches the MCI stage (Albert et al., 2011; Hwang et al., 2014). It is important to note that the comparable performance of the SCD and NC groups does not preclude the possibility of subtle neural changes in the SCD group; rather, it suggests that these changes, if present, have not yet manifested as objective behavioral deficits in inhibitory control as measured by the current tasks. The preserved inhibitory performance in SCD may reflect the contribution of cognitive reserve or compensatory mechanisms (Pappalettera et al., 2024), which allow individuals with subjective concerns to maintain behavioral performance within the normal range despite possible underlying brain changes.

Updating Function. In contrast to inhibition, updating function (assessed by the spatial 1-back task) showed a markedly different pattern. The SCD group exhibited significantly lower accuracy than the NC group, with performance declining to a level comparable to the MCI group. This finding indicates that the ability to continuously monitor and update spatial information in working memory is particularly vulnerable to the cognitive changes associated with the SCD clinical state. Notably, the SCD group's accuracy in the updating task was as impaired as that of the MCI group, suggesting that updating deficits may emerge early and plateau in severity across these clinically defined groups. This pattern aligns with prior studies that have reported early working memory updating deficits in at-risk populations (Belleville et al., 2007; Kirova et al., 2015).

Further analysis of the working memory system revealed a dissociation between higher-order updating and more basic visuospatial storage. While spatial 1-back updating (requiring continuous online manipulation of information) showed a steep decline in SCD, the basic visuospatial storage capacity (assessed by the delayed match-to-sample VWM task) followed a more gradual pattern of impairment: MCI individuals showed significant deficits, while SCD individuals showed only a trend toward decline under specific conditions (i.e., simple maintenance in the same-color condition). This dissociation suggests that within the working memory system, higher-order cognitive processes demanding active resource allocation are more vulnerable to early cognitive changes than basic storage capacity, consistent with the findings of Saunders and Summers (2010). Additionally, the MCI group exhibited a pattern of faster reaction times but lower accuracy in the VWM task, which may reflect a speed–accuracy trade-off or impulsive response strategy—a phenomenon commonly reported in MCI and early dementia, indicating impaired executive control over the balance between speed and accuracy (Gazzaley et al., 2005).

Shifting Function. Shifting function, assessed by the cued task-switching paradigm, exhibited a gradual and continuous decline pattern across the three groups. On several indices, the SCD group showed intermediate performance between the NC and MCI groups, although some of these differences did not reach full statistical significance. This pattern suggests that cognitive flexibility may be progressively compromised as clinical cognitive status worsens, with measurable deficits becoming more pronounced in the MCI stage. The gradual nature of shifting decline is consistent with previous studies that have reported task-switching impairments in MCI (Belleville et al., 2007; Seo et al., 2016) and suggests that shifting function may be sensitive to the cumulative effects of cognitive decline across clinical states.

Sensitivity of updating to subtle cognitive decline in SCD: clinical implications

Among the three executive subcomponents examined, updating function—particularly spatial working memory updating—demonstrated the most pronounced sensitivity to the cognitive changes associated with SCD. This finding has important clinical implications. SCD is a clinically defined syndrome characterized by self-reported persistent cognitive decline in the absence of objective impairment on standardized neuropsychological tests (Jessen et al., 2014; Molinuevo et al., 2017). However, the SCD population is heterogeneous, with a subset of individuals showing an elevated risk of progression to MCI and dementia (Slot et al., 2019). The identification of objective cognitive markers that can detect subtle decline in SCD individuals is therefore a priority for early screening and risk stratification.

Our results suggest that the spatial 1-back task, which captures the efficiency of updating spatial information in working memory, may serve as a sensitive behavioral marker for detecting subtle cognitive decline in the SCD group. This is consistent with prior evidence that working memory updating tasks are among the most sensitive cognitive measures for detecting early cognitive changes in at-risk populations (Jaeggi et al., 2010; Kirova et al., 2015).

It is critical to emphasize that the present study is a cross-sectional investigation of clinically defined groups, not a longitudinal tracking of disease progression. NC, SCD, and MCI are clinical cognitive status categories, not stages of Alzheimer's disease (AD) pathology. Although SCD and MCI are associated with an elevated risk of future AD dementia (Jessen et al., 2020; Slot et al., 2019), they are clinically defined syndromes rather than biological stages of AD itself (Albert et al., 2011; Jack et al., 2024; Jessen et al., 2014). The present study did not include any biomarkers (e.g., amyloid PET, CSF Aβ/tau, or plasma p-tau), and therefore cannot distinguish AD-related cognitive decline from cognitive decline due to other etiologies (e.g., cerebrovascular disease, Lewy body pathology, or normal aging). The executive function differences observed in this study reflect clinical phenotypic heterogeneity across groups; they do not directly inform the underlying neuropathological processes. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted as evidence of cross-sectional differences in cognitive performance among clinically defined groups, rather than as evidence for a specific trajectory of AD pathology.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design precludes within-individual trajectory analysis; longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether updating deficits in SCD predict subsequent cognitive decline. The parallel-group design limits direct cross-task individual-level comparisons, which future crossover designs or large-sample cross-validation could address. This study relied exclusively on behavioral measures; without biomarkers, it is not possible to distinguish AD-related from non-AD-related cognitive decline. Integrating neuroimaging (fMRI, EEG) and fluid biomarkers (plasma Aβ, p-tau) would help elucidate the neurobiological basis of the observed behavioral differences (Grady, 2012, Montoliu-Gaya et al., 2025). The laboratory-based tasks may not fully capture real-world executive demands; more ecologically valid assessments (e.g., digital IADL endpoints) are needed (Lawson et al., 2023). Finally, the clinical heterogeneity of SCD and MCI definitions warrants more refined subtyping (e.g., SCD-plus features, amnestic vs. non-amnestic MCI) to better characterize the relationship between executive function profiles and clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrates that the three core subcomponents of executive function—inhibition, updating, and switching—exhibit significant phenotypic heterogeneity across clinically defined NC, SCD, and MCI groups in a cross-sectional clinical cognitive population. Among these subcomponents, updating function, particularly spatial working memory updating, is more sensitive to the subtle clinical cognitive decline associated with the SCD stage, suggesting its potential utility as a behavioral indicator for early clinical cognitive screening in older adults. It is important to emphasize that the present findings are based on cross-sectional clinical phenotypic differences and do not reflect AD pathological progression or temporal patterns of impairment. Future longitudinal studies incorporating biomarker measurements are needed to further elucidate the mechanisms underlying these cross-sectional differences.
