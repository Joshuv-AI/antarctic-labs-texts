Burden and Predictors of Repeat Diagnostic Upper Endoscopy for Abdominal Pain, Heartburn, and Dysphagia in General Gastroenterology
Authors: Priya Kathuria, Eric D. Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEndoscopy access is limited nationwide.AimsWe aimed to characterize the burden of repeated upper endoscopies for indications of abdominal pain, heartburn, and dysphagia.MethodsWe reviewed all patients undergoing diagnostic endoscopy in a large health system between 2000 and 2023 who did not have medical conditions requiring repeat endoscopy. A Cox model was developed to predict the hazard of repeating a diagnostic endoscopy within three years as a primary outcome, represented using hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsOver a 23 year period, three-fifths of all diagnostic upper endoscopies performed were for abdominal pain (n = 5176), heartburn (n = 1535), or dysphagia (n = 3266). 90% of these procedures were ordered by gastroenterologists. The endoscopy was repeated within three years in 4.4% of patients with abdominal pain, 4.3% of patients with heartburn, and 5.6% of patients with dysphagia, usually for the same indication as the original procedure and without any intervention performed. In a multivariable model, the risk of repeating an endoscopy was either not influenced or only weakly influenced by age, sex, and mortality risk (measured using the Charlson Comorbidity Index). Findings were similar regardless of the dominant gastrointestinal symptom complaint. Gastroenterologists were no less likely to order a repeat endoscopy than primary care. Most repeat endoscopies occurred within one year of the index endoscopy.ConclusionAn average gastroenterologist performs a repeat diagnostic upper endoscopy almost every day.

Introduction

Wait times for outpatient endoscopy performed by gastroenterologists are increasing across the country [1]. Patients with urgent medical needs wait behind patients who are already routinely scheduled for upper endoscopy (e.g., gastroscopy) to evaluate chronic, bothersome upper gastrointestinal complaints that may already have been evaluated [2–4]. The fix has been to remove endoscopy as a tool from the arsenal for the majority of patients due to its low yield [5], providing opportunity for healthcare insurers to consider blanket prior authorization requirements aligned precisely with practice guidelines [6]. While recent gastroenterology professional society efforts successfully blocked blanket prior authorization efforts by insurance companies [7], most recent efforts have focused on the indication and quality of index examinations. To our knowledge, no contemporary study has looked at the burden of repeat examinations.

The most common indications for upper endoscopy are abdominal pain (as a common symptom within the broader classification of dyspepsia), heartburn, and dysphagia [8–10]. For dyspepsia, guidelines recommend against upper endoscopy, especially in younger patients with dyspepsia without warning signs and instead advocate an empiric proton pump inhibitor trial with evaluation for Helicobacter pylori, recognizing a low risk of finding cancer [5]. Yet upper endoscopy for dyspepsia continues to be performed. The distinction is that no study advocates endoscopy instead of empiric proton pump inhibitors, rather whether endoscopy should be performed at all among patients that fail empiric measures. One-in-ten index diagnostic upper endoscopies (performed after an empiric proton pump inhibitor trial, when indicated) having a meaningful finding other than cancer, such as erosive esophagitis or peptic ulcer disease (i.e., the number-needed-to-diagnose major pathology [NND] with diagnostic upper endoscopy is 10) [11]. For patients that fail an empiric proton pump inhibitor trial, offering a one-time high-quality upper endoscopy to evaluate abdominal pain (e.g., to differentiate peptic ulcer disease from non-ulcer dyspepsia and other causes of abdominal pain in the appropriate patient) may improve cost-effectiveness and patient satisfaction compared to removing endoscopy entirely from the algorithm. For patients with heartburn, endoscopy is useful to diagnose and check healing of erosive esophagitis and to offer a one-time screening for Barrett’s esophagus for appropriate patients [12–14]. For both dyspepsia and heartburn, repeated endoscopy outside of these parameters is not routinely advocated by guidelines or evidence. Distinct from dyspepsia and heartburn, dysphagia is an alarm symptom. There, the utility of repeat endoscopy would be to obtain an adequate examination that may have been missed and to then perform an appropriate therapeutic intervention, such as dilation of a subtle stricture missed on initial endoscopy for dysphagia or careful identification with forceps disruption of a previously missed Schatzki ring [13, 15–17].

From an access standpoint, are repeat diagnostic upper endoscopy performed commonly enough to create access delays for other patients? We aimed to define the burden of repeat upper endoscopy for indications of abdominal pain, heartburn, and dysphagia in general gastroenterology practice.

Methods

This study was conducted according to the STROBE checklist for cohort studies. We identified patients who underwent diagnostic upper endoscopy across a regional multi-site health system over a 23 year period between January 2000 and May 2023. This included Computerized Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes 43,235 (upper endoscopy without biopsy) and 43,239 (upper endoscopy with biopsy). The primary dataset included patients referred for evaluation of abdominal pain (International Classification of Diseases Version 10 [ICD-10] R10.* or ICD Version 9 [ICD-9] 789), heartburn (ICD-10 R12.*, K20.*, or K21.* or ICD-9 787.1, 530.1, or 530.8), or dysphagia (ICD-10 R13.1 or ICD-9 787.2). The specific billing code for uninvestigated dyspepsia is included in our R10.* definition for abdominal pain, which is important because uninvestigated dyspepsia is the clinical reason that gastroenterologists would use endoscopy among patients presenting with the complaint of abdominal pain. These procedural indications were chosen as they constitute the majority of diagnostic upper endoscopies, and repeat endoscopy solely for diagnostic purposes is not recommended in routine contexts for these indications when the index procedure non-diagnostic. Our study was designed to evaluate low-risk patients by excluding patients with conditions that might require a repeat endoscopy in practice guidelines (cancer, cirrhosis, Barrett’s, esophagitis, eosinophilic esophagitis, peptic ulcer disease, melena, inflammatory bowel disease, iron deficiency anemia). Institutional review board approval was obtained prior to the start of this study.

Study Endpoints and Analysis

Cox regression analysis was performed to predict the hazard of repeating the upper endoscopy over time. The co-primary outcome was whether a patient received a repeat diagnostic upper endoscopy within one year or three years of their index upper endoscopy, reported using hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A higher hazard ratio represents a greater likelihood of undergoing repeat endoscopy within one year or within three years.

Exploratory analyses included whether referral patterns for repeat endoscopy differed between gastroenterologists compared to non-gastroenterologists who can order open/direct-access endoscopy without gastroenterologist consultation.

We also evaluated whether age, patient sex, or general mortality risk (using the Charlson comorbidity index [CCI] as a valid 10 year mortality measure) influenced repeat endoscopy patterns in a multivariable model fitting this variables. Goodness-of-fit was assessed by plotting Cox-Snell residuals. The proportionality assumption was evaluated for each variable by assessing the linear time-by-covariate interactions for each covariate. Analysis was performed using STATA version 15.0 (STATACorp, College Station, TX).

Burden of Repeat Diagnostic Upper Endoscopy

4.4% of patients (1-in-23 patients) had a repeat upper endoscopy within three years for the indication of abdominal pain, and 54.4% of those repeat upper endoscopies occurred within one year following the index upper endoscopy. 4.3% (1-in-23 patients) of patients had a repeat upper endoscopy withing three years for the indication of heartburn, and 95.4% of those repeat upper endoscopies occurred within year following the index upper endoscopy. 5.6% of patients (1-in-18 patients) had a repeat upper endoscopy within three years for the indication for dysphagia, and 67.6% of those repeat upper endoscopies occurred within year following the index upper endoscopy.

Factors Influencing the Burden of Repeat Diagnostic Upper Endoscopy

Patients at least 65 years of age had a slightly higher hazard of repeating a diagnostic upper endoscopy for abdominal pain within three years (HR = 1.41; 95% CI 1.002–1.983) or for dysphagia within one year (HR = 1.60; 95% CI 1.094–2.330) and three years (HR = 1.42; 95% CI 1.036–1.946) (Table 1). Patients under 65 were just as likely to undergo repeat endoscopy for heartburn. Patients with a higher calculated mortality risk based on aggregate comorbidities defined by the Charlson Comorbidity Index were slightly more likely to undergo repeat diagnostic endoscopy without a new intervention. Female sex and direct/open-access referrals from non-gastroenterologists did not influence the hazard of repeating endoscopies with the exception of upper endoscopies to investigate heartburn. Patients referred through direct/open-access programs from non-gastroenterologists were slightly more likely to have their upper endoscopy repeated within one year (HR = 1.022; 95% CI 1.117–4.374), compared to patients who saw a gastroenterologist for consultation before undergoing upper endoscopy.

Discussion

About one-in-20 patients with abdominal pain, heartburn, or dysphagia will repeat a diagnostic upper endoscopy within three years. Given the endoscopy volume for these common symptom indications and considering that gastroenterologists will perform, a typical general gastroenterologist will therefore encounter this patient almost every day [18]. Age, sex, and mortality risk either do not or only weakly affect the likelihood of a patient repeating their upper endoscopy. Gastroenterologists are no less likely to order repeat diagnostic upper endoscopies for dysphagia or abdominal pain than primary care and other non-gastroenterology colleagues. Patients who undergo open/direct-access upper endoscopy for heartburn ordered by a non-gastroenterologist are slightly more likely to undergo a repeat procedure within one year compared to patients who see a gastroenterologist first.

Contemporary efforts focus on removing upper endoscopy from the diagnostic armamentarium as an up-front test for the majority of patients who lack alarm symptoms and warning signs. Yet one-in-10 appropriately performed index endoscopies finds major pathology [11], and at a cost that is less than a one-year-supply of a typical guideline-recommended prescription drug, much less a single hospital admission for upper gastrointestinal bleeding or biologic to treat inflammatory bowel disease [14, 19, 20]. Index endoscopies have been consistently performed in low-risk patients as far back as four decades ago [21], three decades ago [22], last decade [23], and today [24] suggesting an entrenched divergence between guidelines and practice. We posit that success might be better achieved in focusing value-based efforts on repeat endoscopies for the same low-risk patient, rather than on the index endoscopy that has already been performed.

Our study was not designed to identify the root cause of repeat procedures that, if structural, might go beyond what is available in chart review. More broadly, repeat procedures are performed for myriad and complex reasons unrelated to financial incentives [25]. First, spending more time upfront with the patient to explain their illness decreases the need for investigations, yet the typical face-to-face time between a gastroenterologist and patient during an office visit is now only seven minutes as cognitive physician work time is less and less valued [26]. Face-to-face time also decreases as electronic health record complexity increases [27], as reimbursement for physician work time decreases [28], and with the loss of physician-directed practice support that accompanies consolidation in healthcare by hospitals (e.g., health systems) [29] or private equity [30]. Second, systematic emphasis on tracking patient satisfaction scores as a means to achieve value-based payments by policymakers has designed a system that may yield more procedures beyond the index endoscopy [19, 31]. Third, the frequency of repeat upper endoscopy for heartburn within 1 year specifically may be driven by the need for an appropriately performed upper endoscopy OFF of proton pump inhibitors supported by documentation of proton pump inhibitor use at the time of the endoscopy [32]. Fourth, the sheer size of any large consolidated system, as opposed to a smaller physician-owned practice, are well-known to come with inherent barriers to communication that affect care quality [33, 34]. The extent to which these general trends impact the burden of repeat procedures in gastroenterology deserves attention, because they might identify systematic opportunities to improve physician-led, patient-centered care.

Open/direct-access programs are common across gastroenterology practices and important for colorectal cancer screening program access in which gastroenterologist consultation is generally unnecessary [35]. Assessing inclusion of upper endoscopy within open/direct-access programs is important, such as if the burden of repeat examinations is substantially higher when such testing is broadly available without office consultation with a gastroenterologist [36]. Among patients without an established gastrointestinal diagnosis, 91.5, 86.8, and 88.9% of all upper endoscopies for abdominal pain, heartburn, and dysphagia were ordered by gastroenterologists rather than by primary care or other subspecialties. We found that non-gastroenterologists (mostly primary care providers) did not order repeat endoscopies any more than gastroenterologists. This suggests that closing such programs would not reduce the burden of repeat endoscopy that is deemed unnecessary, and that efforts to study and reduce this burden should be focused elsewhere, such as on the quality of the index examination that is performed.

Specific to patients with heartburn, upper endoscopy is more likely to be repeated within one year, if the index procedure was performed through direct/open-access from a non-gastroenterology provider. This finding could align with limited documentation of proton pump inhibitor use at the time of an upper endoscopy [32] or to clinically appropriate value in performing testing off of proton pump inhibitors among patients who initially decline to hold their proton pump inhibitor for the index test [37] again noting that our current study was not designed to identify the root cause of the repeat endoscopies in our cohort.

Unlike abdominal pain (under the classification of dyspepsia) and heartburn, dysphagia is an alarm symptom and so our findings should be viewed differently for the indication [20]. Repeating a non-therapeutic endoscopy for dysphagia can be avoided by performing an adequate index examination. For example, biopsies should be routinely taken to evaluate for eosinophilic esophagitis [17]. Schatzki ring can be difficult to diagnose without adequate insufflation and time for examination [38]. Once structural causes of dysphagia are eliminated from the differential diagnosis, esophageal physiologic testing should be considered for motility disorders when appropriate and functional dysphagia should also be considered [15]. In addition to motility disorders, oropharyngeal dysphagia should also be considered early and up-front in the differential diagnosis [39]. The point for dysphagia is not that testing should be avoided, but rather that endoscopy should be comprehensive on the index examination.

Our findings have several limitations that appropriately set the context for interpretation. First, our findings reflect a major primary and tertiary care health system and may over-represent access to gastroenterology that is limited in under-served areas [1]. Second, given the single system nature of our study, we likely underreport the likelihood (or more correctly: the hazard) of repeating a diagnostic upper endoscopy that may have been performed outside of our large health system. Third, validity of using claims data was accomplished by defining diagnostic procedures as those with and without biopsy and no other interventions, with coding verified by health system-employed billing experts during charge capture. > 80% of procedures should be performed with an acceptable indication as an endoscopy unit standard [40]. Fourth, we did not focus our analysis on whether procedures were performed by the same gastroenterologist. Like many centers, interpretation of such results may be confounded by our routine scheduling practices to arrange general gastroenterology procedures with the next available gastroenterologist to minimize access delays. Fifth, the total number of patients undergoing endoscopy for abdominal pain, dysphagia, and heartburn should not be compared, as our study was not designed to evaluate the likelihood of index endoscopy. Sixth, our study does not intend to evaluate patients with cancer diagnoses at the index endoscopy who were excluded from our study. Lastly, our study was conducted in the US and our findings may not be applicable to clinical practice in other countries.

Similarly to many other administrative claims studies, we are not able to discretely study dyspepsia. Dyspepsia is defined on epigastric pain or post-prandial distress and comprises several upper gastrointestinal symptoms [5], however the ICD-10 coding structure places dyspepsia under abdominal pain as a parent category. We therefore use and reference the term abdominal pain rather than dyspepsia for our claims analysis study to accommodate the fact that endoscopy is typically coded for abdominal pain related to uninvestigated dyspepsia (R10.13), which is a highly specific and potentially underutilized billing code within the context of abdominal pain (R10.0) that may be used more often than R10.13 as a billing term by US gastroenterologists as found in our dataset. That abdominal pain is a main driver of healthcare utilization may also reflect this coding nuance, as many of these patients actually receive care for dyspepsia [41]. Two means to resolve this are proposed. First, a future survey study comparing gastroenterology intentions to billing codes is critically needed to evaluate our hypothesis. Second, the ICD-11 classification should consider dyspepsia as the parent category, thereby allowing administrative claims analysis to better understand dyspepsia as a whole.

At the root cause: what are quality and value, and who gets to define these terms [42]? What is the environmental impact of repeat procedures with limited value [43]? Ongoing efforts to define the appropriate indication for repeat examinations, with emphasis on the quality of the index endoscopy, may reduce the burden of unnecessary repeat endoscopies.
