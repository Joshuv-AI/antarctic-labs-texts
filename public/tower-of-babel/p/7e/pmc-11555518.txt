Lipoprotein(a) among normotensive patients and risk of incident hypertension
Authors: Alexander R. Zheutlin, Joshua A. Jacobs, Brian Stamm, Regina Royan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLipoprotein(a) has been shown to be disruptive to local endothelial cells, whose integrity is critical to blood pressure (BP) regulation. Cross‐sectional analysis has shown an association between lipoprotein(a) and prevalent hypertension, though it is unclear if lipoprotein(a) increases risk of incident hypertension. To assess this, the authors measured baseline lipoprotein(a) among 5307 normotensive patients (median age 26 years (interquartile range [IQR] 12–50) and used Cox proportional hazard models to generate hazard rations (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI; median follow‐up 10‐years). The authors categorized lipoprotein(a) as <15 mg/dL, 15–<30 mg/dL, 30–50 mg/dL, >50 mg/dL, and performed subgroup analysis of adults >50 years at baseline. Incident hypertension was defined as a measured BP ≥140/90 mm Hg or a new ICD‐9/10 code. After adjustment, hypertension for patients with baseline lipoprotein(a) 15–<30 mg/dL, 30–50 mg/dL, and >50 mg/dL was 0.91 (0.72–1.16), 1.05 (0.79–1.38), and 1.02 (0.83–1.26; vs. <15 mg/dL). However, among adults >50 years, lipoprotein(a) >50 mg/dL was associated with increased incident hypertension (1.62 [1.17–2.26]).

INTRODUCTION

Hypertension is a leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD); however, prediction of who will develop hypertension remains elusive. Modeling individual risk of developing hypertension often relies on conventional metabolic risk factors which are known to fluctuate over time. Identifying stable biomarkers with reliable measurements early in life that can help distinguish individuals at risk of developing hypertension could improve early intervention strategies and reduce the burden of hypertension with improved long‐term cardiovascular outcomes.

Endothelial disruption and impaired vasodilation are central to the pathophysiology of vascular damage and elevated blood pressure. 1 The subsequent disruption of endothelial function can lead to increased vascular resistance overtime and contribute to higher blood pressure. Lipoprotein(a) is an atherogenic lipoprotein which is largely genetically inherited and demonstrates relatively stable concentrations throughout the lifespan. 2 Lipoprotein(a), through the apolipoprotein(a) component, can cause endothelial dysfunction and inflammation. 2 Additionally, unlike other metabolic biomarkers, lipoprotein(a) is minimally influenced by lifestyle and metabolic variation.

To study the potential of baseline lipoprotein(a) levels to predict incident hypertension in a large cohort of normotensive patients, we used de‐identified longitudinal data from Nashville Biosciences spanning from 1997 to 2022 to examine whether initial lipoprotein(a) measurements among normotensive patients could serve as a predictor for the future development of hypertension. By understanding if elevated levels of lipoprotein(a), which is stable throughout the lifetime, increases the risk of incident hypertension targeted strategies to maintain normotension throughout life can be designed and implemented.

METHODS

Data were obtained from Vanderbilt University Medical Center's BioVu and provided by the American Heart Association with all analysis performed via the Precision Medicine Platform (https://precision.heart.org/). 3 We included patients from birth to age 93 who received care at Vanderbilt University Medical Center between 1997 and 2022, and who were normotensive (blood pressure [BP] <140/90 mm Hg) at the time of lipoprotein(a) measurement without a prior diagnosis of hypertension. 3 Of 11 172 patients with lipoprotein(a) measured, 5629 had hypertension at the time of measurement, 58 had measurement errors, and 178 had no follow‐up, leaving a sample of 5307 patients. Baseline lipoprotein(a) was categorized as <15 mg/dL, 15‐<30 mg/dL, 30–50 mg/dL, >50 mg/dL. Incident hypertension was defined as the first measured BP ≥140/90 mm Hg or a new ICD‐9/10 code for hypertension. Covariates for adjustment were selected based on literature review and models were adjusted for baseline age, self‐reported sex, race, ethnicity, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, atherosclerotic CVD, total cholesterol, body mass index (BMI), and statin use. 4 , 5 , 6 Primary analysis included a time‐to‐event analysis using Cox proportional hazards models to generate hazard rations (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) and Kaplan–Meier to evaluate the association between baseline lipoprotein(a) level and incident hypertension. Sensitivity analyses were performed with lipoprotein(a) exposure dichotomized as ≤50 mg/dL versus >50 mg/dL, as well as ≤30 mg/dL versus >30 mg/dL. We repeated the above analysis after restricting the sample to adults >50 years of age at the baseline lipoprotein(a) measurement (n = 1252) for subgroup analysis. This study followed STROBE reporting guidelines for observational studies.

RESULTS

Among 5307 included patients, the median age was 26 years (interquartile range [IQR] 12–50), and 51.6% self‐identified as female, 66.1% self‐identified as non‐Hispanic White, while 6.6%, 7.2%, 2.2%, and 17.9% identified as non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, non‐Hispanic other, or were missing data (Table 1). Visualization of the relationship between baseline lipoprotein(a) and baseline SBP, DBP, and mean arterial pressure (MAP) is seen in Figure 1(A), while the relationship between baseline lipoprotein(a) and the highest SBP, DBP, and MAP is seen in Figure (B). Over a median of 10 years, incident hypertension was 25.8%, 23.0%, 26.5%, and 21.1% among patients with a baseline lipoprotein(a) of <15 mg/dL, 15–<30 mg/dL, 30–50 mg/dL, and >50 mg/dL. After adjustment, the HR (95% CI) of incident hypertension for patients with baseline lipoprotein(a) 15–<30 mg/dL, 30–50 mg/dL, and >50 mg/dL was 0.91 (0.72–1.16), 1.05 (0.79–1.38), and 1.02 (0.83–1.26; Kaplan–Meier curve visualized in Figure 1A) compared to patients with a baseline lipoprotein(a) <15 mg/dL. We found no meaningful difference in incident hypertension between baseline lipoprotein(a) of ≤50 mg/dL versus >50 mg/dL (HR 1.04 95% CI 0.86–1.26; Figure 1B) nor of ≤30 mg/dL versus >30 mg/dL (HR 1.06 95% CI 0.89–1.26; Figure 1C). Subgroup analysis restricted to adults >50 years of age at inclusion demonstrated a HR (95% CI) of incident hypertension for patients with baseline lipoprotein(a) 15–<30 mg/dL, 30–50 mg/dL, and >50 mg/dL was 1.14 (0.78–1.69), 0.92 (0.55–1.57), and 1.62 (1.17–2.26) compared to those with a lipoprotein(a) <15 mg/dL after adjustment (Figure S2). Among adults >50 years of age, when lipoprotein(a) exposure was dichotomized to <30 mg/dL versus ≥30 mg/dL and ≤50 mg/dL versus >50 mg/dL, a significant difference was found between lipoprotein(a) exposure and incident hypertension after adjustment (HR 1.35 95% CI 1.02–1.79 and HR 1.58 95% CI 1.18–2.12, respectively; Figure S2).

DISCUSSION

In this longitudinal cohort of normotensive adults, we did not find a significant association between higher baseline lipoprotein(a) and the risk of incident hypertension over a median follow‐up period of 10 years overall. However, notably, in our analysis restricted to adults >50 years of age, a baseline lipoprotein(a) >50 mg/dL was associated with incident hypertension. These findings suggest that there may be a time dependent relationship between lipoprotein(a) and hypertension pathogenesis, in which the risk of hypertension increases more prominently in mid‐ and late‐life. Given the relatively young median age of our study, as well as short follow‐up period, it may be that studies among older populations with a longer follow‐up time is necessary to truly elucidate this relationship as hypertension is more often a disease of mid‐ and late‐life.

Improved identification of adults at risk of hypertension, paired with appropriate non‐pharmacologic interventions may stave off the development of hypertension and reduce cardiovascular risk. Prior work has demonstrated the association between increased lipoprotein(a) and decrease in flow‐mediated dilation in healthy adults. 7 Further, lipoprotein(a) can increase intercellular adhesion molecule‐1, vascular cell adhesion molecule‐1, as well as reactive oxygen species to further cause endothelial disruption. 8 Observational research among adults with hypertension suggests that at lipoprotein(a) levels ≥125 nmol/L (approximately 50 mg/dL) there are significant associations with 24‐h ambulatory blood pressure, daytime blood pressure, nighttime blood pressure, and pulse pressure. 9 Further, cohort data from the Multi‐Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis and real‐world clinical data have found an elevated lipoprotein(a) lead to a more notable hazard of CVD among adults with hypertension. 10 , 11 While a causal link is yet to be definitively demonstrated, it may be that lipoprotein(a) induced damage to endothelial integrity and propagation of a locally pro‐inflammatory environment exacerbates the risk of hypertension – and in addition to the atherogenicity of the cholesterol content of lipoprotein(a) portends risk of CVD through multiple mechanisms.

There are notable limitations to the current analysis. As included subjects already completed lipoprotein(a) testing, we cannot determine if patients received lipoprotein(a) testing for a specific reason or as general screening, which may limit generalizability. The included population in this study was relatively young with a median age at the time of baseline lipoprotein(a) measurement of 26 years and a median follow‐up time of 10 years. It may be that a longer time horizon is required to better determine if prolonged exposure to lipoprotein(a) increases risk of incident hypertension later in life. Further, due to our relatively low sample size caution must be exercised in interpreting our findings. Additionally, the assay used to measure lipoprotein(a) changed during the study period and variance between the assays may exist. To account for this, we categorized lipoprotein(a) values to minimize the impact of possible assay variation. Another point of consideration is the change in the definition of hypertension from 140/90 to 130/80 mm Hg by the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association Guideline for the Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Management of High Blood Pressure in Adults. 12 Moreover, despite the recommendation from the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association, a threshold of 140/90 mm Hg was not uniformly adopted after 2017 and a threshold of 140/90 mm Hg is likely to be consistent with broader practice patterns. 13 , 14 Further, this is an observational analysis and subject to unmeasured confounding.

In this longitudinal retrospective cohort study among normotensive adults, while higher levels of baseline lipoprotein(a) were not associated with greater incident hypertension overall, there was an association with incident hypertension among adults >50 years of age, particularly with a lipoprotein(a) ≥50 mg/dL. Further work is required to delineate how higher lipoprotein(a) may translate into clinically‐meaningful increases in blood pressure.
