Global mapping of sexually transmitted Chlamydia trachomatis disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Fatemeh Sameni, Tala Hajiarab, Sara Bayat, Mehrshad Ahmadi, Alex van Belkum, Ali Dadashi, Mehdi Goudarzi, Masoud Dadashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChlamydia trachomatis (C. trachomatis) is known as a leading cause of one of the most prominent sexually transmitted diseases (STD) worldwide. A meta-analysis was performed in this study to comprehensively determine the prevalence of C. trachomatis in the STD population.MethodPubMed/Medline, Embase, and Web of Science databases were used to conduct an extensive literature search on relevant papers published from 2000 to 2023. After that, statistical analysis was performed utilizing comprehensive meta-analysis (CMA) software (version 2.0, Biostat, USA) to report the global prevalence of C. trachomatis in the STD population.ResultIn prevalence studies involving an overall number of 40,159 STD individuals, 17,289 C. trachomatis isolates were collected in 86 countries worldwide, either by culture or by indirect DNA testing. The pooled prevalence of C. trachomatis within the global STD population was 41.6% (95% CI,40.1–43%), while a higher rate was recorded for males (12807 vs. 18871). The prevalence of C. trachomatis infection was highest in Oceania, followed by Europe, America, Asia, and Africa (49.9%, 46.3%, 41.3%, 41.1%, and 36.1%, respectively). According to data from case reports and case series, lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV) and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection were the most common concurrent diseases and co-infections.ConclusionThe elevated prevalence of C. trachomatis in 86 countries suggests that innovative screening and infection-prevention programs are required to reduce the burden of this clinically very significant bacterial infection. Our results support the demand for further fundamental investigations in this area especially among HIV-positive patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11809-2.Keywords: Chlamydia trachomatis, Sexually transmitted diseases, STD, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Sexually Transmitted Diseases (STDs) represent a significant category of infections primarily transmitted through sexual contact [1]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), an estimated 376 million new cases of treatable STDs occur annually, equating to over one million infections daily [2]. Notably, STDs rank among the top 10 health concerns for young adult women globally [3]. Young women are particularly vulnerable to STDs due to factors such as reduced cervical mucus production and a higher prevalence of cervical ectopy. Adolescents, in particular, face the greatest risk of long-term health complications from untreated STDs [4]. However, studies suggest that reported cases account for only 50–80% of all STD occurrences, indicating a significant gap in detection and reporting [3]. Key risk factors for STD susceptibility include unprotected sexual activity, intravenous drug use, and alcohol dependency [5].

STDs are caused by pathogenic microorganisms residing in the male and female genital tracts, including Chlamydia trachomatis (C. trachomatis), Neisseria gonorrhoeae, and Mycoplasma genitalium [1, 6]. Among these, C. trachomatis is a leading cause of STDs in both developed and developing nations. This obligate intracellular Gram-negative bacterium infects the epithelial cells of the lower genital tract in both men and women [7]. In 2023, 394,163 cases of chlamydial infection were reported in 27 European Union/European Economic Area member states, with 39% occurring among individuals aged 20–24 years [8]. Most C. trachomatis infections are asymptomatic, allowing the pathogen to evade immune responses and remain undetected clinically and microbiologically [9, 10]. Prevalence also varies across racial groups; for example, a US study found that the case rate among black individuals was 5.9 times higher than among white individuals [8].

In women, C. trachomatis primarily infects the cervix, leading to conditions such as cervicitis, urethritis, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), perihepatitis, or proctitis. Untreated infections can result in infertility, ectopic pregnancy, and complications during pregnancy, including risks to the fetus [11]. Infants born vaginally from infected mothers may develop chlamydial conjunctivitis or pneumonia [12]. In men, the infection typically causes urethritis and, occasionally, epididymitis, with symptoms such as mild to moderate urethral discharge. If untreated, the infection can spread to the epididymis [13]. The asymptomatic nature of many infections delays diagnosis and treatment, increasing the risk of complications and transmission to partners [14]. Current STD control measures often focus on symptomatic cases, primarily in men, while asymptomatic cases, particularly in women, frequently go undetected until complications arise [6].

Diagnosing C. trachomatis traditionally relies on cultivation, which is technically challenging due to the bacterium’s intracellular replication [15]. Modern molecular diagnostic techniques, such as nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs)—including polymerase chain reaction (PCR), transcription-mediated amplification (TMA), and strand displacement amplification (SDA)—and genetic probe approaches, have been developed to address these challenges. These commercially available tests provide a standardized platform for detecting single or multiple pathogens simultaneously, improving the identification methods of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) [1, 8, 16].

The main aim of this study was to comprehensively analyze the global prevalence and distribution of C. trachomatis infections among patients with STDs across different continents and countries. Additionally, this study aimed to explore the coinfections, diagnostic methods employed, and the types of complications arising from C. trachomatis infections. By examining trends in published research over time, the study also sought to highlight gaps in knowledge and areas requiring further investigation. Ultimately, the findings aim to inform public health strategists for the prevention, diagnosis, and management of C. trachomatis infections worldwide.

Methods

Search strategy

The selection process was based on an extensive literature search for original research papers available on Medline, Web of Science, and Embase databases, published between 2000 and 2023. The keywords used for the systematic search were: “sexually transmitted infections OR sexually transmitted diseases OR STI OR STD AND Chlamydia trachomatis OR C. trachomatis”.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All English original papers that reported the prevalence of C. trachomatis in STD were included in our study. Papers that were focused on patients with other STDs besides C. trachomatis, review articles, duplicate studies, non-clinical studies, and studies without enough data were excluded. Thereafter, three authors (TH, SB, and MA) selected appropriate papers based on inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Data extraction and definitions

Following this initial screening procedure, data extraction was conducted, considering the listed items: first author’s last name, time and/or period of the study, date of publication, region, and all inclusion criteria listed in the previous section. The data extraction was done by two individuals independently (SB and MA), followed by verification by another researcher (TH).

Meta-analysis

The data were processed statistically using comprehensive meta-analysis (CMA) software (version 2.0, Biostat, USA) to create a detailed report on the frequency of C. trachomatis among STD cases. Then, the Q-test and the I2 statistical methods were performed to evaluate the statistical heterogeneity. If heterogeneity is low (I2 > 75%), the fixed-effect model may be appropriate. If heterogeneity is moderate (25% < I2 ≤ 75%) to high (I2 > 75%), the random-effects model is generally preferred. Begg’s test was used to examine publication bias statistically (P value < 0.05 indicated statistically significant publication bias).

Results

Characteristics of included studies

Overall, our systematic search yielded 13,377 records from the three databases. After that, duplicates were eliminated, and 6406 non-duplicate studies remained. Most of the duplicates are due to the fact that the same manuscript is listed in more than one of the databases. Of these, 4972 irrelevant papers were deleted from the list, after their titles and abstracts were checked. Following the full-text screening, 928 unrelated articles were also excluded. Eventually, 506 publications proceeded to the final analysis step (Fig. 1). Of the 40,159 STD population, the most reports belonged to females (18871), followed by males (12807), and transgenders (98), respectively. However, gender was not mentioned in 8383 cases.

Frequency of C. trachomatis among patients with STD across different continents based on prevalence studies

A total of 506 publications reported C. trachomatis infections among patients with STDs, of which 403 articles were classified as prevalence studies. These studies were distributed as follows: 92 from Africa, 84 from Asia, 118 from America, 89 from Europe, and 19 from Oceania (Supplementary Table S1).

An overall frequency of C. trachomatis infections was 41.6% (95% CI 40.1–43) among 40,159 STD patients (Table 1). The funnel plot represented the result of publication bias (Fig. 2). The meta-analysis of epidemiological research discovered that the frequency of C. trachomatis among STD cases was 49.9% (95% CI 45.2–54.6) among 2657 patients in Oceania, 46.3% (95% CI 43.4–49.2) among 8962 patients in Europe, 41.3% (95% CI 38.8–43.8) among 11,015 patients in America, 41.1% (95% CI 38.1–44.1) among 8893 patients in Asia, and 36.1% (95% CI 33.1–39.3) among 8632 patients in Africa (Table 1).

Frequency of C. trachomatis among patients with STD across different countries based on prevalence studies

In general, among STD patients, 17,289 C. trachomatis isolates were collected from patients in 86 countries in the world. USA, China, Brazil, France and South Africa had the highest number of samples. As provided in supplementary Table S1, the highest number of C. trachomatis isolates in STD patients in Oceania was reported from Australia (681/1332, 51.1%) and Papua New Guinea (200/1332, 15%). In Europe, France registered the highest number of samples (1291/4209, 30.6%), followed by Sweden (429/4209, 10.1%). In the USA (3378/4641, 72.7%), Brazil (795/4641, 17.1%), and Peru (267/4641, 5.7%) the highest numbers were scored. China (1970/3865, 50.9%), India (298/3865, 7.7%), and Vietnam (313/3865, 8.09%) in Asia had highest levels of C. trachomatis in STD patients, respectively. The biggest group of STD patients with C. trachomatis infection in Africa originated from South Africa (1340/3242, 41.3%), Uganda (357/3242, 11.01%), and Nigeria (209/3242, 6.4%) respectively. The frequency of C. trachomatis among patients with STD based on case reports and case series

Supplementary Table S2 provides more detailed information on the characteristics of case reports and case series studies included in our review. Of these 430 STD patients (i.e., 195 female, 134 male, 4 transgender, and 97 for which a gender was not reported), the population of infected women was almost 1.4 times that of men. Excluding patients whose age was not reported (n = 38 studies), the mean age of patients was 30.1 years. According to the results of our study, 372 (84.7%) C. trachomatis infections were reported among 430 STD patients from 35 countries, mostly observed in the USA (46 cases), then India (35 cases), France (23 cases), and Spain (23 cases) (Supplementary Table S2).

Additionally, the type of post infectious complications occurring in STD patients after C. trachomatis infection was taken into consideration. Twelve were identified, based on the published literature. Of these, lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV) (33/430, 7.6%), cervical cancer (18/430, 4.1%), urethritis (12/430, 2.7%), proctitis (11/430, 2.5%), and cervicitis (10/430, 2.3%), were the most common. Examination of patients showed that human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) (65/430, 15.1%), herpes simplex virus (HSV) (22/430, 5.1%), human papillomavirus (HPV) (14/430, 3.2%), and hepatitis C virus (HCV) (9/430, 2%), were concurrent infections.

Of the 14 types of isolation sources reported in the studies, urine (98/430, 22.7%), cervical swabs (81/430, 18.8%), rectal swabs (28/430, 6.5%), and blood used for detection of specific IgG and IgM molecules (22/430, 5.1%) were the most common isolation sources associated with C. trachomatis infection.

Regarding the diagnosis of C. trachomatis in STD patients, PCR (238/430, 55.3%) was the predominantly used method, followed by culture (36/439, 8.3%), enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (22/430, 5.1%), ligase chain reaction (LCR) (22/430, 5.1%), and hybrid capture testing (16/430, 3.7%).

Discussion

C. trachomatis genital infection is recognized as one of the most common and generally well-curable bacterial STDs in the world [17]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study evaluating the prevalence and distribution of C. trachomatis in STD patients on a global scale. Our results identified a high prevalence of C. trachomatis infection at 49.9% across 403 included studies, indicating a significant burden of this infection in the studied populations. In contrast, Huai et al., (2020) reported a much lower pooled prevalence of C. trachomatis infection at 2.9% among the general population, based on 29 studies. This striking difference highlights the varying risk levels across different population groups and underscores the need for targeted public health interventions, particularly in high-prevalence settings [18].

Moreover, based on our current data, the prevalence of C. trachomatis infection was highest in women. Females, especially sexually active young women, should be prioritized for routine screening. This is particularly important because C. trachomatis infections are often asymptomatic in women, leading to underdiagnosis and untreated infections. Moreover, reducing the prevalence of C. trachomatis in women can lower healthcare costs associated with treating complications. Similarly, Huai et al., [18] and Rodrigues et al., [19] reported that C. trachomatis cases were mostly females in the general population. In this regard, scientists have suggested the following factors as potential reasons of higher C. trachomatis prevalence in females: (i) the anatomy of the reproductive tract; (ii) cervical ectopy—a physiological condition commonly seen in young women—which exposes the columnar epithelium to infectious agents; (iii) as a consequence of men having more non-monogamous partnerships than women, the chance of a man transmitting C. trachomatis to multiple female partners is higher [18].

Our findings demonstrated Oceania had the highest C. trachomatis prevalence, which was possibly confounded by the low sample size and the small number of studies conducted on this continent. These factors may have introduced bias or overestimated the prevalence, highlighting the need for more robust and representative studies in Oceania to confirm these findings. On the other hand, Europe ranked second in C. trachomatis prevalence, with France reporting the highest number of cases. This could reflect differences in screening practices, healthcare accessibility, or sexual behaviors across European countries. The higher prevalence in France may also indicate effective diagnostic efforts leading to increased case detection. Overall, these findings underscore the importance of addressing regional disparities in C. trachomatis prevalence while considering methodological limitations and contextual factors that may influence the data.

Based on our study, the number of studies on C. trachomatis in STDs has increased significantly over time, reflecting its growing importance as a public health issue. Some regions (including Europe and America) have contributed more studies than others (including Africa and Oceania), possibly due to differences in research funding, infrastructure, and public health priorities. Notably, the mainly factor that causes differences in the number of regional reports is due to the limitations of highly sensitive detection techniques in developing countries. The management and treatment of chlamydia vary significantly across countries, reflecting differences in healthcare systems, diagnostic capabilities, and public health policies. In some regions, symptom management is still adopted due to limited access to diagnostic tools or resources, while in others, chlamydia is often treated as non-gonococcal urethritis without specific testing. In agreement with our findings, Shetty et al., (2021) also demonstrated the necessity of such screening programs, especially in developing and underdeveloped countries, since the number of new cases is increasing rapidly [20].

The mean age of individuals diagnosed with C. trachomatis varies depending on the population studied and regional factors. However, C. trachomatis is most frequently diagnosed in sexually active adolescents and young adults [21]. The results of case reports/case series studies demonstrated that the mean age of STD caused by C. trachomatis was 30.1 years globally. Ngangro et al., (2019) reported that the most affected age groups in females and males infected with C. trachomatis were 15–24 year-olds and 15–29-year-olds, respectively [22]. Regular screening and appropriate education are critical for this age group to reduce transmission and complications.

Symptomatic C. trachomatis disease can be counted as a serious and/or life-threatening condition if not treated, causing PID, endometritis, ectopic pregnancy, and infertility. Nonetheless, it can also lead to less serious pathological presentations such as urethritis and cervicitis [23]. In the present study, chlamydia detection and reporting were more frequent in LGV cases. It should be noted that in a C. trachomatis-positive case, further tests, including molecular techniques to distinguish LGV from non-LGV serovars, are required [24]. The presence of cervical cancer in 4.1% of cases underscores the potential long-term consequences of chronic C. trachomatis infections, which may contribute to inflammation and co-infections with oncogenic viruses like HPV [25]. Urethritis, proctitis, and cervicitis were also notable, reflecting the diverse anatomical sites affected by C. trachomatis and the importance of site-specific diagnostic and treatment strategies.

Following our data assessment, it was revealed that the most common coinfections in the patients with C. trachomatis infection were HIV, HSV, and HPV. In terms of the mechanism of action, C. trachomatis remains inside the cells and evades the cellular defense mechanisms through the cell-mediated immune response for a prolonged period of time [26]. According to the results of a recent systematic review and meta-analysis study conducted by Zhang et al., [27] the overall global co-infection prevalence of C. trachomatis in people living with HIV, was 2.5%. The difference in the studied population indicates a higher prevalence of HIV among Chlamydia-infected individuals compared to a lower rate of Chlamydia in HIV-infected individuals.

Similarly, earlier research has shown that C. trachomatis can promote the transmission and infection of other sexually transmitted viruses, including HIV, HPV, and HSV [28–32]. For example, C. trachomatis increases the susceptibility to HIV by upregulating the expression of HIV-1 primary and co-receptors on epithelial cells. This process weakens the epithelial barrier, making it easier for cell-associated viruses to migrate across it. Additionally, C. trachomatis promotes the recruitment of CD4 + T cells that express HIV co-receptors, C-C chemokine receptor type 5 (CCR5) and C-X-C chemokine receptor type 4 (CXCR4), at infection sites [33]. It is worth mentioning that, HPV is demonstrated as a risk factor of C. trachomatis infection and C. trachomatis also was demonstrated as a risk factor for HPV infection. Moreover, C. trachomatis infection is proven to be strongly associated with HPV persistence [34]. Notably, both HPV and C. trachomatis infections may have played an independent co-factor role in the development of cervical neoplasia [35].

We found that the most common isolation sources for C. trachomatis infection were urine (22.7%), cervical swabs (18.8%), and rectal swabs (6.5%), respectively. For infections of the lower genital tract, the recommended samples for testing are first-void urine for males and vaginal swabs for females [36].

Although there are a variety of techniques (culture- or non-culture-based) available for detecting C. trachomatis infection, NAATs are currently the Gold Standard assays due to their high sensitivity, specificity, and fast turnaround time [37]. Even though culture is shown to have higher specificity, it was not recommended to be used to diagnose C. trachomatis infection as its sensitivity ranged from 3.9 to 80%, which is associated with high numbers of false negatives (~ 39%) [19]. Based on our results, PCR was primarily used as the preferred C. trachomatis diagnostic method (55.3%), followed by culture at 8.3%. The reliance on PCR underscores the need for accessible and affordable molecular diagnostic tools, particularly in resource-limited settings where C. trachomatis infections are prevalent.

It is also worthwhile to discuss the available treatment options for STD patients with C. trachomatis although this was not a main target of the current literature investigation. According to the analysis performed in the present study, the most commonly prescribed antibiotics included azithromycin, doxycycline, and ofloxacin. According to the WHO guidelines, azithromycin, as well as doxycycline, have been recommended as first-line treatments for C. trachomatis. Generally, doxycycline is considered as the first-line therapy due to its higher efficacy in uncomplicated genital infections, and the others are counted as substitute options to prescribe in cases of drug contraindication, resistance, or other reasons, based on WHO, International Union Against Sexually Transmitted Infections (IUSTI), and centers for disease control and prevention (CDC) guidelines [38]. We did not investigate the clinical and epidemiological relevance of antibiotic resistance in C. trachomatis.

There are several limitations to our current study. First, we could not demonstrate the morbidity and mortality rate of C. trachomatis among STD patients because the patients’ outcomes were not generally reported. Second, considering that in most studies there were no reports of C. trachomatis infection being symptomatic or asymptomatic, we could not discuss these two conditions in STD patients. Third, the aim of the present study was to determine the prevalence of C. trachomatis among all patients with STDs. Therefore, the specific prevalence of C. trachomatis in different patient groups, including infertile compared to fertile or patients visiting STD clinics was not detailed. This could have provided a better understanding of the role of STDs in many other diseases. Fourth, since the average age of patients with C. trachomatis was not mentioned in all studies, we were unable to perform subgroup analysis based on the prevalence of this STD across different age groups.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we noted a high prevalence of C. trachomatis infection among STD groups across the globe between the years 2000 and 2023. It was also found that there is a high prevalence of chlamydial infection among STD patients in all continents, especially Oceania. This implies an urgent public health threat, as undetected and untreated C. trachomatis infections may cause serious complications in both males and females. Rapid diagnostic testing and subsequently proper treatment of this infection could protect people from PID and infertility. Moreover, C. trachomatis screening should be expanded among high-risk individuals, especially when they have HIV (co-)infection. The increase of more than 5 times in the number of studies published during the last decade is an alarm for health policy planners and decision makers to try and better control chlamydial infection in all global societies.
