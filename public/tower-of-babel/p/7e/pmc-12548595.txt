Emergency department-initiated palliative care screening among older adults: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol

Introduction

The US Census Bureau expects the total population of older adults (individuals aged 65 years and older) to more than double to over 80 million by 2040.1 Many of these patients are also terminally ill (mortality expected in 1 year) or otherwise compromised and hence present to the emergency department (ED) with complex and life-threatening conditions such as chronic cancer, late-stage liver disease and congestive heart failure, as well as terminal neurodegenerative ailments, including Alzheimer’s disease/related dementias, multiple sclerosis and Parkinson’s disease.2 ED encounters are often a pivotal point in a seriously ill patient’s course of care (especially for older adults)3 as they serve as both an initial point of entry for care as well as a junction for further medical treatment. Given that care in the ED often establishes the trajectory for additional interventions and can be stressful or potentially harmful for older patients, palliative care clinicians and models of care can help ensure patients receive goal-concordant care and effective management of symptoms.

In a clinical setting, palliative care is defined as the provision of active and holistic care to minimise pain and suffering for individuals across all ages with severe and life-limiting health conditions, including but not limited to those nearing the end of their life.4 Palliative care focuses on improving the quality of life (QOL) through aggressive symptom management, clarification of care goals and interdisciplinary support for patients and their families. The provision of palliative care has been shown to improve patient QOL, reduce healthcare utilisation and costs and the need for additional medical procedures, and improve patient health outcomes.5

As more older adults with serious illnesses present to the ED, it is increasingly important to screen for and identify individuals who will benefit most from palliative care interventions. Currently, screening for palliative care needs in the ED is both feasible in execution and helpful in producing positive health outcomes, especially with the wide range of methods and tools available for screening. Despite this feasibility and a growing literature on the general application of palliative care in the ED, there are no current recommendations for screening older adults for palliative care needs. In addition, there are a few systematic reviews regarding ED-based palliative care screening in the older adult population.6,8 Thus, we hope that our execution of a broad study across existing medical research that further analyses the efficacy of palliative care screening in an emergency setting while including additional outcomes (such as length of stay (LOS)) and adhering to gold standard Cochrane and Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) methodologies will drive further inquiry into the question while providing optimal outcomes for patients and clinicians alike.

Our systematic review builds on existing research on palliative care screening in the ED by addressing the lack of strictly standardised (eg, GRADE level) and age-specific reviews, while evaluating patient-centred outcomes.9 Specifically, the majority of existing systematic reviews on the provision of palliative care in the ED did not exclusively focus on older adults, even if an older adult majority existed within the study population. This focus on older adults is particularly pertinent, especially given prior research showing differences in palliative care needs and effects in younger versus older individuals.10,12 As such, we aim to conduct a systematic review to synthesise peer-reviewed data related to the impact of ED-based palliative care screening on health and health service outcomes such as the length of stay, percentage of hospitalisation, cost of care and QOL in older adults. Thus, the novelty of our review lies mainly within three domains: (1) a focus on older adult populations, unlike prior studies; (2) an examination of palliative care screening’s effectiveness on patient-centric outcomes (eg, LOS) and (3) adherence to standardised GRADE-level guidance and rigorous risk-of-bias assessments (ROB 2.0, ROBINS-I). To this end, we hypothesise that those who are screened for palliative care needs will have better patient-centred health outcomes. To establish and define our population of interest, this project will be part of the Geriatric Emergency Department Guidelines 2.0 effort, which intends to create transparent GRADE-level guidance on improving geriatric quality of care.13,17

Methods and analysis

Our protocol describes an outline for a systematic review evaluating the efficacy of palliative care screening guidelines in the ED. The review was submitted to the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews. This systematic review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols standard (Research Checklist).

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria are (1) original peer-reviewed research articles, (2) studies that identify patients who might benefit from palliative care consults, (3) studies only regarding older adults (patients aged 60 years or older)18 and (4) have sample sizes of 50 or greater. Only studies explicitly stated to take place in the ED (or equivalents) will be considered. There is no current focus on any specific pathology or clinical specialty.

We will exclude all case reports, literature searches, systematic reviews and studies relating to emergency medical services or prehospital care. A sample size of 50 was selected as the minimum threshold to ensure statistical robustness and reliability in estimation, as smaller sample sizes often do not account for moderate effect sizes given a standard level of significance (eg, α=0.05) and power (eg, 0.80) when applying standard statistical tests.19,21

Only peer-reviewed studies were chosen due to the uncertainty in data quality in non-peer-reviewed studies, such as thesis documents and unpublished articles.

Population

Older adult patients, defined as those 60 years of age or older presenting to the ED with terminal illness, serve as the population in this study. Serious illness is defined as a chronic, incurable condition with high mortality (expected mortality in 1 year) that negatively affects the QOL of patients and caregivers. These conditions include advanced chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart failure, kidney disease, dementia, neurological conditions and cancer.22 23 Instead of excluding studies based on ED-presenting diagnoses (eg, sepsis), we used chronic, comorbid conditions to identify eligible studies.

Intervention

Here, we defined ‘screening for palliative care needs’ as any systematic evaluation that identifies patients who may need palliative care and helps determine their needs in the ED (eg, physical, functional, emotional, social and spiritual needs). In other words, we selected for any form of palliative care screening in the ED while excluding mandated referral. However, we intend to pool only within specific screening instruments and methods (eg, Palliative Performance Scale (PPS), etc).

Comparison or comparator

This study compares groups that have or have not received ED-based palliative care screening.

Outcome

The primary outcome considered is inpatient length of stay, measured as the time from the initial admission to the ED until discharge. The index admission is considered if it is reported in days (unit) or is part of prospective tracking or retrospective chart review. Our secondary outcome is the percentage of hospitalisations. The percentage of hospitalisation is measured as (1) the frequency of hospital admission for intervention and control groups, (2) the proportion of ED patients in the study who were screened or not screened or (3) the relative risk of hospitalisation for ED patients for screened and unscreened patients. We will also examine any cost of care, which includes either (1) the weighted estimate of total charges, (2) estimated direct cost of hospitalisation for ED patients admitted in the study, (3) absolute change in cost difference for ED patients admitted in the study (cost for intervention vs control groups), (4) relative change in cost difference for ED patients admitted in the study (cost for intervention vs control groups) or (5) the calculated return on investment for the ED intervention.

Furthermore, we will also consider the following as secondary outcomes if they are applicable: (1) changes in code status (as defined by any change in code status designation during the length of the ED/index admission), (2) 6-month re-presentation rate (measured by the number of inpatient admissions and ED visits during a 6-month period following the index ED visits), (3) proportion of intervention versus control group with death during index encounter and the relative risk of death between interventions and control group, as well as (4) patient, caregiver and clinician satisfaction, (5) intensive care unit admission and (6) QOL. We also included ED visit mortality (indicated by the frequency of death during an index encounter) because most studies do not include long-term mortality in their data.

While we do not plan to conduct any subgroup analyses between age groups, we will still perform subgroup analyses between screening tools (eg, PPS, etc) and between high- and low-risk-of-bias studies.

Given the heterogeneous nature of palliative care screening tools, screening for fidelity measures in a systematic review is not feasible and out of the scope of this review. Additionally, existing methodological systematic reviews and meta-analyses already focus on this issue.

Design

This systematic review will consider only studies with an applicable comparator. As such, randomised controlled trials (RCTs), cohort studies, prospective longitudinal studies, quality improvement studies and controlled before-and-after studies will all be considered, while simple observational studies, uncontrolled trials and other studies without comparators will not.

Search methods, information sources, study selection and data management

A comprehensive electronic search of the medical and rehabilitation literature using medical subject headings (MeSH) and text related to responsiveness to the application of palliative care in the ED (online supplemental file 1). This study will undertake a comprehensive electronic search of existing medical literature surrounding the use and development of palliative care guidelines for ED-based care, specifically those focused on screening and selecting for the need for end-of-life-related procedures. Ovid MEDLINE, Embase, EBSCO CINAHL, Web of Science and Cochrane will be the primary online databases for this systematic review. The search will be executed using a combined text and MeSH search strategy. Online supplemental information 1 provides the search strategies used in this study. Studies not authored in English will be given to a co-investigator fluent in the language to determine eligibility for inclusion. Only fully published and peer-reviewed studies will be considered. Citations will be exported to Covidence, a systematic review software used throughout the study selection process. Two team members will independently screen the title and abstract of each reference. All items will be reviewed independently and in duplicate. Every author will participate in this process. Two team members will then independently review the full-text studies to determine final inclusion. Any disagreements will be resolved with a third reviewer as a tiebreaker. More specifically, Covidence will be used to remove duplicate studies. In the first phase of the search, two independent reviewers will assess each study by titles and abstracts for relevance and inclusion while being blind to the other reviewer’s decision. A third independent reviewer will then adjudicate instances of disagreement. In the second phase, for studies deemed potentially relevant, two independent reviewers will assess the full text of each study for inclusion. Two reviewers will then use a standard data extraction form to independently extract data on study design, study setting, study size, inpatient length of stay, percentage of hospitalisation and hospitalisation costs. Authors will then be contacted for further information if necessary.

Data collection process and data items

Two reviewers will independently extract data in duplicate. A collection form formatted as a ‘decision tree’ containing the inclusion and exclusion criteria will be used throughout the selection process. Eligible studies will have the following data extracted: author(s), publication year, study design, single site versus multisite collection, country, inclusion/exclusion criteria, recruitment time frame, follow-up length, total sample size, the proportion of adults ≥60 years of age, definition and timing of outcomes, number of events, baseline demographics (eg, age, sex, frailty status/score, triage acuity), unit of change for continuous outcomes, classification for categorical outcomes, the unadjusted and adjusted point estimates of risk and lower and upper CIs. Extracted data will be rounded to two decimal places and imported into a guided spreadsheet. The screening tool used in the study, whether that be a custom or previously published tool, will also be recorded in the spreadsheet. Examples of such palliative care screening tools include the Surprise Question, SPEED, P-CaRES and SPICT, with others noted where reported.

Risk of bias in individual studies

We will examine the risk of bias within each study, individually and in duplicate. We will leverage the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) 2.0 assessment for clinical trials.24 Specifically, the risk of bias will be determined by examining five domains: the randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement and selection of the reported result. The ROBINS-I bias assessment25 instrument will evaluate observational studies for confounding factors, intervention classification, participant selection, missing data, measurement of outcomes and reported outcomes. Each study will be rated for low or high risk of bias, and any article with a high risk of bias across any single domain will be classified as high risk of bias regardless of the study’s design or methodology.

Addressing existing meta-biases is critical to ensuring the integrity and applicability of our systematic literature reviews and GRADE-level clinical practice guideline development. This is especially true given the nascent state of geriatric emergency medicine (EM) research and the scarcity of RCTs in EM palliative care. This evaluation will assess each study’s methodology and execution to identify potential internal biases such as selection, performance or detection, ensuring only high-quality data informs our guidelines. Furthermore, we will actively reach out to study authors to obtain additional geriatric-specific data and analysis not included in published articles. This effort will help fill data gaps, clarify ambiguous results and provide access to raw data, which is invaluable for re-analyses or assessing the robustness of reported findings.

We also plan to conduct sensitivity analyses to determine how robust our results are to changes in inclusion criteria or analysis methods. Specifically, sensitivity analyses will be performed should there be a truncated distribution of screening tools or risk of bias (eg., if there were only one high RoB study among 10 low RoB studies). This will allow us to assess the impact of including or excluding studies with a high risk of bias and to examine how different data assumptions might affect the overall conclusions of the review. Our team also serves as an expert panel in palliative care and EM and will convene to review the study selection and data extraction processes. Their clinical expertise and insights into the relevance and quality of studies ensure that our review captures all pertinent research and appropriately interprets findings in the context of existing clinical practices.

Evidence certainty

The GRADE approach will be employed to determine the confidence in our estimates. Confidence will be rated as high, moderate, low or very low, as dictated by the GRADE framework. Funnel plots will be produced to examine the distribution of positive and negative findings for meta-analyses with more than ten studies to detect bias. An individual assessment of confidence will be given for each outcome. We will consider how congruent our pooled estimates are between our sensitivity analyses (which include the studies deemed insignificant regarding the predictors of interest) when discussing the confidence in our estimates.

Data synthesis and meta-analysis estimation

For each Patient/Population, Intervention, Comparison, and Outcome (PICO) question, data from included studies will be synthesised qualitatively via summary tables and a narrative assessment of the evidence. Following this, the strength of the evidence will be assessed and reported for the studies. We anticipate significant heterogeneity among our studies. If adequate studies (>2) evaluate and report the same intervention and outcome, a meta-analysis will be performed. I2, a standard measure of variability caused by differences between trials, will be calculated to assess for heterogeneity.26 27 As I2 depends on the number of participants rather than the number of studies, we will only employ this statistic if we have more than 200 individuals per polling. We also plan to use a random-effects model given heterogeneity in models of care (instead of heterogeneity in point estimates). In addition to this, we will report continuous outcomes as mean differences and dichotomous outcomes by relative and absolute effects, while reporting narratively any data that cannot be pooled. Finally, we will attempt to supplement missing metrics of data normality (eg, CIs, IQRs, SDs) from the data provided.

Patient and public involvement

No patients or the public are involved in this systematic review. Here, patient involvement is not required in a systematic review, as the review analyses existing data for the efficacy of palliative care screening techniques rather than seeking to collect new data. While patient input can help prioritise outcomes or inform relevance, it is not essential for the core functions of a systematic review, particularly in the evidence collection and data synthesis stage.

Discussion

This systematic review will assess whether palliative care screening techniques in the ED for older adults with serious illness may reduce length of stay and admission, diminish care costs, and improve QOL.28 29 This is especially important as emergency clinicians are increasingly caring for older adults with serious illnesses whose medical needs and goals of care may differ from the traditional ED population. Thus, it is essential to successfully screen and identify who requires palliative care and who does not, as well as the appropriate interventions and recommendations for each situation. As such, we hope that our systematic review will help determine if screening improves health outcomes among older adults.

Minimising the length of stay and admission, reducing costs of care and increasing patient QOL represent important goals for physicians and care providers not only in the ED but in other disciplines and fields of medicine as well. This is especially true considering the rapidly growing senior population and their predicted morbidities.30,33 This review aims to evaluate and summarise the evidence surrounding these questions to provide insight and direction into the development of screening methods for patients presenting to the ED who may benefit from palliative or end-of-life care.

We foresee several limitations. First, there may be publication bias as we only review peer-reviewed published studies. Also, current data may not be able to answer our PICO question thoroughly and reliably since research surrounding this field is still developing.

Ethics and dissemination

The proposed study does not involve the collection of primary data. Therefore, no ethical or internal review board approval is required. The systematic review and meta-analysis results will be presented in a peer-reviewed journal and at conferences.
