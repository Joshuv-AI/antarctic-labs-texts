Breast Reconstruction in a Female Patient With Poland Syndrome
Authors: Ahmed Elsaftawy, Marta Jagosz, Paweł Stajniak, Patryk Ostrowski
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Summary:Poland syndrome (PS) is a congenital anomaly characterized by unilateral hypoplasia or absence of the pectoralis major muscle, often associated with breast asymmetry. Achieving symmetry while preserving the contralateral breast remains a challenge in reconstruction. We present a case of a 19-year-old woman with left-sided PS, a fully developed right breast, and limited autologous soft tissue (body mass index 19.8 kg/m2). A 2-stage approach was performed: initial tissue expansion followed by implant-based reconstruction with fat grafting. A 395-mL microtextured polyurethane implant was placed, and 130 mL of fat graft was injected to optimize contour. The procedures were uneventful, and the patient achieved satisfactory symmetry in size. However, nipple–areola height asymmetry occurred, which the patient declined to have corrected. This report expanded on previous literature by illustrating how a hybrid expander–implant–fat grafting approach was tailored to lean patients with PS, offering a reproducible and less invasive strategy compared with traditional flap-based reconstructions.

Poland syndrome (PS) is a rare congenital musculoskeletal disorder characterized by unilateral absence or hypoplasia of the pectoralis major muscle. It is frequently associated with breast hypoplasia; amastia; chest wall deformities; and, occasionally, ipsilateral upper limb abnormalities.1 Due to the varied presentation, reconstruction requires individualized planning to restore form and function.

Breast reconstruction in PS poses challenges, especially when the contralateral breast is fully developed. The goal is to restore volume and achieve symmetry in shape, projection, and positioning of the inframammary fold (IMF) and nipple–areolar complex (NAC). With an absent pectoral muscle, soft tissue coverage over the implant must be carefully planned.

We present a case of a 19-year-old woman with left-sided PS, marked by breast and pectoralis major hypoplasia and a normal contralateral breast. Given her low body mass index (19.8 kg/m2) and limited donor tissue, we performed a 2-stage reconstruction with tissue expansion, implant, and autologous fat grafting.

CASE REPORT

A 19-year-old woman presented with left breast and pectoralis major muscle hypoplasia consistent with PS. The right breast was fully developed, making symmetry the primary challenge (Fig. 1). The patient had no significant medical history, and her body mass index was 19.8 kg/m2, limiting available autologous soft tissue. Preoperative ultrasonography confirmed the absence of the left pectoralis major muscle and helped assess the chest wall anatomy to guide surgical planning. Given the well-formed right breast, the reconstructive plan focused on the left breast, addressing shape and size matching, alignment of the IMF, preservation of a natural intermammary distance, and optimization of NAC positioning. A 2-stage approach was planned with tissue expansion to stretch soft tissue and create space, followed by implant-based augmentation using a microtextured polyurethane implant with autologous fat grafting.

Photographs presenting the patient with PS, without (A) and with (B) preoperative markings. The breast asymmetry is significant.

Under general anesthesia, the planned sternal gap was marked to match the right breast. A new IMF was designed at the same level as the right side (Fig. 1). The expander used was a Mentor 450-mL round, high-profile, smooth surface model without tabs. Its base diameter matched the footprint of the contralateral breast, with the IMF surgically lowered for symmetry. Projection was estimated based on the patient’s bra cup size, and the pocket was dissected to mirror the width and medial contour of the healthy side, ensuring balanced cleavage. The final volume was guided by intraoperative judgment and adjusted through serial expansions during follow-up.

A remote filling port was selected and placed laterally via a subcutaneous tunnel to minimize the risk of damage during expansion, particularly given the limited soft tissue envelope. Weekly saline injections were performed, ranging from 20 to 40 mL, guided by clinical assessment of skin tension. Once 400 mL was achieved, a second-stage procedure was performed with placement of a 395-mL anatomical polyurethane (Replicon, Polytech) implant. The anatomical design provided optimal projection and shape in the absence of pectoralis coverage. Liposuction from the waist area yielded 130 mL of fat, enriched with platelet-rich plasma, and transferred to the medial, upper, and lateral poles to refine the transition zones and compensate for muscular deficiency. The surgery was completed without complications.

Both surgical stages were uneventful, and the patient achieved a satisfactory aesthetic outcome. The reconstructed breast closely matched the contralateral side in size and shape, allowing the patient to wear swimwear and go braless with confidence. Considerable asymmetry in NAC height remained (Fig. 2). This is a recognized challenge in congenital breast deformities, particularly in the absence of muscular support. Although contralateral mastopexy was considered to reduce the asymmetry, the patient declined further intervention, expressing satisfaction with the outcome.

DISCUSSION

Tissue expansion ensures skin laxity for implant placement while minimizing tension-related complications. In our case, 12 weeks of gradual expansion allowed soft tissue adaptation and a natural breast contour before implant insertion. This aligns with studies supporting 2-stage expander-to-implant methods for PS patients.1 Polyurethane-coated implants reduce the risk of capsular contracture and promote better tissue adherence.2 Given the congenital absence of the pectoralis major, achieving a smooth transition at the upper pole and medial breast required fat grafting. Platelet-rich plasma-enhanced fat grafting has been shown to improve graft survival and minimize resorption rates.3

Despite achieving good breast symmetry, NAC height asymmetry remained, a frequent issue in congenital deformities. Although NAC repositioning or contralateral mastopexy could improve symmetry, the patient preferred to avoid further surgery. Breast reconstruction enhances psychosocial and sexual well-being in PS patients. Less invasive techniques are associated with lower emotional distress than autologous reconstruction.4 Many patients prioritize symmetry without compromising the unaffected breast.5 Thus, shared decision-making is key to choosing the right approach.

Our aim was not to introduce a novel technique but to demonstrate a reproducible strategy for congenital asymmetry in patients desiring less invasive treatment. Although implant–fat grafting combinations have been described, this case highlights a tailored approach for lean patients with limited donor tissue. Flap-based options such as latissimus dorsi or deep inferior epigastric perforator flaps were less suitable due to invasiveness and recovery demands. In contrast, our hybrid method achieved excellent outcomes with minimal burden. Key technical considerations included gradual tissue expansion based on skin tension, remote-port expander placement to reduce mechanical risk, and strategic fat distribution to optimize contour and minimize implant visibility. Importantly, this patient-centered approach, exemplified by the shared decision to avoid further correction of NAC asymmetry, underscores the importance of aligning surgical planning with patient priorities, especially in cases involving congenital breast deformities. We believe this approach may serve as a practical guide for similar cases involving congenital breast deformities.
