Dexamethasone-induced 3D cortical spheroid model of stress-related neuroplasticity impairment
Authors: Mi Kyoung Seo, Jung Goo Lee, Jung An Lee, Ji Won Park, Deok-Gyeong Kang, Dae-Hyun Seog, Myoung Hun Kim, Sehoon Jeong, Sung Woo Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThree-dimensional (3D) in vitro models of depression are valuable platforms for investigating disease pathophysiology, elucidating the mechanisms of antidepressant action, assessing drug efficacy, and facilitating the development of novel therapeutics. In our previous study, 3D cortical spheroids exposed to the synthetic glucocorticoid dexamethasone exhibited marked impairments in neuroplasticity, representing a key pathological feature of depression. The present study aimed to evaluate the applicability of this model under escitalopram treatment and to elucidate the molecular mechanisms involved. Primary rat cortical cell-derived 3D spheroids were exposed to dexamethasone (100 µM) and subsequently treated with escitalopram at concentrations of 0.1, 1, and 10 µM. Western blot analysis was performed to measure brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), mTORC1–related signaling proteins, and synaptic proteins including PSD-95 and GluA1. Neurite outgrowth was visualized via immunofluorescence and quantified using Sholl analysis. Dexamethasone significantly reduced BDNF expression, neurite complexity, and phosphorylation of mTORC1, 4E-BP1, and p70S6K, along with decreasing synaptic protein levels. Escitalopram dose–dependently reversed these deficits, with the most pronounced effects observed at 10 µM. These findings demonstrate that escitalopram is associated with enhanced neuroplasticity and increased activation of mTORC1 signaling in dexamethasone-treated cortical spheroids, validating this system as a 3D in vitro for mechanistic investigation of stress-related neuroplasticity impairment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47738-6.Keywords: Escitalopram, Dexamethasone, in vitro model of depression, mTORC1 signaling, Neuroplasticity, Cortical spheroidSubject terms: Diseases, Drug discovery, Neuroscience

Introduction

Depression is a prevalent mental disorder affecting approximately 3.8% of the global population1. It is clinically characterized by anhedonia, persistent low mood, and functional impairment. Beyond its personal impact, depression imposes a substantial societal burden, contributing to increased suicide risk, reduced productivity, and diminished quality of life2,3. Currently, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs) are the most widely prescribed pharmacotherapies for alleviating depressive symptoms. Although these agents are generally effective, conventional antidepressants have notable limitations. More than one-third of patients experience treatment-resistant depression, showing inadequate response to existing therapies, and the onset of therapeutic effects typically requires several weeks to months. This delayed treatment response represents a critical risk factor, particularly for patients exhibiting suicidal ideation or behavior4. Therefore, there is an urgent need for the development of novel pharmacological agents with rapid and robust antidepressant effects. Elucidating the precise therapeutic mechanisms of existing antidepressants may facilitate the identification of novel molecular targets, thereby enabling the development of agents with greater efficacy and faster onset of action. Although the pathophysiology of depression remains incompletely understood, accumulating evidence indicates that impaired neuroplasticity represents one of the key mechanisms underlying the disorder5–8. To date, antidepressants have been proposed to exert their therapeutic effects, at least in part, by enhancing neuroplasticity9–12.

Research on the molecular pathways regulating neuroplasticity has primarily focused on the activation of the mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) and the brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF)/tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) signaling pathways12. Notably, significant reductions in the activity of these pathways have been observed in the prefrontal cortex of patients with depression, underscoring the critical role of mTORC1 in the pathophysiology of the disorder13,14. mTORC1 integrates upstream signals from phosphatidylinositol-3 kinase (PI3K)/Akt and extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2 (ERK1/2) via α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methylisoxazole-4-propionic acid (AMPA) and TrkB receptors, subsequently activating the downstream effectors eukaryotic initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1) and p70 ribosomal protein S6 kinase (p70S6K). This signaling cascade regulates neuronal protein synthesis, including the production of synaptic proteins such as postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD-95), glutamate receptor subunit GluA1, and BDNF15. Accordingly, activation of mTORC1 function, followed by the upregulation of mTORC1–dependent synaptic proteins and the synthesis of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), may constitute a key mechanism underlying antidepressant effects. Indeed, previous studies have reported that the antidepressant effects of ketamine-an N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist-as well as certain conventional antidepressants, are mediated through increased mTORC1 activity, leading to enhanced synaptogenesis16–19.

To investigate drug mechanisms of action, most studies have traditionally employed two-dimensional (2D) cell culture systems, such as immortalized cell lines or primary neuronal cultures20–22. However, simple 2D culture systems fail to replicate the structural and functional complexity of the brain. In contrast, three-dimensional (3D) cell culture systems can more accurately recapitulate the brain microenvironment, including its specialized architecture, biochemical properties, and neuron-to-neuron communication and signal transduction23. Moreover, 3D cultures exhibit enhanced neuronal survival and differentiation compared with conventional 2D cultures24. Therefore, in vitro 3D models of depression may provide a more effective platform than conventional 2D systems for elucidating the molecular mechanisms of antidepressant action. In our previous work, we established 3D cortical spheroids from primary rat cortical cells and developed an in vitro 3D depression model by exposing them to the synthetic glucocorticoid dexamethasone, thereby modeling sustained glucocorticoid receptor activation associated with stress-related hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis dysregulation, a hallmark of depression. Dexamethasone exposure impaired neuroplasticity in these spheroids by downregulating mTORC1 signaling. In subsequent studies, it will be essential to evaluate the effects of antidepressants, such as SSRIs or ketamine, in this model to further validate its utility as a robust in vitro 3D model of depression. Escitalopram was chosen as a representative SSRI due to its high selectivity for the serotonin transporter and previous evidence linking SSRI treatment to activation of the mTORC1 pathway25. This 3D spheroid system provides an intermediated-complex platform for mechanistic studies within a controlled in vitro environment.

In the present study, we aimed to evaluate the utility of this system as a 3D in vitro model of depression characterized by impaired neuroplasticity. As a representative SSRI, escitalopram was selected for investigation. To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying escitalopram-induced neuroplasticity, we examined whether escitalopram treatment is associated with modulation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway in dexamethasone-exposed 3D cortical spheroids.

Results

Effects of escitalopram on BDNF expression and neurite outgrowth in 3D cortical spheroids exposed to dexamethasone

In this study, we employed a previously developed and characterized 3D in vitro model of depression26 to assess the effects of different doses of escitalopram (0.1, 1, and 10 µM) under dexamethasone treatment.

To assess whether escitalopram increases BDNF expression in a 3D in vitro model of depression, cortical spheroids exposed to dexamethasone (100 µM) were treated with escitalopram (0.1, 1, and 10 µM). One-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect on BDNF levels (F[4,10] = 5.370, p = 0.014, η2p = 0.682, 95% CI [0.489, 0.924]). Dexamethasone treatment significantly reduced BDNF expression to 68.5% of the control value (p = 0.033), and this reduction was restored by escitalopram in a dose–dependent manner, with a significant increase observed at 10 µM (102.0% of control, p = 0.025; Fig. 1A).

To determine whether escitalopram enhances neuroplasticity in this model, cortical spheroids were cultured with dexamethasone (100 µM) and treated with escitalopram (0.1, 1, and 10 µM). Neurite outgrowth was visualized by immunofluorescence staining with an anti–MAP-2 antibody (Fig. 1B) and quantified using the Sholl Analysis plugin in ImageJ software (Fig. 1C). Dexamethasone treatment reduced the total number of neurite intersections within the 200–500 μm radial range from the spheroid center by approximately 22% compared with controls (control vs. dexamethasone: 138.31 ± 5.03 vs. 107.29 ± 4.95 intersections at 300 μm; 99.92 ± 5.64 vs. 63.38 ± 5.13 intersections at 400 μm; Fig. 1C). Under dexamethasone conditions, escitalopram increased the total number of intersections in a concentration-dependent manner (dexamethasone vs. dexamethasone + escitalopram 0.1 µM: 107.29 ± 4.95 vs. 129.91 ± 6.40 intersections at 300 μm, 63.38 ± 5.13 vs. 89.34 ± 6.80 intersections at 400 μm; dexamethasone vs. dexamethasone + escitalopram 1 µM: 107.29 ± 4.95 vs. 141.12 ± 5.84 intersections at 300 μm, 63.38 ± 5.13 vs. 109.94 ± 6.96 intersections at 400 μm; dexamethasone vs. dexamethasone + escitalopram 10 µM: 107.29 ± 4.95 vs. 152.94 ± 5.04 intersections at 300 μm, 63.38 ± 5.13 vs. 124.66 ± 7.68 intersections at 400 μm; Fig. 1C). To compare overall neurite outgrowth among groups, the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated from Sholl plots and averaged for each group. One-way ANOVA revealed a significant effect of treatment on AUC (F[4,163] = 11.300, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.217, 95% CI [0.117, 0.317]; Fig. 1D). Dexamethasone significantly decreased AUC compared with controls (p = 0.001). Escitalopram produced a significant, dose-dependent increase in AUC under dexamethasone conditions, with robust increases observed at 1 µM (p < 0.001) and 10 µM (p < 0.001), indicating a consistent enhancement of neurite outgrowth.

Effects of escitalopram on mTORC1 signaling in 3D cortical spheroids exposed to dexamethasone

To examine the effects of escitalopram on mTORC1 signaling in a 3D in vitro model of depression, phosphorylation levels of mTORC1, 4E-BP1, and p70S6K, as well as the expression levels of the synaptic proteins PSD-95 and GluA1, were measured by Western blotting.

One-way ANOVA revealed significant differences among groups for mTORC1 (F[4,15] = 8.220, p = 0.001, η2p = 0.687, 95% CI [0.366, 0.950]; Fig. 2A), 4E-BP1 (F[4,10] = 11.400, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.820, 95% CI [0.790, 0.976]; Fig. 2B), p70S6K (F[4,10] = 12.600, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.835, 95% CI [0.793, 0.978]; Fig. 2C), PSD-95 (F[4,15] = 7.790, p = 0.001, η2p = 0.675, 95% CI [0.546, 0.804]; Fig. 3A), and GluA1 (F[4,15] = 4.260, p = 0.017, η2p = 0.532, 95% CI [0.423, 0.924]; Fig. 3D). Dexamethasone treatment significantly reduced the phosphorylation levels of mTORC1 (72.5% of control, p = 0.045), 4E-BP1 (64.7% of control, p = 0.013), and p70S6K (78.6% of control, p = 0.036), as well as the expression levels of PSD-95 (69.4% of control, p = 0.026) and GluA1 (68.7% of control, p = 0.017). Under dexamethasone conditions, escitalopram treatment at concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 10 µM increased the phosphorylation of mTORC1, 4E-BP1, and p70S6K, as well as the expression of PSD-95 and GluA1, with significant increases observed at 10 µM (phospho-Ser2448-mTORC1: 119.0% of control, p < 0.001; phospho-Thr37/46–4E-BP1: 113.0% of control, p = 0.001; phospho-Thr389-p70S6K: 122.0% of control, p < 0.001; PSD-95: 114.0% of control, p = 0.001; GluA1: 95.3% of control, p = 0.048). Immunofluorescence analysis confirmed a widespread distribution of PSD-95 and GluA1 throughout the cortical spheroids, including prominent expression in neurites (Fig. 3B and E). Quantitative analysis of PSD-95 immunofluorescence revealed a significant group effect (F[2,33] = 11.700, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.415, 95% CI [0.183, 0.572]; Fig. 3C). Dexamethasone treatment significantly reduced PSD-95 fluorescence intensity compared with control spheroids (p < 0.001). Under dexamethasone conditions, escitalopram treatment (10 µM) significantly increased PSD-95 fluorescence intensity compared with dexamethasone alone (p = 0.038), indicating partial restoration of synaptic protein expression. Similarly, quantitative analysis of GluA1 fluorescence intensity demonstrated a significant group effect (F[2,33] = 6.670, p = 0.004, η2p = 0.288, 95% CI [0.071, 0.444]; Fig. 3F). Dexamethasone significantly decreased GluA1 fluorescence intensity compared with controls (p = 0.009). Escitalopram treatment (10 µM) significantly increased GluA1 fluorescence intensity relative to dexamethasone treatment alone (p = 0.009), further supporting the restoration of synaptic protein expression.

Discussion

In this study, we validated the utility of a previously established 3D in vitro model of depression. Exposure of 3D cortical spheroids to 100 µM dexamethasone significantly reduced cell viability, BDNF levels, and neurite outgrowth, and downregulated the mTORC1 signaling pathway, including phospho-mTORC1, phospho-4E-BP1, phospho-p70S6K, and the synaptic proteins PSD-95 and GluA1. Treatment with the selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor escitalopram was associated with enhanced neuroplasticity accompanied by increased activation of mTORC1 signaling in this 3D in vitro model.

The HPA axis is a stress hormone system composed of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) secreted from the hypothalamus, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) secreted from the pituitary gland, and glucocorticoids (primarily cortisol) secreted from the adrenal cortex27. These hormones regulate their secretion via a negative feedback loop. Depression is associated with dysregulation of the HPA axis, such as glucocorticoid hypersecretion resulting from disruption of this negative feedback mechanism28. Antidepressant treatment has been shown to normalize HPA axis hyperactivity and reduce glucocorticoid secretion28. To model HPA axis dysregulation characterized by glucocorticoid hypersecretion, the synthetic glucocorticoid dexamethasone was applied to cortical spheroid cultures. To date, the neurobiological effects of glucocorticoids have been investigated in a variety of in vitro systems, including animal primary neuronal cultures, ex vivo brain slices, human neuroblastoma cell lines, and induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-derived neural models29–34. These studies were conducted in 2D cultures and in 3D organoid cultures modeling specific brain regions such as the cortex or hippocampus. Collectively, these studies have shown that glucocorticoids in vitro modulate neurogenesis, neuroplasticity, inflammation, and noradrenergic as well as serotonergic signaling pathways implicated in depression. In particular, in vitro exposure to glucocorticoids has been reported to impair neuroplasticity by downregulating synaptic proteins, reducing dendritic spine density, and suppressing neurite outgrowth35. In various animal models, chronic administration of dexamethasone induces depression-like behaviors and disrupts neuroplasticity, including reductions in soma size and dendritic arborization in the prefrontal cortex36. Consistent with these findings, the present study also demonstrated decreases in neural cell viability, BDNF levels, and neurite outgrowth in cortical spheroids exposed to dexamethasone. This cortical spheroid model therefore represents a controlled 3D platform for investigating glucocorticoid receptor-mediated neuroplasticity impairment relevant to stress-related pathology.

The synthesis of synaptic proteins is regulated by mTORC1 signaling and its downstream effectors, 4E-BP1 and p70S6K12. Increasing evidence suggests that deficits in synaptic proteins resulting from dysregulation of mTORC1 signaling may contribute to the molecular mechanisms underlying depression13,37. Accordingly, activation of mTORC1 signaling and the subsequent enhancement of synaptic protein synthesis have emerged as major targets of antidepressant action. Numerous animal studies have demonstrated that the rapid and sustained antidepressant-like effects of ketamine and LY341495, a metabotropic glutamate receptor subtype 2/3 (mGluR2/3) antagonist, are mediated through AMPA receptor activation and subsequent upregulation of mTORC1 signaling38,39. Furthermore, these drugs were shown to restore deficits in synaptic proteins induced by chronic unpredictable stress, an established animal model of depression, via activation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway40,41. Additional evidence linking antidepressant action to mTORC1 regulation has been provided by studies with the mTORC1 inhibitor rapamycin, which not only blocked the antidepressant-like effects of chronic fluoxetine treatment in a chronic unpredictable mild stress model, but also abolished the upregulation of mTORC1 signaling in the hippocampus and amygdala11. Our previous study demonstrated that chronic restraint stress decreased the phosphorylation levels of mTORC1, along with its upstream regulators (Akt and ERK1/2) and downstream effectors (4E-BP1, p70S6K, eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4B [eIF4B], and ribosomal protein S6 [S6]), in the rat hippocampus. These effects were reversed by chronic treatment with the antidepressants escitalopram and paroxetine19. In particular, studies in 2D primary hippocampal neurons have shown that several antidepressants promote dendritic outgrowth and spine formation and increase synaptic protein levels through activation of the mTORC1 signaling pathway21,25. In the present study, escitalopram treatment was associated with increased activation of mTORC1 signaling in dexamethasone-treated cortical spheroids. Previous studies using primary neuronal cultures have demonstrated that escitalopram activates mTORC1 signaling and that this effect can be blocked by the mTORC1 inhibitor rapamycin25, supporting a potential mechanistic link between antidepressant treatment and mTORC1 signaling. Taken together, these findings highlight the potential of targeting proteins within the mTORC1 signaling pathway as a novel therapeutic strategy for the development of next-generation antidepressants.

3D in vitro neural models can help bridge the gap between conventional 2D cultures and in vivo animal models23. The rat cortical spheroid model requires 15 days of culture, which is sufficient to evaluate the chronic effects of drugs. In contrast, animal models generally require 3–4 weeks42, and iPSC-derived 3D brain models typically require approximately 8 weeks from neuronal differentiation to organoid formation43. Thus, the in vitro cortical spheroid culture provides a more rapid approach than animal models for investigating the molecular mechanisms of drug action. In addition, spheroid cultures are more cost-effective, accessible, and reproducible than animal models. The micro-spheroid device used in the present study reliably generated approximately 1,400 uniformly sized spheroids (200 ± 20 μm in diameter) per device, enabling a variety of experiments to be conducted from a single culture. Importantly, the cortical spheroid model of depression induced by dexamethasone allows for the investigation of molecular targets beyond those implicated in the “neuroplasticity hypothesis of depression.” For instance, inflammatory molecules can be extensively studied in this 3D in vitro model as candidate targets for the “inflammation hypothesis of depression”44.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although corticosterone is the endogenous glucocorticoid in rodents, dexamethasone was selected to achieve stable and sustained glucocorticoid receptor activation under long-term 3D culture conditions. The present model therefore represents a controlled in vitro system to study glucocorticoid receptor-mediated neuroplasticity impairment rather than a full physiological reproduction of stress hormone dynamics. Second, escitalopram concentrations up to 10 µM exceed therapeutic plasma levels; such concentrations are often required in vitro but limit direct extrapolation to clinical settings. Similarly, the concentration of dexamethasone used in this study was selected to reproducibly induce neuroplasticity impairment in a 3D culture system and do not directly correspond to physiological glucocorticoid levels observed in vivo. Third, this study evaluated only short-term exposure and did not assess chronic or systemic effects. In addition, dexamethasone and escitalopram were applied concurrently in the present study; therefore, the experimental design may reflect both preventative and restorative components of antidepressant action. Fourth, other mechanisms relevant to stress-related pathology, such as inflammatory or monoaminergic pathways, were not addressed. Fifth, phosphorylated proteins were assessed after blocking with skim milk, which may affect phospho-signal sensitivity. Although original blot images were provided and the overall band patterns were interpretable, this methodological issue should be considered when interpreting the phosphorylation data. Although human iPSC-derived brain organoids may offer increased species relevance, the present study was designed as a mechanistic validation in a controlled and reproducible 3D in vitro system. Future studies incorporating human-derived spheroids treated with endogenous glucocorticoids such as cortisol may further extend the biological applicability of these findings.

In summary, our findings indicate that escitalopram treatment is associated with enhanced neuroplasticity and increased activation of mTORC1 signaling in a 3D in vitro model of stress-related neuroplasticity impairment (Fig. 4). This system serves as a bridge between basic in vitro experiments and preclinical studies. Given the urgent need for novel classes of antidepressants with rapid therapeutic effects, this model provides a valuable platform for investigating molecular targets of antidepressant response. In future studies, we aim to apply novel drug candidates with rapid-acting antidepressant properties to this 3D in vitro model to further assess their effects on neuroplasticity.

Methods

Isolation of rat primary cortical cells

Pregnant rats were first anesthetized with Alfaxan (alfaxalone, 40 mg/kg, intraperitoneally; Jurox, NSW, Australia) to induce deep anesthesia prior to euthanasia. Following confirmation of anesthesia, euthanasia was performed in a dedicated chamber by gradual CO₂ exposure at a flow rate of approximately 30–70% of the chamber volume per minute, in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and the IACUC-approved protocol of the College of Medicine, Inje University (approval no. 2021–010).

Embryonic day 19 Sprague-Dawley rats were obtained from Hana Biotech (Pyeongtaek, Republic of Korea). Cortical tissues were harvested from the fetal brains. Primary cortical cells were isolated using the method described by Pacifici and Peruzzi45, with minor modifications. Briefly, the cortical tissue was placed in neurobasal medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA) containing 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) and mechanically dissociated into small fragments by pipetting approximately 30 times. Following a 20-min incubation at room temperature, the cell solution was suspended in growth medium (neuro-basal medium containing 1% fetal bovine serum, 1% horse serum, 2% serum-free B27 growth medium, 250 mM glutamine, and 1% penicillin-streptomycin; all from Gibco). The cell suspension was then passed through a 100-µm cell strainer (SPL Life Science, Pocheon, Korea) to remove tissue debris. The cell suspension was centrifuged at 180 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was carefully removed. The resulting cell pellet was gently resuspended in growth medium by repeated pipetting. Cortical cells were then seeded at a density of 1 × 107 cells per micro-spheroid device (see 3D neural spheroid culture).

3D neural spheroid culture

Spheroids with a uniform diameter of 200 μm were generated using a micro-spheroid device (Fig. 5). The device was fabricated from polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS; 10:1 mixture, Sylgard 184, Dow Corning, USA) via one-step photolithography and soft lithography techniques by a collaborating laboratory at Inje University specializing in biomedical device engineering. Each micro-spheroid device was placed in a 48-well cell culture plate, and cortical cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 107 cells per device. Cells were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C to allow cortical cells to settle into the microwells of the device. Cells attached to the outer surface of the microwells were removed by washing with growth medium. The cells were then aggregated within the microwells for 3 days at 37 °C to form spheroids. On day 3 in vitro (DIV 3), the self-assembled spheroids were suspended in 3 mL of growth medium and transferred to culture dishes pre-coated with poly-D-lysine (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and Matrigel (BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). For the cell viability assay, 10–15 spheroids (100 µL) were plated into each well of a 48-well culture plate. For Western blotting, 40–60 spheroids (400 µL) were seeded into each well of a 6-well culture plate. For immunofluorescence, 20–30 spheroids (200 µL) were seeded into a single-well cell culture slide (SPL Life Science). Spheroids were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO₂ and 95% relative humidity, with growth medium replaced every 2–3 days. As reported in our previous study, cortical spheroids at DIV 3, 4, and 10 contained diverse neural cell types, including neurons (βIII-tubulin, MAP-2), astrocytes (GFAP), microglia (CD11b), oligodendrocytes (O1), and neural stem cells (Nestin)26. Quantitative analysis based on normalized fluorescence intensity further indicated that neurons accounted for approximately 47.8% of the total cell population, glial cells for 36.6%, and other progenitor populations for 15.2%. These findings confirm that the spheroid cultures contain heterogeneous neural cell populations rather than neurons alone. Data for all experiments were obtained from at least three independent spheroid cultures (three biological replicates).

3D cortical spheroid culture using micro-spheroid device. The micro-spheroid device consistently generated uniformly sized spheroids with a diameter of 200 μm. Primary cortical cells were seeded into the micro-spheroid device and allowed to aggregate within the microwells for 3 days. On DIV 3, approximately 1,400 self-assembled spheroids were produced in a single micro-spheroid device and subsequently transferred to various culture plates for downstream experiments. Images were acquired using a digital camera and an inverted microscope.

Drug treatment

On DIV 10, cortical spheroids were cultured with escitalopram (0.1, 1, and 10 µM; Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK) in the presence of 100 µM water-soluble dexamethasone (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 5 days. The culture medium and drug solutions were replaced every 2 days. On DIV 15, spheroids were harvested for subsequent experiments. As reported in our previous study, exposure of spheroids to 100 µM dexamethasone reduced the levels of BDNF, ERK1/2 phosphorylation, and components of the mTORC1 signaling pathway-including phosphorylation of mTORC1, 4E-BP1, and p70S6K, as well as synaptic proteins-by approximately 20–40%26. Based on these findings, this concentration was selected for establishing the 3D in vitro model of depression. The range of escitalopram concentrations (0.1–10 µM) used in the present study was determined according to a previous report demonstrating that this drug enhances neuroplasticity in 2D cell cultures25. In 3D spheroids, Escitalopram (0.1, 1, and 10 µM) did not reduce cell viability under dexamethasone treatment in the 3D spheroid model, indicating non-cytotoxicity at the tested concentrations (see Supplementary Information). Preliminary experiments indicated that lower concentrations (< 0.1 µM) had no detectable effect on BDNF levels, whereas higher concentrations (> 50 µM) exerted cytotoxic effects by reducing cell viability (data not shown).

Cell viability

To assess the cytotoxic effects of escitalopram, cell viability was measured using the 3-[4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl]-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Amresco, Solon, OH, USA). Spheroids seeded into 48-well culture plates were cultured and stabilized for 7 days, and subsequently treated with 0.1, 1, or 10 µM escitalopram under dexamethasone-treated conditions for 5 days. MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was then added to each well and incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, the MTT solution was removed, and 400 µL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; GenDEPOT, Katy, TX, USA) was added to dissolve the formazan crystals formed during the reaction. A 100-µL aliquot of the dissolved solution was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax M2e; Molecular Devices, Cridersville, OH, USA).

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from spheroids using RIPA buffer (Elpisbiotech, Daejeon, Korea) supplemented with 1× protease inhibitor cocktail (GenDEPOT) and 1× phosphatase inhibitor (GenDEPOT). The lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay (Bradford Protein Assay Plus Reagents; GenDEPOT). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per sample) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane (GenDEPOT) for 2 h at a constant voltage of 100 V. The membrane was blocked in Tris-buffered saline containing Tween-20 (TBS-T; GenDEPOT) and 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature, and then incubated with primary antibodies (Table 1) overnight at 4 °C. After washing three times with TBS-T for 10 min each, the membrane was incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Table 1) at room temperature for 1 h. Immunoreactive bands were visualized using an Amersham™ Imager 600 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) with West-Q Pico Dura ECL Solution (GenDEPOT) and quantified using Multi-Gauge software (Fujifilm Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Immunofluorescence

Spheroids were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; GenDEPOT) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Biosesang, Seongnam, Korea) for 20 min at room temperature. After fixation, spheroids were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (GenDEPOT) for 3 min and blocked with 4% bovine serum albumin (BSA; GenDEPOT) for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies (Table 1) were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Following PBS washes, spheroids were incubated with secondary antibodies (Table 1) for 1 h at room temperature. After additional PBS washes, spheroids were stained with Hoechst 33,258 for nuclear labeling and incubated for 5 min at room temperature. Spheroids were then washed and mounted onto a single-well culture slide using Biomeda Gel/Mount (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Foster City, CA, USA). Images at a resolution of 1024 × 1024 pixels were acquired using an A1 + confocal laser scanning microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). For each spheroid, a series of 20 optical sections was obtained from the top to the bottom. The 10th optical section, corresponding to the middle plane, was selected as the representative image for PSD-95, GluA1, and anti-microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP-2) immunostaining. Fluorescence intensity was quantified in raw grayscale images using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each spheroid, the middle optical section was analyzed. A circular region of interest (ROI) with a radius of 500 μm centered on the spheroid core was applied to capture both the spheroid body and neurite outgrowth region. Mean fluorescence intensity was measured after background subtraction. All images were acquired using identical microscope settings and analyzed without brightness or contrast adjustment.

Neurite outgrowth analysis

Following the immunofluorescence procedure, spheroids were fixed in 4% PFA and incubated with the primary antibody against MAP-2. The 10th optical section, corresponding to the middle plane of each spheroid, was analyzed for neurite outgrowth using the Sholl Analysis plugin in ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/Sholl_Analysis). Neurite outgrowth was quantified according to a previously described method for 3D neural spheroids46,47. Consecutive concentric circles were drawn at 20-µm intervals from the center of the spheroid. The number of neurites intersecting each concentric circle was measured up to a diameter of 400 μm, corresponding to the average size of spheroids at DIV 15. The number of intersections as a function of radial distance from the spheroid center was plotted and represented as a line graph. The area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for each spheroid, and the mean AUC values for each group were used for statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0.2; GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Normality of the data distribution was examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For the assessment of variance homogeneity across groups, the Brown-Forsythe test was applied. Parametric statistical methods were used only when both assumptions of normality and equal variances were met (p > 0.05). Group differences were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc comparisons. Effect size estimates were reported as partial eta squared (η²p), interpreted according to conventional benchmarks: small (η²p = 0.01), medium (η²p = 0.06), and large (η²p = 0.14). In addition, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for η²p were calculated using the MBESS package in R (version 4.3.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
