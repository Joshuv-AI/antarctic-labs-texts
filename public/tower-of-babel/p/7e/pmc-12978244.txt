Identifying strategies to leverage electronic health records and health information technology in colorectal cancer screening in primary care clinics
Authors: Joshua E Richardson, Sonja Hoover, Madeleine Jones, Sarah Kobrin, Sujha Subramanian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWe report on using electronic health records (EHRs) and other health information technology (IT) (eg, REDCap, Excel, and population-health tools) for tracking patients and managing interventions to improve colorectal screening (CRC) among primary care practices who participated in the National Cancer Institute’s Accelerating Colorectal Cancer Screening and Follow-up through Implementation Science (ACCSIS) program.MethodsWe conducted semi-structured, recorded interviews with staff from 7 ACCSIS Research Projects (RPs). Using the interview notes, we conducted content analysis to report on the characteristics of the EHR systems and health IT, and thematic analysis to identify key concepts related to the ability to capture and monitor data for CRC screening.ResultsRPs used different data capture models to support EHRs and health IT: (1) centralized data capture models from projects or third-party services; or (2) direct data capture models, relying on features and functions within commercial EHRs. Respondents reported challenges to using EHRs and health IT, including generating patient reports to track interventions, working across EHR and research platforms because of lack of interoperability, and training for clinic staff on EHR and research platforms.DiscussionRPs would benefit from more streamlined data capture and reporting for managing CRC screening in primary care. Efforts reportedly fell onto staff who could have benefited from training around data handling and EHR-specific navigation.ConclusionsRPs experienced challenges in leveraging data capture models for EHR and health IT data management. Our research calls for technical capabilities that promote more efficient data capture and reporting, as well as greater capacity building among clinic staff.Keywords: electronic health records, colorectal neoplasms, early detection of cancer

Background

In the United States (US), colorectal cancer (CRC) is the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths for both men and women combined.1 Yet, CRC is preventable when found early through screening.2 Despite evidence about the benefits of CRC screening, adherence to CRC screening protocol remains low, as an estimated 26% of US adults between 50 and 75 have never been screened.3 Primary care practices play a key part in promoting CRC screening,4 as physician recommendation is an important reason why people are screened.5,6

Within primary care practices, health information technology (IT)—electronic health records (EHRs), in particular—can act as a key tool for supporting CRC screening. EHRs can provide means for identifying patients who are eligible for CRC screening and have the potential to serve as a mechanism for tracking screening activities around home-based testing and coordinating follow-up screening procedures with specialists.7,8 Using EHRs to monitor the delivery of CRC screening is particularly complex, in that recommendations include using one or more tests, including fecal immunochemical test (FIT), fecal occult blood testing (FOBT), and colonoscopy. The tests have different preparations and are repeated on different schedules.1

Despite the potential of EHRs to optimize CRC screening in primary care practices, previous research has shown numerous challenges with EHRs’ use for CRC screening and other preventive care: achieving data accuracy,9 generating automated reports of eligible patients,10,11 generating manual reports,12 sharing data with specialists and health plans,10 implementing workarounds that result in duplicative data entry,13 and creating misalignments between EHR functions and evidence-based care.14 Further, rural or smaller health systems and hospitals may not have the technology or resources to support using health IT.15

The purpose of this qualitative analysis was to describe how primary care clinics participating in the Accelerating Colorectal Cancer Screening and Follow-Up through Implementation Science (ACCSIS) Program used electronic health records (EHR) and other health IT to support tracking interventions to improve CRC screening, the challenges they had, and some supplemental tools they used.16 The ACCSIS Research Projects (RPs) included the University of Kentucky/The Ohio State University; the University of Chicago; the University of North Carolina; Oregon Health & Science University/Kaiser Permanente Center for Health Research; the University of California San Diego/San Diego State University; and the American Indian Colorectal Cancer Screening Consortium of the University of Arizona Comprehensive Cancer Center; The University of New Mexico Comprehensive Cancer Center; and the University of Oklahoma Stephenson Cancer Center. RTI/Implenomics was the Coordinating Center.17–24 The goal of ACCSIS, funded by the National Cancer Institute (NCI), was for ACCSIS RPs and their partner health systems to improve CRC screening and follow-up through multilevel interventions among populations who were medically underserved. This study provides a comparative perspective focusing on health system-level interventions (eg, EHR and other tools) across the ACCSIS RP.

Methods

Research question and study design

We conducted a qualitative study to answer the following research question: what electronic health records (EHRs) and other health IT do ACCSIS RP and their partner health clinics use for tracking patients and managing interventions to improve CRC screening. We defined health IT as software, such as REDCap and Excel, and population-health tools.

Sampling

We used a convenience sampling approach to identify participants to interview from the ACCSIS RPs. We asked RPs to nominate individuals within the RP, such as project leaders, data analysts, or project managers, who had knowledge and experience using EHRs and other health IT and data for their CRC efforts. We then emailed the suggested individuals about their willingness to be interviewed for the study. When they agreed, we scheduled interviews with them.

Data collection

We used the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR, 2009)25 as a guide to develop the interview protocol. CFIR is a framework that consists of overarching domains to help identify barriers and facilitators to program implementation. With guidance from the ACCSIS Data, Design, and Analysis Work Group (DDA), an internal committee of ACCSIS principal investigators and co-investigators that represented all RPs, we selected the following domains that were most pertinent to our research question: intervention characteristics, inner setting, and process. In addition, we added questions about RPs’ health clinic partners’ use of health IT in five areas: (1) collecting information on the CRC patient and clinician characteristics; (2) collecting data on CRC screening; (3) collecting data on follow-up colonoscopy; (4) collecting data for intervention tracking; and (5) collecting feedback and lessons learned for using health IT in CRC screening and follow-up.

To collect the qualitative data, we (JR, MJ, AM) conducted recorded, semi-structured interviews over Zoom between August and December of 2023 during work hours. All respondents provided verbal consent. The lead interviewer (JR), a trained health informaticist with experience conducting interviews and other qualitative research throughout his career, was the task lead for this activity and conducted all interviews. For each interview, a research assistant (either MJ or AM) took thorough notes concurrently as the interview was occurring. The notetaker also asked follow-up or clarification questions as needed. Both the notetaker and lead interviewer reviewed the notes after each interview. Based on the interviews and interview recordings, all notes were recorded in Microsoft Word and answers were organized for analysis using Microsoft Excel. We did not have the meeting recordings professionally transcribed initially in order to remain within time and resource constraints of the project; however, we later transcribed the notes using Microsoft Copilot (premium). We included quotations in the results, where applicable.

Lastly, we drafted a supplemental questionnaire for RPs to collect additional standardized information about the EHRs used in each ACCSIS RP. We asked about the number of clinic partners the RP had, the EHR vendors the clinic partners used, and whether the clinic partners used other types of platforms to collect research-related data. The questionnaire was completed by the respondent at each RP and returned to RTI via email. The respondent reviewed and approved the RTI’s summary of their questionnaire.

Data analysis

The RP was the unit of analysis, and we deidentified the RPs in our findings by substituting the RP name for RP A, RP B, etc. Using the investigator triangulation approach, as there were more than two researchers analyzing the data,26,27 we conducted the analysis as follows:

Step One: The lead researcher (JR) used the CFIR domains to begin the coding process. The lead researcher (JR) then added additional codes and themes based on review of the data.

Step Two: The lead researcher (JR) coded the data. One notetaker (MJ or AM) reviewed the codes and discussed any potential discrepancies with the lead researcher (JR). The lead researcher also asked RPs any clarifying questions via email; no interview was repeated.

Step Three: The lead researcher (JR) and notetaker (MJ or AM) drafted findings and discussed them with other members of the research team (SH and SS). Where there were differences in opinions, the team discussed until they reached consensus.

Step Four: The lead researcher (JR) presented the findings to the ACCSIS DDA for review and comment. The lead researcher (JR) then presented findings for review and comment at the annual ACCSIS Consortium meeting in 2023, in which co-authors, respondents, and staff from NCI attended. Attendees were able to review findings and provide comments and feedback.

Step Five: The lead researcher (JR) and notetakers (MJ and AM) drafted summary analyses for each RP. One person from each RP reviewed and approved the respective summary. Based on review of the summary, the research team (JR and SS) determined that saturation was reached.

Institutional Review Board approval was not required because it was determined that this study was not research involving human subjects. COREQ reporting guidelines were followed for development of this manuscript.

Results

In this section, we describe the characteristics of the respondents, RPs, and types of health IT used. We also present barriers and solutions identified by the RPs.

Description of resulting interviews

We interviewed representatives from 7 of 8 RP. We note that all trans-ACCSIS research activities are voluntary, and one RP declined to participate in this activity, the reason for which is unknown. We conducted 15 interviews in total (average duration of 52 minutes, range of 33–62 minutes) with respondents who self-described their job titles as a mix of researchers (n = 6), directors (n = 4), or miscellaneous roles (n = 5). Nine interviews were conducted with one participant, while 6 interviews were conducted with two participants.

Description of ACCSIS RP characteristics, EHR and health IT

Respondents represented seven ACCSIS RPs that investigated and supported primary care–based interventions to facilitate and improve patient CRC screening (Table 1). RPs employed a variety of organizational structures for capturing and analyzing CRC-related data that loosely fit into two data capture models: (1) centralized services that relied on the RP and partners to collect and aggregate data (n = 3); or (2) direct services that relied on the clinics themselves to collect and aggregate data (n = 4). Two RPs partnered with Native American/American Indian communities. RPs partnered with a wide number of clinics ranging in number from 6 to 27. The clinics were located in the same regions as the ACCSIS RP, varied in sizes and populations served, and were generally federally qualified health centers and community health centers.

Summary of interview findings

We describe multilevel challenges RPs had to use health IT and some of the implementation strategies they used, also shown in Figure 1.

Multilevel barriers and potential implementation strategies.

Using health IT for capturing and monitoring data for patient and clinician characteristics

Respondents indicated that the collection of patient and clinician characteristics via EHRs and health IT was difficult due to varying EHR capabilities and staff capacity to generate relevant reports. Therefore, the processes for data gathering were oftentimes manual, in which clinic staff, with RP support, captured characteristics in REDCap or Excel. “It is quite challenging for [patient navigators] to pull that kind of information [from the EHR], just judging by the fact that they are still tracking this information using their own Excel sheets” (Respondent at RP-G). One respondent reported that EHRs varied in their ease of use for capturing and reporting patient and clinician characteristics data; some EHRs “worked well,” whereas clinics using a different EHR were unable to report (Respondent at RP-C). One respondent reported a lack of integration between EHRs and REDCap, which therefore required manual transcription of results from one platform to another.

Using health IT for capturing and monitoring data for CRC screening

RPs may have instituted the REDCap platform to capture screening data, but because REDCap was not designed for data capture in clinical care, it was not integrated well into clinic workflows and could thereby act as a barrier to effective data capture. For example, one respondent reported that it was unable to track FIT kits and expressed a need for a health IT system with the capacity to monitor population health. The respondent further explained a need for a health IT “system [that] supports tracking and reporting needs—plus ensuring everyone is trained (which would require more resources), a technology that is user-friendly to clinic staff, and can be more easily operationalized locally” (Respondent at RP-D). To potentially build on Excel within its workflow, one RP developed its own Python script that (if clinics used it) would transform patient-level tracking data into aggregate Excel reports that clinics could share with the RP. Given some of the EHR limitations for monitoring and reporting CRC screening data, one RP that worked directly with clinics employed a third-party product for its EHR as a facilitator, which provided a “template to extract data easily” (Respondent at RP-F). However, most RPs relied on REDCap or Excel as a means for capturing screening data and sharing the results (oftentimes manually), and faxing results for manual data entry was leveraged. “We just ask them: send us a demographic file, send us a FIT order file, send us a colonoscopy order file, send us your lab results, and send us your UDS data…most of the time [the colonoscopy results] come as a fax” (Respondent at RP-F). Another respondent reported, “It was challenging to get data, and the vendor was expensive and difficult for clinics to follow up with—results were sometimes faxed” (Respondent at RP-B).

One RP, which took a centralized approach, reported that their third-party platform integrated CRC screening data across EHRs and worked well in “standardizing and normalizing a data platform across EHRs systems” (Respondent at RP-E). Another RP that took a centralized approach employed a process by which they accessed claims data to determine if patients’ CRC screening occurred during the period. The data were then audited by research staff. In sum, RPs executed and managed CRC screening data in varying ways that relied on centralized services or occurred directly via clinic EHRs, yet each approach encountered barriers that required implementation of additional tools to support data collection for research activities.

Using health IT for capturing and monitoring data for CRC follow-up

Using health IT for capturing and monitoring data for CRC follow-up was challenging, regardless of an RP’s centralized or direct approach. One respondent expressed a sentiment that was shared by others: there were no easy systems in place with any EHRs to track follow-up and that “a lot of follow-up was done by hand” (Respondent at RP-C). Two RPs that took a centralized approach reported having to conduct manual chart reviews to track follow-ups and enter results into the REDCap database. And another, despite planning and having conducted workflow analyses with patient navigators who worked with clinics, noted that “very few [patient navigators] entered [data] into REDCap” because REDCap is a research tool and not a clinic tool (Respondent at RP-B). One RP developed a workaround involving staff from Care Coordination Organizations (CCOs) to match claims data against EHR chart review data to identify patients receiving follow-up procedures. A respondent commented, “the best system would involve one single data person leading extraction from all data systems” (Respondent at RP-B). Overall, RPs noted a lack of systematized approaches and standardized data for capturing and monitoring CRC follow-up data.

Health IT training strategies to enable CRC screening and follow-up

Training clinic staff (including care coordinators, patient navigators, and frontline clinic staff) to work with EHRs and other health IT was variable across RPs. Some RPs focused more than others on introducing and familiarizing staff to EHR forms, interfaces, and reporting mechanisms. Training support in multiple cases was provided in person or via remote sessions. One respondent reported sitting “with clinic staff on Zoom or in person [and going] through patient by patient” (Respondent at RP-B). For some RPs, training included REDCap and associated data entry processes. More specifically, respondents discussed training to support clinic staff in using EHRs to generate reports of patients who were eligible for CRC screening, received CRC screening, and received follow-up colonoscopies.

Reflecting and evaluating health IT for CRC screening

In discussing their experiences with health IT for CRC screening, respondents described future needs for greater EHR sophistication in managing patient screening activities, data synthesis, and clinic capacity building. First, respondents generally conveyed that EHRs were not well-suited for managing data and processes around CRC screening and follow-up. They observed that while clinicians could find CRC screening-related answers on specific patients very quickly, clinicians and staff had difficulty pulling aggregated data for reporting to RPs. It could be challenging for them to pull information from the EHR, “judging by the fact that they are still tracking this information using their own Excel sheets” (Respondent at RP-G). Respondents observed that EHR data collection took a surprising amount of time. Therefore, respondents recommended that future efforts start with an EHR that is functional for research purposes. “Ultimately, EHRs are billing tools, not patient health tools” (Respondent at RP-B). Respondents recommended developing a space within the EHR to document research project-specific information, such as data on patient navigation or follow-up colonoscopy.

Second, respondents explained that EHRs for CRC screening and follow-up produce data that were fragmented and messy. One respondent estimated that 90% of the research work was data cleaning. Therefore, data fragmentation across the EHR itself, as well as across systems, was a problem that resulted in missing data and the need for manual solutions to assure data quality.

Third, in order to achieve more effective use of health IT for managing CRC screening, clinic staff required training to work with EHRs and other forms of health IT. Respondents observed that clinics have lacked the capacity to run their EHRs and were not familiar enough with the systems to program and generate research results for monitoring CRC screening and follow-up. The challenge was exacerbated by staff shortages and clinic turnover.

Discussion

We conducted a qualitative study to investigate how clinic staff leveraged health IT, particularly EHRs, to support CRC screening in primary care clinics that participated in ACCSIS. We found that while RPs made efforts to use EHRs and health IT (REDCap, third-party products, etc.) to track patient and clinician characteristics, screening, and follow-up, they encountered multiple barriers that required laborious (and creative) workarounds to leverage data efficiently and effectively for CRC screening. Our findings are similar to Petrik et al. (2023) who found that clinic staff have difficulty using EHRs to collect eligible patient population data, screening uptake, and screening results by modality.10 In our research, all 7 of the participating RPs reported significant challenges with using EHRs and health IT to capture and report data around CRC screening, which extended beyond collecting data for research tracking purposes.

To facilitate CRC screening, RPs broadly used different strategies for leveraging EHRs and health IT.28 This was necessary as neither could comprehensively collect data to identify and track patients through the CRC screening process. The gaps in EHR capabilities resulted in workarounds, and RP staff used secondary platforms to collect additional needed information. Respondents recommended workable approaches for identifying eligible patients for CRC screening and among them, those who received screening and the results of that screening. There is a need for further development and sophistication of data standards for capturing relevant data, from clinic order to home testing, to third-party laboratories, and other stakeholders, and future studies should identify best practice tools.

Given that data collection and reporting for the most part fell onto clinic staff using various tools such as REDCap, it is imperative that future CRC efforts build staff capacity, from basic data handling concepts to step-by-step instructions for generating EHR-specific patient and screening reports. Without such training and support, comprehensive CRC screening within primary care practices for research, and for quality clinical practice, are likely to falter.

Another lesson for future researchers on health IT for CRC screening in primary care settings could be that it is not enough for participating practices to have EHRs; any EHR needs to be formatted with at least the ability to capture patient and clinician characteristics, as well as generate reports of eligible patients for CRC screening. Therefore, future research may want to consider CRC efforts around practices that use a single or small number of EHR platforms and allocate resources accordingly. Otherwise, the technical requirements may be too great to support multiple EHRs. Additionally, in order to ensure that screening data are collected in a standardized manner, health care settings could use the same algorithms to identify screening tests. For example, in Lee et al. (2015), researchers developed a successful algorithm to identify colonoscopy for screening, surveillance, and diagnosis in EHR and an algorithm such as this one could be shared across multisite projects.29

We have limitations to report. First, this study used a small sample size that, albeit purposefully recruited, still relied on the decisions of executive sponsors and on respondents’ availability. Furthermore, one of the RPs declined to participate for unstated reasons, thereby reducing our sample size. Second, we did not interview on-the-ground staff, such as primary care clinicians or practice facilitators, due to time and budget limitations. We recommend future research include these valuable perspectives to get a richer understanding around the barriers and facilitators for EHRs and health IT for CRC screening. Third, we did not have our interviews professionally transcribed at first, while conducting initial analyses. However, we later transcribed the notes using Microsoft Copilot (premium), which supplemented our thorough notes for each interview. Fourth, we did not provide demographic information for the respondents, which we have not done in previous qualitative studies when the focus of the paper has been on programs.30–32

Conclusion

ACCSIS RPs reported numerous challenges in their abilities to leverage EHRs and other health IT in the collection, reporting, and managing of data around CRC screening. These limitations existed despite some RPs using tailored data capture models to supplement her data collection. EHR optimization is necessary so that future CRC efforts may more efficiently and effectively identify and generate reports of patients eligible for CRC screening and track results. In addition, future research endeavors can emphasize needs around data handling and management at the clinic level to better utilize limited staff time and resources.
