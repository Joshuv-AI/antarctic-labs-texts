Fracture resistance and color stability of monolithic CAD/CAM crowns: effect of occlusal thickness and thermocycling
Authors: Erkin Özcan, Deger Ongul, Rafat Sasany, Ibrahim Bulent Sermet, Ilknur Özcan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis in vitro study evaluated the effects of occlusal thickness and thermocycling on the fracture resistance and color stability of monolithic CAD/CAM crown materials.Materials and methodsStandardized full-coverage crowns were fabricated from four CAD/CAM materials: resin nano-ceramic (CM), extra-translucent zirconia (VXT), translucent zirconia (VT), and zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (VS). Two occlusal thicknesses (1.0 mm and 1.5 mm) were designed. Specimens from each material–thickness combination were divided into non-aged and thermocycled subgroups (n = 10 each). The thermocycled subgroups were subjected to 10,000 cycles between 5 °C and 55 °C. Fracture resistance was measured using a universal testing machine. Color measurements (L*, a*, b*) were obtained using a spectrophotometer before and after thermocycling, and color differences (ΔE₀₀) were calculated. Data were analyzed using two-way and three-way ANOVA with post hoc Tukey tests (α = 0.05).ResultsMaterial type (P < .001; ηp² = 0.52) and occlusal thickness (P = .003; ηp² = 0.11) had significant effects on fracture resistance. Increasing occlusal thickness resulted in higher fracture resistance values across all tested materials. VXT demonstrated the highest fracture resistance, whereas VS exhibited the lowest values. Thermocycling did not have a significant main effect on fracture resistance (P = .178; ηp² = 0.02). Color differences (ΔE₀₀) varied significantly among materials (P < .05); however, all values remained below the clinically acceptable threshold.ConclusionsOcclusal thickness and material type significantly influence the mechanical performance of monolithic CAD/CAM crowns. Although thermocycling had a limited effect on fracture resistance, all materials maintained clinically acceptable color stability. These findings support the use of reduced-thickness restorations with appropriate material selection. Keywords: Monolithic CAD/CAM crowns, Occlusal thickness, Fracture resistance, Color stability, Thermocycling

Introduction

Dental CAD/CAM technologies have significantly transformed restorative dentistry by enabling the fabrication of high-strength, esthetic, and minimally invasive restorations [1–3]. Monolithic restorations, in particular, have gained widespread acceptance due to their improved mechanical reliability and elimination of veneer chipping complications associated with bilayered systems [4, 5].

Among currently available CAD/CAM materials, lithium disilicate (LD), zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (ZLS), and monolithic zirconia (MZ) are commonly used for full-coverage restorations [6–8]. These materials differ in their microstructure, crystalline phase composition, and optical properties, all of which directly influence their mechanical performance and esthetic behavior [9–13]. In particular, zirconia materials with different translucency levels exhibit variations in yttria content and cubic-to-tetragonal phase distribution, which may directly influence their mechanical behavior, translucency, and aging susceptibility [13, 14]. Therefore, materials representing different microstructural classes were included in the present study. Monolithic zirconia exhibits a polycrystalline structure with a transformation toughening mechanism, providing superior mechanical strength [12, 13]. Zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate has been developed as a hybrid material that combines the favorable optical properties of glass-ceramics with the mechanical advantages of zirconia [15].

In contemporary restorative dentistry, there is a growing emphasis on minimally invasive treatment approaches aimed at preserving sound tooth structure [16, 17]. Consequently, reduced occlusal thickness has become an important consideration in crown design. However, decreasing restoration thickness may compromise fracture resistance, particularly under functional loading conditions [18–20]. Therefore, understanding the mechanical behavior of CAD/CAM materials at reduced thicknesses is essential for ensuring long-term clinical success.

In addition to mechanical performance, color stability is a critical factor influencing the long-term esthetic outcome of restorative materials [21, 22]. Intraoral conditions such as temperature fluctuations, moisture, and aging processes may affect the optical properties of restorative materials over time [23, 24]. Thermocycling is widely used as an artificial aging method to simulate these oral conditions and evaluate material durability [25].

Although numerous studies have investigated either the mechanical properties or the optical behavior of CAD/CAM materials, limited evidence is available regarding the combined effects of occlusal thickness and thermocycling on both fracture resistance and color stability [26].

However, the clinical relevance of reduced occlusal thickness cannot be interpreted solely by comparing fracture load values among materials. The response of a restoration to thickness reduction is strongly influenced by its microstructure, elastic behavior, crystalline phase composition, and susceptibility to aging-related degradation. Therefore, evaluating different CAD/CAM material classes under the same crown geometry may provide insight into whether reduced thickness affects all materials similarly or whether the effect is material dependent. This approach may help clarify the interaction between occlusal thickness, material composition, and thermocycling on the mechanical and optical behavior of monolithic crowns.

Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the effects of occlusal thickness and thermocycling on the fracture resistance and color stability of monolithic CAD/CAM crown materials. The null hypotheses were that: (1) material type would not affect fracture resistance, (2) occlusal thickness would not affect fracture resistance, and (3) occlusal thickness and thermocycling would not affect color stability.

Materials and methods

Study design and sample size calculation

An a priori power analysis was performed using G*Power (v3.1; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) for a fixed-effects factorial ANOVA (f = 0.40, α = 0.05, power = 80%), indicating a minimum of 10 specimens per cell [25]. Based on the factorial experimental design, a total of 160 crown specimens were fabricated. For each material-thickness combination, 20 specimens were prepared and subsequently divided into non-aged and thermocycled subgroups (n = 10 each). The experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Flowchart of the experimental design including specimen preparation, aging procedures, and mechanical testing

Specimen preparation and digital design

A standardized maxillary first molar acrylic tooth (No. 26; Frasaco GmbH, Tettnang, Germany) was prepared for a full-coverage crown restoration with approximately 1.0-mm buccal and lingual axial reductions, 1.5-mm occlusal reduction, a circumferential chamfer finish line, and approximately 6° axial convergence. The prepared tooth was digitized using a laboratory scanner (Ceramill Map; Amann Girrbach, Koblach, Austria). Based on the obtained digital file, 160 identical polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) dies (Telio CAD; Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) were fabricated using a 5-axis computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) milling unit (Ceramill Motion 2; Amann Girrbach, Koblach, Austria) to standardize the supporting structures.

An anatomically contoured maxillary first molar crown was designed using CAD software (Ceramill Mind; Amann Girrbach) based on the morphology of a natural tooth. All anatomical features, including cuspal inclines, developmental grooves, marginal ridges, and occlusal anatomy, were preserved throughout the design process. Two occlusal thickness configurations (1.0 mm and 1.5 mm) were defined at the central fossa region, while the axial wall thickness was standardized at 1.0 mm for all specimens to eliminate geometric variability and enable reliable color comparison. The same anatomically standardized STL file was used for all restorations to ensure consistency among groups (Fig. 2).

Representative design configurations of the monolithic restorations (A: 1.0-mm occlusal thickness; B: 1.5-mm occlusal thickness)

CAD/CAM materials and crown fabrication

Four CAD/CAM materials representing contemporary restorative material classes were evaluated: resin nano-ceramic (GC Cerasmart; GC Corp., Tokyo, Japan) (CM), extra-translucent zirconia (Vita YZ XT; Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) (VXT), translucent zirconia (Vita YZ T; Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) (VT), and zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (Vita Suprinity PC; Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) (VS). All restorations were milled using a 5-axis milling unit (Ceramill Motion 2; Amann Girrbach, Koblach, Austria) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Extra-translucent zirconia (VXT) and translucent zirconia (VT) restorations were sintered in a high-temperature furnace according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. The final sintering cycle was performed at approximately 1450–1530 °C with a holding time of 2 h. Zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (VS) restorations were crystallized in a ceramic furnace at approximately 840 °C according to the manufacturer’s crystallization program. Following firing procedures, all restorations were finished and glazed according to the manufacturers’ recommendations before cementation.

Cementation procedure

Each crown was cemented onto the corresponding acrylic die using a dual-cure resin cement (RelyX U200; 3 M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA). A standardized seating force of 50 N was applied using a custom loading device to ensure uniform cement thickness (Fig. 4). Excess cement was removed, and light polymerization was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All specimens were stored in distilled water at 37 °C for 24 h before aging procedures [26]. The PMMA dies were then embedded in autopolymerizing acrylic resin blocks (Meliodent; Heraeus Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) to facilitate standardized positioning during fracture resistance testing. Before cementation, the internal surfaces of the zirconia restorations (VXT and VT) were air-abraded with 50-µm aluminum oxide particles at 2 bar pressure for 10 s and treated with an MDP-containing ceramic primer (Clearfil Ceramic Primer Plus; Kuraray Noritake Dental Inc., Tokyo, Japan). The zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate restorations (VS) were etched with 5% hydrofluoric acid (IPS Ceramic Etching Gel; Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) for 20 s, rinsed thoroughly, air-dried, and treated with a silane coupling agent (Monobond Plus; Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein). The resin nano-ceramic restorations (CM) were air-abraded with 50-µm aluminum oxide particles, ultrasonically cleaned, and treated with a ceramic primer prior to cementation. Polymerization was performed using an LED light-curing unit (Bluephase N; Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) with an output intensity of approximately 1200 mW/cm² for 20 s per surface.

Experimental setup used for fracture resistance testing. (A) Custom loading device used to apply a standardized seating force of 50 N during cementation. (B) Specimen positioned in the universal testing machine prior to load-to-fracture testing

Thermocycling

Specimens assigned to the aging groups were subjected to thermocycling using a thermocycling device (THE-1100; SD Mechatronik GmbH, Feldkirchen-Westerham, Germany). The thermocycling protocol consisted of 10,000 cycles between 5 °C and 55 °C, with a dwell time of 30 s and a transfer time of 10 s, to simulate intraoral thermal fluctuations. This thermocycling protocol has been widely used in previous in vitro studies and is considered to approximately simulate one year of clinical service [24].

Color measurements were performed using a calibrated digital spectrophotometer (VITA Easyshade V; Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Säckingen, Germany) under standardized illumination conditions. The device was calibrated according to the manufacturer’s instructions before each measurement session. To ensure reproducible positioning and measurement geometry, a custom silicone index was fabricated for each specimen and used throughout the color assessment procedure. The index enabled consistent placement of the spectrophotometer tip at the same predefined anatomical measurement location on the crown surface during all measurements, thereby minimizing positional variability and improving measurement reproducibility. All restorations were fabricated using an identical CAD design and standardized dimensions. The measured area exceeded the active measuring aperture of the spectrophotometer, thereby minimizing potential edge-loss effects. All measurements were performed under identical environmental conditions and using the same background configuration. During all measurements, the spectrophotometer tip was positioned perpendicular to the measured surface. Three consecutive measurements were recorded for each specimen, and the mean values were used for statistical analysis. Color coordinates were recorded in the CIE L*, a*, b* color space, and color differences (ΔE₀₀) were calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula with parametric factors set as 1 [27].

The clinically perceptible and acceptable thresholds were set at ΔE₀₀ = 0.8 and ΔE₀₀ = 1.8, respectively [28].

Fracture resistance testing

Fracture resistance was evaluated for both non-aged and thermocycled specimens using a universal testing machine (Instron 3345; Instron Corp., Norwood, MA, USA). Each specimen was positioned in a custom metal jig to ensure standardized axial loading. A compressive load was applied vertically to the central fossa using a 6-mm-diameter stainless steel spherical indenter at a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min until catastrophic failure occurred. The maximum load at fracture was recorded in Newtons (N) (Fig. 4). After fracture testing, failure modes were visually evaluated and classified as superficial crack formation, partial crown fracture, or catastrophic bulk fracture [Fig. 3].

Representative fracture patterns observed after load-to-fracture testing. A Initial crack formation. B Partial fracture involving limited structural damage. C Catastrophic bulk fracture with complete structural failure

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using statistical software (SPSS v25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Homogeneity of variance was evaluated using Levene’s test. All datasets satisfied the assumptions required for parametric statistical analyses (P > .05). Descriptive statistics were calculated as mean ± standard deviation. A three-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of material type, occlusal thickness, and thermocycling on fracture resistance. Color difference values (ΔE₀₀) were calculated from the paired baseline and post-thermocycling measurements obtained from the same specimen. Because thermocycling was inherently incorporated into the ΔE₀₀ outcome variable, two-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the effects of material type and occlusal thickness, followed by Tukey post hoc tests. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (ηp²). The level of statistical significance was set at α = 0.05.

Color stability

Two-way ANOVA revealed that material type significantly affected ΔE₀₀ values (P < .05), whereas occlusal thickness did not have a significant effect (P > .05). No significant interaction was detected between material type and occlusal thickness (P > .05). Among the tested materials, the resin nano-ceramic group (CM) exhibited the highest color change, whereas the extra-translucent zirconia group (VXT) demonstrated the lowest ΔE₀₀ values. Post hoc comparisons indicated that CM exhibited significantly higher color change than both zirconia groups (P < .05). Nevertheless, all ΔE₀₀ values remained below the clinically acceptable threshold, indicating satisfactory color stability regardless of material type or thickness (Table 4).

Fracture resistance

Fracture resistance values according to material type and occlusal thickness are summarized in Table 3. Three-way ANOVA demonstrated that both material type (P < .001) and occlusal thickness (P = .003) significantly influenced fracture resistance, whereas thermocycling did not exhibit a significant main effect (P = .178). A significant interaction between material type and occlusal thickness was observed (P = .004), indicating that the influence of thickness on fracture resistance was material dependent (Table 2). Effect size analysis revealed a large effect of material type (ηp²= 0.52), a moderate effect of occlusal thickness (ηp² = 0.11), and a small effect of thermocycling (ηp² = 0.02). The significant interaction between material type and occlusal thickness is illustrated in Fig. 5, demonstrating that the effect of thickness on fracture resistance varied according to the CAD/CAM material tested.

Among the tested materials, VXT exhibited the highest fracture resistance values, followed by VT and CM, whereas VS demonstrated the lowest fracture resistance across both thickness conditions. Increasing occlusal thickness significantly improved fracture resistance in the zirconia-based materials and in zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate (P < .05). In contrast, the resin nano-ceramic material did not demonstrate a significant thickness-dependent increase in fracture resistance (P > .05).

Effect of thermocycling

Thermocycling significantly reduced fracture resistance only in the VT group at the 1.0-mm thickness condition (P < .05). No significant effect of thermocycling was observed for the remaining material–thickness combinations (P > .05).

Failure modes

Failure mode analysis revealed material-dependent fracture patterns (Table 6). VS specimens predominantly exhibited catastrophic bulk fractures (70%), whereas CM specimens mainly demonstrated crack formation (60%). In contrast, VT and VXT specimens most frequently exhibited partial fractures (55% and 50%, respectively), indicating material-dependent differences in fracture behavior after load-to-fracture testing.

Discussion

The results of the present study demonstrated that both material type and occlusal thickness significantly influenced fracture resistance, leading to rejection of the first and second null hypotheses. Additionally, although statistically significant differences in color stability were observed among materials, all ΔE₀₀ values remained within clinically acceptable limits, partially supporting the third null hypothesis.

The superior fracture resistance observed in the monolithic zirconia groups may be attributed to their polycrystalline structure and transformation toughening mechanism, which enhances crack resistance and energy dissipation [13, 29, 30]. The influence of occlusal thickness was material dependent. Zirconia-based and zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate crowns exhibited a significant thickness-related increase in fracture resistance, whereas the resin nano-ceramic material was less affected by thickness reduction. This finding may be associated with differences in microstructure, elastic modulus, and deformation behavior among the tested materials. Resin-containing CAD/CAM materials may absorb part of the applied stress through limited elastic deformation, whereas ceramic-based materials are generally more dependent on material bulk for crack resistance [11, 12, 29].

Zirconia materials with different translucency levels differ in yttria content and phase composition, factors known to influence both optical and mechanical properties [13, 14, 30]. However, because no phase analysis was performed in the present study, these observations should be interpreted cautiously. The reduction in fracture resistance observed in the VT group after thermocycling at 1.0-mm thickness may be related to aging-associated degradation mechanisms previously reported for zirconia materials [22, 23, 31].

The lower fracture resistance values observed in the zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate group may be associated with its glass-ceramic microstructure and lower resistance to crack propagation compared with zirconia-based materials [10, 15, 19]. In contrast, all zirconia materials maintained fracture resistance values substantially above reported posterior chewing forces, even at reduced thicknesses [25, 32].

Occlusal thickness significantly affected fracture resistance. Increasing thickness from 1.0 mm to 1.5 mm improved fracture resistance, which is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that greater material bulk improves stress distribution and load-bearing capacity [16, 25, 29, 32].

Thermocycling had a limited effect on fracture resistance, with a significant reduction observed only in the VT group at 1.0-mm thickness. Similar findings have been reported in studies showing that moderate thermocycling protocols may not produce substantial degradation in CAD/CAM restorative materials [22, 23, 31]. Longer aging periods combined with cyclic fatigue loading may result in more pronounced material deterioration.

Although significant differences in color stability were detected among materials, all ΔE₀₀ values remained below the clinically acceptable threshold of 1.8 [28, 33]. Therefore, the observed differences are unlikely to be clinically unacceptable despite their statistical significance. These findings suggest that all evaluated materials maintained acceptable esthetic performance after thermocycling [21, 24, 28, 33].

From a clinical perspective, all tested materials demonstrated fracture resistance values exceeding average posterior masticatory forces reported in the literature [25, 32]. Therefore, both thickness configurations may be considered clinically acceptable when appropriate material selection is performed. However, caution should be exercised when materials with lower fracture resistance are used at minimal thicknesses [16, 18, 25, 32, 34].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted under in vitro conditions and could not fully reproduce the complexity of the oral environment. In addition, cyclic fatigue loading was not included, PMMA dies were used instead of natural teeth, cement thickness was not directly measured, and failure modes were assessed only visually. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as laboratory outcomes rather than direct predictors of long-term clinical performance. Future studies should evaluate the combined effects of thermocycling and cyclic fatigue loading, use natural tooth abutments, and incorporate fractographic analyses to provide a more comprehensive understanding of long-term clinical behavior.
