Are Depressive Symptoms Associated With Biological Aging in a Cross-Sectional Analysis of Adults Over Age 50 in the United States
Authors: Herong Wang, Kelly M. Bakulski, Freida Blostein, Brittany R. Porath, John Dou, César Higgins Tejera, Lindsay H. Ryan, Erin B. Ware
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Major depressive disorder accelerates DNA methylation age, a biological aging marker. Subclinical depressive symptoms are common, but their link to DNA methylation aging in older adults remains unexplored. This study analyzed the cross-sectional relationship between depressive symptoms and accelerated DNA methylation aging, considering gender and race/ethnicity in U.S. adults aged over 50. We used data from 3,882 diverse participants in the 2016 Health and Retirement Study wave, measuring blood DNA methylation age against chronologic age for acceleration. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CES-D) scale. Multiple linear regression evaluated the association between depressive symptoms and DNA methylation age acceleration, adjusting for sociodemographic factors, blood cell proportions, and health behaviors (physical activity, alcohol use, smoking, and chronic conditions). Gender and race/ethnicity modifications were also tested. Depressive symptoms, measured by continuous CES-D score, high depressive symptoms (CES-D ≥ 4), or any symptoms (CES-D ≥ 1), significantly correlated with increased GrimAge DNA methylation age acceleration (all p ≤ .001) in unadjusted and sociodemographic-adjusted models but were nonsignificant in fully adjusted models. No significant gender or race/ethnicity effect modifications were found in fully adjusted models. Health behaviors significantly influence DNA methylation age acceleration and depressive phenotypes, underscoring the need to understand their roles in assessing psychological factors related to DNA methylation age acceleration.

Aging is an important risk factor for several diseases that are the leading causes of morbidity and mortality (Niccoli & Partridge, 2012). Older adults also carry a profound burden of mental disorders, such as major depressive disorder (Reynolds et al., 2022). Prior studies investigating associations of depression with age suggested that after lifetime highs in young adulthood, a decline of depressive symptoms in midlife is followed by relative increases in older adults (Fiske et al., 2003; Sutin et al., 2013; Tampubolon & Maharani, 2017). The population sampled and operationalization of depression are key factors in understanding associations with age. Studies focused on major depressive disorder diagnoses tend to find lower prevalence with increasing age among noninstitutionalized adults/older cohorts (Mirowsky, 1994). However, a curvilinear pattern arises where depression risk increases in the oldest old when institutionalized adults are included in the sample and when longitudinal study attrition is adjusted for (Mirowsky & Reynolds, 2000; Wu et al., 2012). Importantly, within-person study designs consistently find a U-shape trend of depressive symptoms with age and accelerated increases in depressive symptoms as adults approach late life from middle age (Abrams & Mehta, 2019; Rothermund & Brandtstädter, 2003).

Depression risk factors and prevalence vary across gender and race/ethnicity in important ways. Disparities in depressive symptoms are well documented by gender and race/ethnicity (Hargrove et al., 2020). A recent study of adults over age 50 in the United States found higher levels of depressive symptoms reported by women relative to men, as well as among Hispanic and Black relative to White adults (Abrams & Mehta, 2019). Although the prevalence of higher depressive symptoms was higher among women, men’s depressive symptoms increased faster at age 80 (Abrams & Mehta, 2019). And although Black adults have more lifetime stress exposures compared to White adults (L. L. Brown et al., 2020; Thoits, 2010; Turner & Avison, 2003), some studies find lifetime depressive episodes are lower among Black older adults compared to their White counterparts (Lincoln et al., 2010; Mezuk et al., 2013; Woodward et al., 2012). One theory with growing support is the “age as leveler” hypothesis, whereby population subgroup disparities in depression shrink with older age when physical and social losses are normative regardless of gender, race, ethnicity, and so on (Abrams & Mehta, 2019; Angel et al., 2016). If increasing age is indeed a leveler of depression risk across diverse populations, better understanding what underlies the depression–age association is critical.

An important consideration is what age represents. With increased age, adults are more likely to experience social losses (e.g., widowhood) and their own health and mobility declines, all of which are associated with risk of depression (Wu et al., 2012; Yang, 2007). Further, medical conditions which are more common among older adults often share symptoms with depression (e.g., fatigue, lack of energy), which can make it difficult to disentangle comorbidities, accurate diagnoses, and causal directions among associations of depression and age. Understanding the mechanisms underlying associations of depression with age requires novel approaches to measure what “age” represents. As noted by Wu and colleagues (Wu et al., 2012), the association between age and depressive symptoms in a sample of older adults was explained away after adjusting for physical health and dementia status.

Because aging is a complex phenotype, age-related risk arises from the interaction between gene and environment (Jylhävä et al., 2019). One unique construct reflecting the biological impact of a lifetime of exposures and behavior is estimated biological age, frequently assessed via DNA methylation. As a biological process that regulates DNA activity and gene expression, DNA methylation has a close relationship with age (Johnson et al., 2012). DNA methylation is described as the covalent attachment of a methyl group onto the cytosine residue of DNA, primarily occurring on CpG dinucleotides (Moore et al., 2013). Changes in DNA methylation level occur throughout the lifetime (Jones et al., 2015). Extrinsic factors can also contribute to DNA methylation modification. Recent findings show that psychiatric symptoms and disorders initiate anomalous DNA methylation patterns (Dalton et al., 2014; Lam et al., 2012). High levels of depressive symptoms are also associated with increasing risk for a number of age-related disease states. Specifically, persons with depressive symptoms are at higher risk of diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and Alzheimer’s disease (Kennedy et al., 2014). The associations between depressive symptoms with a variety of chronic age-related diseases suggest that mental disorders are a precursor of accelerated aging (Moffitt & Caspi, 2019; Wertz et al., 2021). They also raise the possibility that an underlying mechanism of accelerated biological aging explains links among psychiatric conditions, diseases, and mortality (Wolkowitz et al., 2010, 2011).

DNA methylation-based biomarkers are one promising way to measure biological aging, which refers to the level of biological functional capability of a person or organ compared to an expected level for a given chronological age (Sayed et al., 2021) and reflects the individual’s health status (Qiu et al., 2023). Levels of DNA methylation at sets of CpG sites and traits are used to develop DNA methylation age estimators (Horvath & Raj, 2018) and yield an estimate of biological aging (Protsenko et al., 2021). When a person’s estimated DNA methylation age is higher than their chronological age, accelerated DNA methylation age or accelerated biological age is said to occur. The existing DNA methylation age estimate has different targets of prediction. The first-generation DNA methylation age estimates, including the Horvath clock (Horvath, 2013) and the Hannum clock (Hannum et al., 2013), aimed to identify the CpG sites that were most predictive of chronological age. Unlike the initial DNA methylation clocks targeting predictions of true chronological age, second-generation estimates of biological aging sought to capture the departure from chronological aging and perform better on morbidity and mortality measures (Levine et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2019). A recent study examining how estimated biological age, measured via DNA methylation PhenoAge estimator, found that a higher subjective age (feel older than their chronological age) was positively correlated with accelerated biological age, which was partially explained by an inflammation biomarker and self-rated health (Stephan et al., 2021). As a second-generation estimate of biological aging, GrimAge was originally constructed in a complex two-stage process in order to predict lifespan and time-to-death (Lu et al., 2019). First, researchers defined surrogate DNAm biomarkers of physiological risk factors, stress factors, and smoking pack-years. Second, time-to-death due to all-cause mortality (dependent variable) was predicted using an algorithm derived by DNA methylation biomarkers defined in the first stage, as well as gender and age. These biomarkers were combined into a single composite biomarker of lifespan, GrimAge, which is expressed in units of years. The GrimAge two-stage derived DNA methylation age estimate stands out among existing ones by predicting morbidity and mortality better, showing strong associations with numerous age-related conditions in many population studies (Hillary et al., 2020; Lu et al., 2019), and is therefore likely to be sensitive for assessing biological aging related to psychiatric conditions (Protsenko et al., 2021).

DNA methylation age acceleration has been observed in middle-aged adults with major depressive disorder. Among 1,130 Dutch participants aged 41 on average, major depressive disorder was associated with 0.64 years accelerated blood DNA methylation age, using a custom estimator (Han et al., 2018). Similarly, among 109 participants aged 40 on average, major depressive disorder was associated with 2 years of accelerated GrimAge measured in blood (Protsenko et al., 2021). However, these studies in younger participants were limited by small sample sizes and strict diagnosis criteria of the condition. The association of subclinical depressive symptoms with biological aging among more vulnerable and diverse samples of older adults remains less clear and is a gap in the extant literature.

Thus, to investigate the underlying association of depressive symptoms on aging processes, we first must map the molecular pathophysiologic associations of depressive symptoms with estimated biological age. In light of the insufficient evidence of biological aging related differences in depressive symptoms among older adults, we examine the association between depressive symptoms and GrimAge age acceleration in a large and diverse cross-sectional sample (N = 3,882) from the U.S. Health and Retirement Study (HRS). We also assessed the effect modification of gender and race/ethnicity, given the lack of evidence on the disparities across gender and race/ethnic groups in the association between depressive symptoms and biological aging.

Method

Transparency and Openness

We report our sample size and describe data exclusions, manipulations, and all measures in the study. All data, analysis code, and research materials are available. Data were analyzed using R (R Core Team, 2022). This study’s design and its analysis were not preregistered.

Study Sample

The HRS is an ongoing, nationally representative, panel study of Americans over 50 years old (Juster & Suzman, 1995). The cohort began in 1992 with surveys occurring every 2 years. The HRS uses a multistage area probability design with geographical stratification and oversampling of African American and Hispanic households. For this analysis, we use the 2016 wave, where the HRS collected and processed venous blood samples for DNA methylation measures (Crimmins et al., 2017), leaving an eligible sample of 4,018 participants with DNA methylation measures.

Venous blood was collected by a trained phlebotomist, using a whole blood EDTA vacutainer blood tube (Crimmins et al., 2017). Samples were shipped overnight to the University of Minnesota Advanced Research and Diagnostic Laboratory and processed within 48 hr of collection (Crimmins et al., 2020). DNA methylation levels were quantified using the Infinium MethylationEPIC BeadChip (Illumina Inc.) for 4,104 samples randomized across plates by key demographic variables including age, cohort, gender, education, and race/ethnicity. Measures from 86 samples were excluded during quality control assessments (Crimmins et al., 2020). DNA methylation age estimates were calculated and released by the HRS (Crimmins et al., 2020).

We visualized sample inclusion criteria using a flowchart (Figure 1). To assess selection bias, included participants were compared to participants excluded due to missing data using the Wilcoxon rank sum test for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-squared test for categorical variables. We calculated two-sided P values from these tests at an α of .05 in all models. Compared to participants excluded due to missing data, included participants differed significantly on race/ethnicity, education, and the number of days per week drinking alcohol, with our included sample more likely to be non-Hispanic, have a degree, and have a higher number of days drinking alcohol on average. Our included sample did not significantly differ from excluded participants on our key variables (GrimAge age acceleration and depressive symptoms; Supplemental Table S1). The analytic sample was restricted to participants who had complete data for depressive symptoms, DNA methylation age, and all covariates, leaving the final analytic sample at 3,882 participants.

The HRS data is sponsored by the National Institute on Aging (Grant Number U01AG009740) and is conducted by the University of Michigan. This secondary analysis was approved by the University of Michigan Institutional Review Board (HUM00128220). Participants provided written informed consent. Data (Health and Retirement Study, 2017) are publicly available at https://hrsdata.isr.umich.edu/data-products.

Depressive Symptom Measures

Depressive symptoms were assessed by self-report on the modified eight-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression (CES-D) scale (Radloff, 1977). The eight-item version of the CES-D scale is consistent, validated, and reliable in measuring depressive symptoms in older persons in comparison to the original full-length 20-item version of the CES-D (Briggs et al., 2018; Karim et al., 2015). The yes/no response format of the eight-item CES-D used in the HRS was found to share similar psychometric properties with the full scale and is less taxing to administer to older adults over the phone (Turvey et al., 1999). In the questionnaire, participants responded “yes” or “no” as to whether they experienced certain symptoms much of the time during the past week, including feeling depressed, everything they did was an effort, restless sleep, happy, lonely, enjoying life, sad, and could not get going. The individual “no” responses to the “enjoyed life” and “happy” items and the “yes” responses to the rest of the questions were summed for a final score ranging from 0 to 8. We created two dichotomous depressive symptoms variables from the continuous CES-D score. First, a variable indicating high depressive symptoms (CES-D ≥ 4) versus low/no depressive symptoms (CES-D < 4), using a cutoff that has been found to produce comparable results to the 16-symptom cutoff for the well-validated 20-item CES-D scale (Steffick et al., 2000). We also created an indicator variable of any depressive symptoms (CES-D ≥ 1) versus no depressive symptoms (CES-D = 0). These two dichotomous variables are incorporated as exposures to identify associations between DNA methylation age acceleration and depressive symptoms at different burdens of depressive symptoms.

DNA Methylation Age Acceleration Measures

Our primary estimate of biological age acceleration was calculated using the GrimAge method. GrimAge uses the mortality risks of age together with other lifestyle factors to predict lifespan (Lu et al., 2019). To create the age acceleration values, we regressed the DNA methylation age estimate against chronological age and captured the residuals from this model (Krieger et al., 2023; Lu et al., 2019). We show the relationship between chronological age and the GrimAge estimates, along with chronological age and the GrimAge age acceleration residuals used in these analyses in scatter plots (Figure 2). We modeled these residuals as continuous in linear regressions, and we dichotomized the residuals into accelerated aging (residuals > 0) or no age acceleration (residuals ≤ 0) for bivariate descriptive analyses.

Covariate Measures

Sociodemographic Variables

Sociodemographic information was provided through self-reports at baseline interviews including gender, race/ethnicity, and education. Gender was self-reported by participants as male (reference category) or female at their baseline interview when they joined the HRS. Gender in our sample is concordant with biological sex through genetic analyses. We combined self-reported race and Hispanic ethnicity and collapsed them into three categories as non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black or other, and Hispanic. We used educational level as a proxy for socioeconomic status. At baseline, participants were asked to report the highest level of education they received, ranging from no degree to a professional degree such as a PhD or MD (no degree, General Education Development degree, high school diploma, 2-year college, 4-year college, master, professional degree, type unknown). These categories were then collapsed to no degree (reference category), General Education Development/high school diploma, and college or more than college.

Interviews at the 2016 wave provided chronological age (years) and marital status (currently married, not currently married) in 2016. We calculated chronological age using birthdate and interview date. Marital status was initially measured using the categories of married, separated/divorced, widowed, never married, and marital status unknown. We collapsed this variable to two categories, currently married and not currently married, with currently married as the reference category.

Cell Type Measures

Cell type composition is an important precision variable for DNA methylation analyses conducted in heterogeneous tissues containing multiple cell types, such as blood. Since cell types have unique DNA methylation profiles, the proportion of a given cell type in a composite sample often explains a high proportion of the variability in the DNA methylation measure and it is important to control for cell type distributions (Campbell et al., 2020). Percentages of granulocytes, monocytes, and lymphocytes were assessed from a complete blood count using a Sysmex XE-21000 instrument (Crimmins et al., 2017) and were released by the HRS with the DNA methylation age estimates (Crimmins et al., 2020). Including all three measures introduces collinearity issues in linear modeling, and therefore, we keep lymphocyte percentage from our models.

Health and Health Behavior Variables

We include additional health and health behavior covariates in our fully adjusted models. These variables include physical activity, alcohol use, cigarette smoking, and number of chronic health conditions. Physical activity level was calculated by summing up frequencies of each physical activity intensity multiplied by matched average Metabolic Equivalent of Task, which was 2.5, 4, and 8 for light, moderate, and vigorous physical activity, respectively (Craig et al., 2003; Klimentidis et al., 2018). Alcohol use is calculated as the number of days per week a participant drinks alcohol. Smoking status includes categories of never (reference), former smoker, and current smoker. These three variables are summarized for the HRS by the RAND Center for the Study of Aging with funding from the National Institute on Aging and the Social Security Administration, Santa Monica, CA (produced July 2022; Bugliari et al., 2022). In the HRS, general health status was measured by asking participants to report the number of chronic health conditions a doctor had diagnosed them with out of the following eight conditions: high blood pressure, diabetes, cancer, lung disease, heart disease, stroke, psychiatric problems, and arthritis. The number of chronic health conditions variables sums “yes” responses to these questions and ranges from 0 to 8. The health and health behavior variables were all assessed at the 2016 wave.

Sensitivity Model Variables

Alternative DNA Methylation Age Acceleration Measures

To see if the relationship between depressive symptoms and DNA methylation age acceleration replicated in other age acceleration variables, we examined four additional classic and well-validated DNA methylation age estimators calculated and released by the HRS in sensitivity analyses. These additional age acceleration estimates included Levine, DunedinPoAm (MPOA), Horvath, and Hannum. Horvath and Hannum were selected as the first generation of DNA methylation estimators of chronologic age. Levine was selected as a second generation of measures of phenotypic aging. The Levine age estimator was trained on 513 CpGs to capture the combination of age-related and disease phenotypes as well as phenotypic age (Levine et al., 2018). The MPOA algorithm was trained to predict the rate of aging (Belsky et al., 2020), and we transformed the MPOA rates into years for alignment with the other age estimators. The Horvath age estimator was trained on DNA methylation at 353 CpG sites across 51 different tissues and cell types. This clock is strongly correlated with chronological age (Horvath, 2013). The Hannum age estimator was trained on 71 CpGs selected in blood to capture changes in chronological age (Hannum et al., 2013). We regressed each DNA methylation age estimate on chronological age and took the residuals from the linear model to obtain age acceleration values, as we did for GrimAge. Residuals were modeled as continuous in linear regressions for use in sensitivity analysis. We plotted the distributions of all five age acceleration variables for comparison and included the alternative age acceleration measures in the Pearson correlation plot with quantitative analysis variables.

Statistical Methods

Univariate and Bivariate Descriptive Statistics

In the included sample, we examined the univariate distribution (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, or n and percent) of all variables. We also include bivariate distributions for our analysis variables by dichotomous depressive symptom score (both high depressive symptoms, CES-D ≥ 4 vs. < 4, and any depressive symptoms, CES-D ≥ 1 vs. not), dichotomous GrimAge age acceleration (residuals > 0 vs. ≤ 0), gender (male vs. female), and race/ethnicity (non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black or other, Hispanic).

Linear Regression Models

We conducted analyses with continuous CES-D as our primary exposure and GrimAge age acceleration residuals as our primary outcome. Subsequent models replace the continuous depressive symptom score with the dichotomous high depressive symptoms and any depressive symptoms, separately. Our initial unadjusted model included a variable for depressive symptoms as the exposure and GrimAge age acceleration as the outcome. For Model 1, in the primary adjusted model, we include chronological age, gender, race/ethnicity, marital status, educational attainment, percentage of granulocytes, and percentage of monocytes as covariates. For Model 2, in our fully adjusted model, we added the number of chronic health conditions, number of days per week of alcohol use, smoking status, and physical activity to the primary adjusted model.

Effect Modification by Gender or Race/Ethnicity

To examine if the association of DNA methylation and depressive symptoms is modified by gender or race/ethnicity, we included product terms between depressive symptoms and gender or race/ethnicity in Models 1 and 2. Though our models are not independent from one another, we consider a conservative multiple testing threshold (α = .05/5 = 0.01). We performed all statistical analyses using R Version 4.0.3. Code to perform all analyses is provided (https://github.com/bakulskilab/Depressive-DNAm-Acceleration; Wang, 2023).

Sensitivity Analyses

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our results. For each sensitivity analysis, we follow the modeling strategy described above using an unadjusted model, Model 1 adjusting for sociodemographics and cell type composition estimates, and Model 3 adding health and health behavior covariates. We include effect modification estimates by gender and race/ethnicity in all sensitivity analyses as well.

Alternative DNA Methylation Age Acceleration Measures

To determine whether the relationship between depressive symptoms and accelerated DNA methylation aging was specific to the GrimAge estimator, we examined four alternate DNA methylation age estimators including Levine, MPOA, Horvath, and Hannum.

Excluding Individuals Over Age 85

Noting that the linear modeling constant variance of population errors assumption for the GrimAge age acceleration residuals may be violated in the older age group, we include a sensitivity analysis for our GrimAge linear regression and effect modification by gender and race/ethnicity excluding participants older than 85.

Results

Univariate and Bivariate Descriptive Statistics

We examined the univariate distribution (mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, or n and percent) of all variables (Table 1). Among the analytic sample, the average depressive symptom score was 1.4 (SD = 2). Fourteen percent (n = 556) of participants reported high depressive symptoms (CES-D ≥ 4), and a majority of participants (n = 2,059, 53%) reported any depressive symptoms. The average chronological age in 2016 was 69.8 (SD = 9.6), and 58% (n = 2,260) of the sample reported female gender. Our sample is a majority non-Hispanic White (n = 2,595, 67%), with high school equivalent or higher education (n = 3,276, 83%), and never or former smokers (n = 3,435, 88%). The average number of chronic conditions in our sample is 2.4 (SD = 1.5), and the sample consumes alcohol 1.2 (SD = 2) days per week, on average.

We examined the bivariate correlation between all quantitative variables using a correlation plot (Figure 3). The strongest correlations between variables were within the cell type composition estimate (lymphocyte and granulocyte percentage, r = −0.97), as these variables represent the percentage of cells in a sample of a certain type and sum to 100%. The GrimAge and MPOA age acceleration residuals were significantly, highly correlated (r = 0.62) as well as the Hannum age acceleration residuals with both the Levine age acceleration residuals (r = 0.41) and the Horvath age acceleration residuals (r = 0.43). The MPOA age acceleration residuals were significantly correlated with percent granulocyte (r = 0.36) and percent lymphocyte (r = −0.36). All correlation p values reported here are <.05.

In additional bivariate analyses, those with high depressive symptoms had significantly higher mean GrimAge acceleration residuals than those with low/no depressive symptoms, 1.1 (SD = 5.1) versus −0.2 (SD = 4.7), Supplemental Table S2. Those with high depressive symptoms also had a significantly higher mean number of chronic health conditions, a higher proportion of current smokers, lower mean physical activity, and were more likely to be female, not married, and have no degree than those with low/no depressive symptoms. Those with GrimAge age acceleration residuals > 0 had significantly higher mean CES-D scores, lower mean physical activity, and a higher mean number of chronic health conditions than those with nonage acceleration. Importantly, those with GrimAge age acceleration had a higher proportion of current smokers (n = 414, 24%) compared to those with no GrimAge age acceleration (n = 33, 1.5%). Female and Hispanic participants were more likely to have high depressive symptoms. Male and non-Hispanic Black or other participants were more likely to have higher GrimAge acceleration. Bivariate comparisons by dichotomous depressive symptoms and GrimAge age acceleration, gender, and race/ethnicity can be found in Supplemental Table S2.

Linear Regression Models

We performed linear regression models to test whether depressive symptoms are associated with GrimAge age acceleration (Table 2). In the unadjusted model, continuous depressive symptoms score was significantly associated with GrimAge age acceleration (B = 0.28, SE = 0.04, p < .001; Table 2). High depressive symptoms (B = 1.26, SE = 0.22, p < .001) and any depressive symptoms (B = 1.07, SE = 0.15, p < .001) were also significantly associated with increased GrimAge age acceleration (Supplemental Table S3). In each model, depressive symptoms explain slightly over 1% of the variation in GrimAge age acceleration.

Depressive symptoms, regardless of the operationalization, remained strongly significantly associated with GrimAge age acceleration in our multivariable Model 1, adjusted for age, gender, race/ethnicity, education, marital status, and cell type distribution (Table 2, Supplemental Table S3).

In our fully adjusted Model 2, adding adjustment for physical activity, alcohol use, smoking status, and number of chronic health conditions, the association of continuous depressive symptom score attenuated to null (B = 0.15, SE = 0.12, p = .24; Table 2). The effect of both high depressive symptoms (B = 0.22, SE = 0.17, p = .19) and any depressive symptoms (B = 0.19, SE = 0.18, p = .18) also attenuated to null after additional adjustment (Supplemental Table S3). Model fit based on adjusted R2 value and likelihood ratio test both improved significantly after adjustment for Model 2 covariates. It is possible that these factors likely partially mediate the association between depressive symptoms and DNA methylation age acceleration, so our results may underestimate the full effect.

Effect Modification by Gender or Race/Ethnicity

To examine if the association of DNA methylation and depressive symptoms is modified by gender or race/ethnicity, we included product terms between depressive symptoms and gender or race/ethnicity in Models 1 and 2. For the continuous depressive symptom score, we did not see a significant interaction between depressive symptoms and gender in either Model 1 (demographics) or Model 2 (health and health behaviors). This null finding was consistent for any depressive symptoms. We did see a preliminarily significant interaction between gender and high depressive symptoms in Model 1 (B = −0.83, SE = 0.41, p = .04). That is, holding all covariates constant and setting them to the mean or reference category, reporting high depressive symptoms increases GrimAge age acceleration differentially for males (from 2.1 to 3.8 years) and females (from −1.0 to −0.1). This means that experiencing high depressive symptoms for males increases the average difference between biological age and chronological age by 1.7 years for males and by 0.9 years for females. When adjusting for additional health and health behavioral covariates, the gender-high depressive symptoms interaction effect became nonsignificant.

When we estimated effect modification by race/ethnicity with continuous depressive symptoms, we again saw no significant interaction in either Model 1 or Model 2. We observed no significant interaction between race/ethnicity and high depressive symptoms.

Sensitivity Analyses

Alternative DNA Methylation Age Acceleration Measures

The distributions of each alternative DNA methylation age acceleration residuals (Levine, Horvath, MPOA, Horvath, and Hannum) were normal and centered around zero (Supplemental Figure S1). When examining other DNA methylation age estimators, depressive symptoms were not significantly associated with any of the four alternative DNA methylation age acceleration measures in fully adjusted models (Supplemental Table S4). For Levine age acceleration, we did observe a significant interaction with continuous depressive symptom score and both Hispanic (B = −0.3, SE = 0.14, p = .03) and non-Hispanic Black or other (B = −0.33, SE = 0.14, p = .02) race/ethnicity. Holding all variables constant, those reporting the highest depressive symptom score (CES-D = 8) and self-reporting non-Hispanic Black or other race/ethnicity had a Levine age acceleration 1.14 years higher than White participants and 0.63 years higher than Hispanic participants with the highest depressive symptom score, on average. We also noted a significant interaction between high depressive symptom score (CES-D ≥ 4) and female gender (B = 1.29, SE = 0.62, p = .04) for the Horvath age acceleration outcome. Females with high depressive symptoms demonstrated an average Horvath age acceleration 0.31 higher than females with no depressive symptoms. Both of these findings were in fully adjusted models (Supplemental Table S4). Neither of these interactions remains after multiple testing corrections.

Excluding Individuals Over Age 85

To test the robustness of our results to the age of our sample, we performed linear regression models for the GrimAge age acceleration residuals in a sample of participants 85 years and younger. Unadjusted and Model 1 associations between each depressive symptom operationalization and GrimAge age acceleration were consistent in strength, direction, and effect size with our full sample and all strongly significant (Supplemental Table S5). In Model 2, adjusting by our second set of covariates, all depressive symptom–GrimAge age acceleration associations attenuated to null, as in our primary models. We again saw a significant interaction between female gender and high depressive symptoms (CES-D score ≥ 4) in our sociodemographic and cell type adjusted Model 1. All of our sensitivity analyses with the sample of individuals 85 years and younger were consistent with our primary analyses (Supplemental Table S5).

Discussion

This cross-sectional study examined the relationship between depressive symptoms and accelerated DNA methylation age among a large, diverse sample of Americans over 50 years old. Among 3,882 participants, depressive symptoms—regardless of how they were operationalized—were associated with accelerated DNA methylation aging measured using GrimAge in unadjusted models and models adjusted for sociodemographic and cell type composition measures. However, the depressive symptom effects attenuated to nonsignificance after adjustment for health and health behavior covariates, including physical activity, alcohol use, smoking status, and number of chronic health conditions. We ultimately found no significant effect modification of the depressive symptom–GrimAge age acceleration relationship by gender or self-reported race/ethnicity in the fully adjusted models.

Support From Existing Literature

We investigated depressive symptoms as an exposure to capture the effects of potential preclinical indicators of depressive disorder on DNA methylation aging. Depressive symptoms affect a larger share of the population than clinically diagnostic forms of major depressive disorder. Previous studies have generally focused on major depressive disorder. Major depressive disorder involves an increased burden of depressive symptoms for an extended period that interferes with daily life and represents a more severe phenotype than our use of depressive symptom counts. Studies have found varying effect sizes for the relationship between major depressive disorder and accelerated DNA methylation age. These effects range from an average DNA methylation age estimate of 0.64 years higher for those with major depressive disorder than controls (Han et al., 2018), to around a 2-year increased median GrimAge acceleration for those with major depressive disorder compared to controls (Protsenko et al., 2021). These samples were from middle-aged participants with a mean age around 40 years (Han et al., 2018; Protsenko et al., 2021). Additionally, postmortem brain tissue for those with major depressive disorder has shown an average increase of 1.11 years for the Horvath DNA methylation age acceleration compared to controls (Han et al., 2018). Our unadjusted observation of an increase of 1.26 years in GrimAge age acceleration for those with high depressive symptoms is in line with these results.

Causal Hypotheses of the Association Between Depressive Symptoms and Accelerated DNA Methylation Aging

There are several hypotheses explaining the biological mechanism through which depressive symptoms may lead to accelerated DNA methylation age. Chronic diseases such as major depressive disorder could be prolonged stress exposures, which is supported by studies which found chronic stress has multiple physical and epigenetic effects throughout the body (McEwen, 2017; Ridout et al., 2016). Prolonged exposure and increased severity of depressive symptoms may produce chronic stress throughout the body that affects DNA methylation and other cellular characteristics.

After adjusting for health behavioral variables, the association between depressive symptoms and DNA methylation age acceleration became nonsignificant. This raises the possibility that there is no direct association between depressive symptoms and the biological aging process and the association is mediated by other characteristics. Increases in depressive symptoms could also result in behavioral changes, such as smoking, less physical activity, and dietary changes (Boden et al., 2010; Jacka et al., 2010; Jerstad et al., 2010; McManus et al., 2010). These consequent health behavioral changes are all risk factors for epigenetic effects (Kim et al., 2022; Klopack, Carroll, et al., 2022; Kresovich et al., 2021) and likely to mediate the association between depressive symptoms and DNA methylation aging. The average burden of chronic conditions and disability in daily activities increases with age. The mediating effect of the health status and health behaviors will also become stronger when we look at the depressive symptoms–aging association among older adults. Another study assessing the mediating role of elevated depressive symptoms between perceived discrimination and DNA methylation age acceleration also reported a nonsignificant relationship of depressive symptoms with GrimAge and MPOA after adjusting health-related factors (Beydoun et al., 2022). Health behavioral variables could confound the association and lead to an attenuated and even nonsignificant result after adjusting for them. Future studies could investigate the effect of long-term depressive symptoms and use a more complex analytic method to identify whether the attenuation is due to the confounding of health behavior variables or the overadjustment of potential mediators.

Alternative Estimates of Accelerated DNA Methylation Aging

First-generation clocks (e.g., Horvath and Hannum) were designed to predict chronological age based on DNA methylation sites (Hannum et al., 2013; Horvath, 2013). Second-generation clocks (e.g., GrimAge and Levine) built on the foundation of the chronologic clocks and incorporate additional factors to better reflect an individual’s health and aging process (Levine et al., 2018; Lu et al., 2019). Similar to our findings with GrimAge age acceleration, we found no significant associations between depressive symptoms and our four alternative DNA methylation age acceleration measures (Levine, MPOA, Horvath, and Hannum) in fully adjusted models. Our findings are also consistent with the sparse literature on depressive symptoms and DNA methylation age. When examining depressive symptomology among a cohort of 724 Danish monozygotic twins, the depressive symptomatology score was not associated with Horvath age acceleration, after adjustment for chronological age (Starnawska et al., 2019).

Effect Modification by Gender or Race/Ethnicity

We did not find robust evidence of moderation of our biological age–depressive symptom relationship by either gender or race/ethnicity. While we did note a preliminary interaction between gender and high depressive symptoms interaction with the Horvath DNA methylation age acceleration measure as well as a race/ethnicity by continuous depressive symptom score interaction for the Levine DNA methylation age acceleration measure, these effects did not survive multiple testing. There is biological precedent for considering gender and race/ethnic moderation in biological aging and psychopathology. Hormones such as estrogen and testosterone can significantly influence DNA methylation patterns. Estrogen, for example, has been found to mediate epigenetic changes in the brain, which can affect mood and depression susceptibility (Kigar & Auger, 2013). Further, there is emerging evidence that race and ethnicity interact with DNA methylation in the context of psychopathology (Uddin et al., 2010). Our results are preliminary and not definite. There is potential for replication in larger sample sizes, which is crucial in clarifying these interaction findings. Further, the temporality of these effects remains unclear, as few large studies have multiple measures of DNA methylation along with depressive symptoms at different time points in a diverse sample.

Limitations and Strengths

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting these results. The cross-sectional nature of this analysis prevents us from establishing that depressive symptoms precede DNA methylation age acceleration and consequently may be subject to reverse causation bias. There are several studies showing the association with biological aging as exposure and depression as outcome (Beydoun et al., 2019; P. J. Brown et al., 2018), which raise the possibility of bidirectional relations. Therefore, building the timeline using longitudinal settings in future work is necessary. In addition, more research is needed to understand how the cumulative experience of depressive symptoms across an individual’s lifetime may accelerate DNA methylation aging. Depressive symptoms in this study were measured by self-reported feelings 1 week before the interview in our study. Evaluation of recent depressive symptoms may not accurately capture long-term exposure impacting the course of biological aging. Tissue specificity of DNA methylation signatures may be a concern in considering these results. We used DNA methylation age measured from whole blood, though the brain is the preferred target tissue for these measures when looking at depressive symptoms (Li et al., 2018). However, blood DNA methylation may correlate with brain DNA methylation and whole blood is useful in cases where brain tissue would not be available, such as when using large, population-based studies of living participants (Hannon et al., 2015). Future research into this area could focus on longitudinal designs while controlling for additional confounders and potentially investigating DNA methylation in disease-specific tissue. Finally, for the purposes of this analysis, we did not use sampling weights, so our results are only generalizable to people over 50 years old living in the United States with similar demographic characteristics to the nonrandom sample from whom the DNA methylation clock data was collected. The selectivity into the study may also introduce selection bias by demographic factors, which will bias our results on testing effect modifiers by gender and race/ethnicity. Future research should investigate whether this association is robust across different scales of measuring depressive symptoms and in other populations.

We were able to include a large number of participants in this study due to the availability of our measures of interest in a large population study. Additionally, we were able to adjust for cell type proportions in our analysis, an important precision variable in measuring DNA methylation aging. Our focus on depressive symptoms rather than major depressive disorder diagnosis makes these results more generalizable to those experiencing varying levels and severity of depressive symptoms rather than diagnosable major depression. Finally, in prior studies assessing associations of estimated biological age with health in the HRS sample, researchers used raw DNA methylation age to estimate biological aging (Beydoun et al., 2022; Klopack, Crimmins, et al., 2022). In the present study, we used the residuals of DNA methylation age estimators against chronological age as a dependent variable, which captures the difference in DNA methylation age with chronological age and we believe better captures biological aging. Due to these strengths, our study adds to the emerging literature investigating links between psychological conditions and accelerated DNA methylation age.

Summary

In summary, in this cross-sectional analysis, we observed no significant association between depressive symptoms and DNA methylation age acceleration after controlling for sociodemographic, cell type proportions, health, and health behavior covariates among participants in the HRS. Depressive symptoms fluctuate, and as depressive symptoms change in the population over time, it is important to study their long-term impact on epigenetic effects. Using longitudinal studies, it would also be useful to establish a temporal relationship between experiencing depressive symptoms and estimated biological age acceleration for the purpose of understanding the effects of depressive symptoms over the life course. It is evident that health and health behaviors play a large role in DNA methylation age acceleration as well as depressive phenotypes, and uncovering their specific role will be an important part of evaluating any psychological factors as a correlate of DNA methylation age acceleration.
