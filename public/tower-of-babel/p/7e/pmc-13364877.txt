A multimodal imaging approach to predict carpal tunnel syndrome after distal radius fractures
Authors: Mehmet Önüt, Nihat Alkol, Yiğit Önaloğlu, Mete Özer, Eren Apaydın, Mehmet Ali Talmaç
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDistal radius fractures are commonly associated with the development of carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS), yet current evidence primarily focuses on radiographic alignment parameters. The role of carpal tunnel morphology, particularly total carpal tunnel cross-sectional area (CSA) measured on computed tomography (CT), remains insufficiently investigated. This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between CSA, volar tilt, and the development of CTS in conservatively managed distal radius fractures, and to assess the predictive performance of these parameters when used in combination.MethodsThis retrospective case-control study included 150 patients, comprising 50 patients who developed CTS and 100 controls without CTS. CSA was measured on CT scans obtained at initial presentation, while volar tilt was assessed using standard radiographs. Additional variables, including the presence of ulna fracture and fracture type, were analyzed. Group comparisons were performed using nonparametric tests. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify independent predictors of CTS. Diagnostic performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis.ResultsPatients who developed CTS had significantly lower CSA and higher volar tilt values (both p < 0.001). The presence of an ulna fracture was inversely associated with CTS (p = 0.004). Multivariable analysis identified CSA (OR = 0.748, 95% CI: 0.643–0.871, p < 0.001), volar tilt (OR = 1.132, 95% CI: 1.051–1.219, p = 0.001), and ulna fracture (OR = 0.249, 95% CI: 0.076–0.817, p = 0.022) as independent predictors of CTS. ROC analysis showed moderate discriminative ability for CSA and volar tilt individually (AUC = 0.685 and 0.708, respectively), which improved when combined (AUC = 0.772). The highest predictive performance was achieved with the combined model including CSA, volar tilt, and ulna fracture (AUC = 0.802).ConclusionIn conservatively managed distal radius fractures, carpal tunnel cross-sectional area (CSA) and volar tilt are independently associated with the development of CTS. While CSA alone demonstrates limited discriminative ability, its combination with radiographic parameters appears to improve predictive performance. These findings suggest that incorporating CT-based morphological measurements alongside conventional radiographic assessment may provide additional value in early risk stratification for CTS.Level of evidenceLevel III, retrospective casecontrol study.Keywords: Distal radius fracture, Carpal tunnel syndrome, Computed tomography, Carpal tunnel cross-sectional area, Volar tilt, Radiologic predictors

Introduction

Distal radius fractures are among the most common fractures in the adult population and may lead to significant functional impairment, particularly in elderly patients. A substantial proportion of these fractures are managed conservatively. The clinical outcomes of distal radius fractures depend not only on bone healing but also on complications such as malunion, functional limitation, chronic pain, complex regional pain syndrome, and particularly carpal tunnel syndrome resulting from median nerve compression [8, 11, 12]. Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) is one of the most important neurological complications that may occur following distal radius fractures and may develop in the acute, subacute, or delayed period.

Acute traumatic carpal tunnel syndrome develops due to increased compartment pressure within the carpal tunnel and is associated with median nerve compression resulting from volume reduction caused by fracture fragments, intratunnel hematoma, and soft tissue edema [5]. The incidence of acute CTS associated with distal radius fractures has been reported to range between 3% and 17%, and if not diagnosed and treated early, it may lead to serious complications such as permanent median nerve dysfunction and complex regional pain syndrome [9]. However, particularly in the acute period, the diagnosis of CTS may be challenging due to non-specific symptoms and variability in clinical findings, which complicates clinical management.

Most studies aiming to predict the development of CTS following distal radius fractures have focused on radiographic parameters. The relationship between measurements such as volar tilt, radial inclination, and fracture displacement and CTS has been investigated, and volar tilt in particular has been reported to be associated with delayed CTS development [3, 7, 8]. However, in the majority of these studies, these assessments have been performed based on post-reduction or late follow-up radiographs. This raises the question of whether the anatomical and morphological features present at the time of fracture can provide predictive value for the development of CTS in the subsequent period.

Computed tomography (CT) allows detailed evaluation not only of fracture morphology but also of the cross-sectional anatomy of the carpal tunnel and surrounding osseous structures in distal radius fractures. The carpal tunnel cross-sectional area (CSA) is a quantitatively measurable parameter and has been shown to be associated with carpal tunnel syndrome. Such cross-sectional measurements allow objective evaluation of anatomical narrowing and volume changes that may predispose to median nerve compression [2]. However, studies evaluating the prediction of future CTS risk based on the assessment of carpal tunnel morphology using CT images obtained at the time of fracture in conservatively treated distal radius fractures are limited in the literature.

Therefore, the primary aim of this study was to evaluate the relationship between the total carpal tunnel cross-sectional area measured on CT images obtained at the time of fracture and the development of CTS during follow-up in conservatively treated distal radius fractures. The secondary aim was to determine the prognostic performance of these parameters in predicting CTS development when evaluated together with volar tilt and fracture characteristics measured on initial radiographs. This approach aims to predict the risk of future complications based on data obtained at the time of initial presentation, in contrast to the existing literature that predominantly relies on late-stage deformity-based assessments. In addition, since CT imaging is frequently obtained in routine clinical practice during the evaluation of distal radius fractures, this method provides a practical and cost-effective approach without requiring additional imaging.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective case-control study included patients with distal radius fractures managed conservatively at our institution between May 2020 and May 2026. A total of 13,258 wrist CT scans were screened through the institutional radiology database. Among these, 2,852 patients were identified as having isolated distal radius fractures. Of these, 2,282 patients were managed conservatively, while 570 patients underwent surgical treatment.

Patients were divided into two groups according to the development of carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) during follow-up. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 50 patients who developed CTS were included in the CTS group. For the control group, 100 patients without CTS were selected from the same conservatively treated study population using random sampling at a 1:2 case-control ratio.

Inclusion criteria were: age ≥ 18 years, history of conservatively treated distal radius fracture, availability of wrist computed tomography (CT) imaging obtained at the time of initial presentation, availability of standard anteroposterior wrist radiographs, and a minimum follow-up of 12 months. Patients with a prior diagnosis of CTS, previous wrist surgery, pathological fractures, multiple trauma, carpal bone fractures, or inadequate imaging quality were excluded.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our Level III Trauma Center (Approval No: 2026 − 193). Due to the retrospective design of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

CT acquisition and measurement of carpal tunnel area

All CT scans were obtained at the time of initial trauma evaluation. Axial images were reviewed using dedicated imaging software (RadiAnt DICOM Viewer).

To ensure measurement standardization, multiplanar reformation (MPR) was applied in all cases. Coronal and sagittal planes were adjusted to align the dataset according to the anatomical axis of the wrist, allowing the axial plane to be reconstructed consistently at the level of the hook of hamate region, where the carpal tunnel is optimally visualized (Fig. 1).

MPR-based measurement of carpal tunnel cross-sectional area on wrist CT at the level of the hook of hamate

The carpal tunnel cross-sectional area (CSA) was measured on a single standardized axial slice. The boundaries of the carpal tunnel were defined by the surrounding carpal bones and the expected course of the flexor retinaculum.

Using a freehand region-of-interest (ROI) technique, the contour of the carpal tunnel was manually traced along its anatomical borders. The software automatically calculated the enclosed area, which was recorded in square millimeters (mm²). To evaluate measurement reproducibility, interobserver reliability analysis was performed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Measurements were independently performed by two observers, and the ICC value for CSA measurements was 0.92, indicating excellent agreement.

Radiographic measurements

Standard anteroposterior and lateral wrist radiographs obtained at initial presentation were used for radiographic assessment. Volar tilt was measured on lateral radiographs using standard techniques. Additional variables, including fracture type (intra-articular vs. extra-articular) and presence of associated ulna fracture, were also recorded (Fig. 2).

Measurement of volar tilt on lateral wrist radiographs obtained at initial presentation

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics. The primary outcome was the presence of carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS). The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. As normality assumptions were not met, comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation and median (interquartile range), while categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages. Categorical variables were compared using Fisher’s exact test.

A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify independent predictors of CTS, and results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate the discriminative ability of the variables, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated with 95% confidence intervals. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 150 patients were included in the study, of whom 50 comprised the CTS group and 100 the control group without CTS. The mean age of the overall cohort was 46.0 ± 13.8 years.

Among patients who developed CTS, 68% had mild, 22% moderate, and 10% severe disease. No statistically significant association was found between CTS severity and carpal tunnel cross-sectional area or volar tilt (p = 0.346 and p = 0.352, respectively). The mean time from injury to CTS diagnosis was 4.1 ± 1.3 months, consistent with delayed CTS development.

In group comparisons, patients who developed CTS had a significantly lower carpal tunnel cross-sectional area compared to controls (1965.6 ± 238.7 mm² vs. 2193.6 ± 345.1 mm², p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). In contrast, volar tilt values were significantly higher in the CTS group (11.27 ± 4.56 vs. 6.09 ± 9.44, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

The presence of an ulna fracture was significantly less frequent in patients who developed CTS (10.0% vs. 31.0%, p = 0.004). Similarly, the rate of intra-articular fractures was lower in the CTS group, and this difference was significant in univariable analysis (12.0% vs. 27.0%, p = 0.039) (Table 1).

In multivariable logistic regression analysis, carpal tunnel cross-sectional area, volar tilt, and the presence of an ulna fracture were independently associated with CTS. An increase in carpal tunnel area was associated with a reduced likelihood of CTS (OR = 0.748, 95% CI: 0.643–0.871, p < 0.001). In contrast, an increase in volar tilt was associated with an increased risk of CTS (OR = 1.132, 95% CI: 1.051–1.219, p = 0.001). The presence of an ulna fracture showed an inverse association with CTS (OR = 0.249, 95% CI: 0.076–0.817, p = 0.022). Fracture type (intra-articular vs. extra-articular) was not independently associated with CTS in multivariable analysis (p = 0.602) (Table 2).

In ROC analysis, carpal tunnel area and volar tilt demonstrated moderate discriminative ability when evaluated individually (AUC = 0.685 and 0.708, respectively). The combination of these two parameters improved discrimination (AUC = 0.772), although this value did not exceed the 0.80 threshold. The highest discriminative performance was observed in the model combining carpal tunnel area, volar tilt, and the presence of an ulna fracture (AUC = 0.802) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The most important finding of this study is that, in conservatively managed distal radius fractures, carpal tunnel cross-sectional area measured by computed tomography at the time of injury and volar tilt assessed on initial radiographs are independently associated with the development of carpal tunnel syndrome during follow-up. In addition, while carpal tunnel area alone demonstrated limited discriminative ability, its predictive performance increased markedly when evaluated together with volar tilt and the presence of an ulna fracture. These findings indicate that, in the evaluation of distal radius fractures, not only radiographic parameters based on bone alignment but also cross-sectional measurements reflecting carpal tunnel morphology should be taken into consideration.

In the existing literature, the development of carpal tunnel syndrome following distal radius fractures has been mostly evaluated based on radiographic alignment parameters such as volar tilt, radial inclination, and radial shortening [3, 8]. Additional measurements such as the radiolunate angle and lunate-to-axis-of-radius distance have also been described [4]. However, the majority of these studies do not directly assess carpal tunnel morphology and do not clearly demonstrate the effect of these structural features on future neurological complications. In this context, our study confirms the association between volar tilt, a commonly used and clinically relevant parameter, and CTS, while also providing a new perspective based on the direct measurement of carpal tunnel morphology. From a clinical standpoint, this approach may contribute to the evaluation of the need for simultaneous carpal tunnel release during surgical planning in patients with distal radius fractures, and to closer and more careful follow-up for the development of CTS in conservatively managed cases [1, 10].

However, MRI-based studies have shown that both the median nerve and carpal tunnel cross-sectional areas are increased in patients diagnosed with carpal tunnel syndrome [6]. Interpretation of these findings requires careful consideration of the characteristics of the study populations. In those studies, the evaluated patients consisted of individuals who already had underlying CTS and therefore may have had structurally enlarged median nerves and altered carpal tunnel morphology. In contrast, the population in our study consisted of patients without a prior history of CTS before trauma. Therefore, our findings emphasize the role of carpal tunnel morphology, rather than median nerve size, in the development of CTS. As observed in our study, a narrower carpal tunnel area may lead to a more pronounced increase in pressure in response to post-traumatic edema and volume expansion, thereby increasing the risk of median nerve compression. From this perspective, carpal tunnel cross-sectional area may represent a more critical anatomical marker for CTS development than median nerve size.

The inverse association between ulna fracture and CTS development observed in this study should be interpreted cautiously. One possible explanation is that associated ulnar fractures may alter force transmission across the wrist by redistributing trauma energy, thereby reducing localized stress concentration around the distal radius and carpal tunnel region. However, this explanation remains speculative, and the biological mechanism underlying this association is not clearly understood. Moreover, this finding may reflect unmeasured differences in fracture severity, injury patterns, or treatment-related factors. Residual confounding and selection bias inherent to the retrospective design may also have influenced the observed association. Therefore, this finding should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of a protective effect and requires confirmation in larger prospective studies.

3In our study, although individual parameters demonstrated limited discriminative ability, the combined evaluation of variables significantly improved predictive performance. The combination of carpal tunnel area and volar tilt provided moderate discrimination, while the addition of ulna fracture status yielded the highest performance. These findings support the concept that the development of CTS is a multifactorial process involving bone alignment, tunnel morphology, and associated injuries. Clinically, the combined analysis of these parameters may contribute to the early identification of high-risk patients.

Our study has several strengths. The evaluation of carpal tunnel morphology using cross-sectional area measurements on CT with multiplanar reconstruction provides a more objective approach that is less affected by positional variables compared to indirect radiographic parameters. In addition, the use of existing CT images, as an alternative to MRI-based measurements frequently reported in the literature, reduces the need for additional imaging. The use of multivariable analysis and ROC evaluation strengthens the clinical applicability of the findings. Performing measurements on routinely obtained initial CT scans enhances the cost-effectiveness of the method by enabling early risk stratification without additional cost.

This study also has several limitations. First, due to its retrospective design, selection bias and the influence of unmeasured confounding factors cannot be completely excluded. Although patients with a documented diagnosis of CTS prior to fracture were excluded, subclinical or undocumented pre-existing CTS symptoms could not be completely ruled out. The diagnosis of CTS, based on clinical findings and partially on electrophysiological evaluations, may have introduced diagnostic heterogeneity. The single-center design and the limited sample size, particularly in some subgroup analyses, may restrict the generalizability of the results. Measurements were performed on pre-reduction CT images, and fracture-related deformity and acute soft tissue changes may have influenced the results. In addition, due to the limited soft tissue contrast of CT, the median nerve could not be reliably isolated; therefore, the relative contributions of carpal tunnel area and median nerve area to the development of CTS could not be evaluated separately. For these reasons, the findings should be confirmed by larger, multicenter, and prospective studies.

Conclusion

The combined evaluation of carpal tunnel cross-sectional area and volar tilt in distal radius fractures may provide meaningful predictive value for the development of carpal tunnel syndrome. Our findings suggest that the risk of CTS should be assessed not only based on bone alignment but also in conjunction with carpal tunnel morphology. This approach may contribute to the early identification of high-risk patients and to the optimization of clinical management.
