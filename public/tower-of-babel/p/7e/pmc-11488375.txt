Letter to the Editor Response
Authors: Erik F Hauck, Joel C Morgenlander
Published: 2024

We thank Dr. Finsterer for his thoughtful questions and comments. The main observation in our case report was that our patient experienced occipital lobe seizures associated with early venous drainage observed by catheter angiography. Once the seizures were controlled and his symptoms resolved, his repeat catheter angiogram demonstrated normal blood flow without remaining evidence of early venous drainage; the presence of an arteriovenous malformation (AVM) or arteriovenous fistula (AVF) was ruled out.

Our case indicates that the angiographic finding of early venous drainage can be associated with seizures. Many neuroscientists and clinicians are less aware of this association, as early venous drainage (shunting) is more commonly associated with AVMs, AVFs, or ischemic stroke. We are not the first to report this phenomenon, as it is generally known that increased cerebral blood flow can be associated with seizures. We had even identified one other report in the literature in which the authors observed early venous drainage by catheter angiography during seizures similarly to our case.

Of note, as neurosurgeons, we still routinely perform awake craniotomies for certain special indications. If the patient experiences a clinical seizure during test stimulation, cerebral veins turn bright red due to increased blood flow and shunting. The phenomenon is reversed in real time once the seizure is controlled.

Regarding the seizures and treatment in our reported case, electroencephalography (EEG) showed that the seizure focus was occipital. As Dr. Finsterer correctly noted, there was also the spread of seizure activity more anteriorly, causing motor manifestations. EEG was performed soon after angiography. At the time of angiography, the patient did not have any motor symptoms, only hemianopsia.

Ultimately, we believe that the patient likely had seizures for quite some time, as the seizures were difficult to suppress initially. The seizures finally stopped when clobazam was added to a regimen of levetiracetam, lacosamide, and valproic acid. Likely, the seizures originated from an occipital leptomeningeal lesion of uncertain etiology. We are carefully following the patient, who has remained asymptomatic for more than a year so far after initiation of medical treatment without manifestation of another lesion.
