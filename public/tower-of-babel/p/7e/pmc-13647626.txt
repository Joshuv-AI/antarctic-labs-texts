Hand hygiene knowledge, glove use misconceptions, and determinants among healthcare workers in Addis Ababa hospitals: a mixed-method cross-sectional study
Authors: Abayneh Melaku, Tesfaye Admassu, Argaw Ambelu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHand hygiene is a cornerstone of infection prevention, yet compliance among healthcare workers remains low. Despite multiple national initiatives, adherence in Ethiopian hospitals continues to be sub-optimal. This challenge is partly due to knowledge gaps and misconceptions, particularly regarding the WHO “Five Moments” and the appropriate use of gloves. This study aimed to assess the knowledge and misconceptions of healthcare workers concerning hand hygiene practices and glove use in hospitals in Addis Ababa.MethodsA mixed-methods cross-sectional study was conducted from December 2024 to January 2025 among 493 healthcare workers from public and private hospitals. Data were collected using the WHO Hand Hygiene Knowledge Questionnaire and in-depth interviews. Knowledge was assessed with a 33-item tool, categorized as good (> 75%), moderate (50–74%), or poor (< 50%). Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, and Chi-squared Automatic Interaction Detection (CHAID) decision tree analysis, while qualitative data analyzed to contextualize and enrich the findings.ResultsWhile 86% of respondents were aware of the WHO ‘Five Moments,’ detailed understanding remained inconsistent, with a moderate mean knowledge score of 71%. Nearly half (48.2%) believed hand hygiene was unnecessary when gloves were worn. Institutional factors strongly influenced knowledge: visible instructions (χ² = 15.62, df = 1, p < 0.001), functional IPC committees (χ² = 34.55, df = 1, p < 0.001), hospital ownership (χ² = 5.15, df = 1, p = 0.023), and working unit type (χ² = 15.63, df = 1, p = 0.010) were significantly associated with better knowledge. Individual factors such as younger age and higher educational attainment also predicted better knowledge. Qualitative findings revealed limited protocol adherence, reliance on routine habits over guidelines, insufficient training, inadequate institutional support, and misconceptions about glove use, all of which undermined healthcare workers’ knowledge and practice.ConclusionModerate hand hygiene knowledge and significant misconceptions about hand hygiene and appropriate glove use persist among healthcare workers in Addis Ababa, undermining infection prevention efforts. Targeted and continuous education, visible instructions, strengthening Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) committees, and improved institutional support are essential to foster effective hand hygiene practices and reduce healthcare-associated infections.Keywords: Hand hygiene, Healthcare workers, Compliance, Glove use misconceptions, WHO five moments of hand hygiene, Healthcare-associated infections (HAIs)

Introduction

Hand hygiene is a critical, cost-effective intervention to prevent healthcare-associated infections (HAIs) and improve patient safety globally [1, 2]. Despite its critical role, compliance among healthcare workers and focus given remains low, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). In Ethiopia, studies have reported hand hygiene compliance rates ranging from 18.7% to 50.4% across various healthcare settings, including hospitals in Addis [1, 3–5]. Although a meta-analysis indicated that healthcare workers in Addis Ababa demonstrated relatively higher compliance (up to 73%) compared to other regions, significant gaps persist [1]. Qualitative evidence further reveals inconsistent protocol adherence and a reliance on routine habits rather than structured guidelines [6, 7]. Knowledge gaps and misconceptions, particularly about the WHO’s “Five Moments” of hand hygiene and the use of gloves, are the major contributors to poor compliance [3, 6]. For example, some healthcare workers mistakenly perceive glove use as a substitute for hand hygiene practices such as handwashing or alcohol-based hand rubs, thereby increasing the risk of cross-contamination [6].

Studies from other LMICs demonstrate varying levels of hand hygiene knowledge, highlighting the importance of contextual factors. For example, in Mogadishu, Somalia, 95.3% of healthcare workers were aware of hand hygiene, with 86.7% familiar with the WHO’s “Five Moments” [8]. Similarly, in southwestern Nigeria, 98.95% of respondents exhibited strong knowledge [9]. In contrast, knowledge levels were moderate in Dhaka City, Bangladesh (49.7%) [10], and Herat, Afghanistan (84.61%) [11]. Rwanda reported an average knowledge score of 77.5%, with 44% of participants having moderate knowledge [12]. While in the Gulu region, Uganda, 81.2% of respondents demonstrated good knowledge [13]. At Dubti Referral Hospital in Afar, Northeast Ethiopia, 65.9% of healthcare workers were knowledgeable, while 34.1% were not [14]. Notably, no comprehensive studies have been conducted in Addis Ababa on hand hygiene knowledge and glove use misconceptions of healthcare workers working in both public and private hospitals.

Multiple determinants influence hand hygiene behavior, including individual knowledge, availability of hygiene resources, institutional support, and behavioral factors [15, 16]. Inconsistent supply of essential hand hygiene materials, such as clean water and soap, alcohol-based hand rubs, continues to hinder compliance in Ethiopian healthcare settings [16–19]. Additionally, high workloads, time constraints, and a lack of supervision further exacerbate non-compliance [1, 4, 20]. Facilities with reliable access to these resources tend to demonstrate better adherence to hand hygiene protocols, underscoring the need to address infrastructural and logistical challenges [19, 21]. These challenges often lead to shortcuts, such as improper glove use or skipping hand hygiene altogether [15, 16]. Evidence from similar low and middle-income countries suggests that continuous training, supportive leadership, and a culture of accountability are essential for improving compliance [22]. Furthermore, the presence of visual reminders and feedback mechanisms has been associated with sustained behavior change and improved hand hygiene practices [23].

Despite widespread awareness, the persistence of suboptimal practices underscores the need to address knowledge quality and misconceptions. Many healthcare workers acknowledge the significance of hand hygiene, yet their understanding of the “Five Moments” is inconsistent, particularly regarding hand hygiene before patient contact. Misconceptions about glove use as a replacement rather than a complement to hand hygiene further undermine infection prevention efforts. These knowledge deficits and erroneous beliefs contribute to inconsistent practices and elevate the risk of HAIs.

Addressing these gaps is essential for strengthening infection prevention and control in Ethiopian healthcare settings. Accordingly, this study assessed healthcare workers’ knowledge and misconceptions regarding hand hygiene and glove use in public and private hospitals in Addis Ababa, to inform targeted interventions and policy improvements.

Methods and materials

Study area

Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia and headquarters of the African Union, is administratively divided into 11 sub-cities and 123 woredas (districts), which serve as the lower levels of administrative units. The city has a population exceeding five million residents [24]. According to the Ethiopian Ministry of Health, the city has 37 hospitals, comprising 13 government-run and 24 private facilities [25]. This study included 19 hospitals from both the public and private sectors. Study design and period

A mixed-method cross-sectional study was conducted in Addis Ababa city hospitals from December 2024 to January 2025. This design integrated both quantitative and qualitative approaches to comprehensively assess healthcare workers’ knowledge of hand hygiene, prevailing misconceptions surrounding hand hygiene practices, and glove use.

Sample size determination

The sample size was calculated using a population proportion formula [26]. Due to the absence of prior comprehensive local or national data on hand hygiene knowledge and misconceptions related to glove use in Addis Ababa public and private hospitals or comparable urban settings, a conservative prevalence estimate of 50% was applied for sample size calculation. The parameters used included: the estimated proportion (prevalence) of healthcare workers with good hand hygiene knowledge in Addis Ababa hospitals (P) = 50% (0.5), d = 0.05 (margin of error), and Z = 1.96 (standard score corresponding to a 95% confidence level). The sample size (N) was determined using the following formula: = N=384. After adding a 10% (38) non-response rate, the sample size was = 422. Ultimately, 493 healthcare workers were included in the study, exceeding the minimum requirement and thereby enhancing the robustness of the findings.

Sampling techniques

Hospitals were categorized as public and private. From the 508 healthcare workers initially invited, 15 dropped out: nine discontinued participation due to urgent job-related assignments from their supervisors, while six withdrew without specifying reasons. Ultimately, 493 healthcare workers participated, with 346 from public hospitals and 147 from private hospitals. The sampling assumption was based on the Ethiopian Ministry of Health report, which indicates that approximately 75% of healthcare workers are permanently employed in public hospitals while also working part-time in private facilities. Reflecting this distribution, we proportionally allocated the sample size to ensure representativeness (Fig. 2).

Sampling scheme for the assessment of hand hygiene knowledge and associated factors, January 2025

Data collection and achieved sample size

A total of 508 healthcare workers were invited, and 493 participated, yielding a 97% response rate. Of these, 346 were from public hospitals and 147 from private hospitals. In-depth interviews with healthcare providers were stopped after 15 participants were reached, as data saturation had been achieved.

Data collection methods and tools

Data were gathered through a semi-structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire in English, complemented by in-depth interview guides. The questionnaire assessing hand hygiene knowledge was adapted from the World Health Organization’s revised 2009 Hand Hygiene Knowledge Questionnaire, along with insights from relevant peer-reviewed studies [27, 28]. The questionnaire included items on participants’ socio-demographic characteristics and healthcare facility attributes, covering factors such as gender, age, profession, work experience, training, involvement in infection prevention and control (IPC) committees, and familiarity with relevant protocols and guidelines. Although some questionnaire items referred to practice-related behaviors, these were assessed as knowledge-based practices rather than observed compliance. The study therefore measured self-reported understanding and misconceptions about appropriate hand hygiene procedures, consistent with its focus on knowledge. Additionally, in-depth interviews were conducted with healthcare providers purposively selected across all experience levels (< 5 to > 15 years), professions, and hospitals (public/private), using a structured interview guide. The main content of the interview guide focused on knowledge of the WHO five moments, challenges to protocol adherence, and possible solutions.

Outcome and independent variables

The primary outcome variable in this study was healthcare workers’ knowledge of hand hygiene, classified as either “Good” or “Poor.” Independent variables encompassed a range of socio-demographic and institutional factors. These included hospital ownership; demographic characteristics such as sex, age, marital status, educational level, and religion; professional attributes including job category, years of work experience, and current working unit; and facility-specific elements such as receipt of hand hygiene training, presence of a functional Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) committee, visibility of hand hygiene protocols and instructional materials, availability of guidelines, and the presence of designated hand hygiene promoters.

Operationalizing knowledge on hand hygiene

The hand hygiene knowledge of healthcare workers was evaluated using 33 questionnaire items. Each item consisted of multiple-choice, “yes” or “no,” “true” or “false,” or other types of choice questions to assess hand hygiene knowledge. One point was awarded for each correct answer, while incorrect answers received zero points. Knowledge was operationalized into three categories (Good, Moderate, Poor) for descriptive purposes. Overall mean scores were expressed as percentages: scores above 75% were considered good, scores between 50% and 74% were considered moderate, and scores below 50% were considered poor, as in previous studies [27, 28]. For inferential analysis, the Moderate category was combined with Poor to create a binary outcome (Good vs. Poor).

Data processing and analysis

Quantitative data were collected using Kobo Toolbox and subsequently exported to SPSS version 25.0 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were employed to summarize the data, with results presented through tables, charts, and graphs. To identify predictors of hand hygiene knowledge among healthcare workers, Chi-squared Automatic Interaction Detection (CHAID) decision tree analysis was applied. CHAID was chosen because it is particularly suited for categorical data and highlights hierarchical and interactive effects among predictors. Unlike logistic regression, which assumes linear relationships and provides odds ratios, CHAID produces a decision tree that visually demonstrates how institutional and individual factors combine to influence hand hygiene knowledge. Qualitative data were analyzed thematically to contextualize and enrich the quantitative findings, thereby strengthening the overall validity and reliability of the study. Illustrative quotes from participants were incorporated to capture diverse perspectives and enhance narrative depth.

Data quality control

The authors provided two days of training to the data collectors before starting data collection. All data collectors hold a Bachelor of Science degree in Environmental Health and have extensive experience in both quantitative and qualitative data collection. The data collection tools were pre-tested in two hospitals that were not part of the study in Addis Ababa, and feedback was provided to address issues such as question coherence, clarity of the questionnaire, respondent reactions, and the inclusion of missing options.

Ethical considerations

The study received approval from the Addis Ababa University Ethical Review Committee (Ref: 12/EIWR/2024, November 2024) and provided an administrative support letter to facilitate cooperation with all concerned bodies. Then, before starting the data collection at the hospitals, permission was obtained from all hospitals. All participants were briefed about the research protocols and objectives before obtaining oral informed consent.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the respondent healthcare workers and facilities

A total of 493 healthcare workers participated in the study out of the 508 healthcare workers approached, yielding a 97% response rate. The majority of respondents (70.2%) were from public hospitals, while 29.8% were affiliated with private hospitals. More than half of the participants (58.8%) were female. In terms of educational attainment, most held a Bachelor’s degree (78.9%), followed by Master’s degree holders (14.8%) and those with a Diploma (5.9%). Professionally, nurses comprised the largest group (35.7%), followed by medical doctors (18.1%), midwives (13.2%), and medical laboratory professionals (10.3%) (Table 1).

Knowledge of healthcare workers on the WHO five moments of hand hygiene

The majority (86.6%) of healthcare workers correctly identified critical hand hygiene moments recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO). However, knowledge levels varied across the five WHO-defined hand hygiene moments. The lowest recognition was reported for “before patient contact” at 74%, while the highest was for “after body fluid exposure”, with 95% of respondents identifying it as a WHO five critical moments. Figure 3 illustrates the distribution of responses across each moment, highlighting the percentage who recognized each moment as essential for hand hygiene compliance.

Nearly half (48.2%) believed hand hygiene was unnecessary when gloves were worn. Alcohol-based hand sanitizers were recognized as an alternative to hand washing by 56.8% of the healthcare workers. Additionally, 84.6% of the healthcare workers knew the minimal time required for alcohol-based hand rub to effectively kill most germs on their hands. The overall average hand hygiene knowledge score among healthcare workers was 71%, indicating a moderate level of knowledge (Table 2).

Qualitative insights on the knowledge and awareness of hand hygiene

The qualitative data revealed considerable variation in healthcare workers’ understanding and awareness of hand hygiene protocols. While infection prevention and control (IPC) experts and physicians frequently referenced the WHO’s “Five Moments for Hand Hygiene” including before patient contact, after exposure to bodily fluids, after patient contact, before aseptic procedures, and after contact with the patient’s surroundings, many nurses, junior staff, and support personnel appeared to rely more on habitual practices than formal protocols and guidelines. For example, one nurse from a private hospital admitted, “I don’t know the WHO moments of hand hygiene” (IDI: Nurse, Private Hospital), while another commented, “We practice hand hygiene between daily tasks but aren’t familiar with the five WHO moments of hand washing in a healthcare facility” (IDI: Nurse, Private Hospital). Similarly, a medical laboratory staff member from a government hospital stated, “I’m not sure about protocols. I just wash when I feel it’s necessary and important after handling samples” (IDI: Medical Laboratory Staff, Government Hospital).

At the institutional level, the knowledge and awareness of the management in public hospitals placed a notably low priority on hand hygiene infrastructure and practice. One management member at the one hospital remarked, “We have many problems in our hospitals, and this issue is not critical for us” (IDI: Healthcare Worker, Government Hospital). In the private hospitals, the focus and management support are relatively better than in the public hospitals.

Protocol adherence was inconsistent across hospitals and departments. Although some departments had standard operating procedures (SOPs), staff outside IPC leadership roles often ignored or misunderstood them. Several participants reported combining glove use with hand hygiene methods such as hand washing and rubbing. However, some healthcare workers highly relied on, or incorrectly viewed, gloves as a substitute for hand hygiene methods. Among the misconceptions about gloves, one government hospital worker responded, “Gloves are very critical and may substitute other methods” (IDI: Healthcare Worker, Government Hospital). Conversely, a private hospital professional clarified, “Gloves complement but never replace hand hygiene. Hands must be sanitized before donning gloves and after removal” (IDI: Healthcare Professional, Private Hospital).

Training gaps were universally reported in both government and private hospitals. A public hospital worker described training as “almost nonexistent and irregular, reliant on orientations or partner-led programs like Ethiopian Environmental Health Association, Ethiopian Infection prevention and control Association or Ministry of Health of Ethiopia” (IDI: Healthcare Professional, Government Hospital). Private hospital staff echoed this concern, with one nurse noting “no in-depth training on hand hygiene practices” (IDI: Nurse, Private Hospital).

CHAID decision tree analysis of factors affecting the Knowledge of hand hygiene

The CHAID (Chi-squared Automatic Interaction Detection) model identified four predictors of hand hygiene knowledge among healthcare workers in Addis Ababa hospitals: visibility of clear and simple hand hygiene instructions, hospital ownership, presence of a functional Infection Prevention and Control (IPC) committee, and working unit (p < 0.05). Visibility and clear instruction posting emerged as the most influential determinants. The model comprised 9 nodes with 5 terminal nodes (nodes 1, 4, 6, 7, and 8), each representing subgroups with significantly different knowledge levels (all p < 0.05). The model showed good predictive accuracy (risk error = 0.176, SE = 0.017).

Decision tree analysis showed institutional factors strongly influenced knowledge: visible instructions (χ² = 15.62, df = 1, p < 0.001), hospital ownership (χ² = 5.15, df = 1, p = 0.023), functional IPC committees (χ² = 34.55, df = 1, p < 0.001), and working unit type (χ² = 15.63, df = 1, p = 0.010). Younger age and higher education also predicted better knowledge. Clear instruction posting was the strongest determinant, followed by ownership and IPC committee presence. The tree revealed important interaction effects: private hospitals with visible instructions had the highest proportion of staff with good knowledge, while in facilities lacking both instructions and IPC committees, misconceptions such as believing gloves replace hand hygiene were most prevalent. The decision tree, illustrated in Fig. 4, highlights the hierarchical and interactive influence of institutional context and workplace environment on hand hygiene knowledge among healthcare workers.

Discussions

This study presents a comprehensive assessment of hand hygiene knowledge, glove-use misconceptions, and institutional factors influencing compliance among healthcare workers (HCWs) in Addis Ababa hospitals. The findings reveal moderate hand hygiene knowledge (mean score 71%) among Addis Ababa HCWs, despite 86.6% reporting awareness of the WHO’s Five Moments for Hand Hygiene. The lowest recognition was before patient contact (74%), compared with the highest after body fluid exposure (95%) a pattern consistent with previous Ethiopian studies, but our multi-hospital design uniquely shows that this gap persists across both public and private settings.

The relatively lower awareness of hand hygiene before patient contact is a significant concern because this moment is crucial in preventing the transmission of pathogens to vulnerable patients [2]. Similar uneven knowledge patterns have been reported in other Ethiopian studies and neighboring regions [1, 5, 6, 29], reflecting ongoing gaps in comprehensive protocol understanding. For instance, a study in Eastern Ethiopia reported that only 66.3% of nurses performed hand hygiene before aseptic procedures compared to 93.6% exposure to body fluid [28, 30]. Additionally, Worku and Melaku [6] reported inconsistent recognition of certain hand hygiene moments among healthcare providers in Kirkos and Akaki Kality sub-cities. A comparable study in Tanzania also documented notably lower compliance before patient contact [31]. These findings collectively suggest that while general awareness is relatively high, knowledge quality and application remain insufficient, with implications for infection control effectiveness.

This matters because pre-contact hygiene prevents pathogen transmission to vulnerable patients, highlighting the need to move beyond general awareness to moment-specific training to improve compliance and reduce healthcare-associated infections (HAIs). When comparing Addis Ababa’s hand hygiene knowledge findings with those from other LMICs, the results revealed both strengths and areas for improvement. In Mogadishu, Somalia, 95.3% of HCWs were aware of hand hygiene, with 86.7% specifically familiar with the WHO’s “Five Moments,” a level of critical moment awareness that aligns with Addis Ababa, though overall knowledge was higher [8]. Southwestern Nigeria reported even stronger knowledge, with 98.95% of respondents demonstrating high awareness [9]. In contrast, Dhaka City, Bangladesh, showed moderate knowledge at 49.7%, which is lower than Addis Ababa’s score [10]. Herat, Afghanistan, reported 84.61%, and Rwanda achieved 77.5%, both slightly better than Addis Ababa [11, 12]. In Gulu region, Uganda, 81.2% of respondents exhibited good knowledge [13], while at Dubti Referral Hospital in Afar, Northeast Ethiopia, 65.9% of HCWs were knowledgeable, lower than Addis Ababa’s rate [14].

Encouragingly, 84.6% of respondents correctly identified 20 s as the minimum effective duration for alcohol-based hand rub (ABHR) use, aligning with WHO guidelines and indicating a solid understanding of the technique. However, misconceptions remain widespread. Nearly half (48.2%) believed hand hygiene is unnecessary when gloves are worn. Qualitative interviews complemented this misconception, with some HCWs perceiving gloves as substitutes rather than complements to hand hygiene, a critical error documented in previous Ethiopian and LMIC studies [6, 29]. Similar misconceptions have been reported in studies from Ethiopia [18] and other LMIC settings [16, 31–33] emphasized that improper glove use without appropriate hand hygiene increases the risk of cross-contamination. Improper glove use without appropriate hand hygiene increases the risk of cross-contamination, underscoring the need for targeted education to clarify the complementary roles of gloves and hand hygiene.

Moreover, only 56.8% of respondents recognized ABHR as an alternative to handwashing, despite the WHO’s endorsement of ABHR for routine hand hygiene due to its efficacy and convenience [2]. Barriers such as limited access and unfamiliarity with locally available hand rub products may hinder usage in Ethiopian healthcare settings, as observed in other resource-constrained environments [16, 19]. Addressing these infrastructural constraints alongside educational efforts is essential for improving adoption.

Systemic challenges further impede hand hygiene compliance. For instance, protocol adherence was inconsistent across hospitals; despite the presence of standard operating procedures (SOPs) in some departments, staff outside IPC leadership frequently ignored or misunderstood protocols, reflecting gaps in communication and enforcement. Training deficiencies were widely reported in both government and private hospitals, described as irregular, superficial, and overly reliant on external partner-led or webinar-based initiatives. These gaps undermine HCWs’ ability to internalize and apply evidence-based hand hygiene practices, a problem echoed in studies from Addis Ababa’s Eka Kotebe General Hospital [4] and reports from Sierra Leone and Tanzania [34, 35]. Continuous, comprehensive training embedded within institutional frameworks is imperative to foster sustainable behavior change.

The decision tree revealed that Institutional factors such as visible hand hygiene instructions were the strongest determinant of knowledge, followed by hospital ownership and the presence of functional IPC committees. Importantly, interaction effects were evident: private hospitals with visible instructions had the highest proportion of staff with good knowledge, while in facilities lacking both instructions and IPC committees, misconceptions such as believing gloves replace hand hygiene were most prevalent. The presence of clear, visible hand hygiene instructions and functional IPC committees emerged as determinants of good knowledge, consistent with global evidence that these elements enhance protocol adherence [22, 36]. Interestingly, hospital ownership influenced knowledge in CHAID analysis, suggesting that organizational culture and resource allocation may vary across institutions. Public hospitals, potentially constrained by bureaucracy and limited resources, may face challenges in IPC implementation similar to those reported in other LMIC health systems [33, 37]. The moderate knowledge scores, misconceptions, and training gaps observed in this study align with regional studies’ evidence documenting suboptimal hand hygiene knowledge and practice [1, 18, 28]. Overall, the CHAID analysis demonstrates that institutional structures, particularly visible instructions, hospital ownership, and functional IPC committees, interact with individual characteristics to shape hand hygiene knowledge. Addressing these gaps through strengthened institutional support and targeted refresher training is essential to correct misconceptions and improve compliance.

Conclusion

This study reveals that healthcare workers in Addis Ababa hospitals possess moderate hand hygiene knowledge (mean score 71%). While 86.6% recognized the WHO’s Five Moments for hand hygiene, detailed understanding varied across moments, with lowest recognition before patient contact. Nearly half (48.2%) held misconceptions that gloves replace hand hygiene. Institutional factors such as visible instructions, functional IPC committees, protocols, and hospital ownership significantly influenced knowledge levels.

To improve hand hygiene knowledge and correct misconceptions, comprehensive interventions targeting both institutional and individual factors are urgently needed. Priority should be given to continuous training, visible instructions, strengthened IPC committees, and adequate hygiene resources. Mandatory IPC refresher training for all healthcare workers is particularly recommended to correct persistent misconceptions, especially the belief that gloves substitute for hand hygiene. These measures could markedly reduce healthcare-associated infections in Ethiopian hospitals.
