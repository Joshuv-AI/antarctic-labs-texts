Hikikomori in the urban digital era: a psychodynamic, transdiagnostic model and multimodal interventions
Authors: Takahiro A Kato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurpose of reviewHikikomori (prolonged social withdrawal) was first described in Japan and was initially regarded as culture-bound. It is now recognized as a global mental health concern, more prevalent in urban settings and frequently comorbid with psychiatric disorders. In the post-COVID-19 era, home-centered lifestyles have become increasingly normative, prompting a reconceptualization of hikikomori beyond reduced outing frequency. INTRODUCTION

Hikikomori is typically defined as marked social withdrawal lasting 6 months or longer, characterized by staying at home and non-participation in work or education [1]. First highlighted in Japan in the late 1990 s, hikikomori was initially discussed as a culture-bound phenomenon closely tied to Japanese sociocultural contexts [2]. However, epidemiological and clinical studies have since documented hikikomori-like behaviors across diverse cultural and socioeconomic settings [3,4,5▪▪,6]. Reflecting its broader relevance, the DSM-5-TR included hikikomori in the “Cultural Concepts of Distress” section [7].

Community-based and clinical research suggests that hikikomori is more common in urban settings [8,9], and frequently co-occurs with a wide range of psychiatric conditions, including mood disorders, anxiety disorders, neurodevelopmental disorders (e.g., autism spectrum disorder and attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder), and behavioral addictions such as gaming disorder [1,10,11,12–15▪]. In parallel, rapid urbanization, digitalization, and the COVID-19 pandemic have fundamentally reshaped everyday life [16,17▪]. Remote work, online education, and digitally mediated relationships have blurred the boundary between adaptive home-centered lifestyles and clinically significant social withdrawal, rendering purely behavior-based definitions (e.g., outing frequency) increasingly insufficient [18▪▪]. From an anti-stigma perspective, we therefore distinguish “non-pathological hikikomori” from “pathological hikikomori,” rather than equating all homebound lifestyles with disorder [18▪▪].

Historically, systematic research and evidence-based support strategies for hikikomori were very limited. To address this gap, we conducted international vignette-based studies, which indicated that hikikomori-like conditions likely exist beyond Japan [8]. Early commentaries anticipated a future “hikikomori pandemic” [19], and more recent surveys suggest the presence of hikikomori-related phenomena in over 30 countries [5▪▪]. To generate evidence-based support strategies, we established a university hospital based clinical research clinic specializing in hikikomori in 2013 and launched a dedicated research laboratory [20], enabling standardized assessment, longitudinal observation, development of family support programs, and iterative refinement of clinical and digital interventions [1]. Building on these experiences, this opinion review proposes a psychodynamic (developmental and attachment-informed), transdiagnostic, and multidimensional model of hikikomori in contemporary urban digital societies and presents multi-axial assessment and multimodal intervention strategies.

A psychodynamic (developmental and attachment-informed), transdiagnostic, and multidimensional model of hikikomori

Accumulating evidence supports conceptualizing hikikomori not as a discrete diagnostic entity but within a biological, psychological, sociocultural, and developmental framework [1]. Such multidimensional and transdiagnostic approaches are increasingly emphasized even in long-established psychiatric disorders, where reliance on categorical diagnostic criteria alone has proven insufficient to capture heterogeneity and clinical trajectories [21–24].

Below, we outline interlocking components of this model: psychopathology-psychodynamic informed developmental pathways [including adversity and modern-type depression (MTD) as a gateway phenotype], attachment theory as a bridge between psychopathology and hikikomori, and emerging biological underpinnings converging on transdiagnostic vulnerability processes (Fig. 1).

In Japan, hikikomori has long been discussed by psychopathologists and psychoanalysts in relation to early caregiving environments (parental over-protective behaviors such as “Amae” and “Kahogo”) and psychosocial adversities in school contexts such as bullying (“Ijime”) and academic difficulties [1]. Strong evidence suggest that childhood maltreatment and neglect are established risk factors for depressive disorders and anxiety disorders [25,26], which frequently co-occur with hikikomori. Future evidence would suggest that adversity in family and school environments shape developmental pathways toward pathological hikikomori in more details.

We have proposed that a depressive phenotype often referred to as “modern-type” (or “new-type”) depression (i.e., MTD) may act as a gateway state to pathological hikikomori [27]. MTD is originally proposed in Japan as a depression-based syndrome with the predominant symptoms being depression and withdrawal that has rapidly increased among youth since around 2000 [27,28]. In contrast to classical melancholic depression, MTD often observed in younger individuals, and shows context/situation-dependent symptoms that worsen under social demands while remaining relatively stable in low-demand environments [27,29,30]. In routine clinical practice, such individuals may be labeled as “adjustment disorder,” potentially delaying recognition of emerging risk of hikikomori [27]. Recent path-analytic findings provide quantitative support for this gateway model, showing that traits of MTD mediate the association between childhood maltreatment and pathological hikikomori [31].(2) Attachment theory as a bridge between psychopathology and hikikomori

Recent intergenerational research has clarified the central role of attachment in hikikomori. A large-scale online intergenerational survey has indicated that attachment-related avoidance fully mediated the association between parental bonding and the severity of pathological hikikomori, providing empirical support for continuity between psychoanalytically informed developmental theory and contemporary empirical psychiatry [32▪▪]. This study has suggested that pathological hikikomori is associated with affectionless control parenting with low care and high overprotection, and fearful–avoidant attachment patterns. By contrast, non-pathological hikikomori was linked more closely to neglectful parenting and dismissive attachment, suggesting the possibility of partially adaptive withdrawal trajectories.

Across cases in our clinical practices, recurring psychological processes include avoidant coping, reduced self-efficacy, negative self-schemas, shame and fear of social evaluation, and difficulties in emotion regulation [1,33]. For some individuals, social withdrawal is experienced as relief from interpersonal threat; for others, it becomes ego-dystonic and distressing yet difficult to reverse due to fear of failure, stigma, or demoralization [33]. In this context, modern-type depressive tendencies may further strengthen avoidance and retreat, and these processes intersect with developmental stressors such as bullying and stressful transitions [27]. In this context, attachment insecurity – defined as enduring patterns of anxious or avoidant expectations regarding the availability and responsiveness of others – may reduce help-seeking behaviors and increase reliance on solitary avoidance coping strategies, which result in sustained withdrawal.

At the Japan's social and cultural level, performance-oriented education/employment systems, precarious work, and stigma may raise the perceived costs of re-engagement [27]. Contemporary urban environments provide anonymity and convenience that can reduce external pressure to participate while potentially intensifying social comparison and evaluative stress. Urban digital infrastructures offer readily available alternatives to face-to-face interaction, which may provide short-term coping but can also promote longer-term avoidance in vulnerable individuals. Together, these influences may facilitate transitions from transient withdrawal to persistent hikikomori.(3) Possible biological underpinnings

Emerging biological research provides preliminary support for a multidimensional, transdiagnostic vulnerability model of hikikomori. Although direct biological studies of hikikomori remain very limited, the condition shares core features – stress sensitivity, avoidance, anhedonia, circadian disruption, reduced physical activity, and altered social reward – that map onto well-characterized biological pathways in psychiatry and affective neuroscience.

Dysregulation of stress-response systems

Prolonged social withdrawal is increasingly discussed in relation to dysregulation of stress-response systems, particularly the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis [34]. Chronic exposure to psychosocial stressors during sensitive developmental periods may contribute to altered cortisol dynamics, heightened threat sensitivity, and behavioral avoidance [35]. From an allostatic load perspective, repeated activation of stress mediators may lead to enduring changes that bias attention toward threat and reduce motivation for social approach, thereby increasing vulnerability to persistent withdrawal [36].

Oxidative stress/inflammation and reward/motivation processes

Recent evidence suggests that oxidative stress may be involved in the pathophysiology of depressive disorders and anxiety disorders [37,38]. Notably, in individuals with hikikomori, lower serum uric acid levels have been reported irrespective of conventional diagnostic categories [14,39,40]. Because uric acid serves as an endogenous antioxidant, these findings raise the possibility that elevated oxidative stress is associated with hikikomori. In our preliminary data, C-reactive protein levels were higher in patients with major depressive disorder, particularly among those with concomitant hikikomori [41]. Converging evidence further indicates that low-grade systemic inflammation contributes to depressive and anxiety-related phenotypes that may be relevant to hikikomori, including reduced motivation, anhedonia, fatigue, and social withdrawal [42]. Childhood maltreatment and neglect are robustly associated with persistent inflammatory alterations, such as elevated C-reactive protein and pro-inflammatory cytokines [43]. Importantly, inflammatory signaling can alter reward processing and effort-based decision-making through effects on dopaminergic circuitry, providing a plausible pathway by which early adversity increases vulnerability to sustained withdrawal and blunted reward responsiveness [44]. As a working hypothesis, neuroimmune mechanisms involving microglial activation may also contribute to these processes, consistent with broader models of inflammation-related psychopathology [45].

Neurodevelopment and “social brain” circuitry

Functional and structural alterations in fronto-limbic circuits – implicated in emotion regulation, threat appraisal, and social cognition – have been reported across conditions commonly comorbid with hikikomori, including major depressive disorder, anxiety disorders, autism spectrum disorder, and trauma-related disorders [46–49]. Although direct neuroimaging studies specifically targeting hikikomori remain scarce, this overlap is consistent with a transdiagnostic vulnerability model rather than a disorder-specific explanation [21–24].

Epigenetic pathways linking early adversity to later vulnerability

Adverse early caregiving environments may exert long-lasting biological effects through epigenetic mechanisms. Stress-related epigenetic modifications affecting genes involved in stress responsivity and neuroplasticity have been proposed as pathways linking childhood adversity to later psychopathology [50–52]. Such mechanisms may provide a biologically plausible bridge between early relational experiences, attachment insecurity, and the emergence of prolonged social withdrawal-hikikomori.

It should be emphasized that biological evidence specific to hikikomori remains limited and largely indirect. Most available findings are cross-sectional, based on small samples, or extrapolated from comorbid psychiatric conditions rather than hikikomori-specific cohorts. Therefore, the proposed biological framework should be regarded as hypothesis-generating. Priority areas for future biological research include longitudinal biomarker studies, multimodal neuroimaging in well-characterized hikikomori populations, and integrative designs that combine developmental, psychosocial, and biological data to clarify causal pathways and temporal dynamics.

Taken together, available evidence supports conceptualizing hikikomori as a multidimensional phenomenon that can function as a downstream consequence of psychiatric symptoms, a maintaining factor that entrenches psychopathology via isolation and reduced reinforcement, and/or a prodromal or risk state in vulnerable individuals. Based on this layered understanding, we outline practical multimodal support strategies in the following section.

Multimodal interventions: implementing a multidimensional approach for early identification and early support

Step 0: How to assess hikikomori in the post-COVID-19 era: pathological versus non-pathological hikikomori

Assessment is the essential step. Prolonged withdrawal is clinically pathological when accompanied by clinically significant psychological distress and/or functional impairment [18▪▪]. This distinction is particularly important after COVID-19, when reduced outings and home-centered routines have become common and may be adaptive for some individuals. Over-pathologizing risks stigma and iatrogenic harm; under-recognition risks delayed intervention and chronicity.

To reduce diagnostic ambiguity of hikikomori, we developed the Hikikomori Diagnostic Evaluation (HiDE) and its screening version (HiDE-S) [18▪▪,53], which have undergone validation [54▪]. These structured tools assess social withdrawal together with distress and impairment, support stratification into pathological and non-pathological forms, and provide a shared language for coordination across medical, welfare, and community services [55].

In an online survey using HiDE-S, we observed that within the first several months after the onset of pathological hikikomori, depressive symptoms and gaming disorder tendency may emerge or intensify [11▪]. These findings highlight a preventive opportunity: early support should mitigate the risk of problematic gaming while helping individuals regain constructive roles and routines.

Step 1: Family consultation and psychoeducation (indirect intervention)

Primary care providers and community-based workers may play a crucial role in early detection, initial assessment, and timely referral, particularly in urban settings where individuals with emerging hikikomori may first present outside specialist mental health services. Individuals with hikikomori often do not seek help voluntarily, thus family members commonly serve as the first point of contact [1]. Stepped models beginning with family education and consultation followed by gradual engagement of the affected individual are recommended [56]. Our structured family-focused programs (e.g., 3-day or 5-day formats) have demonstrated feasibility and potential benefits in reducing caregiver burden and increasing readiness for engagement [57–59]. Core elements include reducing stigma against mental health and understanding hikikomori as biopsychosocial, reducing high expressed-emotion interactions, setting achievable goals, improving crisis management, and establishing consistent routines. During programs, digital and virtual reality based resources may further support caregiver training and role-play practice [60▪].

Step 2: Engagement and comprehensive evaluation of the affected individual (direct intervention)

When Step 1 succeeds, the affected individual may present to clinical services; in some contexts, outreach or home-visit approaches have been described internationally [61], although high-quality evidence remains limited. When individuals themselves present for care, comprehensive assessment and case formulation are essential. Beyond DSM/ICD-based psychiatric evaluation, detailed assessment of hikikomori itself and biopsychological functioning should be performed.

A transdiagnostic formulation benefits from systematic assessment: diagnosis and severity of hikikomori (e.g., HiDE; HQ-25; HQ-25 M) [18▪▪,54▪,62,63]; symptoms and diagnoses of psychiatric disorders (mood/anxiety, trauma-related symptoms, neurodevelopmental traits, behavioral addictions); risk and safety (suicidality, self-neglect, family violence); functioning (clinically significant psychological distress and/or functional impairment); developmental history (attachment, maltreatment, traumatic episodes); and lifestyle/biological factors (sleep–wake rhythm, physical activity, circadian disruption, inflammation, oxidative stress).

In our specialized outpatient clinic, we routinely combine hikikomori assessments with standardized measures and diagnostic interviews, alongside cognitive and personality assessments. In a pilot case–control study using the Rorschach Comprehensive System, individuals with hikikomori showed fewer color–form responses, suggesting restrained emotional modulation and constrained affective expression during interpersonal communication [64].

Step 3: Individualized interventions targeting comorbidities and maintaining mechanisms

When comorbid psychiatric disorders are present, pharmacotherapy may be indicated, but medication alone rarely improves hikikomori-related impairment when avoidance behavior and family dynamics maintain withdrawal. Psychosocial interventions are often indispensable, and psychotherapies are recommended based on individualized formulation. Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a reasonable first-line option for avoidance behavior, social anxiety, and negative cognitions. When avoidance is driven by PTSD-like symptoms, trauma-focused approaches are indicated. When neurodevelopmental traits are prominent, skills-based interventions such as social skills training (SST) may be helpful.

In cases with limited response to the above brief approaches, or in individuals with substantial attachment and personality-related difficulties, mentalization-based therapy, longer-term psychodynamic psychotherapy or psychodynamic group therapy may be warranted [1,32▪▪,33,65]. Given that attachment difficulties are not uncommon in hikikomori, we further note the potential relevance of psychodynamic concepts fostering the capacity to be alone, which was originally developed by Winnicott, D. W. [66–68].

Step 4: Digital and virtual reality based gateways in urban digital societies

Urban environments provide anonymity, convenience, and abundant digital alternatives to face-to-face interaction. These features may reduce immediate social demands and be protective for some individuals, but they may also facilitate persistent withdrawal when avoidance becomes self-reinforcing. Digital activities – particularly online gaming – can function both as coping and as a risk factor [11▪]. Role-playing games may temporarily substitute for lost social roles after school refusal or job loss by providing structured goals and belonging [11▪]. However, excessive reliance may reduce opportunities for corrective interpersonal experiences and contribute to functional impairment or gaming disorder tendency in vulnerable individuals. Clinicians should therefore assess not only the amount of digital use but also its functions (coping vs avoidance), associated impairment, and potential as an engagement channel.

Non-human interventions and digital tools such as avatar and metaverse can expand access and reduce barriers to engagement, including online consultation and asynchronous messaging. Communication robots may help to support individuals of hikikomori who have a strong hesitation to visit and see mental health professionals [69,70]. Virtual reality based interventions for family and individuals themselves have been proposed as experiential training tools for caregivers and as potential bridges toward real-world contact [60▪]. More broadly, immersive environments may have cognitive and affective regulatory effects relevant to exposure-based interventions, although careful evaluation and ethical considerations are essential.

At the same time, potential risks associated with digital and virtual interventions should be carefully considered. These include the risk of reinforcing digital dependency, privacy and data security concerns, and the possibility that excessive reliance on virtual environments may inadvertently maintain avoidance of real-world interpersonal engagement. Digital and virtual reality based approaches should therefore be positioned as transitional gateways rather than endpoints of care, with explicit goals of supporting gradual real-world reintegration and ongoing clinical monitoring [71].

Step 5: Social reintegration and sustained recovery

Sustained recovery often requires coordinated social interventions such as supported education/employment, graded community participation, peer support, and relapse prevention planning. In urban settings, collaboration with welfare agencies, schools, and vocational services can enable scaffolded participation that reduces all-or-nothing pressure and supports durable re-engagement.

Although much of the existing evidence derives from Japan, hikikomori emerges within diverse cultural contexts. Variations in familial structures, educational systems, labor markets, and welfare policies may shape both the meaning and trajectory of hikikomori and its interventions. For example, extended family support, educational flexibility, or community-based safety nets may buffer against chronic withdrawal conditions in some cultures, while rigid performance-based systems may exacerbate it in others. The proposed model is intended as a flexible framework that can be enriched by culturally informed research across regions.

Future perspectives: psychiatry informed by Future Design

The concept of “Future Design,” originating in economics, is increasingly applied to support sustainable decision-making across domains [72,73]. We have begun exploring application of Future Design to psychiatry, anticipating that future societies may require revisiting diagnostic norms for pathological hikikomori [74]. Under certain ecological or social constraints, long-term home-centered living may become adaptive, highlighting the historical contingency of “normal” social participation. This perspective does not deny the distress and impairment seen in pathological hikikomori; rather, it reinforces a framework prioritizing distress, impairment, autonomy, and personally meaningful goals over behavior-only criteria.

CONCLUSION

Hikikomori in the urban digital era is heterogeneous and evolving. In the post-COVID-19 context, reduced outings alone are insufficient to define pathology. A psychodynamic, transdiagnostic, and multidimensional understanding integrating biological susceptibility, psychopathology, developmental experiences, and attachment with structured assessment, family-based approaches, clinical care, and digital gateways provides a pragmatic framework for early identification and effective intervention to prevent long-term pathological hikikomori.
