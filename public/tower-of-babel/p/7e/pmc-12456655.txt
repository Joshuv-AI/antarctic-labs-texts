Anesthetic implications of elective cesarean section in a parturient with Noonan syndrome and complex cardiomyopathy: A case report
Authors: Victor Toledo Guillarducci, Katarina Lanza Japolino, Emerson Seiberlich, Marina Ayres Delgado
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Noonan syndrome (NS) is an autosomal dominant multisystem disorder characterized by congenital cardiac defects, craniofacial anomalies, and hematological abnormalities. With an incidence of 1 in 1000 to 2500 live births, NS is one of the most common syndromes associated with congenital heart disease, second only to trisomy 21. Pregnant patients with NS present unique anesthetic challenges due to the syndrome’s diverse manifestations and the physiological changes of pregnancy. This report describes a 28-year-old woman with NS who underwent elective cesarean delivery at 36 weeks of gestation. Her medical history included repaired congenital heart defects, pulmonary stenosis, severe scoliosis, and asthma. Spinal anesthesia was successfully employed using a low-dose combination of local anesthetics and opioids to achieve effective sensory block while maintaining hemodynamic stability. The patient’s normal coagulation profile allowed neuraxial anesthesia to be a feasible option despite potential technical difficulties associated with spinal abnormalities. This case underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to pregnant patients with NS, including comprehensive preoperative evaluation and individualized anesthetic planning. Neuraxial anesthesia can be a safe and effective option when tailored to the patient’s specific physiology, ensuring optimal maternal and fetal outcomes while minimizing risks.

Introduction

Noonan syndrome (NS) is an autosomal dominant, multisystem disorder characterized by hypertelorism with a Turner phenotype and complex cardiac malformations.[1] The estimated incidence ranges from 1 in 1000 to 2500 live births. NS is one of the most prevalent syndromes associated with congenital heart disease, only exceeded in prevalence by trisomy 21.[2] This syndrome is a result of abnormalities in the RAS-MAPK signaling pathway, which regulates cellular proliferation, differentiation, survival, and metabolism via extracellular ligands.[3]

The NS clinical spectrum varies widely. In the cardiovascular system, the most observed anomalies include pulmonary stenosis (50–60%), hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (20%), and secundum atrial septal defect (6–10%). Ventricular septal defects, peripheral pulmonary stenosis, atrioventricular canal, aortic stenosis, mitral valve abnormalities, aortic coarctation, and coronary artery anomalies are also reported. Additional features include short stature, abnormal pigmentation, lymphoedema, and cerebrovascular malformations such as arteriovenous malformations and aneurysms. Spinal deformities, which occur in approximately 30% of patients, often require surgical intervention. NS patients are also predisposed to several hematological abnormalities, including coagulopathies and myeloproliferative disorder.[3]

Although puberty is often delayed in individuals with NS, fertility remains unaffected. The physiological changes of pregnancy present additional challenges for anesthetic management, including increased blood volume, cardiac output, and heart rate, coupled with decreased systemic vascular resistance. In cases of pulmonary stenosis, elevated preload intensifies the pressure gradient across the pulmonary valve, exacerbating symptoms such as dyspnea and fatigue. During labor and the immediate postpartum period, significant hemodynamic fluctuations, including sudden blood redistribution following uterine contractions, further increase cardiac workload in patients with severe pulmonary stenosis. Given these heightened cardiovascular risks, a comprehensive multidisciplinary evaluation is essential to optimize the management of pregnant women with NS.[45]

The objective of this case report is to highlight the specific challenges of anesthetic management in pregnant women with NS. This report emphasizes the critical importance of thorough preoperative evaluation to ensure that the anesthetic plan is adapted to the comorbidities present in pregnant patients with NS, given the wide spectrum of abnormalities associated with this syndrome. It also highlights that spinal anesthesia should be considered a viable anesthetic technique for these patients, provided their coagulation profile is normal, and their cardiovascular status is carefully assessed to ensure safety. The need for individualized management, based on comprehensive and collaborative assessment, remains essential to optimize maternal and fetal outcomes while minimizing risks.

Case Report

A 28-year-old, 45 kg, 140 cm, female with NS and complex congenital heart disease was scheduled for an elective cesarean section at 36 weeks of gestation. At birth, she was diagnosed with situs inversus, levocardia, a ventricular septal defect (VSD), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), and right pulmonary hypoplasia, which were surgically corrected at the age of 5. Recent echocardiogram revealed moderate tricuspid regurgitation, mild pulmonary stenosis, dilation of the pulmonary artery, and stenosis of its right branch, with a right ventricular systolic pressure of 33 mmHg. Biventricular function was preserved, with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 67%. The patient also had a history of asthma with exacerbations during pregnancy and exhibited physical features typical of NS, including short stature, triangular face, hypertelorism, severe scoliosis, webbed neck, and shield chest. Airway examination showed Mallampati Class 2, reduced mouth opening, and a high-arched palate, with normal cervical mobility. Significant deviation of the trachea was observed on chest radiography.

Elective cesarean was scheduled at 36 weeks of pregnancy. In the operating room, after noninvasive monitoring, an 18G peripheral venous catheter and an arterial line were placed in the left upper limb. Spinal anesthesia was administered at the L4-L5 interspace using a 27G Whitacre needle, with an injection of 7.5 mg hyperbaric bupivacaine, 15 µg fentanyl, and 60 µg morphine. A T6 sensory block was successfully established. Hemodynamic and respiratory stability was maintained throughout the procedure, with no need for ventilatory support or the use of vasopressors. The patient was referred to the intensive care unit (ICU) stable, eupneic, conscious, without complaints.

Discussion

Cardio-obstetric patients must receive a personalized evaluation and have a care plan designed by a specialized multidisciplinary team experienced in managing pregnancy-related heart conditions. Although the patient had undergone cardiac surgery correction of the VSD and PDA, she was still considered to be at high risk for complications due to the Turner phenotype.[6]

Normally, vaginal delivery is preferred even in high-risk patients due to decreased obstetric complications risk and more gradual hemodynamic changes at the time of delivery. Current evidence suggests that vaginal labor with neuraxial labor analgesia is safe and may prevent the increased preload experienced in the labor process by catecholamines, released due to pain and anxiety.[6]

Cesarean delivery is indicated for obstetric factors, patients with cardiovascular complications, maternal instability, or fetal malpresentation. Moreover, the decompensated asthma and possible anatomical difficulties secondary to the short stature contributed to the decision on cesarean delivery. A combined spinal-epidural (CSE) is an effective way to reduce drug dosage required for anesthesia and combines the flexibility of continuous epidural block with the speed, density, and reliability of subarachnoid block. However, CSE approach can be complex due to the spinal abnormalities.[7] According to Dadabhoy et al., there is a reported increased difficulty in identifying and accessing the epidural space in patients with NS, along with a higher incidence of inadvertent dural perforation in this population.[7]

Another concern when considering neuraxial blocks in NS patients is the potential presence of coagulopathies. Up to 89% of NS patients present bleeding disorders. Platelet dysfunction represents the most common hemostatic abnormality, occurring in 66% of patients. Coagulation factor deficiencies are found in about 16% of cases, particularly deficiencies of factors VIII, XI, and XII. Von Willebrand disease is observed in approximately 24% of patients, with some presenting mild-to-moderate forms. While 55% to 89% of NS patients may have laboratory abnormalities in coagulation tests, only 3% to 5% experience severe bleeding complications. In our patient, all coagulation tests were normal, which allowed neuraxial blocks to be considered the anesthetic technique of choice for this case.[8]

There is no consensus on the optimal anesthetic management for cesarean delivery in these patients. Despite the concerns of unpredictable spinal anatomy and unreliable local anesthetic spread during neuroaxial anesthesia, general anesthesia in this patient can also be challenging due to the risk of difficult airway and previous pulmonary disease.[3]

An arterial line is often recommended to identify and manage rapidly changing hemodynamic status attributable to volume shifts, especially during induction of neuraxial anesthesia, autotransfusion that occurs after delivery, and the possibility of hemodynamic instability. Fluid administration must be cautious, particularly in patients at risk of pulmonary edema.[4]

Achieving uterine tone after removal of the fetus is an extremely important event in preventing postpartum hemorrhage. In patients with cardiovascular disease undergoing cesarean delivery, an infusion pump with continuous oxytocin may be beneficial as bolus dosing can rapidly decrease systemic vascular resistance. In this case, uterus tonus was achieved with a total dose of 2.5 mg of oxytocin.[4]

Conclusion

The expertise of anesthesiologists during the peripartum period is crucial for reducing maternal morbidity. Key aspects include the application of neuraxial anesthesia, careful management with inotropes and vasopressors, ensuring an optimal delivery environment, and providing advanced critical care or mechanical support when required. Pregnant patients with NS could present a wide range of anomalies, needing individualized and early evaluation. Neuraxial blocks can be a viable option for these patients, despite potential technical challenges.
