Comparative efficacy of eyelid cleansing wipes, hypochlorous acid, and saline for blepharitis-associated ocular surface disease: a randomized trial
Authors: Ruijia Shi, Yihan Guo, Ao Li, Lei Tian, Ying Jie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Blepharitis and meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) commonly cause ocular surface symptoms, but comparative evidence for commonly used eyelid-cleansing strategies remains limited. We compared the 4-week efficacy of preformulated eyelid cleansing wipes, hypochlorous acid-assisted cleansing, and saline-assisted cleansing in adults with blepharitis-associated ocular surface disease. In this single-center, randomized, assessor-masked, parallel-group trial, adults aged 18–70 years were assigned to one of three cleansing interventions for 4 weeks. The primary endpoint was the baseline-adjusted Week 4 visual analog scale (VAS) score for the selected study eye in the full analysis set. Pairwise comparisons were performed only after a significant overall test, with Holm adjustment. Seventy-two participants were randomized; 64 were included in the full analysis set, and 59 had observed Week 4 VAS data. The overall between-group difference in baseline-adjusted Week 4 VAS was significant (P = 0.007). Adjusted mean Week 4 VAS values were 27.47 (95% CI, 22.18–32.76) in the wipe group, 33.16 (95% CI, 27.80–38.51) in the hypochlorous acid group, and 40.83 (95% CI, 34.93–46.72) in the saline group. The wipe group had lower Week 4 VAS than the saline group (adjusted difference, − 13.35; 95% CI, − 21.51 to − 5.20; Holm-adjusted P = 0.005). No other pairwise comparison remained significant. Between-group differences in Schirmer test result, corneal fluorescein staining, and MGD expressibility were interpreted as supportive secondary findings. In this single-center, assessor-masked randomized trial, preformulated eyelid cleansing wipes were associated with a lower baseline-adjusted Week 4 VAS symptom score than saline-assisted mechanical lid cleansing. Confirmatory superiority over hypochlorous acid was not established, and sign-based secondary findings should be interpreted as supportive.

Introduction

Blepharitis and meibomian gland dysfunction (MGD) are common chronic disorders of the eyelid margin that frequently coexist with evaporative dry eye and ocular surface discomfort1–3. MGD is a major contributor to tear film instability and evaporative dry eye, and these disorders are commonly accompanied by ocular surface inflammation3–5. Current management frameworks therefore position lid-directed therapy and eyelid hygiene as core components of conservative treatment, particularly when MGD contributes to ocular surface disease6–10. In routine practice, however, the key question is not simply whether eyelid hygiene should be recommended, but which cleansing approach provides the most meaningful short-term clinical benefit.

Common eyelid-cleansing strategies include preformulated disposable wipes, hypochlorous acid-based regimens, and saline- or shampoo-assisted mechanical cleansing9–12. These approaches differ in formulation, degree of standardization, ease of use, and proposed antimicrobial or anti-inflammatory actions12,13. However, evidence syntheses have emphasized that direct comparative data remain limited and methodologically heterogeneous, with important variation in comparators, cointerventions, treatment schedules, and endpoint definitions14–16. As a result, it remains uncertain whether active formulations provide incremental benefit beyond mechanical lid cleansing alone.

Interpretation is further complicated by the well-recognized discordance between symptoms and signs in dry eye disease and related ocular surface disorders5,17,18. Early treatment effects may therefore be detected more readily in patient-reported discomfort than in lid margin or ocular surface signs. Against this background, we conducted a single-center, randomized, assessor-masked, three-arm parallel-group clinical trial to compare cleansing wipes, hypochlorous acid-assisted cotton-pad cleansing, and saline-assisted cotton-pad cleansing over 4 weeks in adults with blepharitis/MGD-associated ocular surface disease. The primary objective was to determine whether a standardized wipe-based regimen confers additional short-term symptomatic benefit beyond mechanical lid cleansing alone while also evaluating sign-based ocular surface and MGD outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This single-center, randomized, assessor-masked, parallel-group clinical trial was conducted at Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, between June 2025 and July 2025. Participants were allocated in a 1:1:1 ratio to one of three eyelid-cleansing interventions and were treated for 4 weeks. This report presents the prespecified efficacy analyses through Week 4.

Ethics approval, informed consent, and trial registration

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University (approval no. TREC2025-KY048). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. The trial was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500101040; date of first registration: 18/04/2025).

Randomization and masking

Randomization was based on a computer-generated sequence prepared by a study team member who was not involved in enrollment or outcome assessment. Allocation was implemented with sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes that were opened only after enrollment. Because the interventions differed in physical form and preparation procedures, masking of participants and treatment providers was not feasible. Outcome assessors remained masked throughout follow-up, and statistical analyses were performed using masked group codes until the primary analysis had been finalized.

Participants

Eligible participants were adults 18 to 70 years of age with dry eye symptoms and lid margin/MGD-related findings consistent with blepharitis-associated ocular surface disease. Participants were diagnosed and enrolled according to the American Academy of Ophthalmology Preferred Practice Pattern framework for blepharitis/MGD-associated ocular surface disease. Operational eligibility required dry eye or ocular discomfort symptoms together with objective lid margin and/or MGD-related clinical findings assessed using standardized grading procedures. OSDI and VAS were assessed as outcome measures and were not used as numeric eligibility thresholds. Participants either were not receiving other active treatment for blepharitis or had discontinued such treatment for more than 2 weeks before enrollment. Participation in another drug trial within the preceding 2 weeks was not permitted.

Key exclusion criteria included ocular or periocular conditions that could interfere with efficacy assessment; recent use of medications or procedures that might affect blepharitis- or ocular surface-related outcomes; active ocular allergy or infection; significant corneal epithelial disease; a history of herpetic keratitis; ocular or periocular malignancy; pregnancy or lactation; uncontrolled systemic disease; known hypersensitivity to the study products or saline components; recent participation in another clinical trial; and use of oral medications associated with ocular dryness.

Interventions

The eyelid-cleansing materials and procedures were standardized across the three intervention groups. Participants in the wipe group used a single individually packaged pre-moistened Aishiyoujia/EYESU+ eyelid-cleansing wipe for each cleansing session. Each wipe measured 100 mm × 140 mm and was made of spunlace nonwoven fabric. The wipe contained EDI purified water, Aloe barbadensis leaf juice, Portulaca oleracea extract, glycerol/glycerine, propylene glycol, panthenol, polysorbate 20, sodium lauroyl sarcosinate, butylene glycol, and p-hydroxyacetophenone. The product was commissioned by AiShiKang (Beijing) Health Technology Co., Ltd.

In the hypochlorous acid group, 0.01% hypochlorous acid solution (Mingxitong/Miesint; Beijing Zhongcan Xingcheng Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was supplied in a spray bottle and applied to a dry nonwoven cleansing pad before eyelid cleansing. In the saline group, sterile 0.9% normal saline was supplied in a spray bottle and applied to the same type of cleansing pad. The cleansing pads used in the hypochlorous acid and saline groups measured 100 mm × 140 mm and were matched to the wipe group in size and substrate material. For each application, participants were instructed to deliver four complete spray actuations onto the cleansing pad before wiping. The number of spray actuations, pad dimensions, pad material, and wiping procedure were standardized to promote comparable moistening across the two spray-applied groups. Participants were instructed to use the pad only after it had been uniformly moistened and not to soak, squeeze, or add additional solution to the pad.

In all groups, participants were instructed to close their eyes gently and wipe horizontally with moderate pressure for 30 s twice daily, focusing on the eyelash roots, lid margin, and meibomian gland orifice region. The assigned eyelid-cleansing intervention was continued for 28 days. Warm compresses and other heat-based eyelid therapies were prohibited during the study period.

Protocol-prohibited concomitant treatments included other active therapies for blepharitis/MGD, restricted antibacterial, antiparasitic, or anti-inflammatory therapies within the protocol-defined washout period, local tea tree oil treatment, hypochlorous acid treatment outside the assigned intervention, other eyelid-cleansing products, intense pulsed light-related therapy, and other ocular or periocular procedures, ocular patches, eye masks, or topical products that could affect blepharitis/MGD-related or ocular-surface outcomes.

Adherence assessment

All participants were instructed to perform eyelid cleansing twice daily for 28 days, yielding 56 expected applications during the treatment period. Treatment diaries were dispensed to participants who initiated the assigned intervention and were reviewed during follow-up together with participant interview regarding treatment use. Participants recorded each cleansing application immediately after use. Adherence was summarized in the operational FAS among participants with evaluable treatment diaries. Adherence was calculated as the number of recorded cleansing applications divided by the 56 expected applications, multiplied by 100%. Use below 80% of the expected applications was considered inadequate adherence and was recorded as a protocol deviation with reinforcement of adherence education.

Outcome measures

The primary endpoint was the Week 4 visual analog scale (VAS) score for the selected study eye. Eye-specific ocular symptoms were assessed for the selected study eye using eight 100-mm VAS symptom items: burning/stinging, itching, foreign-body sensation, blurred vision, dryness, photophobia, pain, and discomfort. Each item was scored from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater symptom severity. The primary VAS score was calculated as the mean of the eight symptom-item scores and therefore ranged from 0 to 100.

Secondary endpoints included lid margin erythema total score, lid margin signs total score, Ocular Surface Disease Index (OSDI) total score, mean fluorescein tear film breakup time, Schirmer test result, corneal fluorescein staining total score, tear meniscus height, overall MGD score, Meibo-Scan total score, MGD expressibility sum, and MGD secretion quality. Change from baseline (Week 4 minus baseline) was summarized descriptively only. Although change in Week 4 VAS from baseline was used for sample size planning, the primary inferential analyses were based on baseline-adjusted Week 4 comparisons rather than change-score models. Day 14 measurements were treated as auxiliary variables in missing-data sensitivity analyses.

Clinical grading procedures

Clinical signs were assessed for the selected study eye using standardized grading procedures, with higher scores indicating more severe abnormality unless otherwise specified. Lid margin erythema was graded separately for the upper and lower eyelids on a 0–3 scale: 0, none/absent or normal; 1, mild erythema or hyperemia; 2, moderate erythema or hyperemia; and 3, severe erythema or hyperemia. Upper- and lower-eyelid scores were summed to obtain the study-eye lid margin erythema total score, ranging from 0 to 6.

Lid margin signs were assessed separately for the upper and lower eyelids. For each eyelid, five signs were recorded: lid-margin rounding, lid-margin thickening, lid-margin hyperkeratinization, anterior lid-margin hyperemia, and vascular proliferation or telangiectasia around the meibomian gland orifices. The number of abnormal signs per eyelid was categorized as 0 signs = normal/absent, 1–2 signs = mild, 3 signs = moderate, and 4–5 signs = severe; these categories were assigned eyelid-level severity scores of 0, 1.5, 3, and 4.5, respectively. Upper- and lower-eyelid scores were summed to obtain the study-eye lid margin signs total score, ranging from 0 to 9.

Meibo-Scan/meiboscore was graded separately for the upper and lower eyelids from 0 to 3 per eyelid and summed to obtain the study-eye score, ranging from 0 to 6. MGD expressibility was assessed in the central five glands of each eyelid using the Korb MGE®-Stick and graded from 0 to 3: 0, all glands expressible; 1, secretion expressed from 3 to 4 glands; 2, secretion expressed from 1 to 2 glands; and 3, no gland expressible. Upper- and lower-eyelid scores were summed to obtain the study-eye MGD expressibility sum, ranging from 0 to 6. Meibum quality was graded separately for the upper and lower eyelids as 0 for clear fluid, 1 for cloudy fluid, 2 for cloudy fluid with debris or granules, and 3 for thick toothpaste-like secretion. Upper- and lower-eyelid scores were summed to obtain the study-eye meibum quality total score, ranging from 0 to 6.

The overall MGD score was calculated as the sum of the study-eye Meibo-Scan/meiboscore, lid margin signs total score, MGD expressibility sum, and meibum quality total score. The total score ranged from 0 to 27, with higher scores indicating more severe MGD-related abnormality.

Corneal fluorescein staining was assessed using the NEI scale. Five microliters of 2% preservative-free sodium fluorescein were instilled into the lower conjunctival sac. After 2–3 min, corneal staining was evaluated using a Wratten #12 yellow filter. The cornea was divided into five regions, each graded from 0 to 3; the study-eye total score ranged from 0 to 15, with higher scores indicating more severe staining.

Participants with clinical features suggestive of Demodex-associated blepharitis, including ocular itching, foreign-body sensation, dryness, lid margin hyperemia, scales, or cylindrical sleeve-like deposits at the eyelash base, underwent Demodex examination. At least two eyelashes were epilated from each of the upper and lower eyelids of each eye, with preferential sampling of eyelashes with visible sleeve-like deposits when present. The number of mites and eyelashes was recorded under microscopy, and the mite count per eyelash was calculated for each eye.

Safety and tolerability assessments

Safety and tolerability were assessed descriptively during the 4-week intervention period. Standard ophthalmic safety monitoring included best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure, and slit-lamp biomicroscopy at scheduled visits. Ocular and periocular adverse events, clinically relevant ocular-surface deterioration, and interruption or discontinuation of the assigned intervention because of intolerance were recorded when reported by participants or identified during follow-up examinations. Corneal fluorescein staining was evaluated both as a secondary ocular-surface endpoint and as an epithelial safety indicator. Patient-reported ocular symptoms, including burning or stinging, itching, pain, blurred vision, and overall discomfort, were reviewed to contextualize local tolerability.

Study-eye definition and analysis units

The OSDI was analyzed as a participant-level questionnaire outcome. The primary VAS score and other eye-specific outcomes were analyzed using a study-eye dataset. The study eye was selected at baseline according to the more severe eye-specific symptom/sign profile, with lid margin erythema and then the right eye used as tie-breakers. The same study eye was used consistently across all eye-level analyses.

Analysis populations

The operational full analysis set (FAS) included randomized participants with at least one analyzable post-baseline VAS assessment. Randomized participants without analyzable post-baseline VAS data were not included in this analysis set. The primary Week 4 observed-case analysis included participants in the operational FAS with an analyzable Week 4 VAS assessment. Secondary endpoints were analyzed in endpoint-specific observed-case datasets. A supportive complete-case subset was used only in sensitivity analyses and was not used for confirmatory inference.

Sample size calculation

The study used a three-arm parallel-group design, with change from baseline in Week 4 VAS as the design-driving endpoint. Sample size planning was performed under a one-way analysis-of-variance framework. Assuming a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, 80% power, and a Cohen f of 0.40, the required evaluable sample size was approximately 64 participants. Allowing for an anticipated attrition rate of 10%, the planned total sample size was set at 72 participants with a target allocation ratio of 1:1:1.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. The primary inferential analysis compared Week 4 VAS across treatment groups using analysis of covariance adjusted for baseline VAS. Because Week 4 was the prespecified principal comparison time point, the primary analysis strategy used a baseline-adjusted Week 4 model rather than longitudinal repeated-measures modeling.

The confirmatory analysis consisted of an overall between-group test for VAS. Pairwise comparisons among treatment groups were performed only after a significant overall test, with P values adjusted using the Holm procedure to control the family-wise error rate across the three pairwise contrasts.

Secondary endpoints were analyzed using prespecified baseline-adjusted models and were interpreted as supportive only. No formal multiplicity adjustment was applied across the full set of secondary endpoints; accordingly, secondary findings were considered nominal and exploratory. For bounded or ordinal-like summed scores, the main baseline-adjusted linear framework was retained for consistency of presentation, with selected rank-based and permutation analyses used as supportive robustness checks where appropriate.

Primary analyses were conducted on an observed-case basis within the FAS. Supportive sensitivity analyses for the primary endpoint included a complete-case subset, multiple imputation, HC3 robust standard errors, bootstrap confidence intervals, and permutation ANCOVA. The Week 4 complete-case ANCOVA for VAS corresponds to the primary observed-case analysis and is presented to document the analyzed sample rather than as an independent confirmatory analysis. For selected bounded or ordinal-like secondary outcomes, endpoint-specific complete-case, multiple-imputation, rank-based, and permutation analyses were used as supportive robustness checks where appropriate.

Forty imputed datasets were generated using an iterative regression-based procedure with Bayesian ridge models and posterior sampling. The imputation model included treatment group, baseline VAS, Day 14 VAS, age, and sex. Imputed datasets were analyzed using the same baseline-adjusted model and combined according to Rubin’s rules.

Safety and tolerability findings were summarized descriptively by treatment group. No formal between-group hypothesis testing was performed for safety outcomes because the trial was not powered for comparative safety assessment. Statistical analyses were performed using R, version 4.3.1.

Results

Participant flow and baseline characteristics

Of the 72 randomized participants, 64 had at least one analyzable post-baseline VAS assessment and were included in the operational FAS (21 in the wipe group, 22 in the hypochlorous acid group, and 21 in the saline group). Eight randomized participants were excluded from the FAS because no analyzable post-baseline VAS data were available; the distribution was two in the wipe group, one in the hypochlorous acid group, and five in the saline group. Among these eight participants, two had withdrawal recorded at Visit 2, five had no recorded post-baseline visits, and one had withdrawal recorded at Visit 3.

Within the operational FAS, Week 4 VAS was observed in 21 participants in the wipe group, 20 in the hypochlorous acid group, and 18 in the saline group. Week 4 VAS was not completed in 0, 2, and 3 participants, respectively. A supportive complete-case subset comprised 52 participants (21 in the wipe group, 16 in the hypochlorous acid group, and 15 in the saline group) and was used only in sensitivity analyses. Primary outcome

In the FAS, the overall between-group difference in baseline-adjusted Week 4 VAS was significant (P = 0.007). The adjusted Week 4 mean VAS values were 27.47 (95% CI, 22.18 to 32.76) in the wipe group, 33.16 (95% CI, 27.80 to 38.51) in the hypochlorous acid group, and 40.83 (95% CI, 34.93 to 46.72) in the saline group. In pairwise comparisons with Holm adjustment, the wipe group had significantly lower Week 4 VAS than the saline group (adjusted difference, − 13.35; 95% CI, − 21.51 to − 5.20; Holm-adjusted P = 0.005). No other pairwise comparison remained significant after multiplicity adjustment. Secondary outcomes

Among the secondary endpoints, nominal between-group differences were observed for the Schirmer test result (overall P = 0.002), corneal fluorescein staining total score (overall P = 0.032), and MGD expressibility sum (overall P = 0.018). The adjusted Week 4 estimates for Schirmer testing were 12.43 (95% CI, 9.81 to 15.06) in the wipe group, 12.37 (95% CI, 9.69 to 15.06) in the hypochlorous acid group, and 6.02 (95% CI, 3.20 to 8.85) in the saline group. The corresponding adjusted estimates for corneal fluorescein staining total score were 0.67 (95% CI, 0.21 to 1.13), 0.20 (95% CI, − 0.23 to 0.63), and 1.15 (95% CI, 0.59 to 1.71), respectively. The adjusted estimates for MGD expressibility sum were 2.41 (95% CI, 1.97 to 2.86), 2.31 (95% CI, 1.85 to 2.78), and 3.27 (95% CI, 2.74 to 3.80), respectively. Safety and tolerability

No serious ocular adverse events or intolerance-related discontinuations were identified during the 4-week intervention period. Best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure, and slit-lamp biomicroscopy did not show clinically meaningful changes suggestive of ocular safety concerns. Corneal fluorescein staining did not suggest treatment-related epithelial toxicity or clinically relevant ocular-surface deterioration. Patient-reported ocular symptoms were reviewed as supportive tolerability information.

Sensitivity analyses

Supportive sensitivity analyses did not materially alter the primary interpretation of Week 4 VAS. The overall between-group signal remained similar in the complete-case subset, the multiple-imputation analysis, the HC3 analysis, and the permutation analysis. The contrast between the wipe and saline groups was generally preserved across these analyses, whereas the other pairwise contrasts were not consistently supported across all robustness procedures. Bootstrap percentile confidence intervals were directionally similar to those of the main analysis, although pairwise inferences were not identical across all procedures.

For selected sign-based secondary endpoints, supportive rank-based and permutation analyses did not alter the conclusion that these outcomes remained secondary and nonconfirmatory.

Discussion

In this randomized, assessor-masked, three-arm parallel-group trial, preformulated eyelid cleansing wipes yielded a lower baseline-adjusted Week 4 VAS score than saline-assisted cotton-pad cleansing, and this contrast remained directionally consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses. These findings suggest greater short-term symptom relief with a standardized wipe-based regimen than with saline-assisted mechanical cleansing alone in blepharitis-associated ocular surface disease. By contrast, confirmatory superiority over hypochlorous acid was not demonstrated for the primary endpoint.

Several secondary endpoints showed nominal overall between-group differences, including the Schirmer test result, corneal fluorescein staining total score, and MGD expressibility sum. Because these analyses were secondary and were not adjusted for multiplicity, they should be interpreted as supportive rather than confirmatory. These findings may indicate that active cleansing formulations influence tear-related, epithelial, and gland-related domains in addition to symptoms, although this interpretation requires confirmation in larger studies.

These results are consistent with current therapeutic frameworks in which lid-directed treatment is regarded as a fundamental component of blepharitis and MGD management6,9,10. At the same time, both systematic reviews and scoping reviews have emphasized that the evidence base for specific eyelid-cleansing products remains limited and heterogeneous14–16. Our trial therefore addresses a practical clinical question beyond the general recommendation for lid hygiene: whether a standardized active cleansing regimen provides incremental short-term benefit beyond mechanical cleansing alone.

The symptomatic advantage observed with wipes is broadly aligned with prior studies of dedicated lid cleansers and eyelid wipes. Guillon et al. reported symptomatic improvement with Blephaclean wipes in anterior blepharitis and MGD and, in a companion study, improvement in eyelid margin status and meibomian gland patency19,20. Sung et al. found that both a dedicated eyelid cleanser and diluted baby shampoo improved blepharitis over 4 weeks, whereas only the dedicated cleanser reduced ocular surface inflammation and was preferred by participants21. Similarly, Kobayashi et al. reported that a novel eyelid shampoo improved lid hygiene-related findings and dry eye symptoms in patients with MGD22. By contrast, Aryasit et al. found that baby shampoo and a commercial eyelid cleanser, both used with warm compresses, improved symptoms without a significant between-group difference and without a clear difference in compliance or complications23. Collectively, these studies suggest that standardized commercial cleansing regimens can provide meaningful short-term benefit, although the magnitude of any incremental advantage may depend on comparator intensity and coadministered therapies.

Our secondary pattern is also broadly concordant with prior work suggesting that lid hygiene may affect more than discomfort alone. In a randomized pilot trial after cataract surgery, Eom et al. found that perioperative eyelid hygiene improved subjective symptoms and attenuated worsening of anterior blepharitis and meibomian gland parameters24. More broadly, staged management frameworks for MGD emphasize that treatment selection should be aligned with disease severity and gland status rather than inferred from symptoms alone25. In the present study, the nominal signals in Schirmer testing, corneal fluorescein staining, and MGD expressibility are consistent with the possibility that active cleansing formulations influence tear-related, epithelial, and gland-related domains, although these secondary findings remain nonconfirmatory.

The hypochlorous acid findings also merit balanced interpretation. Zhang et al. reported that 0.01% hypochlorous acid delivered by ultrasonic atomization over 2 weeks was tolerable and effective for blepharitis26. Mencucci et al. likewise observed favorable short-term clinical outcomes together with antimicrobial activity for a hypochlorous acid hygiene solution, compared with hyaluronic acid wipes, in blepharitis with mild-to-moderate dry eye27. However, those studies differed materially from the present trial in delivery route, comparator regimen, and treatment context. In addition, adjunctive heat-based therapies can themselves modify symptom and sign outcomes in MGD, which further complicates cross-trial comparisons when cointerventions are not matched28,29. Our findings therefore should not be interpreted as evidence against hypochlorous acid itself; rather, they indicate that hypochlorous acid delivered by cotton-pad wiping twice daily for 4 weeks did not demonstrate confirmatory superiority on the prespecified primary endpoint in this specific trial context.

The divergence between symptom and sign outcomes is also clinically plausible. Dry eye and MGD research consistently show imperfect alignment between patient-reported symptoms and ocular surface signs5,18. In the present 4-week trial, the clearer between-group signal in VAS than in OSDI or several sign-based measures may therefore reflect both symptom-sign discordance and differences in the time course of response across outcome domains. A short follow-up period may be sufficient to capture early changes in discomfort while being less sensitive to slower changes in lid margin or gland-related signs. Another consideration is disease heterogeneity: blepharitis phenotypes such as Demodex-associated disease may differ in pathophysiology and treatment responsiveness, but we did not stratify participants at that level30.

Several limitations should be considered. Participants and treatment providers could not be masked because the interventions differed in physical form and preparation procedures, and the primary endpoint was a participant-reported VAS symptom score; therefore, expectation effects, perceived product differences, placebo effects, or performance effects may have contributed to the observed between-group differences, and the magnitude of this bias cannot be quantified in this design. Baseline VAS scores differed across groups despite randomization, and residual imbalance or regression-to-the-mean effects cannot be fully excluded despite baseline adjustment. The operational FAS excluded randomized participants without analyzable post-baseline VAS data, and Week 4 VAS was not completed for five participants within the operational FAS; although supportive sensitivity analyses yielded a similar primary interpretation, attrition-related bias cannot be fully excluded. The clinical importance of the adjusted VAS difference should be interpreted cautiously because a disease-specific minimal clinically important difference for this eye-specific VAS instrument has not been firmly established. Schirmer testing is variable and may be influenced by reflex tearing, test conditions, and timing; therefore, the nominal Schirmer signal should be interpreted cautiously. Generalizability is limited by the single-center design, short follow-up, and the predominance of older women in the enrolled population despite broader age eligibility.

Conclusions

In adults with blepharitis-associated ocular surface disease, 4 weeks of preformulated eyelid cleansing wipes were associated with a lower baseline-adjusted Week 4 VAS symptom score than saline-assisted mechanical lid cleansing. Confirmatory superiority over hypochlorous acid was not established, and sign-based secondary findings should be interpreted as supportive. Larger and longer-term studies are needed to determine whether different cleansing modalities provide sustained advantages across symptom-dominant and sign-dominant disease phenotypes.
