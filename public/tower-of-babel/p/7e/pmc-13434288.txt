A quantitative framework for translating architectural heritage into cultural product design through ancient theatrical stages in Shanxi
Authors: Fanrui Sun, Xiao Zhang, Lingsheng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study proposes a quantitative design transformation framework to revitalize Shanxi’s historical opera stages by aligning heritage semantics with consumer needs. A multi-criteria decision-making model integrating architectural meme theory, AHP, QFD, TOPSIS, and Stable Diffusion was employed. Cultural ‘genes’ extracted from 3D scans formed a meme lineage, where AHP and QFD quantified attributes to guide generative design, and TOPSIS facilitated performance evaluation. Of the resulting cultural products, the “Spire-Top Penholder” yielded the highest relative affinity coefficient, successfully balancing structural authenticity with modern utility. The study confirms that reinterpreting elements like flying eaves through product semantics augments the public perception of heritage value. This study proposes a data-driven paradigm for sustainable heritage preservation, providing a scalable framework for integrating historical architecture into digital platforms for cultural documentation, interpretation, and dissemination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-57445-x.Subject terms: Cultural and media studies, Cultural and media studies, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The theatrical stages of the Jin and Yuan dynasties (12th–fourteenth centuries) in Shanxi Province represent the earliest and most significant extant corpus of theatrical architecture in China1–4. As a synthesis of tangible structures and intangible cultural practices, these monuments provide critical evidence of the socio-religious dynamics and the evolution of performative space in pre-modern society5. While traditional conservation has focused on physical stability and documentation, the digital dissemination and sustainable transmission of these heritage sites remain a significant challenge in the contemporary era. Advances in high-fidelity digital recording technologies, the methodological gap remains in enhancing architectural heritage value through design transformation—a critical component of digital dissemination. Existing interpretation practices often reduce complex three-dimensional structural logic to simplistic two-dimensional visual symbols. This reductionist approach fails to convey the authentic morphological characteristics and structural ingenuity of the original buildings, leading to the loss of cultural semantics during digital conversion.

Drawing on Dawkins’ Memetics6, this study conceptualizes architectural heritage as replicable "cultural genes." This theoretical lens facilitates the quantitative deconstruction of complex assets into standardized units7, an approach validated in recent computational design studies8,9. Specifically, the attributes of Shanxi Jin-Yuan Opera Stages are stratified into explicit domains (morphological features including structure, patterns, materials, and colors) and implicit domains (semantic connotations such as spatial hierarchy and historical narrative)10. Visual generative tools are employed to isolate these memes, creating a standardized “gene pool” for subsequent analysis. To mitigate the subjectivity inherent in cultural product design, a hybrid Multi-Criteria Decision Making framework11–13 is proposed. This integrated model establishes a rigorous computational logic via three stages:Weighting (AHP): Utilizing Saaty’s Analytic Hierarchy Process14, expert judgment matrices quantify the relative importance of the identified attributes, ensuring design priorities align with intrinsic heritage values15. Translation (QFD): Serving as a semantic bridge16, Quality Function Deployment maps these weighted requirements to specific morphological parameters through the House of Quality (HOQ). This mechanism ensures the output preserves cultural authenticity while enhancing cognitive accessibility for contemporary audiences. Evaluation (TOPSIS): The Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution ranks design alternatives based on their geometric proximity to the "Positive Ideal Solution"17. The final optimality is determined by the closeness coefficient (Ci), where a value approaching 1 indicates superior performance18.

This study proposes a systematic design translation framework integrating architectural meme theory, AHP, QFD, TOPSIS, and AIGC. First, sample images of ancient opera stages were captured using photographic equipment. Based on point cloud data obtained from 3D laser scanning, the stage structures were analyzed to construct a meme map of ancient opera stages. Cultural and creative products are not merely functional carriers but comprehensive expressions of cultural connotations, user needs, and contemporary aesthetics. This study takes the Jin Yuan Theater as a cultural representation object, introducing Memetic Theory to deconstruct the structure of existing architectural heritage. By extracting its core cultural factors, a digital translation model for heritage elements is established, laying the theoretical foundation for subsequent design practices. Second, the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) was employed to construct a hierarchical model of cultural elements for Jin-Yuan opera stages, calculating weights for key architectural features. Quality Function Deployment (QFD) mapped user requirements into specific design specifications to guide creative direction. Subsequently, Stable Diffusion was employed for visual translation, generating diverse design iterations based on weighted prompts. Designers standardized and optimized the products. Finally, the Technique for Order Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution (TOPSIS) was applied to scientifically evaluate the generated solutions across dimensions of cultural fidelity, aesthetic quality, and functional innovation.

In this study, we use the AHP-QFD-TOPSIS approach as a tool for aligning heritage significance with design decisions. It is not merely a technical mechanism for selecting visual elements. For this research, we take the ancient opera stage in Jinyuan, Shanxi, as a case study. The paper identifies its cultural features and translates them into product design. However, the heritage value of these stages extends beyond their formal architectural symbols. The ancient opera stage acts as a physical carrier of cultural heritage. They embody architectural forms, opera performances, rituals, musical traditions and local identity. This is why we consider the ancient opera stage to be a complete heritage system rather than an isolated set of symbols. In the field of international heritage, heritage value does not only come from the physical structure itself. It also stems from intangible practices, social significance and the values ascribed to the site by local communities. UNESCO defines the Outstanding Universal Value of heritage as having exceptional cultural or natural significance. Its assessment covers multiple dimensions, including history, aesthetics, science and society. From the perspective of cultural transmission, the AHP-QFD-TOPSIS pathway study on product design translation for the ancient opera stages of the Jin and Yuan dynasties in Shanxi can better highlight the multifaceted value of this type of heritage. This is a really useful area to explore.

Historical value

As tangible evidence of regional settlement evolution, architectural systems, and theatrical spatial layout, the ancient opera stages preserve structural methods such as the “reduced-column” and “shifted-column” techniques used during the Jin and Yuan dynasties. Through their design language, they transmit architectural structures and bear witness to local architectural history.

Folklore and cultural value

As venues historically rooted in rituals, temple fairs, and community gatherings, the value of ancient opera stages stems not only from their architectural forms but also from ongoing performance activities and social participation. Extracting their architectural and cultural characteristics for product design can effectively forge emotional bonds across generations.

Craftsmanship value

Ancient opera stages embody local knowledge in timber framing, ornamentation, and construction techniques, reflecting the artisanal wisdom of our ancestors.

This paper applies meme theory to visually deconstruct the ancient opera stage, extracting its key architectural structural features for product design transformation, thereby helping to popularize relevant knowledge. Contemporary sustainable value: The transformation of heritage resources into contemporary products that resonate with the public supports cultural dissemination, local brand development, and sustainable revitalization. This also aids in the dissemination and popularization of relevant cultural heritage knowledge.

Three distinctive cultural design solutions were developed: the Pagoda-Roof Pen Holder, the Flying-Eaves Table Lamp, and the Hip-Roof Pen Stand. TOPSIS-based quantitative evaluation revealed the Pagoda-Roof Pen Holder achieved the highest affinity coefficient, indicating optimal balance between heritage authenticity and user preference. The findings demonstrate that visually translating architectural elements—such as flying eaves, beam frameworks, and roof structures—into contemporary product semantics significantly enhances public perception of heritage cultural value.

Methods

Design framework construction

Stage One: Sample Selection and Data Collection. To ensure historical representativeness and data integrity, six representative ancient theatrical stages from the Jin and Yuan dynasties in Shanxi Province were selected as primary research subjects. Selection criteria were established through field surveys, archival research, and expert consultation, prioritizing architectural authenticity and typological diversity.

Phase 2: Morphological Deconstruction Based on the theory of Memetics, the architectural characteristics of the selected stages were deconstructed to establish a comprehensive attribute spectrum. These attributes were categorized into two domains:Explicit Attributes (Tangible): Physical elements including structural components (e.g., Dou-gong brackets), decorative patterns, and color palettes.Implicit Attributes (Intangible): Abstract elements encompassing spatial hierarchy, historical semantics, and cultural symbolism.

Phase3: Weighting and Translation (AHP-QFD Integration).To determine the relative importance of these indicators and capture target user demands, a structured consultation was conducted with a panel of 25 experts. The panel composition ensured interdisciplinary validity, consisting of 2 architectural historians, 6 cultural heritage conservation professionals, and 17 design scholars.An integrated AHP-QFD model was employed to translate qualitative heritage attributes into quantitative design parameters:AHP (Analytic Hierarchy Process): Used to construct the evaluation hie rarchy. Expert scoring determined the weight of each cultural attribute and user requirement, establishing a prioritized decision matrix.QFD (Quality Function Deployment): The House of Quality (HOQ) was utilized to map the weighted user needs (WHATs) onto specific design characteristics (HOWs), ensuring the design direction aligns with both heritage value.

Phase 4: Generative Design and Decision Making. Leveraging the parameters defined in Phase 3, Generative AI (AIGC) tools were applied to produce multiple conceptual design alternatives. Finally, the TOPSIS (Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution) method was implemented to evaluate these alternatives. By calculating the Euclidean distance to the ideal solution, the optimal design scheme was identified, ensuring a scientifically rigorous selection process for the final cultural product (Fig. 1).

Data collection and gene map construction of ancient theater stages

The theoretical framework for attribute extraction draws upon Memetics in architectural theory. Salingaros19 posits that specific architectural styles are constituted by replicable "visual memes," encompassing form, geometry, and surface treatment. Long11 further categorized architectural memes into three dimensions: conceptual (thought), morphological (form), and spatial.

The selected ancient opera stage samples are: Niuwang Temple Stage (Yuan Dynasty, C1), Wangqu Village Dongyue Temple Stage (Yuan Dynasty, C2), Qiaozedai Temple Stage (Jin Dynasty, C3), the Dongyang Houtu Temple Stage in Linfen (Yuan Dynasty, C4), the Qiaozedai Temple (Yuan Dynasty, C5), and the Dongcun Stage (Yuan Dynasty, C6).Applying this theoretical lens to the point cloud data and historical records, this study deconstructs the Jin-Yuan Opera Stages into a standardized attribute spectrum. The architectural features are codified into seven distinct factors (C1–C7) to facilitate subsequent quantitative analysis: C1 Roof Form Factor, C2 Structural Form Factor, C3 Pattern Factor, C4 Material Factor, C5 Color Factor, C6 Semantic Factor, C7 Spatial Layout Factor (Fig. 2).

Extraction of roof form memes

The quintessential roof form of Jin-Yuan period architecture is the single-eave hip-and-gable roof. The Yingzao Fashi records: “The hip-and-gable roof features nine ridges, with the timber framework centered as a single-eave structure12.” Liang Sicheng described it in A History of Chinese Architecture as “a double-eaved roof with nine ridges, where the purlins above the eaves do not feature the common purlin-to-purlin connection—a design rarely seen after the Yuan dynasty.” The single-eave hip-and-gable roof comprises a main ridge, four hanging ridges, and four cross-ridges. Architectural structures of this period conveyed a visually imposing, grand, and dignified presence13. For example, the interior of the single-eave cross-gabled roof structure at the Dai Temple theater stage in Zezhou during the Jin Dynasty (Table 1).

Structural form memes extraction

The structure of a theater stage primarily consists of six components: the roof, the beam framework, the dougong brackets, the roof columns, the walls, and the base platform. The fundamental architectural framework is mainly expressed through key structural elements such as beams, columns, and dougong brackets. The theater stage conveys cultural hierarchical concepts through its roof components and interior decoration. Influenced by regional factors, the Jin-Yuan theater stage integrates characteristics of northern regional and ethnic cultures, manifesting in a robust and unadorned style. Structural form memes can be distilled into multiple factors. For instance, the spacing of structural elements like bracket sets reflects both load-bearing functions and cultural aesthetics. The chimera roof finials carry cultural symbolism for fire and lightning protection while serving functional purposes of waterproofing and structural reinforcement. “The technique of reducing and relocating columns became a defining feature of Yuan Dynasty timber construction13,” with post-and-lintel timber frameworks exemplifying the period’s emphasis on architectural functionality (Table 2).

Pattern memes extraction

The decorative motifs on Jin-Yuan opera stages revolve around cultural themes of ritual, folk customs, and opera20. Typical patterns such as peonies, scrollwork, and lotus motifs form decorative memes through morphological replication and semantic inheritance. The stage pillars of the Niuwang Temple in Weicun, Linfen, feature high-relief peony carvings in rows, embodying the traditional symbolism of “wealth and auspiciousness.” The stage of the Daitemple in Qiaozhai incorporates interlaced scrollwork patterns, metaphorically representing the water deity nurturing all living things. These stage motifs carry the people’s aspirations for the elimination of floods and abundant harvests (Table 3).

Material memes extraction

The construction materials for Jin-Yuan opera stages primarily consist of wood, supplemented by brick and stone. Influenced by regional conditions, southern Shanxi boasts abundant reserves of both timber and stone. The combination of wood and stone effectively reduces construction costs while enabling flexible spatial layouts for the stages. For instance, the beam joints in the Dongyue Temple stage framework in Wangqu Village employ mortise-and-tenon connections, with some beams reinforced through layered timber techniques to enhance load-bearing capacity. Among auxiliary materials, the stage base is constructed with blue stone masonry, while sections beneath the eaves brackets incorporate embedded blue bricks to seal gaps, thereby reducing moisture exposure risks to the timber structure (Table 4).

Extraction of semantic memes

Ancient theater stages served as material carriers for the transmission of ritual-music culture, with their early functions centered on deity worship and ancestral sacrifice. The popularization of ritual-music ideology among the populace was a crucial aspect embodied by temple theaters22. The stage served to transmit official ideology to the common people, fulfilling the function of ritual-music education23. The form and height of the stage base carried hierarchical symbolism; the elevated base of the Niuwang Temple stage created a visually imposing spatial sense, combining stable architectural utility with ceremonial order and cultural function. Plaques serve as architectural identifiers, their inscriptions reflecting cultural significance. The Dongcun stage plaque bears the inscription “Cloud-Halting Tower” (E Yun Lou), a term derived from Liezi’s Tang Wen chapter. It describes theatrical sound as “vibrating forests and echoing to halt drifting clouds,” embodying the populace’s aspiration to invoke blessings through performance (Table 6).

Extraction of spatial layout memes

Spatial memes in architecture primarily manifest as three-dimensional volumes, planar enclosed spaces, and the arrangement of building clusters. The spatial evolution of the Niuwang Temple Stage embodies ritual and sacrificial spatial expressions24, reflecting a sense of order through central axis symmetry. The Ox King Hall is situated at the courtyard’s center, aligned with the mountain gate and main hall along a single central axis. The opera stage faces the Ox King Hall, with its platform base, lintels, and eaves plaques centered along this axis. The stage at Qiaoze Temple demonstrates spatial hierarchy: oriented south-facing north with its back to the temple’s northern main hall, its platform base sits lower than the main hall’s. The stage’s three-sided openness enhances the distance between performers and audience (Table 7).

AHP calculation process

Based on the memetic analysis of architectural morphology, philosophy, and spatial organization, a hierarchical evaluation system was established to quantify the design translation of Shanxi Jin-Yuan Opera Stages. The model is structured into three levels:

Target Layer (A): Defined as the "Design Translation of Cultural Products for Shanxi Jin-Yuan Opera Stages."Criteria Layer (B): Decomposed into seven primary dimensions corresponding to the extracted cultural memes: Roof Form (B1), Structural Form (B2), Decorative Pattern (B3), Material (B4), Color (B5), Semantic Meaning (B6), and Spatial Layout (B7).

Data Source for Attributes: The specific cultural factors were derived from a morphological study of six representative extant stages, designated as the sample set: Niuwang Temple Stage (Yuan Dynasty, C1), Wangqu Dongyue Temple Stage (Yuan Dynasty, C2), Qiaozedai Temple Stage (Jin Dynasty, C3), Linfen Dongyang Houtu Temple Stage (Yuan Dynasty, C4), Qiaoze Temple Stage (Yuan Dynasty, C5), and Dongcun Stage (Yuan Dynasty, C6).

Translation of user requirements based on QFD theory

This study employs Quality Function Deployment (QFD) theory to transform unstructured user preferences into quantifiable design characteristics for cultural and creative products through the construction of a House of Quality (HOQ) model. The implementation process unfolds in three phases: First, user interviews, field research, and the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) are utilized to identify user needs and establish their weighting hierarchy, thereby establishing foundational demand-side data. Second, product design elements matching the architectural characteristics of the Jin Yuan Theater were extracted. Finally, a demand-design relationship matrix was constructed to quantitatively analyze their correlation. This method establishes a logical mapping between users’ embodied needs and design elements, overcoming the subjective arbitrariness in traditional design processes and enhancing the scientific rigor of cultural heritage creative product development.

To ensure accuracy in cultural factor translation, this study engaged experts in cultural heritage preservation and architecture for multiple rounds of discussions. These discussions translated the initially selected cultural factors of the Jin-Yuan Theater into performance dimensions for cultural and creative products. Guided by AHP weighting indicators during the translation from architectural language to product design language, 12 core product quality characteristics (design elements) were ultimately defined: Form Symbolism Innovation (D1), Stage Structure Recognition (D2), Structural Adaptability (D3), Cultural Product Function Clarity (D4), Decorative Pattern Innovation (D5), Cultural Element Integration (D6), Wood Material Properties (D7), Architectural Color Aesthetics (D8), Semantic Expression Accuracy (D9), Cultural Connotation Transmission Clarity (D10), Spatial Imagery Transformation (D11), and Emotional Resonance and Interactivity (D12).

Building upon this foundation, this study constructs a quality house model for the design of Jin Yuan Theater cultural and creative products. The left input side of the model comprises a set of user requirements weighted via AHP; The ceiling section defines the aforementioned 12 technical characteristics and performance elements of the Golden Yuan Theater. The correlation matrix on the roof employs “ + ” and “–” symbols to represent positive correlations or weak/negative correlations between design indicators, revealing the coupling relationships among design elements. The various relationship matrices at the model’s core quantify the strength of correspondence between user needs and design elements.

Scheme design practice

Diffusion models are a class of generative methods based on Markov chains and variational inference. Their core logic comprises two stages: forward diffusion and backward denoising. During the forward diffusion stage, the model iteratively injects Gaussian noise into the original data, gradually transforming the ordered data distribution into a quasi-Gaussian noise distribution(Fig. 5). During this process, the single-step forward probability distribution and the global distribution at any step are represented by Eqs. (9) and (10), respectively.

Diffusion model principle flowchart.

Subsequently, the model utilizes deep neural networks to learn its inverse process—reverse denoising. In this stage, the network learns to recover the original feature mapping from the noise, enabling reconstruction of the original data distribution. By learning this iterative denoising path, diffusion models can precisely generate high-quality data conforming to the target distribution from random noise samples, thereby accomplishing visual content generation tasks such as cultural and creative design.

During the design implementation phase, large-scale pre-trained models based on the Transformer architecture (e.g., Flux.1-dev) were employed to assist in generating design proposals. By integrating the Stable Diffusion platform with the ControlNet spatial constraint module, precise control over the stage’s external features and topological structure was achieved. Although deep learning models possess formidable image generation capabilities, the conceptual diagrams they produce may exhibit deviations in historical accuracy. Therefore, AI-generated drafts are defined as “inspirational prototypes,” requiring professional designers to standardize and validate them according to architectural norms. This ensures cultural and creative products maintain artistic integrity while preserving historical accuracy, See Tables (Figs. 6, 7).

Option One: Inspired by the “treasure dome” of the Dongyue Temple stage in Wangqu Village. This architectural element carries auspicious and peaceful connotations within traditional contexts. The design extracts the dome’s rounded contours, transforming them into the pen holder’s circular arc-shaped opening. This achieves a morphological evolution from architectural ornamentation to modern stationery functionality. The color strategy employs a low-saturation reddish-brown hue to recreate the warm texture of wood. To enhance portability and practicality, the structure utilizes a rotating screw connection for easy disassembly and cleaning.

Option 2: Based on the aforementioned AHP weighting calculations, the flying eaves factor (C24) was identified as the most representative morphological characteristic. QFD analysis further emphasized the integration of architectural structure and cultural symbolism. Option 2 extracts the curved lines of the flying eaves from the stage of the Houtu Temple in Dongyang, Linfen, translating them into a biomimetic design for the lampshade. This effectively softens the rigidity of modern industrial lines. The lamp base and pillar incorporate traditional mortise-and-tenon joints. This non-adhesive connection not only optimizes packaging volume and assembly convenience but also mechanically recreates the construction philosophy of Jin-Yuan-era opera stages.

Option Three: This design centers on the complex “cross-gabled hip roof” structure atop the stage. It distills the symmetrical aesthetic of the roof’s four corners, transforming the ridge’s “chiwen” ornament into a functional fixed node for the brush holder. Structurally, the vertical ridge extends as the primary support frame, while the horizontal ridge beams spread outward to form minimalist load-bearing points. Visually, the design adopts the emerald green hues of the architectural glaze as its primary palette, imbuing the product with the semantic resonance of “Ink-washed Green Mountains.” This achieves a living reconstruction of intangible cultural heritage architectural elements within the scholar’s living space.

User evaluation and methodological validation via TOPSIS

The Two-Operator Pseudo-Entropy Selection (TOPSIS) method is commonly employed to construct evaluation models for multi-criteria decision analysis26. Based on translational research using the Jin Yuan Xi Tai gene map, the design scheme utilizes the TOPSIS method to validate the fit between the designed product and the required product performance metrics27.

Thirty-five participants with relevant expertise evaluated three design proposals using a 9-point Likert scale for assessment dimensions. The initial evaluation matrix (see Table 19) revealed high scores across key indicators such as “morphological symbolism innovation (D1),” “cultural element integration (D6),” and “structural adaptability (D3).” These evaluation results align closely with the weight distributions previously determined through the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and Quality Function Deployment (QFD). The quantified user feedback not only validates the superiority of the three design proposals but further confirms the effectiveness and rigor of the research methodology—quantitative analysis driving AIGC generation—in the digital translation of cultural heritage.

General discussion

This paper presents a three-stage framework combining AHP, QFD and TOPSIS, comprising three key phases: cultural feature extraction, requirement mapping and scheme optimisation. These phases form a logical framework of 'weighting—mapping—ranking’. The approach is transferable at the methodological level and is not limited to specific architectural types or cultural contexts. It falls within the realm of culture-oriented design methodologies and serves as an implementation pathway focused on systematic decision support.

Similar types of architecture in different regions

For example, traditional theatres, stages or classical gardens in other regions, due to their historical and aesthetic nature, are similar to Shanxi’s Yuan Dynasty theatres. After conducting fieldwork, cultural elements are extracted and AHP-QFD is used to determine the target user’s needs. TOPSIS is then used to evaluate the products, thus helping to promote the living heritage of material culture.

Similar types of heritage from the same period

For example, temples, shrines and residences, which all have historical, artistic and social value. The framework can be adapted by adjusting the indicators, such as increasing the importance of religious rituals for temples and adding community functionality indicators for residences.

Other types of cultural heritage

For example, museum exhibitions, historical districts and non-material cultural heritage projects. Despite their different forms of expression, their protection and utilisation still depend on value assessment. Mapping to user needs. For example, when applying the framework to the design of museum cultural products, the 'cultural nature of the exhibits’ and ‘educational function’ dimensions must be prioritised. The quantitative analysis method used in this study remains the same as in previous studies29. Hou et al.30 proposed a hybrid model that integrates the Kano model with the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) and TOPSIS for the design of bronze cultural and creative products in museums. Yao et al.31 focused on the Wuxing gable walls of the Chaoshan region, combining semiotic semantics with the AHP-TOPSIS method to establish an auditable framework for the translation of cultural symbols. They validated the framework’s effectiveness through two case studies: tea sets and wayfinding systems. The study by Akter et al.32 validated the applicability of the AHP-TOPSIS method in the evaluation of cross-cultural heritage, revealed the impact of different governance models on the effectiveness of heritage conservation and adaptive reuse, and provided empirical evidence for policy-making. Yu and Xie applied the AHP-TOPSIS method to the optimization of intangible cultural heritage exhibition design, thereby expanding the scope of its application. This approach provides a quantitative evaluation method for the living transmission of intangible cultural heritage33.

The core value of this framework is to provide a practical, quantifiable basis for the 'cultural features, user needs, design decisions’ translation chain, rather than a template that can be directly adopted. When using the framework in practice, it is necessary to make adaptability adjustments based on the type of heritage, user group and protection scenario. Subsequent studies can select different types of cultural heritage and use comparative analysis to further verify and correct the framework’s scope.

Analysis of limitations

Qualitative research based on typical case studies may obscure the overall characteristics of rural heritage across a wide geographical area due to sampling bias. This study uses AHP and QFD to determine the weights of cultural elements and design requirements; however, it faces limitations in analyzing the complex spatial heterogeneity of cultural needs at a macro scale. To address this, technologies such as computer vision can be employed to process massive datasets and train semantic segmentation models tailored to historic buildings, thereby better identifying users’ latent needs34.

There are limitations in representing the dynamic evolutionary characteristics of cultural heritage. This study selected relatively well-preserved theater stages from the Jin and Yuan dynasties as its research subjects, without fully considering the patterns of evolution in stage elements across different historical periods. This limitation is similar to the issue of “insufficient time-series data” identified by Xu et al. in their research on Tibetan-style dwellings, and it imposes certain constraints on reflecting the changing characteristics of cultural heritage across different historical stages35.

Conclusion

This study examined six representative Jin-Yuan dynasty theater stages in Shanxi Province. Through documentary research and expert interviews, it employed memetics to construct a cultural gene map of these stages. The research proposed and validated a design translation pathway integrating qualitative analysis with AHP-QFD-TOPSIS quantitative evaluation. Utilizing artificial intelligence technology, it achieved the digital generation of modern cultural and creative products from cultural heritage genes. Findings indicate that as composite carriers of both tangible and intangible cultural heritage, architectural components such as the stage base, roof structure, bracket sets, and coffered ceilings of Jin-Yuan theaters embody profound cultural semantics and symbolic value. The technical framework developed in this study effectively transforms these traditional form elements into digital outputs that meet contemporary aesthetic and functional demands. This not only provides a scientific methodological reference for the digital dissemination and revitalization of architectural heritage but also pioneers new pathways for the systematic preservation and contemporary transmission of cultural heritage. Future research could focus on deeper micro-deconstruction and specialized translation of specific architectural components to further enhance the precision of heritage digitization and broaden the scope of cultural expression.
