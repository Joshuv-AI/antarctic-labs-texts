Defining Sex‐ and Age‐Specific Initial Stay Times for Continuous Heavy‐Intensity Work in Hot Environments Before Heat‐Mitigation Controls Are Implemented—Part B
Authors: Fergus K. O'Connor, Kristina‐Marie T. Janetos, Brodie J. Richards, Roberto C. Harris‐Mostert, Katie E. Wagar, Leonidas G. Ioannou, James J. McCormick, Robert D. Meade, Emily J. Tetzlaff, Ronald J. Sigal, W. Shane Journeay, Glen P. Kenny
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRationaleIndustry guidelines recommend work‐rest schedules to prevent unsafe core temperature elevations (> 38.0°C or > Δ1.0°C above baseline resting) in an “average” worker exposed to occupational heat stress. While permissible initial work durations (initial stay times [IST]) before implementing rest schedules have been suggested, existing data are limited to moderate‐intensity efforts.ObjectivesThe objective of this study was to assess whether factors of sex and age influence IST during heavy‐intensity work.MethodsWe assessed work durations before reaching unsafe core temperature thresholds (i.e., > 38.0°C or > Δ1.0°C above baseline resting) among young (18–30 years) and older (50–69 years) males and females during simulated heavy‐intensity work at 26°C, 29°C, and 32°C wet‐bulb globe temperatures (WBGT).FindingsThe likelihood of surpassing a core temperature of 38.0°C significantly increased at 32°C WBGT (hazard ratio [95% confidence interval]: 2.0 [1.5, 2.7], p = 0.001), but not at 29°C WBGT (1.1 [0.9, 1.4], p = 0.342), compared to 26°C WBGT. Median (interquartile range) durations to reach 38.0°C across groups were 32 (23–52), 29 (23–47), and 26 (18–35) minutes at 26°C, 29°C, and 32°C WBGT, respectively. The likelihood of reaching Δ1.0°C above baseline resting significantly increased at both 32°C (3.8 [2.6, 5.4], p < 0.001) and 29°C WBGT (1.5 [1.1, 2.0], p < 0.001), with median durations being 55 (40–82), 42 (35–56), and 33 (29–42) min, respectively. Older females were more likely to reach 38.0°C (2.4 [1.3, 4.3], p = 0.004) and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting (2.5 [1.4, 4.7], p = 0.003) compared to young males. No other sex‐ or age‐related differences were detected (p > 0.145).ImpactThese findings highlight the heightened vulnerability of older females during heavy‐intensity occupational heat stress, emphasizing the need for tailored guidelines to ensure equitable workforce protection.

Introduction

Extreme heat has forced workplaces to evolve how they approach health and safety. However, many workplaces may be ill‐prepared to take on the added challenge of increasing heat extremes. While workplaces are provided guidance to mitigate the adverse health effects of occupational heat stress [1], they are given limited direction on applying and actioning the guidance available. To reduce the health risks associated with occupational heat stress, workplaces largely rely on upper heat stress limits (defined as a sustained increase in core body temperature of > 1.0°C [assuming a pre‐job resting core body temperature of no greater than 37.5°C]) such as the Action Limits (ALs) and Threshold Limit Values (TLVs) for unacclimatized and heat acclimatized individuals, respectively, provided by the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) to manage the health and safety of workers in hot environments [2]. While these guidelines are assumed to provide comparable effectiveness for all healthy, non‐medicated workers, recent work showed they may be under protective for older (58 ± 5 years) males [3]. This was evidenced by the observation that the prescribed work‐rest allocations did not prevent body core temperature from exceeding the upper allowable limit in most men in all work‐simulated conditions assessed [3].

A core component of current occupational heat guidance is administrative controls designed to limit the time‐weighted average level of heat stress [2]. When increases in metabolic rate or the surrounding ambient conditions cause these upper heat stress limits to be exceeded, rest breaks and other controls are implemented to maintain core temperature within recommended limits (i.e., ≤ 38.0°C, or ≤ 1.0°C above baseline resting) [2]. However, workplaces are not provided direction on the initial work time that continuous work can be performed safely before heat‐mitigation controls should be employed (herein referred to as “initial stay time”); conditions increasingly experienced by workers because of rising global temperatures. This is an important omission of current guidance as initial stay times (e.g., start of the work shift, post‐lunch resumption of work) that are too long may result in core temperatures exceeding recommended limits, which are sustained throughout the work shift, despite the eventual introduction of controls.

While previous data from our lab have shown that factors of sex (young‐to‐middle‐aged males and females) [4] and age (young and older males with and without common chronic diseases (i.e., type 2 diabetes, hypertension) [5, 6] do not influence the duration of continuous work that can be completed before reaching a core temperature of 38.0°C, or Δ 1.0°C above baseline resting, these investigations are limited to moderate‐intensity work in ambient conditions of 28.0°C and 32.0°C wet‐bulb globe temperature (WBGT). Extending on this work [4, 5, 6], in the accompanying companion paper [7], we provide the first estimates of initial stay times in young and older males and females across a range of hot ambient conditions (26°C, 29°C and 32°C WBGT; representative of very warm and hot outdoor ambient conditions experienced in North America [8, 9]) during moderate‐intensity simulated work. While there were no group‐level differences in initial stay times in the 32°C WBGT condition (highlighting the magnitude of heat stress imposed under extreme heat conditions), older females experienced significantly decreased initial stay times compared to young males in ambient conditions of 26°C and 29°C WBGT. Similarly, older males experienced significant reductions in the time taken to reach Δ 1.0°C above baseline resting in the 29°C, but not 26°C WBGT condition. These findings indicate that as ambient heat stress intensified, age‐related differences in heat loss and therefore body heat storage may have become apparent.

Previous investigations assessing the influence of sex and age on the duration of work that can be performed before reaching core (rectal) temperature thresholds of 38.0°C, and a Δ 1.0°C above baseline resting have been limited to moderate‐intensity simulated work [4, 5, 6]. While we can expect that initial stay times will be reduced as work intensity increases, differences amongst groups are more likely evident under higher heat loads, considering that sex‐ and age‐related differences in heat loss capacity and therefore heat tolerance are heat‐load dependent [10, 11, 12]. Consequently, this may widen heat‐health inequalities amongst workers, as an increasingly larger segment of the labor force (i.e., females, older individuals) will remain underprotected during time‐weighted heavy work in hot environments (see Supplement 1 for detailed description of heavy‐intensity work tasks). With the number of females and older individuals engaged in physically demanding jobs globally is rising rapidly [13] and heavy‐intensity work comprising significant portions of the work shift [14, 15], it is critical that employers are provided evidence‐based guidance to mitigate the impacts of heat on worker health and safety.

Thus, we sought to determine the duration of work (initial stay time) that can be completed safely before the implementation of work‐rest allocations during heavy‐intensity work. The older adult age range of 50–69 years was selected to represent a growing, yet underrepresented, population at increased risk of heat‐related illness due to age‐related declines in thermoregulatory function [16]. Young adults (18–30 years) were included to enable direct age‐based comparisons. Indeed, an aging workforce [17] means that older workers (≥ 50 years) are increasingly required to perform physically demanding tasks [18], potentially placing this population sub‐group at heightened risk to the dangers of occupational heat exposure. Assessments were made in a range of ambient conditions exceeding the ACGIH ALs (i.e., WBGT of 26°C, 29°C and 32°C) during 240‐min of work typically performed in many physically demanding occupations such as the start of the work shift and post‐lunch resumption of work. These environmental conditions reflect average (26°C WBGT) and peak (29°C WBGT) conditions recorded during the summer period in northern‐hemisphere continental climates [8], while 32°C WBGT represents an environmental condition that first‐responders and front‐line workers may face during extreme heat events, such as those observed during recent deadly heatwaves in North America [9]. A secondary aim of our study was to provide preliminary estimates of continuous work times when the likelihood that the duration of work will exceed the initial stay time resulting in increases in core temperature exceeding recommended upper limits of 38.0°C by ≤ 1.5°C (equivalent to 39.5°C assuming a resting core temperature of 37.0°C). This is representative of scenarios experienced in the workplace where due to job or time constraints, emergency response, or other situations, the implementation of heat‐alleviation controls in the form of rest breaks is limited or not possible [14, 15, 19]. This information will equip workplaces with the critical information to refine current heat‐stress guidance with the addition of initial exposure times of workers performing their duties in the heat. Thus, helping to protect workers against potentially intolerable levels of heat strain that might otherwise occur without proper guidance.

Methods

This single‐site, laboratory‐based intervention trial was approved by the University of Ottawa Health Sciences and Science Research Ethics Board (H‐11‐22‐8660). The work was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, except for registration in a database. Written informed consent was obtained from all volunteers before participation.

Participants

Of the participants screened for eligibility, 50 met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate. 24 young adults (12 female, median (IQR) age 24 (21–27) years, 43.5 (36.5–49.4) mL/kg/min peak oxygen uptake [V̇O2peak]) and 24 older adults (12 female, 64 (58‐67) years, V̇O2peak of 35.4 (31.3–38.4) mL/kg/min) participated (Table 1). All participants were habitually active, non‐smokers, and reported to have no history of cardiovascular, metabolic, or respiratory disorders. None were heat‐acclimated or engaged in occupations or activities where they were regularly exposed to heat stress.

Experimental Protocol

All participants completed one preliminary session, and three experimental sessions in environmental temperatures of 26°C, 29°C, and 32°C WBGT (equivalent to 34.1°C, 37.5°C, and 41.3°C, with 35% relative humidity for all conditions, respectively). The experimental sessions were conducted on three separate days (minimum 72‐h between sessions) at the same time of day (within participant), in random order. Participants were instructed to refrain from exercise, alcohol, caffeine, and nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs for > 24 h, and were instructed to consume a light meal and 500 mL of water ~2 h before each session.

Preliminary Screening

During the preliminary screening visit, standing height, body mass, body surface area, body fat percentage, and V̇O2peak were determined. Body surface area was derived from measures of height (model 2391, Detecto, Webb City, MO, USA) and body mass (IND560, Mettler Toledo Inc., Mississauga, ON, Canada) [20]. Body fat percentage was estimated using bioelectrical impedance analysis (MC‐780U, Tanita Worldwide, Arlington Heights, IL, USA). Thereafter, participants completed a treadmill (Desmo Evo, Woodway, Waukesha, WI) walking test to determine the speed and percent grade of the treadmill to achieve a fixed rate of metabolic heat production of 260 W/m2, equivalent to a heavy‐intensity work effort [2] as quantified using indirect calorimetry (MCD Medgraphics Ultima Series, MGC Diagnostics, MN). After a short rest period (~5 min), V̇O2peak was quantified using a progressive incremental treadmill running protocol to volitional fatigue [21].

Experimental Protocol

Upon arrival, participants provided a urine sample to confirm euhydration (urine specific gravity ≤ 1.025). After ~45‐min instrumentation in a temperate room (~22°C), where resting data were recorded (rectal temperature, heart rate, blood pressure), the participant's nude and fully clothed body weights (while wearing a T‐shirt, shorts, single‐layer cotton coveralls [100% cotton], socks, and sport shoes [total insulation: ~1 Clo]) were then obtained. Thereafter, participants entered a climate‐controlled chamber with minimal airflow (< 0.2 m/s) regulated to one of three randomized environmental conditions at a WBGT of 26°C, 29°C, and 32°C. They rested for a 15‐min seated habituation period during which time baseline resting data were collected (rectal temperature, heart rate, and perceptual responses). Participants then commenced a simulated work shift (i.e., walking on a treadmill at a pre‐determined speed and grade) until their core temperature (i) reached 38°C or Δ1°C from baseline resting (whichever occurred last), (ii) until volitional fatigue or (iii) 240‐min of exercise was completed. A 240‐min work period was chosen to replicate the duration of work typical in many occupations; such as the start of the work shift and post‐lunch resumption of work. Upon cessation of exercise, participants exited the climate‐controlled chamber, and their weight was measured both fully clothed and nude. Thereafter, participants completed a 2‐h seated recovery in a thermoneutral environment (~22°C) during which time core temperature was recorded every 15 min.

Measurements

Core and Skin Temperatures

Core temperature indexed by rectal temperature was measured using a thermocouple temperature probe (Mon‐a‐therm General Purpose Temperature Probe; Mallinckrodt Medical Inc., St. Louis, MO, USA) inserted at least 12 cm past the anal sphincter. Core temperature was recorded every 15 s on an HP Agilent data acquisition module (model 3497A, Agilent Technologies Canada Inc., Mississauga, ON, Canada).

Skin temperatures were collected at 1‐min intervals using temperature data loggers (DS1922L Thermochron, OnSolution Pty Ltd) affixed to eight body regions using double‐sided adhesives and medical tape. Mean skin temperature was calculated according to the weightings recommended in ISO 9886:2004: 7% forehead, 17.5% right scapula, 17.5% upper left chest, 7% upper right arm, 7% right forearm, 5% left hand, 19% right anterior thigh, and 20% left calf [22].

Cardiovascular Measurements

Heart rate was measured continuously throughout the experimental sessions using a Polar coded WearLink and transmitter (Polar M400 watch, Polar H7 WearLink; Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland). Systolic and diastolic arterial blood pressure were measured after 10‐min of seated rest during the baseline period (before entering the heat) and following the final 15 min of rest after reaching their initial work time. Blood pressure was measured at the brachial artery in triplicate, with a minute in between each measurement, via an automatic blood pressure cuff (Digital Blood Pressure Monitor UM‐211, A&D Medical, Tokyo, Japan). Heart rate responses were presented in absolute values as well as a percentage of the participant's estimated heart rate reserve (% HRR) calculated using the following equation: %HRR=HRt−HRblHRmax−HRbl∙100, where HRt is the heart rate recorded at a given time point (beats/min), HRbl is the heart rate recorded at baseline (beats/min), and HRmax is the participant's maximal heart rate taken from their maximal incremental treadmill exercise protocol during the participant's preliminary screening session.

Urine Specific Gravity

Hydration status was assessed at baseline by measuring urine specific gravity using a hand‐held total solid refractometer (Reichert TS 400 total solids refractometer, Reichert Inc., Depew, NY, USA).

Thermal Sensation

Thermal sensation was recorded at 15‐min intervals throughout exercise and at exercise termination utilizing the modified American Society of Heating, Refrigeration and Air‐Conditioning Engineers scale (0: neutral to 7: extremely hot).

Rating of Perceived Exertion

Rating of perceived exertion was recorded at 15‐min intervals throughout exercise and at exercise termination utilizing the Borg (6–20) scale.

Data Analysis

Minute averages were calculated for all continuous variables (i.e., core temperature, mean skin temperature, and heart rate). All data were blinded and randomized before cleaning using R (Version 4.2.0, R Core Team). The blinded raw data set was then examined to assess completeness and outliers. Missing data and outliers for continuous variables were identified and removed from the data set. Data cleaning was completed in Excel after which the blinded data set was imported into R for unblinding before statistical analysis.

Missing Data

Due to technical difficulties, time to Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was not recorded on one occasion in the 26°C WBGT condition for one older female and for one young female (time to 38°C) in the 29°C WBGT condition, and therefore both were removed from the analysis. Heart rate measurements at 38°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting were not recorded for one young female and one older female in the 32°C WBGT condition. Heart rate data (time to Δ1.0°C above baseline resting) were not recorded for one young male in the 29°C condition, and one older female in the 26°C condition. No perceptual data were recorded in young females in the 32°C WBGT condition at a core temperature of 38.0°C.

Statistical Analysis

To assess the influence of environmental conditions, sex, and age on the time taken to reach a rectal temperature of 38.0°C, and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting, these times were compared between groups (Young Males [reference group], Young Females, Older Males and Older Females), and conditions (26°C WBGT [reference condition], 29°C WBGT and 32°C WBGT) using Cox regression in the ClinicoPath module in Jamovi (v.2.6.13). To evaluate differences in survival distributions across groups and conditions, log‐rank tests were conducted. When assessing responses across environmental conditions, identical methods to those described above were used, accounting for repeated measures within individual using the “survival” [23] package in R [24]. To account for potential effects of physical fitness on safe initial work time, additional analysis identical to the methods described above, incorporating individual V̇O2peak as a continuous covariate were conducted. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Secondary analysis was undertaken to assess whether heart rate (and heart rate reserve), mean skin temperature and perceptual responses at core temperatures of 38.0°C, or Δ1.0°C above baseline resting were influenced by group (Young Males [reference group], Young Females, Older Males and Older Females) or environmental conditions (26°C WBGT [reference condition], 29°C WBGT, and 32°C WBGT) using linear‐mixed effects models, accounting for the individual as a random effect in the GAMLj package [25] in Jamovi.

To estimate the duration of work that could be performed before reaching core temperatures of 38.5°C, 39.0°C, and 39.5°C forecasting simulations were performed utilizing exponential triple smoothing [26]. Linear mixed effects models were utilized to assess the influence of group (Young Males [reference group], Young Females, Older Males and Older Females) on projected work duration, where environmental conditions (26°C WBGT [reference condition], 29°C WBGT, and 32°C WBGT) were included as fixed effects, and the individual was included as a random intercept to account for repeated observations. The final 20‐min before reaching a core temperature of 38.0°C was used for forecasting analysis.

Descriptive analysis was performed to estimate core temperature cooling rates during the first hour of recovery from heavy intensity simulated work, the absolute change in core temperature was divided by duration (i.e., 60 min) to obtain cooling rates in °C/min.

Initial Stay Times

Median [interquartile range] time taken to reach rectal temperatures of 38.0°C and 1°C above baseline resting during continuous heavy‐intensity (260 W/m2) simulated work in ambient conditions of 26°C (A), 29°C (B), and 32°C WBGT (C).

Effect of Varying Environmental Conditions on the Time Taken to Reach a Core Temperature of 38°C, or 1°C Above Baseline Resting

Across all groups, the time taken to reach a core temperature of 38.0°C was significantly influenced by environmental conditions (log‐rank test: p = 0.002, Figure 2A). The median (interquartile range) time to 38°C across groups was 32 (23, 52) min, 29 (23, 47) min and 26 (18, 35) min in the 26°C, 29°C, and 32°C WBGT conditions, respectively, while the rate of experiencing a core temperature of 38.0°C was higher in 32°C WBGT (2.0 [1.5, 2.7], p = 0.001), but not in 29°C WBGT (1.1 [0.9, 1.4], p = 0.342) compared to 26°C WBGT.

Survival curves showing the proportion of participants terminating the 240‐min work bout upon reaching a core temperature of 38.0°C (A) and Δ1.0°C (B) above baseline resting during continuous moderate‐intensity (260 W/m2) simulated work in ambient conditions of 26°C, 29°C and 32°C WBGT.

The time taken to reach a core temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was significantly influenced by environmental conditions (p < 0.0001, Figure 2B). The rate of experiencing a core temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was higher in 32°C WBGT (hazard ratio: 3.8 [95% confidence interval: 2.6, 5.4], p < 0.001), and 29°C WBGT (1.5 [1.1, 2.0], p < 0.001) compared to 26°C WBGT, with the median time to Δ1.0°C above baseline resting across groups being 55 (40, 82) min, 42 (35, 56) min, and 33 (29, 42) min in the 26°C, 29°C, and 32°C WBGT conditions, respectively.

Effect of Sex and Age on the Time Take to Reach a Core Temperature of 38°C, or 1°C Above Baseline Resting

Across all environmental conditions, the time taken for participants to reach 38.0°C rectal temperature was significantly influenced by group (p < 0.001). The rate of experiencing a core temperature of 38.0°C was higher in older females (2.4 [1.45, 3.88], p = 0.001), but not in young females (1.5 [0.95, 2.44], p = 0.082), or older males (1.0 [0.63, 1.62], p = 0.967) compared to young males.

Similarly, across all environmental conditions, the time taken to reach a core temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was significantly influenced by group (p = 0.004). The rate of experiencing a core temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was higher in older females (2.4 [1.3, 4.3], p = 0.004), but not in young females (1.5 [0.7, 3.3], p = 0.292), or older males (1.0 [0.6, 1.7], p = 0.971) compared to young males.

When accounting for physical fitness within the analysis, no between group differences were evident in the rate of reaching a core temperature of 38.0°C, or Δ1.0°C above baseline resting (all p > 0.052).

When performing simulated heavy‐intensity work in environments of 26°C and 29°C WBGT, the rate of experiencing a core temperature of 38°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was greater in older females compared to young males, but not different between young females, or older males and young males (Table 3). In the 32°C WBGT condition, the rate of experiencing a core temperature of 38°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was not influenced by group.

Heart Rate Responses to Simulated Work

A main effect of group existed for heart rate, such that young males had a higher heart at a rectal temperature of 38.0°C (p < 0.001) and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting (p < 0.001) compared to older males, but not young or older females (both p > 0.257, Table 4). Discussion

We provide the first detailed assessment of the effects of sex and age on the estimated initial stay time for heavy‐intensity work across a range of ambient conditions increasingly experienced by workers. While our findings show that progressively more challenging environmental conditions significantly influence initial stay times (i.e., time to reach core temperatures of 38.0°C or Δ1.0°C above baseline resting) these estimates are similar between young males and females and older males. In contrast, older females experience significantly shorter initial stay times. Our data also show that progressively more challenging environmental conditions elicit heightened physiological strain across all groups but have little effect on the perceptual responses to simulated heavy‐intensity work.

Previous investigations have assessed the efficacy of work‐rest allocations to mitigate excursions in core temperature beyond recommended upper limits of 38.0°C for unacclimated individuals [3, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. Extending on these findings, previous work from our laboratory [4, 5, 6] has provided estimates of the duration of work that can be completed before the implementation of recommended work‐rest schedules during moderate‐intensity work, which have been reaffirmed in the accompanying companion paper [7]. However, to the best of our knowledge, no investigations have provided estimates regarding the duration of work that can be undertaken before the implementation of heat mitigation controls during heavy‐intensity work. This represents a significant shortcoming of current guidance as, dependent on task allocation, heavy‐intensity work can comprise a significant proportion of the work shift [14, 15, 32]. Unsurprisingly, and in accordance with field observations where high‐intensity work‐tasks, and progressively more challenging environmental conditions elicit significant increases in core temperature [14, 15, 33], the duration of continuous, heavy‐intensity work that could be undertaken before reaching the recommended upper core temperature limit of 38.0°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting (or pre‐job resting) was significantly reduced as WBGT increased. Despite this, no differences in the duration of continuous work that could be completed before reaching a rectal temperature of 38.0°C in 29.0°C WBGT compared to the 26°C WBGT condition was observed across groups. In contrast, however, the time taken to reach a rectal temperature of Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was significantly reduced in the 32.0°C and 29.0°C WBGT conditions compared to the 26.0°C WBGT condition. This finding may be reflective of the greater duration of work that was performed before reaching Δ1.0°C above baseline resting, compared to obtaining a core temperature of 38.0°C, wherein greater rates of body heat storage were likely to have been observed in the early stages of exercise before the full activation of heat‐loss mechanisms [34] thus eliciting significant increases in core temperature during the early minutes of simulated work. In turn, this would have translated to the lack of differences in the time taken to reach a rectal temperature of 38.0°C despite increased environmental heat load in the 29°C WBGT condition.

While we show variation in initial stay times across groups (discussed in detail below), this suggests that when the prevailing WBGT is ≤ 29.0°C, the intensity of simulated work being undertaken, as opposed to the environmental conditions alone, was a greater determinant of the likelihood of experiencing excursions in core temperature beyond safe limits. This finding supports the seminal findings of Lind et al. [35], where we highlight the similarity in stay times to 38.0°C between the 26.0°C and 29.0°C WBGT conditions and within previous field investigations that have shown excursions in core temperature in environments that would be typically deemed to be compensable [33]. Similarly, despite this notion not directly being assessed in previous work [5], Notley et al., report similar core temperature and heart rate responses following 60‐min of moderate‐intensity work in ambient conditions of 24°C and 28°C WBGT (representative of conditions below and above the ACGIH ALs, respectively) in healthy young (18‐30 years) males, and older (50‐70 years) males with and without common chronic health conditions (i.e., type 2 diabetes, hypertension). In support, our data show that, across groups, heart rate reserve at rectal temperatures of 38.0°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was not different between the 26.0°C and 29.0°C WBGT conditions but was significantly increased in the 32.0°C condition compared to the 26.0°C WBGT condition. Together suggesting that, despite an increased level of environmental heat stress in the 29.0°C WBGT condition compared to the 26.0°C WBGT condition, participants experienced similar level of physiological strain in temperatures ≤ 29.0°C WBGT. In accordance with our findings, where we show significant reductions in the duration of continuous work that can be completed in the 32.0°C WBGT condition, Notley et al. [5], albeit during moderate‐intensity simulated work, showed marked increases in core temperature and heart rate following 60‐min of work under these same environmental conditions. Together, highlighting the thermal challenge posed by working under such conditions. Whether these findings remain consistent when assessed following the implementation of multiple work‐rest cycles following the initial stay time as applied in the initial morning work period, in the later stages of the work shift (e.g., post‐lunch work period) and on the next workday presents as an important area of consideration.

As our prior laboratory‐based work showed, the recommended upper limits may under‐protect older individuals [3] who, relative to young males, have a reduced heat‐loss capacity leading to unsafe increases in core temperature (> 38.0°C) during exercise in the heat. Irrespective of age (18‐70 years), whole‐body heat loss, which is mediated by increases in sweating and skin blood flow is ~5% lower in females relative to males, declining by ~3%–4% per decade in both males and females [12]. Thus, relative to young adults, older adults would be expected to experience greater increases in core temperature for the same metabolic rate (and thus work effort), which is potentially worse in females. These differences would be expected to widen as the intensity of work increases given that both sex‐ and age‐related differences in heat‐loss capacity have been shown to be heat‐load dependent [10, 12, 36]. Notably, however, previous work showed that initial stay times are not different between young‐to‐middle‐aged males (19–45 years) and females (18–43 years) and young (18–30 years) and older (50–70 years) males during moderate‐intensity work [4, 5, 6]. In support of these seminal investigations [4, 5, 6], as overviewed in our companion paper [7], we show no difference in the initial stay time between these population groups. In the present study, we extend upon this previous work by showing no differences amongst groups for heavy‐intensity work. Importantly, we provide the first estimates of the initial stay time for older females undertaking moderate‐ (see companion paper [7]) and heavy‐intensity continuous work in hot environments.

In the context of heavy‐intensity work, our findings highlight that older females experience significant reductions in the amount of continuous work that can be performed before experiencing core temperature excursions beyond 38.0°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting compared to young males. Despite sex‐ and age‐specific normative fitness not varying between groups, older females had significantly lower absolute V̇O2peak compared to all other groups. However, when accounting for V̇O2peak within secondary analysis, main effects of group ceased to exist, suggesting that physical fitness was a key modulator of the duration of work that was permissible before reaching core temperature thresholds of 38.0°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting. This finding is unsurprising given the known relation between increased physical fitness and heat loss capacity [10, 37, 38], and suggests that delays in the activation and magnitude of heat loss mechanisms of skin blood flow and sweating [16, 39, 40, 41, 42], an effect that is likely exacerbated in females [43, 44], has likely led older females to experience an increased rate of heat storage throughout the duration of exercise and thus significantly shorter initial stay times.

While our data show that cardiovascular strain, as indexed by heart rate reserve at core temperatures of 38.0°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting was not influenced by group, thus indicating similar levels of physiological strain (discussed above), it is important to note the significant variation observed when assessing absolute heart rate responses (i.e., as expressed in beats/min). When considering the practical application of this study, where those in the field are unlikely to be able to accurately quantify heart rate reserve (i.e., workers will not have completed maximal exercise tests to volitional fatigue), heart rate measures can be used. This scenario necessitates an understanding of factors that will limit the magnitude of absolute increases in heart rate (i.e., age), and while heart rate cannot be used as a surrogate marker of core temperature, our investigation provides the first estimates of heart rate responses at corresponding core temperatures of 38.0°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting in young and older males and females across a range of environmentally challenging work conditions.

In situations where physiological monitoring is not possible, obtaining perceptual responses to given work‐tasks have been suggested to provide an accurate estimate of physiological strain [45]. While our data show no group level differences in perception of effort or thermal sensation at rectal temperatures of 38.0°C and Δ1.0°C above baseline resting, it is important to note that we also largely failed to observe an effect of the increasing environmental conditions. This finding was observed despite increases in heart rate, and heart rate reserve as ambient conditions increased, and further highlights incongruencies in how individuals perceive their actual level of physiological strain [46, 47, 48].

There are several factors that may limit workplace utilization of industry‐guided work‐rest schedules to mitigate potentially dangerous increases in physiological strain, these include scenarios where due to job and time constraints, emergency work (e.g., repair of downed power lines following severe storm) or other factors, workers are unable to implement appropriate heat‐mitigation measures [14, 19, 49]. In an effort to increase safety during these physically challenging work scenarios, we provide estimates of the duration of work that can be completed before reaching core temperatures of 38.5°C and 39.0°C, for heavy‐intensity work in a range of ambient conditions (Table 6). In situations where it is not possible to implement industry‐guided work‐rest schedules, workforces can use these projected preliminary estimates to establish an informed work plan incorporating the median and IQR estimates to given core temperature thresholds, ensuring individuals remain safe. However, it must be stressed that performing heavy work in the heat will result in elevated levels of physiological strain and fatigue that can impact worker productivity and safety [50, 51]; a response which would be exacerbated in older individuals and individuals with reduced fitness [52, 53, 54]. By anticipating the changes in core temperature that individuals may experience when work is extended beyond initial stay times, workplaces can provide work‐site support and monitoring to ensure that workers remain healthy and safe while performing physically demanding tasks in the heat.

In this context, the following section outlines recommended steps individuals and workplaces should implement when extending duration of heavy, continuous work beyond initial stay times. As in the case of our recommendations for moderate‐intensity work, work may continue following recommended initial stay times (i.e., to a core temperature of 38°C; equivalent to a 1°C above pre‐job resting core temperature of ≤ 37.5°C [2]) employing the recommended ACGIH work‐rest schedules. However, for heavy‐intensity work under the ambient conditions assessed, individuals may continue performing work at a WBGT of 26°C while implementing the recommended work‐rest allocations of 25‐50% for the duration of the 4‐h work period while supervised. While no further work is recommended at 29°C and 32°C WBGT, work could resume following an extended recovery to permit core temperature recovery to or near baseline resting levels (i.e., ~75–90 min, which is based on an estimated core temperature cooling rate of 0.012°C for a passive resting recovery following work in the heat) [55]. However, it is recommended that work be limited to a non‐heat stress environment for the duration of the 4‐h work period. In all cases, if work is resumed, individuals should be supervised while continuing to hydrate.

In situations where extending the duration of continuous work would lead to an estimated increase in core temperature of 38.5°C, it is recommended that individuals recover in a cooled space (e.g., 22°C and relative humidity of 45%; equivalent WBGT of ~17°C) for a duration of 90‐105 min following cessation of work. Of note, in the present study, we observed an average core temperature cooling rate of ~0.013°C·min‐1 across the first hour of recovery (Table 5). This rate aligns with findings from a recent systematic review, which also reports an average core cooling rate of 0.012°C·min‐1 for a passive recovery following work in the heat, resulting in an increase in core temperature of up to ~39.0°C [55]. However, while a similar recovery time is recommended for moderate‐intensity work (i.e., 90‐105 min [7]), a longer recovery is advised to not only ensure a reduction in core temperature to pre‐job resting levels (< 37.5°C [2]) but to also permit individuals to recover from physical fatigue associated with heavy‐intensity work [53, 54, 56]. A longer recovery should be considered for older workers and or individuals with a reduced level of fitness [54]. In all situations, workers should avoid any work during recovery. Thereafter, while remaining in a cooled space, they may perform light duties (e.g., desk work) where prolonged standing is limited.

When duration of work is projected to increase core temperature to 39°C, workers should be monitored closely. While a buddy system can be implemented where workers monitor each other for signs of heat‐related illness, training on recognizing symptoms would be necessary. Recovery in a cooled space should be extended to 105‐120 min. While return to duty is not advised thereafter, especially for older workers or individuals with a reduced level of fitness, individuals may perform light desk duties in a cooled space for the duration of the 4‐h work period under supervision. Given that performing physically demanding work can place workers at an elevated risk of occupational injury and heat‐related illnesses [53, 54, 56, 57], it is important that workers hydrate regularly and are monitored throughout the recovery period for signs of heat‐related illness especially before departing the work site. While we provide work durations that are projected to increase core temperature to 39.5°C, as in the case for moderate‐intensity work [7], extending work duration beyond 39°C is not recommended. The projected times for a core temperature of 39.5°C should only serve to assist individuals and workplaces identify durations of continuous work that would result in intolerable levels of heat strain and that could place individuals at an elevated risk of work‐ and heat‐related injuries if work was to continue [19, 58]. Work of this duration should be avoided at all costs unless additional heat mitigation practices, such as the use of wearable cooling garments are employed to mitigate dangerous increases in core temperature [59] and workers are monitored closely.

Limitations

While we present the first estimates of the duration of work that can be completed before the implementation of industry guided work‐rest allocations during heavy‐intensity simulated work in a range of environmental conditions, we acknowledge our work is not without limitation. First, our study was laboratory‐based, where participants walked continuously on a treadmill at a fixed‐rate of metabolic heat production. While most lab‐based protocols assessing the efficacy of work‐rest allocations to limit dangerous excursions in core temperature employ strict, fixed work intensities, this modality of exercise likely does not reflect the intermittent nature of fieldwork, where workers can often modify behavior to limit metabolic heat production. Second, participants wore standardized attire (single layer cotton overalls, ~1 Clo) during the current investigation, and thus safe work times for individuals wearing more cumbersome work attire remain unknown. Third, participants within our trial were not heat acclimatized and thus the initial stay times observed may not reflect those observed in individuals who are accustomed to performing arduous work in hot environments. Fourth, our findings are limited to a singular work bout, and thus it remains unclear if exacerbations in thermal strain during consecutive days of work in the heat [49] influence the validity of our safe initial stay time estimates. Fifth, while we recommend that work‐rest allocations may be applied following initial stay times, which is supported by recent preliminary findings [7], the verification of initial stay times in combination with industry‐guided work‐rest allocations in field‐based settings is required. Sixth, while guidance for recovery following extended periods of continuous work have been provided, these times have not been validated. It is recommended that further research be conducted to optimize work–rest schedules when a prolonged work bout must be performed at the start of a work shift. Seventh, an a priori power calculation was not conducted; therefore, the analyses may be underpowered to detect some group differences. Age was categorized into two distinct groups: young adults (18–30 years) and older adults (50–69 years), to align with known age‐related differences in thermoregulatory function and facilitate meaningful group comparisons. As a result, our findings do not capture thermoregulatory differences across the full adult lifespan. It is important to note that our results reflect the specific individuals within our cohorts, and both within‐ and between‐group variability in heat tolerance was observed (Table 6). Our findings should be interpreted with these points in mind, considering that the information provided should be used to promote informed decision‐making, and not as a replacement for the clinical assessment of heat stress within the workplace.

Conclusion

We provide the first estimates of the duration of safe work that can be performed before the implementation of industry‐guided work‐rest allocations during heavy‐intensity simulated work in very warm to hot environmental conditions. We propose these preliminary estimates for use in conjunction with work‐rest allocations to optimize worker productivity without compromising safety. The utility of these work estimates becomes apparent when considering the divergent safe work times, dependent on age, sex, and the environment that work is being undertaken within. The information presented in this document is intended to serve as a guidance tool to promote informed decision making and should not replace the clinical assessment or advice of an on‐site occupational health specialist. Users should always verify information with appropriate authorities and seek professional advice for specific situations.
