Recombinant Tissue Plasminogen Activator-assisted Early Drainage of Suprachoroidal Hemorrhage
Authors: Saurabh Verma, Shorya Vardhan Azad, Hemlata Udenia, Akshaya Balaji, Tanuj Dada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA middle-aged male patient with bilateral advanced glaucoma, who had undergone trabeculectomy, developed a suprachoroidal hemorrhage (SCH) on post-operative day 1. Ultrasound (USG) confirmed the presence of appositional choroidal mounds with clotted blood in the suprachoroidal space. The patient underwent recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rtPA) injection into the suprachoroidal space in the first stage, followed by trocar-assisted drainage 5 hours later. This report aims to highlight the effectiveness of rtPA in aiding the lysis and subsequent drainage of clotted blood from the suprachoroidal space. This technique is especially useful when early drainage of SCH is being planned because of appositional/kissing choroidals or uncontrolled intraocular pressure (IOP), especially in one-eyed patients.

Introduction

Suprachoroidal hemorrhage (SCH) is a rare yet devastating complication of ocular surgeries. Glaucoma surgeries are the most prevalent cause of SCH, with an incidence ranging from 0.6% to 1.4% after trabeculectomy and 1.2% to 2.7% after glaucoma drainage device implantation.1 Sudden hypotony secondary to surgery is considered to be the biggest contributing factor. The surgical management of post-glaucoma surgery SCH entails drainage of blood accumulated between the sclera and the choroid; however, early drainage of clotted blood from the suprachoroidal space is quite difficult because of clotting of blood, and therefore a waiting period of 10–14 days is generally recommended to allow time for clot lysis before drainage.2 However, delaying drainage can lead to poor visual outcomes, especially in glaucomatous eyes, which may not tolerate high intraocular pressure (IOP), or in eyes with retinal apposition due to kissing choroidals. Injecting recombinant tissue plasminogen activator (rtPA) into the suprachoroidal space prior to drainage can aid in accelerating clot lysis, subsequently facilitating early and complete drainage of suprachoroidal blood.3

Case Description

A middle-aged male presented with a history of bilateral progressive diminution of vision. Both eyes had undergone cataract surgery about 10 years ago with poor gain in vision. At the time of presentation, the right eye (R/E) was aphakic, and the left eye (L/E) had complicated pseudophakia with a superiorly subluxated intraocular lens. His best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and IOP were hand movement close to face (HMCF) and 32 mm Hg, respectively, in the R/E, and 6/60 and 30 mm Hg, respectively, in the L/E. The patient was receiving maximum topical medications in both eyes. The R/E had total cupping, and the L/E had a 0.9 vertical cup-disk ratio. He underwent an uneventful trabeculectomy with mitomycin C in the R/E. On post-operative day 1, he was diagnosed with SCH with high IOP.

Ultrasound confirmed the presence of appositional choroidal mounds, with the height of choroidal detachment being highest in the superotemporal and inferotemporal quadrants. USG also showed that the blood in the suprachoroidal space was largely clotted. Visual acuity had dropped to perception of light, and IOP was 38 mm Hg.

After thorough discussion of potential complications and obtaining written informed consent for both the procedure and its potential publication, the patient underwent a two-stage surgical procedure for management. In the first stage, 0.1 mL (100 µg) of rtPA was injected into the suprachoroidal space in the superotemporal and inferotemporal quadrants, approximately 8 mm from the limbus. Paracentesis was performed prior to and between the two injections to reduce IOP and ensure that the drug remained in the suprachoroidal space. Five hours later, USG was repeated to confirm increased lysis, and suprachoroidal drainage was performed (Fig. 1). An anterior chamber maintainer was secured, and infusion pressure was kept at 40 mm Hg. A 23 G nonvalved trocar-cannula was inserted about 8 mm behind the limbus into the suprachoroidal space in the inferotemporal quadrant. Assisted by positive pressure maintained with the anterior chamber maintainer, dark red, liquefied blood could be seen egressing from the cannula (Fig. 2). The same procedure was then repeated in the superotemporal quadrant. At the end of the procedure, indirect ophthalmoscopic examination was performed to rule out any retinal breaks. The patient maintained perception of light vision, with an IOP ranging between 10 and 12 mm Hg over a 6-month observation period. Persistent shallow choroidal detachment was observed in all quadrants at the 4-week follow-up examination. However, it completely resolved within the 2nd month of follow-up, demonstrating anatomical success of the procedure.

Discussion

Suprachoroidal hemorrhage drainage can be performed with a posterior sclerotomy or a trocar-cannula-assisted technique. Early drainage is difficult, as the blood is usually clotted, and traditionally, a waiting period of 10–14 days is required for the blood to liquefy before drainage is performed.2 The presence of appositional SCH, however, warrants early drainage, as retina-retinal adhesions may form as early as 3 days, resulting in an increased risk of retinal detachment and poor outcomes.4 Recombinant tPA can accelerate clot lysis and facilitate drainage. While its effect starts within minutes, a few hours are required to adequately lyse the clots, especially because of the large size of the clots in SCH. We decided to perform ultrasonography just prior to rtPA injection and then repeat it after 5 hours to confirm clot lysis, which was seen as reduced amplitude of spikes in the suprachoroidal space. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to document clot lysis on serial ultrasonography before drainage. In our case, based on doses used in previously reported similar cases, a total of 100 µg of rtPA was injected into two quadrants to maximize its spread and contact with clots.3 Injection of a higher dose of rtPA was avoided because of the possible risk of rebleeding and retinal toxicity.5 Continuous anterior chamber infusion was used to provide positive pressure during the procedure, and drainage was performed in the same quadrants where rtPA was previously injected, as clot lysis was observable only in these quadrants.

Conclusion

The rtPA-augmented and USG-guided early drainage of SCH can be recommended in cases of posttrabeculectomy SCH with medically uncontrolled IOP and appositional choroidals.
