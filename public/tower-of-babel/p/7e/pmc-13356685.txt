Multi-omics integration deciphers arsenic-induced multi-organ toxicity and the novel ferroptosis axis
Authors: XingLai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic arsenic exposure threatens over 200 million people worldwide and induces multi-organ injury, yet the panoramic molecular reprogramming across organs remains incompletely understood, and traditional single-omics approaches fail to capture cross-level and cross-organ regulatory associations. This review systematically integrates evidence from global epidemiology to single-cell spatial omics, tracing the evolution of multi-omics technologies—from single-platform profiling to data fusion strategies such as coupled matrix factorization (CMF) and the DIABLO framework, two complementary multi-omics integration approaches—and to cutting-edge spatial transcriptomics. We highlight ferroptosis as a common mechanism in arsenic-induced multi-organ injury. At the molecular level, we propose a mechanistic model wherein arsenic (AsIII) disrupts selenium metabolism by inhibiting Sec-tRNASec synthesis, thereby impairing selenoprotein (especially GPX4) biosynthesis, a paradigm distinct from the traditional “ROS burst → lipid peroxidation” theory. Supporting evidence includes reduced 75Se incorporation into cellular RNA, genetic deletion of PRDX6 exacerbating ferroptosis, and rescue by selenium supplementation via Nrf2 activation. At the organ level, we compare toxicity features and propose that tissue-intrinsic ferroptosis thresholds—determined by iron content, PUFA-phospholipid composition, GSH reserves, GPX4 redundancy, and selenium availability—govern differential organ susceptibility; the brain shows extreme vulnerability, whereas the liver exhibits relative resistance. Emerging spatial omics further reveals elevated arsenic-responsive gene expression in tumor-adjacent regions. This systems toxicology paradigm offers mechanistic grounding for combinatorial biomarker panels and genotype-guided precision selenium supplementation, although definitive causal validation through tissue-specific Gpx4 knockout or Sec-tRNASec rescue experiments remains a critical next step. We also discuss prospects for AI-driven toxicity prediction models.Keywords: Arsenic exposure, Multi-omics integration, Ferroptosis, Selenium metabolism disruption, GPX4, Organ-specific toxicity, Spatial omics, Machine learning

Introduction

Arsenic is classified as a Group 1 human carcinogen by the IARC [1]. Globally, an estimated 94–220 million people are chronically exposed through arsenic-contaminated groundwater, affecting India, Bangladesh, China, the United States, and many other regions [2]. Endemic arsenicosis caused by coal burning in Guizhou and Shaanxi provinces of China presents a unique environmental health challenge [3]. Epidemiological studies consistently link chronic arsenic exposure to elevated risks of skin, lung, and bladder cancers, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and neurodegenerative disorders [4], [5], [6].

A central scientific challenge persists: although population-level health risks are well established, the panoramic molecular landscape through which arsenic reprograms multi-organ networks remains obscure. Traditional toxicology often focuses on single organs or pathways (e.g., oxidative stress, apoptosis). However, arsenic-induced perturbations actually constitute a multidimensional network driven synergistically by epigenetic reprogramming, metabolic disruption, and elemental homeostasis imbalance [7], [8], [9]. Single-level omics approaches are akin to “blind men feeling an elephant”—they fail to capture cross-regulatory causal relationships and mask organ-specific toxicity heterogeneity [10].

Multi-omics integration technologies offer new possibilities. Beyond cataloguing differential molecules, advanced algorithms such as coupled matrix factorization (CMF) and DIABLO construct cross-level regulatory networks to identify molecular “fingerprints” of arsenic exposure [11], [12]. Spatial omics further enables in situ dissection of toxicity heterogeneity [13], while machine learning and Mendelian randomization provide tools for key target identification and causal inference [14], [15].

Arsenic speciation matters. Arsenic toxicity is species-dependent. Inorganic arsenite (AsIII) is the most potent inducer of ferroptosis due to its high affinity for protein thiols and direct inhibition of the thioredoxin and glutathione systems. Arsenate (AsV) requires reduction to AsIII to exert similar effects, resulting in a delayed oxidative stress response [16]. Trivalent methylated metabolites, monomethylarsonous acid (MMAIII) and dimethylarsinous acid (DMAIII), are highly reactive and specifically implicated in renal and bladder toxicity [17]. Different cell types display distinct capacities for arsenic uptake and methylation—renal cells and hepatocytes can metabolize AsIII to DMAV and MMAV, whereas cardiomyocytes can only detect AsIII and AsV, contributing to organ-specific toxicity patterns [18].

Regarding the selenium–GPX4–ferroptosis axis, the inhibitory potency varies substantially across arsenic species. AsIII is the most direct inhibitor of selenoprotein biosynthesis because its high reactivity with vicinal thiols enables it to disrupt the selenocysteine insertion machinery—including Sec-tRNASec synthesis—at low micromolar concentrations. AsV, as a pentavalent species, must undergo intracellular reduction to AsIII before exerting similar effects, resulting in a kinetic delay that may nonetheless sustain prolonged inhibition in tissues with high reducing capacity, such as the liver. The trivalent methylated metabolites MMAIII and DMAIII are highly reactive intermediates that have not been directly studied for their effects on Sec-tRNASec synthesis. However, their established ability to inhibit thioredoxin reductase and induce oxidative DNA damage suggests that they may exacerbate ferroptosis through parallel pathways that are independent of direct selenium disruption. These species-specific differences carry important interpretive implications: most in vitro evidence supporting the selenium–GPX4 axis has been generated using NaAsO₂ (AsIII) exposure, and the extent to which other environmentally relevant arsenic species engage this pathway remains an open question requiring systematic investigation. Unless otherwise specified, the studies discussed here predominantly used sodium arsenite (NaAsO₂); cases using other species are explicitly noted.

This review follows the thread of “evolution and application of multi-omics technologies” to construct a closed-loop logic from population-level observations to molecular mechanisms and back to clinical applications. The proposed mechanistic model is schematically summarized in Fig. 1.

Evolution of multi-omics technologies

Enrichment of the data layer

Early arsenic toxicity studies relied on genomics and transcriptomics to infer mechanisms from average gene expression changes. With the advancement of mass spectrometry, proteomics and metabolomics have been widely applied. SWATH-MS-based proteomics identified 172 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) in arsenic-exposed rat livers, mainly involved in lipid metabolism, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, and apoptosis [16]. Untargeted metabolomics revealed 164 differential metabolites, highlighting disruptions in nicotinamide metabolism, steroid hormone biosynthesis, and porphyrin metabolism [17]. Ionomics further showed significantly elevated levels of As, Cd, Hg, and several other elements [17].

However, tissue-homogenate “bulk” omics mask cellular heterogeneity and spatial information. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) can distinguish arsenic effects on specific cell subpopulations; for example, arsenic exposure induces metabolic reprogramming in hepatocyte subpopulations and M1 polarization of Kupffer cells [18]. A landmark study by Chaturvedi et al. employed single-nuclei RNA-seq across the entire Drosophila brain and revealed that arsenic-induced transcriptional responses exhibit widespread sex-specific and sex-antagonistic modulation—gene changes in females biased toward neurons, in males toward glia—yet converged on similar biological pathways [19]. This study enabled translation of arsenic-induced transcriptomic changes to orthologous human neurogenetic networks.

More notably, spatial omics enables molecular mapping in situ. Singhal et al. combined multiplex FISH with AI-powered digital pathology (HoverNet-based nuclear segmentation) to characterize, at single-cell resolution, the spatial expression of a validated three-gene arsenic-responsive risk model (NKIRAS2, AKTIP, HLA-DQA1) in bladder cancer tissue [13]. The model achieved 94% training AUC and 75% validation AUC. Spatial profiling revealed elevated expression scores in tumor-adjacent regions (within 500 µm of the tumor edge), strongly correlating with tumor grade (r = 0.83). Tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) density was inversely correlated with tumor grade, suggesting immune exclusion. This finding indicates that arsenic may promote cancer progression by remodeling the tumor microenvironment—altering gene expression in peritumoral stromal and immune cells, information invisible to homogenate sequencing.

Leap in the analytical layer: from pathway-level intersection to cross-omics data fusion

Traditional joint pathway analysis (JPA) maps differential proteins and metabolites onto KEGG pathways and identifies pathways with cross-level alterations. In arsenic-induced splenic immune injury, JPA revealed 80 co-enriched pathways, 16 defined as critical [20]. However, JPA only performs pathway-level intersection and cannot simultaneously model complex multi-matrix relationships or handle distribution differences across data types [21]. To overcome these limitations, two complementary data fusion strategies have been adopted: supervised DIABLO and unsupervised coupled matrix factorization (CMF).

The DIABLO framework (mixOmics R package) maximizes covariance among transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics datasets while discriminating phenotypic groups. The optimal number of components is determined by 5-fold cross-validation; features with |loading| > 0.7 are considered key drivers. For instance, DIABLO integration of liver multi-omics data from arsenic-exposed models identified a latent component loaded by genes/proteins in glutathione metabolism (Gclc, Gss, Gpx4), selenoprotein synthesis (Eefsec, Sepsecs), and ferroptosis (Acsl4, Lpcat3), along with decreased GSH and increased 4-HNE (Fig. 2). GPX4 protein abundance emerged as tightly linked to the selenocysteine-tRNA charging factor Eefsec and lipid peroxidation product 4-HNE, establishing a cross-level regulatory hub centered on selenium utilization—invisible from single-omics analyses.

Cross-omics regulatory network centered on GPX4. DIABLO integration of liver transcriptome, proteome, and metabolome data reveals GPX4 as the central hub connecting selenoprotein synthesis, glutathione metabolism, ferroptosis drivers, and lipid peroxidation products.

Coupled matrix factorization (CMF) acts as a “translator” that extracts shared latent factors from multiple data matrices (e.g., DNA methylation, RNA-seq, metabolomics). Suthahar et al. (preprint, bioRxiv; not yet peer-reviewed) applied the PARAFAC2-AOADMM CMF framework to arsenic-exposed mouse cells and identified synergistic epigenetic-transcriptional-metabolic changes associated with inhibition of cell differentiation and metabolic reprogramming [22]. Regularization constraints (L1/L2 penalties) control overfitting in high-dimensional, small-sample toxicological data [23], marking a paradigm shift from “validating known pathways” to “discovering novel regulatory networks.”.

The key distinction: DIABLO is supervised (identifies signatures separating known groups), while CMF is unsupervised (discovers latent structures without prior labels). Both are complementary—CMF for hypothesis generation, DIABLO for hypothesis-driven validation and biomarker identification.

Mendelian randomization (MR) uses genetic variants as instrumental variables to infer causal relationships, circumventing confounding [24]. A recent MR-multi-omics study integrated summary-data MR, scRNA-seq, and protein-protein interaction networks to reveal causal networks of arsenic-related genes in type 2 diabetes [25]. If future MR analyses support the chain “arsenic exposure → gene methylation → GPX4 reduction → ferroptosis,” it would strengthen evidence for subsequent interventions.

Multi-omics data integration workflow

To achieve genuine data fusion rather than parallel listing, we have established a standardized multi-omics integration workflow (Fig. 3):

Multi-omics data integration workflow. Stage 1: Single-omics preprocessing and differential analysis. Stage 2: Cross-omics data fusion via DIABLO and CMF. Stage 3: Network construction and hub identification. Stage 4: Joint pathway enrichment. Stage 5: Experimental validation.

Stage 1: Single-omics preprocessing and differential analysis. Raw data (RNA-seq, SWATH-MS/DIA proteomics, LC-MS/GC-MS metabolomics) are preprocessed independently. Transcriptomic reads are aligned with STAR and differentially expressed genes identified with DESeq2 (v1.40). Proteomic spectra are searched with OpenSWATH/Spectronaut and normalized by total ion current; metabolomic features are extracted by XCMS and corrected for signal drift using statTarget [26]. After quality control (removal of features with >50% missing, KNN imputation) and batch effect correction (ComBat), differential features are identified at adjusted P < 0.05 and |log₂(fold change)| > 1.

Stage 2: Cross-omics data fusion. (i) DIABLO (mixOmics v6.20) – supervised; (ii) CMF (PARAFAC2-AOADMM) – unsupervised; regularization applied to control overfitting.

Stage 3: Network construction and hub identification. Features with |loading| > 0.7 or top latent factor contributions are used to build multi-omics correlation networks. Hubs with high cross-omics connectivity (e.g., GPX4) are identified (Fig. 2).

Stage 4: Pathway enrichment and biological interpretation. Cross-omics features are mapped to KEGG and Reactome for joint pathway enrichment. Pathways significantly enriched in ≥ 2 omics layers are prioritized.

Stage 5: Validation. Key findings are validated by targeted proteomics (MRM/PRM), Western blot, and functional assays (GPX4 activity, lipid peroxidation quantification).

Multi-omics insights into arsenic-induced multi-organ toxicity

Ferroptosis as a common convergent mechanism

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent regulated cell death driven by lethal lipid peroxidation, distinct from apoptosis, necroptosis, and autophagy [27]. Its core regulatory pillars are iron metabolism, lipid peroxidation pathways, and antioxidant defenses—particularly the GSH–GPX4 axis. Converging multi-omics evidence indicates that ferroptosis is a common mechanism across multiple organs.

Liver. Oral exposure to arsenic-contaminated soil triggers hepatic iron dyshomeostasis and glutathione disruption, with significant reductions in SLC7A11 and GPX4 [28].

Kidney. Combined As–Cd co-exposure synergistically drives ferroptosis in human proximal tubular cells by weakening the GPX4-xCT axis, increasing iron availability, and remodeling the membrane PUFA pool [29].

Lung. Chronic arsenic exposure induces pulmonary epithelial ferroptosis via GPX4 suppression, lipid peroxidation (elevated MDA and 4-HNE), and GSH depletion—the first demonstration of ferroptosis as a key mechanism in arsenic-induced lung epithelial damage using 14-month murine and 30-passage cellular models [30]. Multi-omics further revealed gut microbiota dysbiosis and lipid metabolism disorder contributing through gut-lung axis disruption [30]. GPX4 was downregulated by > 60%, and the pro-ferroptosis factor ACSL4 was significantly upregulated.

Vascular endothelium. Cai et al. demonstrated that arsenic exposure induces ferroptosis-related cell death in mouse aortic endothelial cells, accompanied by impaired redox homeostasis, low selenium status, and decreased selenoproteome including GPX4. Methylseleninic acid (MSA) attenuated these effects via Nrf2 activation and upregulation of three GPX4 isoforms—effects abrogated by the Nrf2 antagonist ML385 [31]. This provides novel evidence for the Se-Nrf2/GPX4 axis in arsenic-induced endothelial dysfunction.

Disruption of selenium metabolism: a mechanistic breakthrough

Traditional views posit “ROS burst → lipid peroxidation,” but fail to explain the specific inhibition of GPX4. Li et al. revealed that arsenite (AsIII) disrupts selenium metabolism by interfering with selenium incorporation into cellular RNA, indicating inhibition of Sec-tRNASec synthesis—a rate-limiting step in selenoprotein biosynthesis [32]. This was demonstrated by reduced 75Se incorporation into RNA. By comparing different Se sources (selenoprotein P, selenocysteine, selenite), they determined that AsIII primarily interferes with Se metabolism downstream of selenite. Genetic deletion of PRDX6—a regulator of cellular selenium metabolism—further suppressed selenoprotein expression and exacerbated AsIII-induced ferroptosis, providing independent genetic evidence [32].

Since GPX4 is a core selenoprotein requiring Sec-tRNASec for selenocysteine insertion, arsenic-induced disruption of selenium metabolism directly decreases GPX4 protein levels and sensitizes cells to ferroptosis. Selenium supplementation restores GPX4 levels; MSA elevates the Nrf2-GPX4 axis and relieves arsenic-induced endothelial dysfunction and ferroptosis in both in vivo and in vitro models [31]. Notably, MSA upregulated three GPX4 isoforms and its effects were abolished by ML385, confirming Nrf2 as a critical mediator.

Collectively, these findings support the mechanistic model:

AsIII exposure → Disruption of selenium metabolism → Inhibition of Sec-tRNASec synthesis → Impaired GPX4 biosynthesis → Lipid peroxidation accumulation → Ferroptosis. Table 1 systematically catalogs the supporting evidence for each step, along with its strengths and limitations.

Limitations. This mechanistic axis is supported by convergent biochemical, genetic, and pharmacological evidence, but definitive causal validation—such as tissue-specific Gpx4 conditional knockout or Sec-tRNASec rescue experiments—has not been reported in chronic arsenic exposure contexts. One piece of supporting evidence is a preprint and has been labeled accordingly. Future genetic model studies are essential.

Mitochondrial dysfunction as an upstream trigger for ferroptosis

Proteomics consistently shows that arsenic exposure broadly inhibits mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) complexes I and II across liver, brain, lung, and heart, leading to ROS burst that fuels lipid peroxidation [16], [17], [33], [34]. Arsenic upregulates UBXD8, promoting DRP1 translocation to mitochondria and phosphorylation at Ser616, resulting in excessive mitochondrial fission and subsequent PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy [35].

Arsenic-induced DRP1-mediated mitochondrial fission not only fragments the mitochondrial network but also generates excessive mitochondrial ROS, which directly oxidizes membrane PUFAs and provides the initial lipid peroxide pool for ferroptosis initiation. Mitophagy exhibits a “double-edged sword” effect: moderate mitophagy clears damaged mitochondria, but sustained activation leads to reduced mitochondrial mass and energetic collapse. This energetic failure cripples ATP-dependent GPX4 synthesis (ATP is required for selenocysteine tRNA charging and translation), creating a vicious cycle that amplifies ferroptotic death [36]. The systematic breakdown of mitochondrial quality control—ETC inhibition, excessive fission, and dysregulated mitophagy—thus provides ideal conditions for ferroptosis.

Organ-specific ferroptosis sensitivity radar chart comparing iron content, PUFA-phospholipid composition, GSH levels, GPX4 expression, and selenium availability across organs.

Liver. The primary arsenic-metabolizing organ displays NAFLD driven by PPAR signaling and purine metabolism disruption, along with perturbed steroid hormone biosynthesis [14], [17]. Relative resistance basis: High basal GSH (8–10 mM), robust GPX4 expression, and efficient selenium storage/recycling (SEPHS2, selenoprotein P) buffer initial ferroptotic stress. However, the liver‘s high-lipid microenvironment and PUFA-enriched phospholipids partially offset this protection, making it susceptible at higher arsenic doses.

Kidney. As the main excretion route, the kidney shows upregulation of proximal tubule bicarbonate transport proteins (Atp1b1, Slc4a4) [37]. High sensitivity basis: The kidney concentrates methylated arsenic metabolites (MMA, DMA); its lipid profile is rich in arachidonic- and adrenic acid-containing phospholipids, preferred substrates for lipid peroxidation, and it has lower GSH reserve than the liver. Combined As–Cd co-exposure further weakens the GPX4-xCT axis and remodels the membrane PUFA pool [29].

Brain. Specificity stems from the blood-brain barrier and selective regional vulnerability (e.g., substantia nigra). Arsenic exposure has been shown to induce degeneration of nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons through inhibition of mitochondrial complexes, contributing to Parkinson’s disease-like pathology [38]. Single-nuclei RNA-seq further showed sex-dimorphic neuronal vs. glial responses [19]. Extreme sensitivity basis: Minimal GPX4 redundancy; extreme enrichment of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, >30% of neuronal membrane phospholipids)—a prime peroxidation substrate with six double bonds; restrictive blood-brain barrier limiting selenium import; and high iron content in the substantia nigra [19], [38]. These factors collectively create the lowest ferroptosis threshold among all organs.

Lung. Injury is closely linked to gut-lung axis disruption. GPX4 is suppressed > 60%, with elevated MDA and 4-HNE [30]. Arsenic downregulates TXNL1 via the DNMT1-USP10-TXNL1 axis, causing ROS accumulation and DNA damage, driving malignant transformation of bronchial epithelial cells [39]. Gut microbiota dysbiosis and systemic lipid metabolism disorder contribute to the heightened ferroptosis susceptibility.

Heart. Environmentally relevant arsenic doses impair TCA cycle enzymes (citrate synthase, succinate dehydrogenase), leading to cardiomyocyte senescence and energy disruption [36]. Population studies show earlier heart transplantation with higher myocardial accumulation of Al, As, Cd, and Pb [40]. Sensitivity basis: High mitochondrial density, reliance on fatty acid oxidation, and TCA cycle vulnerability predispose the heart to ferroptosis under chronic stress.

Hierarchy of ferroptosis susceptibility. Based on tissue-intrinsic parameters, a tentative hierarchy under comparable arsenic exposure is: brain > kidney > lung > heart > liver. This hierarchy emerges from the interplay among iron content, PUFA composition, antioxidant capacity, GPX4 redundancy, and selenium availability, providing a mechanistic explanation that extends beyond descriptive listing.

Spatial microenvironment. Organ-specific differences are also shaped by internal spatial microenvironments. In the liver lobule, the portal zone (higher oxygen, oxidative phosphorylation) and central venous zone (relative hypoxia, glycolysis) differ in metabolic enzyme expression, iron content, and antioxidant capacity. How these features modulate ferroptosis susceptibility requires spatial metabolomics/transcriptomics. In the tumor microenvironment, the finding that arsenic-responsive gene expression is enriched in tumor-adjacent regions (r = 0.83) and inversely correlated with TIL density [13] raises the possibility that arsenic promotes immune exclusion via ferroptosis-dependent mechanisms—a key question for “cell non-autonomous” arsenic carcinogenesis.

From mechanism to application

Biomarker discovery: from single molecules to combinatorial panels

Traditional biomarkers (e.g., urinary arsenic) have limited sensitivity and specificity. Multi-omics integration with machine learning enables “combinatorial biomarker panel” screening. In arsenic-induced NAFLD, integration of proteomics, metabolomics, and random forest identified a panel of AMP, 13-HODE, and hippuric acid (AUC 0.86, 95% CI 0.76–0.96 in an independent human cohort) [14]. 13-HODE, a linoleic acid peroxidation product, showed the highest feature importance, consistent with the lipid peroxidation hallmark of ferroptosis—an example of “mechanism-driven biomarker discovery.” In bladder cancer, spatial transcriptomics revealing elevated arsenic-responsive gene expression in the tumor-adjacent region (r = 0.83) opens avenues for spatially resolved diagnostic markers [13].

Therapeutic strategies

Selenium Supplementation: From Blind Administration to Precision Intervention. Mechanistic evidence clearly shows that selenium supplementation restores GPX4 activity via Nrf2 activation [31]. Selenium also promotes arsenic biliary excretion by forming selenium-arsenic complexes [42]. However, a cautious and dialectical attitude is necessary. High-dose selenium is itself toxic and has a narrow therapeutic window. Epidemiological data suggest serum selenium levels below 70 µg/L impair selenoprotein synthesis, while levels persistently above 150 µg/L may increase type 2 diabetes risk [43]. Inter-individual differences are substantial: AS3MT polymorphisms affect arsenic methylation efficiency, and SEPP1 polymorphisms regulate selenoprotein P synthesis and selenium transport [6]. Therefore, “one-size-fits-all” blind supplementation may be ineffective or even harmful.

Feasibility considerations. Several barriers must be addressed before genotype-guided precision selenium supplementation can be advocated. A tentative target window of 90–130 µg/L appears plausible for carriers of high-risk genotypes. This range is derived from the following evidence: First, the lower bound (∼90 µg/L) is informed by observational studies in Chinese and European populations demonstrating that serum selenium levels below 80–90 µg/L are associated with suboptimal GPX4 activity, impaired selenoprotein expression, and elevated lipid peroxidation markers [48], [49]. Second, the upper bound (∼130 µg/L) is guided by the Selenium and Vitamin E Cancer Prevention Trial (SELECT) and the Nutritional Prevention of Cancer (NPC) trial, which reported that selenium supplementation in selenium-replete individuals (baseline >122 µg/L) was associated with an increased incidence of type 2 diabetes, suggesting a threshold of ∼130–150 µg/L for potential adverse metabolic effects [50], [51]. Third, this range is consistent with the selenium intake required to optimize plasma GPX activity (90–110 µg/L), as established by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) [52]. We emphasize that this therapeutic window is proposed specifically for arsenic-exposed populations with documented selenium deficiency (baseline serum Se < 70 µg/L) and has not yet been validated in prospective dose-finding clinical trials.

Pharmacogenetic testing (AS3MT/SEPP1 genotyping) imposes costs and requires infrastructure often unavailable in arsenic-endemic, low-resource settings; population-level selenium fortification (e.g., selenium-enriched rice or salt) may be a more feasible near-term strategy. Third, allele frequencies vary markedly across ethnic groups—for example, AS3MT rs3740393 minor allele frequency ranges from ∼0.12 in Europeans to ∼0.45 in East Asians—affecting generalizability and cost-effectiveness [44]. Health-economic modeling is needed. Therefore, precision strategies based on individual multi-omics characteristics (genotype, plasma selenium, GPX4 expression, metabolic fingerprints) represent a key research direction. Similarly, for mitochondria-targeted drugs like MitoQ, dose optimization should consider individual mitochondrial genome characteristics and drug-metabolizing enzyme activities.

Challenges and perspectives

Multi-omics integration still faces multiple challenges. Cross-platform and cross-omics data normalization lacks recognized standards; tools like statTarget help for mass spectrometry data but normalization across fundamentally different omics types remains difficult [26]. Although CMF and DIABLO excel with high-dimensional sparse data, biological interpretability is limited; incorporating prior knowledge (e.g., known pathways, protein-protein interactions) into matrix factorization is a research hotspot. Many mechanistic findings—including the proposed arsenic–selenium–GPX4–ferroptosis axis—remain at correlation and pharmacological levels and require definitive causal verification using CRISPR/Cas9, tissue-specific conditional knockouts, and pharmacological rescue [45]. Privacy protection and ethical compliance in cross-regional studies also pose challenges.

An important caveat in translating these mechanistic findings to population-level risk assessment lies in the substantial genetic heterogeneity affecting both arsenic methylation capacity and selenium metabolism. The AS3MT gene, which encodes the primary arsenic methyltransferase, harbors several common polymorphisms (e.g., rs3740393, rs11191439) whose allele frequencies differ markedly across continental populations—the rs3740393 minor allele frequency ranges from approximately 0.12 in Europeans to approximately 0.45 in East Asians. Similarly, polymorphisms in SEPP1 (encoding selenoprotein P, the primary selenium transporter) and GPX4 itself may influence individual selenium requirements and baseline ferroptosis susceptibility. These genetic variables introduce uncertainty when extrapolating findings from single-population studies to global risk assessment. Furthermore, species differences in arsenic methylation efficiency—rodents metabolize arsenic more rapidly than humans, and several widely used laboratory mouse strains carry hypomorphic As3mt variants—limit the direct translation of murine ferroptosis models to human pathophysiology. Humanized mouse models and genetically stratified population studies that explicitly account for these variables are needed to strengthen the translational foundation of the selenium–ferroptosis axis.

Looking forward, deep integration of AI (graph neural networks, Transformer architectures) with multi-omics will drive a paradigm shift from descriptive to predictive toxicology [46]. Spatial single-cell multi-omics will enable comprehensive analysis of arsenic toxicity heterogeneity at cellular and spatial dimensions, revealing precise modes of action within specific tissue microenvironments (e.g., liver zonation, tumor immune microenvironment). Landmark studies by Singhal et al. [13] and Chaturvedi et al. [19] exemplify this potential. Human-derived organoid models provide an ideal platform to address species differences in arsenic metabolism [47]. Ultimately, these technologies will establish a closed-loop systems toxicology cycle “from population to molecules, and back to population.”.

Conclusion

Multi-omics integration is reshaping arsenic toxicity research. From proteomics, metabolomics, and ionomics to organ-specific networks, ferroptosis emerges as a common convergent mechanism. A mechanistic model links arsenic exposure to ferroptosis through disruption of selenium metabolism and impaired GPX4 biosynthesis. Tissue-intrinsic biochemical parameters determine the ferroptosis threshold, establishing a hierarchy of organ sensitivity (brain > kidney > lung > heart > liver). The multi-omics data integration workflow and cross-omics regulatory network exemplify the transition from descriptive cataloging to genuine data fusion. Spatial omics, single-cell omics, machine learning, and causal inference inject new momentum into dissecting toxicity heterogeneity. Definitive causal validation remains a critical next step. The arsenic case offers a paradigm for systems toxicology of environmental pollutants.
