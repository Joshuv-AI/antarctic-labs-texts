Knowledge, attitude, and uptake of HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis among students in tertiary institutions in Calabar Metropolis, Nigeria
Authors: Sylvia A. Agba, Margaret I. Akpan, Elvis Anyaehiechukwu Okolie, Unwana Paul Obadare, Abasiofon I. Akpan, Felicity Nneoma Okolie, Antor O. Ndep
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Adolescents and young people represent an increasing proportion of people living with the human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) worldwide.1 Young people between 10 and 24 years of age experience a series of physiological, psychological, and social changes that expose them to unhealthy sexual behaviors such as early sex experimentation, engaging in unsafe sex, and having multiple sexual partners, keeping them at risk of facing sexual and reproductive health problems such as teenage pregnancies, unsafe abortion, sexually transmitted infections, and HIV/AIDS.1-4 In 2019, young people accounted for two of every seven new HIV infections globally.5 Similarly, in 2022, approximately 480,000 young people were newly infected with HIV.6

HIV research has advanced, making HIV a chronic illness that can be prevented, treated, and managed. Nonetheless, HIV continues to disproportionately affect adolescents and young adults between the age 10 and 24 years.7 Global estimates indicate that 5.1 million teenagers and young people were HIV positive worldwide in 2019.6 Improving young people's access to HIV preventive strategies such as pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is critical, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa, where HIV prevalence is the highest.8 PrEP involves a daily dose of antiretroviral drugs to reduce the likelihood of HIV transmission from subsequent exposures.1,9 When taken as prescribed by a physician, PrEP can help you stay protected against HIV if a condom breaks, is not used correctly, or is not used every time.9,10 The Center for Disease Control (CDC) reports that studies on PrEP effectiveness have shown that consistent use of PrEP reduces the risk of getting HIV from sex by about 99% and from injection drug use by at least 74%.10 However, while progress has been observed from the implementation of these strategies, HIV remains a significant problem in Nigeria due to a number of factors. For instance, issues such as lack of awareness, poor access, myths and misconceptions, and low uptake of PrEP are significant challenges in the fight against HIV in Nigeria.9

Nigeria has one of the highest rates of new HIV infections in sub-Saharan Africa and the second largest HIV epidemic globally.11 Despite efforts to stop the virus's spread, some areas continue to see an increase in the number of HIV-positive youths due to their involvement in risky sexual behaviors.12,13 In addition to the National Strategy on HIV for Adolescents and Young People (AYP; 2016–2020), a number of studies have noted the key drivers of HIV among AYP in Nigeria to be poor access to sexual and reproductive health services, multiple and concurrent sexual partnerships, intergenerational sex, sexual coercion, low risk perception, and transactional sex.12-16 These drivers of HIV among young people highlight the complexity of the HIV issue among this group and the need to intensify comprehensive prevention efforts.

When taken regularly and consistently, PrEP offers an additional layer of protection against disease transmission for individuals at high risk.8 One of the primary strategic objectives of the Federal Ministry of Health in Nigeria is to effectively decrease the occurrence of new HIV infections by 90% especially among young people by improving the availability and utilization of HIV prevention, intervention, and prophylactic measures.17 PrEP can contribute significantly to reducing new HIV infections among young people in Nigeria; however, use of PrEP is largely reliant on a number of factors, including awareness and knowledge, willingness to use among individuals at risk of HIV infection, and equitable access to PrEP services.18,19 To date, there are no known published studies that have explored the relationship between knowledge, attitude, and uptake of PrEP among students in tertiary institutions in Calabar Metropolis, Cross River State. Hence, this study assessed the knowledge, attitudes, and uptake of PrEP among students of tertiary institutions in Calabar metropolis, Cross River State, Nigeria. It is anticipated that insights from this study will guide the design and implementation of effective interventions to promote the uptake of PrEP and support the achievement of an HIV-free generation of young people.

Methods

Study design

A quantitative cross-sectional descriptive study design was used to assess the knowledge, attitude, and uptake of PrEP among students of tertiary institutions in Calabar Metropolis, Cross River State, Nigeria.

Study settings

Cross River state is situated in the South-South zone of Nigeria. There are 18 local government areas in the state comprising Abi, Akamkpa, Akpabuyo, Bakassi, Bekwara, Biase, Boki, Etung, Calabar municipality, Calabar South, Obubra, Obudu, Odukpani, Ikom, Obanliku, Ogoja, Yakur, and Yala. Cross River State is administratively divided into three senatorial districts namely Southern, Central, and Northern senatorial districts. Calabar which is the state capital has an area of 406 square kilometers and a population of 371,022 as at 2006 census. It encompasses of Calabar municipality and Calabar south local government areas and lies between latitude 4° 50′N and 5° 10′N and longitudes 8° 17′E and 8° 20′E, bounded to the north by Odukpani local government area (LGA) and to the east by Akpabuyo LGA. Calabar metropolis consists of Calabar municipality and Calabar south only.

Study population and sampling

The study population included students at the University of Calabar (UNICAL) and the Cross River State University (UNICROSS). The sample size was determined using Cochran (1977) formula n∘ = Z2pq/e2. This study used a total sample size of 426 which included a 10% nonresponse rate (see appendix, http://links.lww.com/PBJ/A45 for sample size calculation). Two major tertiary institutions in Calabar metropolis were purposively selected for the study. Using a multistage sampling technique, 5 faculties were selected randomly from the two institutions, and 5 departments from the selected faculties were randomly selected, respectively. Class levels as well as respondents were also selected using simple random sampling.

Data instrument and data collection

An interviewer administered structured questionnaire was used for the data collection. Following literature reviews, the questionnaire was adapted from previous studies that explored similar issue on PrEP.8,18,19 The questionnaire had five sections covering areas such as sociodemographic data, knowledge of PrEP, students' attitudes toward PrEP use, level of uptake of PrEP, and factors influencing the use of PrEP. The questionnaire was validated during the proposal stage by selected subject experts who provided significant insights on the content and structure before it was pretested to assess for clarity, comprehension, flow, completion times, and respondent fatigue. Pretesting was carried out with 10% (42) of the total sample size (426) in the College of Health Technology Calabar which shares characteristics with UNICAL and UNICROSS. As part of the pretest of the questionnaire, we received additional feedback from the respondents on key areas such as clarity, flow, acceptability, and respondent fatigue which informed final updates to the questionnaire.

Kuder-Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20) test was used to test for reliability following the pretest.20 The KR-20 coefficient ranges between 0 and 1. Although there is no universal cutoff, higher values closer to 1 suggest that test items are highly interrelated and consistent, while values closer to 0 are considered to have lower internal consistency and loosely related.20,21 The coefficient is mostly interpreted as >0.9 as high, >0.8 as moderate to high (good), >0.7 as moderate (acceptable), and >0.6 might indicate an unreliable test.21 We recoded specific items in the knowledge construct (See appendix, http://links.lww.com/PBJ/A45) into a dichotomous format (correct/incorrect) to ensure consistency in the measurement scale and that it meets assumptions for the use of KR-20.20 For instance, correct option (s) in the questionnaire items were recoded as “1” while incorrect option (s) in the questionnaire item were recoded as “0.” The KR-20 reliability coefficient for knowledge measurement was 0.83. Hence, the measurement items were considered reliable based on the KR-20 coefficient level >0.7 which is acceptable.21 We used Kobotoolbox to design the questionnaire/form (as a project) on a desktop and deployed the same for use on the Open Data Kit (ODK) Collect mobile application. Final data collection involved using ODK collect mobile software for Android with the aid of three trained research assistants. Each question was properly explained to the assistants so they could generate relevant responses and ensure they understood each. Informed consent was sought from the respondents before participation.

Data analysis

Data were extracted from ODK and downloaded into Microsoft Excel. All data were evaluated for entry completeness and cleaned using Microsoft Excel 2010. Data were exported and analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences, now known as Statistical Product and Service Solution (SPSS) version 26. Findings were presented in percentages, frequencies, tables, and charts. Hypotheses were tested using χ2 test at 95% confidence level (at 0.05 alpha level). To calculate knowledge level for this study, we assigned one point to correct responses to seven PrEP specific questions in the knowledge section and zero point to incorrect answers. Blooms cutoff category was used to grade knowledge level i.e. high level (80%–100%), moderate level (60%–79%), and low level (>60%).22,23

Ethical consideration

Approval to conduct this research was sought and obtained from the Research and Ethics Committee of the University of Calabar and the Cross River State Ministry of Health. The ethical approval number is CRSMOH/HRP/HREC/2023/386. A number of steps were followed to obtain participants' consent to participate in this study. First, the content of the information letter contained in the consent form was read out to the respondents, and informed verbal consent was obtained after discussions. Next, in addition to signing a hard copy of the consent form, the respondents had to sign the electronic consent form on the ODK by clicking on “YES” to confirm their interest in participating in the study. For participants who were younger than 18 years of age, phone calls were made to their parents or guardians, and their consent was obtained before this group participated in this study. Confidentiality was assured, and the participants were given the liberty to opt out of the study if they wished to without any prejudice.

Knowledge of respondents on PrEP

The results in Table 2 show that 217 (51.7%) of the respondents have heard of PrEP. Seventy-eight respondents (36%) reported hearing about PrEP from a HIV program/workshop, while 76 (35%) reported hearing about PrEP from friends. About 113 (52%) of respondents reported having been counseled on PrEP and mentioned to have been counseled by workshop/NGO's (18%), friends (17%), and health worker (11.5%). Overall, more than half of the respondents (126, 58.1%) who have heard about PrEP had a high-level knowledge, 73 (33.6%) had moderate-level knowledge on PrEP, and 18 (8.3%) had low-level knowledge on PrEP.

Attitude of respondents towards the use of PrEP

The results in Table 3 shows that 183 (84.3%) of the total respondents who have heard of PrEP would consider taking PrEP when they expect to engage in sexual activities. Most of the respondents reported that they will prefer to take PrEP before engaging in sexual activities (97.3%). The preference of respondents for taking PrEP before sexual activity were underscored by their worries about the side effects of the medication and not frequently engaging in sexual acts. Other participants who stated that they will never take PrEP before engaging in sexual activities stated mainly that they did not engage in casual sexual activities and that they did not like medication of any sort.

Uptake of PrEP

The result in Table 4 shows that more than half of the students have been sexually active in the past 6 months (215, 51.2%) with 116 (27.6%) of the respondents having one or more sexual partners. Of the 217 who have heard about PrEP, only 13 (6%) have ever used HIV PrEP. Of the respondents who have used PrEP, 3 (23.1%) of the respondents reported having accessed PrEP from health facilities, while 4 (30.7%) reported having accessed PrEP from NGOs and 3 (23.1%) from medical personnel. The results from χ2 analysis (Table 5) revealed that the sociodemographic characteristics significantly associated with uptake of PrEP in the study population were age (χ2 = 8.724, df = 3, P = 0.033) and tribe (χ2 = 3.40, df = 6, P < 0.001).

Factors affecting PrEP uptake

When asked if they have considered having use for PrEP, a total of 88 (40.5%) respondents reported that they have thought they will be exposed and benefit from PrEP. About 60 (27.6%) reported that they have tried to get PrEP but could not get it. Of the respondents who reported not getting PrEP after trying to get it had the following reasons: 29 (48.3%) reported that they did not know where to get it, 27 (45.0%) reported that it was unavailable, and 13 (21.7) reported that they thought it was expensive. Among respondents who had tried accessing PrEP, lack of knowledge (73, 46.5%), friends' disapproval (70, 44.6%), and fear of side effects (60, 38.2%) were their reasons.

Discussion

The use of PrEP is a significant strategy for HIV prevention, especially in areas disproportionately affected by the HIV epidemic. This study assessed the knowledge, attitude, and uptake of PrEP among students in tertiary institutions in Calabar Metropolis. Overall, awareness of PrEP was suboptimal among the study population, as only 51.7% of respondents were aware of PrEP. This finding conforms to the results of studies by Ajayi et al.24 and Shamu et al.19 that reported 18.8% and 44.6%, respectively, as the level of PrEP awareness among young people. Such limited awareness of PrEP among this group indicates that current HIV prevention and PrEP awareness campaigns may be inadequate in reaching young people.19,24 Owing to the correlation between awareness and PrEP utilization, it is critical to rethink, tailor, and intensify current HIV prevention and PrEP promotion initiatives to reach young people with comprehensive information on PrEP.8,24 Such tailored efforts would benefit from leveraging multiple media channels, especially the Internet and social media platforms, to reach young people and improve their awareness.19

Surprisingly, among the group who were aware of PrEP, more than half (58.1%) had a high level of knowledge. This is similar to a study by Leteka et al.,25 which reported that most students (83.3%) were knowledgeable about PrEP. However, it varies from the finding of Santa Maria et al.,26 which documented low PrEP knowledge, as only 29% participants knew the benefits of PrEP. Disparities in knowledge levels across these studies may be attributed to differences in population characteristics, sample sizes, and study settings and designs. Nevertheless, it is critical to scale up PrEP promotion initiatives to improve young people's knowledge of PrEP and the need to use it as part of multifaceted HIV prevention measures.19,24

Focusing on students' attitudes toward PrEP, this study revealed a high level of willingness toward PrEP utilization among participants, as 84.3% of sexually active respondents expressed the desire to use PrEP before engaging in sexual activities. This finding is similar to studies by Geldsetzer et al.27 and Kabaghe et al.28 that observed that the majority of their study participants were willing to use PrEP. Despite the high willingness to use PrEP among respondents in this study, PrEP use was alarmingly low, as only 6% reported using PrEP. Such a suboptimal use of PrEP among participants in this study is not novel, as Okeke et al.29 and Ajayi et al.24 documented 3% and 1.7%, respectively, as the proportion of young people using PrEP in their respective studies. A plethora of reasons were highlighted by respondents in this study as the reason for not using PrEP—reliance on condoms as their preferred method of HIV prevention, aversion to medication, lack of knowledge of PrEP and its benefits, and inability to access PrEP. Knowledge of these reasons for nonuse of PrEP provides an opportunity for HIV program planners and health system stakeholders to tailor interventions that address identified issues facilitating poor PrEP uptake among young people, improve uptake, and prevent new HIV infections in a sustainable manner.19,30

A number of factors were documented as significant challenges impeding access to PrEP among study respondents who were interested in using the same as part of HIV prevention approach. The challenges affecting PrEP access among respondents included knowledge gaps about where to access PrEP, unavailability, concerns around cost and side effects, and being denied access to PrEP. These findings highlight the multifaceted barriers young people face in accessing PrEP, which has enormous potential in HIV prevention and control among this demographic whose vulnerability remains high. Again, the existence of these barriers even in educational settings further underscores the need for deliberate efforts to design and implement effective strategies to ensure equitable access to PrEP for young people who need them.8,9,29,31 Equally important is that interventions directed at scaling access to PrEP must be innovative, informed by the needs and peculiarities of this group, and leverage effective and dynamic channels to reach young people with comprehensive PrEP information and services.16,19 For instance, in addition to periodic HIV prevention outreaches by NGOs, setting up PrEP distribution centers for young people can be pivotal to improving PrEP uptake. Furthermore, PrEP promotion campaigns must prioritize providing appropriate information on PrEP benefits, potential side effects, and where it can be accessed by people interested in using PrEP.

Limitations

This study has some limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. First, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the possibility of social desirability bias, where participants may have provided responses that they perceived as socially acceptable rather than accurate representations of their experiences due to the sensitive nature of some of the questions. In addition, self-reported data are prone to recall bias as the expectation lies on respondents to recall events from the past, and bias is introduced if such recall does not accurately reflect past experiences or events. Finally, using a cross-sectional design restricted the ability to conduct in-depth explorations that could have offered a better understanding of some of the key results from this study.

Despite these limitations, this study demonstrates several strengths that support the validity of its conclusions and underscore its reliability for informing future research and interventions in this area. Notably, it employed best research practices in conceptualizing the study design, conducting a robust literature review to ensure a better understanding of the prevailing issues around PrEP for young people, validating the questionnaire and pretesting same, and implementing data collection and analysis.

Conclusion

This study provides critical insights into the knowledge, attitudes, and uptake of PrEP among students in tertiary institutions in Cross River State. The findings reveal that despite suboptimal awareness of PrEP among participants, there was a high willingness to use PrEP among participants who knew its benefits. Regrettably, uptake of PrEP was poor among respondents, which emphasized the need to tackle the barriers impeding young people's access. Hence, developing innovative and targeted health promotion interventions that reflect the needs of young people, address multifaceted barriers, and expand access to PrEP becomes critical as part of broader HIV prevention efforts in Nigeria. Practical approaches to enhance PrEP uptake among young people in Nigeria include advocacy for subsidized or free PrEP services, strengthening the policy environment, leveraging partnerships between NGOs and relevant authorities to intensify PrEP promotion and service delivery activities, and engaging young people in program planning to ensure the adequacy of interventions toward providing awareness and stimulating PrEP uptake.32 The implementation of a combination of these interventions mentioned above will likely contribute to improvements in PrEP uptake, reduction of new HIV infections within the community, and overall advancement of public health outcomes.
