Stacking ensemble model for predicting chronic kidney disease in the Uddanam region of India with unknown etiology
Authors: Rakesh Salakapuri, Panduranga Vital Terlapu, Kishore Raju Kalidindi, K Eswara Rao, Ramesh Yegireddi, U D Prasan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is a severe public health issue across the globe. It is hard to detect early because there are no apparent symptoms. Many patients do not realize they have the disease. Improved models are crucial for altering early diagnosis and effective treatment. No single cause has been found to predict the disease. This study uses a new dataset from Uddanam villages in Srikakulam district, AP, India, where CKD is common. Data preprocessing was done carefully to maintain data integrity. Outliers were detected and removed to improve dataset quality. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) techniques were used to analyze the processed data. It included statistical analysis, feature analysis, target attributes, and outlier detection analysis. In this study, we collected patients’ data from various clinical centers and hospitals in the Uddanam area of Andhra Pradesh, India. The study uses a Stacking Ensemble method to improve predictions of chronic kidney disease in the Uddanam region. It combines Naïve Bayes, K-NN, and SVM with Sigmoid and Linear kernels. Logistic Regression is used as the meta-learner. We use principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensional reduction to better combine features of CKD analysis and prediction with ML models. The PCA with the Stacking Ensemble approach verified good performance compared to the state-of-the-art models, achieving 98.9% on CA, 98.9% on F1, and 0.999 on AUC. It has higher predictive accuracy and better generalizability across the dataset. The “Uddanam Chronic Kidney Disease (UCKD)” study shows that advanced ML techniques can create effective models for CKD. The results highlight the need to collect and prepare local data. The study helps improve how well machine learning works in healthcare.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Stacking ensemble, Machine learning, Etiology, Exploratory data analysis, CKD hot-spot zoneSubject terms: Chronic kidney disease, Health care, Medical research

The Uddanam Region: a CKD hotspot

The Uddanam region is in Srikakulam District, Andhra Pradesh, India. It is known for CKD of unknown cause. This area is one of the most important hotspots for this disease globally53. The region is located between 18.56219° N and 19.16563° N latitude, and between 84.30934° E and 84.76844° E longitude. The coastal belt extends about 30 to 40 km inland. It includes five mandals: Ichchapuram, Kanchili, Kaviti, Sompeta, and Vajrapu Kotturu (nearly 120 villages) across upper, middle, and lower Uddanam11. It has a high rate of chronic kidney disease (CKD) among its people. This issue is known as Uddanam Nephropathy. Many studies have focused on this problem, which is a serious problem in the region. Reports show that it is affecting many people. This is happening even among those without typical risk factors like high BP and Blood Sugar. The underlying causes of Uddanam Chronic Kidney Disease (UCKD) remain unclear, with various hypotheses being proposed, including environmental toxins, genetic susceptibility, and occupational exposures54,55. Many studies have been done, but the reasons for the high CKD rates in Uddanam are still unclear. This shows we need better research methods and tools to understand this public health issue.

Machine learning role in CKD prediction

Effective prediction models are critical for early detection and improved treatment choices. CKD is a hereditary disorder that has a severe impact on individuals and healthcare resources. Machine Learning(ML) has a lot of benefits for managing CKD, like making early diagnoses more accurate and giving personalized treatment suggestions. ML algorithms can quickly look through vast amounts of patient data and find complicated patterns and risk factors that older methods might miss. However, ML in CKD comes with some problems as well. Some of these are the risk of overfitting specific datasets and the need for high-quality, representative data that is hard to get to train models well. ML models are also sometimes seen as "black boxes," which makes it hard for healthcare workers to understand and trust their predictions. Even with these problems, ML could be useful in CKD care because it could lead to better diagnostics and more personalized treatment plans. Machine learning (ML) has become useful in healthcare in recent years. It helps with predictive analytics and making data-driven decisions. By using standard statistical methods, we can analyzed large amounts of data. It helps us find trends and connections that are not easy to see7–9. This capability is precious in CKD prediction, where early and accurate identification of at-risk individuals can significantly enhance disease management and patient outcomes.

Literature on CKD Hotspot areas

Uddanam is in Andhra Pradesh, India. It has a high rate of CKD. Environmental and occupational factors may contribute to this issue. Researchers and organizations like ICMR & WHO, and AIIMS & NTR University are studying the Uddanam region in Andhra Pradesh10,11. They want to discover why CKD of unknown etiology (CKDu) is common there. They are looking at genetic, environmental, and job-related factors. They suggest improving water quality and healthcare access. They also want to raise community awareness, provide financial assistance, and conduct further epidemiological studies. The ICMR and WHO have partnered to conduct a joint study on the genetic epidemiology of Uddanam nephropathy at Andhra University. The study will involve genetic sequencing of samples from around 2,000 patients undergoing dialysis for chronic kidney disease. A study by AIIMS and NTR University found that 32.2% of the Uddanam population has CKD. Farmers have a 20% higher prevalence of CKD. Age, alcohol usage, and smoking are significant risk factors for CKD. Gender, diabetes, and hypertension are not substantially associated with CKD. Occupational exposures, especially in agriculture, may contribute to the epidemic12,13. The region faces limited healthcare access, with only two government centres serving 150,000 people and 3–5 nephrologists. Dialysis is required by 35–40% of CKD patients, with 25–30% receiving adequate care due to cost barriers. Kidney transplants are available for 1–2%. The government and NGOs are helping in several ways. The Nephrology Research Centre in Uddanam offers monthly free dialysis to over 500 patients. They have water purification projects in more than 50 villages. Studies from MIT and Harvard found high silica levels in drinking water. The Sri Sri Rural Development Program provides clean water to over 30,000 people14,15.

A detailed analysis shows different aspects of CKD in Uddanam, one of which is groundwater contamination that affects many people. Over 60% of those affected depend on groundwater. High summer temperatures reach 40–45 °C. This can cause heat stress, and chronic dehydration can damage the kidneys16. CKD cases are also influenced by medical and genetic factors, with diabetes, hypertension, family history, urinary tract infections, and stone disease present in 30–40% of cases. The highest prevalence of the condition is in people aged 40–60 years, at about 30–35%. Those aged 20–40 years have a moderate prevalence of around 15–20%. For individuals above 60 years, the prevalence is about 20–25%, and for Children (< 20 years): ~ 2–5%. The prevalence of CKD cases is 1.3:1 between men (20–25% prevalence) and women (15–20%). Occupational exposure is highest in farmers (60%-70% of cases), followed by casual laborers (15–20%) and other professions (5–10%). Low-income groups account for 70% of CKD cases, followed by middle-income groups at 25% and high-income groups at 5%17,18. The recommended action for the prevalence of Uddanam CKD is to improve water quality. We should increase access to healthcare. Raising community awareness about hydration and safety is important. Financial assistance through government subsidies is needed. We must conduct more studies to find the exact cause19.

Predictive modelling of chronic kidney disease: a stacking ensemble approach

As a result of its prevalence, chronic kidney disease poses a serious threat to global health. Timely intervention and management of chronic kidney disease (CKD) depend on early and precise prognosis of the disease. This study proposes a robust predictive framework to identify CKD risk factors and develop an optimal predictive model. Figure 1 explains clearly the flow of the proposed work.

Proposed stacking ensemble framework for early CKD detection.

Proposed methodology

The study (Fig. 1) begins by collecting data from CKD hotspots like Uddanam and applying Exploratory Data Analysis for data pre-processing. This includes feature statistical analysis, correlation feature analysis, exploring target attributes, and outlier detection analysis. The dataset is then prepared without PCA, and outliers are addressed using PCA to robustness of the analysis. Next, the data is split and evaluated using ten-fold cross-validation to do classification prediction. Now the training data is then forwarded to a proposed Stacking ensemble model, which contains Support Vector Machines (SVM and SVM with Sigmoid kernel), Neural Networks (NN), Naive Bayes, and K-Nearest Neighbors. The performance metrics, including Accuracy, Precision, Recall, F1-score, and AUC, are evaluated for the ensemble model and the individual models. The optimal predictive model is then compared against a test dataset containing personal and clinical data to predict CKD and non-CKD values. The proposed methodology aims to leverage the strengths of various machine learning algorithms and ensemble techniques to develop a robust and accurate CKD prediction system, contributing to improved early detection and management of this disease.

Dataset description

The real time data is collected from patients, various hospital and clinical centers of in patients of 34 panchayath’s in Uddanam Area, Srikakulam, Andhra Pradesh, India. Table 3 shows a description of various attributes used for analysis. The dataset comprises 1,055 records, with 656 classified as diseased (CKD) and 399 as non-diseased (NCKD). The dataset has 37 features (F1 to F37) and one classification label (C1). The attributes in the dataset can be continuous, which means they have numeric values. The data includes attributes like age and sex, which can be continuous or discrete. Clinical measurements like PackedCell and WBCells have specific ranges. Attributes related to conditions like BloodPressure are categorized as low, high, or normal. Blood test results like Neutrophils vary continuously. The classification of patients as having chronic kidney disease or not is based on these data. This study used clinical records and lab results from patients in Uddanam, Andhra Pradesh, India. This region has a high rate of chronic kidney disease (CKD) with unknown causes. Data was collected by medical staff and research assistants. This happened during routine health screenings and follow-up visits. The data included clinical parameters, demographic information, and diagnosis outcomes.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

PCA is a technique utilized to decrease the number of scopes in data by converting it into a novel coordinate system, which retains the most crucial information. PCs are linear combinations of original variables. Seventeen components were analyzed in this data analysis, including eigenvalues, proportion of variance explained, cumulative variance, and combined attributes contributing to each component. Eigenvalues represent the variance explained by each PC, with higher eigenvalues indicating more variance. The first PC has the highest eigenvalue (6.88987), followed by decreasing values for subsequent components. The percentage of variability accounted for each element is obtained by dividing each eigenvalue. The eigenvalue is obtained by summing all the eigenvalues. The cumulative variance shows the total variance explained by all components up to a certain point, helping determine how many PCs to retain for dimensionality reduction. Combining attributes in each central component indicates the contribution of each original variable to the element. The first few PCs account for most of the variability in the data, and this analysis helps identify which actual variables contribute most to each component. The PCA result in Table 11 presents information on the 17 principal components, including eigenvalues, variance explained, cumulative variance, and top attributes combined. It also highlights the significance of different features in the dataset.

Conclusion

In this research work, we designed and tested a Stacking ensemble system to predict CKD in Uddanam, and it outperformed individual models in precision, accuracy, recall, and F1-score. The Stacking Ensemble approach, which combined outlier removal and PCA, achieved a high accuracy rate of 98.9%. It is confirmed that many predictive methods can capture this demographic’s complicated CKD trends and risk variables. We found various risk factors in Uddanam, including extended complex water exposure. Our technique is vital for early identification and intervention to slow CKD progression and improve patient outcomes through an ensemble framework. While our Stacking ensemble model has shown promise, there are various ways to improve CKD prediction and management in Uddanam. Adding more diverse and granular datasets such as longitudinal health records, environmental data, and dietary habits could increase model accuracy and shed light on CKD development. Genetic markers particular to the Uddanam community could detect inherited CKD risk factors, enabling more targeted and effective interventions. A real-time monitoring system integrating our predictive model with wearable health devices could enable continuous health assessment and timely medical responses for at-risk individuals. Healthcare professionals, environmental scientists, and sociologists could collaborate to develop comprehensive prevention and treatment programs tailored to the Uddanam region and improve the ensemble model’s interpretability. Future work will focus on gathering a balanced dataset. This will include more female CKD patients and data from various regions. The goal is to reduce gender bias and improve model fairness. Using these diverse data sources will enhance the model’s generalizability. Predictive analytics and health education programs are helping us educate the community about CKD risk factors and encourage early health-seeking. Future research can build on our study’s foundations to reduce CKD prevalence and improve Uddanam residents’ quality of life.
