Comparative Evaluation of Pain Reduction with Vibration Using Speech Stimulator vs 2% Lignocaine Topical Gel Application Prior to Greater Palatine Nerve Block in 7–12-year-old Children: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Santhosh Prasanth, B Padmakumari, D Bhageeradhi, B Seethal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAims and backgroundPain is one of the most important causes of anxiety in pediatric dental patients. A painful intraoral nerve block could detrimentally affect the child's behavior in the dental setting. The aim of this clinical trial was to evaluate the pain reduction effectiveness of the vibration technique by speech stimulator compared to 2% lignocaine topical gel in 7–12-year-old children requiring a greater palatine nerve (GPN) block.MethodsThis study was a double-blinded randomized controlled trial that enrolled 60 children between 7 and 12 years old. The randomization list was generated by block randomization, and two groups were designated. The two groups of 30 samples each were selected based on the types of preanesthetic preparations received. The first group (group A) was assigned 2% lignocaine gel while the second group (group B) received vibration using a speech simulator for preanesthetic preparation at the site of the GPN block. The preanesthetic techniques were administered by pretrained and blinded assessors. The response of all the subjects was assessed using the facial pain scale (FPS) and SEM score (Sound, Eye, Motor) by an assessor. The FPS score was recorded using the response of the child with the scale ranging from 0 to 10, following the GPN block. Whereas the SEM score was evaluated by the assessor to assess the pain perception of the child, with scores ranging from 1 to 4. The Mann–Whitney U test was employed to analyze the data from this study.ResultsIt was found that the difference in the mean scores of FPS and SEM scales between group A and group B was statistically significant. The mean scores of both FPS and SEM scales were higher in group A (2% lignocaine gel) (p < 0.001) than in group B (vibration using speech stimulator).ConclusionThe study demonstrated that when vibration was used as preanesthetic preparation, it caused less pain/discomfort during the administration of a GPN block than 2% lignocaine gel application.Clinical significanceThe preanesthetic technique employing vibration could be an alternative or could replace the 2% lignocaine topical anesthetic application, especially in nerve blocks of areas with thick underlying mucoperiosteum (nasopalatine or GPN block).How to cite this articlePrasanth S, Padmakumari B, Bhageeradhi D, et al. Introduction

The sensation of pain is very subjective among individuals, and its perception depends on age, sex, race, fear, apprehension, fatigue, etc. Generally, children and anxious adults have a lower pain threshold compared to relaxed individuals. The management of pediatric patients in the dental office is challenging, especially if the child is anxious. Anxiety/fear causes behavioral issues in the management of pediatric patients. Pain is the leading cause of anxiety in a dental clinic, especially among pediatric patients. In the dental setting, pain is usually associated with the administration of local anesthesia. In the past, numerous techniques/equipment were experimented to decrease pain during local anesthesia administration, such as computer-controlled local anesthetic delivery, needle-free jet injectors, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), etc.

According to Melzack, the stimulus from vibration and temperature transmits through larger diameter fibers, while noxious stimulus, such as pain, is transmitted through narrow fibers, which was depicted through the gate control theory.1 During stimulus transmission through the dorsal horn of the spinal cord, there is a gate that preferentially blocks the stimulus from the narrower fibers while allowing the stimulus from the wider diameter fibers to pass through. When vibration and pain stimuli are simultaneously applied over the mucosa, the stimulus of vibration sensation passes through the gates while that of the pain sensation is selectively blocked. This peculiar property of the nerve conduction can be harnessed to reduce unpleasant sensations such as pain.1

The regional nerve blocks, such as the greater palatine nerve (GPN) block, are one of the most painful intraoral nerve blocks, and a topical anesthetic agent seldom decreases the pain during administration of local anesthesia.2 Previous studies have introduced the use of vibration as preanesthetic preparation, and if injections could be made less painful by vibration, we can reduce anxiety and thereby enhance cooperation of the pediatric dental patients.3 The purpose of this study was to assess the efficacy of the vibration technique using a speech stimulator in the reduction of pain when compared to 2% lignocaine gel in 7–12-year-old children during GPN block. The null hypothesis for this study was that there was no difference in pain perception while using 2% lignocaine hydrochloride gel and vibration using a speech simulator during the GPN block.

Methods

This study was a double-blinded randomized controlled trial initiated with the concurrence of the Institutional Ethics Committee, Government Dental College, Thiruvananthapuram (IEC Approval No.: DCT/IEC/SS/24/52). And it was in line with the Helsinki Declaration and enrolled with CTRI vide no.: CTRI/2024/10/075357 (Clinical Trial Registry of India). The participants were children of the age-group 7–12 years old recruited from the general pool of patients visiting the Pediatric Outpatient Department of Government Dental College, Thiruvananthapuram. An experienced pediatric dentist carried out the initial briefing to the potential subjects. Parents whose children met the inclusion criteria (Table 1) were handed an information sheet containing detailed methodology. The instructions were printed in two languages, English and Malayalam (regional language). Before the commencement of the study, consent and assent were obtained from the parents and children, respectively. The participants recruited had either the diagnosis of chronic irreversible pulpitis or retained maxillary posterior teeth, which were indicated for extraction. Based on the inclusion criteria, the selected participants' caregivers/parents were briefed about the probability of subjects being selected to receive a particular preanesthetic technique/preparation. The child and parents reserved their right to exit from the clinical trial at any stage, and they were entitled to get the indicated treatment.

The sample size was calculated by assuming the power as 98 at a 95% confidence interval and significance level of 5% and found to be 27 (rounded to 30) for each group (total sample size = 60).4 The two groups of 30 each were segregated based on the types of preanesthetic preparations/techniques received. The first group (group A) was assigned 2% lignocaine gel, while the second group (group B) received vibration using a speech simulator for preanesthetic preparation at the site of the GPN block. In this research, 7–12-year-old children coming under ASA-type I (American Society of Anesthesiologists) classification were recruited.5 The FBRS (Frankl's behavior rating scale) was a classification for child's behavior in the dental office and clinically well proven for its efficacy. According to Frankl et al., the behavior of a child was categorized into four groups based on the child's cooperative ability and attitude in the dental office.6 The type of behavior in categories 3 and 4 of FBRS was selected for the subjects in the current study.7

Randomization list was generated by block randomization (Figs 1 to 4) and two groups were designated, which ensured all the subjects had an equal probability to be assigned to any group.8 The individual blocks selected were 4, since the total sample size was 60 a sum of 15 blocks was used. Each block was kept in an envelope that contained an equal number of samples. Double blinding was adopted for this study. The assessor was made to stand at a distance of 1.5 m from the patient for recording the outcome measure from the SEM scale. The speech stimulator was kept in the oral cavity of children belonging to both groups during the preanesthetic preparations. The speech stimulator device was kept in the off position in group A, so neither the outcome assessor nor the subjects were aware of the type of preanesthetic preparations used, thus enabling the blinding of the subjects as well as the assessor.

In this study, 2% lignocaine topical anesthetic gel (Cotec Healthcare, India), speech stimulator (Speechgears V-pen model no: SKU0004-02, India), 2 mL sterile single-use polyethylene syringe, and 2% lignocaine with 1:80,000 adrenaline solution (Lignox 2% A, IR Ltd., India) were required as the armamentarium. For the extraction of maxillary posterior teeth, a GPN block was required to achieve sufficient anesthesia in the region. Since the palatal mucosa is thick and due to its unyielding nature, the GPN block is more painful than areas with loose mucosa. A sterile cotton bud was pushed against the junction of the hard palate and alveolar process adjacent to maxillary permanent first molar and dragged posteriorly. The greater palatal foramen was felt as a depression in the bone. The site of injection was 2 mm anterior to the greater palatine foramen. The palatal mucosa at the site of injection was prepared using a cotton swab of povidone iodine in both groups.

Group A

The topical anesthetic agent used in this study was 2% lignocaine hydrochloride gel (Cotec Healthcare, India), which was applied using a sterile cotton swab 2 minutes prior to the injection. The area around the point of application of the topical anesthetic gel was approximately 2–3 mm diameter.9,10

Group B

The vibration was delivered on the palatal mucosa adjacent to the greater palatine foramen using a speech stimulator (Speechgears V-pen model no: SKU0004-0, India). The device is battery-powered, which requires an AA alkaline battery for its operation, and functions noiselessly during its operation. Since the tip of the speech simulator was nonautoclavable, it was covered with a sterile glove before being placed in the oral cavity and discarded after each use. The tip of the speech simulator was placed 10 mm anterior to the greater palatine foramen over the palatal mucosa. One minute prior to the injection, vibration was started and continued till the administration of the local anesthetic injection was completed.

The response of the subjects belonging to both the groups was assessed and recorded using facial pain scale (FPS) and SEM score (Sound, Eye, Motor) by an assessor.2 The individual FPS score was recorded by child's response immediately following the GPN block injection. The FPS chart was a series of digital images denoting the severity of pain. The chart enables us to assess the pain as perceived by an individual. After the local anesthetic injection, the child was asked to select an image corresponding to his/her pain perception. The digital image (emoji) is coded as 0,2,4,6,8,10, and thus pain perception is converted into numerical variables.11–14 The SEM scale was an objective assessment of the pain perception of the child. In the SEM, three variables, which were denoted by 4 categories, represented the pain perception. The categories ranged from comfort to severe discomfort and were represented by scores which was observed by the researcher.15 This was achieved by observing the sound, eye movement, and motor movement of the child with scores ranging from 1 to 4. This was done by observing the child from 1.5 m while administering local anesthesia and recording according to the SEM scale. The scoring classes of the SEM scale range from comfort, mild discomfort, moderately painful, and painful.16 The scores corresponding to the FPS scale and SEM scale were recorded separately.

Statistical Analyses

Data were entered in Microsoft Excel and evaluated using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0. Armonk, New York: IBM Corp. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage). Comparison of FBRS score, FPS, and SEM score across the two groups with the help of Mann–Whitney U test. For all these statistical interpretations, p < 0.05 was considered the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Sixty subjects were recruited in this study, who satisfied the inclusion criteria, and all recruited patients participated in it with zero attrition. The participants were children of the age-group 7–12 years old recruited from the general pool of patients visiting the Pediatric Outpatient Department of Government Dental College, Thiruvananthapuram, with the diagnosis of either chronic irreversible pulpitis or retained maxillary posterior deciduous teeth. A GPN block was required for surgical procedures in the maxillary posterior region. In this study, group A received 2% lignocaine topical gel while group B received vibration using a speech stimulator as preanesthetic preparation for the GPN block.

A total of 56.67% were males, while the remaining 43.33% werefemales from the selected sample (Tables 3). The mean FBRS obtained for group A and group B were 3.37 and 3.17, respectively (Fig. 1), and there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in terms of FBR scores in this current study. The mean ages for groups A and B were 8.83 and 9.3 years, respectively (Table 2), while the mean age for the total sample was found to be 9.07 years.

Discussion

In this research, the pain scores recorded in group A were higher than group B, demonstrating that preanesthetic preparation of vibration using a speech stimulator caused less discomfort during injection. This implies that the preanesthetic technique employing vibration was more effective than 2% lignocaine gel during the GPN block. The findings in our study were in line with the previous evidence that vibration decreased pain perception during injections.17 Though a previous systematic review showed promising results while using the Buzzy device (vibration device), the results would have been better if homogeneity, good quality of evidence, and proper design were adopted in the examined studies.18 In contrast, few evidence exists that vibration was not as effective as a topical anesthetic in reducing pain.4,17,19,20

The GPN block was selected for standardization of pain perception because, if different local anesthesia techniques were analyzed, bias would occur due to variability in pain perception of different techniques, which in turn affects the mean FPS/SEM scores. It was demonstrated in previous studies that the local infiltrations were comparatively less painful when compared to those of nerve blocks.21–23 The GPN block was more painful because of the tight mucoperiosteum overlying the hard palate. In this trial, no subjects from group B (vibration using a speech stimulator) required reapplication of the topical gel. There were no anesthetic or intraoperative complications noticed during this study.

A possible mechanism for the observed reduction in pain perception during GPN block could be very well explained by the gate control theory put forward by Melzak.1 Moreover, obnoxious stimuli such as pain, which traverses through smaller nerve fibers, are selectively blocked, and stimuli such as vibration, which traverses through larger fibers, are allowed to pass through. Thus, during the simultaneous application of pain and vibration stimuli, the subject preferentially perceived vibration.

Although the study was promising, there are some limitations. Firstly, this research evaluated only the subjective measure of pain instead both objective and subjective measure should have been evaluated. Moreover, the age selected for this research varied from 7 to 12 years, so the results reflect the mean of this age-group. Since there could be a difference in pain perception between younger children and older children. Despite these shortcomings, vibration using a speech stimulator could effectively reduce discomfort during local anesthesia injection, especially intraoral nerve block. Thus, decreasing anxiety leads to better behavioral outcomes in the pediatric dental setting.

Conclusion

The study demonstrated that when vibration using a speech stimulator was used as preanesthetic preparation, it caused less pain/discomfort during the administration of the GPN block than 2% lignocaine gel application.

Clinical Significance

The preanesthetic technique employing vibration could be an alternative or could replace the 2% lignocaine topical anesthetic application, especially in nerve blocks of areas with thick underlying mucoperiosteum (nasopalatine or GPN block).
