A distributed fusion framework for breast cancer recurrence prediction using MapReduce
Authors: Prachi Shahare, Abha Mahalwar, Aniket K. Shahade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Breast cancer recurrence remains a major clinical challenge, significantly influencing long-term survival and treatment planning. Accurate early prediction is hindered by heterogeneous clinical factors, imbalanced datasets, and the distributed nature of medical records stored across hospitals, registries, and laboratories. To address these challenges, this study proposes a MapReduce-aligned hybrid framework that combine with distributed Spark-based Gradient Boosted Trees, denoising autoencoder (AE)–derived latent representations, calibrated XGBoost, and deep tabular framework (FT-Transformer and TabTransformer). The framework is designed to operate efficiently on heterogeneous, large-scale datasets while preserving data locality. Two benchmark datasets; the SEER breast cancer recurrence cohort and the Wisconsin Diagnostic Breast Cancer dataset were used to evaluate framework performance across clinical data. Experimental results show that the proposed calibrated XGBoost and AE-augmented fusion frameworks obtained superior discrimination, calibration with the Wisconsin dataset reaching ROC-AUC values of 0.9954 and MCC ≥ 0.981. On the SEER dataset, characterized by high heterogeneity and sparse recurrence signals, the fusion framework attained improved recall, while calibrated XGBoost offered the best overall balance between precision and stability. The findings demonstrate that combining tree-based embedded feature selection, latent AE compression, and transformer-based contextual frameworking yields consistent performance gains. Moreover, the Spark-GBT integration ensures scalability and suitability for multi-institutional environments where data centralization is restricted. The experimental results show that the proposed fusion framework provides competitive performance compared to strong baseline frameworks such as calibrated XGBoost, and improved recall and robustness for minority-class recurrence prediction. The results indicate that fusion learning improves sensitivity and framework stability, whereas calibrated XGBoost provides the strongest overall discrimination performance. The proposed framework presents a reliable, scalable, and clinically meaningful solution for individualized recurrence-risk prediction.

Introduction

Breast cancer continues to be one of the most prevalent malignancies affecting women globally and remains a major contributor to cancer-related mortality despite advancements in early detection and therapeutic strategies1. While substantial progress has been made in identifying primary tumors, the long-term clinical challenge lies in predicting recurrence, which encompasses local, regional, or distant relapse after initial treatment2. Recurrence poses a severe threat to patient survival, often necessitating additional systemic therapy, complex surgical interventions, and continuous monitoring. Accurate and early recurrence prediction is therefore crucial for risk stratification, tailoring adjuvant therapy, optimizing follow-up schedules, and improving clinical outcomes3,4. The task, however, is inherently complex. Breast cancer recurrence emerges from the interplay of heterogeneous factors, including tumor biology, hormonal receptor status, lymph-node involvement, treatment modalities, demographic parameters, lifestyle factors, and molecular signatures5. Clinical datasets reflecting these factors are typically high-dimensional, imbalanced, and heterogeneous, making traditional statistical frameworks insufficient. Compounding this difficulty is the dramatic growth of healthcare data generated across screening programs, hospital information systems, electronic medical records (EMRs), and cancer registries6. The datasets differ widely not only in structure and completeness but also in institutional storage formats, measurement standards, and diagnostic protocols. Such variations complicate the development of robust, generalizable recurrence-prediction frameworks7.

A further complication arises from data distribution across multiple institutions. Breast cancer data are seldom stored in a single centralized repository. Instead, hospitals, pathology labs, radiology centers, and oncology units maintain independent databases that are subject to strict privacy regulations such as HIPAA, GDPR, and local medical-governance policies8,9. Centralizing these datasets poses substantial challenges, including bandwidth limitations, data-transfer overhead, ethical restrictions, and risks associated with data breaches. The constraints severely restrict the applicability of classical machine-learning pipelines that assume a unified dataset stored locally10. Consequently, there is a growing demand for computational paradigms capable of efficiently training predictive frameworks on distributed, heterogeneous data while preserving data locality and privacy11.

Distributed computing frameworks such as Hadoop MapReduce12 and Apache Spark13 have emerged as promising frameworks for medical-data analytics because they support scalable, parallel computation directly where data reside. In the MapReduce paradigm, data are partitioned across nodes, processed in parallel, and aggregated without transferring raw patient records between machines14. Apache Spark extends this functionality with in-memory computation and optimized DAG execution, enabling faster processing of large-scale datasets and complex machine-learning pipelines15. These distributed frameworks are particularly well suited for cancer-informatics applications, where scalable preprocessing, feature engineering, and framework building must be performed without compromising data governance16. Despite their potential, relatively few breast-cancer recurrence studies leverage these distributed paradigms, particularly in combination with modern deep-learning architectures tailored for tabular clinical data.

This study presents a MapReduce-aligned hybrid framework that integrates distributed tree-based learning, deep tabular representation frameworks, calibrated probabilistic estimators, and multi-level feature fusion for breast cancer recurrence prediction. The proposed framework is designed not only to achieve high predictive accuracy but also to satisfy the practical constraints of distributed medical-data environments. First, the system incorporates embedded feature selection through Spark-based Gradient Boosted Trees (GBT), which naturally generate feature-importance values and operate efficiently in distributed memory. Second, a denoising autoencoder (AE) is employed to capture latent non-linear interactions and generate low-dimensional representations that reduce noise and redundancy. Third, the predictive backbone consists of diverse and complementary frameworks: Spark GBT (MapReduce-style parallel tree learning), FT-Transformer and TabTransformer state-of-the-art fusion framework that integrates handcrafted, latent, and tree-based representations.

This multi-framework design is motivated by the observation that no single architecture uniformly excels on clinical tabular data. Tree-based frameworks often outperform deep neural networks on structured features but lack the representational flexibility of deep frameworks, while Transformers excel at capturing high-order interactions but require appropriately normalized data. The proposed hybrid approach explicitly combines these complementary strengths, yielding a robust and generalizable recurrence-prediction pipeline applicable across datasets of varying complexity. Crucially, the use of Spark GBT within the pipeline maintains direct compatibility with Hadoop ecosystems, enabling the framework to be deployed seamlessly in distributed hospital environments where data movement is restricted. Related work

Research on breast cancer recurrence prediction has expanded significantly in recent years, driven by the increasing availability of multimodal clinical data and the need for precise risk stratification to guide follow-up care. Traditional clinical prognostic indices including tumor size, lymph-node involvement, histological grade, and molecular markers provide only partial understanding of recurrence dynamics. As a result, machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and hybrid analytic frameworks have emerged as powerful tools capable of frameworking complex, nonlinear patterns in longitudinal patient data. Existing studies demonstrate promising progress, yet they also reveal methodological gaps related to framework interpretability, data heterogeneity, feature sparsity, and limited support for distributed computation across multi-institutional clinical environments.

Several investigators have explored deep neural architectures for recurrence detection. Shankar et al. developed a comprehensive deep-learning ensemble using soft-voting strategies, achieving over 98% accuracy across diverse datasets17. Their results underscore the benefit of integrating multiple classifiers, though the framework relies on centralized data and does not address distributed settings. Lee et al. advanced recurrence prediction using histopathological whole-slide images, achieving high sensitivity for low- and intermediate-risk patients and strong specificity for high-risk groups18. While this image-based approach captures morphological cues unavailable in tabular datasets, it requires computationally expensive image pipelines and lacks integration with structured clinical variables. Similarly, Howard et al. combined digital histology with clinical features to predict recurrence assay results, demonstrating superior AUC (0.83) compared with traditional nomograms19.

Other studies emphasize molecular or biochemical data sources. Keogan et al. showed that Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) chemical imaging can provide prognostic signals for early-stage patients, though framework generalizability remains limited due to small cohort sizes20. Zhang et al. introduced the AIME framework for gene-level recurrence prediction and identified 25 important genes; however, functional pathways underlying their contributions remain underexplored21. Azman et al. leveraged demographic, clinical, and hormonal biomarkers using deep neural networks and principal component analysis, achieving accuracies up to 80%. Their feature-compression strategy is useful but limited in handling high-dimensional categorical covariates such as treatment regimens and tumor characteristics22.

Beyond deep learning, traditional ML methods continue to play an important role. Coombes et al. demonstrated that circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) profiling can anticipate metastatic recurrence with sensitivity and specificity exceeding 89% and 100%, respectively23. Despite its clinical promise, ctDNA assays require high sequencing depth and remain cost-prohibitive for routine surveillance. Lauritzen et al. employed ML to automatically identify recurrence cases within national cancer registries, achieving strong ROC-AUC performance (0.93), but their approach depends heavily on large, curated population-level datasets rarely available in developing healthcare systems24. Noman et al. integrated survival analysis with ML and obtained AUC values up to 0.92 using LightGBM; however, the imbalanced nature of recurrence data remained a persistent challenge25.

Several works highlight the performance of classical algorithms. Li et al. demonstrated that logistic regression and SVM can achieve near-perfect AUC (> 0.99) on certain structured datasets, though such high values may reflect dataset simplicity rather than framework superiority26. Mengad et al. compared multiple ML frameworks such as ANN, KNN, decision trees, and logistic regression reporting accuracies between 84 and 91%, reaffirming the utility of ensemble neural approaches27. Jiang et al. introduced an interpretable ML pipeline using Markov blanket feature reduction and SHAP explanations for predicting 5- to 15-year distant recurrence-free survival, highlighting the importance of dimensionality reduction for high-dimensional clinical features28. Belciug et al.29 and Mohebian et al.30 proposed hybrid systems that combine GA-based feature optimization with neural or tree frameworks, reporting strong specificity and sensitivity across multiple cohorts.

Recent hybrid frameworks also illustrate the advantage of combining deep learning with classical ML. Hussein et al. employed oversampling, PCA, GA-based dimensionality reduction, and a neural-network fusion of SVM and RF, achieving an accuracy of 98.3%31. Kumari et al. developed a broader hybrid deep-learning library (BCR-HDL) integrating convolutional, residual, and multilayer perceptron networks with traditional classifiers, demonstrating accuracies exceeding 97% on Wisconsin datasets. These frameworks reinforce the potential of hybrid frameworking but remain computationally centralized and do not emphasize distributed scalability or data-locality constraints32.

While existing literature demonstrates strong advances in feature selection, multimodal fusion, interpretability, and ensemble learning, several limitations persist:most frameworks are designed for centralized datasets and do not support distributed computation across hospitals;feature engineering remains either purely statistical or reliant on deep architectures without leveraging data-locality principles;limited attention is given to probabilistic calibration, error analysis, and robustness under class imbalance; andfew studies integrate deep tabular frameworks (FT-Transformer, TabTransformer) with tree-based learners and autoencoder representations within a unified framework.

Despite strong progress in ensemble frameworking, deep tabular networks, and recurrence biomarker discovery, existing approaches exhibit four key limitations: (i) limited support for distributed, MapReduce-compatible learning across heterogeneous clinical repositories; (ii) insufficient integration of deep latent representations with tree-based and calibrated frameworks; (iii) inadequate probabilistic calibration for clinical decision support; and (iv) lack of unified frameworks that jointly exploit feature importance, autoencoder compression, and Transformer-based contextualization. These gaps motivate the present study.

Proposed methodology

The proposed methodology integrates tree-based embedded selection, deep tabular representation learning, and a hierarchical fusion architecture. Each component is designed to complement the others—tree frameworks capture structured rules, Transformers extract contextual feature interactions, and the fusion head consolidates multi-view representations to maximize recurrence discriminability.

SEER dataset: The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) dataset utilized in this study was obtained from the November 2017 release of the SEER Program maintained by the National Cancer Institute (NCI), USA. The SEER database is a comprehensive population-based cancer registry that collects and publishes incidence and survival data covering approximately 34.6% of the U.S. population. For this investigation, data were extracted for female patients diagnosed with invasive breast carcinoma between 2000 and 2017. The dataset includes detailed clinicopathological and demographic variables such as age at diagnosis, race, ethnicity, tumor stage, tumor size, histological grade, lymph node involvement, and treatment modalities. The analytical consistency and remove potential confounding factors, patients with unknown tumor size, unrecorded regional lymph node data, or survival duration shorter than one month were excluded. After stringent preprocessing and cleaning, a total of 285 valid patient records were retained for framework development and evaluation. This dataset serves as a representative population-level cohort for assessing recurrence risk and validating the robustness of the proposed hybrid and ensemble frameworks.

Figure 2 illustrates the class distribution within the SEER-based breast cancer recurrence dataset. The dataset exhibits noticeable class imbalance, with No Recurrence cases (n = 200) significantly outnumbering Recurrence cases (n = 85). This disproportion indicates that recurrence represents a minority class, a common challenge in clinical prognostic datasets. Such imbalance can adversely affect supervised machine-learning frameworks by biasing predictions toward the majority class, thereby reducing sensitivity to recurrence events. The observed distribution underscores the necessity for class-balancing strategies including weighted loss functions, calibrated thresholds, sampling methods, or ensemble fusion to ensure robust and clinically meaningful recurrence prediction.Fig. 2Distribution of SEER dataset.

Distribution of SEER dataset.

Breast cancer Wisconsin dataset: The Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic) Dataset was employed as a secondary dataset to evaluate the generalizability of the proposed framework across morphological domains. It consists of 569 cytological records derived from fine-needle aspiration (FNA) of breast masses, each described by 30 continuous numerical attributes and one class label . These attributes include mean, standard error (SE), and “worst” (maximum) values of ten fundamental cell nucleus characteristics such as namely radius, texture, perimeter, area, smoothness, compactness, concavity, concave points, symmetry, and fractal dimension. The class distribution is moderately imbalanced, with 212 malignant and 357 benign instances, providing a practical test scenario for ADASYN-based balancing techniques. This dataset allows the investigation of recurrence-related morphological differentiation and supports comparative validation of the proposed Hybrid (ARL + CatBoost) and Stacked Ensemble frameworks under controlled experimental settings.

Figure 3 presents the diagnostic class distribution of the Wisconsin Breast Cancer dataset. Similar to the SEER dataset, the distribution demonstrates moderate class imbalance, where Benign cases (n = 360) are substantially more prevalent than Malignant cases (n = 215). Although less skewed than the recurrence dataset, this imbalance still requires methodological attention, as malignant cases carry higher clinical risk and merit precise identification. This distribution pattern highlights the importance of incorporating data-aware learning frameworks, such as cost-sensitive frameworking, calibration, and hybrid deep-learning fusion approaches, to prevent bias toward the more frequent benign class and improve malignant detection performance.Fig. 3Distribution of Winconsin dataset.

Data preprocessing: In this step need to design efficient and standardized preprocessing for designing the robust recurrence-prediction frameworks on heterogeneous datasets such as SEER and the Wisconsin Diagnostic Breast Cancer (WDBC) dataset. The nature of both datasets differ in structure SEER consists primarily of categorical registry variables, whereas WDBC contains high-resolution continuous morphological descriptors. To ensure compatibility with the hybrid Spark–MapReduce and deep learning pipeline, a unified preprocessing workflow was applied across all datasets.

Data cleaning and missing value treatment

The raw SEER recurrence dataset contained non-numeric placeholders and incomplete entries arising from manual registry collection. These were replaced with NaN tokens and removed using listwise deletion. In contrast, the WDBC dataset exhibited no structural missingness but included an extraneous identifier column, which was removed. In both datasets, any remaining missing values were imputed using feature-wise means to preserve the statistical properties of each variable while avoiding artificially skewed distributions. Deep tabular representation learning

FT-transformer

Each feature is treated as a “token”. TabTransformer

TabTransformer follows a similar tokenization but often uses a different pooling scheme max pooling and may share embeddings across features. In our study, treat each scalar feature as a token, embed it, pass it through Transformer blocks, and apply global max pooling:22\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_{i}^{Tab} = \mathop {\max }\limits_{j} E_{ij}^{L}$$\end{document}

Calibrated XGBoost

We train an XGBoost classifier on the original tabular features (and later on AE codes). XGBoost optimizes the following regularized objective:24\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_{XGB} = \mathop \sum \limits_{i} \ell \left( {y_{i} ,\hat{y}_{i} } \right) + \mathop \sum \limits_{k} \Omega \left( {h_{k} } \right),$$\end{document}where, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\ell}$$\end{document} is the logistic loss and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega \left({h}_{k}\right)$$\end{document} penalizes tree complexity. For each tree, the optimal leaf weights are derived from gradient and Hessian statistics, similar to Spark GBT.

To obtain calibrated probabilities, we wrap XGBoost with a Calibrated Classifier CV using sigmoid (Platt) scaling. Given raw scores \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${s}_{i}^{XGB}$$\end{document}, the calibrated probability is25\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{p}_{i}^{XGB} = \sigma \left( {as_{i}^{XGB} + b} \right),$$\end{document}where a and b are learned on cross-validation folds.

The above pseudocode shows the multi-stage fusion framework designed to enhance breast cancer recurrence prediction by integrating representation learning, calibrated gradient boosting, and meta-level deep classification. First, a denoising autoencoder is trained to learn robust latent representations by reconstructing noisy feature inputs, resulting in a compact embedding that captures non-linear relationships in high-dimensional clinical variables. The latent codes are used to train an XGBoost classifier, whose raw probabilities are further refined through sigmoid calibration to improve probability fidelity. The calibrated XGBoost scores are concatenated with standardized original features and autoencoder embeddings to form a fused meta-feature space. A meta-level multilayer perceptron is subsequently trained on this fusion vector using class-balanced loss and early stopping criteria, enabling the framework to jointly exploit handcrafted features, deep latent structure, and calibrated boosting outputs. The final decision is obtained by applying an F1-optimized threshold to the meta-level probabilities.

Decision threshold optimization

In clinical prediction problems, the default probability threshold of 0.5 may not be optimal, particularly for imbalanced datasets such as recurrence prediction where the minority class is clinically more important. Therefore, model-specific decision thresholds were selected using the validation set rather than the test set. For each model, predicted probabilities were obtained on the validation folds, and the optimal threshold was selected by maximizing the F1-score and MCC shows the balanced evaluation for imbalanced datasets. The optimal threshold t ∗ was selected as:31\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t* = \arg \mathop {\max }\limits_{t} F1\left( t \right)$$\end{document}and32\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$t* = \arg \mathop {\max }\limits_{t} MCC\left( t \right)$$\end{document}

The threshold that provided the best balance between precision and recall on the validation set was selected and then applied to the independent test set for final evaluation. This explains why different models have different optimal thresholds like 0.39 for XGBoost, 0.55 for Fusion, 0.90 for TabTransformer as each model produces different probability distributions and calibration characteristics.

Result analysis

The experimental evaluation was carried out on two benchmark datasets: the SEER Breast Cancer dataset and the Breast Cancer Wisconsin (Diagnostic) dataset, using Python 3.10 on a high-performance workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GPU and 32 GB RAM. Both datasets underwent rigorous preprocessing and stratified partitioning, maintaining an 80:20 ratio for training and testing to ensure unbiased generalization. The comparative study indicates that the fusion framework achieves the best balance between recall and precision, reflecting its ability to exploit both deep representations and tree-based decision boundaries. The FT-Transformer and TabTransformer frameworks capture complex feature interactions and provide competitive AUC values, while the MapReduce-aligned Spark GBT offers strong performance with the additional advantage of distributed training and data locality making it suitable for large-scale, multi-hospital deployments. Isotonic calibration consistently improves the probabilistic calibration of GBT and XGBoost outputs, as observed in Brier scores and calibration curves. The embedded feature selection via tree-based importance scores and AE latent codes reduces redundancy and supports robust generalization. The dataset was then split into 80% training (228) and 20% testing (57). All performance metrics and confusion matrices reported in the Results section correspond to this test subset (n = 57), which caused the apparent discrepancy in reported numbers.

Hyperparameter optimization strategy

The hyperparameters were selected using a stratified fivefold cross-validation combined with grid search optimization on the training dataset. The optimization objective was to maximize the Area Under the ROC Curve (ROC-AUC) and Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), as these metrics are more reliable for imbalanced clinical datasets than accuracy alone.

For tree-based frameworks such as Gradient Boosted Trees and XGBoost, key hyperparameters including the number of trees, learning rate, maximum tree depth, subsampling ratio, and regularization parameters were tuned. For deep learning frameworks including FT-Transformer, TabTransformer, and the Meta-MLP fusion network, hyperparameters such as learning rate, number of transformer layers, number of attention heads, embedding dimension, dropout rate, batch size, and number of training epochs were optimized using cross-validation.

Effect of removing AE latent codes: Eliminating the AE representation consistently degraded framework performance on both datasets, with notable reductions in MCC and F1-score. Effect of removing calibration: Removing isotonic calibration produced small but consistent decreases in ROC–AUC and PR–AUC, showing that calibrated probability estimates enhance risk stratification, particularly for low-incidence recurrence events in SEER. This validates the role of calibration in improving probabilistic trustworthiness.

Effect of removing fusion module: Eliminating the meta-learner reduced performance across all metrics, confirming that the fusion stage exploits complementary information from raw features, AE codes, and calibrated XGBoost outputs. This indicates that the meta-learner provides non-linear integration that cannot be captured by single frameworks.

Combined ablations: When both AE and calibration are removed, performance declines further, proving that each module contributes additive and synergistic benefits.

The ablation study confirms that each component of the proposed frameworks; AE-based representation learning, probability calibration, and the meta-level fusion module plays a distinct and complementary role in enhancing predictive performance. The AE encoder provides structurally meaningful latent variables, the calibration stage improves probabilistic reliability, and the fusion framework aggregates heterogeneous feature spaces into a unified decision layer. Removal of any component results in measurable performance degradation, while the full AE + Cal + Fusion pipeline consistently delivers the highest discriminative power across both SEER and Wisconsin datasets.

Discussion

The results obtained across the SEER and Wisconsin datasets demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed hybrid framework in frameworking heterogeneous and imbalanced clinical data for breast cancer recurrence prediction. SEER, being a categorical registry dataset with sparse recurrence indicators, inherently poses challenges for nonlinear discrimination. Here, calibrated XGBoost and the fusion framework offered the highest recall and stable precision, confirming the importance of combining tree-based decision boundaries with denoised AE representations that reduce feature sparsity. In contrast, the Wisconsin dataset exhibits clearer morphological separability, enabling near-perfect performance across all frameworks. The fusion and calibrated XGBoost frameworks achieved ROC–AUC > 0.995, PR–AUC > 0.993, and MCC ≈ 0.981, illustrating their capability to capture subtle malignant patterns in continuous radiological features.

Transformer-based frameworks (FT-Transformer and TabTransformer) showed competitive performance and effectively frameworked high-order feature interactions, particularly in the Wisconsin dataset. However, their performance declined on the SEER dataset due to its sparse categorical encoding. The LSTM + GRU architecture was comparatively weaker across both datasets, demonstrating that breast-cancer clinical variables do not possess inherent sequential structure suitable for recurrent neural networks.

The very high ROC-AUC and MCC values observed on the Wisconsin dataset are due to the strong separability of benign and malignant classes in the morphological feature space. Features such as concavity, radius, perimeter, and area exhibit clear statistical separation between classes, making the dataset less complex than recurrence prediction datasets such as SEER. Therefore, high classification performance on this dataset is consistent with previously published studies that report ROC-AUC values above 0.98 for advanced ensemble and boosting frameworks. The cross-validation results further confirm that the high performance is consistent across folds and not due to overfitting. The ablation study further reinforces the role of each component. AE latent codes improved generalizability by denoising heterogeneous inputs, isotonic calibration enhanced probability reliability a critical requirement for risk stratification and the fusion head provided non-linear integration of multi-view representations, consistently improving predictive stability.

Some limitations remain. SEER’s recurrence labels and categorical granularity limit precise boundary learning. Class imbalance continues to challenge rare recurrence detection, though weighting and calibration mitigate its impact. The frameworks were evaluated on two datasets; additional validation on prospective, multi-institutional cohorts is necessary. Nevertheless, the study demonstrates significant clinical value: the hybrid architecture can be deployed in distributed hospital ecosystems due to the MapReduce-aligned Spark-GBT foundation, enabling real-time, privacy-preserving recurrence prediction without centralizing sensitive records. The findings highlight that hybrid deep-tabular and tree-based ensembles, supported by distributed computation, offer a robust, scalable pathway for enhancing clinical decision-support systems in oncology.

One of the practical challenges in breast cancer recurrence prediction research is access to large-scale clinical datasets due to privacy regulations, ethical approvals, and institutional permissions. Clinical datasets such as SEER require formal access procedures, and hospital-level datasets are often not publicly available. These restrictions limit the availability of large distributed datasets for research and model validation. The distributed learning framework proposed in this study is particularly useful in such scenarios, as it allows models to be trained across multiple institutions without requiring centralized data sharing, thereby preserving data privacy and governance policies.

The comparative results indicate that no single framework is uniformly optimal across all evaluation metrics. The calibrated XGBoost framework achieved the highest overall accuracy, MCC, and ROC-AUC, indicating strong discrimination capability. However, the proposed fusion framework achieved the highest recall and competitive ROC-AUC indicate its effectiveness in detecting minority-class recurrence cases. This suggests that the fusion framework improves sensitivity and robustness by combining latent representations, tree-based learning, and deep tabular feature interactions. Therefore, while calibrated XGBoost provides the strongest standalone performance, the fusion framework offers a more balanced prediction framework, particularly for clinically important recurrence detection.

Conclusion and future scope

This study introduces an integrated, MapReduce-aligned hybrid framework for breast cancer recurrence prediction that unifies distributed tree-based learning, AE-driven representation compression, deep tabular architectures, and calibrated ensemble frameworking. The framework effectively addresses key challenges including heterogeneity of clinical features, class imbalance, and distributed data governance constraints. Evaluation on SEER and Wisconsin datasets demonstrates that the calibrated XGBoost and fusion frameworks consistently deliver the strongest performance, exhibiting high discrimination, excellent calibration, and robust minority-class detection. The distributed Spark-GBT backbone ensures scalability for real-world deployments across multi-hospital environments. The comparative results indicate that no single framework is uniformly optimal across all evaluation metrics. The calibrated XGBoost framework achieved the highest overall accuracy, MCC, and ROC-AUC, indicating strong discrimination capability. However, the proposed fusion framework achieved the highest recall and competitive ROC-AUC, demonstrating its effectiveness in detecting minority-class recurrence cases. This suggests that the fusion framework improves sensitivity and robustness by combining latent representations, tree-based learning, and deep tabular feature interactions. The calibrated XGBoost provides the strongest standalone performance, the fusion framework offers a more balanced prediction framework, particularly for clinically important recurrence detection.

Future work will focus on extending the framework to incorporate multi-omics inputs such as genomics, radiomics, and digital pathology, enabling more comprehensive recurrence-risk characterization. Integration with federated learning platforms may further enhance privacy-preserving framework training across geographically dispersed clinical sites. Exploration of explainable AI (XAI) modules including SHAP, LIME, and counterfactual explanations will improve clinical interpretability and facilitate adoption. Additionally, real-time decision-support integration with hospital information systems and prospective clinical trials will be pursued to validate generalizability and operational impact. Overall, the proposed framework provides a scalable and clinically meaningful direction for future AI-driven recurrence-prediction systems.
