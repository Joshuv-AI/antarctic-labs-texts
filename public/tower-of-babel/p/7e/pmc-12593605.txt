Acute effects of ischemic preconditioning and isometric exercise on blood pressure in prehypertensive males
Authors: Kheng Win Khor, Kuan-Yu Lin, Chih-Wen Hsu, Jong-Shyan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigates whether 5-min ischemic preconditioning (IR5) more effectively reduces submaximal exercise blood pressure and improves muscle oxygenation compared to 2-min ischemia (IR2) or 2-min isometric priming (IM2).MethodsTwenty untrained men (10 prehypertensive; 10 normotensive) completed, in randomized order, IR5 (3 × 5 min bilateral limb occlusion at 220 mmHg), IR2 (3 × 2 min bilateral occlusion), and IM2 (3 × 2 min 30 % maximal voluntary contraction isometric knee extension), each followed by the submaximal exercise test. Outcomes were exercise heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), and vastus lateralis muscle oxygenation including Δdeoxyhemoglobin (ΔHHb), Δtotal hemoglobin (ΔtHb), and Δtissue saturation index (ΔTSI).ResultsIR5 significantly attenuated the submaximal pressor response, reducing SBP by mean difference (MD) = −2.23 mmHg (P < .001, ηp2 = 0.395) compared with both IR2 and IM2. A significant Condition × Group interaction (P = .023) indicated that the MAP reduction was confined to prehypertensive participants (MD = −3.14 mmHg, P = .003). During exercise, IR5 increased ΔtHb by MD = 1.56 μmol/L (ηp2 = 0.606) and mitigated the decline in ΔTSI by MD = 2.98 % (ηp2 = 0.716) without altering ΔHHb. In contrast, IR2 and IM2 elicited similar occlusion-phase deoxygenation but did not modify exercise hemodynamics or oxygenation metrics.ConclusionIR5 effectively lowers SBP and MAP during submaximal exercise and improves muscle oxygenation. It is a safe, non-pharmacological pre-exercise strategy to manage blood pressure and potentially reduce cardiovascular risk.Keywords: Blood pressure, Cardiovascular risk, Exercise preconditioning, Ischemic preconditioning, Isometric exercise, Prehypertension

Introduction

Hypertension is an urgent global public health concern, affecting approximately 1.2 billion people worldwide and increasing in younger populations. WHO aims to reduce prevalence by 33 % by 2030.1 It is a major risk factor for cardiovascular disease and associated events like coronary artery disease and stroke. Therefore, controlling blood pressure is crucial. The American Heart Association (AHA) recommends non-pharmacological interventions, starting with lifestyle modifications, among which regular exercise is considered the most proactive approach.2 Various exercise forms effectively lower blood pressure, including aerobic exercise, resistance training, walking, yoga, and high-intensity interval training.3 Studies indicate isometric exercise shows the greatest potential for reducing systolic blood pressure.4

Ischemic preconditioning (IPC) has progressed from a medical cardioprotective tool to a pre-exercise ergogenic strategy. Research has demonstrated positive effects on various exercise modes like maximal treadmill testing,5 sprints,6 swimming,7 and resistance exercise.8 IPC physiological mechanisms are not fully understood, but hemodynamically, IPC and isometric exercise similarly involve blood flow restriction. Isometric contractions can induce transient, localized ischemia within the muscle, followed by reperfusion upon relaxation, which may trigger vasodilatory responses, enhance muscle oxygenation, and potentially improve endothelial function, resembling the repeated ischemia–reperfusion pattern of IPC.5,9,10 These interventions have rarely been compared directly. Following blood flow restriction, increased blood flow and oxidative stress are typically observed,8 possibly due to vasodilatory mediators such as nitric oxide (NO); enhanced endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) mRNA after blood-flow–restriction exercise has been interpreted as a shear stimulus–driven upstream regulator of vascular endothelial growth factor signaling.9 Such vascular adaptations could potentially improve oxygen delivery to working muscles during exercise while simultaneously reducing blood pressure. Beyond vascular pathways, IPC may also modulate central drive (reduced inhibitory group III/IV afferent feedback), justifying a pre-match protocol to test its ability to limit neuromuscular fatigue in soccer.11,12

Acute IPC rapidly activates a neuro-humoral–cellular network that preserves endothelial function during subsequent mechanical or metabolic stress.5,10 Brief limb ischemia–reperfusion recalibrates autonomic balance via mechano-/chemosensory afferents, tempering sympathetic vasoconstrictor drive and supporting shear-responsive NO signaling.10 Concurrent adenosine/bradykinin GPCR activation engages PKC–PI3K/Akt–ERK cascades to prime eNOS and sustain NO.5,13 KATP channel opening plus early redox containment limits ROS amplification, delays mPTP opening, and reduces NO scavenging.14,15 Together these processes preserve flow-mediated dilation, forming a rapid endothelial protection network.

Most IPC studies use a protocol of 220 mmHg for 5 min followed by 5 min of reperfusion, repeated 3–4 times; alternative protocols haven't shown superior results.16, 17, 18 Isometric exercise research typically uses four sets of 2-min contractions at 30–50 % maximum voluntary contraction (MVC) with rest, performed multiple times weekly.19 Higher MVC intensities show potential effectiveness but are difficult to sustain due to rapid peripheral fatigue, metabolic stress, and autonomic stress responses.20,21 Although most research examines isometric exercise in the context of long-term interventions, some evidence indicates that acute protocols can also be effective.22

Near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) can measure microvascular oxygenation during blood flow restriction. blood flow restriction at 130 % systolic pressure 5 min before exercise enhanced exercise-induced metabolic stress during low-intensity isometric exercise compared to controls or immediate pre-exercise application.23 Acute IPC intervention has been shown to improve local skeletal muscle oxygenation during exercise24, 25, 26 and may activate mitochondrial enzymes, improving cellular respiratory efficiency and oxygen extraction capacity.24 While previous studies examined long-term blood pressure-lowering effects of both IPC and isometric exercise, acute intervention studies are scarce27,28 Therefore, this study aims to compare different IPC and isometric exercise protocols and their hemodynamic changes to identify the most suitable pre-exercise conditioning method for hypertension.

Methods

Participants

Twenty untrained men (age 30–50 years) were enrolled and allocated to a prehypertensive group (n = 10) or a normotensive group (n = 10). Prehypertension was defined as resting seated systolic blood pressure (SBP) 120–139 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) 80–89 mmHg in the absence of antihypertensive medication. Normotension was defined as SBP <120 mmHg and DBP <80 mmHg.29 All participants were free from regular exercise training in the previous 6 months and had no history of cardiovascular, metabolic, renal, or endocrine disease. Eligibility was confirmed by resting blood pressure screening and the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire (PAR-Q). Exclusion criteria included treated hypertension beyond the prehypertensive range, musculoskeletal injury, or any medical condition likely to influence exercise performance or acute hemodynamic responses. Participants were instructed to abstain from strenuous exercise, alcohol, and caffeine for 24 h before each visit. An a priori sample size calculation (G∗Power 3.1) based on the previously reported effect size30, 31, 32 indicated that 24 participants were required for α = 0.05 and 80 % power. Allowing ∼8 % attrition, we planned to recruit 26 participants.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (IRB No. 202400133B0D0001), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Environmental conditions

The laboratory environment was strictly controlled (25.0 °C, 60–70 % humidity) and free from disturbances to ensure consistent measurements.

Research design

The study used a mixed design with one between-subject factor (blood pressure status: prehypertensive vs. normotensive) and one within-subject factor (condition: baseline control, 2-min ischemic preconditioning (IR2), 5-min ischemic preconditioning (IR5), and 2-min isometric contraction (IM2). The baseline visit functioned as the control (no pre-intervention stimulus). Each participant subsequently completed the three intervention visits in a randomized Latin square order, separated by washout intervals of at least 72 h (Fig. 1).

Blood pressure

Blood pressure was measured with an automated oscillometric device (Tango + exercise blood pressure monitor; SunTech Medical, Witney, UK) at baseline (two visits, ≥24 h apart), during the exercise interventions, and during recovery. Measurements were taken on the non-dominant arm supported at heart level. Participants avoided caffeine, vigorous exercise, and smoking for ≥60 min beforehand. Following a 5-min seated rest, two readings about 1 min apart were averaged for each visit. All sessions were scheduled at the same clock time to minimize circadian variation.

Muscle oxygenation

Muscle oxygenation was monitored continuously via near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS; Oxymon, Artinis, Netherlands) with the probe positioned 15 cm above and 5 cm lateral to the patella on the vastus lateralis muscle. Data were collected at a sampling frequency of 10 Hz throughout all experimental phases (baseline, intervention, exercise test, and recovery periods). The collected parameters included deoxyhemoglobin (HHb), total hemoglobin (tHb), and tissue saturation index (TSI). Skinfold thickness at the measurement site was required to be less than 10 mm to ensure signal quality.

The NIR light consisted of two wavelengths (780 and 850 nm), and micromolar changes in oxygenated (O2Hb) and deoxygenated (HHb) hemoglobin were calculated using age-dependent differential path-length factors (DPF; range: 4.95–6.12).33 Total hemoglobin (tHb, sum of O2Hb and HHb) and tissue saturation index (TSI) were also recorded. Data were collected at a sampling frequency of 10 Hz throughout all experimental phases (baseline, intervention, exercise test, and recovery periods). Skinfold thickness at the measurement site was required to be less than 10 mm to ensure signal quality.

Ischemic preconditioning

Three cycles of 220 mmHg occlusion (5 or 2 min) with equivalent reperfusion periods, following 5-min seated rest, were performed on bilateral lower limbs using a portable intelligent pressure training device (Model YDK-1, Yi Dong Kang, Shenzhen, China). The cuff position was standardized by marking 7 cm above and below the intersection point of the inguinal crease and midline of the thigh proximal to the superior border of the patella.

Isometric exercise

Three cycles of 2-min isometric knee extension at 30 % MVC with 2-min rest intervals were performed using a Biodex isokinetic dynamometer (System 4 Pro, Biodex Medical Systems, NY, USA). The knee joint was positioned at 70° flexion, with the joint range calibrated between 0° and 90°. MVC was determined during the baseline testing period prior to the intervention by averaging two 5-s maximal effort trials with 1-min rest between attempts.

Submaximal exercise

The YMCA cycle ergometer protocol was performed starting at 25W (0.5 kg resistance, 60 rpm). Subsequent stages were determined by first-stage heart rate (HR) response (<80 bpm: 125W; 80–89 bpm: 100W; 90–100 bpm: 75W; >100 bpm: 50W), with 25W increments until reaching 70 % HRR. Each stage lasted 3 min, preceded by 5-min warm-up. Blood pressure and HR (Polar H10, Polar Electro Inc., Kempele, Finland) were recorded every minute; RPE every 3 min.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A two-way mixed ANOVA was conducted with Group (prehypertensive vs normotensive) as the between-subjects factor and Condition (CON, IR5, IR2, IM2) as the within-subjects factor. Sphericity was assessed with Mauchly's test; when violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. Effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared ηp2. Significant omnibus Condition effects or Group × Condition interactions triggered Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons. Pairwise mean differences were expressed as mean difference (MD) ± standard error (SE) with adjusted P values. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.5.

Discussion

Effects of different interventions on cardiovascular responses

IR5 attenuated the rise in SBP and MAP during subsequent submaximal exercise, whereas IR2, IM2, and control showed no differences; DBP remained stable. IR5 elicited only a modest HR reduction, indicating that the acute benefit primarily reflected a lower arterial pressure cost per unit oxygen delivery rather than strong chronotropic suppression or a large fall in systemic vascular resistance. The blunting effect was driven mainly by high-normal/prehypertensive participants, consistent with greater hemodynamic plasticity and a floor effect in normotensives4,19; the group × condition interaction for MAP supports this. Prior work often reports negligible acute BP change after remote or limb IPC in normotensive cohorts.18 A single 5-min bilateral lower-limb protocol likely provides a larger integrated shear and mediator release dose than unilateral or shorter exposures, yielding higher peak and time-integrated reperfusion shear that augments endothelial and metabolic vasodilator mobilization and microvascular flow redistribution.16,34, 35, 36 Extending a single cycle to 5 min outperforms merely increasing the number of short cycles.16 IM2 likely produced only partial perfusion restriction with subthreshold reperfusion shear and metabolic signaling; differing contraction dose structures (e.g., multiple brief vs fewer prolonged sets) produce distinct cardiovascular outcomes.37 Although meta-analytic evidence suggests a limited single-session IPC or isometric hypotensive effect mainly on MAP,18,38 these findings indicate that dose and timing optimization in high-normal/prehypertensive individuals may hold translational value.

A 5 mm Hg reduction in systolic blood pressure lowers major cardiovascular event risk by about 10 %.39 Importantly, the focus should not be solely on the absolute blood pressure values, but rather on the consistent relative risk reduction across different baseline levels. Moreover, acute attenuation of exercise-induced BP spikes may reduce transient cardiovascular stress, particularly in vulnerable populations. Repeated application of these interventions could potentially induce cumulative vascular adaptations, supporting further investigation into their long-term benefits.

Comparative mechanisms: IPC vs isometric contraction

Both IPC and isometric contraction achieve antihypertensive effects through NO-dependent endothelial vasodilation, involving endothelial progenitor cell (EPC) mobilization, VEGF signaling, and activation of the eNOS-NO pathway.35 Additionally, both are accompanied by parasympathetic reactivation and baroreflex sensitivity recovery, reducing peripheral resistance and supporting improvements in endothelial function and vascular compliance.27,36,38,40

IPC induces a context-dependent, bidirectionally modulates skeletal muscle vascular responsiveness: at rest it augments the sympathetic stimulus–induced vasoconstrictive response, whereas during moderate exercise it enhances functional sympatholytic. These findings suggest that IPC can precondition local vascular control in the absence of actual exercise-induced metabolic demand.41 IPC stimulates ischemia-reperfusion, reducing baseline skin microvascular resistance and increasing resting blood flow velocity, demonstrating systemic effects without enhancing maximal heat-induced vasodilation.34 Moreover, IPC is associated with parasympathetic reactivation, including increased high-frequency HRV power and reduced plasma norepinephrine concentration, accompanied by gradual contralateral brachial artery dilation.36

Isometric contraction relies on reactive hyperemia and shear stress activation in local muscles, with blood pressure reduction influenced by exercise intensity, duration, and recruited muscle mass, which also affect muscle oxygenation and autonomic regulation.9,19,42 Peak shear stress activates eNOS-NO-mediated endothelial vasodilation,43, 44, 45, 46 followed by parasympathetic reactivation and baroreceptor sensitivity restoration, reducing peripheral resistance.27,38,40 Enhanced endothelial function, arterial compliance, and dose-dependent modulation of ET-1 and inflammatory markers further support its antihypertensive effects.37,47

Effects of different interventions on skeletal muscle oxygenation

During cuff occlusion, IR5 and IR2 produced nearly overlapping declines in ΔHHb and ΔTSI, both exceeding IM2, confirming that 2 min sufficed to reach deoxygenation saturation; the shallower IM2 change reflects only partial flow restriction. During subsequent submaximal exercise, ΔHHb did not differ, but ΔtHb and ΔTSI followed a graded pattern: IR5 > IR2 ≈ IM2 > control. This indicates that IR5 converted the longer ischemic exposure into superior perfusion–oxygenation support, consistent with more complete capillary recruitment and endothelial-mediated flow regulation.48 Prior work showed transient increases in thigh ΔHHb and tHb shortly after local IPC without performance gains when high-intensity testing was delayed by 45 min.49 The immediate transition to exercise here likely aligned with peak reactive hyperemia and neurohumoral signaling, enabling translation of microvascular and oxygenation advantages into reduced pressure requirements.12,50 Although the optimal IPC-to-exercise interval remains unresolved and timing is only one heterogeneous factor, converging evidence suggests initiating the target exercise within 10–30 min maximizes capture of reactive hyperemia and autonomic modulation.50,51

Limitations

This study included only untrained adult males with prehypertension or normotension, limiting generalizability to females and other populations; sex-specific factors—such as menstrual cycle–related hormonal fluctuations that can modulate vasomotor and cardiovascular responses52—were outside the scope of this male-only design. All post-IPC assessments occurred within an approximately 20-min vasodilatory window,53 so persistence beyond that period is unknown. Furthermore, the study did not directly assess arterial stiffness, endothelial function (e.g., FMD), or autonomic regulation (HRV or MSNA), rendering mechanistic interpretations indirect. A fixed absolute cuff pressure (200–220 mmHg) was used without Doppler confirmation of individualized arterial occlusion pressure, allowing potential variability in the actual ischemic stimulus. Additionally, employing a fixed control group of individuals without hypertension or prehypertension may have introduced selection bias arising from baseline differences (e.g., age, lifestyle factors, or other characteristics) between the groups, which may in turn have influenced the observed associations. Although an a priori power calculation indicated that 24–26 participants were required (80 % power, α = 0.05), only 20 were ultimately enrolled. Only a single acute IPC bout was examined, preventing inference about chronic vascular or blood pressure adaptations.

Future studies should: (1) include women with hormonal status/menstrual phase characterization; (2) individualize arterial occlusion pressure; (3) map time- and cycle-based ischemic dose–response and define the minimal effective dose; (4) incorporate direct vascular and autonomic measures; and (5) evaluate multi-session and longitudinal protocols to determine durability and translational relevance.

Conclusion

In this acute mixed-design experimental study involving untrained prehypertensive and normotensive men, only the 3 × 5-min bilateral ischemic preconditioning protocol (IR5) attenuated the systolic and mean arterial pressure responses to subsequent submaximal cycling; diastolic pressure was unchanged and heart rate reductions were modest. The mean arterial pressure effect was confined to the prehypertensive subgroup, indicating greater acute hemodynamic plasticity and a floor effect in normotensive participants.

During occlusion, 5- and 2-min full ischemic cycles produced virtually identical local deoxygenation (ΔHHb, ΔTSI), indicating that a 2-min bout is sufficient to reach the desaturation plateau. However, only IR5 converted this shared extraction ceiling into superior exercise-phase oxygenation/perfusion (higher ΔtHb and ΔTSI without additional ΔHHb), implicating a duration-dependent augmentation of reperfusion shear, endothelial–metabolic signaling, and possibly autonomic modulation that lowers the arterial pressure cost of a fixed submaximal workload—particularly in prehypertensive individuals. IR2 and IM2 still elevated ΔtHb and ΔTSI versus control and were broadly similar, yielding a graded pattern (IR5 > IR2 ≈ IM2 > control).

Although the absolute reductions in systolic and mean arterial pressure were modest (≈2–3 mm Hg), such acute differences could be meaningful if reproducible across sessions and integrated into regular pre-exercise preparation. Brief 2-min ischemic cycles and short isometric contractions, as implemented here, were insufficient to blunt the pressor response.

In summary, extending each ischemic preconditioning bout to 5 min provides a distinct acute cardiovascular and microvascular advantage over shorter ischemic or isometric priming in prehypertensive men and may be considered a time-efficient pre-exercise conditioning strategy.
