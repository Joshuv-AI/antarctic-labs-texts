Noise Reduction Protocol and its Impact on Sleep Quality and Delirium Among ICU Patients in A Tertiary Care Hospital in New Delhi
Authors: Himanshi Lakra, Ruchi Gupta, Raminder Kalra, Arshdeep S Chadha, Sumit Ray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionNoise in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU) is a major environmental concern affecting patient outcomes. ICUs frequently record excessive noise from alarms, equipment, staff activity, and procedures—often surpassing the WHO limits of 35 dB by day and 30 dB at night. Sustained exposure disrupts sleep and is closely linked to ICU delirium. Although global evidence emphasizes these risks, noise control practices remain limited in many Indian ICUs. This study examined whether implementing a structured noise-reduction protocol could lower sound levels, enhance sleep quality, and reduce delirium among ICU patients in a selected New Delhi hospital.ObjectivesTo determine the effectiveness of a structured noise reduction protocol in:reducing ICU noise levels,improving sleep quality, anddecreasing the incidence of delirium among ICU patients.Materials and methodsA five-month prospective interventional study was conducted in three phases: a two-month pre-intervention observation, a one-month protocol implementation, and a two-month post-intervention assessment. Using purposive sampling, 109 adult ICU patients were enrolled (50 pre-intervention, 59 post-intervention). Environmental noise was recorded every 30 minutes and at eight key daily time points, including nurses’ handovers, visiting hours, quiet times, cleaning hours, doctors’ rounds, and nighttime. Sleep quality was measured using the Sleep Quality Score (SQS), and delirium was assessed with CAM-ICU. The noise-reduction protocol consisted of staff education, alarm optimization, behavioural cues, visual reminders, and minor layout modifications.Resultsthere was a statistically significant reduction in mean environmental noise levels following protocol implementation (p < 0.05). However, the absolute noise reduction was minimal, ranging from 0.2 to 0.5 dB, reflecting the inherent structural limitations of ICU settings. Despite limited measurable reduction in decibel levels, patient-reported sleep quality improved significantly in the post-intervention phase. Moreover, the incidence of ICU delirium decreased substantially from 28% in the pre-intervention group to 11.9% post-intervention (p = 0.033), demonstrating a clinically meaningful impact.ConclusionThe structured noise reduction protocol led to improved sleep quality and a significant reduction in delirium incidence, despite only marginal changes in measured noise levels. Short-term interventions may not dramatically lower physical noise due to ICU operational demands; however, behavioural and organizational strategies can meaningfully enhance patient outcomes. Continued staff training, longitudinal implementation, and multicentric studies are recommended to strengthen evidence and promote noise control as a standard component of ICU care bundles.
