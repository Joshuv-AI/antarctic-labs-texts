Sociodemographic differences in clinical phenotypes among patients with COPD: a latent class analysis
Authors: Caretia JeLayne Washington, Shama D Karanth, Gerard A Silvestri, Susmita Datta, Shantrel Canidate, Michael K Gould, Dejana Braithwaite
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a heterogeneous condition whose clinical severity may be influenced by social factors. We aimed to identify distinct COPD clinical phenotypes and assess variation by social determinants of health.MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, we identified adults aged 50–80 years with a diagnosis of COPD (n=59 797) at a tertiary academic medical centre in North-Central Florida using codes from International Classification of Diseases. Latent class analysis defined COPD clinical phenotypes using indicators of clinical severity, including frequency of acute care encounters (urgent care, emergency department visits and hospitalisations), presence of COPD as the principal diagnosis, comorbidity burden and use of Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease Group D medications. Kaplan-Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards models assessed mortality across phenotypes. Multinomial logistic regression models estimated associations between phenotype membership and race/ethnicity, income, rurality and smoking status, using the minimal phenotype as reference.ResultsFive clinical phenotypes were identified: minimal (20.9%), mild (35.2%), moderate (22.5%), severe (12.2%) and very severe (9.3%). The very severe phenotype had the highest mortality (adjusted HR 2.94; 95% CI 2.72 to 3.18). Odds of very severe COPD were higher among non-Hispanic Black (adjusted OR (aOR) 1.29; 95% CI 1.21 to 1.36) and Hispanic individuals (aOR 1.75; 95% CI 1.63 to 1.87), those in the lowest income communities (aOR 1.25; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.32), rural residents (aOR 1.80; 95% CI 1.68 to 1.92) and individuals who currently smoke (aOR 1.30; 95% CI 1.20 to 1.42).ConclusionMost patients with COPD had mild disease; however, the very severe phenotype, which was associated with higher mortality, was more common among Black and Hispanic individuals, those residing in lower-income and rural areas and those who currently smoke. These clinical phenotypes highlight sociodemographic differences in COPD severity as reflected in healthcare utilisation and outcomes.Keywords: Pulmonary Disease, Chronic Obstructive; Clinical Epidemiology; COPD epidemiology

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive lung condition characterised by persistent airflow limitation.1 It is the sixth leading cause of death in the USA, accounting for about 150 000 deaths annually.2 COPD is clinically heterogeneous, with wide variability in symptom burden, comorbidities and outcomes.3

Historically, COPD severity has been defined primarily by spirometry, using the forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) as the primary measure.1 However, spirometry is inconsistently documented and until 2023, race-based prediction equations underestimated lung function in Black patients.4 5 The 2023 update from the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) reflects a paradigm shift by incorporating symptom burden and exacerbation history in the assessment of COPD severity.6 Emerging evidence also suggests that disease burden varies across population subgroups, highlighting the need for social risk-targeted phenotyping.7

There is growing interest in defining COPD clinical phenotypes, which are subgroups of patients with shared characteristics and clinical trajectories.8 Clinical phenotypes that incorporate comorbidities and treatment patterns may better inform prognosis and personalised care.8 Comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, depression and osteoporosis are highly prevalent in those with COPD and are associated with increased mortality.9 Identifying comorbidity and care-utilisation patterns aligns with a clinically oriented framework that emphasises modifiable aspects of disease management to better characterise heterogeneity in COPD severity and outcomes.10

Despite growing recognition of COPD heterogeneity, clinical classifications still rely heavily on FEV₁.11 There is a critical need for data-driven approaches that identify distinct phenotypes using clinical information captured in real-world healthcare settings, such as comorbidities and medication use. Researchers often use machine learning methods, particularly clustering methods, for this purpose. While hierarchical clustering and k-means are commonly applied,12 latent class analysis (LCA) can identify unobserved subgroups and may provide a more accurate reflection of population-level heterogeneity.13 14

Given Florida’s older, diverse population and high prevalence of COPD, reaching approximately 17% in some North Central Florida counties, defining COPD clinical phenotypes is essential for targeted care.15 This study aims to use LCA to identify clinical phenotypes of COPD using indicators of clinical severity, specifically comorbidities, COPD medications and acute care encounters and to evaluate whether these phenotypes differ by social determinants of health (SDOH). We further examined differences in all-cause mortality across the COPD clinical phenotypes. Since COPD is a major comorbidity among individuals eligible for lung cancer screening (LCS), we restricted our analyses on the LCS-relevant age group (50–80 years). This approach places the findings within a population important for both COPD management and cancer prevention.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using deidentified electronic health record (EHR) data from the University of Florida (UF) Health System, a large tertiary academic centre located in North-Central Florida and a member of the OneFlorida+ Clinical Research Consortium. OneFlorida+ aggregates EHR data, claims and other health data across the state and select southeastern institutions. UF Health was chosen due to its consistent capture of variables, including detailed smoking history, necessary to identify COPD phenotypes. This study was deemed exempt from full review by the UF Institutional Review Board because the data were de-identified.

Patient and public involvement

Patients nor the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research.

Study cohort

The study cohort consisted of 59 797 patients aged 50–80 years who had a recorded diagnosis of COPD between January 2016 and July 2023. This age range was selected to align with the US Preventive Services Task Force LCS guidelines; smoking criteria were not applied since the focus was COPD phenotyping rather than screening eligibility. COPD diagnoses were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes (490, 491, 494, 496) and ICD-10-CM codes (J40, J41, J42, J44, J47, J98.2, J98.3). The index date was defined as the earliest COPD diagnosis observed during the study period, regardless of whether it represented the patient’s initial diagnosis of COPD. Patients were excluded if they had a prior diagnosis of lung cancer, haemoptysis or unexplained weight loss, to avoid diagnostic confounding. Descriptive statistics for the variables included in the LCA are presented in online supplemental table 1.

COPD phenotype indicators

Variables included in LCA were age at index date (<65 years and ≥ 65 years), sex (as a biological variable), principal diagnosis of COPD for the encounter and the following clinically modifiable indicators of disease management and burden: comorbidity profiles, COPD medication use and number of acute care encounters. Age and sex were included to account for their potential influence on COPD presentation and progression. The number of acute care encounters for any indication (inpatient hospital stays, emergency department visits and urgent care visits) was categorised as <2 or ≥ 2 during the study period. The presence of a principal diagnosis of COPD for the encounter was determined based on whether COPD was coded as the primary ICD diagnosis for each encounter. Fourteen key comorbidities associated with COPD (congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular disease, depression, dementia, cancer excluding lung and non-melanoma skin cancer, chronic kidney disease, diabetes with complications, obesity, paralysis, rheumatoid arthritis, moderate/severe liver disease, atopy and weight loss) were classified as binary (yes/no) variables.16 17 A complete list of ICD-9/10 codes is available in online supplemental table 2.

COPD medications were categorised based on the GOLD criteria for pharmacologic treatment groups A through D.1 Group A included short-acting bronchodilators (eg, albuterol and/or ipratropium), Group B included long-acting beta agonists (LABAs) or long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMAs), Group C included LAMA monotherapy and Group D included LAMA+LABA combinations or LABA+inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) therapies. Other medications such as montelukast, roflumilast, systemic corticosteroids (eg, prednisone, Rayos) and cardioselective and non-cardioselective beta-blockers were also recorded. Individuals who were not prescribed COPD medications were classified as untreated. Medication codes and classifications are detailed in online supplemental table 3.1

Exposures

Sociodemographic variables included race/ethnicity, rurality, income, education and smoking status. Self-reported race/ethnicity was categorised as non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH-Black, Hispanic and Other (NH-Asian, NH-Native American/Alaska Native, NH-Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander) in accordance with the US Office of Management and Budget standards for race/ethnicity classification.18 Individual-level data were used for age, race/ethnicity and smoking status. For variables without individual-level data, we used county-level data linked to patients’ ZIP codes. Rurality was classified using the 2013 Rural-Urban Commuting Area Codes, which categorised areas as either metropolitan or non-metropolitan (referred to as rural). Median household income was obtained from the 2023 Florida Health Charts at the county level and categorised according to statewide quartiles: US$38 905–US$54 231 (1st quartile; lowest-income), US$55 070–US$66 154 (2nd quartile; middle-low income), US$66 581–US$74 534 (3rd quartile; middle-high income) and US$75 011–US$106 169 (4th quartile; high income).19 Education was determined by the percentage of adults in the county population without a high school diploma, using data from the 2010 US Census Bureau Statistical Atlas. Counties were categorised as either low-education counties (≥13%) or high-education counties (≤12.9%). Smoking status was categorised as current, former, never or unknown.

Statistical analysis

To identify distinct COPD clinical phenotypes, we conducted a LCA using priori-selected clinical variables based on the literature and clinical relevance.12 16 17 LCA is a statistical method that uses values of a set of variables to identify unobserved groups of individuals with similar characteristics.13 Models with 2–7 latent classes were estimated. Model fit was assessed using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Sample-Sise Adjusted BIC (SABIC), Lo-Mendell-Rubin test, entropy and likelihood ratio χ2 tests, with details in online supplemental table 4.20

The optimal number of classes was based on the combination of statistical fit indices, clinical interpretability and practical utility of the resulting classes. Although AIC, BIC and SABIC values decreased with increasing number of classes, they began to plateau after the five-class model, suggesting diminishing returns in model fit beyond five classes. The five-class solution also demonstrated a significantly better fit than the four-class model based on the Lo-Mendell-Rubin test. Entropy for the five-class model remained excellent (0.91), supporting adequate class separation. Together, these statistical indicators and the clinical interpretability of the resulting subgroups supported the selection of the five-class model. An elbow plot illustrating the relative fit of each model is included in online supplemental figure 1. Patients were assigned to latent classes based on their highest posterior probability of class membership, which was estimated during model fitting using the Expectation-Maximisation algorithm. These probabilities reflect the likelihood that a patient belongs to each class, given their observed characteristics. Class profiles were detailed in online supplemental table 5 and summarised using heat maps in online supplemental figure 2. These latent classes were subsequently interpreted in the context of observed clinical management patterns, with disease severity characterised by acute care encounters, comorbidity burden, principal diagnosis of COPD and use of Group D medications. The five latent classes were labelled as minimal, mild, moderate, severe and very severe, with ‘minimal’ indicating the lowest clinical severity and healthcare utilisation among patients with COPD.

To assess the robustness of the identified latent classes, a sensitivity analysis was conducted restricting the sample to patients with the primary diagnosis of COPD for the encounter. Latent class models with 2–7 classes were re-estimated in this subgroup using the same indicators and model selection criteria (AIC, BIC, SABIC, entropy, Lo-Mendell-Rubin test) (online supplemental table 6). Conditional probabilities and class prevalences in the primary diagnosis of COPD cohort were compared with those from the full cohort to ensure that class formation was not driven solely by the presence of the ICD code (online supplemental tables 7 and 8).20

Descriptive statistics summarised the characteristics of the overall cohort and each phenotype. To assess the prognostic validity of the COPD phenotypes, we examined their association with overall survival and all-cause mortality using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards models. Differences in survival across phenotypes were assessed using log-rank tests. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate adjusted HRs (aHRs) and 95% CIs for mortality, with the minimal COPD phenotype as the reference group. Models were adjusted for race/ethnicity, income, rurality and smoking status. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using were using Schoenfeld residuals and the global proportional hazards test. COPD phenotypes, race/ethnicity and smoking violated the proportional hazards assumption and were therefore modelled as time-varying exposures by including interaction terms with the logarithm of time in the Cox models. Visual inspection of Schoenfeld residual plots from the initial models supported these findings. After inclusion of time-dependent terms, residual plots showed no meaningful departures from proportionality, indicating that the final models adequately satisfied the proportional hazards assumptions (online supplemental figure 3). Missing values for income, rurality and smoking status were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE; 20 imputations).21

We then examined how sociodemographic factors were associated with phenotypes. Multivariable multinomial logistic regression models estimated adjusted ORs (aOR) and 95% CIs for the association between phenotype and sociodemographic factors, using the minimal phenotype as reference. Race/ethnicity, income, rurality and smoking status were modelled as primary exposures, adjusting for one another. Missing data for income, rurality and smoking status was addressed again using the MICE approach. Model assumptions were validated with local independence and multicollinearity tests. Forest plots display aORs. Analyses were performed in R V.4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2024).

Results

A total of 59 797 patients were included in the LCA. Five clinically distinct COPD phenotypes were identified based on the probability distributions: minimal (class 1), mild (class 2), moderate (class 3), severe (class 4) and very severe (class 5). Class 1 was characterised by having the fewest number of acute care encounters (≥2; 25.5%) and no Group C or Group D medications (table 1). Class 2 showed slightly more acute care encounters ≥2 (41.1%) and was more likely to use Group C medications than class 1 (57.4%). Class 3 had a higher probability of using Group D medications (98.7%) and had a higher likelihood of having a principal diagnosis of COPD for the health encounter (21.3%) than class 1 (3.3%) or class 2 (9.8%). Class 4 exhibited high multimorbidity (eg, congestive heart failure: 38.7%; depression: 41%; diabetes with complications: 41.2%), an 11.0% chance of weight loss and frequent acute care encounters (79.5%). Class 5 had the highest probability of acute care encounters (86.1%), the highest principal diagnosis of COPD for the encounter (37.2%), high Group D medication use (99.1%) and demonstrated a high burden of comorbidities (eg, depression: 48.2%). Class 5 was also the youngest (61.7% <65 years) and more likely to be female (59.9%) (online supplemental table 7).

In a sensitivity analysis restricting the cohort to encounters with a primary COPD diagnosis (n=8357), the distribution of class membership differed from the original cohort (n=59 797), with a higher proportion of individuals classified as severe (class 4: 11.7% vs 12.2%) and very severe (class 5: 19.3% vs 9.3%) and fewer classified as minimal (class 1: 5.0% vs 20.9%). In the total study population, most participants were NH-White (74.8%), resided in metropolitan areas (77.4%) and approximately one-third were current smokers (33.2%) (table 2). Nearly two-thirds lived in counties with median incomes that were either middle-low or middle-high.

The minimal COPD phenotype (20.9%) included more individuals from low-education counties (24.5%). The mild phenotype, the most common (35.2%), had a high proportion of NH-White individuals (78.7%) and residents in low-income counties (6.7%). The moderate phenotype (22.5%) had the most rural residents (17.0%), NH-White individuals (79.9%) and current smokers (38.8%). The severe COPD phenotype (12.2%) was associated with a high proportion of NH-Black individuals (30.1%) and those residing in metropolitan areas (81.6%). The very severe group was categorised by the highest proportion of NH-Black individuals (32.6%) and a high proportion of current smokers (36.1%).

Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated significant differences in survival by phenotype, where the very severe group experienced the lowest survival probability (log-rank p<0.0001) (figure 1). In multivariable Cox proportional hazards models, each phenotype was associated with significantly elevated mortality risk compared with the minimal group: mild (aHR 1.45; 95% CI 1.36 to 1.54), moderate (aHR 1.78; 95% CI 1.67 to 1.90), severe (aHR 2.18; 95% CI 2.03 to 2.34) and very severe (aHR 2.94; 95% CI 2.72 to 3.18) (table 3).

Race/ethnicity showed distinct patterns: NH-Black individuals had lower odds of mild COPD (aOR 0.77; 95% CI 0.70 to 0.84) but higher odds of moderate (aOR 1.19; 95% CI 1.02 to 1.42), severe (aOR 1.25, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.42) and very severe COPD (aOR 1.29; 95% CI 1.21 to 1.36). Hispanic individuals also had substantially higher odds of very severe COPD (aOR 1.75; 95% CI 1.63 to 1.87). County-level median household income was inversely associated with COPD severity, as individuals living in the lowest-income counties had higher odds of severe (aOR 1.49; 95% CI 1.20 to 1.84) and very severe COPD (aOR 1.25; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.32). Rural residence had higher odds of all COPD phenotypes compared with minimal COPD, with the greatest odds among the very severe COPD phenotype (aOR 1.80; 95% CI 1.68 to 1.92). Current smoking status was positively associated with very severe COPD (aOR 1.30; 95% CI 1.20 to 1.42), while former smoking status was negatively associated with very severe COPD (aOR 0.77; 95% CI 0.71 to 0.83).

Discussion

In this cohort study of nearly 60 000 patients with COPD, we identified five distinct clinical phenotypes using LCA that reflected patterns of disease severity and clinical management within the healthcare system. These phenotypes were associated with a stepwise increase in mortality, with the very severe phenotype experiencing nearly threefold higher mortality compared with the minimal phenotype. The observed gradient in mortality provides prognostic value, demonstrating that these phenotypes capture clinically meaningful differences in outcomes despite the absence of spirometry or symptom data. This study had three additional key findings. First, phenotypes revealed stark racial/ethnic differences, as Black and Hispanic individuals had increased odds of belonging to the very severe phenotype. Second, SDOH, including income and rurality, were consistently associated with more severe phenotypes. Third, smoking status showed differential associations, with current smoking linked to very severe COPD and former smoking associated with reduced odds, suggesting treatment responsiveness with cessation. Together, these findings demonstrate that comorbidity burden, healthcare utilisation and social context are closely related to COPD outcomes and may not be fully captured by spirometry-based definitions alone. While not intended to replace spirometry or symptom-based assessment, this work adds to the growing body of evidence supporting multidimensional COPD phenotyping and highlights consequences of excluding clinical management and structural factors when such data are unavailable.

These phenotypes are not intended to function as a standalone clinical risk stratification tool or to directly guide individual management decisions. Consistent with established definitions of clinical phenotypes,22 which emphasise characteristics associated with meaningful outcomes, the latent classes were derived from indicators of clinical management and healthcare utilisation. Because of this, they reflect patterns shaped by provider decision-making, access to care and systemic factors.23 Therefore, these findings illustrate how existing clinical and structural factors cluster together and should be incorporated along with spirometry and other measures in COPD phenotyping, as these factors are associated with prognosis at the population level. The underlying indicators, comorbidity, acute care encounters and COPD medications may help distinguish patients with similar airflow limitation but different clinical trajectories.24 Acute care encounters with a principal diagnosis of COPD plausibly capture severe exacerbations requiring urgent intervention, while use of ‘other’ COPD medications, such as roflumilast, represents a history of frequent exacerbations and refractory disease, further supporting the clinical relevance of these indicators.25 Prior studies have shown that exacerbation burden, healthcare utilisation, dyspnoea, quality of life and mortality vary substantially among patients with comparable spirometric severity, emphasising the limitations of relying on airflow limitation alone.25,28

From a clinical perspective, the primary utility of this phenotypic approach lies in population-level risk stratification rather than dictating phenotype-based treatment algorithms. Patients with severe and very severe phenotypes, now shown to have markedly worse survival, may benefit from multidisciplinary care models and enhanced comorbidity management.26 29 Management decisions, however, should remain individualised and guided by patient symptoms, comorbidities and priorities. In addition, phenotype-specific severity may inform assessments of the balance of benefits and harms in LCS among patients with COPD.30 31 Future studies integrating spirometry, patient-reported outcomes and longitudinal clinical data are needed to translate these phenotypes into tools applicable at the individual patient level.

Racial/ethnic differences in COPD clinical phenotypes may reflect disproportionate comorbidity burden, delayed diagnosis and disparities in disease management. Prior work has identified a COPD phenotype, the preserved ratio impaired spirometry with parenchymal abnormalities, that disproportionately affects Black participants, potentially delaying diagnosis and treatment.32 33 Despite lower documented airflow obstruction among African Americans, Hispanic individuals and Native Americans, higher levels of self-reported dyspnoea have been observed.34 Among Hispanic populations, heterogeneity in COPD severity may be influenced by insurance coverage, language proficiency, health literacy and healthcare access.35 Environmental and neighbourhood disadvantages further contribute to disparities, as Hispanic and NH-Black individuals have shown higher odds of COPD exacerbations in response to heat and air pollution compared with NH-White individuals.36

Lower county-level income and rurality were also associated with increased odds of severe and very severe COPD. Prior studies have linked neighbourhood disadvantage and rurality to higher mortality, excess hospitalisations and increased exacerbation risk among patients with COPD, independent of smoking status.37,40 These findings suggest that limited access to care and structural barriers play an important role in shaping COPD severity and outcomes.

Current smoking status was strongly associated with the very severe phenotype, consistent with its proinflammatory effects and known associations with lung function decline and COPD mortality.41,43 Treatment response also differs by smoking status, as demonstrated in the Study to Understand Mortality and Morbidity in COPD trial, where ICS-based therapy was less effective among individuals who currently smoke compared with individuals who previously smoked.44 Individuals who currently smoke are also more likely to exhibit exacerbator phenotypes and more severe dyspnoea patterns.45

This study has notable limitations. Its retrospective, single-institution design may limit generalisability and reliance on ICD codes and EHR data may introduce misclassification due to variability in provider documentation and coding practices. Because the phenotypes were derived from patterns of clinical management and healthcare utilisation, they may reflect provider behaviour, access to care or systemic biases rather than physiological disease severity. In particular, COPD medication use may be influenced by prescribing decisions, documentation practices and differential access to care, potentially biasing class assignments. The absence of spirometry, imaging and symptom data prevented examining correlations between latent classes and comorbidity severity was not fully captured, which may have affected phenotype precision. Importantly, these phenotypes reflect severity as operationalised within the healthcare system rather than patient-experienced severity, as patient-reported outcomes (including symptoms, exercise tolerance, psychosocial outcomes and activities of daily living) were unavailable. Individual-level SDOH factors were also unavailable, which may introduce ecological fallacy. Nonetheless, our study has significant strengths. We leveraged a large, racially, ethnically and geographically diverse cohort with rich EHR data, allowing identification of COPD phenotypes. The application of LCA allowed us to systematically characterise multidimensional heterogeneity in COPD severity and clinical management, integrating multimorbidity, medication use and acute care utilisation in a data-driven manner. Importantly, we assessed these phenotypes through survival analysis, demonstrating their association with all-cause mortality. By linking phenotypes expressed in the healthcare system to poor clinical outcomes, this study provides evidence that commonly available EHR variables capture meaningful dimensions of COPD severity that are highly relevant for population health and health equity research.

Conclusion

Our study identified five distinct COPD clinical phenotypes reflecting patterns of disease severity and management that were strongly associated with sociodemographic characteristics and all-cause mortality. Although these phenotypes do not replace spirometry or patient-reported measures, they provide insight into how COPD severity is expressed within the healthcare system and help identify populations at elevated risk. Future studies incorporating physiologic and patient-centred outcomes are needed to translate these findings into actionable strategies to improve personalised care for patients with COPD.
