Advanced liposomal nanoplatforms for flavonoid delivery: a mechanistic and comparative perspective in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Poonam Lal, Jitendra Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a common and life-threatening type of liver cancer, and its treatment results are frequently restricted by late diagnosis, drug resistance, systemic toxicity, and limited specificity of traditional treatment methods, including chemotherapy, surgery, and targeted therapy. Flavonoids, which are a family of naturally-occurring polyphenolic compounds, have been of significant interest in the past few years, based on their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory, anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic characteristics. These compounds can control major signaling pathways related to tumor development and progression and are good candidates for the treatment of HCC. Poor aqueous solubility, high metabolism, and low bioavailability, however, greatly limit the clinical use of flavonoids. The delivery system of flavonoids based on liposomes is the possible solution to these restrictions as they preserve the stability, solubility, and tumor-targeting characteristics and protect the flavonoid against degradation, encasing it directly into a vesicle of phospholipid. The improvement of therapeutic performance has been implemented through various approaches to formulation, which are inclusive of thin-film hydration, PEGylation, ligand-mediated targeting, and stimuli-responsive systems. The review has provided the latest developments in liposomal delivery of flavonoids as a tool in the application of HCC which include formulation, physicochemical characterization, and therapeutic effects of flavonoids based on in vitro and in vivo experiments. It is also applied by giving a comparative study with conventional therapies. Moreover, such crucial issues about clinical translation as formulation stability, regulatory standards, expandability, and lack of clinical evidence are severely tackled. Lastly, the outlook of the future in the areas of targeted delivery, combination therapy, and clinical validation are described. In general, the liposomal delivery of flavonoids is a compelling approach to enhancing the therapeutic response of hepatocellular carcinoma.Graphical Abstract

The risk of alcohol-related liver lesions and cirrhosis is higher in females than in males, probably because of the effects of hormones, such as estrogen. Drinking alcohol amplifies the inflammatory and oxidative stress caused by elevated levels of steroids and other female hormones, which are processed by the liver, which is responsible for hormone metabolism. In the liver, alcohol metabolism produces acetaldehyde, a highly toxic byproduct that can damage hepatocytes and cause adduct formation, which in turn can damage mitochondria and deactivate the glutathione peroxidase system. Alcohol and its dangerous byproducts have several negative effects, including DNA synthesis and repair problems in liver cells, increased proinflammatory signaling, and ROS generation leading to oxidative stress [7].

A number of studies have linked NAFLD to an increased likelihood of developing HCC. Metabolic syndrome, diabetes mellitus, and obesity are among the most pressing public health issues worldwide. Some 70% of diabetics and 90% of obese adults have NAFLD [8]. That being said, NAFLD could be a cause of HCC. Even when hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) are not co-infected, peril of HCC is 2.5 times higher in diabetic persons [9]. Furthermore, NASH, HLV) and HCC can all develop as a result of the inflammation brought on by fat buildup in the liver. A European study found that hepatocarcinogenesis was more likely in people who smoked. Liver illness and alcohol-related deaths were both associated with tobacco usage [10].

Aspergillus flavus and Aspergillus parasiticus produce aflatoxin B1, a fungal toxin that raises the risk of hepatocarcinogenesis when ingested orally; this contamination is common in resource-poor places with stored grains like wheat and peanuts. Mycotoxins bind to DNA in hepatocytes in chronic or cirrhotic HBV livers, where they induce mutations in mitotically active genes (namely the p53 gene). These mutations lead to changes in tumor suppressor or proto-oncogene genes [11].

Treatment

As a matter of fact, several curative and palliative dealings exist for HCC. Considerations such as patient's age, number of comorbidities, the available resources, the level of experience of the medical staff, and the stage of the cancer should all go into selecting the most suitable treatment.32 By factoring in important HCC criteria, BCLC category divides patients. very early, early, middle, progressed, and terminal [12]. When patients are in initial stages, potential curative therapies contain resection, ablation, and transplantation [13]. The Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system is the most popular model of hepatocellular carcinoma management, which combines tumor burden, liver functioning, and patient performance status to make treatment decisions. The most recent update of BCLC 2022 presents a more adaptable methodology in the form of clinical decision-making (CDM), which enables the selection of treatment options on a case-by-case basis, depending on multidisciplinary assessment [11].

In the new model, the curative treatment of early-stage disease (BCLC 0 0 A) is mainly performed through surgical resection, liver transplantation, or ablation. Intermediate-stage (BCLC-B) disease now incorporates broader indications of transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) and acknowledges heterogeneity in this category. [14] Management has greatly changed to advanced-stage (BCLC-C), with immunotherapy-based combinations (e.g., atezolizumab plus bevacizumab) substituting older tyrosine kinase inhibitors as first-line therapy. Best supportive care is given to terminal-stage (BCLC-D) patients [15, 16].

Notably, the 2022 update focuses on the migration of treatment stages and personalized care, noting that rigid algorithm-driven decisions might not be applicable to every patient and should be tailored to clinical circumstances [17]. The Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system was updated in 2022, introducing a clinical decision-making approach and emphasizing immunotherapy combinations as first-line treatment in advanced HCC [18–20].

This study integrates physicochemical characterization, targeting strategies, pharmacokinetics, and a decision-based design framework, providing a more comprehensive translational perspective.

Lipid-based carrier systems

In vivo medication failure causes due to Toxic effects, low solubility, unanticipated bioavailability, inadequate drug metabolism, and inadequate absorptionand/or distribution. To get around these problems, using the right drug carrier system is crucial for controlled and localized drug release. One potential material for these delivery systems is lipid-based components [28].

Lipids are the perfect nanocarrier system to expand aqueous solubility, bioavailability, and effectiveness of APIs because to their physicochemical characteristics, which include slow water uptake, low susceptibility to erosion events, and biocompatibility [29]. Lipid-based systems also prevent API oxidation, degradation, and decomposition while alliancing API delivery and storage. Because lipid-based drug delivery systems may pass through the blood vessels, gut, GIT, and BBB, they have a significant advantage over other delivery methods. [30]Liposomes, nanostructured lipid carriers (NLCs), SLNs, and self-emulsifying systems are a few lipid-based drug delivery methods [31, 32].

Liposomes

Liposomes are thought to be the most popular medication delivery technique available. This medication delivery mechanism is known as a phospholipid vesicle or lipid bilayer [33]. Liposomes' excellent drug loading efficiency and simple production techniques have garnered a lot of interest. Furthermore, liposomes are able to accurately and gently transport their payloads.Liposomes are mesomorphic structures that contain lipid, phospholipid, and water. Liposomes are hydrophobic vesicles that include phospholipid molecules that encapsulate and amphiphilic, controlled release of lipid-soluble, and water-soluble compounds; this enhances efficiency of medicines, nutraceuticals, and other bioactive substances. Pharma companies have been able to use liposomes thanks to approval from the USFDA since 1995 [34].

Unlike micelles, liposomes typically have a hydrophilic core and closed phospholipid bilayer layers. Amphiphilic, hydrophobic, and hydrophilic substances can all be found in liposomes. To put it another way, the hydrophilic core contains the polar material, whereas the lipid domains' bilayers may contain the hydrophobic substances. The term "liposome" (meaning "body" in Greek) is the origin of the term "nanoliposome," which describes lipidic entities with dimensions on the nanoscale [33, 35]. Nanoliposomes are lipid vesicles that are on the nanoscale, as opposed to the microscale, where the term liposome is used to describe a wider variety of lipid vesicles. The number of internalized vesicles, lamella count, and diameter range are used to classify liposomes and nanoliposomes. Formation of tiny unilammelar vesicles encased in a bilayer membrane is one method for creating lipid vesicles. [38] Double bilayer vesicles are composed of two concentric lipid bilayers and generally possess diameters exceeding 300 nm, whereas large unilamellar vesicles, typically ranging from about 20 to 100 nm, consist of a single bilayer membrane. Multilamellar vesicles contain more than five concentric bilayers, while oligolamellar vesicles are characterized by the presence of three to five bilayers [39]. Giant unilamellar vesicles represent another category of vesicles formed by a single large bilayer structure. In multivesicular vesicles, some lesser, non-concentric vesicles are encapsulated in a larger bilayer liposomal compartment. Commonly employed techniques for liposome preparation include reverse-phase evaporation, thin-film dispersion, spray-freeze-drying,and the alcohol injection method [40].

Liposome formulation methods are very important in the characterization of physicochemical characteristics, drug loading performance, stability and therapeutic efficacy of lipid-based nanocarriers. Thin-film hydration, ethanol injection, reverse phase evaporation, and microfluidics are the most commonly used practices as each of them has its strengths and weaknesses depending on the desired use.

The most common and widely used technique of liposome preparation is the thin-film hydration method. The process follows a similar method incorporating the phospholipids in an organic solvent which is then evaporated to create a thin lipid film that is then hydrated using an aqueous layer with the drug. This process normally forms multilamellar vesicles with extremely big measures, frequently presented past 200 nm, needs extra abridgement measures that may comprise sonication or extrusion. Despite a medium- to high-encapsulation efficiency especially in the case of hydrophobic compounds, e.g., flavonoids, it exhibits batch-to-batch variability and has poor scalability making its use in the industrial manufacturing process limited [41].

An easier and more reproducible alternative is the method of ethanol injection which involves dissolving lipids in ethanol and injecting it into an aqueous phase, resulting in the automatic creation of liposomes. The technique produces small unilamellar vesicles that have a rather homogeneous size distribution with a low polydispersity index, usually less than 0.3. Nevertheless, encapsulation ability of hydrophilic drugs is relatively low because of the fast diffusion rate during encapsulation. Irrespective of this shortcoming, it has the merit of being a powerful technique of creating nanosized liposomes applicable in targeted drug delivery and can be used in heat-sensitive agents [42].

Reverse phase evaporation is a more innovative version of a method that is aimed at increasing drug loading capacity. This technique involves the formation of water-in-oil emulsion, then the organic solvent is removed at reduced pressure to produce large unilamellar vesicles. This facilitates high encapsulation efficiencies, especially with hydrophilic drugs, which is normally in excess of 70–80. Nevertheless, the use of organic solvents poses some concerns on toxicity, traces of solvents and degradation of sensitive substances. Also, the process is not that desirable in the large scale production because of the intricacy and security.

More recently, there has been the development of microfluidics-based techniques which are a very rigorously controlled and scalable measure of fabricating liposomes. This is a method whereby microchannel systems are used, as a way of taking quantitative control over the mixing of lipid and aqueous systems, leading to the formation of consistently-sized liposomes with narrowly-distributed sizes, and with low polydispersity index, usually less than 0.2. Microfluidics also has Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compatible reproducibility, scalability and compatibility, which makes it especially applicable to clinical translation. In addition, it enables finetuning of the formulation variables (e.g., flow rate, lipid composition, and concentration) and therefore, allows optimization of encapsulation efficiency and particle size [43].

In a comparison compared to their conventional counterparts like thin-film hydration, the former has a higher usage in laboratory scale studies and preliminary formulations screening, and the latter is more favored as a technology because of its accuracy and repeatability to the production in the industry and clinical practice. Ethanol injection method is in between, so is more simple and more controllable, and reverse phase evaporation especially is favourable when the loading of drugs in high percentage is to be considered. Thus, the chosen liposome formulation technique must be influenced by the required size of the particles, the properties of the drugs, the efficiency of encapsulation, and the ability to scale, particularly in cancer treatment, whereby particles targeted to a tumor must be precisely controlled [44, 45].

Physicochemical characterisation

Physicochemical characterization is very important in the definition of the performance, stability and therapeutic efficacy of the flavonoid-loaded liposomal systems. These parameters have a direct impact on the biodistribution, cellular uptake, drug release behavior and general clinical translation potential. Thus, to maximize liposomal formulations in the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a criterion assessment of physicochemical characteristics is necessary [64].

Particle size is one of the paramount parameters, it has a great impact on tumor targeting and on the circulation time. The range of liposomes smaller than 200 nm is generally regarded as the best to use in the case of passive targeting using the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Smaller vesicles have better penetration properties of the tumor, and large sizes can be quickly eliminated through the reticuloendothelial system (RES). The polydispersity index (PDI) which is used along with size will give an idea of evenness of size distribution. A PDI of less than 0.3 depicts a homogenous formulation and this is what is needed to be reproducible and predictable in biological behavior [49].

The other important parameter is the zeta potential that represents the stability and the surface charge of liposomes. The values of approximately ± 30 mV are normally regarded to be adequate to avoid the combination of electrostatic repulsion. Neutral liposomes are likely to be extended in their circulation and positively charged liposomes have the ability to increase cellular uptake and also rise susceptibility to cytotoxicity along with early clearance. Hence optimization of the surface charge should be done thoroughly as per the intended use.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) or scanning electron microscopy (SEM) are usually used to study the morphological features of liposomes. These methods verify the shape, the lamellarity and integrity of the vesicles. Well-established liposomal system is manifested by spherical and uniformly distributed vesicles. Any disproportions in morphology could imply instability or inconsistencies in formulation [65].

The other significant parameter, which is used to determine the level of flavonoid that is incorporated into the liposome, is called the encapsulation efficiency (EE%) or the level of flavonoid that is incorporated into the liposome. Encapsulation efficiency is also needed to be high, so that it can be well loaded and achieve therapeutic effect. Lipid composition, the mode of preparation and solubility of drugs are some of the factors that affect EE. In case of hydrophobic flavonoids it is generally an easy task to incorporate the compounds into the lipid bi-layer, however, the hydrophilic compound may need optimization measures.

The in vitro methodology that is employed to determine the drug release profile is the dialysis technique. It should possess controlled and sustained release, which offers long acting therapeutic effect and less frequent dosing. PH-sensitive release, especially in HCC targeted system is beneficial in that tumour microenvironment is marginally low in pH relative to normal tissues. [66]This stimuli- Responsive behaviour improves site-selective drug delivery with reduced toxicity to the body.

The stability testing would be required to determine the shelf life of liposomal formulations and their strong stability. They are normally done under varying storage conditions, including 4 °C and 25 °C in order to determine time variations in the size of the particles, zeta potential, and leakage of the drug. Lipid peroxidation has also been noted to destabilize formulations and hence the oxidative stability of lipid components in a formulation is a crucial factor as well.

Also, the technique of preparation used, e.g., ethanol injection induced, thin-film hydration or microfluidics, also has a significant influence on physicochemical properties. An example would be that microfluidic methods have enhanced capability to control the size of particles and reproducibility as opposed to the traditional methods, which can cause batch variations. Thus, the choice of the methods is to be adjusted to the formulation desirable features and scalability requirements [67, 68].

The characteristics of Flavonoid-loaded liposomes are that they have a particle size of between 80 and 150 nm, which is believed to be the most effective in terms of tumor accumulation using the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect [69]. Polydispersity index (PDI) is usually kept at an index below 0.3 and this implies that the homogeneity of the formulation and homogeneity of the size. Zeta potential values are typically between -20 to -40 mV, and it provides the stability of the colloidal suspension, as it avoids aggregation. Different lipid preparations and methods of preparation are known to offer encapsulation efficiencies of flavonoids in the liposomal systems of 70–95, depending on lipid composition [70]. Therapeutic release extends during in vitro drug release experiments which as a rule show a sustained drug release profile and release approximately 60–80% of drug during 24–48 h of the experiment.

Comprehensive physicochemical characterization, in general, offers comprehensive information in regards to quality, stability, and liposomes characterized by flavonoids loaded. These parameters both shape in vitro and in vivo behavior, as well as are decisive parameters in regulatory approval and achievement of clinical translation.

Comparative analysis

Flavonoids and liposomal delivery strategies are the main strategies that should be critically compared to define which therapeutic approach is the most efficient in managing hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [71]. In spite of promising anticancer activity in a large number of studies, it is necessary to have a structured assessment based on variability in molecular targets, delivery efficiency, and translational potential. Quercetin, luteolin and silibinin are some of the well-researched flavonoids that demonstrate different pharmacological activities. Quercetin is a powerful antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic receptor, and it uses the PI3K/ Akt and JAK/STAT signaling pathways, and is a strong candidate in the inhibition of tumor growth and immune evasion. Its application in the clinic is however limited because of its low solubility and metabolism [72]. Luteolin, conversely, displays strong anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic effects and suppresses NF-xB signaling causing tumor-related inflammation. Silibinin (a flavonolignan) demonstrates better hepatoprotective and antioxidant activity, thus it is specifically applicable to liver-targeted therapy, although its anticancer ability is relatively moderate [73]. It shows that the selection of flavonoids cannot be done in a general manner, and quercetin should be used in cytotoxic effect, luteolin in inflammation prevention, and silibinin in liver protection.

At the same time, liposomal formulation plans have a major bearing on performance during therapy. PEGylated liposomes are able to increase the time of circulation and decrease reticuloendothelial system (RES) remuneration which in effect is beneficial in increasing passive targeting through the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Nonetheless, it can potentially cause the so-called accelerated blood clearance (ABC) phenomenon with repeated use that can restrict its long-term effectiveness. Ligand-targeted liposomes, which are receptor-functionalized (CD44, EpCAM or ASGPR) provide a better cellular-specificity and uptake overcoming the shortcoming of passive-targeting. However, they are susceptible to expression variation of the receptor and could prompt formulation complexity and cost. Stimuli-responsive liposomes which incorporate pH-sensitive systems and enzyme-sensitive systems offer controlled and site-specific release of drugs, especially in the tumor microenvironment where is acidic such as in HCC. These systems however, need careful tuning and can also have problems with stability in the course of storage and circulation.

Another significant analysis of liposomal preparation methodology makes the point of critical differences. The traditional thin film hydration is simple to use in that it commonly leads to non homogenous particle size and low reproducibility. The ethanol injection techniques lead to smaller sized vesicles with relatively homogenous size distribution but which can be affected by low encapsulation efficiency. Microfluidic-based synthesis, in turn, is more controllable in terms of particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and batch-to-batch reproducibility and is more appropriate to large-scale production although it is more expensive to start with. The differences highlight the need to use preparation methods depending on the aspects of scalability and consistency.

There are physicochemical properties that also impact on comparative performance. Liposomes; those that are smaller than 200 nm particle size, PDI is less than 0.3 and has a zeta potential of about ± 30 mV are the ones that exhibit the best stability and can tumor target. Failure to maintain such parameters may cause aggregation, excessive clearance or decreased therapeutic effects. Nevertheless, numerous studies do not present standardized characterization data and thus, cross-study comparison is challenging and reduces reproducibility.

Well, a considerable amount of gaps still exists in the translational perspective. Although numerous publications can prove the in vitro cytotoxicity and in vivo efficacy, a few studies have integrated pharmacokinetic values, safety analysis, and clinical validation. In addition, the variation in the design of the experiments, absence of standardization in report, and over dependence on the EPR-based targeting minimizes the reliability of the inferences. The future studies therefore must focus on comparative benchmarking, characterization which is of a standardized nature and modules that are clinically relevant.

Comparative analysis of the various liposomal approaches reveals that PEGylated liposomes have better pharmacokinetic rates with the half-life of circulation and improvement up to about 2–3 folds in comparison to the traditional liposomes. The microfluidic preparation methods give more uniform vesicles with PDI values of approximately below 0.2 and a smaller variation in size contrast to traditional thin-film hydration methods which tend to have wider size distribution with PDI value above 0.3. Ligand-targeted liposomes are characterized by superior uptake in cells with many suggesting 1.5—twofold higher accumulation of drugs in the cell compared others. These quantitative disparities underscore the fact that the right formulation strategies should be chosen, depending on the needs of therapy.

In general, the comparative review shows that there can be no best flavonoid or liposomal action. Rather, successful therapy is a matter of combining the optimal choice of flavonoid, optimal delivery, and application-directed, which points to the necessity of more rational and evidence-based approach in the development of liposomal drugs at HCC [65].

Mechanism of action

The anticancer practices of the flavonoid interaction loaded liposomes in hepatic cell carcinoma (HCC) are placed under a synergistic interaction between improved delivery through nanocarriers and inherent bioactivity of flavonoids at the molecular level. Liposomal encapsulation enhances solubility, prevents flavonoid degradation by metabolism, and enhances systemic circulation duration, and both passive and active tumor targeting in preferential tumor localization. It leads to the substantially greater intracellular concentrations of drugs in comparison to free flavonoids and therefore increases their effects on oncogenic signaling networks.

Liposomal uptake at the cellular level is mainly acquired through endocytosis-mediated internalisation such as clathrin-dependent, caveolae-mediated, and macropinocytosis. After internalization, liposomes can either escape the endosome or be degraded into the lysosome and release flavonoids to the cytoplasm. This pathway of delivery does not apply to the membrane efflux transporter like p-glycoprotein (p–gp) which tends to be overexpressed in HCC, and also leads to development of multidrug resistance. Therefore liposomal systems have a great impact on increasing intracellular retention and bioavailability of flavonoids.

The second major action mechanism is the repression of PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, a principal cell growth, survival, and metabolism regulator in HCC. The PI3K activation is inhibited by Flavonoid(s) like quercetin and luteolin resulting in decreased phosphorylation of Akt and inhibition of mTOR. The consequences of this cascade are a reduction of protein synthesis, cell cycle arrest and activation of apoptotic signaling. Further, mTOR inhibition disrupts metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells further suppressing tumor growth.

Another important target is the JAK /STAT signaling pathway, mainly STAT3. The constitutive activation of constitutive activation of the STAT3 in HCC facilitates cell growth, angiogenesis, immune evasion, and resistance to apoptosis. Flavonoids have been found to prevent phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of the STAT3, and this procedure suppresses, oncogenic proteins like Bcl-2, survivin, and cyclin D1. Additionally, inhibiting of STAT3 can decrease the expression of PD -L1, which could lead to an increased antitumor immune response. It should not be forgotten though that more elaborate mechanistic evidence of the JAK2/STAT3/PD-L1 modulation has been better confirmed in non-HCC models and has yet to be confirmed in HCC-specific systems.

Flavonoids also have strong inhibitory effects against the NF- kB signaling pathway which is the central mark in inflammation mediated carcinogenesis. Chronic inflammation is part of the tumor initiation and progression in HCC. NF- kB can be suppressed by compounds like luteolin which prevents IkB alpha degradation which prevents transcription of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. TNF- agonist, IL-6) and angiogenic factors (e.g. VEGF). This results in inhibition of inflammation, angiogenesis and metastasis of tumors.

The other important mechanism is the induction of oxidative stress and dysfunction of mitochondria. Although flavonoids are thought to be antioxidants, in cancer cells they may play a pro-oxidant role, producing reactive oxygen species (ROS), which interfere with mitochondrial membrane potential. This initiates the intrinsic apoptotic unhappy, which is marked by the elevated ratio of Bax/Bcl-2, release of cytochrome c, and caspase-9 and caspase-3 activation. Timed intracellular release of these drugs, caused by liposomal delivery systems, and effective retention in tumor cells are essential to these effects.

Another function of liposomes loaded with flavonoids is the regulation of the cell cycle that is arrested in a certain stage (G1/S or G2/M). This is regulated by altering cyclins (e.g., cyclin D1, cyclin B1) and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs) which eventually suppresses DNA replication and mitotic progression. The induction of cell cycle arrest complements the effect of apoptosis and leads to high anticancer efficacy.

Also, these systems demonstrate considerable anti-angiogenic and anti-metastatic properties. Flavonoids block the Signaling of vascular endothelial growth Factor (VEGF) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) thus neovascularizing and invading the tumor. These effects are augmented by liposomal delivery that further accumulates in the tumor microenvironment.

A crucial feature about the liposomal systems is the capacity to utilize the tumor microenvironment (TME). Hypoxic environment, acidic pH of the tumor and enzyme-rich microenvironment of HCC tumors can be exploited to stimuli-responsive drug release; PH-responsive liposomes preferably release their cargo in acidic environments whereas enzyme-specific systems can be used on tumor-specific enzymes, which will localize and reduce systemic toxicity [74].

In addition, liposomal preparations can overcome multidrug resistance (MDR) avoiding efflux processes and regulating transporter expression. Flavonoids in and of themselves can inhibit the activity of P-glycoprotein, and liposomal encapsulation can cause the drug to avoid direct recognition by efflux pumps leading to increased accumulation within the cell.

Although such benefits exist, there are some shortcomings. The variability due to variations in receptor expression and the complex nature of signaling networks could result in inconsistencies in outcomes because of the absence of standardized mechanistic studies that have been performed on various HCC models. As well, although many pathways are involved, the exact role of each of them in clinical environments is yet to be clarified completely.

Altogether, the mechanism of action of flavonoid-loaded liposomes in HCC is multifactorial and includes the promotion of the delivery of drugs, suppression of the key oncogenic signaling pathways (PI3K/Akt/mTOR, JAK/STAT, NF-kB), promotion of apoptosis, prevention of angiogenesis, and surmounting drug resistance. The combination of these processes highlights the possibility of use of liposomal flavonoids as an effective therapeutic option, though more mechanistic and clinical confirmation is required to make the translation successful [75].

With liposomal systems increasing the extracellular delivery of flavonoids into the cell, a significant number of essential oncogenic pathways are modulated, which produces quantifiable biological effects. Apoptotic cells in cancer cells treated with liposomal flavonoids have been reported to increase 2 fourfold over their counterparts treated with free drug preparations. It is accompanied by the augmentation of caspase, dysfunction of the mitochondria, and cell cycle arrest at the G1/S or G2/M stage. Also, the use of signaling pathways (PI3K/Akt and JAK / STAT) inhibitors helps in decreased tumor cell survival and proliferation which further indicates the effectiveness of these formulations in treatment [76].

Targetting strategies

The focus of improving the therapeutic index of flavonoid-loaded liposomes in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is on targeting strategies, which define the biodistribution, penetration of the tumor, and intracellular delivery of the drug. With such a complicated pathophysiology of HCC, a mixture of passive, active and stimuli-responsive targeting mechanisms will be necessary in its effective treatment, that is; abnormal vasculature, prominent extracellular matrix, hypoxic locality, and nonhomogenous receptor expression [77].

Passive targeting and its limitations

Passive targeting is mainly regulated by the mechanism of enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect in which nanoscale liposomes (less than 200 nm) accumulate preferentially in tumor tissues following leaky vasculature, and poor lymphatic drainage. PEGylation of liposomes decreases opsonization and the turnaround time of the liposomes, thus, enhancing the likelihood of tumor concentration. The success of the EPR effect in HCC, however, is quite diverse and most of the time limited. Fibrotic stroma, elevated interstitial fluid pressure and uneven vascular permeability inhibit deep tumor penetration [78]. This, too, can be increased by substantial uptake by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), especially Kupffer cells in the liver, which deplete the amount of drug available at the tumor site. These drawbacks point to the fact that passive targeting is ineffective to treat HCC [79].

Active targeting via receptor-mediated uptake

Active targeting provides a greater degree of specificity with ligands remodeling the liposome surfaces to bind overexpressed receptors in HCC cells allowing receptor-mediated endocytosis. One of the best-researched liver-specific targets is the asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR) among others. It accepts galactose and N-acetylgalactosamine residues, to selectively uptake ligand-modified liposomes in hepatocytes and HCC cells. This targeting method enhances the accumulation in the liver to a considerable extent and reduces the off-target distribution.

Hyaluronic acid binding receptor (CD44) is very active in HCC and correlated with the progression of the tumor, dissemination, and the population of cancer stem cells. HA-functionalized liposomes exhibit cellular delivery and tumor localization, effects of CD44-mediated internalization. Likewise, the epithelial cell adhesion molecule (EpCAM) is upregulated in HCC and contributes to the process of tumor and stemness growth. Liposomes conjugated with antibodies or peptides against EpCAM have the potential to enhance tumor selectivity and anticancer effects [80].

The other targets that are emerging are glypican-3 (GPC3) and transferrin receptors; both are highly selective and overexpressed in HCC cells. Multireceptor (e.g. ASGPR + CD44) dual-ligand systems have demonstrated increased targeting efficacy by overcoming tumor heterogeneity and increasing cellular uptake by multiple pathways.

Stimuli-responsive liposomes introduce one more degree of specificity since they take advantage of the specific features of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Thermosensitive liposomes can be used to deliver drugs in response to localized hyperthermia, which would be externally administered..These systems improve localization of drugs and decrease systemic toxicity but still the clinical translation remains hindered because of stability and reproducibility obstacles [84].

Biomimetic and hybrid targeting approaches

Biomimetic approaches at the coatings in liposomes comprise of coating the liposome with a cell membrane (red blood cell, platelet or cancer cell) to prevent immune detection and prolong their lifespan in circulation. The liposomes coated with cancer cell membrane homotypic targeting and therefore binding with the same tumor cells. Hybrid systems that incorporate synthetic and biological components provide greater biocompatibility as well as targeting efficiency but add complexity to the formulation and standardization process.

Limitations and translational challenges

Even though it is progressed greatly, there are various limitation issues that prevent clinical translation. There is variation in expression of receptors between different patients and different tunes as well as decreasing targeting efficiency. Ligand-functionalized liposomes are typically associated with issues regarding scalability, stability and regulatory approval. Moreover, it is difficult to be sure that the cost of production will not be increased, as well as the problem of reproducibility is also difficult due to the complexity of multi-functional systems [86].

Pharmacokinetics and biodistribution

The drug delivery system of flavonoid-loaded liposomal systems in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is vital to their therapeutic efficacy and clinical translation with respect to pharmacokinetics and biodistribution [87]. Flavonoids of native origin quercetin, luteolin, silibinin are not well soluble in aqueous solutions, have high metabolic rates, are poorly absorbed, and their clearance by the liver is high. The liposomal encapsulation reduces such limitations dramatically in pharmacokinetic effects through increased systemic circulation, stability of drugs, and ability to deliver the drug to the tumor tissue [62].

Absorption and bioavailability

Free forms of flavonoids have a low oral bioavailability because of low solubility and high intestinal and hepatic metabolism. Liposomal preparations enhance absorption on the basis of 1) increasing flavonoids solubility and 2) inhibiting flavonoid breakdown at the gastrointestinal tract. The phospholipid bilayer ensures the communication with the biological membranes, thus facilitating transcellular transport. The liposomes may also be taken up by endocytosis in the intestinal epithelial cells, with enhanced systemic availability [88]. Nevertheless, absorption efficiency may also be influenced by variations in gastrointestinal conditions and stability of liposomes [43].

Distribution and tumor accumulation

Liposomal formulations have different distributions attended to free drugs after being administered systemically. Fluoroscopy is a third method to target liposomes to tumors using the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect due to their nanoscale size (usually less than 200 nm in diameter). PEGylated liposomes also extend the circulation by delaying opsonization and clearance through the reticuloendothelial system (RES) further exposing tumors [89].

In HCC, however, physiology of the liver plays a big role in the biodistribution. Kupffer cell density is high in the liver and it is the site that actively takes up nanoparticles resulting in serious accumulation in the liver tissue other than in the tumor. This has the ability to decrease drug distribution in the tumor site. Active targeting (e.g. ASGPR or CD44 targeting) is a technique used to enhance selective tumor concentration, through receptor-mediated uptake [90].

Metabolism and clearance

Flavonoids are also generally highly metabolized by phase II glucuronidation and sulfation to be cleared. The liposomal encapsulation of flavonoids helps in inhibiting immediate degradation of flavonoids in the metabolism, thus extending its half-life. When flavonoids are expended out of liposomes they go through conventional metabolic pathways in the liver.

The liposomes are removed in the first instance via the reticuloendothelial system (RES) especially in the liver and the spleen. PEGylations also decrease macrophages or accelerate clearance, but with repeated dosing, the effects of PEGylations can result in the accelerated blood clearance (ABC) phenomenon induced by anti-PEG IgM antibodies, and the subsequent doses are rapidly cleared [91].

Circulation half-life and stability

The extension of the half-life of the circulation is one of the greatest benefits of liposomal systems. Free flavonoids are quickly cleared of the circulation, but liposomal encapsulation offers a protective layer that retards degradation and clearance. Several physicochemical variables affect the circulation time among them being the size of the particle, the surface charge, and the composition of lipids. PEGylated liposomes and those that are smaller and neutrally charged have longer half-lives. Stability of the liposomes in the circulation is an issue however. Preservative drug leakage, lipid oxidation and aggregation processes may undermine therapeutic efficacy. Hence, optimization of formulations is to be undertaken to make the formulation stable during physiological conditions.

Tumor microenvironment and drug release

At this point, we will examine the subject of the tumor microenvironment and drug release in greater detail.Liposomal flavonoids are another example whose biodistribution is determined by the tumor microenvironment (TME) [92]. The tumours of HCC have acidic pH, hypoxia and high enzyme activity. These conditions can be utilized in stimuli-responsive liposomes in order to have site-specific delivery of drugs. Indicatively, the pH responsive liposomes deliver their cargo in acidic tumor necroses, which increases the local level of drugs and reduces systemic toxicity [74].

Intracellular distribution

Following tumor accumulation, liposomes are internalized by cancer cells via endocytosis. The intracellular fate of liposomes determines drug efficacy. Efficient endosomal escape is necessary to release flavonoids into the cytoplasm, where they can interact with molecular targets Without effective escape mechanisms, drugs may be degraded in lysosomes, reducing therapeutic activity [93].

The liposomal encapsulation offers a greater benefit of pharmacokinetic profile of flavonoids by promoting bioavailability and increasing the duration of systemic circulation. It has been demonstrated that liposomal formulations are able to enhance oral bioavailability in comparison with free flavonoids by around 2—fivefold. It also has a long circulation half-life, which can be considerably longer than the circulation half-life of free compounds (not more than 2 h), and in PEGylated liposomal systems is 612 h on average. With regards to biodistribution, the stability is increased and the clearance is lowered; that is, tumor accumulation is increased by 1.53 fold. The reticuloendothelial system (RES) uptake in accessing the target has been improved, though still high levels in the liver and the spleen may prove a challenge with regard to achieving overall efficiency on the targeting.

Limitations and translational challenges

Though there are the benefits of liposomal delivery, a variety of challenges still exist. Uptake by the RES which is not specific decreases targeting efficiency and inconsistencies in tumor accumulation occur due to variability of an EPR effect. Furthermore, animal models and human physiology are usually different, so the results on pharmacokinetics are not the same. There is no standardized analysis of pharmacokinetic indices such as bioavailability, tissue distribution and half-life across researches.Further studies are necessary to combine the approaches to targeting and pharmacokinetic optimization towards the most efficient and clinically translatable nanotherapeutics.

Design concept and decision framework of flavonoid- loaded liposomes

The development of flavonoid-loaded liposomes against hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) should be done as a rational step process and not a formulation process, that is, trial and error. The choice of the formulation should be informed by a clear understanding of a key of the properties of the drug, biological barriers and therapeutic objectives since both a drug (flavonoid) and a disease (HCC tumor biology) environment present certain problems [94].

Upon identifying the type of liposome, then there is the issue of the targeting strategy that should be adopted. Passive targeting by EPR effect is equated to be the mechanism of the baseline, but in HCC the mechanism is inconsistent. Fibrotic tissue and dysfunctional vasculature frequently cause a restriction in nanoparticle penetration leading to disrupted drug distribution.

It is on this basis that active targeting is typically required of HCC applications. When selecting targeting ligand, the choice should be made on the expression pattern of the receptor, as opposed to convenience. Indicatively, ASGPR targeting can be used in the delivery of liver-specific therapy, although this technique might not be effective at the cellular level in distinguishing normal hepatocytes and tumor cells. On the contrary, the EpCAM or CD44 targeting can be more tumor selective, at least in metastatic or aggressive tumors.

Tumor heterogeneity is another factor that should be considered. The receptors not all the cancer cells express may be a limitation to the performance of single-ligand systems. Under these circumstances, dual-targeting strategies can enhance the uptake, although complexity and cost of formulation can rise. Hence, the targeting strategies should be enough specific, feasible and reproducible [85].

Selection of drug release mechanism

The drug release profile is a critical design parameter that directly influences therapeutic outcomes. If the drug is released too early, it may be cleared before reaching the tumor site. If release is too slow, therapeutic concentrations may not be achieved.

Conventional liposomes provide passive and sustained release, which is useful for maintaining drug levels over time. However, this approach lacks site specificity. To address this limitation, stimuli-responsive systems are increasingly used.

However, these advanced systems require careful optimization, as they may face stability issues during storage and circulation. Therefore, the choice of release mechanism should consider both biological relevance and formulation stability.

Integration of physicochemical parameters

Physicochemical parameters are incorporated to understand the distribution properties of the unknown composition mixture and also establish the material components and chemical processes that the substance is composed of. Any liposomal system can only succeed by keeping the ideal physicochemical features. Particle size ought to be about less than 200 nm to enable entry into tumor and low PDI (less than 0.3) guarantees uniformity. Both cellular interaction and stability depend on the zeta potential and at around ± 30 mV are considered to be stable. These parameters do not stand alone. To mention but a few, particle size can be augmented by the addition of targeting ligands or PEG chains and the surface charge can be changed. Thus, the design of formulation must balance these factors carefully and ensure stability and functionality [103].

Consideration of biological barriers

There are also biological barriers that have to be considered in designing decisions that concern drug delivery. A significant obstacle is uptake by the reticuloendothelial system, especially in the liver, which may decrease the level of drug available to tumor cell. Dense extracellular matrix is another problem and results in poor penetration through the tumor mass.

PEGylation, size optimization, and penetration enhancers are among contrivances which can be employed to overcome these barriers, nevertheless none of them is wholly effective alone. Hence, an integrated strategy is typically necessary [104].

Translational and practical considerations

Lastly, the formulation design needs to take into consideration the practical aspects like scalability, reproducibility as well as regulatory. Although complex systems like multi-ligand or stimuli-responsive liposomes can be identified to have better performance in small-scale investigations, they are perhaps hard to reproducibly fabricate in large-scale applications.

Microfluidic-based techniques that provide more accurate and reproducible size control of the particles are more suitable in clinical translation. On the contrary, conventional techniques such as thin-film hydration can contribute to batch variability.

Regulatory factors are also considered. Liposomal systems should be of high quality standards, safety standards and stability standards with strict requirements before clinical use to avoid incompatibility. Thus, too complicated formulations can be greatly challenged when approved.

The practical and feasible design of flavonoid-loaded liposomes to HCC has to be balanced and combine the drug properties, tumor biology, as well as the practical feasibility. No one optimal formulation exists; rather the decision will be made on the therapeutic purpose at hand. An effective system must be designed to attain the required stability in circulation, good tumor targeting, controlled drug delivery, and scalability in order to use it in the clinical practice [105].

Clinical studies

Liposomes should be distinctly posed in contrast to other lipid-based delivery systems, including phytosomes. Although the liposomes contain hydrophilic drugs and hydrophobic drugs as can be encapsulated in phospholipid bilayer vesicles, phytosomes are molecular structures of phospholipids interacting with phytoconstituents used to enhance absorption and not to deliver drugs in a controlled manner. The controlled release, the ability to functionalize surfaces to enable active targeting, optimal tumor accumulation are some of the strengths of liposomes compared to phytosomes. Although these have such advantages, the majority of lipid-based phytochemical preparations tested clinically are phytosomal, and there is an important clinical gap in the use of liposomes as carriers of flavonoid delivery of HCC. Clinical translation of flavonoid-based treatments in the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is insufficient in spite of solid preclinical evidence which has shown their anticancer capacity. Free flavonoids and flavonolignans have a number of pharmacokinetic and physicochemical disadvantages such as rapid metabolism, low solubility in aqueous medium, low cellular absorption, and systemic instability. All these contribute to their greatly decreased bioavailability and make it difficult to facilitate their advancement to clinical viable anticancer agents [106]. Due to this, advancement of carrier based delivery mechanisms especially liposomes has been necessary in order to address these barriers [101].

The liposomal drug delivery systems have gained popularity after they were discovered to enhance solubility, stability, increase the duration in circulation as well as deliver drugs selectively. Liposomes are among the most medically proven nanocarrier systems used in oncology with a number of its formulations being used as approved drugs in cancer therapy. They are most likely to succeed when used as translational vectors due to their biocompatibility, capacity to take in hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs, and flexibility in surfaces modification; nonetheless, when particularly using flavonoid-loaded liposomes in HCC, the presence of clinical evidence is limited. The majority of investigations are restricted to in vitro and in vivo systems, and few of them are carried out to the clinical trials. This leaves an enormous gap between the research and clinical practice of the experiment. Compared to traditional chemotherapeutics, flavonoid based liposomal systems are yet to prove their clinical effectiveness in clinical studies in a large scale among human beings [107].

One of the main problems that lead to such a gap is the inconsistency of the design of formulation and the absence of standardized evaluation parameters in the different studies. Its size and variability in size, encapsulation, and targeting strategies complicate the results and rendering the outcomes in an organized manner. Moreover, most of the researches concentrate on the proof of biological activity without properly considering pharmacokinetics, toxicity, and long-term safety, which is an imperative consideration to be approved by the clinic. The other significant issue is the translation of the targeting strategies. Although ligand-functionalized liposomes (e.g., ASGPR or CD44 receptor targeting) perform well in preclinical models, it remains unclear whether these functional liposomes can work in cancer patients because some of these receptors are expressed variably, and some tumors are heterogeneous [108]. On the same note, dependence on the augmented permeability and retention (EPR) effect has been rather unsuccessful in clinical trials, especially in HCC with fibrotic tissue limiting the entry of nanoparticles.Clinical translation is complicated further by manufacturability and regulatory issues. The refined liposomal systems (e.g. multifunctional or stimuli-responsive preparations) typically have more complicated synthesis pathways, which are challenging to scale and normalise in terms of Good Manufacturing Practises (GMP). Extensive data on stability, reproducibility, toxicity and efficacy is also demanded by regulatory approval and are currently unavailable with most flavonoid loaded liposomal systems.

Altogether, the clinical transfer of flavonoid-containing liposomes in the treatment of HCC is still at its initial phase. Its key gaps are a paucity of clinical trials, absent standardized formulation regimens, inadequate pharmacokinetic and safety information and difficulty in producing in large-scale quantities. Limitation of translation research

Over the recent years, naturally occurring compounds have been given an increasing interest based on their pronounced potential in therapeutic interest. Nevertheless, they tend to be restricted by their lack of solubility in water, hydrophilic properties, and quick extraction in the body, thus limiting their application in the clinical professions. In order to address these shortcomings, a number of drug delivery systems based on nanotechnology have been suggested. Among them, lipid-based nanocarriers are significant because they provide the biochemical elevation of bioactive compounds in their hydrophobic domains, then increasing the levels of solubility, resulting in a greater rate of bioavailability.

Although there is a significant literature on these compounds, several areas of study are poorly investigated such as the intracellular pathways, optimum dosage, stability over the long run as well as possible pharmacological interactions. Also, the commercial use of nanomaterials remains limited due to the absence of awareness and acceptance of the society, which is why it is the time to improve education and communication in the sphere. Moreover, lipid-based nanoformulations can have a relatively poor encapsulation efficacy that can potentially lead to the necessity to dose more and the probability of toxicity. High-cost raw materials and high volume of production all lead to economic problems. Further, a large proportion of the research that has been done is confined in small-scale in vitro experiments, with scanty in vivo and clinical validation, thus, limiting their application.

Still, lipid-based nanoparticles are promising in their future therapeutic opportunities. Their capability to perform combination therapy by co-delivering hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs gives the capacity to increase therapeutic effectiveness, decrease drug resistance and enables the ability to reduce dose. Recent developments in the development of lipid nanocarriers, like the ethosomes, the transfersomes, invasomes and other flexible vesicles carriers, have brought with it superior flexiveness of the membrane as well as drug transport capability. To aid effective clinical translation, a research question to ask in the future is on scalable manufacturing methods, more clinical trials, and interdisciplinary approach that incorporates the pharmaceutical, clinical, and regulatory points of view.

Although flavonoid-loaded liposomes have a great potential in the treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), a number of limitations have been identified that are critical to its use in clinical rooms. Formulation stability is one of the major concerns because liposomes are likely to undergo aggregation, fusion, and leakage during storage and circulation. Lipid oxidation may also undermine structural integrity and retention of drugs. PEGylation to enhance stability and cholesterol incorporation are effective but they are not ensuring 100 percent stability.

Issues of reproducibility and batch-to-batch variability can also be a problem. Traditional techniques of preparation, including thin-film hydration, are very sensitive to processing conditions, which causes the particles to vary in the degree of polydispersity, size, and encapsulation activity. Although more sophisticated methods, such as microfluidics, have a higher level of reproducibility, they have a higher cost and technical complication.

Another important impediment is economic feasibility. Liposomal formulations, and in particular surface modifications, are costly and demand specific equipment and vigorous quality control, uppreaching total expense. It is also challenging to scale up such formulations to the level of consistency and compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP).

There is also a large distance between clinical application and preclinical research. Despite numerous studies exhibiting potentially good anticancer activity in vitro and animal models, only a small number of them have undergone clinical testing. The major cause of this gap is the lack of pharmacokinetic and biodistribution and long-term safety data and the application of simplified experimental models that do not necessarily reflect human tumor biology.

Also, dependency on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect constrains the clinical witness because this phenomenon is not as pronounced and very varied in human tumors, especially in HCC as fibrotic tissue inhibits the growth of nanoparticles. There is also an issue with variability in receptor expression and an increase in the complexity of formulation, leading to problems with targeting strategies and potentially stability and regulatory approval.

Last, the issue of safety is also important. Repeated administration of liposomal systems themselves may cause immune reactions, such as the activation of complement, and the accelerated blood clearance (ABC) effect. In the long term, toxicity and biological interactions are yet to be studied and applied clinically. Although flavonoid-loaded liposomal system has shown promising preclinical results, there is a limited availability of clinical translation of flavonoid-loaded liposomal systems. About one in five nanoformulations based on flavonoids has been developed to clinical trials, which would suggest a sizeable gap in translational research. Although liposomal systems enhance pharmacokinetic characteristics and targeting efficiency, issues such as variability of formulations to be used, absence of uniform means of evaluation and inadequate long-term safety figures remain to impede clinical developments. No flavonoid-contained liposomal preparation has been currently approved as a treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma thus there is a need to further validate such a product [109].

To conclude, although flavonoid-loaded liposomes have significant benefits in enhancing drug delivery and treatment effectiveness in the case of HCC, their effective clinical implementation should deal with multiple issues associated with stability, reproducibility, cost, and clinical validation. The gap between the experimental studies and the clinical practice should be bridged by further attempts made to optimize the design of formulation, standardize approaches to evaluation, increase scalability and more importantly carry out well-designed clinical studies.

Liposomal systems safety and toxicity

Liposomal drug delivery systems are typically considered to be biocompatible and not that toxic as compared to traditional preparations but various safety issues need to be exercised with regards to clinical translation. One of the biggest drawbacks linked with PEGylated liposomes is the accelerated blood clearance (ABC) that develops after repeated drugs administration. This occurs through the generation of anti-PEG IgM antibodies which amplify opsonization and subsequent clearance by the liver reticulo endothelial system (RES) especially by the Kupffer cells [110]. This lowers the circulation time and could interfere with therapeutic effectiveness. Moreover, liposomes can cause complement activation-related pseudoallergy (CARPA), which can result in hypersensitivity responses, like infusion-related responses. This reaction is dependent on the liposome composition, size and surface charge [111]. Although positively charged (cationic) liposomes are the most useful when it comes to cellular uptake, cytotoxicity has been linked to a larger proportion of membrane disruption and oxidative stress.The potential long-term toxicity and dose-dependence have not been studied well especially in the case of multifunctional and ligand-targeted liposomes [112]. The deposition in the liver and spleen by the RES uptake could result in organ-specific toxicity when administered repeatedly [113]. Moreover, high systemic variability of the formulation parameters may have an effect on safety profiles.In order to eliminate such shortcomings, a number of approaches have been suggested where PEG alternatives (such as polysarcosine), cleavable PEG coating, biomimetic liposomes, and optimization of surface charge have been used. These strategies are to decrease the immunogenicity, enhance circulation stability and safety [114]. Altogether, liposomal systems have a high potential in terms of drug delivery, but toxicological assessment and long-term safety are necessary to make the clinical translation successful [115].

Discussion

Hepatocellular carcinoma is a condition that still presents a major challenge to therapy because of its complicated pathogenesis, late diagnosis, high recurrence rate, and low efficacy of the available therapeutic actions. Even though there has been some clinical improvement with the introduction of targeted therapies like the sorafenib, the problem of drug resistance and systemic toxicity is yet to be tackled. In that regard, flavonoids and flavonolignans have become more likely to be utilized as such due to their potential to regulate several cancer-relevant pathways and achieve anticancer effects by a variety of different mechanisms.[119] Nevertheless, due to poor pharmacokinetic characteristics such as low solubility, rapid catabolism, and bioavailability, the clinical use of the phytochemicals is highly limited. Liposomal drug delivery systems can present a practical approach to these shortcomings by providing a better approach to drug stability, delivery, and localization. The potential of these systems can be enhanced even more by more complex strategies like PEGylation, ligand-mediated targeting and stimuli-responsive release.Although, there are promising preclinical data that showed better efficacy and less toxicity, the change between experimental work and clinical practice is still scarce. Formulation instabilities, reproducibilities, high cost of production and unstandardized methods of evaluation are some of the challenges that need to be dealt with. Also, the lack of clinical evidence and regulatory complications has remained an obstacle to the use of liposomal flavonoid preparations.Further studies are needed to elaborate more amount of scalable/reproducible formulations, extensive pharmacokinetic and safety studies, and well designed clinical trials. More accuracy in alignment of regulations and interdisciplinary cooperation will also be needed to create successful translation.

Altogether, liposomal flavonoid delivery is a new and upcoming modality of treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma on the basis of liposomes. These systems can bring something new to more effective and more targeted ways to treat cancer on the basis of further optimization and clinical validation.

Conclusion

A hopeful strategy for enhancing therapy of HCC is the administration of flavonoids via liposomes. Flavonoids have potent anti-inflammatory, anti-cancer, and antioxidant qualities, but their low bioavailability, quick metabolism, and poor solubility limit their clinical use. By increasing tumor accumulation, decreasing systemic toxicity, shortening circulation time, and improving medication stability, liposomes aid in overcoming these obstacles. Compared to free flavonoids, studies have demonstrated that liposomal flavonoid formulations offer higher tumor growth suppression and superior cellular absorption. Although promising preclinical findings point to their medicinal potential, issues with large-scale manufacturing, long-term stability, and clinical validation require more investigation. All things considered, liposomal flavonoids are a secure and successful nanotechnology-based approach that could greatly enhance HCC treatment.
