Heatstroke admissions to Northeastern and Southern hospitals in the United States from 2016-2020
Authors: Evans KH Brown, Scott M Wright, Che Matthew Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeatstroke is a life-threatening condition representing the most severe manifestation of heat-related illnesses. Increasing global average temperatures are likely to increase the incidence of heatstroke.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, repeated cross-sectional study, using the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database. We assessed adults hospitalized in the Northeast and South regions of the United States with ICD-10 codes relating to heatstroke. The primary outcome was the number of monthly regional admissions for heatstroke. Multiple logistic and linear regression techniques were used to evaluate secondary outcomes, specifically in-hospital clinical and resource utilization outcomes.FindingsOver the five years studied (2016-2020), more heatstroke admissions occurred in the South (n=3,880) than in the Northeast (n=960). The length of stay in the South was nearly one day longer than in the Northeast (aMD=0.95 days, p=0.02). There was a trend towards higher charges per heatstroke admission in the South compared to the Northeast (aMD=$16,514, p=0.07). Admission rates were positively associated with increasing average monthly temperatures - even after controlling for region and season (p<0.05).InterpretationHeatstroke is a serious condition, and admissions to our nation’s hospitals are likely to increase as our planet gets warmer. Many physiologic, societal, and geographic variables impact vulnerability to heat-related illness. Our society and healthcare systems must actively work towards improving care for those with heat-related illnesses.FundingNot applicable.Keywords: Heatstroke, Planetary health, Hospitalizations

Introduction

Anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions, driven largely by fossil fuel combustion and land use change, have led to rising global average temperatures; this evolving reality is increasing the frequency, duration, and intensity of extreme heat events [[1], [2], [3]]. Global average surface temperatures have increased by approximately 1.1-1.2 C above preindustrial levels, with continued warming projected across all U.S. Regions [4,5]. Scientific consensus attributes the observed increase in extreme heat events primarily to human influence and projects further increases in the frequency and severity of heat extremes throughout the 21st century [6,7]. As global temperatures rise, people are exposed to higher levels of heat, contributing to increased rates of heat-related illness [[1], [2], [3]]. Exposure to high ambient temperatures can produce a range of physiologic impacts on humans; these may be categorized on a spectrum of heat-related illnesses. Heatstroke is a life-threatening condition and represents the most severe form of heat-related illness, characterized clinically by extremely elevated body temperature, neurologic dysfunction, and multiorgan failure. Vulnerability to ambient temperatures is impacted by individual, community, and systemic factors, including neighborhood characteristics, housing quality, socioeconomic status, and access to cooling infrastructure [[8], [9], [10]]. Furthermore, it is reasonable to expect a higher prevalence of heatstroke in hotter climates. However, emerging research indicates that some of the most destructive extreme weather events occur where the structural adaptive capacities of a community are limited - for example, heat waves in communities without air-conditioned homes [11]. These patterns underscore the environmental justice dimensions of extreme heat exposure, in which historically marginalized communities often experience disproportionate thermal burden and reduced adaptive capacity [10,12,13].

This study sought to investigate regional differences in heatstroke admissions, particularly exploring the impact of monthly average temperatures across disparate areas. We compared heatstroke admissions in the Northeast and Southern regions of the United States. These regions were chosen because of their distinct latitudes, regional climates, and how the country is divided in the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) dataset. Our hypothesis was that there would be an increased heatstroke admission rate in the South compared to the Northeast and that there would be positive associations between monthly temperature and heatstroke admissions. Secondary aims were focused on exploring differences in clinical outcomes and resource utilization among patients admitted to hospitals with heatstroke in the Northeast compared to the South.

Methods

Database

Patient data were collected from the National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database years 2016 to 2020. This database is available via the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality via the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) [14]. The NIS is a large, publicly available all-payer inpatient healthcare database for hospitals in the United States (U.S.). It approximates a 20% stratified sample of hospital discharges from 46 participating states and the District of Columbia. Over 7 million unweighted hospital stays each year are in the database. When weighted to represent all admissions, it estimates more than 35 million hospitalizations annually. This represents 95% of hospitalizations in the U.S. Strata include hospital size/volume, academic teaching status, geographic region, and hospital ownership. Our study years utilize the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10 CM) coding system for all discharge diagnoses.

Temperature methodology

Average monthly temperature by state was obtained from the National Centers for Environmental Information (NCEI) for the years 2016 - 2020. These monthly average temperatures for each state were then aggregated to create a monthly average temperature for Northeast and South regions, as defined by the U.S. Census Bureau and used by the NIS. All statistical manipulation of weather information was performed using R Statistical Software v4.3.2 [15].

Study population, patient and hospital characteristics, and outcomes

Hospitalized patients with a primary ICD-10 diagnosis code for heat stroke (T67*) were included. Patients with heatstroke were compared by two regions—Northeast vs. South. The primary outcomes were the variable rates of heatstroke admissions across the study period and how heatstroke admissions varied by monthly ambient temperatures. Secondary outcomes were (i) clinical variables [in-hospital mortality and discharge to home], and (ii) resource utilization - defined by length of stay (LOS) and total hospital charges. For the clinical and utilization data, we controlled for the following a priori hospital and patient potential confounders in our multivariable regression models: (i) hospital teaching status, (ii) hospital volume capacity, (iii) patient age, (iv) gender (male and female only), (v) insurance (Medicare, Medicaid, Private Insurance, Uninsured); (vi) median household income (from <$38,999 to >$63,000 based on home zip code), (vii) severity of illness (no class specified, minor loss of function, moderate loss of function, major loss of function, and extreme loss of function), (viii) risk of mortality (no class specified, minor likelihood of dying, moderate likelihood of dying, major likelihood of dying, and extreme likelihood of dying), and (ix) comorbidity burden assessed using the Elixhauser comorbidity index—this variable consists of 30 comorbidities to measure chronic conditions and was created for in-hospital administrative databases to predict in-hospital outcomes [16].

Statistical analysis

The yearly heatstroke rate per region was determined by aggregating all heatstroke admissions in each region across a single year, dividing by total non-elective hospital admissions in that region. Similar calculations were made for monthly heatstroke rates in each region. Yearly heatstroke admission rates by region were compared using chi-squared tests.

Calculations and analysis involving regional monthly temperature and admissions were performed using R Statistical Software v4.3.2 and the R package data.table v1.14.10 [15]. We specified the following regression model for monthly heatstroke admission rates:

The function f(Temperature) represents a natural cubic spline function with four degrees of freedom selected based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Season and Year were modeled as categorical variables. To evaluate regional differences in temperature–admission dynamics, interaction terms between region and the spline basis functions were tested using nested model comparison (F-test). Model fit was evaluated using R² and AIC statistics. Sensitivity analyses excluding months with very low admission counts were conducted to ensure robustness of findings. Figures were made in R using the package ggplot2 [17,18].

Pearson’s χ2 and Adjusted Wald tests were used for categorical and continuous variables. Adjusted odds ratios [aOR] for in-hospital mortality were obtained using multivariable logistic regression. Adjusted mean differences [aMD] from multivariable linear regression analyses were employed to compare LOS and total hospital charges. P-values were two-sided, and the significance level of type I errors was 0.05. Stata 15.0 statistical software (Stata Corp, College Station, TX) allowed us to account for design complexity (stratification, weighting, and clustering) and incorporated strata and primary and sampling weights [19].

Results

Heatstroke accounted for 4,840 hospitalizations in the Northeast and South regions of the country from January 2016 to December 2020. Over the five years, the South had 3880 total heatstroke admissions (80%) compared with 960 (20%) in the Northeast – yielding a rate of 8.5 heatstroke admissions per 100,000 non-elective hospital admission per year in the South compared to 4.4 heatstroke admissions per 100,000 non-elective hospital admission per year in the Northeast (p-value < 0.00001). Across the study period, the range in monthly admission rates in the South was between 0 and 42 per 100,000, while in the Northeast, it varied between 0 and 22 per 100,000 non-elective admissions. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows regional monthly heatstroke admission rate per 100,000 admissions across the study period.

In-hospital mortality and utilization outcomes

As shown in Table 2, following adjustment, patients admitted with heatstroke in the South and Northeast regions had similar odds for in-hospital mortality (aOR = 0.84 [CI 0.22-3.0], p = 0.79). Patients from both regions were equally likely to be discharged home (aOR = 0.9 [CI 0.5 – 1.4), p = 0.72). Length of stay for patients admitted with heatstroke in the South was significantly longer by nearly one day than in the Northeast (aMD 0.95 days [CI 0.13 – 1.7], p = 0.02). There was also a trend toward higher mean charge per case in the South compared to the Northeast (aMD $16,514 [CI -$1525 – $34,553], p = 0.07). Sensitivity analyses examined outcomes separately for each study year (2016–2020) to assess whether the COVID-19 period disproportionately influenced results. Estimates for total charges, length of stay, and in-hospital mortality were similar across all years, including 2020.

Monthly average temperature and heatstroke admission rate

From 2016 to 2020, the monthly average temperature in the South was a mean of 13.6°F hotter than the Northeast (minimum difference 6.6°F, maximum difference 23.3°F). Seasonal trends were observed in both regions such that the proportion of heatstroke admissions was 5.5 and 5.8 times higher in June, July, and August compared to any other non-overlapping three-month period for the South and Northeast, respectively (Fig. 1). The number of monthly heatstroke admissions was positively associated with increasing average monthly temperatures, even after controlling for region and season (p < 0.05, Fig. 2).

Allowing for nonlinearity in temperature effects improved model fit compared with a linear temperature specification, with the lowest AIC achieved using a natural cubic spline with four degrees of freedom (AIC = 701.1, R² = 0.82). However, there was no evidence that the nonlinear temperature–admission relationship differed by region (interaction F-test p = 0.19). Results from sensitivity analyses that excluded months with very low admission counts were materially unchanged.

Discussion

This study describes the regional differences in heatstroke admissions in the U.S. and the relation between ambient temperature and heatstroke hospitalizations. Heatstroke admissions were more common during hot months compared to cooler months, and more heatstroke admissions occurred in the South compared to the Northeast, even after accounting for regional population differences.

Our data are consistent with prior research that suggests that socioeconomic and community-level factors significantly influence susceptibility to heat-related illness [3,4]. We observed marked socioeconomic disparities in heatstroke hospitalizations, particularly in the South, where over 40% of patients were from the lowest income quartile compared with fewer than 20% in the Northeast. This pattern highlights the role of structural determinants of heat vulnerability, including housing, occupation, insurance coverage, and access to cooling resources [4,8]. The higher number of heatstroke admissions in the South during the hottest months, particularly among non-whites with lower incomes, might suggest that they have living situations without air conditioning and /or lived experiences causing them to spend more time outside. The striking relation between monthly average temperature and heatstroke admissions has been suggested by prior publications focusing on heat exposure [3,4]. While this association is not unexpected, our findings highlight how these dynamics vary across regions and seasons. Importantly, nonlinear modeling demonstrated that the Northeast and South follow a similar temperature–admission response pattern; this suggests that the observed regional differences may be attributable to the Northeast experiencing a lower range of monthly temperatures rather than fundamentally different dynamics of exposure and vulnerability.

Patients in the South had a longer length of stay compared to the Northeast, and we also saw a trend toward an increase in total charges of hospital care for these heatstroke admissions in the South. There are many possibilities for these differences, one of which is increased heat exposure implied by the temperature data which could result in more severe illness. Many conditions that involve environmental or toxic exposure, such as burns, intoxication, or poisoning, exhibit a dose (or exposure) response relationship, in which a greater insult leads to a graver presentation and requires more complex and expensive care for longer time periods [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Other explanations for the observed regional differences in length of stay and cost could involve discharge resources, insurance, or other factors that deserve further investigation in future work. Notably, we saw a significantly larger number of uninsured persons in the South compared to NE, as well as a larger proportion of Black and Hispanic persons. These socioeconomic and societal factors may influence heatstroke vulnerability and deserve further investigation. While there were no statistically significant regional differences in in-hospital mortality, the number of deaths in the hospital was relatively small. Consequently, confidence intervals were wide, and the analysis may have been underpowered to detect modest differences between regions. Also, survivor bias should be considered as some may have died before ever reaching a hospital.

Taken together, these findings underscore the need to improve heat resilience, particularly in the context of a warming climates. As previously noted, vulnerability to heat-related illness is complex, and so any approach to improving community heat resilience should be multifaceted. Population-level climate adaptation strategies include regionally tailored heat action plans, early warning systems, expansion of cooling centers, occupational heat standards, and housing / building policies that promote heat-resilience design [4]. Individual-level protections, such as hydration, air conditioning use, and heat-safety education, are also critical for complementing population-level interventions [4]. While mitigation of greenhouse gas emissions is essential to limit future warming, healthcare systems must also be prepared to respond effectively to periods of extreme heat [2,4].

Several limitations of this study should be considered. First, similar to other studies that use administrative databases, NIS data depend on the accuracy of diagnostic coding. Fortunately, heatstroke is a specific diagnosis that is not usually a diagnostic mystery. Second, the NIS lacks information on laboratory data, imaging, medications, and detailed comorbidity data [24]. As noted above, the relatively small number of mortality events limited statistical power to detect modest regional differences in mortality. Additionally, the overall sample size constrained more detailed stratified analyses across socioeconomic subgroups. Third, the ambient temperature data were aggregated over a large geographic scale (by region) and averaged monthly. This resulted in lower-resolution heat exposure data that did not capture known variation in individual-level exposure, such as the effects of urban heat islands and indoor or outdoor microclimates. Further studies are warranted that involve individual level data to more precisely assess questions involving heat, the built environment, and heatstroke admissions. In addition, the available NIS data, years 2016-2020, predates the extreme temperatures of the past five years, which could impact the generalizability of our findings to the current and future environmental realities. Finally, as with all observational studies, there may be unmeasured confounders that could influence the observed associations, and the associations observed do not establish causality.

Conclusion

This national study, pulling data over several years, confirms that during the hottest months of the year, particularly in the hotter regions, more cases of heatstroke occur in communities that are severe enough to precipitate hospitalization. During these hospitalizations, the charges and length of stay vary significantly between the Northeast and South regions of the U.S. Because susceptibility to heat-related illness is complex, community-based preventive approaches should be multifaceted. Until our public health policies have been strengthened to substantively advance heatstroke resilience, our healthcare systems need to be ready to care for these acutely ill patients.
